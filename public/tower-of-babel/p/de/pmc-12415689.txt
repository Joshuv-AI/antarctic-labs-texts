Multi-anatomical site prevalence of Mycoplasma genitalium infection, resistance and association with coinfections: a prospective study in MSM with HIV
Authors: Ngai Sze Wong, Bonnie Chun Kwan Wong, Denise Pui-Chung Chan, Danny King Fai Cheung, Heather Ki Wai To, Sze Long Chung, Margaret Ip, Shui Shan Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Mycoplasma genitalium is a cause of sexually transmitted infections (STIs). This study assessed its prevalence, resistance and coinfection with Chlamydia trachomatis/Neisseria gonorrhoeae infections in MSM with HIV.MethodsMSM in HIV care in Hong Kong were recruited during 2023–24 for completion of an online survey, and self-collection of urine specimens, rectal and pharyngeal swabs, which were tested for C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae, M. genitalium and M. genitalium resistance-associated mutations (RAMs). The main outcome variables included M. genitalium infection and RAMs at individual and anatomic-site levels.FindingsAmong 562 MSM with HIV aged 20–71 years, all-site prevalence was 20% for M. genitalium, 19% for C. trachomatis and 10% for N. gonorrhoeae. Among C. trachomatis/N. gonorrhoeae infections, 28% were coinfected with M. genitalium. The prevalence was highest at the rectal site for M. genitalium (14%), C. trachomatis (17%) and N. gonorrhoeae (8%). M. genitalium infection was associated with N. gonorrhoeae (OR = 3.29 at the individual level, OR = 4.76 at pharyngeal sites and OR = 4.00 at rectal sites) but not C. trachomatis infection. Among 102 typeable M. genitalium-positive samples, 88 (86%) had RAMs—macrolides (78%), fluoroquinolones (49%) and both (41%). Recent sexualized drug use was a significant predictor for urethral M. genitalium RAMs [adjusted OR (aOR) = 3.21], while prolonged symptoms (aOR = 4.82), N. gonorrhoeae coinfection (aOR = 3.19) and HIV diagnosis in 2015–19 (aOR = 6.20) were predictors for rectal RAMs. The cure rate for non-susceptible M. genitalium infection was satisfactory with moxifloxacin or minocycline but not with azithromycin.Conclusions M. genitalium RAM prevalence is high in MSM with HIV, in whom rectal infection and C. trachomatis/N. gonorrhoeae coinfections are common.

Introduction

Mycoplasma genitalium is an emerging pathogen that can be transmitted sexually. Epidemiologically, the pooled M. genitalium prevalence was 3.2% (95% CI = 2.1%–5.1%) in the MSM community, 16.7% in MSM on HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis, and 12% in people with HIV in meta-analysis studies.1–3

Sharing the same route of transmission and sexual network, M. genitalium coinfection with Chlamydia trachomatis and/or Neisseria gonorrhoeae in MSM is not uncommon. The M. genitalium coinfection rate of MSM with C. trachomatis and/or N. gonorrhoeae with tested urine specimens and rectal swabs was 17% in Australia and 14% in Sweden.4,5 Clinical manifestations of M. genitalium and C. trachomatis infection, such as non-gonococcal urethritis (NGU),6 are difficult to differentiate.7 A syndromic management approach could result in negative consequences by providing shorter monotherapy of chlamydia for treating M. genitalium infection, which requires dual therapy with longer treatment duration.8 In addition, the differential approaches of limited symptomatic M. genitalium testing8–11 in comparison with regular multi-anatomical site C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae screening in MSM10 may result in unintended treatment for undiagnosed M. genitalium infection. M. genitalium infections could be self-limiting without intervention, and asymptomatic testing is currently not recommended in international guidelines,8–11 yet suboptimal treatment of other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) could predispose to the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Mapping the prevalence and relationships among C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae and M. genitalium infections would be important for evaluating testing strategies, especially for MSM in HIV care who have regular C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae screening.

Although it is a natural process of genetic changes of pathogens over time, the speed of AMR escalation could be accelerated by the spread of resistant strains, or misuse or overuse of antimicrobials.12 The prevalence of M. genitalium resistance-associated mutations (RAMs) was high and had an increasing trend despite limited M. genitalium testing clinically. The pooled prevalence of macrolide RAMs increased from 10% before 2010 to 51.4% in 2016–17, and the prevalence of fluoroquinolone RAMs was not low at 7.7% in M. genitalium-positive samples.13 To date, only a few studies have estimated the prevalence of M. genitalium RAMs in MSM with HIV. In the USA, the estimated prevalence of M. genitalium was 11% and 6%, of macrolide RAMs was 71% and 80%, and fluoroquinolone RAMs was 27% and 40% for urogenital and rectal sites from archived samples, respectively.14 Another study, involving anorectal samples collected from MSM with HIV in Germany, gave an estimated 8% for M. genitalium positivity, of which 60% were positive for macrolide RAMs, 30% with parC resistance, and 25% had dual resistance.15 However, neither study explored the factors associated with M. genitalium RAMs in MSM with HIV. In this cross-sectional study, we aim to assess the prevalence of M. genitalium infection and resistance, and examine its association with C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae infections.

Materials and methods

Subject recruitment and logistics

In collaboration with the largest HIV specialist clinic in Hong Kong, with a caseload of over 4000, we conducted a cross-sectional study with recruitment of male participants aged 18 years or above, attending the clinic, and able to communicate in either English or Chinese. Following written consent, recruited participants were invited to complete a self-administered online survey and self-collection of first-void urine specimen, rectal and pharyngeal swabs using the Aptima Urine and Aptima Unisex Swab collection kits (Hologic) at the clinic (Figure 1). If the participant at recruitment was receiving routine STI testing, the corresponding C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae test results were transcribed from the clinic afterwards.

Study layout. CT, C. trachomatis; MG, M. genitalium; NG, N. gonorrhoeae; TOC, test-of-cure.

Clinical referral was offered to M. genitalium-positive participants for treatment and partner testing. Test-of-cure (TOC) was recommended for participants who tested M. genitalium positive 8–10 weeks after treatment initiation. Repeated TOC was performed until negative M. genitalium results were obtained. The procedural arrangement of C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae TOC followed the practice of the clinic. Clinical records including HIV conditions (e.g. HIV diagnosis year, ART initiation year, and measurements of CD4, CD8 and viral load in the study period), routine STI testing (e.g. C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae) results in the study period, and STI treatment prescription records since HIV diagnosis of the participants were collected retrospectively. An incentive of an HKD50 (USD1∼HKD7.8) supermarket voucher was offered. Ethical approval from the Joint Chinese University of Hong Kong and New Territories East Cluster Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval number: CREC2022.547) and the Ethics Committee of the Department of Health, Hong Kong Special Administrative Region Government were obtained.

Laboratory testing

The collected specimens were subjected to molecular testing of M. genitalium infection with the commercially available Aptima Mycoplasma genitalium assay on the Panther system (Hologic). The same set of multi-test swab specimens was used for C. trachomatis/N. gonorrhoeae detection with the Aptima Combo 2 Assay (Hologic). All specimens were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For resistance marker detection, DNA was extracted from M. genitalium-positive samples using the QIAamp DNA mini kit (QIAGEN). Mutations associated with macrolides (23S rRNA gene) and fluoroquinolones (parC and gyrA genes) were screened by nested PCR16–18 and Sanger sequencing. The resulting sequences were compared with the reference genome of the WT strain M. genitalium G37 (GenBank accession no. NC_000908.2).

Data analysis

Only MSM participants without a history of M. genitalium diagnosis in the past 2 years were included for analysis. The main outcome variables included M. genitalium positivity (any anatomical site positive, versus negative results) and M. genitalium RAMs (detection of RAMs to macrolides (23S rRNA) and/or fluoroquinolones (gyrA and parC genes), versus no detection of RAMs, undetermined RAMs or M. genitalium-negative result). Coinfection referred to concurrent detection of M. genitalium with C. trachomatis and/or N. gonorrhoeae at any anatomical site. Prevalence with 95% CI was estimated using the binomial exact method.

Sexualized drug use (SDU) is defined as the use of recreational drugs [e.g. methamphetamine, gamma-hydroxybutyrate (GHB), poppers/rush, ecstasy, erectile dysfunction agents and cannabis] before/during sex. Chemsex engagement is part of SDU, which refers to the use of specific drugs (methamphetamine and/or GHB) before/during sex.19,20 CD4 and viral load measurement recorded within 180 days before and 90 days after sampling date were included. Prescribed daily dose (PDD) of each antimicrobial class was calculated at the individual level by the total amount of drug prescribed divided by the number of treatment days,21 using clinic prescription records between HIV diagnosis and study sampling date.

Factors associated with the main outcome variables were examined in multivariable logistic regression models, with the input of P < 0.1 variable in bivariable logistic regression in a stepwise backward (conditional) approach in SPSS 28. Complete-case analyses were performed.

Results

General characteristics and M. genitalium positivity of participants

Between 24 July 2023 and 8 November 2024, 573 males with HIV were recruited. After excluding 8 heterosexual men and 3 MSM with a previous diagnosis history of M. genitalium infection, a total of 562 MSM with HIV were included in the analysis. Among them (n = 562), the mean age was 39.98 years old (standard deviation 10.21, range 20–71), 95% were Chinese and 63% had attained tertiary-level education or above (Table 1). At recruitment, 94% had been diagnosed with HIV for at least 1 year, 96% were on ART and 94% had achieved viral load suppression (<200 copies/mL).

The all-site M. genitalium prevalence in participants was 20% (95% CI = 17%–23%). Lower education attainment, symptom experience, history of STI diagnosis and concurrent N. gonorrhoeae positivity were significant factors of M. genitalium positivity in the multivariable logistic regression model, but sexual behavioural factors including SDU and chemsex engagement were only significant in bivariable logistic regression (Table S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).

Among 88 participants with M. genitalium-positive typeable samples, macrolide RAMs (23S rRNA) were detected in 70/86 (81%) participants, fluoroquinolone RAMs (parC genes) in 45/78 (58%) participants and fluoroquinolone RAMs (gyrA genes) in 4/81 (5%) participants, and dual macrolide and fluoroquinolone RAMs in 38/70 (54%) participants. M. genitalium RAMs were detected in 14% (77/562) of participants’ samples (Table S2). In comparison with those without M. genitalium infection or undetectable/undetermined RAMs, HIV diagnosis in 2015–19 (aOR = 5.06, 95% CI = 1.44–17.77), symptom experience in the past 1 month lasting for 15–90 days (reference group being no symptom presentation, aOR = 3.10, 95% CI = 1.28–7.51) and more anatomical sites testing N. gonorrhoeae positive (aOR = 2.27, 95% CI = 1.35–3.83) were significantly associated with the detection of M. genitalium RAMs in the multivariable logistic regression model. SDU in the past 6 months was also included in the final model, although the P value was 0.09 (aOR = 1.62, 95% CI = 0.92–2.84).

Distribution of M. genitalium infection and RAMs by anatomical sites

The highest proportion of M. genitalium positivity was detected in rectal swabs (14%, 95% CI = 11%–17%), followed by urine specimens (6%, 95% CI = 4%–8%), and the lowest in pharyngeal swabs (4%, 95% CI = 2%–5%) (Figure 2). Overall, urethral site-related symptoms were significantly associated with urethral M. genitalium RAMs (OR = 2.72, 95% CI = 1.13–6.56), but not for M. genitalium infection. Among the reported symptoms in the past 1 month, anal itch was the most common (15%) (Figure 3). Only a reported experience of urethral discharge was significantly associated with urethral M. genitalium infection (OR = 5.09, 95% CI = 1.57–16.48) and RAMs (OR = 7.24, 95% CI = 2.19–23.99).

Among 102 typeable M. genitalium samples, 88 (86%) tested positive for M. genitalium RAMs, including 78% (80/102) with macrolide RAMs, 49% (50/102) with fluoroquinolone RAMs and 41% (42/102) with both macrolide and fluoroquinolone RAMs. The distribution of RAMs varied by anatomical sites, with the highest proportion at the rectal site (10%), followed by the urethral site (5%) and the pharyngeal site (1%) in all samples, regardless of M. genitalium positivity rate. The difference was significant between sites [reference group being urethral site; pharyngeal aOR = 0.30 (95% CI = 0.12–0.71); rectal aOR = 2.24 (95% CI = 1.30–3.86)], after adjustment by factors of C. trachomatis infection, N. gonorrhoeae infection, reported symptoms in respective sites and history of SDU in the past 6 months.

Among M. genitalium-positive typeable samples, the highest proportion of mutations detected was at the urethral site, including 83% (n = 24) for 23S rRNA genes and 69% (n = 18) for parC genes (Table 2). The proportion of rectal and pharyngeal swabs, respectively, with detection of mutations was 79% (n = 50) and 75% (n = 6) in 23S rRNA genes, 47% (n = 28) and 57% (n = 4) in parC genes and 3% (n = 1) and 5% (n = 3) in gyrA genes.

Symptom experience lasting for 15–90 days was the only factor consistently associated with urethral M. genitalium infection and RAMs, and rectal M. genitalium infection and RAMs in bivariable logistic regression (Table 3). In the multivariable logistic regression model, STI diagnosis outside the HIV clinic (aOR = 2.29, 95% CI = 1.08–4.88) and history of SDU in the past 6 months (aOR = 2.70, 95% CI = 1.25–5.81) were associated with urethral M. genitalium infection. Coinfections of M. genitalium with C. trachomatis and/or N. gonorrhoeae

The all-site prevalence of C. trachomatis was 19% (95% CI = 16%–22%) and of N. gonorrhoeae was 10% (95% CI = 10%–16%) (Figure 2). The proportion of participants with monoinfection of M. genitalium, C. trachomatis or N. gonorrhoeae was 12%, 11% and 4%, respectively. Among participants with C. trachomatis and/or N. gonorrhoeae infection, 28% (40/144) tested positive for M. genitalium. However, M. genitalium positivity was significantly associated with N. gonorrhoeae positivity (OR = 3.29, 95% CI = 1.91–5.65), but not with C. trachomatis positivity.

The highest rate of positivity for both C. trachomatis (17%, 95% CI = 14%–20%) and N. gonorrhoeae (8%, 95% CI = 6%–11%) was at the rectal site, and the lowest at the urethral site. The all-site prevalence of at least two anatomic sites infections (any of C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae and M. genitalium infections) was 15%. C. trachomatis infection was not significantly associated with M. genitalium infection or M. genitalium RAMs at any anatomical sites. Zero N. gonorrhoeae/M. genitalium coinfections and zero C. trachomatis/M. genitalium coinfections were detected in urine specimens, and in pharyngeal swabs of participants reporting an oral ulcer (Tables 4 and 5). N. gonorrhoeae infection was significantly associated with M. genitalium infection and M. genitalium RAMs at pharyngeal and rectal sites, except an insignificant difference of RAM detection in rectal swabs of symptomatic participants.

M. genitalium management and TOC

More than 96% (107/111) of participants who tested positive for M. genitalium were prescribed with antimicrobials: azithromycin (n = 20); moxifloxacin (n = 35); and minocycline (n = 52) (Figure 4). A total of 87 samples were collected from 77 participants for TOC, including 7 participants who received 2 TOCs, and 1 participant who received 3 TOCs. Among 77 (69% of M. genitalium-positive) participants who had undergone at least one TOC, the cure rate at the first TOC in participants with moxifloxacin or minocycline was 100% for undetermined RAMs and above 75% for detected RAMs. The cure rate was only 50% in participants who received azithromycin and the isolates in their samples were not susceptible. The median time from treatment initiation to a negative TOC was 13 weeks (IQR = 11–24 weeks). Of note, among 16 participants with M. genitalium-positive TOC samples, 6 reported sexual activity between treatment initiation and TOC sample collection.

Discussion

Our study provides evidence in support of factors associated with M. genitalium RAMs in MSM with HIV, and demonstrated an STI coinfection pattern with analyses from a large sample size of above 500. With multi-anatomical site sampling, we estimated a high all-site prevalence in participants at 20% for M. genitalium infection, and 87% for M. genitalium RAMs in M. genitalium-infected participants with typeable samples. While the prevalence of urethral M. genitalium infection (6%) in this study was comparable with a previous local study (8%–10%),16,22 we observed a much higher prevalence of RAMs in this study (83% macrolide RAMs and 69% fluoroquinolone RAMs) than previous studies in Hong Kong (47% macrolide RAMs and 56% fluoroquinolone RAMs)16 and the USA (71% macrolide RAMs and 27% fluoroquinolone RAMs).14 Of note, clinical M. genitalium testing and a management service were not available in public service before this study.

C. trachomatis and M. genitalium infections are believed to have potential linkages because both infections are well recognized causes of NGU23 and share similar symptomology.7 Contrary to our expectation, N. gonorrhoeae instead of C. trachomatis infection was associated with M. genitalium infection at rectal and pharyngeal sites. This was different from a study in Taiwan, which presented a similar M. genitalium positivity rate by C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae infection status of participants.24 Our observation of zero coinfections of M. genitalium with C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae at the urethral site suggested that, while C. trachomatis and M. genitalium shared similar symptoms at the urethral site, they were unlikely to coinfect. A previous study in men presenting with urethritis gave a negative association of C. trachomatis and M. genitalium infection.25 Parallel C. trachomatis and M. genitalium testing for individuals presenting with NGU could be important to provide appropriate treatment, while a syndromic management approach may pose the risk of treatment failure with unsolved urethritis and AMR development with M. genitalium. While our study findings of a positive association of symptomology with M. genitalium RAMs at the urethral site were consistent with a previous study,23 we further highlighted prolonged symptom presentation of 15–60 days as a key predictor. The predictive power might, however, drop when the presenting symptom is too distant or too recent from the clinic visit.

The higher M. genitalium positivity at the rectal site than the urethral and pharyngeal sites supported the collection of rectal swabs for STI testing, as recommended in the Australian guideline.10 Our estimated high prevalence of rectal M. genitalium infection (14%) aligned with previous studies in Australia (13% in MSM and 21% in MSM with HIV),26,27 and a meta-analysis suggested higher rectal M. genitalium infections in symptomatic in comparison with asymptomatic individuals.28 In addition, the positivity of M. genitalium was high (17%–19%) in participants reporting symptoms such as anal pain and anal bleeding, and the association of M. genitalium infection and RAMs with N. gonorrhoeae infection was significant at the rectal site. If only urethral site-related symptoms are considered and only urine specimens are collected for M. genitalium testing and management, the rectal M. genitalium infection could be ignored. Incomplete treatment of M. genitalium infection while treating C. trachomatis and/or N. gonorrhoeae with shorter monotherapy could increase the risk of AMR development. There is the need to rethink the current formula of symptomatic M. genitalium testing, which excludes N. gonorrhoeae infection and rectal site testing in some guidelines. M. genitalium testing might be considered in the case of persistent symptom presentation at the rectal site following C. trachomatis and/or N. gonorrhoeae treatment. Our findings also suggested that, without M. genitalium testing, macrolides should not be used for treating chlamydia and/or gonorrhoea to avoid incidental and insufficient treatment for M. genitalium, and the potential risk of RAM development.

In our study, participants diagnosed with HIV in the years 2015–19 were more likely to test positive for M. genitalium RAMs. In Hong Kong, the number of newly diagnosed HIV infections in MSM peaked between 2015 and 2017 (annual number of diagnoses was 449–470) and fell afterwards.29 MSM diagnosed with HIV in these peak years might be in clusters of sex networking. A previous HIV molecular epidemiological study in Hong Kong has highlighted the key position of MSM with SDU within the sexual networks.30 In our study, recent SDU played a role in the final model for predicting M. genitalium RAMs, despite its statistical insignificance. This might be related to the strong association between SDU and STIs,31 which enhanced the treatment rate of C. trachomatis and/or N. gonorrhoeae in the community, leading to a suboptimal M. genitalium treatment effect.

To enhance the cure rate, a resistance-guided sequential treatment approach, referring to macrolide resistance testing in M. genitalium-positive samples to guide the selection of macrolides or fluoroquinolones for treatment after 1 week of doxycycline,32 has been adopted in UK, European and Australian guidelines. We basically follow that approach, except for the prescription of all regimens in one clinic visit after resistance testing. However, it is challenging to strictly follow due to a suboptimal typing success rate,5,33 resulting in high testing cost and pressure on achieving the targeted turn-around time. Nonetheless, M. genitalium infections with undetermined RAMs achieved a 100% cure rate if moxifloxacin or minocycline were prescribed.

There are a few limitations to our study. First, the same as for other studies, the detection of RAMs did not necessarily imply the presence of clinically resistant M. genitalium, especially for fluoroquinolones. Second, only one HIV specialist clinic was set as the recruitment site. However, the clinic is a major HIV specialist clinic in Hong Kong, serving 57% of in-care people living with HIV locally. Third, healthcare workers might have selectively approached participants at higher risk of STI for inclusion in the study. Despite potential selection bias, the estimated prevalence of M. genitalium infection at the urethral site was comparable with that in previous studies. Fourth, our recommended time from treatment initiation to TOC was 8–10 weeks, which was extended from international guidelines of at least 5 weeks. In addition, not all participants turned up for TOC, nor followed the scheduled TOC date. Instead, some participants synchronized the TOC sampling date with their HIV clinic visit, usually in the interval of 4–6 months. The estimation of cure rate based on the interval between baseline and TOC sampling date should be interpreted with caution. In the study, while the median time to negative TOC was 13 weeks, the cure rate was restricted to that achieved at 8 weeks, the recommended TOC interval from treatment initiation. Treatment failure or reinfection could not be confirmed for participants reporting sexual activity between treatment initiation and TOC sampling.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the prevalence of M. genitalium infection was comparable with other studies, but the prevalence of M. genitalium RAMs was exceptionally high, despite the lack of M. genitalium testing and treatment in the public service. Our results support further expansion of the current symptomatic M. genitalium testing recommendation to rectal site- and urethral site-related symptoms instead of just NGU. Macrolides for treating chlamydia and/or gonorrhoea should not be included to avoid incidental and insufficient M. genitalium treatment, which may promote development of RAMs.
