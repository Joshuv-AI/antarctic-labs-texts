Assessment of cadmium and lead as biomarkers of prostate cancer severity
Authors: Michael W. C. Wiles, Ruth-Ann Ngwenyama, Stacy Crane, Li Liu, Soumen Bera, Weiwei Ma, Alan Diamond, John D. Brockman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examines the relationship between cadmium, lead exposure and prostate cancer by quantifying elemental concentrations in prostate biopsy tissue. Tissue samples were obtained from a total of 142 patients (64.8% black versus 35.2% white, mean age of 60.9 years, and mean BMI of 29.4) undergoing prostate biopsies. Only 11 (7.7%) of these patients had Gleason score of less than or equal to 6. Majority (59.2%) of them had a score of 3 + 4, 4 + 3 (19%), or higher (14.1%). Associations between tissue cadmium and age, race, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels, and Gleason Score were evaluated. The median cadmium concentration (0.41 µg/g) was within previously reported ranges. No significant correlation was observed between cadmium and PSA or Gleason score (p > 0.05). Cadmium levels were significantly associated with age and smoking history and inversely correlated with BMI (p < 0.05). The median lead concentration of 0.03 µg/g was lower than literature values but showed a race-specific association with Gleason score (p < 0.05). The results of this study indicate that cadmium accumulation in prostate cancer reflects exposure history but does not track prostate cancer aggressiveness in this cohort. Lead was not associated with prostate cancer aggressiveness, however a race-modified association between lead and Gleason score merits further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47131-3.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most commonly diagnosed cancer and the third leading cause of cancer death among men in the United States. In 2025, an estimated 313,780 new cases of prostate cancer are expected, with approximately 35,770 deaths1. Environmental factors, including toxic metals like cadmium (Cd) and lead (Pb), have been implicated in prostate cancer etiology2. Cadmium and lead are non-essential elements that are widely distributed in the environment and known to accumulate in the human body3. Smokers exhibit 4–6 times higher blood cadmium levels than non-smokers4–6 Cadmium exposure occurs through smoking, diet, and industry source, and once absorbed it can persist for decades7,8. Lead exposure remains prevalent via legacy sources such as lead-based paint, soil, and plumbing9. Both metals are established toxicants and carcinogens that induce oxidative cellular stress, DNA damage, and endocrine disruption4,10–12.

Epidemiological evidence for cadmium’s role in prostate cancer has been mixed. Several large prospective cohort studies report no significant association between estimated dietary cadmium intake and prostate cancer incidence. For instance, no overall link was found in cohorts from Denmark and Japan and only a modest increase in risk of localized disease was observed in a Swedish cohort5,13,14. Case-control studies using biomarker measurements of cadmium have yielded similarly inconsistent results15. Studies using urinary or tissue cadmium reported higher concentrations among prostate cancer cases16–18. Early occupational studies raised the possibility of elevated prostate cancer mortality in exposed workers, but subsequent research did not consistently support the finding19–21. Three meta-analysis studies concluded that cadmium exposure is not significantly associated with overall prostate cancer risk22–24. However, a recent meta-analysis by Firmani et al. suggested that higher cadmium exposure might be specifically linked to more aggressive forms of prostate cancer25. This discrepancy in the literature underscores the need to clarify cadmium’s impact on prostate cancer occurrence and tumor aggressiveness.

Exposure to lead has also been studied as a risk factor for prostate cancer. A recent Korean population study found that men with higher blood lead levels had greater odds of prostate cancer compared with lower levels26. Occupational exposure studies generally show inconclusive or null associates between lead and prostate cancer27. Elevated lead levels in prostate cancer patients have also been reported, further supporting its role as a potential environmental carcinogen28,29.

A key limitation of prior studies on cadmium and lead in prostate cancer is the reliance on indirect exposure metrics, such as diet questionnaires, blood levels, and urine levels. Measurement of cadmium and lead in prostate biopsy tissue analysis offers a novel approach for assessing metal exposure in the target organ, and potentially providing a direct indicator of cadmium and lead accumulation relates to prostate pathology. Only few studies to date have measured cadmium or lead directly in human prostate tissue18,30. Using tissue samples can avoid some limitations of dietary or blood-based assessments, for example recall bias or transient exposure, and may better reflect long-term metal sequestration in the prostate. Notably, a study by Zaichick et al., reported higher cadmium levels in benign hyperplasia than in malignant prostate tissue18, highlighting the complex role of cadmium. This result suggests that tissue-based measurements could reveal patterns not evident from indirect measures of exposure.

This study explores the relationship between cadmium and lead in prostate biopsy tissue and prostate cancer severity. Cadmium and lead levels were directly measured in prostate biopsy tissue and the association with PSA level, Gleason scores, age, BMI and smoking status were examined. Prostate tumors are routinely graded using the Gleason scoring system, which assesses tumor aggressiveness. The Gleason score is determined by the sum of the two most prevalent histopathological patterns in the tumor. Scores range from 6 for indolent tumors, 7 for intermediate risk, and 8–10 for high-risk disease31. This grading system informs patient management decisions; however, it is not always predictive of tumor aggressiveness32. Additional biomarkers are being considered for grading aggressiveness of prostate cancer33,34.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and patient data

We analyzed 142 prostate tissue samples obtained from patients undergoing prostate biopsies for prostate tumors collected by transrectal puncture biopsy of the indurated site in the prostate. Prostate biopsy samples were collected between 2016 and 2020 at the University of Illinois at Chicago clinical pathology service. Patient demographics included race, age, BMI, PSA levels, Gleason score, and smoking status. Detailed protocols have been published previously35. A pathologist determined that the average cellularity, a measure of the percentage of cancer cells in each core, was 73%. For each patient, clinical data were collected, including age, self-reported race, BMI and smoking status (current, former, or non-smoker). All samples were handled and de-identified in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. The University of Illinois Chicago Office for the Protection of Research Subjects confirmed that this work falls outside the definition of human subjects research and is therefore exempt from ethical review and informed consent requirements.

Elemental quantification by ICP-MS

Elemental concentrations in the prostate tissue were measured using a Perkin Elmer NexIon 300X inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Tissue samples were lyophilized and digested in 100 µL of concentrated trace metal grade nitric acid (Fisher Scientific, Hampton, NH, USA). Digestion was performed in 15 mL metal-free polypropylene tubes on a heating block at 90 °C for 2 h. Following digestion, samples were cooled and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 1 min. An additional 80 µL of 30% Fluka H2O2 (Honeywell, Charlotte, NC) was added to each tube to oxidize residual organic matter, followed by a second heating step at 90 °C for 1 h. The samples were collected and centrifuged a second time. The digests were diluted with 2 mL of ultrapure water and the internal standard rhodium.

For instrument calibration, a five-point external calibration curve was prepared from cadmium standard solutions (Inorganic Ventures, Christiansburg, VA, USA). Single element standards of tin and molybdenum were prepared to correct for direct interference from 114Sn and 95Mo 16O on 114Cd and 111Cd, respectively. Quality control materials included analysis of certified reference materials NIST SRM 1577 Bovine Liver and Seronormtm Whole Blood Level 2.

Statistical analysis

The association between natural log transformed lead and cadmium levels with continuous variables (age, log-PSA) were evaluated using Pearson Correlation coefficients. The Spearman rank correlation test was used for ordinal data (BMI category, Gleason Score). T-tests and one-way Anova F tests were used for categorical comparisons with Tukey’s HSD for post hoc contrasts. All statistical tests were 2-sided, controlling for Type I error probability of 0.05.

Results

Tissue samples were obtained from a total of 142 patients (64.8% black versus 35.2% white) undergoing prostate biopsies. The mean age of the patients were 60.9 years with 55.6% at least 60 years old. About 76% of the patients were either overweight or obese. About 1/3 of the study sample were in each of the smoking status categories (never, former, and current). Only 11 (7.7%) of these patients had Gleason score of less than or equal to 6. Majority (59.2%) of them had a score of 3 + 4, 4 + 3 (19%), or higher (14.1%).

Cadmium and lead concentration in prostate biopsy tissue

The ICP-MS method was validated by measuring cadmium and lead in NIST SRM 1577 Bovine Liver and Seronorm Whole Blood Level 2, the results are reported in Supplemental Data Table 1. The distribution of cadmium and lead in prostate biopsy tissue is reported in Table 1, along references ranges in excised prostatectomy tissue reported by Zaichick et al.18. For bovine liver, intra-assay CVs ranged from 2.8 to 12.1% for Cd and 3.6–16.9% for Pb across five analytical batches. For whole blood, intra-assay CVs ranged from 3.9 to 24.6% for Cd and 3.4–13.6% for Pb, with higher variability observed for Cd at low concentrations near the method detection limit. Inter-assay precision was assessed using batch-averaged concentrations obtained from five independent analytical runs. The inter-assay coefficient of variation was 7.6% for Cd and 6.8% for Pb in bovine liver, and 5.7% for Cd and 5.1% for Pb in whole blood, demonstrating good reproducibility across analytical sessions.

Cadmium and lead association with clinical variables

The associations between cadmium and clinical variables are reported in Table 3. A significant positive correlation was observed between cadmium levels and age, indicating higher cadmium accumulation with increasing age. In contrast, no significant correlation was found with PSA levels or BMI as a continuous variable. Among ordinal variables, cadmium was inversely correlated with BMI category, with lower cadmium levels observed in individuals classified as overweight or obese. Cadmium showed no significant association with Gleason score.

Group comparisons revealed that patients ≥ 60 years of age had significantly higher cadmium levels compared to those < 60, Supplemental Data Fig. 1. Smoking status was also significantly associated with cadmium levels. Current smokers had the highest mean cadmium concentration, followed by former smokers and non-smokers, supporting tobacco smoke as a major source of cadmium exposure, Supplemental Data Fig. 2. Pairwise BMI categories also showed differences, with underweight and normal-weight individuals exhibiting higher cadmium levels than those overweight or obese, Supplemental Data Fig. 3. Cadmium showed no association with PSA levels or Gleason score. Multivariable analysis failed to demonstrate associations between cadmium and prostate cancer severity.

Discussion

This study is among the few to assess cadmium and lead exposure directly in prostate biopsy tissue rather than via diet, blood, or urine. The cadmium levels measured in in prostatic biopsy tissue in this study were in line with levels reported in a Russian18,30 and Turkish cohort36 and lower than values from a U.S. cohort37. The lead levels were an order of magnitude lower than the studies from Russia, Turkey36, and the USA37. Our findings are consistent with the tissue-based analysis by Neslund-Dudas et al., who reported that cadmium levels in prostate tumor tissue did not significantly differ by recurrence status among smokers37.

Our null findings are within the spectrum of existing epidemiologic evidence regarding cadmium and lead exposure in prostate cancer. Meta-analysis and systematic reviews have generally reported little or no increased risk of prostate cancer with higher cadmium exposure22–24. However, a recent meta-analysis by Firmani et al. reported a non-significant effect size between PCa and Cd exposure of 1.11 (95% CI 0.85–1.45). The study highlighted that higher Cd exposure was significantly associated with more aggressive histopathological types of PCa, with an OR of 1.50 (95% CI 1.08–2.07). Our findings also showed no trend of higher tumor grade or PSA in men with elevated prostate cadmium. For lead, the epidemiologic evidence is similarly mixed, but largely non-supportive of a strong effect on prostate cancer26–29. Several factors may explain the lack of association in our study. First, the timing and nature of exposure measurement could play a role18,38 Cadmium and lead are known to build up in kidney, bone and other tissues over decades, so a single blood draw may not correlate well with the cumulative dose to the prostate7,8. A recent review hypothesizes that bloodstream levels of cadmium increase the risk of PCa rather than urinary levels25. It is possible that individuals with elevated systemic cadmium or lead sometime in the past accumulated metal in the prostate, but by the time of cancer diagnosis the tissue concentration no longer tracks variations in tumor behavior34. Conversely, loss of zinc update and incidental cadmium uptake has been observed in poorly differentiated prostate cells39,40. This is not supported by the present study, which did not observe an association between cadmium or lead and tumor progression.

Second, heavy metals may influence the onset of prostate carcinogenesis more than the subsequent grade of established tumors13. If cadmium or lead contributes to initiation or early transformation, their role might not be detectable when comparing moderate vs. high-grade cancers. Many epidemiologic studies and animal models have focused on incidence rather than aggressiveness, and the results have been inconsistent41. It may be that once a tumor is established, its grade is governed more by other molecular factors than by additional metal burden. We also consider the possibility of exposure misclassification. Although tissue assays are specific, they sample only a portion of the prostate. In this study, a pathologist determined that the average cellularity of cancer cells in each core was 73%. Heterogeneity in metal concentration across the gland could lead to nondifferential misclassification.

Finally, we note the preliminary finding of a potential race-specific interaction for lead. In stratified analysis, we observed an indication that the lead-Gleason relationship differed by race. Because our study was not powered for this subgroup analysis, we interpret this result with caution. It could suggest biological or exposure differences. Socioeconomic and environmental factors can lead to higher exposure in some communities, and genetic or lifestyle factors could modify metal toxicity42,43. Prior work has suggested possible gene-environment interactions in African American populations, although those studies address cancer risk rather than severity44. We therefore present our race-related lead interaction only as preliminary, and underscores the need for larger, diverse cohorts to investigate heavy metal effects in different population groups.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. Biopsy heterogeneity, as noted, may mask tumor-specific cadmium or lead levels. The cohort size, while larger than prior trace element studies, still limits subgroups analysis, particularly by race. Environmental and dietary exposure data were not collected, restricting the ability to connect external exposures with tissue concentration. Finally, the cross-sectional and observation design precludes causal interference.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that cadmium concentrations in prostate tissue reflect cumulative exposure, particularly from age and smoking, but do not correlate with markers of prostate cancer severity such as PSA and Gleason grade. Lead concentrations were low overall, but notably a race-dependent interaction emerged. Higher lead levels were associated with lower Gleason scores among White patients, but not Black patients. These findings suggest that while prostate biopsy cadmium levels may serve as a long-term exposure biomarker, neither cadmium or lead appear to be independent predictors of prostate cancer progression or aggressiveness.
