Association between readmission and mortality in patients with ischemic stroke: evidence from national health insurance service database of South Korea
Authors: Yu Seong Hwang, Heui Sug Jo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association between hospital readmission after ischemic stroke (IS) and long-term mortality remains insufficiently understood. This study examined the relationship between 90-day readmission and all-cause mortality over a 3-year follow-up period among patients experiencing their first IS.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used claims data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) of South Korea. Patients discharged after hospitalization for IS between 2019 and 2020 were followed for 90 days to ascertain readmission status. Three readmission models were constructed based on clinical breadth: Model 1 (IS recurrence), Model 2 (IS and related diseases), and Model 3 (all-cause readmission). Propensity score–matched cohorts were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methods and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models to assess associations with subsequent 3-year mortality.ResultsAmong 22,548 patients included in the analysis, 83.3% (18,790) survived and 16.7% (3,758) died during the 3-year follow-up period. Kaplan–Meier analyses demonstrated significantly lower survival probabilities among patients readmitted within 90 days compared with those not readmitted across all three models (log-rank p < 0.05). In multivariable analyses, 90-day readmission was consistently associated with higher 3-year mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] range: 1.15–1.38). Higher mortality risk was observed among male patients (aHR ≈ 1.7), adults aged ≥ 80 years (aHR ≈ 5.3), and underweight individuals with body mass index < 18.5 kg/m² (aHR ≈ 2.0). In contrast, engagement in light physical activity and moderate-to-vigorous exercise was associated with lower mortality risk (aHR ≈ 0.74–0.78).ConclusionHospital readmission within 90 days after ischemic stroke was consistently associated with increased long-term mortality. In this observational study, early readmission may serve as a marker of clinical vulnerability, and the findings support the potential value of structured, stroke-specific transitional care models aimed at reducing avoidable readmissions. Implementing tailored transitional care interventions at discharge may help identify high-risk patients early and support continuity of care, thereby potentially mitigating adverse long-term outcomes after ischemic stroke.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14716-7.

Introduction

​Ischemic stroke (IS) is an acute medical condition that requires ongoing post-discharge management. Although mortality from ischemic stroke has declined, its incidence has continued to increase, resulting in a growing population of stroke survivors who require long-term care. Consequently, the global burden of ischemic stroke remains substantial and continues to rise despite improvements in survival [1].

A similar pattern has been observed in South Korea. The age-standardized mortality rate from cerebrovascular diseases declined markedly from 88.3 per 100,000 population in 2002 to 21.3 per 100,000 in 2022 [2]. In contrast, the incidence of stroke increased by 9.5% between 2011 and 2021 [3]. This rising incidence has placed a significant strain on healthcare systems, with stroke-related medical expenditures increasing by 29% between 2018 and 2022 [4].

The global burden of ischemic stroke is further amplified by the high risk of hospital readmission. A recent systematic review of 21 studies reported that 90-day readmission rates for patients with IS ranged from 8.6% to 31.5% worldwide (mean 19.2%, SD 6.4%) [5], which is comparable to the 90-day readmission rate of 17.2% reported in Korea [6].

This elevated risk of readmission is largely attributable to stroke recurrence [7], particularly during the early post-stroke period [8]. In addition, ischemic stroke is frequently accompanied by a wide range of complications. Beyond physical impairments such as hemiparesis, sensory deficits, and dysphagia [8, 9], patients may experience cognitive impairment [10] and depression [11]. Post-stroke complications also include neurological conditions such as cerebral edema [12] and hemorrhagic transformation [13], as well as cardiovascular [14] and respiratory complications [15].

When stroke-related sequelae and complications occur, hospital readmission is often unavoidable. Nevertheless, increasing attention has been directed toward reducing potentially avoidable readmissions through policy-driven initiatives such as the U.S. Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program (HRRP), implemented by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services [16]. These initiatives emphasize early risk identification, comprehensive discharge planning, and strengthened communication and care coordination beginning at the initial admission, with active engagement of patients and caregivers. These efforts are supported by evidence suggesting that prolonged or repeated hospitalization may itself contribute to additional health complications.

The average length of hospital stay for patients with cerebral infarction is approximately 19 days per hospitalization episode, including both index admissions and readmissions [17]. During hospitalization, reduced physical activity frequently leads to muscle loss, and nearly 70% of stroke inpatients experience difficulties with walking and mobility after discharge [18]. Declines in physical function substantially increase the risk of falls and fractures [19]. Furthermore, disturbances in sleep, mobility, nutrition, and mood at admission have been associated with a higher likelihood of subsequent readmission or emergency department visits compared with patients without these disturbances [20].

Although numerous studies have identified factors associated with post-stroke readmission and related complications [21, 22], long-term evidence regarding the impact of readmission on patient outcomes—particularly its association with mortality—remains limited. In particular, the direct relationship between early readmission after ischemic stroke and long-term mortality has not been fully established.

Therefore, using the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) claims database, this study aimed to examine the association between 90-day hospital readmission and 3-year all-cause mortality among patients with ischemic stroke.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study using claims data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) of South Korea. The database provided longitudinal healthcare utilization records from January 1, 2009, to December 31, 2023, which were used to ascertain prior stroke history and follow patients over time. The database comprised comprehensive records of healthcare utilization for the South Korean population.

Because South Korea operates a universal healthcare system, the NHIS database is nationally representative and captures all reimbursed medical visits, diagnoses, treatments, and prescriptions. As a result, it provides a complete and longitudinal record of patients’ healthcare utilization, thereby reducing the likelihood of data loss and selection bias. This feature allowed for robust analyses of hospital readmission and mortality patterns following ischemic stroke.

Participants

Patients were eligible if they were hospitalized for the first time with a primary diagnosis of ischemic stroke (IS) in 2019 or 2020. To ensure inclusion of incident cases, we first excluded individuals with any prior hospitalization or outpatient visit for IS between 2009 and 2018.

Because the exposure of interest was hospital readmission within 90 days after discharge and the outcome was 3-year mortality assessed after the 90-day exposure window, we excluded patients who died within 90 days of discharge.

Next, to ensure the availability of baseline covariates, we restricted the analytic sample to individuals who had completed at least one national health examination during 2019–2020. Patients without health examination records in this period were excluded. After applying these inclusion and exclusion criteria, 63,109 individuals remained in the pre-matching analytic cohort.

This study was conducted in accordance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kangwon National University Hospital (KNUH-2024-02-006, approved on February 14, 2024). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of Kangwon National University Hospital due to the use of anonymized secondary data.

Clinical follow-up

Patients hospitalized with a primary diagnosis of ischemic stroke between 2019 and 2020 were followed for 90 days after discharge to determine their readmission status. The 90-day readmission window was selected to capture the early subacute (7 days to 3 months) and late subacute (3 to 6 months) phases of stroke recovery, during which clinical instability and complications are relatively common [23].

Model generation

Readmissions occurring within 90 days after discharge were categorized into three models of increasing clinical breadth based on the primary diagnosis at readmission. This tiered framework was designed to distinguish narrowly defined recurrent ischemic stroke events (Model 1), clinically related post-stroke or vascular readmissions beyond recurrent stroke alone (Model 2), and overall post-discharge clinical instability regardless of diagnosis (Model 3).

Model 1 (ischemic stroke recurrence) included readmissions with a primary diagnosis of ischemic stroke (ICD-10: I63), representing recurrent ischemic stroke events.

Model 2 (stroke-related readmission) expanded Model 1 to include readmissions for conditions clinically recognized as post-stroke complications or vascular comorbidities closely linked to ischemic stroke pathophysiology. This model was intended to capture a broader but still clinically grounded spectrum of early post-stroke readmissions that may reflect recurrent vascular events, post-stroke complications, or challenges in secondary prevention. Candidate diagnoses were identified through a two-step process: (1) identification of diagnoses frequently associated with 90-day readmission among patients with ischemic stroke, and (2) independent clinical adjudication by a neurologist and a family medicine specialist to determine relevance to ischemic stroke mechanisms, post-stroke complications, or secondary prevention. Detailed diagnosis-level rationale and supporting literature are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Model 3 (all-cause readmission) included any hospital readmission within 90 days, regardless of diagnosis, to capture overall clinical instability during the post-discharge period. Readmissions solely for rehabilitation (ICD-10: Z50) were excluded in accordance with national clinical guidance [23].

Study variables and outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality during the 3-year follow-up period. Follow-up (time zero) was defined as the day after the 90-day post-discharge window to ensure that readmission status during the exposure window was fully ascertained. Participants were followed until death or the end of 3 years of follow-up, whichever occurred first.

To examine the association between 90-day readmission and subsequent mortality, we fitted Cox proportional hazards models adjusting for prespecified covariates (Fig. 1).

Sociodemographic variables included sex (female or male), age group (under 60, 60–69, 70–79, or 80 years and older), insurance type (community-based insurance, employee-based insurance, or Medical Aid beneficiary), income level (quartiles defined as 0–1st ventile, 2nd–10th ventile, 11th–16th ventile, and 17th–20th ventile), and residential area (capital area [Seoul and Gyeonggi Province], metropolitan cities, or provinces).

Health-related variables included body mass index (BMI; <18.5, 18.5–25, 25–30, or ≥ 30 kg/m²) and a history of heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, tuberculosis, and other diseases, each categorized as absent or present. History of other diseases was defined as previously diagnosed medical conditions requiring ongoing pharmacological treatment or outpatient follow-up at baseline and included all types of malignancies. Surgical intervention during the index hospitalization was defined as the presence of a documented procedural claim (e.g., thrombectomy or thrombolysis).

Self-management variables included smoking status (never smoker, former smoker, or current smoker), frequency of alcohol consumption (weekly, monthly, yearly, or none), engagement in light physical activity, and engagement in moderate-to-vigorous exercise. Engagement in light physical activity was defined as participation in activities causing slight shortness of breath on at least two days per week, based on responses to the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) health examination questionnaire. Engagement in moderate-to-vigorous exercise was defined as participation in vigorous activities causing marked shortness of breath on at least two days per week, according to NHIS questionnaire responses.

Covariate selection was guided by established stroke management guidelines and prior literature, incorporating non-modifiable risk factors (e.g., age and sex) and well-documented modifiable risk factors, including hypertension, smoking, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, other cardiovascular diseases, dyslipidemia, diet and nutrition, physical activity, and obesity [23].

Statistical analysis

Propensity score matching (PSM) ratios were selected to balance covariate comparability and sample retention across models. For Models 1 and 2, a 1:5 matching ratio was applied to maximize statistical power while maintaining adequate covariate balance, given the relatively smaller number of patients readmitted under stroke-specific definitions.

In contrast, Model 3 included a substantially larger number of readmitted patients due to the all-cause readmission definition. When higher matching ratios (e.g., ≥ 1:3) were applied, a considerable proportion of readmitted patients could not be matched because of limited overlap in propensity score distributions, resulting in excessive sample loss. Therefore, a 1:2 matching ratio was selected as a pragmatic compromise to reduce matching failure while preserving covariate balance.

Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model including sex, age group, income level, residential area, and surgical intervention during the index hospitalization. Matching was then performed within each model using the prespecified ratios (Models 1–2: 1:5; Model 3: 1:2).

Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute SMD < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. After matching, all covariates met this criterion across the three models. The resulting matched cohorts were used for subsequent survival analyses.

Baseline characteristics were summarized according to mortality status. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test and are presented as frequencies and percentages. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value < 0.05.

The proportional hazards assumption for Cox regression models was primarily evaluated for the main exposure variables (i.e., the three types of 90-day readmission) using Schoenfeld residuals with 1,000 resamples. Testing all covariates simultaneously resulted in unstable or non-convergent estimates; therefore, assessment focused on the primary exposure variables. The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for Models 1 and 2 (supremum test Pr > MaxAbsVal: Model 1 = 0.413; Model 2 = 0.989). In contrast, Model 3 did not meet the proportional hazards assumption (Pr > MaxAbsVal = 0.001), indicating that the association between all-cause readmission and mortality varied over the follow-up period.

Survival probabilities according to 90-day readmission status were estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves. Differences in survival between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test, Wilcoxon test, and − 2 log likelihood ratio test.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were fitted to examine the association between 90-day readmission and subsequent mortality while adjusting for covariates. Model fit was assessed using the − 2 log likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Likelihood ratio, score, and Wald tests were used to evaluate overall covariate contributions (all p < 0.0001). Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s concordance statistic, which demonstrated good discriminatory ability across models (Model 1: 0.768; Model 2: 0.769; Model 3: 0.765).

All analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA) on a Windows platform.

A total of 22,548 patients were included in the Model 1 matched cohort, of whom 18,790 (83.33%) survived and 3,758 (16.67%) died during the 3-year follow-up period. After propensity score matching, no statistically significant differences were observed between survivors and non-survivors in key sociodemographic characteristics, including sex, age group, insurance type, income level, and residential area (all p > 0.05), indicating adequate covariate balance between groups.

Similarly, health-related characteristics such as body mass index (BMI), surgical intervention during the index admission, and histories of heart disease, hypertension, dyslipidemia, tuberculosis, and other diseases did not differ significantly between survivors and non-survivors after matching (all p > 0.05). With respect to health-related behaviors, smoking status, drinking frequency, light physical activity, and moderate-to-vigorous exercise were also well balanced between mortality groups in the matched cohort (all p > 0.05).

Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were conducted to provide an initial, unadjusted comparison of survival according to 90-day hospital readmission status (Fig. 2). Crude Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated consistently lower survival probabilities among patients who experienced readmission within 90 days compared with those who did not. Survival differences between the two groups were statistically significant across all readmission definitions, as assessed by log-rank testing (Model 1: χ² = 9.3, p < 0.01; Model 2: χ² = 5.7, p < 0.05; Model 3: χ² = 66.4, p < 0.001).

Several covariates were also associated with mortality across models. Male sex and older age were consistently associated with higher mortality risk, with the strongest associations observed in patients aged 80 years and older. Underweight BMI (< 18.5 kg/m²), a history of diabetes, heart disease, and other chronic diseases were associated with increased mortality, whereas overweight and obese BMI categories were associated with lower mortality risk compared with normal BMI. Engagement in light physical activity and moderate-to-vigorous exercise was consistently associated with lower mortality risk across all models. Current smoking status was associated with higher mortality, while more frequent alcohol consumption (weekly or monthly) showed inverse associations with mortality compared with non-drinkers.

Sensitivity analyses using alternative propensity score matching ratios for Model 3 yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of the main findings (Supplementary Table S5). Model calibration was evaluated by comparing observed mortality across quartiles of the linear predictor derived from each Cox model. In all three models, observed 3-year mortality increased monotonically across higher-risk quartiles, indicating good agreement between model-based risk stratification and observed outcomes (Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This study examined the association between hospital readmission within 90 days after discharge and 3-year all-cause mortality among patients experiencing their first ischemic stroke (IS), using a large-scale, nationally representative claims database from the NHIS of South Korea. We found that 90-day readmission was consistently associated with higher long-term mortality across three analytic models with increasing clinical breadth, including ischemic stroke recurrence, stroke-related readmissions, and all-cause readmissions. These associations persisted after adjustment for sociodemographic characteristics, comorbidities, and health-related behaviors. In addition, calibration analyses demonstrated good agreement between model-derived risk stratification and observed mortality, supporting the robustness of the estimated associations.

Interpretation and clinical mechanisms

The elevated mortality risk associated with readmission may reflect underlying clinical vulnerability, including suboptimal disease control, rapid health deterioration, and the cumulative burden of post-stroke complications. In this context, strategies aimed at reducing early readmission after ischemic stroke may be particularly important for identifying and supporting high-risk patients during the post-discharge period.

However, the proportional hazards assumption was violated in Model 3, likely reflecting the heterogeneous nature of all-cause readmission. Unlike stroke-specific readmissions, all-cause readmissions encompass a wide range of medical conditions with distinct temporal risk profiles. Consequently, the association between all-cause readmission and mortality may vary over the follow-up period rather than remaining constant, suggesting that all-cause readmission alone may have limited explanatory value in characterizing the temporal relationship between readmission and long-term mortality. Therefore, the hazard ratio estimated for Model 3 should be interpreted as an average association over time rather than as evidence of a constant effect throughout follow-up.

Surgical intervention during the index admission, such as thrombectomy or thrombolysis, demonstrated differing prognostic relevance across models. These procedures may serve as proxies for acute stroke severity and the need for intensive neurovascular management, which are more directly linked to stroke-specific and stroke-related readmissions than to heterogeneous all-cause readmissions. In Model 3, the broader and more heterogeneous composition of all-cause readmission may dilute the prognostic signal of stroke-specific severity markers, which may partly explain why surgical intervention was not statistically significant in that model.

Comparison with previous literature

Our findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that recurrent stroke is strongly associated with mortality and functional decline [24–27]. Patients experiencing recurrent stroke often exhibit greater functional impairment, delayed recovery, and reduced independence, all of which contribute to poorer long-term outcomes [28]. Prolonged hospitalization itself may exacerbate physical and psychological complications, including sarcopenia, anxiety, and depression, further increasing vulnerability after discharge [19, 29].

Beyond readmission, several baseline characteristics were associated with mortality. Advancing age remained one of the strongest predictors of mortality, consistent with prior research [30]. Although crude stroke mortality rates are often higher in women due to older age at onset, our adjusted analyses showed higher mortality among men, aligning with previous systematic reviews [31]. This finding highlights the importance of comprehensive management of underlying health conditions in male stroke survivors.

Metabolic factors also played an important role. Histories of hypertension and diabetes were associated with higher mortality, reinforcing their significance in post-stroke prognosis. Diabetes, in particular, has been linked to increased stroke recurrence, disability, and mortality through mechanisms such as inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and arterial stiffness [32]. In contrast, a history of dyslipidemia was associated with lower mortality, possibly reflecting the widespread use of lipid-lowering therapies, which contribute to both primary and secondary prevention of cerebrovascular disease [33].

Regarding body mass index (BMI), our findings are consistent with previously reported patterns often described as the “obesity paradox” in stroke outcomes. Although obesity is a well-established risk factor for the incidence of ischemic stroke [34–35], we observed that underweight patients exhibited the highest mortality risk, whereas overweight patients had lower mortality compared with those of normal weight. Similar observations have been reported in prior studies and have been attributed to factors such as metabolic reserve, sarcopenia, and inflammatory responses during acute illness [36–41].

Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as indicating a protective effect of obesity against stroke occurrence or adverse outcomes. BMI is a crude measure that does not adequately capture body composition, fat distribution, or nutritional status. Excess abdominal adiposity and metabolic abnormalities, which are closely linked to obesity and metabolic syndrome, remain critical determinants of stroke risk and poor prognosis [42, 43].

Behavioral factors were also relevant. Engagement in light physical activity was independently associated with lower mortality, consistent with evidence that even modest levels of activity can improve blood pressure control and functional recovery after stroke [44, 45]. Similarly, engagement in moderate-to-vigorous exercise was also associated with reduced mortality, reinforcing the potential importance of physical activity across a range of intensities in post-stroke care.

Current smoking was consistently associated with increased mortality, underscoring the importance of smoking cessation in secondary prevention.

In contrast to some previous studies reporting socioeconomic disparities in stroke outcomes [46–48], we did not observe significant associations between insurance type or income level and mortality in the matched analyses. This finding may partly reflect the use of PSM, which balanced key sociodemographic characteristics between readmitted and non-readmitted patients, thereby attenuating observable differences related to socioeconomic status. In addition, South Korea’s universal health coverage system may further mitigate disparities in access to acute and post-acute stroke care.

However, patients residing in provincial areas exhibited higher mortality than those in metropolitan regions in model 2, suggesting that geographic disparities in access to specialized post-stroke care or rehabilitation services may persist despite overall coverage.

Implications for transitional care

Given that early hospital readmission after ischemic stroke was consistently associated with increased long-term mortality, transitional care strategies aimed at preventing avoidable readmissions warrant particular consideration. Transitional Care Models (TCMs), especially nurse-led interventions, have been shown to improve outcomes among older adults during care transitions and may be well suited for post-stroke populations [49–51].

Several practical components could be incorporated into stroke-specific TCMs. Early mobilization should be systematically linked with post-discharge rehabilitation pathways to support functional recovery. Structured discharge planning informed by stroke severity and functional status, such as the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) and the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), may help anticipate the risk of recurrence and post-stroke sequelae and guide individualized support after discharge. Home-based monitoring during the early recovery period—through periodic telephone follow-up or home visits—may facilitate early detection of clinical deterioration. In addition, medication education and review, particularly for older patients with multiple comorbidities, may support secondary prevention and reduce medication-related complications.

Finally, risk stratification using predicted readmission probability may help prioritize transitional care resources. Readmission prediction models for stroke, including machine learning–based approaches [6], as well as a recent systematic review [52], suggest that high-risk patients can be identified with reasonable accuracy. Integrating such risk prediction tools into TCMs may enable more targeted and efficient post-discharge interventions for patients at greatest risk.

Strengths

This study has several strengths. First, it used a large-scale, nationally representative NHIS claims database, which enhances the generalizability of the findings at the population level. Second, by evaluating readmission using three analytic models with increasing clinical breadth, the study was able to compare the prognostic relevance of stroke recurrence, stroke-related readmission, and all-cause readmission within a unified framework. Third, the robustness of the findings was supported by calibration analyses and sensitivity analyses using alternative matching ratios.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, stroke severity measures (e.g., NIHSS, mRS) and stroke subtypes were unavailable in claims data, limiting adjustment for clinical severity. Second, longer-term readmission patterns beyond 90 days were not examined. Third, discharge destination and medication adherence were not directly assessed, although these factors likely influence post-discharge outcomes. Fourth, hospital length of stay was not included as a covariate, as hospitalization duration in South Korea is influenced not only by clinical severity but also by non-medical factors such as insurance coverage, caregiving availability, and institutional practices, which may complicate its interpretation as a proxy for disease severity.

Finally, cause-specific mortality could not be reliably determined using claims-based data, as multiple diagnoses are often recorded near the time of death, increasing the risk of misclassification; therefore, all-cause mortality was used as a more robust outcome. In addition, the proportional hazards assumption was not satisfied for the all-cause readmission model, indicating potential time-varying associations that warrant cautious interpretation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, hospital readmission within 90 days after ischemic stroke was consistently associated with increased long-term mortality among patients experiencing their first stroke. Using a large, nationally representative claims database with longitudinal follow-up, this study provides robust population-level evidence highlighting the prognostic relevance of early readmission events. These findings underscore the importance of early identification of high-risk patients, as well as the implementation of timely and tailored post-discharge management strategies. In particular, the development and application of stroke-specific, evidence-based transitional care models at discharge may represent a promising approach to improving long-term outcomes after ischemic stroke.
