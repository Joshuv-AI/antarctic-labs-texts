Socket regeneration using a composite regenerative matrix: A simplified clinical workflow with a 5-year follow-up
Authors: Chi-Shan Tseng, Ming-Ko Li, Cheng-Chia Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alveolar ridge remodeling following tooth extraction remains a clinical challenge in implant dentistry, particularly in the esthetic zone where dimensional stability of hard and soft tissues is critical for subsequent implant placement. This short communication describes a simplified clinical workflow for socket regeneration using a composite regenerative matrix (CRM) composed of concentrated growth factor (CGF), autologous fibrinogen glue (AFG), and freeze-dried bone allograft (FDBA). The technique was applied following atraumatic extraction in a single maxillary esthetic-zone case. Clinical and radiographic observations were documented for up to 5 years. Healing was uneventful, and cone-beam computed tomography performed at 3 months demonstrated qualitative socket fill and preservation of ridge contour. Implant placement was subsequently performed using a digitally guided approach. Radiographic follow-up at 1 and 5 years demonstrated stable peri-implant bone levels and maintained buccal plate morphology. The results suggest that CRM provides a simplified, biologically integrated, and predictable workflow for socket regeneration, particularly suitable for esthetic-zone applications requiring long-term dimensional stability.

Introduction

Physiological remodeling of the alveolar ridge following tooth extraction is an inevitable process characterized by horizontal and vertical dimensional changes, with the buccal aspect being particularly susceptible to resorption.1–3 These post-extraction changes may complicate implant placement, compromise prosthetically driven positioning, and adversely affect esthetic outcomes, especially in the anterior maxilla.

Socket preservation procedures have been widely advocated to mitigate ridge collapse. Conventional approaches commonly involve particulate bone grafts, often combined with barrier membranes, to reduce dimensional changes during healing.3 Although such techniques may attenuate ridge resorption, clinical outcomes remain variable and are influenced by multiple factors, including defect morphology, graft stability, soft tissue management, and patient-related healing capacity.

Autologous platelet concentrates, including platelet-rich fibrin–based matrices, have been introduced as adjuncts to regenerative procedures to enhance angiogenesis, early wound healing, and soft tissue stability.4,5 When combined with particulate bone grafts, fibrin matrices may also improve graft cohesion and handling, potentially contributing to improved space maintenance during early healing. However, many reported biologically enhanced protocols involve multiple preparation steps or lack standardized descriptions, which may limit their adoption in routine clinical practice.

The aim of this short communication was to describe a simplified and clinically oriented workflow for socket regeneration using a composite regenerative matrix (CRM) that integrates autologous fibrin components with a particulate bone substitute. The workflow was illustrated through a single esthetic-zone case with a long-term radiographic follow-up and an emphasis placed on handling characteristics, clinical feasibility, and observed outcomes.

Materials and methods

A 50-year-old male patient presented with severe periodontal attachment and bone loss affecting tooth 21 in the maxillary esthetic zone (Fig. 1A and B). The patient was systemically healthy, a non-smoker, and not taking medications known to influence bone metabolism. Adjacent periodontal conditions were stable. Written informed consent was obtained for treatment and publication. Given the esthetic location and anticipated ridge alteration after extraction, ridge preservation was planned to support subsequent implant placement and prosthetic rehabilitation. A composite regenerative matrix was selected to provide biological support with improved mechanical cohesion during early healing.

Results

The clinical workflow is summarized in Fig. 1. Approximately 30–40 mL of autologous venous blood was collected to prepare concentrated growth factor and autologous fibrinogen glue via centrifugation according to previously described protocols (Fig. 1C).4,5 These fibrin derivatives were combined with freeze-dried bone allograft and repeatedly folded and compressed to form a cohesive, moldable construct (Fig. 1D), enhancing graft cohesion and enabling handling as a single unit rather than loose particulate material.

Atraumatic extraction was performed to preserve the socket walls, followed by debridement and irrigation with sterile saline to establish a clean recipient site (Fig. 1E). The matrix was gently adapted without excessive compression to maintain vascular ingrowth. Its cohesive architecture facilitated adaptation to socket morphology and supported graft stability. Socket coverage was achieved using a barrier membrane and cross-mattress sutures to protect the graft and promote early wound stability (Fig. 1F). Healing was uneventful with no complications observed.

Implant placement was later performed using a prosthetically driven approach, achieving adequate primary stability and favorable peri-implant architecture (Fig. 1G). The 2-year follow-up (F/U) and 5-year F/U periapical radiographs demonstrated maintained peri-implant bone levels and stable ridge morphology. Clinically, peri-implant soft tissues remained stable, and the definitive restoration achieved satisfactory functional and esthetic outcomes (Fig. 1H).

Discussion

This short communication presented a clinically practical workflow for socket regeneration that integrated autologous fibrin components with particulate bone graft into a cohesive composite regenerative matrix (CRM). Rather than proposing a novel biomaterial, this report emphasized handling characteristics, clinical feasibility, and procedural efficiency, which were highly relevant to routine surgical practice yet often underreported in regenerative protocols.

Biologically, CRM incorporates three elements associated with regeneration: a provisional scaffold, endogenous growth factors, and a space-maintaining framework. Fibrin matrices derived from concentrated growth factor (CGF) and autologous fibrinogen glue (AFG) form a network that may support early angiogenesis and cellular migration while enabling localized release of platelet-derived growth factors during initial healing.4,5 Freeze-dried bone allograft functions as an osteoconductive scaffold, contributing to space maintenance during bone formation and resorption–replacement processes.

A distinguishing feature is the mechanical compression applied during CRM preparation, producing a cohesive, moldable construct with greater structural integrity than loose particulate grafts. This unified architecture facilitates transfer and adaptation as a single unit, potentially reducing particulate dispersion, simplifying intraoperative manipulation, and supporting early graft stability. Although these advantages remain qualitative, they highlight the clinical importance of materials with predictable handling properties.

The workflow was straightforward and reproducible, requiring no equipment beyond standard centrifugation. Long-term radiographic follow-up demonstrated maintained ridge morphology and stable peri-implant bone levels, findings consistent with reports that ridge preservation may contribute to peri-implant tissue stability. However, these outcomes should be interpreted cautiously, as peri-implant stability is multifactorial and influenced by surgical, prosthetic, and patient-related variables.

This report is limited by its single-case design and the absence of quantitative ridge measurements, histologic evaluation, or comparison with alternative techniques. Accordingly, no conclusions regarding predictability or superiority can be drawn. Instead, this communication illustrates a biologically supported and clinically manageable approach that may be considered in appropriate cases. Further controlled studies are needed to determine whether the handling advantages observed translate into measurable regenerative benefits. In conclusion, despite the inherent limitations of a single clinical case, the composite regenerative matrix workflow demonstrated favorable handling characteristics and sustained clinical stability over a 5-year follow-up period. The results in this short communication highlight a clinically practical and stream-lined approach to socket regeneration in the esthetic zone, underscoring the potential value of techniques that enhance surgical manageability while supporting predictable long-term outcomes.
