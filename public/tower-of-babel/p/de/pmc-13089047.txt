Study on quality evaluation of the entire stir-frying process of charred stir-fried malt based on intelligent sensing and machine learning
Authors: Xin Wang, Yanchen Zhu, Songhong Yang, Yin Su, Tulin Lu, Lingyun Zhong, Ming Yang, Hongmin Yu, Haiyan Liu, Jing Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to establish objective indicators for assessing the stir-frying degree of charred stir-fried malt (CSFM) to overcome the limitations of traditional empirical judgment. By integrating visual recognition, texture analysis, Heracles NEO electronic nose, and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), we systematically monitored dynamic changes in color, texture, odor, and active components during stir-frying. Results indicated that color parameters (R, G, B) and hardness decreased significantly, with defined quantitative ranges established for CSFM. Odor profiles altered markedly, and five key processing markers, including 5-methylfurfural, were identified. The content of hordenine decreased by 56.6%, while 5-HMF increased strongly and correlated closely with stir-frying time. Other components such as catechin and ferulic acid showed varied trends. The study successfully quantified key attributes of CSFM, identified reliable processing markers, and revealed correlations among multi-dimensional indices, thereby providing a scientific foundation for standardizing the production of CSFM decoction piecesKeywords: Charred stir-fried malt, Stir-frying process, Intelligent sensing, Machine learning

Introduction

Malt is a processed product derived from mature grains of Hordeum vulgare L. via steeping, germination, and drying. With a global annual output exceeding 20 million tons, it is widely used in brewing, baking, and sugar production. Rich in proteins, enzymes, phenolic acids, and alkaloids, malt exhibits high nutritional value (Yousif & Evans, 2020). Modern pharmacological studies have validated its multiple bioactivities; including gastrointestinal regulation (Nesreen Aljahdali et al., 2020); antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects; inhibition of prolactin secretion (Wang et al., 2020), and antitumor properties. Owing to these combined nutritional and functional benefits, malt is officially designated as a “medicine-food homologous” substance in China.

According to traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) theory, stir-frying malt to a charred state (CSFM) enhances its digestion-promoting effects compared with raw malt (Ge et al., 2021). Modern studies have confirmed that processing improves gastrointestinal motility (Wu et al., 2019). Since differently processed malt slices serve distinct clinical purposes, precise control of processing intensity is essential for quality assurance. However, CSFM production still relies mainly on empirical judgment based on subjective visual and odor evaluation. This method lacks objective quantitative indicators, leading to poor batch-to-batch consistency. Studies have shown that differences in processing experience can lead to extremely significant changes in the contents of index components in processed products. This is particularly true for components sensitive to processing conditions, such as 5-HMF, whose content can fluctuate by several times or even more between different batches. The absence of robust techniques for objectively assessing the processing degree and clear digital standards for quality judgment has become a major bottleneck hindering the standardization and modern quality control of CSFM processing.

At present, existing research on CSFM is mainly focused on the following aspects, all of which have obvious limitations: one focuses on component analysis, using techniques such as high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) to identify and quantify its active components (Gu et al., 2023); but these methods cannot reflect the dynamic changes during the processing process; the other focuses on the optimization of a single processing parameter (He et al., 2017; Luo et al., 2024), which can only optimize a single variable and fail to achieve a comprehensive and objective evaluation of the processing degree. In addition, some studies have attempted to use instruments such as colorimeters to explore the relationship between the color change of CSFM and its chemical composition, but colorimeter detection requires crushing samples, which cannot reflect the actual color change of malt during the stir-frying process. More importantly, existing research has a lack of systematization, mostly being fragmented laboratory-level operations, lacking a complete and systematic evaluation system, and unable to support the “real-time detection and precise regulation” needs of intelligent online production of decoction pieces. In this context, intelligent sensing technology has become the key to breaking the bottleneck, which can realize the objective quantification of subjective indicators, accurately capture the dynamic changes during processing, and be suitable for industrial production.

Intelligent sensory technology is a cutting-edge approach that simulates human sensory functions by integrating sensors with intelligent algorithms to enable the objective evaluation of product quality. It has been widely applied in various aspects of quality assessment for food and TCM. (Li et al., 2022; Zhang, Chen, et al., 2025). As a core branch of artificial intelligence, machine learning enables automatic learning of patterns from data and independently accomplishes prediction, classification, and decision-making. Its advantage lies in processing complex information and mining patterns that are difficult for humans to detect, thereby driving the intelligent transformation of various industries. This technique has been widely applied in the field of food development (Pan et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023; Zhang, Wang, et al., 2025a). This study introduces a novel, integrated approach combining visual recognition, texture analysis, Heracles NEO ultra-fast gas-phase electronic nose and HPLC. By fusing these techniques with multivariate statistics and machine learning, we systematically investigate the dynamic changes and interrelationships among color, odor, texture, and chemical constituents during the stir-frying process. The primary innovation lies in establishing quantifiable correlations between observable traits and intrinsic quality markers by Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall correlation analyses, which systematically reveals the relationships among color, texture, volatile compounds, and chemical components. These statistically validated correlations provide a reliable data-driven foundation for the precise and intelligent control of CSFM processing.

Materials and methods

Malt samples and chemical reagents

Malt medicinal materials were purchased from Bo zhou City (Anhui, China), Zao zhuang City (Shandong, China), and Yongcheng City (Henan, China), with detailed information provided in Table S1. Malt is prepared from fresh barley grains in accordance with the processing guidelines (0213) of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (Chinese Pharmacopoeia Commission, 2025). It was identified by Professor Jing Zhu from the Research Center for Traditional Chinese Medicine Resources and Ethnic Minority Medicine, Jiangxi University of Chinese Medicine as the germinated and processed product of the mature fruits of Hordeum vulgare L., a plant of the Poaceae family.

Chromatographic grade methanol and acetonitrile (ACS Enke Chemical Co., Ltd., USA); water was ultrapure water; other reagents were of analytical grade (Xi long Chemical Co., Ltd.).

Preparation of CSFM with different stir-frying times

The raw malt was purified by removing impurities and then stir-fried in batches using an electromagnetic stir-frying machine (Kang hua CYD-900, China). The stir-frying was conducted at 260 °C and 30 rpm for 30 min. Samples were collected at 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 22.5, 25, 27.5, and 30 min during the process. This yielded nine samples per batch with varying processing degrees, resulting in a total of 81 samples. Based on the standard operating procedure (SOP) for industrial production of CSFM, the quality requirements for CSFM specified in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2025 Edition), and the evaluation criteria from Professor Lingyun Zhong, a national-level expert in TCM processing, director of the National Inheritance Base for Traditional Chinese Medicine Processing Technology. Samples were classified according to color, texture, and odor characteristics: the 0 min sample was raw malt (RS); 5–20 min samples were under-processed (UPS, color significantly different from the “brown” standard specified in the Pharmacopoeia, mostly showing light yellow or light brown under-stirred states); 22.5 and 25 min samples were properly processed (PPS, 25 min fully consistent with the enterprise's mature production process parameters, 22.5 min with highly similar external characteristics to 25 min samples and confirmed to meet quality requirements by Lingyun Zhong); 27.5 and 30 min samples were over-processed (OPS, completely carbonized with black color and loss of basic edible and medicinal properties). Detailed information is shown in Table S2.

Color analysis

An imaging chamber (500 × 400 × 400 mm3, L × W × H) was placed in a darkroom to eliminate environmental light interference. Two LED light strips were mounted inside for illumination. The chamber featured a top opening, where a high-resolution camera (Nikon D750, Japan) was fixed vertically. The camera parameters were set as follows: shutter speed 1/30, aperture f/6.3, exposure compensation +2.0, and ISO 125. Malt samples were spread flat on a background board, and each sample was photographed five times, all 81 samples were involved in color information collection. The RGB color values were extracted from all images using Image J software. Hardness analysis

The hardness of malt samples with 9 different processing degrees was tested using a texture analyzer (TA new plus, ISENSO, China) with the Basic Single Test. In the Compression test mode, the target Strain value was set to 15%, the trigger mode was selected as Force, the trigger force was set to 1000 gf, and the probe descending speed was set to 2 mm·s−1 before the test and 5 mm·s−1 during the test. All 81 samples were used for hardness testing and each sample was tested in parallel 3 times.

Odor analysis

The odor characteristics of CSFM samples with different processing degrees were analyzed using an ultra-fast gas chromatography electronic nose (Heracles Neo, 143 Alpha MOS, FR). For all 81 samples, 2 g of powder was weighed into a 20 mL headspace vial, which was then sealed. Three replicates were prepared for each sample. The detection conditions were set as follows: injection volume 3500 μL; incubation temperature 60 °C; trapping temperature 60 °C; trapping temperature 40 °C; incubation time 10 min; injection flow rate 125 μL·s−1; injection port temperature 200 °C; trapping duration 33 s; initial column oven temperature 50 °C; programmed temperature rise: 1 °C·s−1 to 80 °C, then 3 °C·s−1 to 250 °C; acquisition time 110 s. After detection, data analysis was performed using the instrument's built-in analysis software. The peak area threshold was set to 600, and chromatographic peaks with a peak area above 600 were extracted. The retention time was converted to retention index, and the chromatographic peaks were identified using the Arom Chem Base database.

Active components analysis

The contents of hordenine, ferulic acid, catechin, and 5-HMF in the samples were determined using HPLC (Waters e2695, Waters Corporation, USA). Both standard substances and samples were extracted by ultrasound (300 W; 40 kHz) with methanol as the solvent. Separation was performed on a Diamonsil C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, Agilent, USA). Mobile phase A was methanol, and mobile phase B was an aqueous solution of potassium dihydrogen phosphate (0.08 mol·L−1). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–3 min, 5% ∼ 7%A; 3–9 min, 8% ∼ 9%A; 9–12 min, 11% ∼ 13%A; 12–19 min, 13% ∼ 18%A; 20–26 min, 19% ∼ 23%A; 27–40 min, 25% ∼ 39%A. A 10 μL sample was eluted at a flow rate of 1.0 mL·min−1, the column oven temperature was maintained at 35 °C, and the detection wavelength was set to 270 nm. All 81 samples were subjected to component content detection.

Establishment of linear regression equations

A total of 1.6 mL of reference standard solutions of hordenine, catechin, and ferulic acid were mixed accurately with 8 mL of 5-HMF solution, and 0.4 mL of methanol was added to prepare a mixed standard stock solution. The final concentrations were 32 μg·mL−1 for hordenine, catechin, and ferulic acid, and 320 μg·mL−1 for 5-HMF.Two-fold serial dilution was performed to prepare seven mixed standard solutions with concentrations of 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 μg·mL−1 for hordenine, catechin, and ferulic acid, and 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320 μg·mL−1 for 5-HMF. A 10 μL aliquot of each mixed standard solution at seven concentration levels was injected. Regression equations were established with the injected amount (μg) as the abscissa (x) and the absorption peak area as the ordinate (y). The linear relationships are listed in Table S3.

Precision test

A 20 μL aliquot of the mixed standard solution was injected six times consecutively under the aforementioned chromatographic conditions. The RSD values of the peak areas for hordenine, 5-HMF, catechin, and ferulic acid were 1.99%, 1.10%, 1.82%, and 2.38%, respectively. All RSD values were less than 3.0%, indicating good instrumental precision.

Repeatability test

Six parallel sample solutions were prepared from malt samples and analyzed under the aforementioned chromatographic conditions. The RSD values were respectively 2.28%, 3.15%, 2.60%, and 2.40% for hordenine, 5-HMF, catechin, and ferulic acid, demonstrating satisfactory repeatability of the method.

Stability test

The same sample solution prepared was analyzed at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h. The RSD values of the peak areas for hordenine, 5-HMF, catechin, and ferulic acid were respectively 2.73%, 2.49%, 2.91%, and 3.02%. The results indicated that the components in the sample solution were stable within 24 h at room temperature.

Sample recovery test

Six 2 g portions of a known sample were accurately weighed. Reference standards of hordenine, 5-HMF, catechin, and ferulic acid were added at a spiking ratio of 1∶1 relative to the endogenous contents. Six parallel test solutions were prepared and analyzed. The average recoveries and RSD values were calculated.The average recoveries of hordenine, 5-HMF, catechin, and ferulic acid were 87.54%, 95.31%, 89.66%, and 87.95%, with RSD values of 2.90%, 2.83%, 2.98%, and 3.13%, respectively.

Data analysis

Multivariate statistical analysis

All data were obtained as the mean of at least three independent experimental results and shown as average value average value ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 27.0 software, and one-way analysis of variance was applied for significance analysis with a significance level of p < 0.05. All graphs and tables were created using Origin 2024 software. SIMCA software was used for principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). Heatmaps illustrating correlations were created using CHIPLOT, which is accessible at https://www.chiplot.online/.

Classification analysis of ResNet152 + color attention mechanism

The ResNet152_attention mechanism model was used to extract and classify the color features of malt images with different stir-frying times (Zhang et al., 2020). ResNet152 benefits from a deep architecture that effectively alleviates gradient vanishing and enables robust extraction of complex color features from malt images. The integrated color attention mechanism enhances the weight of color features closely correlated with CSFM stir-frying degree while reducing background interference; leading to better adaptability than conventional CNN models and baseline models without attention modules. The feature information; totaling 82 dimensions; was adopted as the extracted features; including the HSV color histogram (hist) designed in the H × S × V (4 × 4 × 4) quantized space; and the mean; standard deviation; and skewness (mom) of color moments from RGB and HSV 6 channels (Liu & Wei, 2020). All images were divided into a training set and a test set at a ratio of 8:2. The feature weights of the two models were respectively 0.578 and 0.422.

Multi-model regression analysis

Regression fitting between stir-frying time and changes in hardness, volatile components, and active components in CSFM was carried out using eleven models, namely Exponential, Logistic, Linear, Quadratic, Power, SVR, Random Forest, Gaussian Process, Ridge, ARIMA, and Holt Winters (Zhang, Wang, et al., 2025b). These models exhibit complementary data adaptability: Linear and Ridge are suitable for linear relationships; SVR; Random Forest; and Gaussian Process can effectively fit nonlinear relationships; while ARIMA and Holt Winters are designed for time-series data to capture temporal trends. They also complement each other in complexity; interpretability; and generalization ability to satisfy diverse modeling requirements and avoid the limitations of a single model. Model training and prediction were implemented in Python using scientific computing libraries including numpy; pandas; scikit-learn; and statsmodels. The dataset was divided into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%). To further improve the reliability of the models; 5-fold cross-validation was applied; and the models were found to be robust during the training and testing processes (Sun et al., 2021). The coefficient of determination (R2) and root mean square error (RMSE) were used as evaluation metrics. The R2 value and RMSE between the predicted values and the actual values were calculated to measure the models' ability to explain the data and their prediction accuracy.

Results and discussion

Color

Color changes of CSFM at different stir-frying degrees

Color served as a crucial indicator for evaluating the stir-frying degree of malt. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, the color of the malt transitioned progressively from light yellow to brownish-yellow and finally to dark brown during processing. Analysis of the color histogram (Fig. 1B) indicated a gradual decrease in intensity across all three color channels throughout stir-frying, among which the R channel showed the most significant change. The trends of specific color parameters are shown in Fig. 1C, with corresponding numerical values provided in Table 1. During the initial stir-frying stage, the R, G, B, and V values changed only slightly. However, most color parameters began to differ significantly (p < 0.05) from those of the raw malt starting at sample S4. In the mid-stage samples (S4, S5), the malt appeared dark yellow, primarily due to the most substantial changes in the B and S values, which drove the transition from light yellow to brownish-yellow. PPS exhibited a dark brown color, mainly resulting from changes in the R and B values. At this stage, the reference values for the color parameters of CSFM were determined as follows: R value is 104.239, G is 78.433, B is 53.327, V is 0.297, H value is 29.524, and S value is 0.326. The observed color transformations were closely related to progressive physicochemical reactions during stir-frying, which occurred in distinct stages and were supported by the color parameter changes measured in this study. In the initial stage, moisture evaporation was dominant, leading to only slight color changes (p > 0.05). As heating continued, early-stage Maillard reactions occurred between reducing sugars and amino compounds in malt, forming colorless or pale-yellow Schiff bases and Amadori products—this process was quantitatively reflected by the significant changes in B and S values at S4 (p < 0.05), as these intermediates weaken the blue light absorption capacity of malt, leading to a lower B value, and enhance color saturation, resulting in a higher S value. At higher temperatures and longer processing times, advanced Maillard reactions and caramelization were accelerated, generating melanoidins and brown nitrogenous polymers. These dark brown products caused the marked decreases in R and B values at S6 and S7 (p < 0.01) due to strong absorption of red and blue light (Murata, 2021). Concurrently; chlorophyll degradation (Rudra et al., 2008) reduced green light reflection; with a slight decrease in the G value as shown in Table 1. Oxidation of phenolic compounds to quinones and subsequent polymeric pigments (Schieber, 2018) also intensified browning, consistent with the continuous decline in V value during stir-frying.

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a gradient distribution of samples along the first principal component based on stir-frying time (Fig. 1D). Notably, samples S1 to S3 were clustered closely together, indicating minimal differences in their initial stages. The remaining samples were clustered separately, suggesting distinct color characteristics among different samples. Based on SPSS software and a 95% confidence interval, reference ranges for each color parameter were established to define the different processing degrees (Table 2). For properly processed malt, the R value ranged from 96.373 to 138.527, the G value from 72.675 to 111.666, and the V value from 0.378 to 0.543. Although the value ranges of B, H, and S showed some overlap across different processing degrees, the overall trends remained clear and may still serve as useful indicators for preliminary assessment.

Classification results of ResNet152_color attention mechanism model

To further validate the feasibility of using color information as a criterion for evaluating processing degrees, we employed ResNet152_color attention mechanism model to extract and classify color features from malt images at different stir-frying time points. The model input consisted of a fused feature vector combining HSV color histograms and first-order color moments, with an 8:2 split between training and testing sets. As shown in Fig. 1E, the top 15 color features with the highest contribution to classification indicated that lower-order moments of the H-channel, higher-order moments of the S-channel, and local extrema of the V-channel served as the most discriminative features. Classification results Table 3 demonstrated that the ResNet152_color attention mechanism model achieved near-perfect discrimination among all nine processing time points based solely on color features, attaining an overall accuracy of 99%. The F1-scores reached 1.00 for both S1 and S7 categories, suggesting that the color characteristics of these two categories were significantly distinct from others. All remaining categories exhibited F1-scores higher than 0.96. The confusion matrix (Fig. 1F) revealed that the most common errors involved a very small number of misclassified samples between S2 and S3. These results confirm that color information can serve as a reliable indicator for determining the stir-frying degree of malt.

Hardness

Different stir-frying times on hardness changes of CSFM

Hardness of malt samples at different stir-frying times was determined using a texture analyzer. As shown in Fig. 2A, hardness gradually decreased from 40.54 N to 12.49 N, a total decrease of 69.2%, indicating that CSFM developed a crispier texture. This significant hardness decrease was not only attributed to moisture loss and structural alterations but also quantitatively correlated with the thermal denaturation and degradation of starch and protein in malt, which was verified by the texture data and existing research. High temperatures promote water evaporation within the malt, leading to structural shrinkage and reduced tissue plasticity (Sharma & Kotari, 2017). Simultaneously; macromolecules such as proteins and starches undergo denaturation and degradation under thermal treatment; weakening the internal binding forces (Xu et al., 2020). Starch gelatinization destroyed the crystalline structure of starch granules, reducing the mechanical support capacity of the malt matrix. Protein denaturation broke the intermolecular hydrogen bonds and disulfide bonds, further loosening the internal microstructure. A systematic cluster analysis of all samples was performed, and the resulting dendrogram (Fig. 2B) classified the nine time-point samples into four distinct categories corresponding to four stir-frying progression (RS, UPS, PPS, OPS). Using a 95% confidence level, we established the hardness reference ranges for malt with different processing degrees (Table 4); the range for optimally processed malt was 11.56 N ∼ 22.66 N. This study achieves a quantitative assessment of CSFM texture variation and provides a scientific reference for standardizing CSFM production.

Multi-model regression analysis of hardness changes

To clarify the hardness variation pattern of CSFM with stir-frying time, 11 regression models were applied to analyze hardness changes over time, with results presented in Fig. 2C. The coefficient of determination R2 of most models is higher than 0.90, indicating that the hardness change has a significant time dependence. Among them, Random Forest performs the best, with a training set R2 = 0.934 and root mean square error RMSE = 2.58, which is better than the suboptimal (R2 = 0.931, RMSE = 2.62). Its residuals were normally distributed without obvious heteroscedasticity, confirming model robustness. Thus, the Random Forest model enables accurate prediction of stir-frying time from measured hardness values, supporting objective and quantitative evaluation of CSFM stir-frying degree.

Odor

Odor analysis of CSFM at different stir-frying degrees

Raw malt has a mild and gentle odor, with the natural fresh fragrance of grains; CSFM, on the other hand, has a rich burnt aroma similar to coffee and caramel. According to the classic theory of Chinese materia medica processing “jiao xiang xing pi”, the burnt aroma produced by processing medicinal materials is aromatic and penetrative, which can regulate the spleen and stomach, improve their transportation and transformation functions, and promote gastrointestinal peristalsis, thereby enhancing digestive function. Therefore, the burnt aroma of CSFM is not merely a sensory attribute but also a critical indicator of its quality. This study compared the odor profiles of malt at different stir-frying times. As shown in Fig. 3A, the chromatographic profiles revealed significant differences in the odor characteristics between raw malt and charred stir-fried malt with different processing degrees. Raw malt exhibited fewer volatile components with relatively low response signals. With the extension of stir-frying time, the number of volatile components increased markedly. Specifically, the peak areas of most chromatographic peaks within the 10–50 s retention time gradually increased with prolonged stir-frying time; in contrast, some peaks eluting at 55–85 s showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing in the middle and late stages of the stir-frying process. PCA of the odor characteristics revealed that the first two principal components accounted for 95.40% of the total variance, indicating that the model captured most of the information in the original data. The PCA results (Fig. 3B) showed clear separation among samples S1 ∼ S4, while samples S5 ∼ S9 exhibited partial overlap. To further explore these differences, partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed (Fig. 3C), which effectively discriminated malt samples across different stir-frying times. These results demonstrate that the odor of malt undergoes significant changes during stir-frying.

Analysis of changes in volatile components

Chromatographic peaks were identified using the Arom Chem Base database, leading to the detection of 23 volatile compounds categorized into seven major classes (Fig. 3D, Tables 5 and S4). Aldehydes (30.4%) and alcohols (26.1%) represented the most abundant categories, followed by heterocyclic compounds and alkenes (13% each), carboxylic acids (8.7%), and ketones and esters (4.3% each). As illustrated in Fig. 3E, stir-frying significantly altered the volatile composition of malt, notably increasing the content and variety of carboxylic acids and heterocyclic compounds. Raw malt contained 16 volatile compounds, including ethanol, 1-propanol, acetaldehyde, limonene, and 2-propanol, which collectively contributed fermented notes and a fresh, fruity aroma. In contrast, the PPS contained 21 volatile compounds (Fig. 3F), forming a more complex and intense aroma profile characteristic of CSFM. The dynamic changes in volatile composition were closely associated with thermally driven reactions. During the early stages of stir-frying, the content of alcohols such as ethanol and 2-butanol increased (Fig. 3G), primarily due to the pyrolysis of macromolecules including polysaccharides and proteins (Ma, Jin, Wang & Tian, 2022). As processing continued, the concentration of these alcohols decreased, likely a result of their consumption in Maillard reactions (Guo et al., 2023). Simultaneously, low-molecular-weight flavor aldehydes accumulated via the Maillard pathway (Ma, Jin, Wang & Tian, 2022). Notably, seven new volatile compounds were generated during stir-frying, predominantly heterocyclic compounds such as 2-methylfuran, pyrrole, and 2-furanmethanol. These compounds impart characteristic burnt aroma notes reminiscent of chocolate, baked goods, and nuts, and are considered key contributors to the distinctive sensory profile of CSFM. In summary, stir-frying reconstructs the volatile composition of malt, with aldehydes and heterocyclic compounds produced under Maillard reaction conditions playing a critical role in the development of its signature burnt aroma.

Screening of key odor components

To identify the key volatile components contributing to odor changes during malt stir-frying, the OPLS-DA model was used to study the odor characteristics of malt with different stir-frying degrees (Fig. 4A). The constructed model (R2Y = 0.995, Q2 = 0.983) showed high accuracy and interpretability. To verify the model's accuracy, 200 permutation tests were performed (Fig. 4B), and the results (R2 = 0.387, Q2 = −1.2) indicated that the model was not overfitted. As shown in Fig. 4A, the four types of samples were clustered in different quadrants, indicating that the OPLS-DA model successfully distinguished malt at different stir-frying stages. Key volatile components responsible for odor changes during malt stir-frying were screened by calculating the variable importance in projection (VIP) scores of volatile components. VIP evaluates the importance of variables in the model by analyzing their contribution to overall fitting and classification ability; variables with a VIP value greater than 1 are generally considered important (Bi et al., 2019). As shown in Fig. 4C, 11 key volatile components were screened out, namely 3-Methyl-1-butanol, 2-methylbutanal, 3-Methylfuran, pyrrole, 2-methylfuran, Pentanal, alpha-Pinene, Hexanal, 5-Methylfurfural, 2-methylbutanoic acid, and furfural. Among them, pentanal, alpha-pinene, and hexanal are the main sources of the fresh grassy and fermented notes in raw malt, while 3-methylfuran, pyrrole, and 2-methylfuran impart CSFM with a burnt aroma characteristic similar to coffee and caramel.

Multiple regression analysis of key odor components

To more accurately identify key odor components indicative of malt stir-frying time, we employed a combination of seven traditional regression models and four machine learning models to systematically model the relationship between stir-frying time and the content of key volatile components. The traditional regression models include Exponential, Logistic, Linear, and Quadratic models, while the machine learning models consist of SVR, Random Forest, Gaussian Process, and Ridge models. The results are shown in Fig. 4 (D ∼ H). The fitting accuracy (R2) of different models for various components varies. Among them, the Logistic, SVR, and Random Forest models showed good fitting effects for multiple components: the Logistic model had good fitting effects for pyrrole (R2 = 0.98), 5-Methylfurfural (R2 = 0.94), and 2-methylfuran (R2 = 0.94); the SVR model performed well in fitting pyrrole (R2 = 0.93), 2-methylfuran (R2 = 0.92), 2-furanmethanol (R2 = 0.95), and 5-Methylfurfural (R2 = 0.95); the Random Forest model achieved extremely high fitting accuracy for pyrrole (R2 = 0.99), 2-methylfuran (R2 = 0.97), 2-furanmethanol (R2 = 0.98), and 5-Methylfurfural (R2 = 0.98). The regression results confirmed that the changes in volatile component contents during stir-frying are not a simple linear relationship, but a complex process influenced by multiple non-linear factors such as temperature, time, and Maillard reaction. To avoid the blind spot of “single model assumption”, a component is considered to have a significant and reliable correlation between its content variation trend and stir-frying time only if it is successfully fitted by at least two models (R2 > 0.9). Based on this standard, we consider Furfural, 5-Methylfurfural, alpha-Pinene, 2-Methylfuran and Pyrrole as suitable heat markers for indicating the stir-frying degree. Taking furfural as an example, when its response intensity reaches the peak, it indicates that the CSFM has reached the optimal stir-frying degree.

Changes in active components

Changes in active components of CSFM at different stir-frying times

Stir-frying affects both the composition and content of active components in malt (Chen et al., 2019). The main active components of malt include alkaloids; flavonoids; and phenolic acids. Among them; hordenine can directly excite gastrointestinal smooth muscles and is a key prokinetic substance (Xu et al., 2023); catechin can alleviate gastrointestinal mucosal damage through antioxidant effects and improve overall intestinal health (Shabbir et al., 2021); ferulic acid indirectly regulates smooth muscle function by relieving intestinal inflammation (Ghasemi-Dehnoo et al., 2023); 5-HMF is a key intermediate product of the Maillard reaction during malt stir-frying; which can regulate intestinal microecology (Liu et al., 2024). These four components are all potential active components of CSFM for promoting gastrointestinal motility. Focusing on the core efficacy of charred malt in enhancing gastrointestinal motility; this study selected hordenine; catechin; ferulic acid; and 5-HMF as representative active components of CSFM for research. The detection results are shown in Fig. 5A. Hordenine content decreased from 43.82 μg/g in raw malt to 19.05 μg/g at the PPS; attributable to thermal decomposition under high-temperature conditions (Mirza Alizadeh et al., 2025). 5-HMF; a key Maillard reaction intermediate; accumulated consistently throughout stir-frying; stabilizing at 1059.47 μg/g in the mid-to-late stages. Catechin levels exhibited a non-linear trajectory; initially increasing; then decreasing; and rising again later; reaching 25.70 μg/g at the optimal endpoint. This fluctuation is likely attributable to competing physicochemical processes during thermal processing. The initial increase may be due to the release of bound forms or the conversion of precursors under mild heating (Inns et al., 2007). The subsequent decline possibly results from catechins' degradation; isomerization; or polymerization into complexes like theaflavins under prolonged thermal stress. The final resurgence could be associated with the breakdown of larger phenolic polymers or further release from the matrix as moisture decreases and heating continues in the late stage (Lu et al., 2007). This complex pattern aligns with the dynamic behavior of heat-sensitive phenolics reported in similar thermal processing studies (Liu et al., 2019). Ferulic acid remained relatively stable around 7.76 μg/g; owing to its high thermal stability (Gong et al., 2025). In summary, the variation patterns of various active components during stir-frying are different. Among these, the content change of 5-HMF is consistent with the stir-frying time, indicating its potential as a fire marker for CSFM.

Multiple regression analysis of active components

To identify thermal markers capable of accurately indicating the degree of stir-frying, the same set of 11 models previously used for volatile components was applied to perform regression analysis on the relationship between stir-frying time and the content changes of these four active components. The results are presented in Fig. 5 (B ∼ E). The Random Forest model showed a strong regression fit for hordenine (R2 = 0.93), and the SVR model performed well for catechin (R2 = 0.91), suggesting the presence of complex nonlinear relationships between the contents of these two components and stir-frying time. The Power model provided a good regression result for ferulic acid (R2 = 0.94); the Power model is typically used to describe power-law relationships between variables, implying that the relationship between ferulic acid content and stir-frying time is most likely a non-linear power-law one. In the regression analysis of 5-HMF, most models produced fitting curves with R2 values exceeding 0.9, indicating a strong and consistent correlation between its content and stir-frying time. Based on the criterion established earlier, 5-HMF is confirmed to be a reliable thermal marker for evaluating the stir-frying degree of CSFM.

Correlation analysis

Correlation analysis between external properties and internal components

To elucidate the relationship between external traits and internal chemical changes during malt stir-frying, we conducted a comprehensive correlation analysis involving color parameters, hardness, processing time, odor components, and active compounds (Fig. 6). Pearson correlation analysis revealed several significant associations: hordenine was strongly negatively correlated with 2-methylfuran (r = −0.97, p < 0.001) and isobutanol (r = −0.86, p < 0.05). In contrast, 5-HMF showed significant positive correlations with pyrrole (r = 0.87, p < 0.05) and 5-methylfurfural (r = 0.95, p < 0.001). Mantel test results further indicated that color changes were significantly associated with variations in hordenine (r = 0.91, p < 0.05), 5-HMF (r = 0.80, p < 0.05), pyrrole (r = 0.95, p < 0.05), and 2-methylfuran (r = 0.93, p < 0.05). Notably, the functional relationship between 5-HMF and color changes has been established in thermal processing studies of baked goods (Gabriella Giovanelli, 2021). Changes in hardness were significantly correlated with the contents of 5-HMF (r = 0.77, p < 0.05), ethanol (r = 0.75, p < 0.05), and 2-methylfuran (r = 0.72, p < 0.05), suggesting that alterations in hardness are closely related to the Maillard reaction (Baggenstoss et al., 2008; Li et al., 2020). Additionally, furfural content showed a significant correlation with stir-frying time (r = 0.70, p < 0.05). Correlation analysis revealed that the changes in malt properties during stir-frying are primarily driven by the Maillard reaction, along with other processes such as thermal decomposition and moisture evaporation. At the intermediate stage of the Maillard reaction, 5-HMF along with volatile compounds such as furans and pyrroles are generated, imparting a characteristic burnt aroma. In the later stage, brown pigments including melanoidins are formed, promoting color transition. High temperatures facilitate moisture evaporation and polysaccharide degradation, disrupting cellular structure and resulting in reduced hardness. Additionally, thermal exposure induces bond cleavage and degradation of hordenine, leading to a decrease in its content.

Correlation analysis between key indicators and color characteristics

To further elucidate color characteristics associated with chemical components and hardness, this study performed correlation analyses using Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall models. A conservative approach was adopted to ensure reliability: a correlation was considered valid only if all three models indicated statistical significance. The results demonstrated that 5-HMF was associated with 32 color features, 5-methylfurfural with 27, hordenine with 23, and hardness with 39 color characteristics. The top 10 most strongly correlated features for each indicator were selected to generate heatmaps, as shown in Fig. S1 (A ∼ I). Among these, hist_3, hist_5, hist_7, hist_4, mom_7, and mom_8 were correlated with multiple indicators, identifying them as key features for constructing robust correlation models between color and other quality parameters.

Verification experiment

To verify the stability of the established processing parameters and their controllability over the quality of CSFM, verification experiments were carried out, aiming to provide experimental support for the subsequent construction of an intelligent production line and the realization of online quality control. The detailed experimental design, procedures, and results are presented as follows.

Six batches of malt raw materials were collected from three production regions, namely Xiangyang (Hubei Province), Anguo (Hebei Province), and Xuzhou (Jiangsu Province). All raw materials were standardized processed in strict accordance with the processing technology described in Section 2.2 of this paper. After processing, comprehensive detections of color, hardness, and active components of the six batches of samples were carried out in accordance with the methods described in 2.3, 2.4, 2.6, respectively.

The hardness detection results are shown in Table S4. The hardness range of RS was 38.86–44.57, the hardness range of UPS was 20.38–38.63, the hardness range of PPS was 12.12–22.29, and the hardness range of OPS was 9.68–15.29. The hardness of samples at different stages all fell within the previously established range thresholds.

The color detection results are shown in Table S5. The R, G, B values of RS were 183.12–190.2, 162.21–169.32, and 109.01–119.37, respectively; the R, G, B values of UPS were 149.42–188.17, 117.59–165.46, and 63.15–108.99, respectively; the R, G, B values of PPS were 96.97–137.42, 73.1–111.43, and 51.57–62.84, respectively; the R, G, B values of OPS were 55.29–81.45, 50.04–65.74, and 46.84–56.34, respectively. The R, G, B values of different samples also fell within the previously established ranges.

The content of active components varied among different batches from different producing areas. In PPS of different batches, the contents of hordenine, 5-HMF, catechin, and ferulic acid were in the ranges of 9 μg/g ∼ 50 μg/g, 800 μg/g ∼ 1000 μg/g, 9 μg/g ∼ 38 μg/g, and 6 μg/g ∼ 14 μg/g, respectively. Although there were differences in the absolute content of active components, the change trend of each active component with stir-frying time was consistent with the results obtained in the previous study: hordenine content gradually decreased with the extension of stir-frying time; 5-HMF content gradually increased due to the Maillard reaction during stir-frying; catechin content showed a change process of increasing, then decreasing, and then increasing; ferulic acid content was less affected by the stir-frying process and remained relatively stable.

In summary, the verification experiments confirmed that the stir-frying process parameters established in this study have good stability and controllability. Under the guidance of these parameters, the color and hardness of CSFM products from different origins and batches are stably distributed within the preset thresholds, and the change law of active components is consistent. These findings effectively respond to the concern about whether experimental parameters can stably control product quality, and provide reliable experimental support for the in-depth research and industrial application of CSFM intelligent processing.

Research limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged in the present study. First, the detection of active component indicators can only be performed under laboratory conditions, which cannot be directly integrated into industrial production for online real-time monitoring. This restricts the immediate application of the established evaluation system in actual intelligent production lines. Second, although the nine malt batches used in this study are sufficient for statistical analysis and model construction, more malt samples from different origins and batches are still required to further validate and optimize the model, so as to improve its universality and robustness in practical applications.

Conclusion

This study established a comprehensive multi-dimensional evaluation system for the stir-frying degree of charred stir-fried malt (CSFM), encompassing color, hardness, odor, and chemical composition. By integrating computer vision, ultra-fast gas chromatography electronic nose, HPLC, and machine learning, we achieved the objective quantification of traditional quality indicators and clarified the dynamic variations during the stir-frying process. We determined the optimal reference ranges for CSFM color parameters (R: 96.373–138.527; G: 72.675–111.666; B: 50.825–63.016) and validated that the ResNet152_color attention mechanism model delivered 99% classification accuracy for samples with different stir-frying times, demonstrating strong potential for automated quality evaluation. The optimal hardness range of 11.56–22.66 N can be directly applied to judge the ideal stir-frying degree. Using machine learning, we screened six core markers closely associated with the stir-frying degree: furfural, 5-methylfurfural, α-pinene, 2-methylfuran, pyrrole, and 5-HMF. A 5-HMF content of 1000 ppm was defined as the stir-frying endpoint of CSFM, and the optimal processing degree was reached when volatile aroma compounds plateaued or peaked, corresponding to the most abundant volatile components, the strongest roasted aroma, crisp texture, and favorable nutrient dissolution.

This multidimensional system provides a solid scientific basis for the standardized processing of CSFM. In the future, the established quantitative thresholds and markers can be directly integrated into an intelligent production monitoring and control system for real-time, closed-loop quality management. First, inline vision sensors installed at the discharge end of stir-frying equipment will monitor color in real time based on the thresholds determined in this study, triggering early warnings to adjust processing parameters when deviations occur. Second, portable hardness testers will be used at intervals for random sampling, and the optimal hardness range will guide dynamic adjustment of stirring speed to ensure texture consistency. Third, targeted electrochemical sensors will enable rapid online detection of 5-HMF; the system will automatically stop heating once 5-HMF reaches 1000 ppm to avoid over-processing. Meanwhile, ultra-fast gas chromatography electronic nose sensors will track the 6 key volatile markers, forming a multi-index collaborative monitoring network. This integrated system will realize full-process automation of CSFM processing and drive the transformation of traditional functional food production from empirical operation toward data-driven, precise, and intelligent quality control.
