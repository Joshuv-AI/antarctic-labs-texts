Travel barriers to amyloid‐targeting infusion access among older adults
Authors: Jeremy Burke, Albert Weerman, Mark Hanson, Quanwu Zhang, Amir Abbas Tahami Monfared, Soeren Mattke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAmyloid‐targeting treatments (ATT) for Alzheimer's disease require regular infusions and caregiver accompaniment for approximately 80% of patients. Travel time to infusion sites and caregiver availability can therefore impede access to care, especially in rural and lower income populations.METHODSWe conducted a survey in a nationally representative Internet panel with more than 2300 adults aged ≥65 years to estimate willingness to travel for bi‐weekly infusion treatment, and access to an accompanying caregiver. Addresses of respondents and infusion sites were geocoded and driving times to the nearest infusion centers were calculated using Google Maps road network data. To estimate potential access gains under subcutaneous (SC) delivery, we estimated access by relaxing the travel and accompaniment requirements in a scenario analysis.RESULTSMedian willingness‐to‐travel time was 97.5 minutes (interquartile range [IQR]: 67.5–187.5 minutes) one way for bi‐weekly infusions over a 5‐year period. Approximately 90% of respondents were willing to travel the required time to reach an infusion site. However, 18% indicated that it would be unlikely that they could identify someone to accompany them. When caregiver accompaniment was incorporated as a constraint, 75% met both the travel and caregiver criteria. In regression analysis, respondents with annual incomes above $60,000 and those residing in metropolitan areas were 9 and 19 percentage points more likely, respectively, to meet travel and caregiver criteria. In a scenario analysis, SC delivery increased overall access by up to 33%, in relative terms, with larger proportional gains for low income and rural respondents.DISCUSSIONA meaningful number of individuals in a nationally representative sample live farther from their nearest site than they would be willing to travel, and obstacles to access worsen if individuals need a caregiver to accompany them, as is common among early‐stage AD patients. Treatments that do not require infusion delivery could improve equitable access to care.

BACKGROUND

The emergence of amyloid‐targeting treatments 1 , 2 represents a paradigm shift for Alzheimer's disease (AD) patients and their families, as these treatments allow for the first time to slow the trajectory of the disease. Importantly, recent data from the open‐label extension study of lecanemab, one of the two treatments available in the United States, suggest that the treatment effects may be greatest when initiated earlier in the biological disease course (e.g., lower tau burden). 3 This finding highlights the need to identify eligible patients early and start them on treatment in a timely manner. However, the diagnostic journey to determine treatment eligibility is complex 4 , 5 and fraught with wait times because of health system capacity constraints. 6

Delivery of treatment is equally complex, as the drugs require clinic‐based intravenous administration and safety monitoring. Thus, frequent and lengthy clinic visits are required over a substantial time period, and the burden of these visits expands beyond the patient, as a recent study estimated that ∼80% of patients are accompanied 7 by a caregiver to these visits. Since travel time to an infusion site will add to that burden, the question arises whether distances will create obstacles to access to amyloid‐targeting treatments.

Evidence from rheumatology and oncology suggests that travel time to specialist practices may be associated with delays in treatment receipt 8 and lower rates of treatment receipt. 9 , 10 To our knowledge, no studies have quantified distance‐related barriers at the point of initiation for amyloid‐targeting infusion therapies. Thus, it is poorly understood which travel times to infusion sites would be seen as prohibitive and how that threshold differs by individual characteristics, such as age, health status and socioeconomic status. Against this background, this study used a nationally representative panel to elicit maximum acceptable travel times to undergo bi‐weekly infusion treatment. We then geocoded the addresses of respondents and of their nearest infusion sites listed as offering amyloid‐targeting infusions to calculate travel times to determine which respondents would face prohibitive travel times and how individual characteristics would be associated with facing prohibitive travel times. We tested two additional constraints: (1) the infusion site needed to be in the state of residence of the respondent, as individuals with full or partial Medicaid coverage might have that restriction and (2) the respondent needed to have someone who would accompany them to the treatment visit. These results are then used to estimate how the transition to treatments, which do not require intravenous delivery, might improve access.

METHODS

We used the Understanding America Study (UAS), a nationally representative, probability‐based Internet panel, 11 , 12 , 13 to obtain estimates of willingness to travel for infusion care. The UAS recruits exclusively using address‐based sampling to include hard‐to‐reach populations, who may not have a phone or Internet connection. Prospective panelists without Internet access are provided with Internet and a tablet. This approach mitigates selection biases facing convenience panels that draw from existing Internet users, which harms representativeness, particularly by underrepresenting older adults and individuals with low socioeconomic status.

Our survey was fielded to all UAS panelists aged 65 and above (n = 3390) from April 23 to June 8, 2025. The survey captured respondents’ willingness to travel to receive an infusion treatment every two weeks for at least five years. We intentionally presented this high‐burden, long‐duration infusion regimen to elicit a conservative travel‐time threshold but framed it as a “serious medical condition” to reduce heterogeneity introduced by differences in disease‐specific knowledge across respondents. Data on self‐reported demographic characteristics were available through regular UAS surveys. We merged data on self‐reported health status from another UAS survey fielded at the same time (May 1 to May 31, 2025). To capture potential caregiver constraints, we resurveyed participants between December 1 and December 31, 2025, and asked “how likely is it that you could find someone to take you to a 2‐hour infusion appointment every 2 weeks for 5 years?” using a five‐point scale. We compared baseline characteristics of those who did and did not complete the caregiver resurvey and find no evidence of differential attrition (Table S1).

Willingness to travel elicitation

We conducted an adaptive bracketing procedure that elicited how long participants would be willing to travel to receive an infusion every 2 weeks for 5 years before they reported that they would be likely to delay or forgo care under the vignette. This approach is analogous to double‐bounded dichotomous choice formats used in contingent valuation, in which sequential higher/lower follow‐up questions narrow the interval containing an individual's threshold. 14 , 15 Stated‐preference methods are frequently used to measure individuals’ preferences in health care settings and to quantify trade‐offs in healthcare decisions 16 , 17 , 18 —such as preferences for primary care 19 and willingness to pay for universal access to Alzheimer's treatments 11 —and have also been used to measure willingness to travel in situations such as commuting. 20 Starting from a randomly chosen travel duration, participants were presented with longer or shorter travel times depending on their prior responses in a range from 5 minutes to 4 or more hours. We used an identical method in a related paper examining older adults’ willingness to travel to receive routine medical care. 21 Details on the procedure are contained in the Figure S1 and Table S2. Our exposition follows the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) for survey and methodological disclosure, including details on sample construction, methods used to recruit the sample, survey administration and dates, the sample size and response rate, respondent characteristics, survey mode, the sponsor, the full survey instrument, and associated limitations. 22

Travel time to infusion center calculations

We obtained a national list of infusion sites which reports that they were providing amyloid‐targeting treatments from the National Infusion Center Association 23 on November 5, 2025, and geocoded their addresses using the Google Geocoding Application Programming Interface (API). We refer to the sites as “listed sites” as we could not independently validate that they had open capacity to deliver amyloid‐targeting treatments.

We used the geocoded addresses of survey respondents and estimated one‐way driving times between respondents and infusion sites in two steps: first, identifying the five nearest centers to a respondent in a Euclidean distance matrix of respondent‐center pairs, and, next, identifying from among these pairs the three shortest drive times using the Google Routes API (mode = driving; traffic model = best_guess; departure time = 1:00 PM local time on February 21, 2026.) Local time zones were assigned via the Google Time Zone API. This specification is meant to reflect typical, non‐holiday weekday travel conditions.

Estimating access to infusion centers

We used respondents’ individual stated willingness to travel reports and estimated actual travel times to infusion centers to determine access. In particular, we coded a respondent as having access to an infusion site if their willingness to travel exceeded the calculated travel time required to reach their nearest infusion center. As robustness checks, we estimated access after imposing a series of additional constraints. First, to approximate potential capacity and health plan network constraints, we defined a respondents’ required travel time to be the average travel time to the three nearest infusion centers. Second, we imposed that respondents may only travel to infusion centers in their state of residence, as individuals covered by Medicaid may face this constraint. Third, since ∼80% of patients are accompanied by a caregiver to an infusion, 7 we additionally required that it is plausible that our respondent could find someone to travel with them to an appointment. We defined respondents as having an available caregiver if they did not report that it is either “unlikely” or “very unlikely” that they could find someone to accompany them to an infusion. As a sensitivity analysis, we defined caregiver availability more stringently (only “likely/very likely”) in the Supplement. All driving distance calculations were conducted in the UAS Enclave for privacy protection.

Statistical analysis

We described respondents’ willingness to travel distribution, the distribution of calculated required travel times, and what fraction of older adults have access to infusion centers under our series of constraints. We used weighted regression models appropriate for a binary outcome to explore the associations of sociodemographic characteristics and access to an infusion center. Our specification estimated differences using a linear probability model with robust standard errors. Table S3 describes how covariates were constructed. We used two‐sided t‐tests to examine whether regression coefficients were equal to zero, identifying differences as statistically significant if the p‐value was less than 0.05. All analyses were conducted using STATA survey procedures (STATA version 19.5). The study was approved by the Biomedical Research Alliance of New York Institutional Review Board (Protocol #22‐030‐1044), and participants consented electronically for participation.

Extrapolation to national impact

To generate estimates that are more representative of the population with early AD, we weighted our survey sample to match the age distribution of individuals living with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and mild dementia due to AD. The reweighting addresses age composition only and does not convert our sample into a clinically diagnosed early AD population; therefore, we present national extrapolations as scenario estimates under stated assumptions. To do so, we used U.S. population estimates of prevalence of MCI 24 and mild dementia 25 and the share of cases due to AD 26 by 5‐year age groups (e.g. 65–69) and calculated the expected number of older adults in the United States with early‐stage AD in each age category by multiplying by the estimated number of adults in that age group using Census population estimates. 27 We weighted each respondent so that the realized proportion of respondents in each age group would match the expected proportion of adults with early AD in that age category. Details are contained in Table S4. All statistical analyses use weights, although results are similar when using the unweighted sample.

RESULTS

Sample description

We received 2762 responses without missing covariates and matched 2329 of our respondents to the surveys capturing self‐reported health status and access to an accompanying caregiver, resulting in an effective response rate of 69% for our analytic sample. A total of 65% of our sample was over the age of 70, 51% of the sample was female (49% male), 52% had a household income over $60,000 per year, 46% had a bachelor's degree or more education, and 79% was non‐Hispanic white (Table 1). A total of 19% of our sample reported to be in fair or poor health, while 78% lived in a metropolitan area, and 77% were retired. A total of 79% reported that it was either “very likely”, “likely”, or “neither likely nor unlikely” that they could find someone to accompany them to infusion appointments. Column 3 of Table 1 demonstrates how these proportions change when we apply our disease burden weights, showing that mostly the age composition is affected by the weighting.

Willingness to travel distribution

Median willingness to travel time was 97.5 minutes (IQR 67.5‐187.5 minutes) one way to receive infusion care (Figure 1). Importantly, most respondents were willing to travel substantial durations–77% were willing to travel at least 1 hour and 17% were willing to travel 4 or more hours (the maximum travel time presented). Only 6% of respondents were willing to travel fewer than 30 minutes. Willingness to travel times varied slightly across demographic characteristics. For example, those living in more rural areas had a higher median willingness to travel time of 112.5 minutes, while individuals with household incomes below $60,000 per year had a median willingness to travel time of 82.5 minutes.

Geographic coverage of infusion sites

Figure 2 shows the locations of the 2003 infusion sites (green dots) that are listed as administering amyloid‐targeting treatments. The distribution of infusion centers mirrors U.S. population, with a higher concentration of facilities along the densely populated Eastern seaboard and major metropolitan areas than in sparsely populated rural areas, particularly in the Mountain West and Northern Plains. Note that small populations in the Hawaiian Islands (including Kauai and Molokai) must travel by air or boat to the nearest center, likewise for residents of the Aleutian Islands and Alexander Archipelago in Alaska.

Geographic accessibility

Five respondents resided on a Hawaiian or Alaskan island without an infusion center and accordingly could not reach an infusion center by car. Excluding these respondents, median driving time to the nearest infusion site was 16.6 minutes (IQR 10.2–33.0 minutes). When using average travel time to the nearest three infusion sites to approximate network and capacity constraints, eight additional respondents would be unable to access a second or third infusion center as they resided on a Hawaiian island with only one center. Omitting these individuals, median travel time was 23.0 minutes (IQR 14.8–44.9 minutes). Requiring one to remain in state to receive care altered required travel times only slightly; median travel time to the nearest within state infusion center was 16.8 minutes (IQR 10.2–34.3 minutes).

As the distribution of infusion centers mirrors the U.S. population, there are notable differences in required drive times by urbanicity. Using average travel time to the nearest three centers, median drive time for those in metropolitan areas was 18.8 minutes (IQR 13.5–31.3 minutes), while median drive time was 57.1 minutes (IQR 46.2–73.3 minutes) for those living in more rural areas.

The vast majority of our survey sample had geographic access to infusion care (Table 2): 94% of respondents were willing to travel for the requisite duration based on their individual willingness to travel time to access their nearest infusion site. This percentage drops slightly to 90% when requiring that an individual travel the average travel time to one's nearest three infusion centers. Adding an additional restriction requiring one to stay in state reduces access to 88%. Caregiver access is a larger barrier in our sample. As noted above 82% of respondents reported that it is not unlikely that they could find someone to accompany them to infusion appointments. When requiring access to an accompanying caregiver, 75% of respondents would be willing and able to travel the average travel time to their nearest three infusion centers. This figure drops to 74% when additionally requiring that those infusion centers be within state. Using the stricter requirement that respondents must be “likely” or “very likely” to have access to a caregiver, these figures fall to 61% and 60%, respectively (Table S5). Among respondents without access under the “Avg 3 nearest” constraint, the median shortfall (required drive time minus stated willingness) was 23.4 minutes (IQR 13.2–55.8 minutes).

Association between demographic characteristics and accessibility

Table 3 examines how demographic characteristics are related to whether one has geographic access to infusion care. We found no evidence of differences for most individual characteristics, although higher income respondents and those that live in metropolitan areas were significantly more likely to live in proximity to an infusion site that required a shorter travel time than what they were willing to accept. When imposing an accompanying caregiver requirement (Table 3, Column 2), individuals with annual household incomes above $60,000 were 9.0 percentage points (p‐value = 0.006) more likely to have access than those with lower incomes, while those who live in metropolitan areas were 18.8 percentage points (p‐value < 0.001) more likely to have access than those who live more rural areas.

Potential impact of transition to subcutaneous treatments

Given the high prevalence rate of early‐stage AD in older adults (Gustavsson et al.), 26 the 235 respondents in our sample who were not willing to travel the average travel time to their three nearest infusion centers would hypothetically represent 706,358 early AD cases. When adding an accompanying caregiver as an additional constraint, the 586 respondents who did not have geographic access represent 1,765,896 early AD cases.

Should all the individuals facing travel‐related constraints be willing to receive a subcutaneous formulation, which could reduce or eliminate routine travel to infusion facilities (depending on site‐of‐care model), access to treatment could increase by as much as 11% – 33% in relative terms, assuming no counteracting factors, such as additional monitoring requirements and benefit design considerations. Increases in access could be proportionally larger for individuals with incomes below $60,000 (up to 42%) and those living in rural areas (up to 67%).

DISCUSSION

Our results show that despite a network of 2003 listed infusion sites with national coverage and a median willingness to travel 97.5 minutes each way, approximately 10% of older adults live farther from a site than their stated willingness to drive. That number would increase to almost 25%, if the company of a caregiver were required, as is common among patients receiving amyloid‐targeting treatments. 7 The problem of geographic access to intravenous treatments is a well‐known phenomenon in therapeutic areas with a long history of infusion drugs, such as oncology 28 and multiple sclerosis. 29 Lin et al. reported that patients with longer travel distance to infusion sites were less likely to undergo adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery for colon cancer. 10 Adams et al. found a numerical but statistically non‐significant association of travel distance and delay in receipt of chemotherapy in breast cancer patients. 30 In multiple sclerosis treatment, a two‐way travel distance between 61 and 120 miles compared with ≤60 miles from the infusion center was associated with a ∼10% lower odds of treatment adherence. 31 Limited data exist for AD treatment. A study by Curd et al. found no association between distance to the University of Utah Hospital and treatment discontinuation for lecanemab in spite of an average distance of 191 kilometers from patients’ residences. 32 However, the study does not provide insights into travel distances that might have prevented patients from starting treatment.

We also found that geographic barriers to care were more common among less advantaged populations. Controlling for other demographic and health characteristics, individuals with incomes below $60,000 per year and those who live in rural areas are significantly less likely to have an infusion center within the distance they are willing to travel. In contrast, the requirement to seek treatment within one's state of residence had only limited implications for geographic access.

Transition to a subcutaneous formulation potentially allowing home administration and thus alleviating constraints associated with traveling to an infusion center and locating a travel companion, could therefore meaningfully increase access to care and make access more equitable, as prevalence of cognitive impairment is higher in rural 33 and lower income 34 populations. Some studies have shown that switching from intravenous to subcutaneous route of administration was associated with improved adherence. 35 To our knowledge, no study has demonstrated directly that this switch makes treatment uptake more likely although several have provided indirect evidence based on patient preference studies. 29 , 36

Limitations

The results should be interpreted in the context of the limitations of our study. First, we based our estimates on stated willingness to travel, and actual willingness may differ. Second, we estimated availability of an accompanying caregiver using self‐reported access, which may not perfectly capture actual access. Third, the results are based on a nationally representative sample of older adults rather than actual AD patients. Fourth, we used daytime driving conditions and travel times might be longer or shorter depending on the time of the appointment. Fifth, we used the infusion schedule for one amyloid‐targeting treatment with high treatment intensity, and our results may not generalize to treatments with different schedules. Sixth, we elicited travel times for a “serious medical condition”–willingness to travel may vary across medical condition, prognosis, treatment efficacy, and safety concerns, factors not explored here. Seventh, our estimates of access rely on geographic proximity, and lack of capacity 37 to accept new patients at conveniently located sites might decrease accessibility. Eighth, our infusion‐site list may be incomplete and may not reflect real‐time operational capacity for amyloid‐targeting therapies, which could lead to under‐ or overestimation of geographic access. Ninth, geographic accessibility of other medical services, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans and specialist visits might affect the net benefit of avoiding travel to infusion sites. Lastly, we note that geographic barriers are only one type of barrier to care; others include financial burden, insurance coverage, and language barriers.

CONCLUSION

In spite of the sizeable number of infusion sites that offer amyloid‐targeting treatments and good geographic coverage, a meaningful number of individuals in a nationally representative sample live farther from their nearest site than they would be willing to travel. The potential obstacle to access worsens if individuals need a caregiver to accompany them, as is common among early‐stage AD patients. Treatments that do not require infusion delivery could reduce an important travel‐related barrier to care and improve equitable access, especially among populations with lower income and those living in rural areas.
