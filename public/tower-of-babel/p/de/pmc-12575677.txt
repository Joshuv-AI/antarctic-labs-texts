Evaluation of normalized T1 signal intensity obtained using an automated segmentation model in lower leg MRI as a potential imaging biomarker in Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 1 A
Authors: Jae-Hun Kim, Hyun Su Kim, Ji Hyun Lee, Young Cheol Yoon, Seung-Ah Lee, Majid Chalian, Byung-Ok Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe evaluated the potential utility of imaging parameters derived by normalizing muscle signal intensity on T1-weighted lower leg MRIs in Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 1 A (CMT1A) patients, using a deep learning-based automated muscle segmentation model. We retrospectively analyzed lower leg MRI data of 107 CMT1A patients. An automated deep learning-based muscle segmentation model was employed to extract muscle signal intensities from four compartments (anterior, lateral, deep posterior, and superficial posterior) of the lower leg. Mean normalized signal intensities (MNSI) were calculated by dividing the mean signal intensity of each segmented muscle compartment by the reference signal intensity for each patient. Correlations between MNSIs and clinical parameters (Charcot–Marie–Tooth Neuropathy Score version 2, functional disability scale [FDS] score, 10-m walk test time, and 9-hole peg test time) were assessed using partial correlation analysis adjusting for age and body mass index. The MNSIs of the anterior, lateral, deep posterior, and superficial posterior compartments of the lower legs, as well as the total MNSI, showed significant positive correlations with all clinical measures, suggesting that higher MNSI values are associated with more severe disease (p < 0.05). The strongest correlation was observed between the MNSI of anterior compartment and FDS score (r = 0.57). Introduction

Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (CMT) is the most common hereditary neuromuscular disorder worldwide, affecting 1 in 2,500 people1. It encompasses a group of genetically and clinically heterogeneous disorders, characterized by symmetric, distal–predominant muscle wasting and sensory loss in the limbs2. The most common type of CMT, CMT type 1 A (CMT1A) accounts for approximately half of all CMT cases3. Although there is currently no approved disease–modifying therapy for the disease, encouraging results have been reported regarding treatment strategies for CMT, including small molecule–based approaches and viral–mediated gene replacement4,5. Additionally, due to the slowly progressive nature of CMT, there is a growing recognition of the need for objective and sensitive disease biomarkers that could serve as surrogate endpoints in clinical trials1.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has played a significant role in the evaluation of various muscle pathologies, as well as in providing imaging biomarkers for neuromuscular disorders6–8. T1-weighted spin-echo images are a conventional imaging sequence that has traditionally been used as one of the primary MRI sequences for evaluating muscle volume and intramuscular fat replacement in patients with muscle denervation9–13. Irreversible fat replacement within chronically denervated muscles results in high signal intensity on T1-weighted images14. The use of T1-weighted images in CMT patients has been limited to the semiquantitative assessment of fat replacement. This is because the signal intensity within the region of interest is highly dependent on acquisition parameters, making it challenging to apply these values for quantitative measurement and comparison9,15. Intensity normalization, a process that adjusts the intensity values of an image so that they fall within a specific, standardized range, can address this issue by enabling reliable quantitative analysis and comparison of conventional MRI acquisitions16. Promising results have been reported regarding their role in the evaluation of pathologies across various anatomical regions, including the brain, head and neck, prostate, and uterus15,17,18. However, to our knowledge, not much has been investigated regarding the potential clinical value of MRI signal intensity normalization on T1-weighted images in muscles. This contrasts with the fact that numerous recent studies employing advanced techniques have investigated the intramuscular fat fraction in CMT patients19–21.

Extracting MRI biomarkers in CMT patients requires muscle segmentation while excluding other structures such as the subcutaneous fat tissue or bones. This process has been challenged due to the need for time-consuming manual segmentation process and the risk of intra– and interobserver variances22,23. Recent technological advancements have led to the development of artificial intelligence–enabled automated segmentation models, which can offer effective solutions for MRI analysis that has traditionally relied on manual segmentation24. Thus, it is desirable to use an automated segmentation model to obtain objective and reproducible imaging biomarkers.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the potential utility of novel imaging parameters derived by normalizing muscle signal intensity on T1-weighted lower leg MR images in patients with CMT1A, using a deep learning-based automated muscle segmentation model. We assessed the utility of these MRI–derived parameters as quantitative imaging biomarkers for intramuscular fat replacement by examining their correlations with clinical parameters.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Samsung Medical Center Institutional Review Board, file No. 2024-09-074-001). Informed consent was waived by Samsung Medical Center Institutional Review Board owing to its retrospective nature. Further, this study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Initially, we identified 198 patients diagnosed with CMT1A confirmed to have a PMP22 duplication through genetic analysis at our neurology department and who underwent lower extremity MRI between January 2015 and December 2023. We excluded 82 patients who had missing clinical assessment data. Furthermore, we excluded 4 patients with suboptimal MRI quality and 5 patients who had undergone previous lower extremity surgery. Finally, 107 patients were included for analyses.

MRI protocol

Images were obtained using a 3T MRI system (Ingenia; Philips Healthcare, Best, Netherlands) with a 16-channel anterior coil and a posterior built-in coil. MRI protocols including the following sequences were obtained for the pelvic girdle, bilateral thighs, and lower legs to evaluate the lower extremity muscles: axial and coronal T1-weighted turbo spin echo. To acquire the axial T1-weighted turbo spin-echo sequence of the lower legs, the following parameters were used: repetition time, 612 ms; echo time, 11 ms; flip angle, 90°; number of signals averaged, 1; field of view, 284 × 379 mm; section thickness, 3 mm; gap, 8 mm; matrix size, 448 × 269; echo train length, 3; and number of slices, 32.

MRI analyses

We utilized an automated muscle segmentation model developed in our previous study, which employed a deep learning-based 2D U-Net and manually-labeled lower leg MRI data in 120 patients with CMT, to obtain MRI parameters25. MRI scans of 60 patients with mild intramuscular fat replacement and 60 patients with severe intramuscular fat replacement were used as the training dataset. Boundaries were delineated to exclude extramuscular fat tissue, the bony cortex, and neurovascular bundles during the ground truth segmentation. The automated model performed the segmentations of the four muscle compartments of the lower legs as follows: anterior, lateral, deep posterior, and superficial posterior compartments (Fig. 1). A radiologist (H.S.K) determined the range of the lower leg MRIs of each patient to be automatically segmented by selecting the axial T1-weighted images of the uppermost and lowermost level based on anatomical landmarks, which have been used in a number of studies employing MRI to evaluate lower leg muscles in CMT patients10,11,26,27. The axial image of the uppermost level was at a level just inferior to the inferior margin of the popliteus muscle, where the muscle was no longer visualized. The axial image of the lowermost level was selected at the level of the uppermost part of the gastrocnemius tendon, where the gastrocnemius muscle was no longer visualized. Median number of image slices used for segmentation was 9 (interquartile range, 8–11).

Thus, the normalized signal intensities can be interpreted as approximating the ratio of the signal intensity of the designated muscle compartment to that of the fat tissue. Severely denervated muscles, which have a high proportion of fat and contain a greater number of high-intensity voxels, may exhibit values close to 1 due to the high density of high-intensity voxels within the region, similar to that of the reference region. In contrast, muscles with less denervation will show lower values, as they contain fewer high-intensity voxels per unit area. Through the normalization processes, mean normalized signal intensities measured on T1-weighted images (MNSI) were acquired for anterior, lateral, deep posterior, and superficial posterior compartments (MNSI Anterior, Lateral, Deep Posterior, and Superficial Posterior) of the non-dominant lower legs. Additionally, normalized MRI data encompassing all four muscle compartments of the lower legs (MNSI Total) were calculated.

Clinical assessments

We collected clinical data for patients with CMT1A from the clinical assessment data archives, including age at onset, disease duration, Charcot–Marie–Tooth Neuropathy Score version 2 (CMTNSv2), functional disability scale (FDS) score, 10–m walk test (10–MWT) time, and 9–hole peg test (9–HPT) time. The age at onset was determined by asking the patient when symptoms such as distal muscle weakness, foot deformity, and/or sensory changes initially appeared. The CMTNSv2 is a composite score consisting of neurological symptoms, clinical signs, and electrophysiological parameters. It ranges from 0 (no deficit) to 36 (maximum deficit)30. A nine–point FDS was used to determine disease severity in terms of the ability to walk and run as follows: 0 = normal; 1 = normal, but with cramps and fatigability; 2 = inability to run; 3 = walking difficult, but still possible unaided; 4 = able to walk with a cane; 5 = able to walk with crutches; 6 = able to walk with a walker; 7 = wheelchair-bound; and 8 = bedridden31. For the 10–MWT, which was performed to evaluate locomotor ability, patients were asked to walk a 10–m distance, and the time taken was measured in seconds. For the 9–HPT, the non–dominant hand was tested as a measure of fine motor ability. Patients were asked to take nine pegs from a bowl, insert them into nine holes in a board as quickly as possible, and then remove them one at a time and return them to the container. The time taken for this process was clocked in seconds.

Statistical analyses

We evaluated the associations between MRI parameters and clinical measures in patients with CMT1A, including CMTNSv2, FDS score, 10–MWT time, and 9–HPT time. Partial correlation analysis was performed, adjusting for age at examination and body mass index (BMI), as both factors are known to affect intramuscular fat content32,33. Descriptive statistics for MRI parameters, clinical variables, and demographic variables are presented as median [interquartile range] and range. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

We evaluated the potential of MNSIs of the lower leg muscles as a quantitative imaging biomarker in patients with CMT1A by correlating them with clinical parameters. MNSIs were calculated as the ratio of the muscle compartment signal intensity to that of adjacent fat tissue on T1-weighted images. To obtain these values, we extracted imaging parameters for each lower leg muscle compartment from multiple MRI slices using a deep learning-based segmentation model.

Our result demonstrated that MNSIs of all the lower leg muscle compartments, as well as MNSI Total show significant positive correlations with all clinical parameters. Overall, our results align with previous studies using Dixon-based proton density fat fraction (PDFF), which showed that higher intramuscular fat fraction is associated with more severe clinical manifestations1,7,22,34. These correlations have been demonstrated in several studies reporting that longitudinal increases in intramuscular fat fraction are associated with worsening clinical scores20,21. However, there are some discrepancies between our findings and those of previous studies, as well as among studies that have utilized PDFF of the muscles. Sun et al. found that PDFF in most lower leg muscle compartments, except the lateral compartment, positively correlated with CMTNSv235, whereas Bas et al. reported no significant correlation7. These studies were performed using manual segmentation of the muscles on a limited number of MRI image slices, and adjustments for patients’ age and BMI were not made. It would be valuable to investigate the relationship between both MNSI and PDFF with clinical parameters under the same conditions in the future studies.

Our study is the first to demonstrate potential value of MNSIs of the muscles encompassing proximal to distal level of the lower legs in patients with CMT1A. Although a few studies have reported MRI signal intensity normalization in musculoskeletal structures36,37, to our knowledge, little has been investigated regarding the potential clinical value of MNSIs in muscles. Thus, there has been limited investigation into optimizing techniques for normalization, compared to those explored for other anatomical structures15,16. Since conventional T1-weighted images are routinely acquired for muscle evaluation, MNSI can be obtained more easily than PDFF, which requires dedicated sequences. This facilitates retrospective analyses in large cohorts due to the widespread historical use of T1-weighted images. Further studies are needed to explore additional clinical applications and improve the normalization technique.

The pattern of muscle denervation, particularly regarding muscle compartments in the lower legs, has been a subject of research in CMT disease, as these patterns tend to vary among individual patients and disease subtypes11,38–40. Although the reported findings regarding the pattern of muscle denervation are variable, several studies have reported relative preservation of the deep posterior compartment muscles in CMT1A patients41,42. Our results also showed that the MNSI Deep Posterior exhibited the lowest value among all the measured parameters, which could be interpreted as this compartment had the least severe fat replacement. Regarding the most severely affected compartments, it has been reported that CMT1A patients typically exhibit a peroneal-type pattern of fat replacement, with a predominant involvement of the anterior and lateral compartment muscles of the lower leg7,12,43. Our result showed MNSI Lateral has the highest value, which is in agreement with previous study results. However, further studies are needed to clarify whether these intercompartmental differences in MNSIs are truly disease-specific or partly reflect physiological variability.

Our study has several limitations. First, while the reference anatomical regions used for this study included fat-rich structures with relatively uniform high signal intensity, such as bone marrow and subcutaneous fat, the signal intensity of these structures may be affected by physiological conditions. Factors such as cachexia or severe anemia could reduce the T1 signal of bone marrow or subcutaneous fat, potentially impacting the obtained MNSIs44–46. These issues may require future studies to develop a more reproducible normalization method. Second, extracting imaging biomarkers from PDFF imaging with automated muscle segmentation may offer a more efficient and reproducible method. However, we believe that the results of our study offer valuable insights into the potential of T1 signal normalization in musculoskeletal structures as an imaging biomarker, without the need for acquiring PDFF images. Using T1 signal normalization would be advantageous for performing retrospective image analyses in institutions where PDFF images are not routinely obtained, and it would also offer broader availability across clinical centers. Furthermore, T1-weighted images are less susceptible to motion artifacts compared to PDFF sequences. Nevertheless, we did not perform a direct comparison between normalized T1 signal and fat fraction derived from Dixon-based techniques in this study, although such a comparison would be clinically meaningful. It would be valuable to compare the efficiency of imaging biomarkers acquired from both imaging sequences in a future study. Third, our study did not assess whether alterations in the acquired MRI parameters are associated with significant changes in clinical function, whereas such data have been reported for Dixon-based fat-fraction imaging. Conducting a longitudinal study in the future would be valuable to determine whether these MRI parameter changes correlate with clinical outcomes. Fourth, a validation study using T1 signal normalization in healthy volunteers was not performed, which may be important for assessing the true value of this imaging technique. Fifth, our study focused on automated segmentation of the muscle compartments in the distal lower legs, as these regions are predominantly affected in patients with CMT1A. However, the evaluations were performed on a median of nine axial slices per patient, which may be insufficient to comprehensively assess regional variations along the longitudinal muscle axis. Furthermore, a more comprehensive evaluation could have been achieved if the imaging coverage had included the thigh and pelvis.

In conclusion, our study has highlighted the potential utility of MNSI derived from T1-weighted MRI of the lower legs in CMT1A patients as imaging biomarkers that reflect disease severity. This was achieved using a deep learning-based automated muscle segmentation model. Future research comparing the effectiveness of this imaging parameter to those obtained from PDFF imaging would be valuable. Such efforts could aid in the development of reliable and reproducible quantitative imaging biomarkers for clinical trials involving CMT1A patients.
