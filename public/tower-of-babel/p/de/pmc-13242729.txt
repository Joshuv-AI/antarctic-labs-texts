Time‐course characterization of intraocular pressure in murine steroid‐induced ocular hypertension: Effects of route, schedule, and concentration
Authors: Nicholas Y Q Tan, Sai Bo Bo Tun, Hla Myint Htoon, Veluchamy Amutha Barathi, Tina T Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntraocular pressure (IOP) is the only modifiable risk factor for glaucoma, making animal models of ocular hypertension (OHT) foundational for research. Steroid‐induced OHT is a strong candidate model, yet published steroid models rarely provide quantitative guidance on how route, dose, and schedule shape the pressure–time course, leaving laboratories to pilot protocols and hindering cross‐study comparisons. We examined an OHT model using depot dexamethasone acetate injections, and systematically characterized IOP profiles in C57BL/6J mice across two injection routes (subconjunctival or periorbital), two dosing schedules (3 doses/1 week; 5 doses/4 weeks), and two concentrations (20 μL, 10 or 20 mg/mL). Steroid exposure produced OHT in every treated mouse, occurring rapidly by day 2, peaking on days 27–31, and declining to baseline by day 98. Maximum IOP increased ~90%–100% above baseline (from ~11–12 mmHg to ~22–23 mmHg). Increasing the dosing regimen from three to five doses modestly raised mean IOP over the study period without changing maximum IOP and peak timing. Increasing dexamethasone concentration increased maximum and mean IOP, without prolonging the duration of IOP elevation. Subconjunctival and periorbital injections were performed similarly, with consistent responses across sexes and minimal diurnal variation. These results provide researchers with clear benchmarks for selecting steroid regimens, guide interpretation of therapeutic effects, and offer a reproducible, practical platform for glaucoma research.Keywords: animal models, glaucoma, neuroscience, ocular hypertension, steroid‐induced

INTRODUCTION

Experimental models of ocular hypertension (OHT) are essential for investigating glaucoma pathophysiology and evaluating therapeutic interventions, as elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) is the primary modifiable risk factor for glaucoma. Steroid‐induced OHT is a particularly useful model: it shares similar clinical and mechanistic features with human primary open‐angle glaucoma (POAG), arising from increased resistance to trabecular outflow. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Compared to other experimental glaucoma models, such as microbead injections, episcleral vein cauterization, or laser‐induced trabecular meshwork injury, the steroid‐induced model avoids extensive structural damage or anatomical disruption. 5 , 6 Steroid‐induced OHT also occurs across multiple animal species, including rodents, supporting its translational value. 2 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11

Mice are commonly used experimentally as they are economical, tractable, and genetically amenable. Their trabecular outflow pathway closely resembles the human pathway (featuring a lamellated trabecular meshwork, with Schlemm's canal connected to episcleral veins via trans‐scleral collector channels), 12 , 13 with similar fractional aqueous turnover (relative to eye size) and baseline IOP, 14 , 15 , 16 and without the “wash‐out” artifact seen in other species. 17 , 18 Steroid‐induced IOP elevation in mice also produces extracellular‐matrix changes in the trabecular meshwork and juxtacanalicular tissues that mirror human pathology. 2 , 19

However, the “steroid response” has significant variability in humans: the occurrence, magnitude, and timing of onset of IOP elevation can differ widely among individuals. 20 , 21 , 22 Similar inherent variability in steroid responsiveness may also potentially exist in mice. Additionally, methodological inconsistencies (including differences in injection route, dosing schedule, and steroid concentration) occur in animal studies in the literature, complicating direct comparisons and standardization of procedures.

Critically, what is missing from the literature is a systematic, quantitative characterization of the IOP trajectory itself—its magnitude, timing, and duration; the consistency of response; and how these features depend on route, dose, and schedule. This gap in the literature hampers reproducibility, limits meaningful cross‐study comparisons, complicates benchmarking of novel interventions, and in practice often leaves laboratories piloting protocols before settling on a workable steroid regimen.

To address this gap, we systematically characterized IOP responses in C57BL/6J mice receiving dexamethasone acetate by two alternative injection routes, using two dosing frequencies and two clinically relevant concentrations. By providing quantitative benchmarks across these commonly varied parameters, we aim to enable a priori regimen selection, support confident model replication, and improve the interpretation of therapeutic efficacy in preclinical glaucoma research.

METHODS

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Singhealth Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (2024/SHS/1910; AAALAC accredited) and complied with guidelines set forth by the ARVO Animal Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. C57BL/6J mice of both sexes were obtained from InVivos Pte Ltd. (Singapore) and maintained at the SingHealth Experimental Medicine Centre. Mice were acclimatized for 5 days prior to experimentation and were 6–8 weeks old at the start of the study. Animals were housed in individually ventilated cages under specific pathogen‐free conditions, with controlled temperature (22 ± 2°C) and humidity (40%–60%), and a 12‐h light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum.

Treatment groups and experimental design

To study the effects of dexamethasone acetate on IOP, mice were divided into six experimental groups receiving dexamethasone and one control group receiving saline. Treatment was administered via either subconjunctival or periorbital injections, with varying dosing regimens. Dexamethasone acetate suspensions were prepared in the vehicle (containing sodium chloride, creatinine, disodium EDTA, carboxymethylcellulose, polysorbate 80, benzyl alcohol, sodium bisulfite, sodium hydroxide, and water for injection) described in Table S1, 23 and sterilized by autoclaving at 121°C for 20 min, and thereafter the mixture was allowed to cool to room temperature and stored at 4°C in a sterile container wrapped in aluminum foil until further use. To prepare the suspension, dexamethasone 21‐acetate (≥99%; Sigma‐Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA; catalog no.: D1881; CAS 1177‐87‐3) was weighed and mixed with the appropriate volume of vehicle to achieve final concentrations of either 10 mg/mL or 20 mg/mL, then vortexed in a sterile tube for ~5 min until a homogeneous suspension was achieved. Suspensions were freshly prepared before each use.

Under ketamine (50 mg/kg body weight; Ceva Animal Health Pty Ltd., Australia)–xylazine (10 mg/kg body weight; Ilium Xylazil‐20, Troy Laboratories Pty Ltd., Australia) anesthesia with topical proparacaine, a 500 μL U‐100 insulin syringe was used (with a permanently attached 31G needle) to deliver 20 μL dexamethasone acetate to one eye via either subconjunctival bleb or periorbital (inferior fornix) injection; the needle was briefly retained for ~15 s to minimize reflux, and a white depot confirmed delivery. Saline was administered to controls via the same routes.

Mice in the first set of experiments received dexamethasone acetate suspension (20 μL, 10 mg/mL) via either subconjunctival (groups 1 and 2) or periorbital (groups 3 and 4) injections (Table S2). To assess the effects of different dosing regimens, groups 1 and 3 received three injections over 1 week (on days 0, 3, and 7), whereas groups 2 and 4 received five injections over 4 weeks (on days 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28). A control group received subconjunctival or periorbital injections of 20 μL normal saline on the same five‐dose schedule. IOP was measured twice a day (between 8–10 a.m. and 2–4 p.m.) every day for the first 42 days, and subsequently twice a day on a weekly basis until day 98. IOP was measured in conscious mice using a calibrated tonometer (iCare TonoLab, iCare Finland Oy, Vantaa, Finland), with each value representing the mean of 6–8 consecutive readings.

In the second set of experiments, the effect of different dexamethasone concentrations on IOP was examined. Mice received 20 μL of dexamethasone acetate at concentrations of 10 mg/mL (group 5) or 20 mg/mL (group 6). Both groups followed the five‐dose regimen over 4 weeks, with periorbital injections administered on days 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28. IOP was measured twice a day (between 8–10 a.m. and 2–4 p.m.) for 55 days.

Statistical analysis

IOP measurements recorded longitudinally over multiple days were analyzed using a linear mixed‐effects model. An autoregressive [AR(1)] covariance structure was specified to account for within‐subject correlation across repeated measures, and each mouse had a random intercept to capture individual baseline differences. Day was treated as a numeric variable with a quadratic term (Day2) to model the expected rise‐and‐fall pattern in the IOP trend over time. To determine the Day at which IOP peaked, we used the vertex formula for a quadratic function, −β 1/[2 × β 2], where β 1 is the linear coefficient (Day) and β 2 is the quadratic coefficient (Day2). Estimated marginal means and pairwise comparisons were derived from this model to evaluate group differences over time. Single‐measure outcomes (e.g., maximum IOP) used one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or independent‐samples t‐tests. To visualize IOP changes over time, daily mean IOP values were calculated for each group and plotted as a line graph over the study period. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Macintosh (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and significance was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Overall steroid response (groups 1–4)

All dexamethasone‐treated mice developed OHT. In groups 1–4 (Table 1 and Figure 1A), mean baseline IOP ranged from 10.5 to 11.9 mmHg and increased to maximum values of 21.7–22.5 mmHg (p < 0.001 vs. baseline for every group), with a mean increase of 99.2% (standard deviation [SD] = 21.4), and no significant differences between groups (p = 0.58). The estimated marginal mean (EMM) IOP, accounting for repeated measures across time, revealed a marginal nonsignificant effect of group (p = 0.06). Pairwise comparisons indicated a small but statistically significant difference between groups 4 and 3, with group 4 exhibiting slightly higher EMM IOP (Δ = + 0.43 mmHg; p = 0.01). All other pairwise comparisons were not significant.

Effect of dosing frequency

The impact of dosing frequency on IOP response was explored by comparing mice receiving three doses over 1 week (groups 1 and 3) to five doses over 4 weeks (groups 2 and 4), as shown in Figure 1B. The five‐dose regimen produced a slightly but significantly higher EMM IOP (Δ = +0.2 mmHg; p = 0.04), although maximum IOP did not significantly differ between the dosing regimens (Table 2). The model‐derived peak day, after which mean IOP progressively declined, was similar across both dosing frequencies (day 30).

Effect of injection route

The influence of injection route on IOP was evaluated by comparing subconjunctival (groups 1–2) with periorbital (groups 3–4) injections (Figure 1C and Table 2). No significant differences were observed in estimated marginal mean IOP, maximum IOP, or time to peak IOP between the two routes (p > 0.05 for all comparisons).

Effect of dexamethasone concentration (groups 5 and 6)

With a higher dexamethasone concentration of 20 mg/mL versus 10 mg/mL (Figure 2 and Table 2), maximum recorded IOP was higher (Δ = +1.4 mmHg; p = 0.004), and EMM IOP was greater (Δ = +0.8 mmHg; p < 0.001), but both had similar peak timing (day 27), without significant interactions between concentration and time.

Mean intraocular pressure (IOP) over time in mice receiving 10 mg/mL versus 20 mg/mL dexamethasone. Mice received five periorbital injections of 20 μL dexamethasone acetate at either 10 mg/mL (group 5) or 20 mg/mL (group 6) (n = 12/group; days 0, 7, 14, 21, 28), with IOP measured twice daily for 55 days. Estimated marginal mean IOP (mmHg, 95% confidence interval [CI]) was 17.9 (17.6–18.2) for 10 mg/mL versus 18.7 (18.5–19.0) for 20 mg/mL (Δ = +0.8; p < 0.001). Maximum IOP was 21.7 ± 0.7 versus 23.1 ± 1.3 mmHg (Δ = +1.4; p = 0.004), with similar model‐estimated peak day (day 27) (Table 2).

Diurnal and sex effects on IOP, and effect of dexamethasone on weight

Control mice (n = 10) exhibited a small but significant increase in afternoon compared to morning IOP (Δ = 0.3 mmHg; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.1–0.5; p = 0.001), although dexamethasone‐treated mice displayed no diurnal difference. No significant differences in IOP were observed between male and female mice across the treatment groups, indicating a consistent steroid response regardless of sex. Control mice maintained stable body weight (estimated mean change from baseline +0.16 g, 95% CI: −1.51 to 1.84). Compared with controls, dexamethasone 10 mg/mL (groups 1–5) was associated with a 1.1 g lower change from baseline (95% CI: −2.90 to 0.68; p = 0.22), although this was not statistically significant; dexamethasone 20 mg/mL (group 6) was associated with a 2.52 g lower change from baseline (95% CI: −4.72 to −0.33; p = 0.03), representing ~10% of baseline body weight.

DISCUSSION

This study provides a systematic quantitative map of the murine steroid‐induced OHT response across commonly used administration parameters. Dexamethasone injections administered either subconjunctivally or periocularly consistently resulted in steroid‐induced OHT in mice. Notably, all treated mice were steroid responders, which contrasts with the ~30%–40% steroid responder rate typically observed in humans. 20 , 21 This high steroid responsiveness in C57BL/6J mice is consistent with previous systemic and local glucocorticoid models in this strain. 19 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 Maximum IOP in all treated groups reached the low 20s mmHg, representing an approximately 90%–100% increase from baseline (~11 mmHg). IOP elevation was rapid, detectable as early as 2 days post‐treatment, peaked between 27 and 31 days, and returned to baseline levels by day 98, demonstrating the reversible nature of the steroid‐induced IOP elevation in this model. Regarding steroid concentration, the higher dexamethasone concentration (20 mg/mL) led to significantly higher maximum and estimated marginal mean IOP compared to the lower concentration (10 mg/mL). Nevertheless, the overall temporal pattern of IOP elevation was similar between the two concentrations, suggesting that increasing the dexamethasone concentration primarily elevates maximum and average IOP values without extending the duration of IOP elevation. Comparing dosing regimens, the maximum IOP and timing of the IOP turning point (corresponding to the apex of the model‐fitted IOP curve) were similar between the three‐dose regimen (over 1 week) and the five‐dose regimen (over 4 weeks). However, the estimated marginal mean IOP was significantly higher with the five‐dose regimen, suggesting that this approach maintained elevated IOP for a longer cumulative period. With regard to the effect of treatment route, both subconjunctival and periorbital injections produced comparable results.

Although dexamethasone injections are a recognized means of producing OHT in mice, IOP time‐profiles have previously been reported for only a limited number of periocular injection protocols. For example, Patel et al. 23 and Sharif et al. 28 have provided IOP trajectories (measured 1–2 times per week) following once‐weekly periocular injections of dexamethasone acetate (10 mg/mL), demonstrating robust, sustained OHT, with onset within 3 days to 1 week, with peak IOPs in the high teens to low 20s mmHg with this single steroid regimen. In our series, significant IOP elevation was detectable as early as 2 days after the first injection, which likely reflects the more frequent initial IOP sampling (twice daily in conscious mice). To extend these prior studies, we systematically characterized IOP responses across two injection routes (subconjunctival and periorbital), two dosing frequencies, and two clinically relevant concentrations, providing full pressure–time curves, responder rates, and parameter‐specific effect sizes to guide regimen selection in future experiments.

Prior studies have reported varying degrees of success with different steroid delivery methods in murine models. For instance, systemic dexamethasone administration using subcutaneous osmotic pumps by Whitlock et al. 24 resulted in only modest IOP elevations (~25%) and a subset of non‐responders (~20%). Overby et al. 19 using a similar subcutaneous osmotic pumps regimen also only had mild IOP elevation of 2–3 mmHg. Additionally, systemic delivery via pumps requires surgical implantation and carries a high dropout rate due to systemic adverse effects and loss of body weight, reducing its utility. 19 Topical dexamethasone eye drop regimens, such as those reported by Zode et al. 25 and Razali et al., 26 can induce substantial IOP elevations (up to ~7 mmHg with thrice‐daily dosing). However, topical administration necessitates frequent dosing by trained technicians, repeated animal handling, and is associated with variable responder rates (80%–95%) and comparatively lower IOP elevations (~40%–50%) if the eye drops are given less frequently. 26

Our injection‐based approach addresses these limitations and builds upon periocular and subconjunctival injection methods previously explored. 23 , 27 , 29 For example, Zeng et al. 27 demonstrated periocular injections of dexamethasone acetate (20 μL, 10 mg/mL), achieving approximately 60% IOP elevation, with all mice responding. Patel et al. 23 showed that weekly periocular dexamethasone injections (20 μL of 10 mg/mL for 4 weeks) produced rapid and consistent OHT, with IOP increases of up to 8–9 mmHg and minimal systemic toxicity. Additionally, Patel et al. 23 noted in pilot experiments that alternative routes, including subconjunctival injections, did not yield reproducible IOP elevation in their study. Similarly, Kumar et al. 30 found no significant increase in IOP after a single subconjunctival triamcinolone acetonide injection, although aqueous outflow facility was measurably reduced. This contrasts with our results, where both periocular and subconjunctival routes were equally effective. Nevertheless, other groups, such as Kowal et al., 29 also demonstrated that weekly subconjunctival injections of dexamethasone suspension (20 μL, 10 mg/mL over 4 weeks) markedly increased IOP to 18–22 mmHg compared to baseline levels of 13–16 mmHg, further aligning with our findings.

The injection‐based model presented here eliminates the need for invasive surgical implantation (e.g., osmotic pumps) or repetitive daily dosing (e.g., topical application), reducing animal stress and handling. Our local dexamethasone acetate injections given at a concentration of 10 mg/mL also did not significantly affect body weight, consistent with results from Patel et al. 23 (also utilizing 10 mg/mL concentrations). However, with higher concentrations at 20 mg/mL, there was ~10% weight loss, indicating that higher concentrations given locally still risk adverse systemic effects—an important practical limitation. Nonetheless, our method consistently produced robust steroid responses with rapid onset and substantial IOP elevations, typically higher than those of topical or systemic dexamethasone delivery, suggesting superior potency likely due to sustained higher local drug concentrations in ocular tissues, due to an insoluble dexamethasone acetate suspension that forms a depot at the injection site. The greater efficacy in inducing a steroid response with insoluble dexamethasone suspensions compared to aqueous‐soluble formulations (e.g., dexamethasone sodium phosphate) was similarly highlighted by Patel et al. 23 Furthermore, our finding that both subconjunctival and periocular injections had similar efficacy allows for flexibility from researchers in choosing injection routes in their OHT models based on technical preference or experimental needs.

In addition to examining the magnitude and timing of IOP elevations, we evaluated diurnal variation in IOP. Under normal physiological conditions, IOP exhibits diurnal fluctuations, which have been linked not only to endogenous cortisol rhythms but also to changes in aqueous humor production, conventional outflow facility, episcleral venous pressure, autonomic tone, and behavioral factors such as activity and posture. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 Consistent with this, saline‐treated control mice showed a small but significant increase in afternoon IOP compared to morning measurements. In contrast, dexamethasone‐treated mice showed no diurnal IOP variation, suggesting that exogenous steroid exposure may override or dampen these endogenous circadian influences. Practically, this finding indicates that once‐daily IOP measurements are sufficient in this model, streamlining experimental protocols and minimizing animal handling. Additionally, the absence of sex‐based differences in IOP responses indicates that mice of either sex can be reliably used in this model.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we focused on IOP and did not directly measure conventional outflow facility or trabecular meshwork (TM) remodeling; accordingly, our data benchmark the magnitude and time‐course of steroid‐induced OHT but do not address the underlying mechanism. Prior murine glucocorticoid models have demonstrated reduced outflow facility and characteristic TM molecular/ultrastructural changes. 19 , 23 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 30 Second, we did not include a topical dexamethasone arm. Although topical dosing is clinically relevant and widely used experimentally, our intent here was to benchmark depot dexamethasone acetate injection regimens; head‐to‐head comparisons with topical protocols remain important future work. Third, steroid dosing was limited to 4 weeks (although IOP monitoring extended beyond this); it is possible that prolonged steroid exposure may lead to higher maximum IOP levels, extended elevation, or diminished reversibility, but this remains uncertain without further investigation. Finally, we used only young adult C57BL/6J mice, potentially overlooking variability in steroid response that might occur in other strains or ages. 15 , 35

In conclusion, this study reports on a reliable murine model of steroid‐induced OHT, clearly delineating the effects of injection route, dosing frequency, and drug concentration on IOP response. All mice treated with dexamethasone acetate developed OHT, which was of rapid onset and reversible. Both subconjunctival and periocular injection routes were similarly effective. Increasing dexamethasone concentration raised the maximum and average IOP without affecting the temporal IOP profile. Although the extended five‐dose regimen did not significantly alter maximum IOP or timing at which IOP peaked and subsequently declined, it resulted in a slightly but significantly higher overall IOP, suggesting a cumulative effect of extended dosing. This flexible and robust murine model of steroid‐induced OHT enhances the toolkit for preclinical glaucoma research, providing researchers with options tailored to their experimental requirements.
