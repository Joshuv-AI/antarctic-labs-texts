The first Mpox case in Zambia: a case report
Authors: Musole Chipoya, Cephas Sialubanje, Daliso Ngulube, Muzala Kapin’a, Joseph Chikuluma, Chipo Mambo, Kelvin Mwangilwa, Davie Simwaba, Dabwitso Banda, Nyambe Sinyange, Roma Chilengi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMpox, a viral infection caused by an Orthopoxvirus, has reemerged outside its historically endemic regions in Central and West Africa. This geographical expansion introduces a potential risk of transmission in Zambia, where geographic proximity and active trade routes increase vulnerability. Physical examination revealed a papular rash over the face, trunk, and extremities with extension to the palms but spared the soles of the feet. No lesions were noted in either the mucosal or genital areas. However, enlarged lymph nodes were palpable in the cervical region. Biological specimens were collected and submitted to Zambia National Public Health Reference Laboratory. Samples collected, including oral swabs in transport media, whole blood, skin scrapping from lesions, swabs from lesions, and urine samples. Analytics conducted on the samples included testing for polymerase chain reaction for Mpox, human immunodeficiency virus, rapid plasma reagin for syphilis, and hepatitis B and C. This highlights the need for countries to strengthen surveillance systems resulting in increased sensitivity, specificity, and timeliness required for early detection and response to Mpox. Improved supply chain management for laboratory reagents, sample collection, and personal protective equipment coupled with training of health workers is crucial for adequate preparedness and successful containment of outbreaks. Background

Mpox, formerly known monkeypox, was first reported among human cases in 1970 in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) as an incidental finding resulting from the intensified smallpox eradication efforts during this period [1]. Mpox, an Orthopoxvirus, is a double stranded virus in the genus variola virus, and is classified into clade 1 and clade 2. Early outbreaks reported between 1981 and 2017 reported in Central Africa were attributed to clade 1, whereas the outbreak reported in West Africa (Nigeria) in 2017 was attributed to clade 2. In addition to geographic distinction among the two clades, clade 1 is known to have a higher case fatality rate of 1–12% compared with clade 2 at 0.1% [2, 3].

On average, incubation period for Mpox varies between 5 and 21 days and the systemic symptoms largely include headache, rash, fever, body weakness, sore throat, and lymphadenopathy [4]. Mpox virus is transmitted to humans from either infected humans or animals. Animal-to-human transmission occurs through bites or scratches from an infected animal during hunting activities, cooking, and handling of carcasses, whereas person-to-person transmission occurs through close contact with an infected person’s respiratory droplets, contaminated objects, skin contact, and sexual contact, especially among men sleeping with men (MSM), and vertical transmission[5, 6].

Globally, from 1 January 2022 to 22 December 2024, a total 117,663 laboratory-confirmed Mpox cases have been reported from 127 member states in the 6 Wrold Health Organization (WHO) regions. This highlights the significant increase in the number of cases reported as well as the geographical distribution of cases, which previously were predominately reported in the African region [7]. Currently, of the ten most affected countries, globally only one is found in the African region. The USA reported the highest number of cases, accounting for 29% of the total number, seconded by the DRC, accounting for 9% of total global cases. Over the same time period, the African region alone recorded 16,495 laboratory-confirmed cases from 24 countries. Of the total number of cases reported since 2022, 85% of the cases were reported in 2024. Furthermore, the annual number of Mpox cases in Africa has increased since 2014, surpassing the cumulative reports from the previous 40 years [8]. This resurgence might be attributed to factors such as environmental and ecological changes, population migration, cessation of routine smallpox vaccination, improved disease detection and diagnosis, and potential genetic changes in the virus itself [9, 10].

Zambia’s vulnerability to Mpox transmission is heightened due to its land-linked geography bordering several other African nations, combined with its active trade and economic ties across porous borders, allowing for extensive population movement within the region [9, 11, 12].

This results in heightened vulnerability to importation of infectious diseases across its porous borders. Recognizing the threat of Mpox, Zambia developed the Mpox Contingency Plan, which is in alignment with the WHO’s Mpox Global Strategic preparedness and response plan (2022–2026), [13], which emphasizes early detection and rapid response, containment, and coordination via the Public Health Emergency Operations Center (PHEOC).

Zambia implements a robust surveillance system headed by the Zambia National Public Health Institute (ZNPHI), utilizing both indicator-based and event-based surveillance systems to detect and report suspected cases of Mpox. This is particularly heightened in border areas that are characterized as high-risk areas. Additionally, the country conducts simulation exercises at the various administrative levels to assess readiness and response coordination.

Case presentation

On 4 October 2024, a male truck driver of Tanzanian origin presented to Mukando Health post with a history of 3-day itchy rash, accompanied by joint pains, fatigue, and sore throat. He reported that he had no known underlying conditions, was human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) negative and not on any medication. His general condition was stable, vital signs showed normal temperature of 36.7 °C; blood pressure 145/87 mmHg; and pulse rate 97 beats per minute.

Physical examination showed papular rash with pustules over the face, trunk, and extremities with extension to the palms but spared the soles of the feet (Figs. 1, 2). No lesions were identified in either the mucosal or genital areas. However, enlarged lymph nodes were palpable in the cervical region. Physical examination of the respiratory system, abdomen, and neurological system were all clear. Preliminary diagnosis of chickenpox was given with treatment provided to treat presenting symptoms. History of travel, from Zambia to Tanzania on 2 September 2024 to Mokambo border post in Mufulira District, Copperbelt Province, was indicated (Fig. 3).

Following his arrival in Mokambo on 6 September 2024, he revealed having had close contact with a female sex worker and interaction with a fellow Tanzanian truck driver who had itchy skin lesions. The interactions with the fellow driver involved face-to-face contact, skin contact through greetings, sharing beer from the same cup, and sharing a cigarette. Notably, the fellow truck driver sought traditional medicine in a nearby location in the DRC and reportedly recovered within 10 days. The patient then proceeded to Mukando in Chitambo district and arrived on 24 September 2024. He had no recent food exposures other than consuming pork for the first time while in Mukando. He subsequently developed a skin rash, which he noticed on 2 October 2024. He sought medical attention at Mukando Health Post 2 days later on 4 October 2024.

A total of six primary contacts were identified, comprising two sexual partners, two healthcare workers, and two friends, who had interacted with him within the 3 weeks prior to the onset of symptoms. Of the two sexual partners, one was successfully traced in Mukando area, Chitambo district. However, the second sexual partner, reportedly from Mokambo area, Mufulira district, could not be traced due to insufficient information. Through active case search and contact tracing, an additional 17 secondary contacts were identified. All traced contacts (n = 22) were monitored for 14 days and reported no travel history or symptoms suggestive of Mpox.

Every 48 h, samples were collected from the patient and submitted to the Zambia National Public Health Reference Laboratory (ZNPHRL). Samples included oral swabs in transport media, whole blood, skin scrapping from lesions, swabs from lesions, and urine samples. Analytics conducted on the samples included testing for HIV, rapid plasma reagin for syphilis (RPR), and hepatitis B and C; all of which tested negative. To test for Mpox, molecular analysis was done to detect Mpox DNA using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). To characterize the genetic relationship of the Mpox strain and provide insight on the lineage evolution, phylogenetic testing was conducted.

Using QIAamp mini kit all samples were extracted by employing a solid-phase extraction method, particularly the silica membrane technology. The silica-based approach allows for efficient isolation of high-quality DNA, with minimal contamination from proteins or RNA.

PCR analysis was conducted using the Bosphore® Monkeypox Detection Kit v1, which has been designed to detect Mpox virus in human biological samples including lesion swabs, whole blood, and serum. The Bosphore® Monkeypox Detection Kit v1 was used for real-time PCR. A region within the F3L gene was amplified, and fluorescence detection was performed.

The initial detection was from blood sample [cycle threshold (CT) value: 33.6] in Ethylenediaminetetraacetic Acid (EDTA), dry skin scrapping (CT value: 21.253), and throat swabs (CT value: 31.805), all collected on 4 October 2024 and received at ZNPHRL on 7 October 2024. The same set of samples were collected and tested for Mpox with the following results reported from the samples: 12 October 2024: positive results of dry skin scrapping (CT value: 31.289) and 14 October 2024 positive results for Mpox on swabs from dry skin lesions. Of the samples collected on 18 October 2024, only the dry skin scraping from lesions was positive with a CT value of 33.834. Follow-up samples of urine, whole blood, dry skin scrapings from lesions, and oropharyngeal swabs collected on 20 and 21 October 2024 were all negative for Mpox. Dry skin scrapings from lesions collected on 22 October 2024 were negative for Mpox.

The PCR results revealed varying positive results across different specimen types (Table 1). Initial testing on 7 October 2024 detected Mpox virus DNA in whole blood. However, follow-up tests indicated that, while lesion swabs and skin scrapings continued to test positive, blood samples subsequently tested negative. Genomic sequencing of the first set of positive samples and the last positive samples identified Mpox virus clade 1b on 1 November 2024.

Patient continued to receive care at the Mukando health post, receiving symptomatic treatment with Brustan 400 mg three times a day (3 days), and continued with Piriton 4 mg twice daily, penicillin V 500 mg four times daily, Brustan 400 mg three times daily, and benzyl benzoate lotion for one week. He was advised to continue with good hygiene practice and remain in isolation to prevent transmission to others. He reported that the fever and malaise resolved. By 18 October 2024 he reported mild itching with no new lesions noted and scabs over initial lesions, with most lesions having new skin and some pustular lesions transitioning with crust formation (Figs. 4, 5, 6). On 22 October following two consecutive negative results and no new signs and symptoms, the patient was cleared from any follow-up examination and advised to seek medical attention if any new lesions, signs, or symptoms were noted.

Discussion

This first reported case of Mpox in Zambia is of a 32-year-old male truck driver presenting with symptoms suggestive of Mpox, including an itchy body rash, joint pain, malaise, sore throat, and cervical lymphadenopathy, followed by progression to scabs. The rash was papular and widely distributed across the face, trunk, upper limbs, and palms but spared the soles (Fig. 7). This presentation is consistent with the prodromal and active phases of Mpox, characterized by systemic symptoms and characteristic skin lesions. His stable vital signs and lack of respiratory or systemic distress suggest a relatively mild disease course. The patient’s symptoms align with previously documented Mpox cases. However, sparing of the soles of feet contrasts with typical presentations where lesions are often found on both palms and soles [3, 14]. The absence of genital or oral lesions may suggest the disease’s limited involvement in mucosal tissues for this patient.

Mpox is primarily transmitted through direct contact with lesions, bodily fluids, or respiratory droplets. While recent studies highlight the role of intimate and sexual contact in transmission [15], we are unable to definitively determine whether this case falls in that category. This case fits into multiple alternate transmission pathways: direct skin contacts through interaction with a fellow truck driver with Mpox lesions, including handshakes during greeting, sharing of cups and cigarettes, and potential fomite transmission via shared items. The detection of clade 1b aligns with its documented propensity for continuous human-to-human transmission, as demonstrated in the Kamitunga outbreak in the DRC [16]. These findings reflect the evolving understanding of Mpox epidemiology and underscore the importance of addressing high-risk behaviors and cross-border movements in surveillance and prevention strategies.

The diagnostic workup using PCR revealed varying positive results across different specimen types. While the results from the initial whole blood sample revealed Mpox DNA, follow-up tests indicated that while blood samples subsequently tested negative; the lesion swabs and skin scrapings continued to test positive. The initial positive Mpox PCR result in whole blood, followed by sustained detection in skin lesions, supports the diagnosis of active Mpox infection primarily localized in skin lesions, with potential transient viremia. These findings emphasize the importance of repeat sampling from various sources across multiple specimen types for Mpox confirmation and provide insight into the progression of infection.

Persistent positivity in skin scrapings supports the dermatotropic nature of Mpox, characterized by localized viral persistence. Detection of Mpox DNA in throat swabs suggests possible respiratory involvement or upper airway shedding, a feature increasingly recognized in recent outbreaks. In contrast, Mpox PCR tests on whole blood and urine consistently returned negative results after initial detection, suggesting limited viral presence outside the lesion sites or effective clearance of the virus from other sample types over time. Decreasing viral loads on repeat testing reflect the resolution phase of infection [5, 17]. Negative sexually transmitted infection (STI) panels for HIV, hepatitis B and C, and syphilis ruled out co-infections.

This report has several limitations, including incomplete contact tracing and constrained better characterization of transmission dynamics. Analysis was also restricted to specific specimen types, missing the opportunity for broader sample types, such as saliva and fecal matter. This could have offered additional insights into viral shedding. Suboptimal sample collection from contacts may have resulted in false negatives, potentially underestimating secondary transmission. The fact that the diagnosis was confirmed when the patient was over 500 km (Chitambo) from the possible source (Mokambo) made the logistics of contact tracing quite difficult. Another missed opportunity is the lack of immunological assays to screen for and quantify Mpox-specific immunoglobulin M (for acute response) and immunoglobulin G for more longer-term response.

The case report aligns with and expands current knowledge of Mpox, confirming direct skin contact and intimate interactions as significant transmission modes. The clinical symptoms are consistent with established Mpox presentations, with minor variability in rash distribution.

Conclusion

This is a report on a case of Mpox infection in an immunocompetent adult male cross-border truck driver of Tanzanian origin with a 3-day history of widespread itchy body rash, joint pains, malaise, sore throat, and lymphadenopathy characterized by sustained viral detection in skin lesions despite initial viremia. The findings highlight the importance of cross-border transmission and the growing risk of transmission in areas where the virus has not been historically endemic. The findings can provide important target areas for interventions. Our finding highlights strategies to aid in improved case detection and timely response by strengthening mechanisms of diseases surveillance and response to emerging infectious such as Mpox; and also for regular evaluation of sensitivity, specificity, and timeliness of the surveillance system. Furthermore, the findings demonstrate a need for a comprehensive approach focusing on: (a) formalizing cross-border coordination and collaboration mechanisms in line with international health regulations (IHR), [18]; (b) deploying mobile clinics and leveraging digital platforms to enhance access to healthcare services for mobile populations and foster timely reporting of symptoms; (c) improving supply chain management to maintain adequate stocks of personal protective equipment (PPE) and ensure continuous diagnostic support and timely case management; (d) prioritizing health worker training; and (e) strengthening comprehensive Risk Communication and Community Engagement (RCCE) initiatives. This approach will enable the country to not only contain Mpox outbreaks effectively, but also to improve its preparedness for future emerging infectious diseases, build a resilient Mpox response system that protects public health, and mitigate the risks of Mpox becoming an endemic threat.
