Background

Cardiovascular (CV) mortality is the leading cause of death in kidney transplant (KT) patients [1]. Although transplant patients have better CV outcomes than those on dialysis, their CV mortality remains higher compared to the general population [2].

Pulse wave velocity (PWV), a marker of arterial stiffness, is a key prognostic factor for CV events and mortality post-KT. In a retrospective cohort study by Dahle et al., which included 1040 kidney transplant patients with PWV measurements at 8 weeks post-transplant, each 1 m/s increase in PWV was associated with higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.36, 95% CI 1.14 to 1.62, p = 0.001) [3]. Additionally, several studies indicate that PWV is linked to an increased risk of CV events up to 5 years after transplantation [4–6]. This underscores the importance of managing arterial stiffness progression in these patients.

Data on the evolution of PWV indicate a decrease in PWV during the first year after KT. A meta-analysis of 12 studies, involving measurements from 473 renal transplant recipients between 6 months and 1 year post-transplant, revealed a reduction in central PWV of 1.20 m/s [7]. Several other studies also support a decrease in PWV during the first year after KT [8–14]. Data on the long-term evolution of post-transplant PWV are limited and more controversial. Few studies have examined PWV beyond 1 year, and those that have suggest a subsequent increase in PWV [15].

The mechanisms underlying arterial stiffness in kidney recipients are multifaceted. They include the pretransplant history of the patients, such as age, duration of chronic renal failure and dialysis, and comorbidities like hypertension or diabetes. Post-transplant factors also contribute, particularly cardiovascular risk factors associated with immunosuppressive therapy, especially calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs), among which cyclosporine is the most widely used. Since the 1980s, CNI have been a cornerstone of immunosuppressive therapy in organ transplantation. However, they can cause vascular toxicity, either directly through harmful effects on the vascular wall or indirectly by promoting hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Moreover, nearly 15% of patients develop post-transplant diabetes (NODAT), primarily due to KT-related factors, particularly immunosuppressive treatments like tacrolimus and corticosteroids [16].

CNI are likely to play a significant role in the persistence of increased arterial stiffness in KT patients, even after renal function has been restored [17]. Additionally, several studies have reported an improvement in arterial stiffness after discontinuation of CNI in favor of alternative treatments, such as mTOR inhibitors [18, 19].

Preclinical studies have shown that the vascular toxicity of cyclosporine is mediated through the activation of the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMC) [20, 21]. Amador et al. investigated this by using mice with selective MR deletion in VSMCs and endothelial cells [20]. Their findings suggest that cyclosporine’s vascular toxicity is primarily driven by MR activation in VSMCs, which become hyper-activated following cyclosporine administration. This activation leads to protein phosphorylation that contributes to vasoconstriction and the activation of calcium channels, increasing arterial resistance. This study underscores the detrimental role of MR activation in cyclosporine-induced vascular toxicity.

Hypothesis

Given that cyclosporine induces MR activation in VSMCs, which may contribute to persistent or worsening arterial stiffness after initial improvements following KT, we hypothesize that eplerenone, as a MR antagonist, could mitigate these vascular effects. This trial aims to investigate, for the first time, the impact of a MR antagonist on the progression of arterial stiffness in KT recipients undergoing cyclosporine therapy.

Methods/design

Study design

As detailed in version 2.0 (dated 22/06/2022) of the protocol, this study is a prospective, monocentric, randomized crossover blinded endpoint study (PROBE) conducted at Nancy University Hospital. Renal transplant patients on cyclosporine who meet the inclusion criteria will be eligible for participation. Following a run-in phase (25 mg/day of eplerenone for 2 weeks, increased to 50 mg/day for two additional weeks if tolerated), a washout period was introduced to ensure that baseline assessments reflect the untreated state. Patients are then randomly assigned (1:1) to one of two groups: group A receives eplerenone (50 mg/day) for 6 months followed by a 6-month period without eplerenone (with a washout period in between), while group B receives the same sequence in reverse.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria are verified at the time of study entry, i.e., prior to the run-in period.

Participant recruitment

Recruitment is performed by the investigators during routine follow-up visits of kidney transplant patients at the transplant center, ensuring direct access to the target population.

Eligible patients who meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria will be contacted by phone to explain the study, and an information letter will be sent by mail. During a routine post-transplant follow-up visit, patients will receive oral information from their nephrologist, who will answer any questions they may have about the study. Prior to the first study visit, the investigator will provide detailed information about the study and obtain the patient’s free, informed, and written consent.

Randomization will be performed using a centralized, computer-generated sequence (1:1 allocation, non-stratified), managed through the dedicated randomization software of the Nancy Clinical Investigation Center CIC. The randomization list will be generated and implemented by the CIC data manager, ensuring allocation concealment. Investigators will have controlled access to the system to randomize eligible patients.

Intervention

Timing of administration of the study treatment

The study treatment will be dispensed by the hospital pharmacy at visit 1, marking the start of the run-in phase for all patients. During the randomized period, patients in group A will receive eplerenone 50 mg/day as a single oral dose for 24 weeks. Eplerenone will be dispensed at visits 4 and 5 (V4 and V5). This will be followed by an 8-week washout period, after which there will be a 24-week eplerenone-free period until the end of the study.

Patients in group B will receive eplerenone during the second 24-week period, with the medication dispensed at visits 7 and 8 (V7 and V8) (see Fig. 1).

Appropriate administration of treatment units

At the end of each eplerenone treatment period, participants will be asked to return the remaining medication blisters to the hospital pharmacy to verify treatment adherence. The remaining tablets will be collected to assess patient compliance.

Concomitant medication

During the study, patients must not receive potassium-sparing diuretics (such as amiloride, potassium canrenoate, spironolactone, or triamterene) or any oral potassium supplements. The combination of eplerenone with ACE inhibitors (ACEi) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) is permitted, but a dual blockade with both ACEi and ARBs is an exclusion criterion. Significant pharmacokinetic interactions may occur with CYP3A4 inhibitors, including ritonavir-boosted protease inhibitors, cobicistat, azole antifungals, macrolides, amiodarone, diltiazem, verapamil, nefazodone, and grapefruit; therefore, the use of CYP3A4 inhibitors is contraindicated. Additionally, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are contraindicated due to the risk of hyperkalemia. As the impact of eplerenone on patients under cyclosporine is the focus of this study, the combination of eplerenone and cyclosporine is permitted. Family doctors will be informed and instructed not to introduce or increase the dosage of any medication known to induce hyperkalemia or hypotension, or any drugs that are contraindicated during the study period.

Study outcomes

The primary endpoint is the change in pulse wave velocity (PWV, in m/s), adjusted for blood pressure, measured using Sphygmocor® after 24 weeks of treatment with eplerenone. The PWV measurement will be performed according to standard methods using Sphygmocor® Analysis. Sphygmocor®-acquired PWV data will be preferred for the final analysis unless signal quality does not permit accurate measurement. In such cases, PWV data acquired using Complior® Analyse will be used. The operator will be blinded to the randomization arm.

The carotido-femoral distance (L), sterno-femoral distance, and sterno-carotid distance are initially estimated with a tape measure. Arterial landmarks are identified at the sites of femoral and carotid pulse palpation, as well as at the sternal notch. The aorto-femoral distance, which accounts for the anatomical configuration of the aortic arch, is approximated by subtracting the distance between the sternal notch and the femoral measurement site (inguinal arch) from the distance between the carotid measurement site and the sternal notch.

The propagation time (t, in ms) of the carotido-femoral pulse wave is measured by the Sphygmocor® device with a probe placed sequentially at the carotid and femoral pulses. Complior® Analyse measures the propagation time using two sensors positioned simultaneously at the carotid and femoral pulses.

The carotido-femoral PWV (m/s) is calculated by both devices according to the ratio between the direct carotido-femoral distance (L) *0.8 and the carotido-femoral propagation time (t) using the formula: PWV = L*0.8/(t). The PWV will be measured at the beginning and end of each period for both groups (V4, V6, V7, V9).

Tolerance to eplerenone will be assessed by weekly creatinine and potassium measurements, performed in certified private medical laboratories where participants attend for blood sampling. Only patients who exhibit a creatinine increase of 30% or less (from the start of the run-in phase) and have a potassium level of <5.5 mmol/L while receiving eplerenone 50 mg daily for 2 weeks, and who have not used any potassium binders, will be randomized.

Adverse effects

A moderate and transient increase in potassium levels and creatinine is expected after starting eplerenone. These levels will be monitored weekly during the run-in period. It is important to note that if a potassium binder is required or if eplerenone needs to be discontinued during this phase, the patient will be ineligible for randomization. The investigator will contact patients 14 days after the initiation of treatment (visit 2) and again 14 days after any dosage increase (visit 3) to collect self-measured blood pressure readings and to assess any symptoms related to hypotension.

After randomization, blood potassium and creatinine levels will be monitored at least once a month. Serious adverse events (SAEs) will be recorded according to Good Clinical Practice (GCP) standards and in compliance with French regulations. Suspected unexpected SAEs or reactions must be reported by the attending physician to the sponsor immediately upon discovery. Investigators are responsible for documenting all adverse events, whether serious or non-serious, in the electronic case report form (e-CRF) throughout the patient’s follow-up. Adverse events will be recorded until the end of the study. Eplerenone has a short half-life (approximately 4 h), allowing for prompt discontinuation in the event of an unanticipated adverse event.

Sample size and power calculation

We estimate that the study intervention will result in a decrease in pulse wave velocity (PWV) by 0.2 m/s, which corresponds to 25% of the difference in PWV observed between patients on cyclosporine and those who switched from cyclosporine to mTor inhibitors [19]. Ignace et al. reported that the variance of the observed PWV difference between pre-kidney transplantation (KT) and 3 months post-KT was 50% greater than the observed difference [9]. This estimate is conservative, as the standard deviations of PWV differences in their study ranged from 50 to 100% of the mean difference (−5.5% ± 2.2% in younger patients and 2.1% ± 1.9% in older patients). To detect a PWV difference of 0.2 m/s with a variance of 0.3 m/s, an alpha risk of 5%, and a beta risk of 10% (power of 90%), 24 patients are needed. Anticipating a 10% randomization rate for non-analyzable patients and a 20% rate of failure to complete the run-in phase, a total of 36 patients should be recruited.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome will be analyzed using a bilateral paired t-test to compare the pulse wave velocity (PWV) at the beginning and end of each study period. The normality of the distribution of the PWV changes (deltas) between the two periods will be assessed. If the distribution is not normal, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test will be employed. Additionally, the interaction between the sequence of interventions and the effect of the treatment will be examined using a linear or non-linear mixed-effects model, incorporating a random effect for the patient.

For secondary outcomes with continuous measures, hypotheses will be tested using a bilateral paired t-test if the distribution of deltas is normal, or a Wilcoxon signed-rank test if it is not. Differences in proportions for dichotomous variables will be evaluated using a bilateral McNemar test, while variables with three or more levels will be analyzed using a chi-square test. No interim analysis is planned. Analyses will be conducted using an intention-to-treat approach, with a per-protocol sensitivity analysis performed as a secondary step.

Ethical issues

Ethics committee approval

This study follows the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and complies with Good Clinical Practice (GCP). The trial has been approved by the French health authorities (Agence Nationale de Sécurité du Médicament et des Produits de Santé, ANSM) and by the ethics committees (Comités de Protection des Personnes, CPP).

Information and consent forms

Patients are included based on written information and by signing an informed consent form (see Additional file 2). The informed consent form clearly states that patients can withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their current or future medical treatment.

All planned analyses of biological samples are included in the initial informed consent signed by participants.

Interim analyses and study termination rules

An independent Data Safety and Monitoring Board (DSMB) consisting of four international experts in kidney transplantation and biostatistics will oversee the safety of the trial and monitor research progress in accordance with the protocol. Serious adverse events (SAEs) will be reviewed during DSMB sessions, with the board blinded to the randomization arm. No interim analyses are planned. The DSMB has the authority to terminate the trial early for the following reasons: detection of adverse events, poor data quality, inadequate implementation, fraud, or new information that would render the trial unnecessary, futile, or unethical.

Discussion

Challenge of CNI treatment in the current epidemiological context of kidney transplantation

Over the past decades, the number of KT recipients has risen, along with the average age of both recipients and donors (https://rams.agence-biomedecine.fr/greffe-renale-0). Arterial stiffness, partly attributed to the toxicity of CNIs, significantly contributes to CV mortality among these patients [3]. CV death remains the leading cause of mortality among KT patients, with a risk that remains significantly higher compared to the general population [22, 23].

Although arterial stiffness appears to decrease within the first year after KT [4, 7] some studies suggest that this initial improvement may be followed by subsequent stiffening. Kim et al. observed that 52.4% of patients at 1 year post-transplant had arterial stiffness (PWV) levels comparable to those before KT, which remain higher than those of the general population [4]. Desjardins et al. found that while the stiffness index—a marker of arterial stiffness—initially decreased within the first 3 months after transplantation, it increased again at 6 and 24 months [24]. Age-related changes likely continue to play a significant role, but the long-term deterioration in arterial stiffness suggests the influence of additional risk factors associated with KT. Several studies indicate that CNIs may have a detrimental effect on arterial stiffness [5, 17].

In recent decades, the average age of cadaveric kidney donors has significantly increased (https://rams.agence-biomedecine.fr/greffe-renale-0) with a rise in the number of expanded-criteria kidney donors (ECD). Delahousse et al. [25] reported that donor age is an independent factor affecting PWV at 1 year in KT recipients from deceased donors. Specifically, PWV was found to be 1 m/s higher in recipients of kidneys from donors over the age of 53, the majority of whom were ECD donors.

Finally, the increasing age of KT recipients and the associated burden of comorbidities, enhancing CV prognosis in these patients, presents a significant public health challenge. This elevated risk underscores the importance of targeted CV risk management strategies in the specific population of older KT recipients [22, 23].

Choice of inclusion criteria

Cyclosporine remains widely used in transplantation despite tacrolimus being the preferred CNI in Europe and the USA. Cyclosporine may be particularly beneficial for patients at low immunological risk but at high risk for post-transplant diabetes, such as obese individuals. The DIRECT trial demonstrated that the incidence of NODAT 6 months post-KT was significantly lower in patients treated with cyclosporine compared to those treated with tacrolimus (26.0% versus 33.6%, p = 0.046) [16]. However, cyclosporine appears to have greater vascular toxicity than tacrolimus [17, 26]. Preclinical models have shown that cyclosporine directly affects mineralocorticoid receptor in vascular smooth muscle cells in mice [20].

Only KT patients over 50 years of age will be included in this study. Research indicates that in the general population, the increase in arterial stiffness with age becomes more pronounced after 50 years, suggesting that pulse wave velocity (PWV) is a more sensitive marker of age-related arterial stiffness in individuals over this age [27].

Thus, we chose to include only KT patients who are beyond 1 year post-transplantation. During the first year after KT, changes in PWV can be influenced by various confounding factors, such as improved blood pressure control, correction of renal failure (uremia), and the resolution of chronic overload and inflammation associated with dialysis. While there is limited data on the long-term evolution of aortic stiffness post-transplant, a study by Desjardins et al. suggests an initial reduction in stiffness index 3 months after KT, followed by a subsequent increase at 6 and 24 months [24]. Additionally, using eplerenone is likely safer after 1 year, once renal function has stabilized, thereby reducing the risk of acute renal failure related to rejection.

Justification of the choice of MRA, dosing and timing of drug administration

Among the available MRAs, we chose to administer eplerenone in this study due to its superior selectivity for the mineralocorticoid receptor compared to spironolactone [28]. This selectivity reduces the risk of certain side effects, such as gynecomastia.

We decided to administer eplerenone at a dosage of 50 mg/day. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in heart failure after myocardial infarction have used similar dosing regimens: 25 mg/day of spironolactone in the RALES study [29] and 25–50 mg/day of eplerenone in the EPHESUS [30] and EMPHASIS-HF studies [31]. Thus, administering eplerenone twice daily should be both effective and safe (see dedicated section).

Eplerenone will be administered for 6 months in each group during the study period. A randomized trial demonstrated a reduction in pulse wave velocity (PWV) with linagliptin after 6 months, independent of blood pressure, in diabetic patients [32]. London et al. randomized 40 hypertensive hemodialysis patients to a 4-month treatment with either nitrendipine or placebo, showing a significant reduction in carotido-femoral PWV (−2.15 m/s [95% CI −3.6; −0.71], p < 0.01) [33]. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that administering eplerenone for 6 months could similarly lead to a reduction in PWV.

Justification of the primary outcome

We selected carotid-femoral PWV measurement as the primary endpoint. Numerous studies have underscored the importance of arterial stiffness as a risk factor for CV disease in the general population [34, 35]. Additionally, PWV has been identified as an independent risk factor for CV mortality in various populations, including hypertensive patients [36] and those with chronic renal failure [37, 38]. Arterial stiffening also independently predicts CV events in KT patients, as demonstrated by several prospective studies [5, 6].

Carotid-femoral PWV is considered the gold standard for assessing arterial stiffness, according to the European Expert Consensus on Arterial Stiffness (2006) [39] due to its extensive epidemiological evidence supporting its predictive value for CV events and its relatively simple technical requirements. Among various measures of arterial stiffness, only carotid-femoral PWV, which reflects the stiffness of the central arteries, has demonstrated a predictive value for morbidity and mortality.

Safety concerns

The potential risks of eplerenone in KT patients include hyperkalemia and acute renal failure. However, the limited data available suggest that eplerenone is generally safe for use in this population.

Firstly, in a study of 31 renal transplant patients who had been on cyclosporine for over a year with an estimated GFR of 30 to 50 mL/min, and 61% of whom were treated with ACE inhibitors or ARBs, Bertocchio et al. reported that eplerenone administered at a dose of 25 mg/day for 8 weeks presented a low risk of hyperkalemia [40]. Notably, no cases of severe hyperkalemia (>6 mmol/L) were reported in this study. Additionally, a clinical trial involving pediatric renal transplant patients with an estimated GFR greater than 60 mL/min investigated the impact of 25 mg/day of eplerenone versus placebo over 6 months. The study focused on the progression of allograft nephropathy as confirmed by biopsy [41]. The authors reported a higher estimated GFR at the end of the follow-up period in the 13 children receiving the MR antagonist compared to the 10 children on placebo, although the difference was not statistically significant due to limited statistical power. These results suggest a potential nephroprotective effect of MR antagonists in renal transplant patients. Additionally, the authors noted that there were no significant variations in potassium levels with this dosage regimen.

Finally, Mortensen et al. recently reported results from the SPIREN RCT, which included 188 kidney transplant (KT) patients randomized to receive either spironolactone (25 to 50 mg/day) or placebo for 3 years [42]. Plasma potassium levels increased significantly in the spironolactone group, with increases ranging from 0.3 [95% CI, 0.2 to 0.4] to 0.4 [95% CI, 0.2 to 0.5] mEq/L. This increase was of moderate clinical significance, and no adverse events specifically related to hyperkalemia were reported. However, four patients required a reduction in their study drug dosage due to hyperkalemia (potassium levels > 5.5 mEq/L), and all of these cases occurred in the spironolactone group.

Therefore, current literature suggests that the increase in potassium and creatinine levels due to MRAs in KT patients is generally small. In the present study, EVATRAN, potassium and creatinine levels will be closely monitored throughout the study period, with appropriate corrective measures implemented if hyperkalemia or acute renal failure occurs. Additionally, the run-in phase will help exclude patients for whom the risk of side effects appears to be too high.

Clinical implications

We hypothesize that eplerenone, by blocking the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR), will reduce arterial stiffness in KT recipients under cyclosporine beyond 1 year post-transplant. Identifying an intervention that can effectively reduce CV morbidity and mortality is a crucial challenge in kidney transplantation.

Rodriguez et al. systematically reviewed studies evaluating pharmacologic interventions in patients with end-stage renal disease to assess their effects on pulse wave velocity (PWV) [43]. They reported that management of hypertension with calcium channel blockers may reduce aortic stiffness versus placebo, but the quality of evidence was very low. However, there are not any clinical trials in transplant recipients aiming to assess whether therapeutic interventions would reduce arterial stiffness.

CNIs remain the first-line therapeutic class used in KT due to their efficacy in preventing rejection and their lower cost compared to alternatives such as belatacept. If MR antagonists can mitigate the vascular toxicity associated with cyclosporine, this could lead to a reconsideration of cyclosporine’s role in transplantation. Although tacrolimus is more effective in preventing rejection, it is associated with an increased risk of de novo diabetes after transplantation. Consequently, cyclosporine may be particularly beneficial for patients at high risk of diabetes, such as those who are obese, but who have low to moderate immunological risk.

Conclusion

This study aims to evaluate the impact of treatment with a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist on the progression of arterial stiffness in renal transplant recipients under cyclosporine. If a beneficial effect of eplerenone on arterial stiffness is demonstrated in this study, it could pave the way for a larger multi-center clinical trial to further explore its therapeutic potential in this population.
