Precision detection of micro-damage on conveyor belt surfaces using laser scanning and deep learning techniques
Authors: Yingxiu Li, Fule Yang, Jianye Dong, Zhuolin Wang, Chunhong Yuan, Ranran Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents an advanced conveyor belt inspection system that integrates laser scanning technology with deep learning to achieve high-precision micro-damage detection. The system is designed to overcome two major challenges in industrial inspection: ensuring reliable operation under harsh environmental conditions such as dust, vibration, and low illumination, and enabling the early identification of subtle belt defects that are often overlooked by conventional approaches. To this end, an innovative hardware platform was developed, combining laser-based illumination with high-speed imaging to enhance defect visibility. On the algorithmic side, an improved You Only Look Once (YOLO)v7 model was proposed, incorporating four enhancements-funnel rectified linear unit (F-ReLU) activation, spatial pyramid pooling fast cross stage partial convolution (SPPFCSPC) module, efficient intersection over union (EIoU) loss function, and squeeze-and-excitation (SE-Net) attention mechanism. A comprehensive dataset was constructed from both laboratory test benches and field-collected samples in a coal coking plant, ensuring robustness across diverse operating conditions. Experimental results demonstrate that the improved YOLOv7 achieves a mean average precision (mAP@0.5) of 96.6%, significantly surpassing the baseline YOLOv7 (90.7%) and outperforming recent detectors such as DETR and RT-DETR in both accuracy and efficiency. Moreover, long-term reliability tests, including 72-hour continuous operation and low-light industrial deployment, validated the system’s stability and adaptability. These contributions highlight not only the technical novelty of combining laser-enhanced imaging with deep learning, but also the practical value for predictive maintenance, safe production, and sustainable operation. This work offers a robust and scalable inspection framework, advancing the digitalization and intelligent automation of conveyor systems in line with Industry 4.0 principles.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Computer science

Introduction

Conveyor belt systems are vital in industries such as coal mining, power plants, and heavy manufacturing, transporting large volumes of materials over long distances1,2. However, harsh operating conditions often cause damage, especially longitudinal tears, that lead to economic losses, production stoppages, and safety hazards3,4. Traditional manual inspections are labor-intensive and error-prone5,6, prompting the development of automated, non-destructive methods. For example, Yang et al.1 introduced an online machine vision system using high-brightness linear lights and CCD cameras.

Subsequent studies improved detection by integrating various imaging techniques and data fusion methods, such as infrared-visible integration7, multispectral methods8, and laser-based systems9. With advances in AI and deep learning, real-time defect detection systems emerged10,11, including models based on generative adversarial networks12, spatio-temporal video analysis13, and lightweight CNNs14. Enhanced robustness was achieved through multi-sensor fusion and multi-modal sensing. Audio-visual fusion was used for tear detection15, laser-excited thermography for micro-crack detection16, and multi-sensor algorithms for health monitoring17. Moreover, mobile and robotic platforms have extended inspection capabilities to challenging terrains and deep mines6,18–21.

Further innovations include improved YOLO-based real-time damage detection22–25, reviews of fault-detection devices26, machine vision health monitoring27, TOF-based coal flow detection28, and UHF RFID-based crack detection29. Additional techniques involve efficient monitoring using 2D laser scanners4,30, multi-modal imaging for foreign particle detection31,32, and deep learning with GANs33–36. Other notable methods include laser-assisted visual detection of longitudinal tears37,38, 3D point cloud-based damage detection39, attentional deep convolutional networks for mining belt damage40, and inspection robot-based deviation detection41. Additionally, Varna and Abromavi?ius42 developed a system for real-time electronic component detection and classification on a conveyor belt, while Korchagin et al.43 proposed an algorithm for detecting damaged potato tubers. Robotic UGV platforms have also been applied for acoustic fault detection44, and early defect detection combined with fusion-based techniques further enhances system safety and reliability5,45–48.

Overall, significant progress has been made in conveyor belt inspection thanks to advances in computer vision, deep learning, and multi-modal sensing technologies1,2. However, challenges remain in early defect detection, maintaining robustness in harsh industrial environments, and integrating inspection systems with broader automation and control frameworks3,4. In particular, reliable operation in dusty, vibrating conditions and early identification of minor damage are crucial for preventing escalation and enabling intelligent, self-learning inspection systems.

However, several important research gaps remain insufficiently addressed in the current literature. First, most existing systems focus on detecting large-scale or easily visible defects, while the early identification of micro-damage has received limited attention. Second, ensuring robust performance under real-world industrial conditions-such as dust, vibration, and unstable lighting-remains a challenge, with many algorithms showing reduced accuracy when deployed outside controlled environments. Third, the integration of detection systems with industrial automation frameworks is still limited, making it difficult to achieve real-time monitoring, alarms, and predictive maintenance in practice. Finally, while recent studies employing deep learning have improved detection accuracy, the computational complexity of advanced models such as DETR restricts their suitability for real-time deployment in industrial plants. These gaps highlight the need for a conveyor belt inspection system that is not only accurate in micro-damage detection but also robust, lightweight, and seamlessly integrated into industrial control systems.

To address these challenges, this research develops an advanced conveyor belt inspection system that synergistically combines laser scanning technology with deep learning techniques, focusing on micro-damage detection. The system is designed to incorporate an innovative hardware setup that integrates laser scanning with high-speed imaging to enhance defect visibility, along with an optimized algorithm based on YOLOv7 for detecting and classifying micro-damage on belt surfaces. In addition, the system’s detection accuracy, processing speed, and reliability are comprehensively evaluated under various operating conditions, and its potential for real-time monitoring and early warning in industrial settings is explored.

System design overview

The proposed system aims to address the inadequate detection and protection of long-distance conveyor belt surfaces in heavy industrial production and processing facilities such as thermal power plants, coal coking plants, and coal preparation plants. The system uses a programmable logic controller (PLC) as the lower-level controller and an industrial computer as the upper-level controller, combining laser scanning and deep learning algorithms to achieve high-precision, real-time belt surface damage detection.

System architecture

The system’s main functions are real-time surface scanning, intelligent defect identification, real-time alarms and linkage, data recording and analysis, and remote monitoring. High-precision laser emitters and industrial cameras continuously collect conveyor belt surface images. Deep learning algorithms are applied to identify surface defects such as cracks, perforations, and wear. When anomalies are detected, the system immediately triggers alarms and interacts with the main control system. All detection data and abnormal events are automatically recorded to support subsequent trend analysis. Remote access and control are enabled through network interfaces, improving management efficiency.

The hardware architecture has three key parts: detection device, on-site control box, and central control host. The detection device is installed on the conveyor idler frame and comprises industrial cameras, laser emitters, and cleaning modules. The on-site control box, located at the production site, contains a PLC, a Power over Ethernet (PoE) switch, and related components responsible for power supply, on-site control, and data transmission. The central control host is installed in the control room, running the Human-Machine Interface (HMI) software for image analysis and system management (see Fig. 1).

The software architecture adopts a two-layer design: PLC control program and HMI software. The PLC program handles basic operation control, coordination of remote and local operations, and critical tasks such as alarms and emergency shutdowns. The HMI software provides an intuitive user interface, realizing real-time image display, detection result visualization, remote operation control, and historical data query and analysis functions. Hardware design

The detection equipment is designed for efficient and reliable operation in harsh industrial environments. It primarily consists of a visual inspection module and a cleaning module, using innovative structural design and material selection to ensure easy installation, simple maintenance, and high operational efficiency.

The inspection module comprises an industrial camera, an “I”-shaped laser emitter, and related adjustment and fixing devices. The cleaning module includes industrial brushes, small motors, gearboxes, synchronous belts, and gears. Additional components such as the base frame, housing, and various fixing devices were designed by the research team or outsourced for manufacturing. The overall structure is shown in Fig. 2. The device is mounted on the idler frame between the carrying and return belts, with a gap above to allow the industrial camera to capture a wider belt area. It is installed perpendicular to the idler frame in the horizontal plane to maintain a low profile. Given the dusty environment that can degrade imaging quality, the cleaning module is essential. When activated, the motor drives the cleaning brush via gears and a synchronous belt to sweep dust from the glass cover. Three views of the device are provided in Fig. 2.

Inside the detection drum, the laser emitter produces an “I”-shaped auxiliary laser line to enhance defect features, while the industrial camera captures belt surface images. Their control and power cables run through a hollow fixed shaft secured to a custom-made bracket with set screws, which is then attached to the base frame with hex socket screws. A cylindrical tempered glass cover is clamped onto a custom flange plate with a translucent rubber ring; the flange plate features an internal groove that holds both the glass cover and an external transparent rubber ring for fixation and waterproofing.

The right flange plate, connected to the large glass cover housing the industrial camera, has an opening at the camera angle adjustment bracket for rapid alignment. It is fixed to deep groove ball bearings with circlips and secured to the fixed shaft using a spacer and two hex nuts. The fixed shaft is attached to the base frame via a bracket, ensuring that the industrial camera remains stationary when the glass cover rotates.

On the left side, the tempered glass cover is fixed to a middle flange plate via a groove and matching rubber ring and connected to the synchronous pulley through a fixed seat and bearing spacer. The synchronous pulley is mounted on a deep groove ball bearing, which is in turn fixed to the drive shaft. One end of the drive shaft connects to the flange plate of the small glass cover through a fixed seat, with the shaft passing through the synchronous pulley at the small glass cover.

The motor and its gearbox are directly connected to the large brush via couplings. The large brush is mounted to the base frame using a custom bracket with hex socket screws. A groove at the other end of the large brush shaft secures a synchronous pulley with a flat key. When the motor operates, it drives the large brush, which rotates its synchronous pulley and in turn drives both the large and small glass covers. The synchronous pulley on the small glass cover drives the small brush’s synchronous pulley via a synchronous belt, achieving the dust sweeping function.

For transmission, a synchronous belt system was chosen over gear transmission to reduce size, weight, and cost. The open design of the base frame under the glass cover and brushes allows dust to fall away, preventing accumulation and reducing overall weight.

Overall, the design fully considers the harsh industrial environment, achieving reliable operation through an innovative structure and tight integration of the visual inspection and cleaning modules. The use of a synchronous belt transmission mechanism reduces cost and size while improving power transmission efficiency. Environmental adaptability is enhanced by the fluorine-based nano-coating and the automatic cleaning system, effectively handling dust. The compact, adjustable, and modular structure simplifies installation and maintenance, making the detection equipment well suited for long-term, stable operation in challenging industrial settings while maintaining high efficiency and reliability.

Improved YOLOv7 algorithm for micro-damage detection

YOLOv7 architecture overview

The YOLOv7 network architecture, illustrated in Fig. 4, comprises two primary components: the backbone and the head. The backbone, forming the core, integrates a Convolutional-BatchNorm-SiLU (CBS), an extended efficient layer aggregation network (ELAN), and max pooling (MP) modules. The CBS module, fundamental to the network, includes a convolutional (Conv) layer, a batch normalization (BN) layer, and a sigmoid linear unit (SiLU) activation function layer. As shown in Fig. 4, CBS modules vary by convolution kernel size and stride, indicated by color depth: 11 convolution and stride of 1 for channel adjustment, 33 convolution and stride of 1 for feature extraction, and 33 convolution and stride of 2 for downsampling.

Detailed architecture of YOLOv7: backbone and head components with key modules.

The head component, also depicted in Fig. 4, combines CBS, spatial pyramid pooling convolutional spatial pyramid convolution (SPPCSPC), ELAN, MP-2, reparameterized convolution (RepConv), and Conv layers, assembling three detection heads of different sizes. YOLOv7 introduces the ELAN and a cascade model scaling, incorporating re-parameterized very deep generalization (ReVGG) concepts and convolutional re-parameterization. ELAN, with multiple Conv layers, optimizes network learning and convergence by managing gradient paths. The MP module, with a max-pooling layer and Conv layers, features a dual-branch structure for downsampling and feature fusion.

The head employs the path aggregation feature pyramid network (PAFPN) for multi-scale feature fusion, enhancing the pyramid feature set by transmitting semantic features top-down and structural features bottom-up.

The detection process begins with preprocessing the input image to a 6406403 Red-Green-Blue (RGB) format, suitable for the backbone. The backbone outputs three layers connected to concatenation (Concat) and SPPCSPC layers in the head, producing three differently sized feature maps. The reparameterization (Rep) module and Conv layers then handle classification, foreground-background classification, and bounding box regression, resulting in the final detection outputs, as shown in the overall structure in Fig. 4.

SPPFCSPC module

A spatial pyramid pooling (SPP) structure can mitigate image distortion from cropping and scaling, enhancing feature extraction and accelerating candidate box generation. This structure evolved into the spatial pyramid pooling fast (SPPF) module in YOLOv5, which improved detection speed. Subsequent enhancements led to the simplified SPPF (SimSPPF) in YOLOv6, and the simplified cross stage partial SPPF (SimCSPSPPF) module. In YOLOv7, it further evolved into the spatial pyramid pooling cross stage partial convolution (SPPCSPC) module, as illustrated in Fig. 7. This module significantly improves detection accuracy by using three max-pooling layers with kernel sizes , albeit at the cost of increased computational load.

To enhance detection speed while maintaining accuracy for small objects, this study replaced the SPPCSPC module in YOLOv7 with the spatial pyramid pooling fast cross stage partial convolution (SPPFCSPC) module, as shown in Fig. 8. This modification involves reducing hidden layer channels and standardizing the spatial pyramid pooling with for all three max-pooling layers, thereby decreasing parameter count and computational cost. These changes enhance detection speed without compromising the accuracy of small object detection. Whereas Fig. 7 illustrates the SPPCSPC module with sequential max-pooling operations, Fig. 8 shows the SPPFCSPC module with parallel max-pooling operations, preserving the receptive field and boosting speed. This modification improves the computational efficiency of the YOLOv7 network, making it more suitable for real-time applications such as belt conveyor defect detection, where rapid and accurate detection is essential.

SE-Net attention mechanism

Attention mechanisms, valued for their effectiveness and ease of integration, are increasingly used in deep learning. The Squeeze-and-Excitation Network’s (SE-Net) block uses this mechanism to establish interdependencies among feature maps, learning each map’s importance and updating data accordingly. Integrating SE-Net into YOLOv7 enhances the significance of useful features and reduces the importance of irrelevant ones, optimizing resource allocation across channels.

Experimental results

Dataset construction

This study constructed a dataset for detecting tears and scratches on conveyor belts. The dataset comprises two parts: 1) simulated data generated from a custom-built test bench, and 2) data collected from an actual prototype installed in a domestic coal coking plant. The data collection process utilized a 2-megapixel black and white industrial camera (Hikvision MV-CS-10GM) paired with a fixed 6-mm focal length 6-megapixel lens (MVL-HF-6MPE). While the 2-megapixel resolution may limit the ability to capture micro-cracks or extremely fine surface anomalies compared with higher-resolution alternatives, this configuration was adopted to balance detection accuracy with real-time processing efficiency, system cost, and robustness in industrial environments. The use of laser line illumination further enhances defect visibility, partially compensating for the resolution constraint by highlighting subtle height differences and surface irregularities. A 6-mm fixed-focus lens was chosen because it offers high mechanical stability, better sealing against dust, and lower cost compared with industrial varifocal lenses. At the selected installation distance, the 6-mm focal length provides a field of view wide enough to cover the conveyor’s load-bearing region while maintaining sufficient pixel density to detect sub-millimeter cracks. Although it lacks zoom flexibility, this trade-off ensures robust long-term performance in harsh industrial environments, making the 6-mm fixed lens more suitable for the target application than a more complex and fragile varifocal design. The monochrome configuration was also chosen because grayscale imaging preserves fine edge and contour features essential for detecting structural defects (such as tears, scratches, and foreign objects) while significantly reducing GPU resource consumption and inference latency compared with RGB imaging. These combined advantages make the present setup suitable for real-time deployment in industrial environments, though future work will consider integrating higher-resolution RGB or multispectral cameras and adjustable focal length optics to further improve adaptability and sensitivity to ultra-fine and color-dependent defects.

To enhance the model’s generalization ability and robustness, data augmentation techniques were applied in image processing, including Mosaic and Mix-up. Data augmentation was performed through image scaling, rotation, cropping, flipping, and color enhancement. The dataset used for training the model included data simulated from the custom-built test bench and data collected from actual industrial sites.

In the object recognition process, three-dimensional grayscale images were used to more intuitively display the distribution of grayscale values. For computers with lower computational performance, using grayscale images for inference significantly reduces graphics processing unit (GPU) computational resource consumption and inference time compared to RGB color images. Eventually, a black and white industrial camera for grayscale images was chosen because grayscale images retain the edge features of objects, which is sufficient for the object recognition in this project.

A total of 8,936 photos were collected from the custom-built test bench and industrial sites, randomly divided into training and validation sets at a ratio of 7:3. The software LabelImg was used to annotate the dataset, categorizing the abnormal conditions of the belt surface into three types: foreign objects, tears, and shallow scratches. Training settings

To ensure fairness and reproducibility, all experiments in this study—including the ablation studies, the comparison between the baseline and improved YOLOv7 models, and the benchmarking against other state-of-the-art detectors—were conducted under identical training and evaluation conditions.The dataset was split into training and validation sets at a ratio of 8:2, and all images were resized to 640 640. Each model was trained for 300 epochs with a batch size of 32, using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) as the optimizer with a momentum of 0.937 and a weight decay of 0.0005. The initial learning rate was set to 0.01 and scheduled with cosine decay, preceded by a three-epoch linear warm-up. To enhance generalization, a consistent data augmentation pipeline was applied across all models, including Mosaic (probability 1.0), MixUp (probability 0.2), random scaling (0.5–1.5), random cropping, horizontal flipping (probability 0.5), and color jittering (hue±0.015, saturation±0.7, exposure±0.4). During training, exponential moving average (EMA) updates of model parameters were enabled, while inference used non-maximum suppression (NMS) with an IoU threshold of 0.65 and a confidence threshold of 0.25. Under these strictly controlled settings, the only differences among models arose from the architectural components being tested, ensuring that the reported performance variations are solely attributable to the introduced modules or different detector backbones.This design also rules out the possibility that longer training schedules or stronger augmentation alone account for the observed improvements, ensuring fairness across models.

The improved YOLOv7 model stands out as a highly effective solution for tasks requiring high accuracy and performance, particularly in complex detection scenarios where the benefits of improved detection capabilities outweigh the marginal increase in computational requirements.

Discussion

This research successfully developed and validated an advanced conveyor belt inspection system that effectively addresses the challenges of micro-damage detection in harsh industrial environments. The integration of laser scanning technology with an improved YOLOv7 deep learning algorithm yielded significant advancements in detection accuracy and system reliability. The hardware design, featuring a custom-built detection device with industrial cameras, laser emitters, and an innovative cleaning module, demonstrates robust performance in dusty and vibrating conditions. The modular structure and protective housing ensure ease of maintenance and longevity in industrial settings. The present system employs a monochrome imaging device, which is effective for detecting edge-related defects but cannot capture color-dependent anomalies such as rust stains, lubricant leaks, or thermal discoloration. Future work will incorporate RGB or multispectral cameras to broaden the range of detectable defects and enable identification of chemical or thermal wear phenomena.

The improved YOLOv7, enhanced with the F-ReLU activation function, SPPFCSPC module, EIoU loss function, and SE-Net attention mechanism, raised detection performance substantially, achieving a mAP@0.5 of 96.6% compared to 90.7% for the baseline. Extensive experiments, including ablation and cross-model comparisons, confirmed its advantages. The system’s ability to detect defects such as foreign objects, tears, and scratches with high precision and recall, along with its stable performance in a 72-hour continuous test, demonstrates its suitability for long-term industrial deployment.

Some limitations remain. The dataset, though combining laboratory and field samples, is still limited in scale, especially for rare micro-cracks, which may restrict generalization. The improved YOLOv7 requires significant computational resources, limiting edge deployment. Hardware validation has so far focused on coal coking plants and moderate low-light settings, leaving performance in more extreme environments untested. More critically, the current system relies on monochrome imaging, which excels at capturing structural edges but cannot detect color-dependent anomalies such as rust stains, lubricant leakage, or thermal discoloration-limitations that RGB or multispectral cameras could address.

Looking forward, future research will aim to overcome these challenges by expanding datasets with larger-scale, multi-environment samples and employing augmentation and GAN-based synthesis to improve robustness, while integrating multi-modal sensing-such as infrared imaging, thermography, and acoustic signals-to broaden detectable defect types. In particular, introducing RGB and multispectral cameras will make it possible to capture color-dependent and chemical-related anomalies, such as corrosion, staining, or thermal wear, which monochrome imaging cannot reliably identify. At the same time, the development of lightweight architectures through pruning and knowledge distillation will facilitate real-time deployment on edge devices. Embedding the detection framework into predictive maintenance systems could further create a closed-loop process that combines fault detection, alarm generation, and repair recommendation. Beyond coal industry applications, the methodology also holds potential for extension to other sectors such as steelmaking, port logistics, and manufacturing, where reliable conveyor operation is equally critical to safety and efficiency.
