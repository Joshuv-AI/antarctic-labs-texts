Induced opening of conformational switch I in GTP‐bound Ran GTPase
Authors: Janka Czigleczki, Balint Dudas, Erika Balog
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRan is a small GTPase that regulates nucleocytoplasmic transport by cycling between an inactive GDP‐bound and active GTP‐bound state. Structurally, Ran consists of a globular G‐domain and a C‐terminal region that includes a C‐terminal helix. The switch I and II regions of the G‐domain undergo major conformational changes upon GTP hydrolysis, with switch I shifting from a closed to an open conformation, regulating cargo loading/unloading. In this study, we describe an induced conformational change in RanGTP that drives switch I from its closed, active state (state 2) to a fully open, inactive state, resembling the RanGDP conformation. This transition is consistently triggered by disruption of two key interactions: Thr42‐Mg2+ coordination (leading to state 1) and the Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad, the latter due to Lys152 reorientation. These findings identify Lys152, alongside Thr42, as critical for stabilizing the active Ran conformation, and show that disruption of their interactions promotes full opening of switch I. This structural mechanism highlights a previously unrecognized mode of RanGTP inactivation and may provide a conceptual basis for considering therapeutic approaches targeting dysregulated Ran and potentially other small GTPases, particularly in cancers driven by mutations that lock switch I in its active conformation.

INTRODUCTION

Ran is a small GTPase belonging to the Ras superfamily (van Dam et al., 2011). It is the main regulator of nucleo‐cytoplasmic import/export (Gorlich, 1998; Gorlich & Kutay, 1999; Melchior & Gerace, 1998; Paci et al., 2021). As with other small GTPases, it is a molecular switch, cycling between GDP‐bound inactive and GTP‐bound active states (Hilgenfeld, 1995). The intrinsically slow GDP/GTP exchange is accelerated by guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs), while GTP hydrolysis is catalyzed by GTPase activating proteins (GAPs) (Bernards & Settleman, 2004; Cherfils & Zeghouf, 2013; Scheffzek & Ahmadian, 2005).

The function of Ran relies on the localization of its regulators: RanGEF, the chromosome condensation 1 protein (RCC1), is restricted to the nucleus (Ohtsubo et al., 1989; Seki et al., 1996), while RanGAP is confined to the cytoplasm (Bischoff et al., 1995). This spatial separation establishes a RanGTP gradient across the nuclear pore complex (NPC), resulting in the GDP‐bound form located in the cytosol and the GTP‐bound form in the nucleus (Gorlich & Kutay, 1999). Nuclear transport factor 2 (NTF2) facilitates the import of RanGDP into the nucleus, where the complex dissociates (Gorlich et al., 1996). Inside the nucleus, RCC1 promotes the exchange of GDP to GTP on Ran. RanGTP then drives the export of exportin‐bound cargo molecules and nuclear transport receptors (NTRs) back to the cytoplasm (Askjaer et al., 1998). In the cytoplasm, RanGTP is hydrolyzed by RanGAP in the presence of Ran‐binding proteins (RanBP1/RanBP2) (Koyama & Matsuura, 2010), converting it back to RanGDP (Bischoff & Gorlich, 1997; Seewald et al., 2002).

The structure of Ran is composed of a G‐domain (GTP binding domain) characteristic of small GTPases; however, in contrast to other GTPases, its C‐terminal segment contains a distinct C‐terminal helix. In the GDP‐bound state, the C‐terminus is associated with the G‐domain (Scheffzek et al., 1995); while it is detached from it in the GTP‐bound state (Monecke et al., 2009) (Figure 1a). In fact, the full‐length structure of RanGTP in the absence of binding partners has not been determined experimentally as it has only been crystalized in macromolecular complexes. In crystal structures of RanGTP analogs in complex with Ran Binding Proteins (RanBP), the C‐terminal region of Ran is resolved (Rudack et al., 2015; Seewald et al., 2002; Sun et al., 2013; Vetter et al., 1999a; Vetter et al., 1999b), embracing RanBP. In macromolecular complexes lacking RanBP, only the structure of the G‐domain of RanGTP is determined (Forwood et al., 2008; Guttler et al., 2010; Monecke et al., 2009; Renault et al., 2001; Vetter et al., 1999a), indicating a flexible C‐terminus (Czigleczki et al., 2023).

The functionally important elements of the G‐domain are the phosphate‐binding loop (P‐loop) which, together with a Mg2+ ion, stabilizes the nucleotide binding, and the structural elements switch I and switch II, which undergo major conformational changes upon the GDP/GTP exchange. This conformational switching enables Ran to regulate the cargo loading/unloading of nuclear transport receptors (Gorlich et al., 1996).

In this study we report an induced conformational change of RanGTP switch I from the closed active form (referred to as state 2 (Araki et al., 2011; Geyer et al., 1996; Rosch et al., 1986)) to conformations resembling the fully open inactive structure of RanGDP. We show that to induce the fully open switch I conformation the disruption of both the Mg2+ coordination, stabilized by Thr42 (yielding to a partially open conformation, known as state 1 (Araki et al., 2011; Geyer et al., 1996; Rosch et al., 1986)), and the disruption of Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad is required. In our simulations the latter occurred as a result of the reorientation of Lys152, indicating its crucial role in the full conformational transition of switch I.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Functional significance of the Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad

During our exploration of RanGTP conformational dynamics, one of the three parallel MD simulations (hereafter referred to as md1) exhibited a large conformational change of switch I, transitioning from closed to an open conformation closely resembling that observed in RanGDP (Figure 1a,b; Video S1). This substantial conformational change is also evident in the RMSF analysis shown in Figure S1a.

To structurally characterize this opening motion, we monitored the distances between switch I residues and GTP throughout the simulation. As shown in Figure 2a, during the first 114 ns only residues Phe35 and Glu36 maintain continuous contact with GTP; these interactions diminish at approximately 114 ns, coinciding with the onset of switch I opening. Figure 2c depicts the time evolution of the distance between the center of mass (COM) of the Phe35 aromatic ring and the COM of the guanine moiety of GTP, as well as the distances between the hydrogen‐bonding partners Glu36:O and GTP:O2′/O3′. These distances increase sharply as switch I detaches from the G‐domain, marking the transition from the closed to the open conformation, as illustrated in the inset of Figure 2c.

Distances between the aromatic ring of Phe35COM—the guanine part of GTPCOM (red), and between Glu36:O—GTP:O2′/O3′ (turquoise) during md1 (c) and md2 simulation (d). The insets show enlarged views of the Ran‐GTP in md1 and md2 simulations, displaying Phe35, Glu36 and GTP as sticks.

The results of the md2 simulation, in which switch I remains in the closed conformation (Video S2), are shown in Figure 2b,d. These Figures show that the distances between Phe35/Glu36 and GTP remain stable throughout the simulation time. The respective distances for md3 simulation, where switch I also remains closed, are presented in Figure S1b, d.

To further investigate and attempt to reproduce the closed‐to‐open conformational transition of switch I, we performed a series of independent MD simulations (Table S1), each initiated from the structure of the md1 trajectory at 103 ns, a few nanoseconds before switch I opening occurred (Figure S2a), with the C‐terminus still in the proximity of the G‐domain and differing only in their random initial velocity distributions. Among these seven simulations, the opening of switch I was observed in only one trajectory (denoted as md1′, Video S3).

Figure 3a illustrates the time dependence of key inter‐residue distances during the md1′ simulation. An increase in the distance between Phe35ringCOM—GTPguanineCOM is first observed at ~43 ns (red star), preceding the dissociation of Glu36 from GTP at ~191 ns (turquoise star). Notably, despite these changes, switch I remains in a closed conformation at this stage. As shown in the structural snapshot of the Figure Lys152 maintains close spatial proximity to Phe35. In this closed RanGTP conformation, the guanine base of GTP engages in an edge‐to‐face π–π interaction with the aromatic ring of Phe35. This interaction is further stabilized by hydrophobic contacts between Phe35 and the aliphatic portion of the Lys152 side chain, forming the Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad. This hydrophobic interaction of the triad anchors switch I in the closed state, with additional stabilization provided by hydrogen bonds between Glu36 and GTP.

A subsequent sharp increase in the distance between the COM of Phe35's aromatic ring and the hydrophobic carbon atoms of the Lys152 side chain, indicates the disruption of the triad resulting in switch I dissociation from GTP (orange star). Altogether, the distance analyses presented in Figure 3a indicate that the closed‐to‐open transition of switch I is enabled by the disruption of both the Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad and the Glu36–GTP hydrogen bonds.

Structural basis of the triad disruption

To identify the cause of the triad disruption, Figure 3c shows that the approach of Glu186 toward Lys152 (blue star and structural inset) results in the formation of a salt bridge. This new interaction displaces the hydrophobic side chain of Lys152 from the triad, thereby destabilizing and ultimately disrupting it, which in turn triggers the opening of switch I.

Given that the position of the C‐terminus in the RanGTP md1 simulation closely resembled that observed in the RanGDP crystal structure (Rudack et al., 2015) (Figure 1a,b), we performed three parallel simulations of RanGDP for comparison. As shown in Figure 3b, switch I remains in an open conformation throughout all three simulations, displaying a degree of opening comparable to that observed in md1' RanGTP (Figure 3a). Furthermore, Figure 3d demonstrates that Lys152 and Glu186 maintain persistent contact throughout the entire duration of all RanGDP simulations, indicating their role in maintaining the open conformation of switch I.

Figure 4 provides a structural summary of the conformational transition of RanGTP from the closed to the open state. In the closed conformation (Figure 4a), switch I is stabilized by the Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad and hydrogen bonds between Glu36 and GTP. In the intermediate state (Figure 4b), formation of the Glu186‐Lys152 salt bridge displaces the Lys152 side chain, leading to the reorientation of the Phe35 aromatic ring away from GTP accompanied by the disruption of the Glu36‐GTP hydrogen bonds. These changes progressively destabilize switch I, leading ultimately to the open state (Figure 4c), in which switch I is fully detached from the G‐domain. (In Figure S3 the general location of the triad is represented relative to the whole G domain.)

The corresponding distance analyses for md′ simulations, in which switch I remains in closed conformation, are presented in Figure S4. Among these, in several cases (md2′, md3′, md4′, md5′, md6′) we observe a transient disruption of the Glu36–GTP hydrogen bonds, while the Phe35–Lys152–GTP triad remains stable. The preserved triad stabilizes the closed state of switch I permitting increased flexibility in the region adjacent to switch II (Figure S1c).

Having identified the Lys152‐Glu186 interaction as a key factor in disrupting the Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad, starting from a closed switch I conformation of the md1 simulation (at 20 ns, Figure S2b), we next applied a distance constraint to bring Lys152 and Glu186 into close proximity (3.5 Å). From this configuration, three unconstrained simulations with different velocity distributions were performed (md_constr152_). Switch I shows a closed‐to‐open conformational transition in one out of three of these simulations. Figure 5 demonstrates that when the Lys152‐Glu186 interaction is present, the Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad is disrupted, leading to open switch I conformation (Figure 5a). In contrast to this, when Glu186 departs from Lys152 after equilibration, that is, in the absence of the Lys152‐Glu186 interaction, switch I remains in the closed conformational state (Figure 5b).

A recurring behavior is observed in both simulations where switch I remained in the closed state (denoted by orange stars on Figure 5b): the aromatic ring of Phe35 undergoes transient displacements from GTP. As illustrated in the inset of Figure 5b, these events correspond to aromatic ring flipping of Phe35 (Akke & Weininger, 2023; Dreydoppel et al., 2020; Gauto et al., 2019). Despite these transient flips, the hydrogen bonds between Glu36 and GTP continue to stabilize switch I in the closed conformation. Following each ring backflip, since Lys152–GTP remained in contact, the Phe35–Lys152–GTP triad is reestablished, maintaining switch I in its closed conformation.

In rare instances, as it was observed in the original md1 simulation (Figure 2a), if the flipping of the Phe35 ring occurs in a switch I conformation where the Glu36–GTP hydrogen bonds are disrupted, the Phe35 flip breaks the Phe35–Lys152–GTP triad without any perturbation of Lys152, leading to switch I to open.

Rearrangement of the Mg2+ coordination

The elevated RMSF values of the switch I region adjacent to switch II in our RanGTP simulations (Figure S1a), even when the triad stayed intact, such switch I remained in a closed conformation compared to the RanGDP crystal structure, prompted us to examine the Mg2+ coordination (Kjeldgaard et al., 1996). As shown in Figure 6a (open switch I) and Figure 6b (closed switch I), in 12 out of 14 RanGTP simulations reported here, the Mg2+—Thr42:OG1 distances exceed 4 Å, indicating a displacement of Thr42 from the Mg2+ coordination sphere.

The loss of Thr42–Mg2+ coordination. (a) Average distance between Thr42:OG1—Mg2+ during the simulations with switch I is in the open or (b) closed conformation. (c) The octahedral Mg2+ coordination in the crystal structure of RanGDP‐BeF‐RanBD1 (PDB code 5CLL) with GDP‐BeF replaced by GTP; (d) the loss of the octahedral Mg2+ coordination in the energy minimized structure after gradually decreasing the harmonic restraints on the heavy atoms during energy minimization.

Figure 6c provides an enlarged view of the Mg2+ coordination in the RanGTP crystal structure (PDB 5CLL) used in this study. The structure exhibits the typical octahedral Mg2+ coordination with the Mg2+ interacting with the hydroxyl groups of Thr42 and Thr24, nonbridging oxygens of the γ‐ and β‐phosphates of GTP, and two water molecules (Rudack et al., 2015).

After the energy minimization protocol, we observed that the negatively charged Asp65 enters the Mg2+ coordination sphere, displacing both the γ‐phosphate and one of the two coordinating water molecules (Figure 6d). This rearrangement is accompanied by the formation of a hydrogen bond between the γ‐phosphate and Thr42, while the displaced water molecule shifts toward the α‐phosphate and establishes a hydrogen bond with it. Early in the subsequent dynamics, the γ‐phosphate re‐establishes its coordination with Mg2+; however, the displaced water molecule does not return, and Asp65 permanently remains in the coordination sphere. As a result, Mg2+ becomes transiently hepta‐coordinated, deviating from its preferred symmetric octahedral geometry, which likely contributes to local structural instability (Wolf & Cittadini, 2003). This distortion is eventually relieved as Thr42 exits the coordination sphere, leading to the partial opening of switch I.

In the two simulations where the Mg2+–Thr42 distance remained below 4 Å (md3, md_constr152_3), Mg2+ maintained coordination with Thr42 (Figure S5), thereby stabilizing the switch I region in proximity to switch II in a closed conformation. The ion regained its octahedral coordination geometry through interaction with Asp65:OD1, replacing a water molecule that had been displaced from the coordination sphere.

Taken together, the loss of the Thr42–Mg2+ interaction, observed in both fully open (Figure 6a) and partially closed (Figure 6b) switch I conformations, indicates that disruption of the Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad is a key step for switch I to achieve a fully open state similar to that of RanGDP.

Inducing the fully open conformation of switch I

To test whether disrupting the Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad alone can drive a full switch I opening, we performed MD simulations from an energy‐minimized structure where heavy‐atom restraints preserved the Mg2+'s octahedral coordination (see Methods). Then, to enhance triad disruption during the dynamics, an upper‐wall harmonic distance restraint (≤4 Å) was applied between Lys152 and Glu186 to promote their interaction (md_mgoct_152_).

As shown by the Phe35–GTP distances in Figure 7a, the Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad was disrupted in all five simulations, resulting in the displacement of Phe35 away from GTP. However, the data in Figure 7c demonstrate that Mg2+ coordination remained stable throughout the entire 1 μs in each simulation. Consequently, switch I adopted only a partially open conformation on the side of the disrupted triad, as illustrated in the inset of Figure 7c (see also Video S4). These results indicate that disruption of the triad alone is not sufficient to drive switch I into the fully open conformation as observed in the RanGDP crystal structure.

(b and d) Show the same distances after gradually removing Thr42 from Mg2+ to 10 Å. To induce the fully open switch I conformation, starting from the final structure of previous simulations, we gradually increased the distance between Thr42 and Mg2+ to 10 Å—mimicking the effect of a GAP (Seewald et al., 2002)—and then performed parallel, unconstrained MD simulations (md_152_mg42_). As shown in Figure 7b,d, both the Phe35–GTP and Thr42–Mg2+ distances increase, indicating the transition to a fully open switch I conformation, as illustrated in the inset of Figure 7d (Video S5).

The critical role of Phe35, Lys152, and Thr42 is also supported by mutational experiments, which reveal that changes at these sites compromise binding to several effectors, RanBP1 or RanGAP1 (Ly et al., 2010; Milano et al., 2012; Murphy et al., 1997).

Switch I rearrangements in Ran relative to Ras

Our simulations demonstrate that disruption of Mg2+ coordination alone results in a partially open switch I conformation, predominantly affecting the region near the GTP triphosphate chain, as described above. This observation agrees with structural features reported for Ras–GTP complexes (Araki et al., 2011; Milburn et al., 1990; Pai et al., 1990; Rosch et al., 1986; Spoerner et al., 2001). In these structures, the Mg2+‐coordinating Thr35—analogous to Thr42 in Ran—was mutated, thereby disrupting the Mg2+ interaction with switch I. As shown in Figure 8, although part of switch I shifts outward, the Phe28–GTP–Lys147 triad of Ras remains intact, producing a partially open conformation. The detected conformation is referred to as “state 1” (the open, effector‐nonbinding form), in contrast to “state 2,” the closed, effector‐binding conformation. A broader relevance of state 1 has also been noted in the Ras family (Parker 2018), showing that K‐Ras populates state 1 to a greater extent than H‐Ras, and this intrinsic tendency has motivated drug‐discovery efforts aimed at stabilizing the state 1 conformation (Keeton et al., 2017).

State 1 conformation of switch I. Aligned structure of H‐RasT35S GppNHp (2LCF) and RanGTP md2. The Phe28–GTP–Lys147 triad and the mutated Ser35 of H‐Ras as well as Thr42 of Ran are displayed as sticks. Switch I of H‐RasT35SGppNHp is presented cyan, of RanGTP blue.

Within our simulations summarized in Figure 6b, we observe both conformational states. In 8 of the 10 trajectories, switch I adopts a state 1 conformation consistent with the partially open conformation described above, whereas in two trajectories switch I remains in a state 2, closed conformation. Figure 8 provides an example by overlapping structures from the RanGTP md2 simulation to the solution NMR structure of H‐RasGppNHp, demonstrating the correspondence between our simulated state 1 conformation and the experimentally determined Ras state 1.

Furthermore, our results indicate that a fully open switch I conformation—as seen in the RanGDP crystal structure—can only be achieved when both Mg2+ coordination and the Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad of Ran are disrupted. The loss of Mg2+ coordination can be triggered by GAP activity; in our simulations, the disruption of the triad is driven by the reorientation of Lys152 toward Glu186.

Extending the Ran–Ras comparison, the situation differs in Ras. The residue corresponding to Glu186 in Ran is absent in Ras, meaning that the Ras triad is not destabilized from the Lys147 side (Lys147 being the equivalent of Lys152 in Ran). Nevertheless, experimental evidence demonstrates that H‐Ras can also adopt a fully open switch I conformation in the absence of the nucleotide (1BKD (Boriack‐Sjodin et al., 1998), 1NVV (Margarit et al., 2003), and 8BE7 (Van Holsbeeck et al., 2023)). It should be noted, however, that in all of these cases H‐Ras is in complex with SOS, which may contribute to the opening of switch I. In these cases, the triad is disrupted due to the absence of the nucleotide.

Since spontaneous opening of the switch I conformation is rare, it remains to be clarified how Ran binding partners are able to promote this transition. From one side, loss of the Thr42–Mg2+ interaction is well established as a GAP‐induced effect (Seewald et al., 2002; Rudack et al., 2015). From the Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad side, X‐ray structures of Ran in complex with RanBP1 (1K5D (Seewald et al., 2002)) and the Ran‐binding domains of RanBP2 (5CLL (Rudack et al., 2015); 7MNW (Bley et al., 2022); 9B62 (Baytshtok et al., 2025)) show that Phe35 might be contacted by these effectors, indicating that partner binding can modulate the stability of the triad. In parallel, Lys152 has been identified as an in vivo SUMO acceptor (Sakin et al., 2015; Tammsalu et al., 2014), and the SUMO‐E3 ligase region of RanBP2 can engage Ran in the vicinity of this residue. These interactions could provide reasonable routes to disrupt the triad and suggest that partner binding or post‐translational modification could create the conditions needed to induce the switch I opening mechanism described in this study.

CONCLUSION

In this study, we revealed the mechanism underlying the opening of switch I in RanGTP using molecular dynamics simulations. Our results reveal that full transition to the open switch I conformation requires two perturbations: (i) disruption of the Phe35–GTP–Lys152 triad, initiated by the reorientation of Lys152 through interaction with Glu186, and (ii) the displacement of Thr42 from the Mg2+ coordination sphere. These changes, in turn, also destabilize the hydrogen bonds between Glu36 and GTP, enabling switch I to transition into an open conformation that resembles the inactive state of RanGDP.

Together, these findings define a new mechanistic basis for the switch I “on”‐to‐“off” transition and point to potential therapeutic strategies for modulating small GTPases in cases where pathogenic mutations stabilize the active state.

METHODS

For human RanGTP, the crystal structure with PDB entry 5CLL (Rudack et al., 2015) was used as a starting point. Since this structure does not contain the C‐terminal helix, the missing C‐terminal part (resid 185‐207) was completed from 5CLQ (which is a Y39A mutant) by overlapping the backbone of the C‐terminal segment from residues 176 to 184. The GTP analog GDP‐BeF was replaced by GTP. The starting coordinates of the human RanGDP were taken from PDB entry 5CIQ (Rudack et al., 2015). The protonation state of the titratable residues, at pH 7.0 was set by PROPKA (Sondergaard et al., 2011), yielding residues in their standard protonation forms.

For RanGTP, a rectangular water box containing TIP3 water molecules with 39 Å in all directions from the surface of the protein was created, since large conformational changes can be expected due to the presence of the C‐terminal helix. For RanGDP, a rectangular box was built extending 14 Å in all directions from the surface of the molecule. The NaCl concentration was set to 0.15 M in both cases. The structures were solvated using CHARMM‐GUI (Jo et al., 2008; Jo et al., 2014). The molecular dynamic simulations were performed using NAMD3.0 (Phillips et al., 2020) with CHARMM force field C36m (Best et al., 2012).

For energy calculations, the dielectric constant was set to 1. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method was used to calculate the electrostatic interactions with a grid spacing of 1 Å or less having the order of 6; the real space summation was truncated at 12.0 Å, and the width of the Gaussian distribution was set to 0.34 Å−1. Van der Waals (vdW) interactions were reduced to zero by “switch” truncation operating between 10.0 and 12.0 Å.

The solvated systems were energy minimized with gradually decreasing harmonic restraints applied to Cartesian coordinates of the heavy atoms: first, steepest descent was used with the harmonic force constant of these restraints decreased every 500 steps having successively 10, 1, and 0.1 kcal/mol/Å2, followed by 200 conjugate gradient steps with a force constant of 0.1 kcal/mol/Å2. Unrestrained minimization was then applied for 100 steps with steepest descent, 200 steps with conjugate gradient, and 1000 steps with the adopted basis Newton–Raphson method.

Three parallel simulations were carried out starting from the energy minimized system, followed by an NVE equilibration at 300 K for 200 ps, and a 5 ns NPT run at a pressure of 1 atm. Langevin dynamics was used with a damping coefficient of 1 ps−1, a piston oscillation period of 50 fs, and a piston oscillation decay time of 25 fs. The integration time step was set to 2 fs. For each run, before the NVE equilibration step, random velocities were assigned according to the Maxwell‐Boltzmann distribution at 300 K. For clarity, simulations are summarized in Table S1.

Starting from the equilibrated systems, 1 μs long production runs were carried out with the frames saved at every 20 ps. The parameters for the production runs were identical to those used in the previously described 5 ns NPT equilibration.

To reproduce the opening of switch I, we used two approaches: (i) from the structure at 103 ns of the md1 simulation, prior to the opening of switch I (Figure S2a) we initiated seven parallel simulations with different velocity distributions; and (ii) we applied harmonic distance constraint between Lys152:NZ and Glu186:OE2 in the structure after 20 ns of the md1 simulation, bringing these atoms to 3.4 Å, as observed in the RanGDP structure (PDB: 5CIQ; Figure S2b). All production runs were 1 μs long.

In order to clarify the effect of Mg2+ coordination on the opening of switch I, the 5CLL (Rudack et al., 2015) crystal structure of RanGTP, completed with the conformation of the C‐terminus of the md1 20th ns simulation (see Figure S2c), was solvated using CHARMM‐GUI in a rectangular box extending 14 Å in all directions from the surface of the molecule with a 0.15 M NaCl concentration. The structure was energy minimized with NAMD, which utilizes the conjugate gradient combined with a line search algorithm. Throughout the 10,000 steps of minimization, harmonic restraints to the Cartesian coordinates of all heavy atoms were applied, with force constants of 1.0 kcal/mol/Å2 for backbone and 0.5 kcal/mol/Å2 for side chain atoms. Following energy minimization, a 20 ns molecular dynamics simulation was performed with a harmonic distance restraint applied between the atoms of Lys152:NZ—Glu186:OE2. The equilibrium distance of the restraint was gradually decreased, bringing the atoms into close proximity (3.4 Å), with a force constant of 20 kcal/mol/Å2.

Starting from this structure, five parallel 1 μs long MD simulations were carried out maintaining an upper wall restraint between the amino group of Lys152 and OE1/OE2 of Glu186 with a force constant of 20 kcal/mol/Å2 for distances higher than at 4 Å. On the final structure of each 1 μs production run, besides the upper wall restraint described above, an additional distance restrain was applied between Thr42:OG2 and Mg2+, distancing Thr42 up to 10 Å from the Mg2+, thus removing Thr42 from the Mg2+ coordination sphere. Then a 5 × 1 μs unconstrained simulation was performed.

Three parallel 1 μs long production runs were executed for RanGDP. The parameters were identical to those described above.
