Finite element analysis of implant selection and screw positioning in proximal femoral basicervical fractures
Authors: Tianhao Yang, Faez Noraddin, Baozhe Liu, Zegen Zhang, Hai-Lun Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To determine the optimal implant for proximal femoral basicervical fractures by comparing the biomechanical properties of the Dynamic Hip Screw (DHS), Proximal Femoral Nail Anti-Rotation (PFNA), and InterTAN using finite element analysis (FEA). Five fixation configurations were simulated: DHS (central screw AP view), PFNA with central helical blade (PFNA-C), PFNA with inferior helical blade (PFNA-I), InterTAN with central hip screw (InterTAN-C), and InterTAN with inferior hip screw (InterTAN-I). Following construction and validation of an intact femur model, three progressively unstable basicervical fracture patterns (simple, intertrochanteric defect, lateral wall defect) were created and incorporated with the implants. FEA simulated a 700 N load (70 kg patient single-leg stance). Displacement and implant stress distribution were analyzed. Primary comparisons assessed DHS, PFNA-C, and InterTAN-C performance; secondary comparisons evaluated screw position (C vs. I) in cephalomedullary nails (CMN). For simple fractures, DHS, PFNA, and InterTAN exhibited minimal differences in displacement and implant stress. In intertrochanteric and lateral wall defect fractures, significant differences emerged. Displacement was significantly greater with DHS, followed by PFNA, then InterTAN (minimal PFNA-InterTAN difference). Maximum stress was highest with InterTAN, higher with PFNA than DHS, but remained below medical titanium fatigue limits. The inferior screw position demonstrated biomechanical advantages over the central position in CMNs. DHS, PFNA, and InterTAN are suitable for simple basicervical fractures. For fractures with intertrochanteric or lateral wall defects, CMNs (PFNA, InterTAN) provide superior stability over DHS, with InterTAN offering marginally better stability than PFNA. The inferior hip screw position is biomechanically preferable in CMNs.

Introduction

Proximal femoral fractures, accounting for approximately 5–10% of all fragility fractures in older adults, represent a major public health burden due to their high morbidity and socioeconomic costs1. Among these, proximal femoral basicervical fractures—a distinct subtype often understudied compared to intracapsular (subcapital/transcervical) or extracapsular (intertrochanteric) fractures—merit particular attention. The proximal femoral basicervical fracture is defined as a fracture line located at the junction of the femoral neck and the greater/lesser trochanters, typically classified as an extracapsular fracture based on its anatomical position: the hip joint capsule attaches to the base of the femoral neck (approximately 1–2 cm distal to the femoral head), rendering fractures at this level outside the capsular envelope2,3. Unlike intracapsular fractures (which risk avascular necrosis of the femoral head due to disruption of the retinacular vessels), basicervical fractures primarily disrupt the lateral cortical bone and lateral trochanteric structures, predisposing to shear forces, proximal fragment medial migration, and high rates of displacement/varus collapse2,4.

Clinical management of basicervical fractures remains challenging due to their heterogeneous presentation, ranging from “simple” fractures (intact lateral wall and intertrochanteric region) to complex variants with intertrochanteric bone loss or lateral wall defects5. Current treatment options include extramedullary fixation (e.g., dynamic hip screw [DHS]) and cephalomedullary nailing (CMN: proximal femoral nail anti-rotation [PFNA], InterTAN). DHS relies on screw-bone interface stability, while CMNs leverage intramedullary fixation to distribute load. However, clinical outcomes vary significantly due to fracture severity, bone quality, and implant design, with no consensus on optimal fixation strategies6.

Finite element analysis (FEA), by simulating load transfer and stress distribution under physiological conditions, provides a standardized framework to compare implant biomechanics objectively. Previous FEA studies have evaluated individual implants in specific fracture types7,8, but few have systematically compared DHS, PFNA, and InterTAN across varying basicervical fracture severities (simple, intertrochanteric defect, lateral wall defect) or assessed the impact of screw position (central vs. inferior) in CMNs.

This study aimed to: (1) evaluate displacement and von Mises stress (VMS) in DHS, PFNA, and InterTAN across simple, intertrochanteric defect, and lateral wall defect basicervical fractures; (2) compare the biomechanical performance of central vs. inferior screw positioning in CMNs; and (3) provide evidence-based recommendations for implant selection based on fracture stability. By addressing these gaps, we seek to inform clinical decision-making and improve outcomes for patients with proximal femoral basicervical fractures.

Materials & methods

Ethical approval

Informed consent was obtained from the subject. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital Affiliated to China Medical University (2024PS850K) and conducted per the Declaration of Helsinki.

Fracture model design

Femoral CT scans from a healthy 60-year-old male volunteer (70 kg, 172 cm) were imported into Mimics 17.0 (Materialise, Belgium) for 3D reconstruction. Surface processing utilized Geomagic 2016 (Geomagic, USA). Cortical and cancellous bone were differentiated using offset functions. Reflecting clinical heterogeneity3,5–7, three fracture models of increasing instability were designed in SolidWorks (Dassault Systèmes, France):1.Simple fracture (Fig. 1A),2.Fracture with intertrochanteric defect (Fig. 1B),3.Fracture with lateral wall defect (Fig. 1C).

Implant modeling & assembly

DHS, PFNA, and InterTAN(Double Medical, China) geometries were modeled in SolidWorks (Dassault Systèmes, France). For PFNA and InterTAN, central (sagittal and frontal planes) and inferior (central sagittal, inferior frontal) screw positions were simulated. The fifteen models (5 implants × 3 fractures) were imported into Abaqus 6.13 (Dassault Systèmes, France) for finite element meshing and analysis.Additionally, implants used 2 mm tetrahedral meshes; cortical and cancellous bone used 3.5 mm tetrahedral meshes.

Material properties & contacts

Bone and implants (TiAl6V4) were modeled as isotropic, linear elastic materials4,8 (Table 1). Fracture surfaces had contact (friction coefficient µ = 0.2)4. Bone-implant contact used µ = 0.2; threaded areas were bound; non-threaded areas used contact (µ = 0.2)4.

Loading & boundary conditions

Based on physiological loading during single-leg stance9,10, a 700 N vertical load and 7 N·m external rotation torque were applied to the femoral head. Muscle forces were omitted for simplicity. The distal femur was fully constrained.

Evaluation

FEA recorded femoral head displacement (stability index) and maximum Von Mises stress on implants (failure risk indicator). Primary comparison: DHS vs. PFNA-C vs. InterTAN-C. Secondary comparison: Central vs. Inferior screw position in CMNs.

Results

Model validation

Stress distribution in the intact femur model (Fig. 2A) showed concentration in the lower/middle femur, femoral neck, and trochanteric regions, with significant stress at the lesser trochanter, consistent with literature11,12. Maximum displacement was 1.37 mm (Fig. 2B), confirming model validity.

Biomechanical parameters (Table 2)

As fracture complexity increased, implant stress and displacement significantly increased.

Simple fracture

All implants showed minimal displacement (DHS: 1.16 mm, PFNA-C: 1.38 mm, InterTAN-C: 1.16 mm) and low, comparable stress (DHS: 147 MPa, PFNA-C: 168 MPa, InterTAN-C: 192 MPa). Values were close to the intact femur.

Intertrochanteric/lateral wall defect fractures

Significant differences emerged.

Displacement

DHS exhibited the highest displacement (Intertroch Defect: 3.32 mm; Lat Wall Defect: 4.12 mm), followed by PFNA-C (2.13 mm; 2.41 mm), then InterTAN-C (1.83 mm; 1.99 mm). Inferior screw positions reduced displacement in CMNs (e.g., PFNA-I: 1.67 mm / 2.17 mm; InterTAN-I: 1.65 mm / 1.88 mm).

Implant stress

InterTAN-C showed the highest stress (600 MPa; 767 MPa), followed by PFNA-C (474 MPa; 515 MPa), then DHS (424 MPa; 485 MPa). Inferior positions reduced stress (e.g., PFNA-I: 353 MPa / 494 MPa; InterTAN-I: 400 MPa / 583 MPa). Crucially, all stresses remained below the Ti fatigue limit (~ 900 MPa).

Screw position effect

The inferior screw position consistently demonstrated lower femoral head displacement and lower maximum implant stress across all fracture types compared to the central position (Table 2; Fig. 5). This is attributed to enhanced support from the denser inferior femoral neck cortex (Fig. 5 arrows).

Discussion

This FEA study provides a comprehensive biomechanical comparison of DHS, PFNA, and InterTAN for different basicervical fracture patterns. Key findings confirm the suitability of all three implants for simple fractures, where anatomical reduction ensures load transfer through bone contact, minimizing implant stress and displacement13. This aligns with Kim JW et al.‘s FEA findings14.

For unstable fractures (intertrochanteric/lateral wall defects), CMNs (PFNA, InterTAN) demonstrated superior stability over DHS, evidenced by significantly lower femoral head displacement. This advantage stems from the CMN’s shorter lever arm, intramedullary position within the stable diaphysis, and inherent load-sharing capability15. DHS’s eccentric design and longer lever arm amplify compressive forces, increasing displacement risk, particularly in lateral wall defects where cortical support is compromised16,17. Our results show DHS displacement nearly doubled in lateral wall defects compared to intertrochanteric defects, while CMNs showed only modest increases. The intact greater trochanter interacting with the CMN’s proximal end likely provides crucial lateral buttressing in lateral wall defects18. InterTAN showed marginally lower displacement than PFNA, potentially due to its dual-screw design offering greater load distribution19 and cross-sectional support within the femoral neck20.

Implant stress analysis revealed CMNs facilitate stress relief via load sharing, contrasting with DHS’s load-bearing character21. While InterTAN exhibited the highest peak stress (attributable to greater force absorption at the screw-nail junction reducing displacement22,23, all recorded stresses remained safely below the fatigue limit of medical titanium (850–900 MPa)4,24, indicating theoretical safety for all constructs. The inferior screw position consistently demonstrated biomechanical advantages (lower displacement and stress) over the central position across CMNs and fracture types. This is primarily due to enhanced support from the high-modulus inferior femoral neck cortex25, corroborating computational26 and experimental27 studies on other proximal femur fractures.

Critical analysis of model assumptions and clinical translation

Model simplification

Our model utilized isotropic, linear elastic material properties for bone, a common simplification in FEA4,8,14. However, bone, particularly cancellous bone, exhibits anisotropic and viscoelastic behavior. This simplification may affect absolute stress and strain magnitudes, although the comparative trends between implants under identical conditions are likely robust. Future models incorporating anisotropic properties and bone yield criteria would enhance physiological relevance.

Patient specificity & generalizability

The model was based on a single, healthy 60-year-old male femur. This limits direct extrapolation to populations with significant osteoporosis (common in elderly fracture patients) or vastly different anatomy (e.g., very small or large femora). Osteoporotic bone, with reduced modulus and strength, could significantly alter implant stability and failure modes (e.g., higher cut-out risk, different stress distributions). Future studies should incorporate bone density variation and probabilistic modeling.

Loading conditions & failure modes

The simulation used a static load representing single-leg stance. It did not account for dynamic loading (e.g., walking, stumbling), cyclic fatigue, or complex muscle forces. These factors are crucial for predicting long-term implant performance and failure mechanisms like screw cut-out or breakage. While static FEA provides valuable comparative insights, dynamic simulations and fatigue analysis are needed for comprehensive failure prediction.

Clinical applicability

While FEA identifies biomechanical advantages for CMNs (especially InterTAN with inferior screw) in unstable basicervical fractures, clinical outcomes depend on numerous factors beyond pure mechanics: surgical technique (reduction quality, screw placement accuracy), patient bone quality, comorbidities, and rehabilitation protocols. The higher stresses observed in InterTAN, although sub-yield, warrant clinical monitoring for potential long-term fatigue issues, especially in osteoporotic bone. Our findings support the biomechanical rationale for using CMNs in unstable patterns but must be validated by prospective clinical studies comparing functional outcomes and complication rates. The significant advantage of the inferior screw position suggests it should be the surgical target when using CMNs.

To our knowledge, this is the first FEA study comprehensively comparing intramedullary and extramedullary fixation across defined basicervical fracture instability patterns. Despite limitations inherent to computational modeling28, our results under controlled, comparative conditions provide valuable biomechanical insights to guide implant selection and surgical technique.

Conclusion

For simple basicervical fractures, DHS, PFNA, and InterTAN demonstrate comparable biomechanical stability. In unstable fractures with intertrochanteric or lateral wall defects, cephalomedullary nails (PFNA, InterTAN) provide significantly superior stability compared to DHS, with InterTAN offering marginally better stability than PFNA. The inferior position of the hip screw consistently demonstrates biomechanical advantages over the central position in both PFNA and InterTAN and is recommended. These findings provide a biomechanical rationale for implant selection, though clinical validation is essential.
