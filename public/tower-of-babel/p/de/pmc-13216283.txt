Surveillance of drug resistance and drug resistance genes in Enterococcus spp. from migratory birds in Nansha Wetland Park, Guangzhou, China
Authors: Xianguang Lin, Jiekun Chen, Bingqian Du, Tiegang Li, Yun Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the antibiotic resistance profiles and predominant tetracycline (TET) resistance genes in Enterococcus faecalis isolated from migratory birds, we collected 393 fecal samples from migratory birds at Guangzhou Nansha Wetland Park between January and December 2019.The samples were processed for bacterial isolation, culture, and identification, 79 samples were culture-positive for Enterococcus, the sample positivity rate was 20.10% (79/393), which included Enterococcus faecium (28 strains,35.44%) and Enterococcus faecalis (29 strains,36.71%). To avoid bias caused by clonal strains from the same host, only one representative isolate per positive sample was included in subsequent analyses.followed by antimicrobial susceptibility testing, PCR amplification of vancomycin (VAN) and TET resistance genes, and subsequent sequencing.Of these isolates,69.62% exhibited resistance to at least one antibiotic; the highest rates of resistance were observed for tetracycline (54.43%),erythromycin (ERY) (48.10%),and high-level streptomycin (STR) (17.72%).Furthermore, multidrug resistance (MDR) was detected in 27.85% of the strains examined, including four vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE).Sequencing analysis revealed that among the isolates tested,29 strains harbored the tetM gene—accounting for approximately 7.38% of all samples collected.These findings indicate a significant prevalence of antibiotic and multidrug resistance among enterococci in migratory birds within Guangzhou, underscoring a potential risk for the dissemination of VRE.

Introduction

The discovery of antibiotics is widely acknowledged as one of the most transformative milestones in medical history.These agents are extensively utilized in clinical medicine, veterinary practice, agriculture, and livestock production.However, the widespread and often inappropriate use of antimicrobial agents has accelerated the emergence and dissemination of drug-resistant bacteria across human, animal, and environmental reservoirs, constituting a growing and urgent threat to global public health.The overuse of antibiotics, coupled with inadequate infection control practices and deficiencies in healthcare infrastructure, facilitates the dissemination of resistant strains across various environmental reservoirs, including water systems, soil, and wildlife populations1.

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) is recognized as the primary mechanism driving the spread of antibiotic resistance genes among bacterial populations.Migratory birds-particularly those inhabiting anthropogenically altered environments-have increasingly been identified as key vectors for the long-distance transmission of resistant microorganisms2.As these birds traverse polluted aquatic ecosystems contaminated by medical effluents, livestock runoff, industrial wastewater, and domestic sewage, they can acquire antibiotic-resistant bacteria and resistance genes from such environments and subsequently transport them across geographically distant regions3. Consequently, migratory birds are now regarded not only as reservoirs but also as active disseminators of antimicrobial-resistant pathogens4.A growing body of evidence has confirmed their role in the spread of clinically relevant multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains5–7, underscoring their potential contribution to the global circulation of resistance determinants.

Enterococcus species are among the leading causes of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), particularly urinary tract infections, soft tissue infections, and device-related bloodstream infections. The prevalence of multidrug-resistant Enterococcus in clinical settings has become a significant concern, contributing to prolonged hospital stays, increased treatment costs, and elevated risks of therapeutic failure and mortality8.This genus exhibits intrinsic resistance to several classes of antibiotics, which enables it to persist and dominate the intestinal microbiota under selective pressure9.Although Enterococcus isolates have historically been susceptible to vancomycin (VAN), tetracycline (TET), and erythromycin (ERY), there has been a marked increase in acquired resistance, particularly to TET and ERY10.Vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) has emerged as a critical global public health threat and is recognized as a leading cause of nosocomial infections. Moreover, high levels of antimicrobial resistance have been documented in bacterial isolates from urban-adapted bird species such as pigeons11,12, suggesting that wild birds may serve as long-term reservoirs for resistant bacteria during migration and contribute to the intercontinental spread of resistance genes.Nansha Wetland Park, located on the western shore of the Pearl River Estuary in Guangzhou, encompasses an area of approximately 10,000 mu (about 6.7 km²),making it the largest wetland scenic zone within the city.As part of the East Asian-Australasian Flyway—one of the world’s major migratory bird routes—the park provides favorable climatic conditions along with rich aquatic ecosystems that support thousands of migratory birds each year.Given the frequent direct and indirect human interactions with local wetland and river ecosystems, a potential zoonotic transmission pathway has emerged: antibiotic-resistant bacteria excreted in bird feces may contaminate water sources and subsequently be transmitted to humans and other animals3. Nevertheless, the role of migratory birds as reservoirs and vectors of antibiotic-resistant bacteria remains underrecognized.To date, high-quality, geographically comprehensive epidemiological data on the prevalence of multidrug-resistant Enterococcus spp. among migratory birds arriving in Guangzhou are lacking.

Therefore, this study aims to (i) identify the predominant Enterococcus spp. isolated from migratory birds sampled at Nansha Wetland Park; (ii) comprehensively characterize their antimicrobial resistance phenotypes across clinically relevant antibiotic classes; and (iii) detect and quantify key acquired resistance genes—specifically tetA, tetK, and tetM for tetracycline resistance, and vanA and vanB for vancomycin resistance.These target genes were selected based on robust global epidemiological evidence: tetA, tetK (efflux-mediated), and tetM (ribosomal protection-mediated) constitute the principal molecular mechanisms of tetracycline resistance in Enterococcus spp. worldwide13,14, with consistently high detection frequencies in wildlife-derived isolates15. Similarly, vanA and vanB account for > 90% of clinically significant vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) strains globally16 and are designated by the World Health Organization (WHO) as priority targets for AMR surveillance due to their mobility, clinical impact, and epidemic potential. Critically, even low-abundance detection of VRE signals the environmental presence—and potential horizontal dissemination—of transferable vancomycin resistance determinants. Southern China’s intensive aquaculture systems have historically involved substantial use of tetracyclines and glycopeptide antibiotics; regional studies confirm that tetA, tetM, and vanA are the dominant resistance determinants in environmental and livestock-associated enterococci—including high prevalence in Guangdong duck–fish polyculture systems17.Targeting these regionally prevalent, clinically consequential genes enhances assay sensitivity, analytical relevance, and translational utility, while minimizing resource allocation toward rare or epidemiologically insignificant markers.Although phenotypic resistance to macrolides(e.g.,erythromycin) was frequently observed in preliminary screening, comprehensive molecular characterization of associated resistance determinants (e.g.,ermB, msrC) will be performed in subsequent analytical phases.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Fresh fecal (guano) samples were collected from leaves and tree trunks in areas frequented by migratory birds at Guangzhou Nansha Wetland Park, with the approval of the Guangzhou Forestry and Garden Bureau, between January and December 2019.The samples exhibited a viscous consistency and appeared as lumpy solids.Given the inherent constraints of field-based sampling, absolute sterility cannot be achieved; however, strict adherence to aseptic technique—including the use of sterilized equipment, glove changes between samples, and on-site surface decontamination—was rigorously implemented to mitigate contamination risks from soil, freshwater, or other environmental matrices.Immediately after collection, the samples were placed into sterile test tubes containing 10 mL of physiological saline and transported to the laboratory within four hours.Upon arrival at the laboratory, they were stored at temperatures ranging from 0 to 4 °C.Isolation and culture of bacteria were completed within 24 h post-sampling.A total of 393 guano samples were collected for this study.

Isolation, culture, and detection of bacteria

Using aseptic technique, 1 mL of each sample was inoculated into 10 mL of 6.5% NaCl nutrient broth (Beijing Land Bridge Technology Co.,Ltd.,China). The inoculated broths were incubated at 37 °C for 18 h to enrich for Enterococcus species.Following incubation, a loopful of the enriched culture was streaked onto Pfizer Selective Enterococcus (PSE) agar medium (Beijing Land Bridge Technology Co.,Ltd.,China) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to promote colony formation. Colonies exhibiting a brownish-black color with a surrounding brown halo on PSE agar were presumptively identified as Enterococcus spp. and preserved for further analysis.

Suspicious colonies were selected using a sterile inoculation loop and subcultured onto blood agar plates, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Typical Enterococcus colonies appeared small, grayish-white, opaque, and smooth-surfaced.Hemolysis patterns varied: some isolates displayed α-hemolysis, characterized by a zone of partial red blood cell lysis around the colony, while others exhibited no hemolytic activity.

Identification of Enterococcus

Three milliliters of sterile diluent were added to a designated test tube, into which a purified suspect colony from blood agar (as described in Sect. 2.2) was transferred.The bacterial suspension was adjusted to a turbidity equivalent to 5.0–6.2 McFarland units.Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed using the BioMérieux VITEK 2 Compact system (BioMérieux, France), with results automatically generated by the instrument.

Antimicrobial susceptibility profiling

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) of the isolated Enterococcus spp. was performed using a gram-positive aerobic AST panel (customized plate for gram-positive bacteria) provided by Shanghai XingBai Biotechnology Co.,Ltd.,China.Twelve commonly used antibiotics were evaluated: ampicillin (AMP), penicillin (PNC), erythromycin (ERY), norfloxacin (NOR), ciprofloxac.

-in (CIP), levofloxacin (LEV), vancomycin (VAN), tetracycline (TET), chloramphenicol (CHL), high-concentration gentamicin (GEN), and high-concentration streptomycin (STR).The quality control strain was Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212,provided by Shanghai Xingbai Biotechnology Co.,Ltd. (with the strain origin traceable to the American Type Culture Collection, (ATCC).A 100-µL aliquot of the standardized inoculum suspension—prepared in nutrient brothand adjusted to 0.5 McFarland turbidity—was transferred from a sterile glass tube (containing 12 mL of suspension) to the negative control well of the Gram-positive aerobic antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) panel. The inoculation broth and its accompanying glass tubes were all provided by Shanghai Xingbai Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

A single bacterial colony from the blood agar plate (as described in Sect. 2.3) was selected using a sterile inoculation loop and suspended in 2–3 mL of sterile saline.The bacterial suspension was adjusted to a turbidity equivalent to 0.5 McFarland unit (approximately 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL). Subsequently,60 µL of this suspension was added to 12 mL of nutrient broth for drug sensitivity testing, mixed thoroughly, and vortexed at 1,200 rpm for 10 s.The resulting mixture was dispensed into a 96-well microdilution AST strip, with 100 µL added to each well.The strips were incubated at 35 °C for 18–20 h.

The results were interpreted using the Microbiology Identification and Antibiotic Susceptibility Analysis System. Susceptibility categories—susceptible, intermediate, or resistant—were determined according to clinical breakpoints established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI)18. The interpretive criteria are detailed in Table 1.

Detection of drug resistance genes

Genomic DNA was extracted from 5 mL of bacterial culture grown in 6.5% NaCl nutrient broth (as described in Sect. 2.3) by Guangzhou Haotian Biotechnology Co.,Ltd.,China.The target antibiotic resistance genes amplified by PCR were vanA, vanB, tetK, tetA, and tetM.Gene-specific primers were designed and synthesized according to previously published sequences19–22, with primer details provided in Table 2.

The optimal PCR amplification conditions for each gene were as follows:vanA—initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min;35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 58 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 55 s, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min;vanB—initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min;35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 54 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 55 s, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min;tetK—initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min;40 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 52 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 60 s, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 2 min;tetA—initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min;35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at 61 °C for 45 s, and extension at 72 °C for 90 s, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min;tetM—initial denaturation at 93 °C for 2 min;35 cycles of denaturation at 93 °C for 60 s, annealing at 55 °C for 60 s, and extension at 72 °C for 60 s, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

PCR amplicons were resolved by electrophoresis on a 1% (w/v) agarose gel in 1×TAE buffer at 120 V for 30 min using the Jenoptik Rotaphor 6.0 electrophoresis system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).Gel documentation was performed by Guangzhou Haotian Biotechnology Co.,Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). As the supplied gel images were digitally cropped by the service provider, the original uncropped gels are no longer available for archival retrieval.Amplification was deemed successful only when a single band of expected size was observed, with absence of primer dimers and non-specific products.Successfully amplified products were submitted to Guangzhou Haotian Biotechnology Co.,Ltd. for bidirectional Sanger sequencing. Raw sequence reads underwent quality filtering and adapter/primer trimming using Geneious Prime v2023.2;low-quality bases (Q < 20) and residual primer sequences were removed.High-quality consensus sequences—confirmed to span the full length of the target gene—were subjected to nucleotide BLAST analysis against the NCBI RefSeq database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/refseq/) to assign taxonomic identity.

Results

Isolation and identification of Enterococcaceae

Among 393 fresh fecal samples of migratory birds,79 (20.10%) yielded Enterococcus spp. upon culture-based isolation and identification.To avoid overrepresentation of clonal strains derived from a single host—which could confound antimicrobial resistance (AMR) profiling—only one phylogenetically representative isolate was selected per positive sample for downstream antimicrobial susceptibility testing and molecular detection of resistance determinants.A total of 79 non-duplicate Enterococcus isolates were thus included in the final analysis.Among these isolates, Enterococcus faecium and Enterococcus faecalis were the predominant species, with 29 and 28 strains identified, respectively. These corresponded to detection rates of 7.38% and 7.12% of the total samples and accounted for 36.71% and 35.44% of all Enterococcus isolates.Additional species identified included Enterococcus casseliflavus(n = 10), Enterococcus gallinarum(n = 6),Enterococcus hirae (n = 5), and Enterococcus durans (n = 1) (see Table 3).

Drug susceptibility test

Among 79 Enterococcus isolates (20.10% of the total bacterial collection),Enterococcus faecium(n = 28,35.44%) and Enterococcus faecalis(n = 29,36.71%) were the two predominant species.Overall,55 isolates(69.62%) exhibited resistance to at least one antimicrobial agent. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed the highest resistance rates against tetracycline(TET; 43 isolates,54.43%),followed by erythromycin(ERY;38 isolates,48.10%).Lower but notable resistance frequencies were observed for high-concentration streptomycin (12 isolates,17.72%), chloramphenicol (10 isolates,12.70%), norfloxacin (7 isolates, 8.87%), and ciprofloxacin (7 isolates, 8.87%).Vancomycin resistance was detected in 4 isolates (5.07%),and penicillin resistance in a single isolate(1.27%).No resistance was observed to ampicillin, levofloxacin, or high-concentration gentamicin.Species-specific resistance patterns were evident:Enterococcus intestinalis and Enterococcus durans displayed 100% tetracycline resistance, whereas Enterococcus faecium exhibited the highest erythromycin resistance rate (71.43%).Resistance to high-concentration streptomycin varied modestly across species (16.67–20.00%). Molecular screening identified the tetM gene in 29 isolates (7.38% of total), with no detection of vanA or vanB alleles.Isolates harboring tetM showed significantly elevated resistance rates to tetracycline compared to tetM-negative strains. Four vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus(VRE) isolates were confirmed: two E. faecium,one E. casseliflavus, and one E. gallinarum (see Table 4).

Among the 55 drug-resistant strains,20 distinct resistance patterns were observed.The most common types of resistance spectrum are T (tetracycline), with a total of 10 strains (12.66%);the second is E-T(erythromycin-tetracycline), with a total of 7 strains (8.86%);the third is E-T-S (H) (erythromycin-tetracycline-streptomycin(high concentration)) and E-T-C (erythromycin-tetracycline-chloramphenicol), both with 5 strains (6.33%)( See Table 4).

A total of 22 multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains were identified, representing approximately 5.59% of all fecal samples and 27.85% of the Enterococcus isolates. The MDR rate was markedly higher in Enterococcus faecium (32.14%) than in Enterococcus faecalis (17.24%). Notably, one strain each of Enterococcus faecium and Enterococcus casseliflavus exhibited resistance to five different antibiotic classes, highlighting the presence of highly resistant genotypes in this ecosystem(see Fig. 1).

Discussion

Enterococci are among the leading causes of healthcare-associated infections(HAIs) worldwide, particularly urinary tract infections, soft tissue infections, and device-related bloodstream infections.

These bacteria exhibit intrinsic resistance to a broad spectrum of antibiotics, and multidrug-resistant (MDR) Enterococcus strains have been frequently reported in clinical settings and animal reservoirs. Each year, millions of migratory birds traverse vast geographic regions, playing a significant epidemiological role in the global dissemination of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria during migration. Enterococci are part of the normal intestinal microbiota in migratory birds and are especially prevalent in the gastrointestinal tracts of carnivorous species23. Previous studies on wild birds have demonstrated that Enterococcus spp.,particularly Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium,exhibit resistance to multiple antibiotics and carry clinically relevant resistance genotypes such as CC17,CC81,and CC11624,25.Notably, these genotypes have also been identified in resistant isolates from hospitalized patients in several European countries, community-acquired infections, livestock, companion animals, food products, and wastewater systems26, suggesting potential pathways for cross-species transmission.Given the limited data on MDR bacteria originating from migratory birds, this study was conducted to investigate fecal samples collected from migratory birds at Nansha Wetland Park in Guangzhou, aiming to assess the potential public health and environmental implications of Enterococcus carriage.

Our findings revealed the presence of 79 Enterococcus strains in 393 fecal samples, yielding an overall detection rate of 20.10% (79/393).The dominant species were Enterococcus faecalis (n = 29) and Enterococcus faecium (n = 28), followed by Enterococcus casseliflavus(n = 10),Enterococcus gallinarum(n = 6),Enterococcus hirae(n = 5),and Enterococcus durans(n = 1).These results are consistent with previous reports by Dolka27 and Poeta28, indicating that the observed prevalence aligns with expected levels in avian populations. The detection rate of Enterococcus faecium in this study was lower than that reported in a Guangzhou hospital between 2015 and 2017,whereas the prevalence of Enterococcus faecalis was comparable to clinical isolation rates29.The relatively high abundance of Enterococcus faecalis may increase the risk of infection in immunocompromised or susceptible human populations and could contribute to severe clinical outcomes.It is well documented that Enterococcus spp. can be transmitted from animals and environmental sources to humans, causing opportunistic infections—such as Enterococcus casseliflavus infections linked to swine30 and Enterococcus hirae cases associated with avian reservoirs31.Therefore, monitoring the distribution of drug-resistant Enterococcus spp. in migratory birds is essential for mitigating the introduction and spread of exogenous resistant strains and zoonotic pathogens in this region.

Among the 79 isolated Enterococcus strains,55 (69.62%) were resistant to at least one antibiotic.Tetracycline(TET) exhibited the highest resistance rate(54.43%),followed by erythromycin(ERY) at 48.10%.Additionally, four vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) strains were identified: two Enterococcus faecium,one Enterococcus casseliflavus, and one Enterococcus gallinarum. Both Enterococcus faecium and Enterococcus faecalis showed the highest resistance frequencies to TET and ERY.These resistance patterns are consistent with previous studies indicating that Enterococcus isolates from wildlife commonly exhibit resistance to tetracyclines and macrolides32,33.However, the resistance rates to ERY and TET observed in this study were lower than those reported in the CHINET national surveillance program and clinical isolates from Guangdong Province, yet higher than those found in wild animals with minimal antibiotic exposure.This discrepancy suggests that migratory birds in Guangzhou may acquire resistant bacteria through indirect or direct contact with anthropogenic sources, such as hospitals, agricultural runoff, or urban wastewater.Notably, the prevalence of vancomycin resistance among fecal samples in this study was 1.02% (4/393),exceeding previously reported rates in both clinical settings29,34 and animal populations35, indicating an emerging trend of vancomycin resistance dissemination among migratory birds in the Guangzhou area.Therefore, monitoring the carriage of drug-resistant Enterococcus,particularly VRE, in these birds is critical for preventing the intercontinental spread of high-risk resistance determinants.

The overall multidrug resistance (MDR) rate among the 79 Enterococcus isolates was 27.85%, which is lower than rates typically reported in domestic clinical settings15,36,37.Although the overall antimicrobial resistance rate of Enterococcus faecium(85.7%) exceeded that of Enterococcus faecalis(55.2%), the multidrug resistance(MDR) rate was significantly higher in Enterococcus faecalis(32.1%) than in Enterococcus faecium(17.2%).These findings indicate distinct species-specific patterns in both resistance profiles and MDR risk.In this region, Enterococcus faecium poses a greater overall antimicrobial resistance threat, whereas Enterococcus faecalis is the predominant reservoir of multidrug-resistant Enterococcus enterococci.

Among the 79 Enterococcus isolates analyzed, tetM was detected in 29 strains (36.7%), whereas tetA, tetK,and other canonical tetracycline resistance determinants were not amplified under the validated PCR assay conditions.This observation is consistent with tetracycline resistance gene distributions reported in Enterococcus isolates from African wildlife and migratory birds15, reinforcing tetM as the dominant and functionally central tetracycline resistance determinant in this migratory bird-associated enterococcal population.Although four vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus(VRE) isolates were identified, neither vanA nor vanB—the principal genetic mediators of high-level vancomycin resistance—were detected.These results suggest that the observed vancomycin resistance may be attributable to cryptic genetic variants, non-canonical regulatory adaptations, or novel resistance pathways yet to be characterized.Comprehensive genomic sequencing coupled with phenotypic validation will be essential to decipher the molecular basis of vancomycin resistance in these isolates.Statistical analysis revealed that the tetracycline resistance rate of tetM-positive Enterococcus isolates (29 strains) was 31.7%, significantly higher than that of tetM-negative isolates (50 strains) at 24.1% (χ²=10.11,P < 0.01),indicating a significant positive correlation between the tetM gene carrying status and the tetracycline resistance phenotype of Enterococcus.This association has a clear molecular basis: the tetM gene encodes a ribosome protection protein that specifically binds to the 30 S ribosomal subunit of bacteria, inhibiting the binding of tetracycline to the ribosome through a steric hindrance effect, thereby maintaining the accuracy of protein translation and ultimately conferring a stable tetracycline resistance phenotype to the host bacteria14. This mechanism has been experimentally verified in various important clinical pathogens, including Enterococcus faecalis38, Enterococcus faecium39,40, Neisseria gonorrhoeae41, Mycoplasma hominis42, and Ureaplasma urealyticum43, confirming the functional conservation and significant clinical importance of the tetM-mediated resistance mechanism across species.

In this study, four vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus(VRE) isolates tested negative for both vanA and vanB genes, and a subset of tetracycline-resistant isolates lacked the canonical tetracycline resistance determinants tetM, tetA, and tetK.This phenotype–genotype discordance is consistent with the presence of established resistance mechanisms not targeted by our multiplex PCR assay. Regarding tetracycline resistance, beyond the core screening targets tetA, tetK,and tetM, Enterococcus spp. harbor several experimentally validated resistance determinants: tetL (encoding an efflux pump),tetO and tetS (encoding ribosomal protection proteins), and point mutations in rpsJ—the gene encoding ribosomal protein S10.These rpsJ mutations frequently co-occur with tetL tetM and can independently confer low-to-moderate-level tetracycline resistance44.Furthermore, tetracycline-inactivating enzymes—particularly the FAD-dependent monooxygenases Tet(X3) and Tet(X4)—catalyze site-specific oxidation at the C11a position of tetracyclines, leading to irreversible loss of antimicrobial activity; functional expression of these enzymes in enterococci has been shown to confer high-level, plasmid-borne tetracycline resistance45.With respect to vancomycin resistance, nine acquired van gene clusters—vanA, vanB, vanD, vanE, vanG, vanL, vanM, vanN,and vanX—have been functionally validated as mediators of vancomycin resistance in enterococci, whereas vanC represents an intrinsic chromosomal determinant strictly limited to Enterococcus gallinarum and Enterococcus casseliflavus46.vanC confers low-level vancomycin resistance (MIC 4–32 µg/mL) while retaining full susceptibility to teicoplanin—a phenotypic profile that aligns precisely with the Enterococcus casseliflavus VRE isolate identified herein, providing robust genetic–phenotypic concordance for vanC-mediated resistance.Due to its low inter-species sequence conservation and narrow taxonomic distribution,vanC is inherently undetectable by standard vanA/vanB-targeted PCR assays; definitive identification therefore requires species-specific molecular testing or whole-genome sequencing(WGS).Moreover, acquired van types—including vanD, vanE, vanG,vanL, and vanN—can mediate high-level vancomycin resistance independently of vanA and vanB; vanN, in particular, has been experimentally confirmed to reside on a conjugative plasmid and to transfer horizontally between enterococcal strains with high efficiency via conjugation46.Collectively, the observed phenotype–genotype discordances are fully attributable to resistance determinants outside the scope of our molecular screening panel—including unassayed acquired resistance genes (e.g.,vanD–vanN, vanX),intrinsic chromosomal elements (e.g.,vanC), non-canonical efflux systems, and enzymatic inactivation pathways.To comprehensively resolve the genetic architecture and functional basis of resistance, future studies should integrate whole-genome sequencing(WGS) with transcriptomic profiling and targeted genetic validation—such as allelic exchange or complementation assays—in representative isolates.

This study has several limitations.First, resistance genes for erythromycin and chloramphenicol were not screened.The resistance rate to erythromycin in our Enterococcus isolates was 48.10%, and notably, the resistance rate in Enterococcus faecium reached as high as 71.43%,indicating that erythromycin resistance is highly prevalent in this migratory bird population. ermB is the dominant erythromycin resistance genotype in Enterococcus,located on transposon Tn917 and often co-transferred with tetM in multidrug-resistant enterococci.Due to the lack of detection for corresponding resistance genes (e.g., ermA,ermB for erythromycin resistance; cat, fexA, cfr for chloramphenicol resistance), the molecular mechanisms underlying these resistance phenotypes remain unclear, and the potential for co-prevalence or co-transfer of these genes with tetM could not be assessed.Future studies should include screening for resistance genes corresponding to drugs with high resistance rates (e.g.,ermB, cat) to fully reveal the resistome characteristics of Enterococcus from migratory birds.Secondly, sample collection was limited to the entire year of 2019,making it difficult for single-year data to reflect long-term trends in drug resistance.Thirdly, although the tetM gene was detected, its transmissibility was not assessed through conjugation experiments or plasmid analysis, so it is impossible to determine the risk of horizontal transmission of this gene. Fourthly, vanA/vanB genes were not detected in four strains of vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE).Due to the lack of whole-genome sequencing, the mechanisms of their resistance (such as inherent vanC resistance or other acquired resistance genes) remain unclear.Fifthly, the study only collected fecal samples from migratory birds, without simultaneously testing environmental samples (such as water and soil), making it difficult to assess the role of migratory birds in the local drug resistance ecosystem. Sixthly, the species of migratory birds from which the feces were collected were not identified.Differences in the ecological habits of different bird species may affect the characteristics of drug-resistant bacteria carriage. In addition, the sample sizes for some bacterial species (such as Enterococcus durans and Enterococcus hirae) were small, so the related resistance rate results should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should combine multi-year continuous monitoring, whole-genome sequencing, environmental sample analysis, and host species identification to more comprehensively assess the ecological and public health significance of migratory birds in the spread of drug-resistant bacteria.

Conclusion

Migratory birds serve as effective vectors for the long-distance dissemination of resistant bacteria and resistance genes across international flyways, facilitating both intraspecies and interspecies horizontal gene transfer. This study reveals a substantial reservoir of Enterococcus spp. in migratory birds in Guangzhou, characterized by high levels of antibiotic resistance, detectable vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE), and predominant tetM gene carriage. The elevated resistance and multidrug resistance (MDR) rates in Enterococcus faecium suggest that this species poses a greater public health threat compared to Enterococcus faecalis.Therefore, continuous surveillance of drug-resistant Enterococcus in migratory birds is imperative for early detection, risk assessment, and control of emerging antimicrobial resistance threats, particularly in ecologically sensitive and densely populated regions such as the Pearl River Delta.
