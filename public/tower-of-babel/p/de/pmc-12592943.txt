Pitfalls in the Diagnosis of Acute Kidney Injury and Hepatorenal Syndrome in Cirrhosis
Authors: Manoj Kumar, Vijay Sundarsingh
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Vinayak et al. titled “Spectrum of Acute Kidney Injury and Its Outcome in Chronic Liver Disease Patients in the Emergency Department.”1 The authors are to be congratulated for their valuable contribution to understanding the clinical profile and outcomes of acute kidney injury (AKI) in patients with chronic liver disease (CLD), a subject of high clinical relevance. We would like to offer a few observations that we believe may enhance the interpretation of the findings.

The study screened 146 patients, of whom 85 were included. However, the manuscript does not explicitly define the inclusion criteria, beyond identifying patients with CLD and AKI. A clearer description of the parameters used for patient selection would enhance methodological transparency. Additionally, there is an apparent inconsistency in the AKI diagnostic criteria. While the abstract refers to the KDIGO definitions, the methods section specifies the use of ICA–AKI criteria for inclusion. This discrepancy could create confusion, and clarification on which framework was consistently applied would improve the interpretability of the results.

Fractional excretion of sodium (FENa) <1% was used to classify prerenal AKI, and >1% for intrinsic/ATN. However, as highlighted in recent literature, FENa has significant limitations in cirrhotic patients, particularly in the setting of diuretic therapy and systemic inflammation. Attieh and Wadei emphasize that in cirrhosis, FENa <1% demonstrates high sensitivity but very low specificity, and thus may not reliably distinguish hepatorenal syndrome (HRS) or prerenal states from intrinsic renal injury.2 Given these limitations of FENa, incorporating FEUrea, which is less influenced by diuretics, offers greater diagnostic accuracy in cirrhotic patients and should be prioritized in future research.

The study cohort demonstrated high illness severity, with a mean SOFA score >10. Hypotension and vasopressor requirements are established contributors to AKI in cirrhosis.3 While mean arterial pressures were reported, additional details regarding the prevalence of shock, hypotension, and vasopressor use would have provided valuable context, as these hemodynamic parameters are closely linked with the onset and progression of AKI in CLD. Moreover, the absence of shock is a key diagnostic criterion for HRS, and it seems unlikely that patients with such advanced illness severity would have presented without some degree of shock.

In conclusion, this study provides an important contribution to a challenging field. Nevertheless, if both KDIGO and ICA definitions were used, it raises uncertainty regarding the actual incidence of AKI. Furthermore, reliance on FENa and the lack of detailed shock/vasopressor requirements reporting increases the risk of misclassification, with under- or over-representation of AKI subtypes, and in particular, the potential overestimation of HRS. These factors merit cautious interpretation when extrapolating the findings to clinical practice.
