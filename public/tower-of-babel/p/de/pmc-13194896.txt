A hybrid experimental-numerical framework for prestressed concrete bridge model validation and sensor placement optimization: a case study
Authors: S C Jayasinghe, M Mahmoodian, A Alavi, A Sidiq, Z Sun, F Shahrivar, R Chan, J Thangarajah, S Setunge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConducting numerical modal validations of operational bridges are vital for their structural health monitoring (SHM). This type of validation fully depends on the accurate assessment of the dynamic characteristics of bridges. Accordingly, the arrangement and placement of sensors to measure vibration data also play a crucial role in ensuring the effective capturing of both global and localised dynamic behaviours. Hence, a proper sensor layout is required to accurately identify the bridge behaviour. This study investigates the effectiveness of different sensor configurations in identifying modal parameters of a case study bridge, located in New South Wales, Australia using three sensor layouts: full-width coverage and two partial-width configurations, each covering one side of the bridge. The performance of these configurations was evaluated through field testing and numerical validation. The results showed up to 97% similarity for the first mode shape under partial-width layouts. The partial-width configurations demonstrated high accuracy in capturing both global and localized modal responses and were effective in monitoring localized stress variations as they are aligned with traffic flow zones. However, the full-width layout is preferable for global structural assessments and showed limitations in detecting higher-order modes. This study underscores the critical importance of sensor placement in achieving accurate modal analysis and robust numerical validation while also highlighting the comparative advantage of integrating dynamic response metrics into sensor placement strategies.Keywords: Finite element modelling, Frequency domain decomposition, Accelerometers, Bridge validation, Sensor placement, OptimisationSubject terms: Civil engineering, Software

Introduction

Bridges are critical components of the infrastructure system of a country, directly influencing its development and connectivity1. Ensuring the safety of bridges is paramount to safeguarding the well-being of the entire community2–4. In this context, structural health monitoring (SHM) practices play a pivotal role in maintaining the safety, reliability and longevity of bridge infrastructure5,6. SHM can be conducted by evaluating either the static structural response, such as deflections, strains, and stress distributions7 or the dynamic response, such as natural frequencies, mode shapes, and damping ratios8,9. Amongst, the assessments through dynamic responses, specifically, assessing natural frequencies is particularly important to consider during the design stage, as upon the construction, resonance may occur if the operational frequencies of external loads align with the natural frequencies of the structure10,11. Such conditions can induce excessive vibrations, potentially leading to structural damage or potential failure of the bridge12,13. For instance, in 1940 the Tacoma Narrows bridge collapsed due to the matching between the wind-induced vibrations and the natural frequency of the bridge, causing large amplitude vibrations that ultimately led to its failure14. These catastrophic failures are critical and therefore it is very important to undertake preliminary monitoring by identifying the dynamic performance of a bridge and ensuring that the dynamic level is within the desired level that prevents bridges from excessive vibration and to avoid any possible failures during the design or maintenance processes. Additionally, an accurate assessment of dynamic characteristics leads to prevent from extreme natural or man-made events including the earthquakes and hurricane occurrences and/or extreme traffic conditions or pedestrian loads15. For instance, in 2000, the London Millennium Bridge experienced excessive lateral vibrations on its opening day due to synchronized pedestrian walking. This is a phenomenon known as synchronous lateral excitation and it is linked to unanticipated dynamic behaviour, led to the temporary closure of the bridge and the installation of dampers to stabilize its motion16.

Engineers have put great efforts into bridge designing that could significantly reduce the dynamic behaviour of the bridges and shift the rigid bridges to more flexible bridges that fluctuate their performance in accordance with the applied loading or experiencing the dynamic mechanism. For instance, suspension bridges have been introduced for the past two decades where spatially inclined cables and hangers attached absorbs the vibration and therefore leads the bridge less susceptible to vibrations as it experiences the dynamic forces, therefore enhance resilience, prevent resonance, and ensuring long-term performance17,18. Design engineers are progressively considering the minimum dynamic impact on the bridges during the design process to withstand extreme forces, ensuring safety and extending their operational lifespan. However, identifying the different factors due to the traffic loadings and the natural events presents significant challenges during the design procedure as halting traffic is often challenging or inconvenient for measuring excitations and/ or the impact of hurricanes, earthquakes …etc19. Therefore, the sensor placement at different locations on the bridge is a critical aspect of dynamic analysis, that accurately capture the global structural responses and localised behaviours of the dynamic characteristics of a bridge. Figure 1 illustrates how the sensor placement affects the dynamic analysis of a bridge while facilitating the structural monitoring of bridges. While a bridge is subjected to external dynamic loads, it undergoes vibrations. To understand and predict this dynamic behaviour, it is required to place the sensors strategically to capture both global and localised responses. Various types of sensors are being used for this purpose. Amongst, accelerometers are the most commonly used sensors and they usually operate based on the piezoelectric effect that converts acceleration from structural vibrations into electric signals20,21. These signals can then be processed using methods like frequency domain decomposition (FDD) to extract the key dynamic parameters of bridges22. In addition to that velocity transducers23displacement gauges24 and strain gauges25 can also be used for dynamic parameter estimations of bridges in different ways. It is crucial to determine the locations of these sensors as a proper and optimised sensor placement enhances both real-time vibration analysis and the accuracy of FE-based predictions, ultimately contributing to structural health monitoring (SHM) and long-term performance assessment26.

Sensor placement for dynamic analysis.

Researchers have put great effort into identifying the various approaches to address the challenges of identifying dynamic characteristics of bridges. Finite element modelling (FEM) is one of the most common approaches in identifying the critical areas of a structure that have high stress concentrations27 where the sensors should possibly be located to predict the structural behaviour11. Also, the optimal sensor placement plays a key role in damage detection28. For instance, Meo and Zumpano29 compared six different techniques for optimal sensor placement to identify the dynamic behaviour of Nottingham Wilford suspension bridge and found that the effective independence driving-point residue (EFI-DPR) was the most effective technique where the placement of the sensors was symmetrically distributed, and high signal strength and superior modal accuracy were obtained. Tong, et al.30 suggested a method that incorporates simulated annealing algorithm for optimum sensor placement of a concrete slab and identified that it outperforms the conventional optimisation methods such as genetic algorithm. Gao, et al.31 suggested an optimum sensor layout for long-span cable-stayed bridges under existing traffic loading while minimizing the number of sensors and gaining the maximum data from the structural behaviour. Lizana and Casas32 determined the optimum sensor placements to evaluate the dynamic performance of a pedestrian bridge and they recommended that the EFI method was the most predominant method for its high information yield and computational efficiency. Also, Li, et al.33 suggested a methodology using multi-scale attention mechanism embedded within deep recurrent neural network to identify the optimum sensor locations for predicting the full-field seismic response of a building. Chai, et al.34 proposed a method for the optimization of sensor placement strategy for the SHM of bridges by combining sensitivity coefficients and an effective independence method to enhance modal observability. Yang, et al.35 proposed a generalized framework using Bayes risk as the objective function for the optimal sensor placement. Masciotta, et al.36 suggested a data-driven sensor optimisation strategy for SHM using digital twins of bridges.

Several identified key sensor placement optimisation methods are summarised in (Fig. 2). However, the key challenge lies in determining optimal sensor placement under real-world conditions to accurately estimate the dynamic characteristics of a bridge without disrupting daily traffic flow. Furthermore, the impact of different sensor layouts on the precision of dynamic characteristic estimations and their influence on the validation of numerical models remains a critical area of investigation.

Different optimisation strategies for sensor placement.

This study explores the optimisation of sensor configurations to accurately capture the structural behaviour and enabling precise validation of numerical models that enhances the structural health monitoring strategies by identifying the dynamic characteristics of an existing bridge at ambient conditions. A comparison of three distinct sensor layouts on a prestressed concrete bridge with I-shaped girders in New South Wales, Australia has been evaluated as the case study. To quantitatively assess sensor layout effectiveness, this study also employs k-median clustering to evaluate the coverage efficiency of each configuration. This study also introduces a hybrid sensor optimization method combining k-medoids clustering with simulated annealing, offering a novel and efficient framework for identifying optimal sensor configurations. While both techniques have been independently applied in structural monitoring, their integration for modal parameter identification in bridge structures remains underexplored. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Sect. 2 presents the details about the case study bridge while Sect. 3 presents the experimental program. Section 4 discusses the results of the sensor layout evaluation, optimization performance, and impact on modal analysis and numerical model validation. Section 5 highlights the limitations of the current study and potential future research directions. Finally, Sect. 6 presents the conclusions drawn from the findings.

Case study bridge

The fieldwork study focuses on the Bunnerong Bridge, located in New South Wales, Australia. The validation of the numerical model of the bridge was conducted using the Frequency Domain Decomposition (FDD) method under three distinct sensor instrumentation plans. The study aims to compare the findings from these configurations and identify the optimal sensor placement for accurately determining the dynamic properties of the bridge.

The Bunnerong Outfall Bridge is located in the Eastern Harbour Zone of Sydney over watercourse of Bunnerong creek. The construction of the Bunnerong outfall bridge has been completed in 1979, and since then it has been serving as the main connector in the local transport and infrastructure network. This bridge consists of a single span with a length of 27.36 m supported by a stable foundation comprising sandstone and sand. The bridge width is 20.2 m which consists of 17 pre-tensioned girders in I-sections aligned with the length direction, see Figs. 3 and 4, where the dimensions are in mm. The reinforced concrete girders of the Bunnerong Bridge are designed with a robust combination of prestressing strands and conventional reinforcement see (Fig. 5). All dimensions are presented in mm. Each strand was designed to have a minimum ultimate breaking load of 184 kN and each of them has sustained a force of 140 kN during the concreting process. Also, the girders follow two distinct reinforcement layouts at the centre and on the edges with a concrete strength of 45 MPa. Additionally, the reinforced concrete deck of the bridge featured both top and bottom reinforcement layers, designed with a concrete strength of 30 MPa. The bridge traverses the Bunnerong Outfall Canal and is designed with a minimum clearance of 1.4 m above the high flood level (HFL), safeguarding it against potential flooding.

In this study, the Bunnerong bridge serves as a critical case study due to its structural configuration and operational importance, which provide an ideal framework for evaluating the impact of sensor placement on modal parameter identification.

Experimental program

This study leverages the Bunnerong bridge to explore the effect of the different sensor configurations on the identification of dynamic properties accordingly by using the natural frequencies and mode shapes. In addition to evaluating predefined configurations, this study introduces a structured sensor optimization approach to identify the most effective sensor locations for accurate modal parameter identification. The optimization process involves clustering-based grouping using k-medoids followed by layout refinement via simulated annealing. Accelerometer configurations

The placement of accelerometers significantly influences the accuracy and completeness of modal parameter identification. To investigate the impact of sensor placement on modal analysis, three distinct configurations of accelerometers were employed. These configurations were tested to evaluate both the global and localized dynamic behaviour of the bridge while considering practical constraints. The basis for selecting these configurations was informed by the findings of29 who demonstrated that symmetric sensor layouts provide improved modal identification performance when assessed using the Effective Independence and Driving Point Residue (EFI-DPR) method. Accordingly, sensors were symmetrically arranged across the full width and half-width of the bridge to ensure balanced and representative data capture. A total of ten accelerometers were used in the experiment, and their distribution was adjusted into three different configurations to cover either the entire bridge or one half of the bridge at a time for the comparison of effectiveness of them in capturing the dynamic behaviour of the bridge. Table 1 presents the coverage of each configuration and a detailed explanation of each configuration is mentioned below. The configuration 1 was allowed to cover a significant portion of the bridge deck, which is approximately 73% of the total width, with the remaining portion consisting of shoulder zones on either side. The other two configurations were limited to one side of the bridge only, covering around 36% of the total width, and they were tested to simulate practical scenarios where full-width instrumentation may not be feasible due to cost or accessibility constraints.

Data acquisition

To facilitate the data acquisition, a traffic management undertaken due to the high volume of vehicles using the bridge and to safely place and corporate the dynamic characteristics testing for the bridge. The plan was determined based on the maximum safety of the team and equipment while minimising disruptions to regular traffic flow. Traffic was managed in a way that it allowed vehicles to continue crossing the bridge during the experiment, as their presence was essential for generating natural vibrations for operational modal analysis (OMA). The data acquisition process was undertaken separately for the individual configurations. In order to prevent any displacement of the sensors caused by vibrations or traffic-induced forces, ensuring consistent and reliable data collection, adhesive materials were used to bind the sensors to the asphalt surface. Figure 10 shows the traffic management and the data acquisition process during the fieldwork program.

Traffic management and data acquisition process (configuration 2).

Once the sensors were temporarily placed on the asphalt surface, the sensors were activated spontaneously through wireless connection to the software to prevent any delay in data reading during the experiment. Through a dedicated system interface, the sensors streamed real-time data to the acquisition system. This setup allowed for efficient monitoring and data collection remotely, without the need for direct integration with the devices during the experiment. With traffic flowing on the bridge, acceleration data were recorded 3 coordinates (i.e. x, y, and z) by using a sampling rate of 200 Hz. In order to capture a sufficient set of data to support modal analysis the duration of the test was set to 15 min for each of the configurations. Data were then collected and analyses in for the validation and dynamic characteristics of the bridge. Although no direct traffic monitoring was conducted on-site during the testing period, traffic conditions were consistent with normal operational use of the bridge. According to the NSW Traffic Volume Viewer, the nearest traffic counting station reports an average daily traffic volume of approximately 55,212 vehicles, with 87% light vehicles (cars) and 13% heavy vehicles (trucks and buses). Therefore, it is clear that the bridge was subjected to a representative range of dynamic loading and also, the measured vibration signals exhibited clear spectral peaks.

Finite element modelling of Bunnerong bridge

In this study, the ANSYS finite element software, a widely used commercial software, was chosen for the bridge modelling due to its compatibility and computational process. The design of the Bunnerong Bridge was carefully monitored and the bridge geometry was developed. Then, the Menetrey-Willam material model in ANSYS was used for 30 MPa and 45 MPa concrete, along with structural steel, were utilized and coupled with the prestressed reinforcement and tendon profiles. The material properties were considered based on the given drawings and Australian standards. However, with the modal analysis the model is acting only in the elastic region. Then a complete finite element (FE) model of the bridge was created using a structured mesh. Initially, the element size was defined as 0.5 m and the mesh was consisted of both HEX20 and WED15 elements. The final FE mesh comprised of 361,314 nodes and 97,230 elements. Finite element model validation

Alongside the fieldwork study to investigate the impact of different sensor configurations on the identification of modal parameters, different mode shapes of the bridge were also evaluated by using FE modelling for all the three configurations for the validation of the bridge37. Notably, a prestressed modal analysis was conducted to validate the model. The data collected the fieldwork which captures the ambient vibration response of the bridge were then processed by using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) method to convert the time-domain signals into the frequency domain. Subsequently, a Power Spectral Density (PSD) matrix is computed, providing insights into the energy distribution across various frequencies. The next step involves applying Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to the PSD matrix. This step isolates the dominant frequency components to identify the peaks in the SVD analysis, which then correspond to the natural frequencies of the bridge. Using these identified frequencies, the mode shapes of the structure are estimated based on the fieldwork data. In parallel to the fieldwork analysis, a numerical model of the bridge is developed and analysed to extract the frequencies and mode shapes. The numerical results were compared with the fieldwork results to identify the model validation. If discrepancies are observed, the numerical model parameters are updated, and the modal analysis is repeated until satisfactory agreement is achieved38. Once the results align, the model is considered validated. The validation process was carried out to evaluate the dynamic behaviour of the bridge under all three tested scenarios. Results and analysis

This section presents the results of the fieldwork modal analysis performed on the bridge using the three sensor configurations. The analysis focuses on identifying the modal parameters, including natural frequencies and mode shapes extracted through FDD method. Comparison between the results obtained from each configuration to assess the impact of sensor placement on the accuracy and completeness of the identified modes are made. Additionally, the identified mode shapes are evaluated against the FE model predictions, using modal assurance criterion (MAC) to quantify the level of agreement. The findings provide critical insights into the dynamic behaviour of the bridge and the effectiveness of the chosen experiment methodology.

Identification of natural frequencies

After collecting the data for the first configuration, the analysis process was initiated, the data was first extracted from the text files and then subjected to filtering to eliminate noise and irrelevant components, ensuring that the critical dynamic responses were preserved for accurate analysis. The pre-processed data was then formatted and organized in a structured manner, enabling the entire dataset to be efficiently utilized for modal analysis.

Configuration 1

Figure 13 shows the SVD spectrum of configuration 1 captured the dynamic response of the bridge at ambient vibrations conditions induced by traffic flow, with significant peaks corresponding to the natural frequencies of the structure.

SVD spectrum for vertical direction of configuration 01.

Five dominant peaks were identified for further analysis, corresponding to modal frequencies of 4.0039 Hz, 4.4128 Hz, 8.2153 Hz, 13.3362 Hz, and 15.4480 Hz. These frequencies represent the most prominent vertical modes of the bridge. The first two frequencies, 4.0039 Hz and 4.4128 Hz that are closely spaced indicates the presence of closely coupled vertical modes. This phenomenon is typical in symmetric bridge structures, where modes of similar dynamic behaviour occur at frequencies in close proximity due to the structural symmetry39,40. The third mode, at 8.2153 Hz, represents a combination of vertical and localized effects influenced by structural features such as joints, bracings, or deck discontinuities41. These localized influences can create mode shapes that deviate from purely global behaviour, introducing subtle complexities to the dynamic responses. The fourth and fifth modes, at 13.3362 Hz and 15.4480 Hz, correspond to higher-order vertical modes, which typically involve more intricate vibration patterns. These modes provide valuable insights into the dynamic responses, particularly in regions susceptible to fatigue. Such a high-frequency modes are critical for evaluating the structural components of the bridge that respond to localized stresses and potentially influence long-term performance and durability. The presence of these dominant modes highlights the ability to capture the global and localized dynamic behaviour of the bridge under configuration 1.

Furthermore, investigations were conducted for the longitudinal (x) and transverse (y) directions to provide a comprehensive understanding of the dynamic characteristics of the bridge. The identified modal frequencies for these directions are presented in Fig. 14, seemingly, the SVD values of 0.1770 Hz, 13.3362 Hz and 26.6663 Hz with identical frequencies can be observed in both axes (i.e. x and y). The frequency overlap is due to the structural symmetry of the bridge, material properties such as stiffness and mass distribution in the longitudinal and transverse directions. However, the SVD values for the y-axis are significantly higher in the y-axis in comparison to the x-axis, indicating that the dynamic responses are greater in the transverse direction. This behaviour reflects the influence of the geometry of the bridge, specifically its low aspect ratio (1.35), resulting from its deck width (20.2 m) and length (27.36 m). Such a low aspect ratio tends to amplify transverse modal responses due to the increase relative flexibility and reduced stiffness in the transverse direction42,43 The prominent peaks at 13.33362 Hz and 26.6663 Hz are associated with higher-order global modes which can also be identified in the vertical direction, which could involve a combination of vertical bending and torsional motion. The differences in SVD values suggest that while the modal frequencies are identical, the dynamic participation of each direction varies.

Impact of sensor placement and modal characteristics

The placement of sensors plays a crucial role in accurately identifying the dynamic characteristics of a bridge, such as its natural frequencies and mode shapes. Sensor configurations determine the extent of coverage across the structure, directly influencing the ability to detect global and localized modes. A well-distributed sensor layout ensures the critical dynamic behaviours are captured comprehensively, whereas suboptimal placement may lead to incomplete or inaccurate representation of the modal properties of the structure. It is, therefore, essential to investigate how sensor placement influences the accurate identification of the modal characteristics of the bridge.

Natural frequency identification

Variations in sensor configurations can lead to differences in sensitivity to certain modes, especially when localized effects or asymmetric responses are involved. This is particularly significant for bridges, where the dynamic response is influenced by factors such as boundary conditions, stiffness distribution and structural geometry. Overall, the results demonstrate that sensor placement affects the identification of certain modal characteristics, particularly for modes influenced by localized effects. Partial-width configurations (2 and 3) are more sensitive to these dynamics, whereas full-width coverage (in configuration 1) captures robust global modes effectively. The dimensions of the bridge which are 27 m in length and 20 m in width, significantly influence the dynamic properties identified through different sensor configurations. In the full-width sensor placement (configuration 1), sensors were distributed across the 20 m width, resulting in relatively large spacing between sensors. This wide spacing may have limited the ability to detect finer localized effects or subtle transverse dynamics, as critical points along the width of the bridge could remain underrepresented. In contrast, partial-width configurations provided more focused sensor arrangement, enhancing the resolution of localized and transverse responses by covering smaller regions more effectively. This highlights the importance of selecting sensor layouts based on the objectives of the modal analysis, whether for global behaviour or localized dynamics.

Normalized displacement

The mode shapes corresponding to the identified natural frequencies are analysed for the further investigation of the dynamic characteristics of the bridge. The visualisations of each vertical mode shape on the sides of the bridge for the tested configurations have been illustrated in (Fig. 19).

The normalised displacement profiles for the mode shape 1 have a very similar pattern for the three sensor configurations. The profiles display a symmetric vertical bending mode, which is mostly known as the characteristic of the fundamental mode of the bridge. The displacement reaches its maximum near the mid span while the displacements decrease toward the supports as expected for a global vertical bending mode. It can be seen that the mode shapes obtained from all three configurations for mode 1 are similar whilst minor variations can observe in the normalised displacement values near the supports, which can be due to the differences in sensor placement and coverage, which might slightly influence the resolution of localized effects near the boundary conditions. Which also follows a symmetric deformation pattern with maximum displacements occurring near the mid-span of the bridge. When compared to mode 1, the curvature in the displacement profile is more pronounced, reflecting a higher frequency mode with greater stiffness influence. Also, in the identified mode shape across the length of the bridge at the displacements at the centreline of the bridge span are very small and closely similar in magnitude, indicating that the edges of the bridge exhibit significantly higher displacement values for this mode.

For mode shape 3, the normalised displacement profiles show a higher-order vertical bending mode with greater complexity compared to the mode shapes 1 and 2. FE model validation using FDD for different sensor configurations

FE model validation is identified as a critical step when the FE models of real structures are employed for simulation and analysis. A validated FE model ensures that the numerical representation accurately reflects the physical behaviour of the actual structure under given conditions while providing confidence in the ability of the model to predict the structural responses. For this study, the FE model of the bridge was validated using the FDD method. FDD is a widely used operational modal analysis technique that extracts modal parameters, such as natural frequencies and mode shapes, from measured vibration data under ambient excitation. To validate the FE model, a prestressed modal analysis was performed using Ansys. The process began by applying the prestressing force to the reinforcement using the static structural module in Ansys. Subsequently, a modal analysis was conducted on the same model to account for the effects of prestressing in the dynamic behaviour, thereby implementing a prestressed modal analysis. The natural frequencies of the first five mode shapes were determined through the Ansys analysis and compared with those obtained from the fieldwork analysis. Then, the locations of the accelerometers from the fieldwork were replicated on the numerical model, and the directional deformation at each location was extracted. These deformations were then used to compare the fieldwork and Ansys mode shapes by calculating the MAC matrix.

The natural frequencies corresponding to the first five mode shapes in the vertical direction are summarised in (Table 3), along with different factors, including the participation factor, effective mass, and cumulative mass fraction for each mode. This information was extracted from the solution data of the prestressed modal analysis conducted in Ansys. The participation factor provides information on the structural responses in different types of dynamic loading. A high participation factor indicated that a particular mode is more likely to be excited by forced in that direction. Depending on the mode shape and the participation factor, some modes contribute more significantly to the overall dynamic response than others. The effective mass, on the other hand, measures the amount of mass associated with each mode. The ratio of the effective mass to the total mass is a useful indicator to determine whether a sufficient number of modes have been extracted. Typically, the effective mass is less than the total mass because it only accounts for the contribution of each mode in a specific direction, rather than the complete structural distribution.

Mode shape clustering for sensor optimization: a comparison with experimental placement

As presented in the experimental procedure, ten accelerometers were used to test three sensor layouts to identify the dynamic properties of this bridge. This sensor density is commonly used for short-to-medium span bridges5. The choice of the number of sensors or the k in terms of clustering was guided by practical considerations such as installation constraints, cost limitations and data acquisition efficiency. It can be identified that a higher k or higher number of sensors improves spatial resolution while also increasing the data redundancy and computational burden, whereas a lower k may lead to insufficient coverage of modal responses11,46. Mainly, in this section, we detail sensor placement study that combines a synthetic experiment with mode shape clustering approaches for sensor optimization. The objective is twofold: first, to demonstrate the effectiveness of clustering-based sensor placement using a synthetic dataset that mimics a prestressed concrete bridge deck, and second, to investigate the impact of stress-informed weighting and experimental sensor configurations (full-width versus half-width) on modal identification accuracy. Figure 23 presents the step-by-step approach of the optimization strategy of sensor locations.

Optimization strategy of sensor locations.

This k-medoids optimisation algorithm successfully converged to a set of 10 optimal medoids, minimizing the average distances between substructure centroids and their nearest sensor. This ensures that the selected sensor locations capture essential structural dynamics while maintaining computational feasibility. The obtained medoid-based layout was further refined by simulated annealing (SA) to enhance the placement efficiency. Also, the k-median for the three sensor layouts were also calculated and compared with each other to identify the best sensor layout which could be used for concrete bridge validations while minimizing the effect of traffic conditions as much as possible.

The nodal coordinates used in this study were generated from the FEM of this bridge. Data were extracted from the effective area of the bridge deck, and nodes outside the high-stress or critical load regions were removed. This ensured that the sensor optimization focused on areas where the structural response was most significant. Prior to sensor placement, the dataset was filtered to remove outliers or missing values. When the variation in the vertical direction was minimal (less than 0.5 m), a dimension reduction was applied so that the data could be treated in two dimensions. The remaining nodes were then divided into grid-based substructures, such as 5 m by 5 m in two dimensions or 5 m by 5 m by 0.5 m in three dimensions, with each substructure represented by its centroid. This step helped reduce the computational load by summarizing the key features of the FEM data.

Optimisation of the sensor placement

The algorithm was implemented using Python for this process. The initial sensor locations were determined using the k-median (or k-medoids) algorithm. In this method, the centroids of the substructures are used, and the goal is to select ten medoids, or sensor positions, such that the sum of the Euclidean distances from each centroid to its nearest sensor is minimized. The algorithm began with a random selection of ten nodes as initial medoids, and the Euclidean distances between each node and the medoids were computed so that nodes could be assigned to the closest medoid. For each cluster, the medoid was updated by selecting the median node position, and this process was repeated until the medoids no longer changed. This uniform k-median configuration achieved an average distance of approximately 2.65 m.

After the initial sensor placement, simulated annealing was applied to refine the sensor locations. In this refinement process, sensor coordinates were slightly changed by adding a small amount of random noise, which was controlled by a temperature parameter. New configurations were accepted if they reduced the overall distance cost or, if not, with a probability that depended on the change in cost and the current temperature. The temperature was gradually reduced over 10,000 iterations. In this study, simulated annealing did not lead to significant improvements beyond the k-medoids solution, which suggests that the initial configuration was near optimal under the given conditions, see Fig. 24 for sensor locations.

Optimised sensor layout based on the k-median method.

A mode shape-based weighting approach was applied to account for variations in the structural response, particularly in high-stress regions to further enhance the sensor placement strategy. Although the k-median configuration produced a uniform sensor layout, it did not prioritize areas with higher dynamic significance as observed in the FE-derived mode shapes. In this phase, the total stress variation across the first five mode shapes was calculated for each node. The absolute stress values were added together and then normalized between 0 and 1 so that nodes with higher cumulative stress were given more importance. This stress-based weighting was then incorporated into the k-median algorithm to guide the sensor placement toward the critical regions. The final optimized placement of sensors incorporating stress weighting is presented in Fig. 25, showing a shift in sensor positions compared to the initial k-medoids clustering.

Optimised sensor layout based on the k-median method and stress variation.

It can be seen that the stress-based weighted k-median method has suggested the sensors to be placed in critical stress regions while the initial sensor configuration shows a more evenly spaced sensor layout. In the stress-informed placement, sensors have shifted towards structurally significant zones, prioritizing areas with high stress variations. For instance, the sensors initially placed at (1.99, 13.62) has shifted (2.76, 15.31) in the stress-weighted layout, aligning better with critical regions with respect to the identified stress variations in the first five mod shapes.

A further mathematical comparison was done in between stress-informed k-median layout and the uniform k-median layout to assess the effectiveness of stress-based sensor placement. The analysis results are presented in Table 5 covering k-median coverage, average sensor displacement, and convex hull area. The k-median coverage values were 2.65 m for the uniform layout while it was 1.11 m for the stress informed layouts, indicating that sensors in the stress-based approach were positioned close to critical regions47. It was also identified that the average sensor displacement between two layouts was 5.32 m and it suggests that a significant relocation of sensors were done to prioritize high-stress regions based on the mode shapes48. Additionally, as depicted in Fig. 26, the convex hull area for stress-informed layout was 206.01 m2compared to 215.32 m2 in the uniform layout. The reduced convex hull area of the stress-based layout suggests that it is more clustered around critical structural locations, ensuring better monitoring efficiency49,50.

Limitations and future work

While this study successfully validates the finite element (FE) model using fieldwork data and demonstrates the influence of sensor configurations on modal analysis, certain limitations are also identified. These limitations arise from the specific characteristics of the bridge, the fieldwork layout and the assumptions inherent in the modelling and the analysis process. Identifying these limitations is essential for understanding the scope of the findings and for paving the way for future research in this research area.

The findings of this study are based on a specific prestressed concrete bridge and the optimal sensor layout may not be directly applicable to other types of bridges with different structural characteristics, such as cable-stayed or suspension bridges. Hence, future research is required to assess the adaptability of the optimisation framework across a broader range of bridge configurations.

The optimal sensor configuration was presented based on mode shape-based stress weighting, which prioritized high-stress regions. Future studies should incorporate these additional loadings as well to assess the robustness of sensor placement.

Conclusion

The identification of the dynamic characteristics of a bridge is crucial for evaluating its current structural health, validating numerical models and conducting further analyses of them. However, this process poses significant challenges for operational structures due to the practical limitations of halting traffic. Additionally, an optimized sensor layout is critical to accurately capture modal characteristics while ensuring efficient data acquisition under real-world conditions. To address these challenges, this study investigated three distinct sensor configurations on a prestressed concrete bridge in New South Wales, Australia and ultimately, an optimal sensor layout is suggested to effectively capture dynamic responses of the bridge. The findings provide valuable insights into sensor optimization strategies while offering a practical framework for future structural health monitoring applications.

A key aspect of sensor placement in this study was the consideration of starting and ending offsets from the supports and these offsets were provided in order to avoid the regions near the supports where vibration amplitudes might be minimal due to boundary conditions. The sensor layouts were also optimised by prioritizing areas where modal displacements are more prominent to maximise sensitivity to dynamic responses while also maintaining consistent sensor spacing for accurate mode shape interpolation without excessive data redundancy. Since the study compares the full-width and half-width sensor layouts, it can be assessed their influence on both global and localised monitoring to identify the dynamic properties of the bridge.

Based on the estimation of modal parameters and numerical model validation of the bridge, it was identified that the sensor configurations influence the frequency identification accuracy, with the half-width configurations yielding to more consistent results with the numerical model. The full-width configuration demonstrated better performance in lower-order mode identification, whereas the other two configurations provided a more balanced representation across both lower and higher-order modes. MAC analysis also confirmed that the partial width configurations outperform the full-width configuration, achieving higher correlation coefficients and improved mode shape identification. The symmetrical behaviour of the bridge has mainly influenced these findings and the due to the asymmetrical mode shape identification of half-width configurations has led to their improved performance.

This study also demonstrates that combining finite element modelling with clustering-based sensor optimization techniques provides a strong framework for improving bridge health monitoring. By integrating a uniform k-median approach with stress-informed mode shape clustering, sensor placements can be fine-tuned to achieve both global and local monitoring goals. Overall, it is clear that the full-width layout provides better overall coverage, ensuring sensors span the entire bridge even though it is deviated from the optimal layout when compared to the half-width configurations. Also, the half-width layouts have achieved relatively low k-median values while improving a localised coverage. Hence, it is clear that the full-width layout is preferable if the global monitoring is the priority for the concrete bridge and the half-width coverages are recommended if the localised high-stress region monitoring is required. These findings clearly demonstrate that based on the monitoring objectives the sensor placement strategies should be carefully selected while balancing spatial coverage a local efficiency to ensure the optimal usage of sensors. However, this is adaptable for non-prestressed bridges such as steel or composite bridges as well by replacing the prestressed modal analysis step with a standard modal analysis approach. Since the study focused on ambient vibration data, the results may vary under different environmental and operational conditions. Furthermore, the analysis was limited to the first five mode shapes, leaving higher-order modes unexamined, which could play a critical role in capturing localised dynamic responses. Future work should explore these limitations by applying the methodology to a wider variety of bridge types, considering a broader range of environmental conditions, and incorporating higher-order modes into the analysis.
