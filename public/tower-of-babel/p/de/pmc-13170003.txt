Components of the Support-Education System of Orem’s Nursing Systems Theory in educational technologies for HIV prevention among adolescents and young people: a scoping review
Authors: Luzia Cibele de Souza Maximiano, Vanessa Carla do Nascimento Gomes Brito, Fernanda Belmiro de Andrade, Rogéria Moreira de Abrantes, Jocellem Alves de Medeiros, Raissa Martins de Andrade, Elanna Nayele de Freitas Costa, Aliete Cristina Gomes Dias Pedrosa da Cunha Oliveira, Alexsandra Rodrigues Feijão
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital educational technologies have been widely used in HIV prevention among adolescents and young people, offering opportunities for health education in educational and community settings. However, many interventions lack explicit grounding in nursing theories, limiting the understanding of how they promote autonomy, self-care agency, and decision-making, core concepts for nursing practice and education. The Supportive-Educative System of Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Nursing Theory provides a robust framework to guide and evaluate educational interventions, highlighting the role of nursing in promoting self-care. This study aimed to map and analyze the presence of components of the Supportive-Educative System in digital technologies aimed at HIV prevention among adolescents and young people.MethodsA scoping review was conducted according to the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and reported in accordance with PRISMA-ScR. Searches were performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, Embase, LILACS, SciELO, and gray literature, with no language or time restrictions. Two independent reviewers conducted study selection and data extraction. Findings were synthesized narratively using the PAGER framework, with Orem’s Supportive-Educative System as the analytical lens.ResultsA total of 10,149 records were found; of these, thirty-six studies were included. The analysis showed greater emphasis on components related to teaching and guidance for self-care, particularly regarding HIV prevention. Components related to decision-making, autonomy development, and therapeutic interaction were less frequent. Despite the absence of explicit reference to Orem’s Theory, many contents demonstrated implicit alignment with its principles.ConclusionsThe study shows that Orem’s Supportive-Educative System is a useful framework for analyzing and guiding digital technologies in HIV prevention, offering concrete contributions to nursing. The findings highlight the relevance of considering nursing theories in the analysis of digital health technologies, particularly in relation to autonomy and self-care. This perspective reinforces the conceptual contribution of nursing to discussions on digital health education.Keywords: HIV, Nursing, Prevention & control, Health education, Nursing theory

Background

HIV infection remains a global public health problem, especially among adolescents and young people, a group that historically presents greater vulnerability due to social, behavioral factors and inequalities in access to information and health services. Currently, 40.8 million people are living with HIV worldwide, 1.3 million new infections occurred in 2024, and 9.2 million people still do not have access to treatment [1]. Recent studies highlight the increase in HIV incidence among young people, which requires appropriate prevention strategies, with an emphasis on health education and the promotion of self-care [2].

Despite advances in biomedical prevention strategies, health education remains a central component in promoting preventive behaviors and self-care, particularly in this age group.

Health education, particularly aimed at the prevention of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and HIV, has been described as a central tool in reducing risks and empowering young people to adopt safe sexual practices. Educational interventions, including those conducted in school settings by health professionals, especially nurses, are described in the literature as strategies aimed at disseminating knowledge, promote awareness, and foster preventive behaviors [3].

In parallel, advances in information and communication technologies enable the development of digital educational technologies, such as applications, platforms, online courses, educational games, and videos, with great potential to reach adolescents and young people in an accessible, attractive, and context-adapted manner worldwide. These technologies can facilitate engagement, learning, and the adoption of HIV prevention behaviors, especially in contexts where stigma or structural barriers limit access to in-person care [4].

In this context, Dorothea Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Nursing Theory stands out as a particularly relevant theoretical framework for the analysis of educational interventions, as it proposes that nursing should act systematically in identifying self-care deficits and supporting the development of individual autonomy [5, 6]. The Supportive-Educative System, a central component of this theory, offers a conceptual structure that can inform the analysis of educational content related to self-care, decision-making, and the development of health-related skills. The theory is based on the premise that individuals have the desire and capacity to care for themselves, and that when there is a self-care deficit, the role of nursing is to provide support, guidance, and education to foster autonomy and self-care [7].

Within this theory, the Nursing Systems Theory includes the Supportive-Educative System, which assumes that the patient or health service user can or is able to learn to perform self-care but requires guidance and teaching from the nurse [8, 9]. This approach is aligned with the development of educational content and can be appropriately adapted to digital prevention technologies, such as those aimed at HIV/STI prevention among young people.

In addition, there is evidence that care models based on Orem’s framework have been used to structure educational nursing interventions, including those related to self-care in chronic conditions and situations of vulnerability [10, 11].

Although digital educational technologies have expanded significantly in HIV prevention strategies aimed at adolescents and young people, it is observed that a large proportion of these interventions lack explicit grounding in nursing theories. Most studies focus on outcomes of effectiveness, engagement, or technological acceptance, offering limited understanding of how these educational resources promote autonomy, self-care agency, and decision-making capacity in a structured manner elements central to nursing practice and knowledge [12, 13].

Existing scoping and systematic reviews predominantly address types of technologies, informational content, or behavioral outcomes of interventions, but rarely use nursing theoretical frameworks as an analytical lens. This absence limits the ability to understand the specific role of nursing in the design, implementation, and evaluation of educational health technologies.

Despite the relevance of Orem’s Supportive-Educative System for educational nursing practice, the systematization of knowledge regarding how its components are present in the content of educational technologies aimed at HIV prevention among adolescents and young people remains incipient.

However, the literature presents inconsistent approaches regarding the use of nursing and behavioral theoretical frameworks in digital health interventions for HIV prevention. While some studies highlight the benefits of theory-driven designs in supporting autonomy and self-care behaviors [14, 15], others report that many digital interventions are developed with limited or no explicit theoretical grounding [16, 17]. This inconsistency restricts a clearer understanding of the specific contribution of nursing in structuring such interventions and in promoting sustainable self-care behaviors, thereby justifying the need for the present study.This study aims to contribute systematically mapping and analyzing the incorporation of Dorothea Orem’s Nursing Systems Theory in educational technologies aimed at HIV prevention among adolescents and young people. In terms of practice, may inform nurses in planning educational interventions oriented toward self-care processes. For nursing education, the study highlights gaps in the articulation between theory and the development of educational technologies. In the field of health policy, the findings may inform discussions on the adoption of theoretically grounded educational technologies in HIV prevention programs.

In light of this gap, the present study aimed to map and analyze the presence of components of the Supportive-Educative System of Orem’s Nursing Systems Theory in the content of educational technologies aimed at HIV prevention among adolescents and young people.

Methods

Design

This study is a scoping review developed in accordance with the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [18] and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Reviewers’ Manual [19], with a research protocol registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) platform (10.17605/OSF.IO/G7MU6). Clinical trial number: Not applicable.

The following stages proposed by the JBI for conducting a scoping review were used: (1) development of the research question; (2) description of inclusion and exclusion criteria aligned with the research question; (3) planning of the search, selection, extraction, and presentation of evidence; (4) searching for evidence; (5) selection of evidence; (6) extraction of evidence; and (7) analysis, presentation, and synthesis of results [19].

Search strategy

For the development of the research question, the PCC mnemonic was adopted: P (Population): adolescents and young people; C (Concept): components of the Supportive-Educative System of Orem’s Nursing Systems Theory applied to educational technologies for HIV prevention; C (Context): health educational technologies aimed at HIV prevention [19]. The guiding question of this review was: “Which components of the Supportive-Educative System of Orem’s Nursing Systems Theory are present in educational technologies aimed at HIV prevention among adolescents and young people?”

The following databases were consulted: Nursing Database (BDENF), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Cochrane Library, Excerpta Medica Database (EMBASE), Web of Science Core Collection, PubMed/MEDLINE, Elsevier Journals Collection (ScienceDirect), Library and Archives Canada (Theses Canada), Elsevier Abstract and Citation Database (Scopus), Scientific Electronic Library Online (SciELO), and Latin American and Caribbean Literature in Health Sciences (LILACS) [20]. Gray literature sources were also searched: Google Scholar, the Brazilian Ministry of Health website, the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) website, and the Portuguese Directorate-General of Health (DGS).

It is noteworthy that terms related to nursing theories or Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Theory were not included in the search strategy, since studies on educational technologies for HIV prevention are rarely indexed with nursing theoretical frameworks. The theoretical analysis was performed a posteriori, using Orem’s Supportive-Educative System as an analytical lens to classify and interpret the identified educational contents, an approach compatible with the scoping review design [19].

The formulation of the research question was guided by Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Nursing Theory, considering the Supportive-Educative System as the theoretical framework for analyzing the educational contents identified in technologies aimed at HIV prevention for adolescents. Eligibility criteria

For the purposes of this study, educational technologies were defined as interventions mediated by digital technologies or technological resources (such as mobile applications, online platforms, educational games, interactive videos, or digital courses) developed with the objective of promoting structured health education and supporting HIV prevention behaviors.

Primary studies, as well as integrative and systematic reviews, with no temporal or language restrictions, were included if they answered the research question and addressed health education interventions for HIV prevention targeted at adolescents, including educational technologies. Studies whose target population corresponded to the adolescent age group and that presented results relevant to the scope of the review were considered eligible. Reflection articles, experience reports, editorials, abstracts published in conference proceedings, as well as studies whose population did not correspond to the defined age group or whose focus was not related to HIV prevention, were excluded.

Selection process

After the selection and determination of descriptors and keywords, searches were conducted for each database and repository. Access was obtained through the CAPES Journal Portal, via the CAFe platform, a service that facilitates access to digital benefits through the login used for the respective registered university, the Federal University of Rio Grande do Norte. During this stage, when studies were not available through the selected access route in the CAPES Portal, the following steps were taken: (1) search for the study in Google Scholar; (2) search for the study via ResearchGate; and (3) request of the article directly from the authors using the provided contact information.

Data collection occurred between October and December 2025, conducted independently and in duplicate by two reviewers who were members of the research group. Only the start time was predetermined, with completion defined after exhaustion of the initiated cross-search. References were imported into a reference manager (Rayyan software) to identify duplicates.

For the search process, indexed descriptors from the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were used for English-language databases, and Descriptors in Health Sciences (DeCS) were used for Latin American repositories. The search strategy was adapted using truncations and customized field codes for each database to optimize search syntax.

Initially, studies were screened by reading titles and abstracts to verify adherence to the theme, research question, and eligibility criteria. Studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded at this stage. Subsequently, potentially eligible articles were selected for full-text reading, culminating in the final selection of studies included in the review. Disagreements during the selection process were resolved through evaluation by a third researcher.

For data extraction, the JBI Template Source of Evidence Details, Characteristics and Results Extraction Instrument was used, including information relevant to the construction of review evidence, such as author, year of publication, methodological design, and study population. Additionally, to meet the objective and research question, specific fields were included to identify the types of educational technologies used, the HIV prevention topics most frequently addressed among adolescents and young people, and the patterns of the Supportive-Educative System of Orem’s Nursing Systems Theory.

Data synthesis

After selecting the final sample, the data obtained were organized and presented descriptively in a table with the following variables: article title, year of publication, country, objective, method, social network/media addressed, content about HIV/AIDS, and data sources. The results were analyzed using descriptive statistics.

Furthermore, the PAGER methodology was adopted, proposed with the aim of providing a more in-depth analysis of the results and improving the quality of reports in scoping reviews. This approach seeks to increase the rigor of the synthesis through a structured evaluation based on five components: Patterns, Advances, Gaps, Evidence for Practice, and Research Recommendations [21].

The application of the PAGER framework was integrated with the thematic analysis guided by Orem’s Support-Education System. Initially, the educational content of the identified technologies was categorized according to the components of the support-education system. Then, for each component, the recurring patterns (Patterns), the identified advances (Advances), the existing gaps (Gaps), the evidence for nursing practice (Evidence for practice), and the recommendations for future research (Research recommendations) were analyzed, allowing for a structured and theoretically grounded analytical synthesis. No analyses of the effectiveness of the interventions were performed.

In accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute’s recommendations for scoping reviews, no critical appraisal of the methodological quality of the included evidence sources was performed, since the objective of this study was to map and describe the available body of evidence, and not to evaluate the effectiveness or quality of the studies.

This review addresses HIV primary prevention strategies, focusing on digital educational technologies designed to promote risk reduction, autonomy, and self-care among adolescents and young people.

Results

A total of 10,149 records were found; of these, 340 were removed before screening because they were duplicates. Therefore, 9,809 articles were screened for title and abstract reading, with 9,581 being excluded. Thus, 228 articles were selected for full-text reading; of these, 99 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria and 93 were excluded for not answering the study’s research question. The temporal analysis of the included articles revealed a progressive and significant growth in scientific production on health education support systems over the last few decades. Most studies (23 articles; 63.8% of 36 articles) were published between 2010 and 2024, a period marked by advances in digital technologies, greater access to health information, and the increasing value placed on patient-centered self-care. Between 1990 and 2009, 11 articles were published (30.5% of 36 articles), reflecting an initial phase of theoretical and methodological consolidation, when the first models of health education were established and the role of nurses as facilitators of self-care was recognized. Only 2 articles (5.6% of 36 articles) were published before 1990, indicating that the initial studies were sparse and predominantly exploratory, often restricted to specific or experimental contexts. This overview suggests that the evolution of academic interest is related to factors such as global health policies, the dissemination of scientific evidence, and the integration of technologies in patient care.

This temporal evolution also reveals that, although the theme has ancient roots, recent scientific investment has been decisive in consolidating and diversifying approaches based on Orem’s support-education system.

The analysis of the geographical origin of the articles revealed that the United States leads in scientific production, with 22 studies (61.1% of 36 articles), followed by African countries, including South Africa, Uganda and Kenya, with 7 articles (19.4% of 36 articles). Other countries, such as Vietnam, India, Brazil, Canada, Mexico, Thailand, Colombia, and Ecuador, totaled 8 articles (22.2% of 36 articles), demonstrating a more heterogeneous distribution and representing distinct cultural and socioeconomic contexts.

Analysis of the methodological design showed that cross-sectional studies were the most frequent, totaling 16 articles (44.4% of 36 articles), followed by randomized clinical trials with 10 articles (27.7% of 36 articles), highlighting the strong presence of rigorous experimental interventions in the evaluation of educational technologies, especially in structured programs based on the Orem model. Qualitative studies accounted for 6 articles (16.6% of 36 articles), quasi-experimental studies for 2 articles (5.6% of 36 articles), cohort studies for 1 article (2.8% of 36 articles), and literature reviews for 1 article (2.8% of 36 articles). This distribution demonstrates that, although there is methodological plurality, cross-sectional studies and clinical trials predominate, for a deeper understanding of the characteristics and processes of educational interventions.

Regarding the origin of the articles, 13 studies (36.1% of 36 articles) were located in Scopus, 11 studies (30.5% of 36 articles) in PubMed, 6 studies (16.6% of 36 articles) in ScienceDirect, 5 studies (13.8% of 36 articles) in the Cochrane Library, and 1 study (2.8% of 36 articles) in the Web of Science.

The predominance of articles found in Scopus and PubMed highlights the relevance of these databases for nursing and health research, ensuring access to peer-reviewed journals and studies with methodological rigor. The lower representation of Cochrane and ScienceDirect indicates that systematic reviews and synthesis studies are still less frequent in this field, possibly due to the complexity and cost of controlled clinical trials. These data emphasize the importance of searching multiple databases to minimize selection bias and ensure the comprehensiveness of systematic reviews, strengthening the scientific basis of educational support interventions.

The predominance of studies conducted in the United States may be understood within a broader contextual framework. The country has historically played a central role in the development and early adoption of digital educational technologies in health, supported by advanced technological infrastructure, substantial research funding, and a strong emphasis on preventive health strategies. In addition, nursing theories, including Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Theory, originated and were widely disseminated within the U.S. academic and healthcare context, which may have facilitated their incorporation into educational and digital health interventions. Together, these factors contribute to the higher concentration of studies conducted in this setting. This does not necessarily imply greater effectiveness, but rather reflects structural, historical, and academic conditions that favor research production in this field.

Overall, these findings provide a descriptive overview of the current evidence and serve as a basis for a more in-depth theoretical interpretation within the framework of Orem’s support–education system, as discussed below.

Discussion

The synthesis of the findings was organized in light of Orem’s Supportive Education System Theory, allowing the identification of patterns, advances, and gaps in how the educational content of the analyzed technologies incorporates components related to autonomy, self-care, and decision-making. Axis I: Capacity for self-care with need for guidance

The axis “capacity for self-care with a need for guidance” was identified in 32 studies (88.9%), highlighting its centrality in educational interventions focused on self-care. From the perspective of Orem’s Systems Theory, this component integrates the Support-Education System, whose objective is to address specific deficits of the individual, offering structured guidance that enables the patient to develop progressive autonomy in self-care practices.

The Supportive Education System proposes that the nurse’s intervention should be planned according to the identified deficit, adapting the level of guidance to the patient’s degree of independence. Recent studies show that structured interventions, which combine self-care assessment with targeted educational strategies, are described in the literature as addressing patients’ ability to manage their clinical condition and engage in self-care-related activities [22].

Furthermore, focusing on self-care deficits allows the nurse to identify specific needs that vary between individuals and health conditions, ensuring that guidance is personalized. This systematic approach is characteristic of the Supportive Education System, in which care is based on continuous assessment, feedback, and monitoring of the patient’s self-care skills.

The predominant presence of this axis in the studies suggests that the literature recognizes guidance as the first critical step in the nursing education process, reinforcing that the success of self-care depends on the balance between the guidance provided and the patient’s ability to assimilate and apply it.

Finally, the integration of the guidance axis with other components of the Support-Education System, such as skills development and decision support, is described in the literature as supporting educational interventions aimed at autonomy and competence in complex health contexts [23].

Axis II: Nurse as an educational agent

The “nurse as an educational agent” axis was identified in 26 studies (72.2%), being one of the pillars of the Support-Education System in Orem’s Systems Theory. Within this system, the nurse assumes the role of a central educational agent, responsible for analyzing self-care deficits, planning educational interventions, and facilitating the internalization of health-related behaviors. This perspective expands the nurse’s traditional role beyond that of a simple transmitter of information, reinforcing their function as a mediator in the patient’s learning and empowerment process for self-care.

Studies have shown that educational interventions conducted by nurses are reported not only as aiming to promote knowledge but also as positively influencing self-care behaviors in various population groups, such as patients with diabetes mellitus, the elderly, and people with chronic conditions. For example, nurse-led educational interventions have been described as reporting improvements in adherence to self-care and therapeutic regimens in individuals with diabetes, showing that nursing education is described as contributing to the modification of health-related and treatment-related behaviors [24].

Contemporary literature on health education describes nurses, when acting as educational agents, as contributing to the support of self-care agency through interventions that combine technical-scientific knowledge with communication skills, motivation, and competency development. These characteristics are discussed in the literature as relevant to educational actions aimed at supporting behavioral change processes, without implying direct or measured effects on health outcomes [25, 26].

It is important to note that most of the included studies did not explicitly adopt Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Theory as a guiding framework. Rather, the analytical approach used in this review identified conceptual convergence between certain intervention components and elements of Orem’s Supportive-Educative System. This alignment should not be interpreted as intentional theoretical application, but as an a posteriori analytical interpretation based on nursing theory. Distinguishing between interventions explicitly grounded in nursing theory and those that incidentally align with its principles is essential to avoid overinterpretation and to accurately represent the theoretical contribution of nursing within multidisciplinary digital health interventions.

Axis III: Cooperative and interactive relationship

The cooperative and interactive relationship between nurse and patient, although explicitly identified in only 3 studies in the sample (8.3%), constitutes an essential component of the Interactive Support-Education System in Orem’s Systems Theory. This dimension goes beyond the simple exchange of information; it represents the construction of a dynamic and mutual relationship in which nurse and patient engage in a process of continuous communication, establishing common goals and negotiating self-care strategies. In terms of systems theory, this interaction is seen as part of the operational processes in the educational support subsystem, where the patient’s response to the educational stimulus and their active participation influence care-related processes.

Within nursing practice, therapeutic and interactive communication between nurses and patients is discussed in the literature as relevant to health-related processes. Concepts such as patient-centered communication and therapeutic communication emphasize reciprocal interactions that are responsive to patients’ concerns and guided by mutual trust and respect. These approaches are discussed in the literature as facilitating patients’ understanding, engagement, and active participation in self-care processes. Attributes such as information exchange, mutual respect, and engagement are described as pillars of collaborative relationships which, within Orem’s framework, are discussed as facilitating the internalization of self-care behaviors and the progressive autonomy of individuals [27, 28].

From a systems perspective, the cooperative relationship should be understood as a continuous and adaptive process in which the nurse adjusts their educational strategies to the patient’s verbal and nonverbal responses [28, 29].

Although this axis was less frequently represented in the analyzed studies, the cooperative and interactive relationship warrants greater attention due to its conceptual relevance for research and clinical practice, as it represents the human dimension of care and is conceptually linked to other components of the Supportive–Educative System.

A well-established cooperative relationship facilitates not only the acquisition of knowledge but also the internalization and practical application of this knowledge in self-care routines, consolidating patient autonomy. In short, strengthening this interpersonal relationship may theoretically support educational processes in nursing educational interventions, which is discussed in the literature as relevant to patient engagement and perceptions of care [27].

Axis IV: Support for decision-making

The “decision-making support” axis was identified in 10 articles (27.8% of the sample). Although less prevalent than other components, it is described in the literature as relevant to self-care processes and patient autonomy.

The Support Education System is based on the idea that, in order for patients to effectively manage their health, they must be enabled to weigh therapeutic options, understand risks and benefits, and integrate this information with their preferences and values. This requires not only the transmission of knowledge but also critical guidance that fosters the ability to make well-informed decisions based on scientific evidence and personal context [30].

Contemporary international literature on decision-making in nursing describes decision support as a relevant component for patient autonomy and quality of care. The concept of shared decision-making (SDM), widely examined in recent decades, refers to a collaborative process in which healthcare professionals and patients exchange information, discuss therapeutic options, and consider values and preferences when making care-related decisions. Qualitative studies describe this patient-centered approach—characterized by open communication, dialogue, and consideration of mutual perspectives—as influencing patients’ perceptions of being “seen and understood,” which is discussed in the literature as favoring active participation in self-care decisions and health management [31].

Furthermore, recent research in different clinical contexts shows that training nurses in decision-support skills, including active listening, clear presentation of options, and support for patient deliberation, facilitates the inclusion of patients in decisions about their treatments, contributing to more informed choices that are aligned with their life goals [32].

Even in complex situations, such as the management of chronic conditions like HIV or end-of-life interventions, structured decision-support models have been associated with greater alignment between the therapeutic plan and patient preferences, reinforcing that decision support goes beyond the transmission of knowledge and encompasses communicative and interactive practices that empower the patient in the decision-making process [33, 34].

Axis V: Support for developing practical and intellectual skills

The axis “support for developing practical and intellectual skills” was identified in 9 of the 36 studies (25.0%) and corresponds to a fundamental component of the Education Support System, which emphasizes not only the transmission of knowledge but also the development of skills that allow the patient to apply this knowledge practically and in self-care activities. This support includes both practical skills, such as performing health procedures, using medical devices, taking continuous medication, and monitoring clinical signs, as well as intellectual skills, including clinical reasoning, critical thinking, and the ability to integrate theoretical concepts with real care/risk situations.

Nursing education literature aligns with literature that discusses interventions combining theory and practice, such as clinical simulations, problem-based learning, and authentic practice environments. These are described as approaches aimed at improving performance in self-care tasks and supporting decision-making processes. High-fidelity simulations and clinical practice centers allow learners to experience near-real-life scenarios with immediate feedback and are discussed as contributing to perceptions of self-confidence and the transfer of skills to the real-world care context. Important studies show that simulation-based training is described not only as addressing technical skills but also as addressing critical thinking and problem-solving, essential aspects for intellectual competence in self-care [35, 36].

The integrated development of practical and intellectual skills is aligned with pedagogical approaches that include experiential learning and active learning, such as problem-based learning and the use of hybrid methods that combine educational technologies with supervised clinical practice. International literature suggests that such methods are described as contributing to the consolidation of more complex skills, such as the interpretation of clinical results and continuous assessment of self-care needs [37].

In the context of the Education Support System, the nurse’s role in this area is to act as a facilitator of practical and intellectual learning, offering structured didactic experiences and continuous support that link theoretical content to real practice. This includes structured feedback, progressive assessment of skills, and encouragement of critical thinking. Strategies such as the simulation of complex self-care situations, clinical case studies, and clinical reasoning exercises help the patient not only to perform tasks but also to understand the underlying reasons for their self-care choices, promoting greater autonomy and adaptability [38].

Furthermore, the development of practical and intellectual skills is discussed in the literature as theoretically associated with health-related processes, particularly in relation to how individuals engage with self-care activities and respond to changes in their health condition. International literature describes competency-based approaches, which combine practical, simulated, and reflective components, as addressing self-care processes without implying measured effectiveness or long-term outcomes [39].

Although most of the educational technologies analyzed did not explicitly adopt Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Theory, the findings indicate that several central elements of the Supportive Education System were implicitly incorporated into the educational content. These elements manifested predominantly through informative and instructional strategies, while components related to interaction, shared decision-making, skills development, and experiential learning were less frequent. This pattern suggests a conceptual convergence with the theoretical framework, but applied in a partial and poorly structured manner.

The predominance of instructional approaches highlights a persistent trend of prioritizing the transmission of information in digital technologies aimed at HIV prevention, which may limit the potential of these interventions to support autonomy, self-care processes, and youth empowerment. A broader and more intentional incorporation of the different components of the Support-Education System can expand the capacity of educational technologies to support more dialogical, participatory, and skills-oriented educational processes, especially among adolescents and young people.

Orem’s Theory can be considered a conceptual reference in educational technologies for HIV prevention in adolescents, providing a structured framework for the development of self-care-centered interventions. By identifying which components of the support-education system are present or absent, nurses can plan theoretically informed educational strategies, app designers can structure functionalities that promote engagement and autonomy, and prevention program managers can integrate these technologies into consistent educational policies and protocols.

In terms of practical implications, interventions anchored in Orem may inform reflections on clinical practice, the design of digital solutions, and the management of prevention programs. For future research, it is recommended to evaluate the implementation and effectiveness of these technologies, as well as to explore the perceptions of adolescents and health professionals, investigating factors that influence engagement, usability, and impact on the adoption of self-care behaviors. These lines of investigation can support the development of theoretically grounded, user-centered educational solutions with potential relevance for public health discussions.

In this sense, the findings of this review broaden the understanding of how nursing theories can be used as analytical and guiding frameworks in the field of digital health, highlighting opportunities for the conceptual and methodological improvement of educational technologies in HIV. These findings highlight the potential of Orem’s Support-Education System as a conceptual framework to inform the planning and analysis of educational interventions, serving as a theoretical basis capable of qualifying the planning, evaluation, and innovation of educational interventions developed or implemented by nurses in different contexts.

This scoping review has some limitations that should be acknowledged. Although a comprehensive search strategy was conducted across multiple databases and grey literature sources, some relevant studies may not have been identified. In particular, terms explicitly related to nursing theories were not included in the search strategy, as the theoretical analysis was conducted a posteriori based on the interpretation of the included studies. As a result, it is possible that studies explicitly grounded in Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Theory but not using specific keywords related to digital educational technologies were not captured.

Additionally, the inclusion of studies in different languages may have led to variations in the interpretation and reporting of data. The heterogeneity of methodological designs, study populations, and interventions also limits the generalizability of the findings. Finally, as this is a scoping review, no methodological quality assessment of the included studies was performed, which may affect the robustness of the conclusions.

Conclusion

In summary, the findings of this review demonstrate that the Support-Education System of Orem’s Nursing Systems Theory is conceptually compatible with educational technologies aimed at HIV prevention among adolescents and young people. However, its application remains predominantly restricted to instructional components, with less incorporation of elements related to interaction, decision-making, skills development, and youth empowerment.

From a nursing practice perspective, the findings indicate patterns described in the literature suggest that nurses involved in the development or implementation of educational technologies may consider adopting the different components of the educational support system, ensuring that interventions are not only informative but also participatory, practical, experiential, and geared towards strengthening autonomy and self-care.

The integration of nursing theories, digital technologies, and participatory methodologies is discussed in the literature as relevant to health education initiatives targeting adolescents and young people.

Within the scope of research, this study contributes to filling a theoretical gap by highlighting the applicability of Orem’s Support-Education System as an analytical and guiding framework for educational technologies in HIV. It is recommended that studies explicitly use nursing theories as a conceptual basis, as well as longitudinal evaluations that investigate the evolution of self-care agency. These approaches can may inform theoretically grounded digital interventions and consolidate the role of nursing as a protagonist in innovation in digital health education.
