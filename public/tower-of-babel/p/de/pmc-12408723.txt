A systematic review of protein post-translational modifications in sepsis
Authors: Lishun Yang, Zhuang’e Shi, Fuping Wang, Guoyun Shi, Couwen Li, Guobing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis originates from the host's dysregulated response to pathogens, and its pathophysiological mechanisms are extremely complex. Recent research has found that post-translational modifications (PTMs) can regulate gene transcription without altering the genetic sequence, thereby playing a key role in the occurrence and development of sepsis.ObjectiveThis review aims to systematically categorize the main types of PTMs and elucidate their roles in the pathogenesis of sepsis, thereby providing new perspectives for a deeper understanding of the complex pathophysiological processes of this disease.MethodsWe searched databases including PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), covering the period from their establishment to July 2025. The search strategy combined keywords related to "sepsis" (such as "sepsis", "septic shock") and "post-translational modifications" (such as "PTM", "lactylation", "acetylation", "methylation", "phosphorylation", "ubiquitination", "glycosylation"). After removing duplicates and low-quality literature, the remaining articles were analyzed and summarized to ultimately complete the writing of this review.ResultsPTMs exert profound influences on the inflammatory process, immune cell function, cell death modes, and energy metabolism in sepsis by regulating key effector molecules and signaling pathways. Research indicates that PTMs play a dual role in this process, which can either exert protective effects or lead to destructive outcomes.ConclusionPTMs represent a core regulatory mechanism in the pathophysiology of sepsis. A comprehensive understanding of the functions and interactions of various PTMs is of great significance for profoundly elucidating the complexity of sepsis and developing novel therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Sepsis, Protein post-translational modifications, Immune, Pathophysiology

Introduction

Sepsis is a critical medical condition triggered by serious infections and is associated with high rates of disease complications and fatal outcomes [1]. Although medical knowledge continues to advance, the fundamental pathological processes of sepsis are still not fully understood. Nevertheless, investigations into sepsis pathogenesis have achieved notable breakthroughs, especially in epigenetic research [2, 3]. These findings are essential to deepen our understanding of sepsis and identify new therapeutic targets.

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) are inheritable epigenetic alterations that are vital to both normal biological processes and disease states. They regulate protein structure, function, and localization; enhance protein diversity; and modulate interactions with other cellular molecules [4, 5]. Nearly 70% of proteins in biological systems experience PTMs, with the prevalent forms encompassing phosphorylation, acetylation, methylation, ubiquitination, and lactylation [6]. Aberrant PTMs have been associated with multiple pathological conditions, including malignancies [7], senescence-related disorders [8], neurodegenerative diseases [9], inflammatory diseases [10], infectious diseases [11], and so on.

Emerging data indicate that PTMs significantly contribute to sepsis pathogenesis by dynamically and reversibly modifying pivotal signaling proteins that govern inflammatory response, immune system imbalance, and cellular demise [12]. In this review, we examined the pathophysiological processes involving PTMs in sepsis.

Data collection

To comprehensively evaluate the role of PTMs in sepsis, we conducted a systematic literature search. Databases including PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and other relevant sources such as China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) were searched from their inception to July 2025. The search strategy employed a combination of keywords related to “sepsis” (e.g., “sepsis”, “septic shock”) and “post-translational modifications” (e.g., “PTM”, “Lactylation”, “acetylation”, “methylation”, “phosphorylation”, “ubiquitination”, “glycosylation”). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) original research articles; (2) studies directly investigating the mechanisms or functions of PTMs in sepsis or sepsis-related models; and (3) publications in peer-reviewed journals. Exclusion criteria included conference abstracts, reviews, and articles for which the full text was unavailable. Literature screening was performed independently by two authors, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Key information was ultimately extracted from the eligible studies and narratively synthesized.

Lactylation and sepsis

Lactate is a key metabolite produced during glucose glycolysis [14]. In sepsis, hyperlactatemia is strongly correlated with disease severity and patient prognosis [15]. Lactate was first discovered in muscle tissues and has been shown to have a variety of metabolic functions [16, 17]. Recent studies on the novel signaling functions of metabolic intermediates have promoted our understanding of potential metabolic regulatory mechanisms [18]. During sepsis, intense cytokine storms lead to profound metabolic adaptations in immune cells to support host defense requirements, a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect [19]. This process significantly increases lactate production, leading to elevated lactoyl group formation [14]. Increased numbers of lactyl groups are transferred to specific amino acid residues of target proteins via lactyltransferases, a process termed acylation modification [20]. Protein lactylation regulates gene expression, cellular metabolism, and immune responses by modifying chromatin structure and function [21]. The degree of lactification depends on the level of lactate inside and outside the cells, as well as regulation by glycolytic enzymes [22, 23].

Zhang et al. were the first to report histone lysine lactylation, an epigenetic modification derived from lactate metabolism. Their findings revealed that such lactylation markedly changes chromatin configuration and enhances transcriptional activity [21]. The team later confirmed the presence of lactylation in non-histone proteins, further expanding its role in gene expression regulation [24]. Protein lactylation has gained increasing attention in sepsis research as an emerging PTM. Recent studies have continued to uncover the various regulatory mechanisms involved in the pathogenesis of sepsis. (Table 1)

Roles of lactylation in sepsis

Histone lactylation and sepsis

Current research on histone lactylation mainly focuses on histone H3, particularly on lactylation modification at the Histone H3 lysine 18(H3K18). Most studies suggest that histone lactylation has harmful effects in sepsis, which are shown in the following aspects. One major finding is that H3K18 lactylation worsens the inflammatory response in sepsis by activating the inflammatory pathways. In sepsis-induced kidney injury models, lactylation at the H3K18 site activates the Ras homolog family member A(RhoA)/Rho-associated coiled-coil containing protein kinase 1 (ROCK1) and Nuclear Factor Kappa B (NF-κB) pathways. This activation causes a significant increase in pro-inflammatory cytokine production (such as TNF-α 、 IL-1β), leading to stronger inflammatory responses and kidney damage [25]. Another important aspect is that H3K18 lactylation can trigger ferroptosis and disrupt cellular balance. P300-mediated H3K18 lactylation regulates the Methyltransferase-Like3 (METTL3)/Acyl-CoA Synthetase Long-Chain Family Member 4(ACSL4) axis, causing ferroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells [26]. Moreover, Histone H3 lysine 14 (H3K14) lactylation is involved in sepsis. H3K14 lactylation can also induce ferroptosis. Gong et al. found that lactate-dependent H3K14 lactylation accumulates in the promoter regions of ferroptosis-related genes, thereby promoting ferroptosis in lung endothelial cells. This leads to blood vessel problems during sepsis-induced lung injury and worsening of organ damage [27]. Furthermore, H3K18 lactylation breaks down the endothelial glycocalyx and damages the vascular barrier. H3K18 lactylation increases heparanase (HPSE) production in lung endothelial cells by activating downstream Early Growth Response Protein 1 (EGR1) transcription. HPSE breaks down the glycocalyx of lung blood vessels and damages the vascular barrier, allowing inflammatory cells and mediators to leak out and worsen lung injury [28].

While most studies indicate that histone lactylation has harmful effects on sepsis, some studies present contrasting findings. For instance, in sepsis caused by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), researchers have observed metabolic reprogramming in macrophages that increased H3K18 lactylation levels. This modification enhances the expression of Arg1, promoting macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. Given the immunomodulatory and pro-repair attributes characteristic of M2 macrophage polarization, this lactylation mechanism appears to reduce inflammatory responses in septic mice and may provide protective benefits [29].

Non-histone lactylation and sepsis

Lactylation of non-histone proteins also plays a significant regulatory role in sepsis. Some studies have suggested that the lactylation modification of specific non-histone proteins may exacerbate inflammatory responses. High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1), a chromatin-associated nuclear protein, undergoes extracellular release following cellular injury or stress [30]. In 2021, Yangkun et al. showed that lactylation is crucial for HMGB1 translocation from the nucleus to the cytoplasm [31]. Further research further supported these findings. Experimental evidence from septic mouse models has identified lactate as the driving factor for HMGB1 lactylation in M1-polarized macrophages. Lactylated HMGB1 then exits the nucleus via exosomes, activating the Cyclic GMP-AMP Synthase (cGAS)/Stimulator of Interferon Genes(STING) pathway. This activation induces neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, ultimately worsening sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SAKI) [32]. Additionally, the extracellular release of lactylated HMGB1 through exosomes can increase endothelial permeability and dysfunction [31]. This process may redistribute tight junction proteins between endothelial cells through specific signaling pathways, enhancing vascular permeability and inflammatory cell extravasation. However, its exact mechanism of action remains unclear. Non-histone lactylation disrupts mitochondrial function. In SAKI, elevated Fission 1 protein (Fis1) lactylation at lysine 20 (K20) causes excessive mitochondrial fission in the renal tubular cells. This leads to ATP depletion, excessive mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) production, and apoptosis, further aggravating SAKI [33].

Collectively, lactylation modifications exhibit bidirectional regulatory effects on sepsis pathogenesis. On the one hand, it can worsen organ damage by activating inflammatory pathways, inducing cell death, and disrupting vascular barriers. However, it may reduce inflammation and provide protection by promoting M2 macrophage polarization. These opposing effects suggest that the role of lactylation likely depends on the sepsis type, disease stage, cell type, and specific downstream molecules. Current lactylation research has primarily examined histone H3, while other histones remain to be explored. In addition to HMGB1 and Fis1, other non-histone proteins may also undergo lactylation and influence sepsis progression. However, this research field remains in its early stages, and the complete regulatory network requires further investigation.

Acetylation and sepsis

Protein acetylation is a biochemical process in which an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA is transferred to histones or other protein residues [34]. This reaction is controlled by two enzyme groups: acetyltransferases (Lysine Acetyltransferases, KATs), catalyzing acetylation reactions, and deacetylases (Histone Deacetylases, HDACs; Silent Information Regulator Two-s, SIRTs) that mediate deacetylation processes, making the system dynamically reversible [35]. Acetylation exists in three distinct forms - Nα-acetylation, Nε-acetylation, and O-acetylation–differentiated by the specific attachment sites of acetyl groups [36]. Acetylation plays a key role in controlling gene activity and affects various cellular processes, including metabolism, cell growth, proliferation, and autophagy [34, 37]. As a major type of protein modification after translation, acetylation is particularly important in sepsis, influencing inflammation, metabolic changes, and autophagy [38]. Recent studies have significantly advanced our understanding of how acetylation contributes to the development of sepsis through multiple mechanisms. (Table 2)

Roles of acetylation in sepsis

Acetylation and inflammation

Acetylation promotes inflammatory processes in sepsis via multiple pathways. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) serve as essential pattern recognition molecules that mediate crucial signaling cascades in sepsis pathogenesis [39]. They are responsible for sensing the Lipopolysaccharide(LPS) components of gram-negative bacteria and triggering downstream inflammatory signals [40]. The lysine acetyltransferase, CREB-Binding Protein (CBP), is recruited to the TLR4 signaling complex, leading to enhanced acetylation of the TIR domain within the TLR4 signaling pathway. This modification triggers NF-κB pathway activation, leading to a pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage state [41]. Lys310 acetylation in the Nuclear Factor Kappa B Subunit p65 transactivation domain potently amplifies the pro-inflammatory activity of NF-κB signaling [42]. Subsequently, abnormal overactivation of the inflammasome (NLRP3) leads to early excessive inflammation in sepsis [43]. The function of NLRP3 depends on its acetylation [44]. Additionally, acetylation of Transforming Growth Factor Beta-Induced Protein (TGFBIp) is essential for its secretion and induces acute systemic inflammation [45]. In addition, histone acetylation increases NMDA Receptor Subtype 2B (NR2B) and Interleukin-1 Receptor (IL-1R) levels, worsening brain inflammation and impairing sensory function in sepsis-related brain injury (SE) [46].

While most studies have highlighted the pro-inflammatory effects of acetylation in sepsis, emerging evidence suggests that it may also play a protective role by modulating inflammatory responses. Fang et al. demonstrated that HDAC3 inhibition maintains acetylation at the K218 site of the NF-κB p65 subunit. This modification suppresses NF-κB activation and decreases the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as Interleukin-6(IL-6), ultimately reducing organ damage in the lungs and kidneys [47]. Complementing these findings, another study revealed that short-term high-dose acetate treatment in LPS-stimulated macrophages caused a transient increase in histone acetylation. This response inhibits acetyl group transfer to NF-κB and attenuates pro-inflammatory macrophage activation through Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1 Alpha (HIF-1α) -dependent glycolytic regulation [48].

In summary, protein acetylation serves as a critical regulatory mechanism in inflammatory responses, modulating the activity of key signaling molecules (e.g., NF-κB) and chromatin accessibility to either amplify or resolve inflammation. Further investigation into cell-type-specific acetylation patterns may uncover novel therapeutic targets for inflammatory diseases.

Acetylation and metabolism

Acetylation critically modulates septic glucose/lipid metabolism. Under septic inflammatory conditions, metabolic processes of glycolipid synthesis undergo significant alterations to meet the physiological demands of the body [49]. ATP citrate lyase (ACLY), a key enzyme in glycolipid anabolism, helps maintain systemic metabolic homeostasis while influencing transcriptional responses and cellular functions [50]. Through acetylation of Forkhead Box Protein O1(FoxO1) and histone H3, ACLY activates MYC proto-oncogene (MYC) transcription, thereby enhancing glycolipid anabolic metabolism and promoting pro-inflammatory responses in endothelial cells (ECs). These changes contribute to severe endothelial dysfunction during sepsis [51]. Further evidence comes from Cao Jia et al.‘s study on LPS-stimulated obese mouse models, which revealed that acetylation regulates glucose metabolism. LPS has been shown to induce hepatic E1A Binding Protein P300 (P300) expression, leading to the acetylation of insulin receptor substrates Insulin Receptor Substrate 1 (IRS1) and Insulin Receptor Substrate 2 (IRS2). This modification inhibits their binding to insulin receptors, disrupts insulin signaling, and consequently reduces insulin sensitivity. Notably, P300 depletion in the liver significantly lowers blood glucose levels [52]. Additional insights were gained from the global lysine acetylation analysis of LPS-stimulated HepG2 cells. Researchers have identified significant acetylation at three sites of pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), which is a key regulator of lactate levels in sepsis. Follow-up experiments confirmed that PKM2 hyperacetylation serves as an important metabolic regulator under septic conditions [53].

Acetylation and autophagy

Acetylation plays an important regulatory role in cellular autophagy during sepsis. Autophagy, a lysosome-dependent self-degradation process, is essential for maintaining cellular balance and responding to stress [54]. Both excessive activation and suppression of autophagy can significantly affect disease progression [55]. Sun et al. have demonstrated that acetylation can inhibit autophagy, resulting in harmful consequences. Their study revealed that in the renal cortex and HK-2 cells, p53 increasingly moved from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, accompanied by elevated p53 acetylation levels. This acetylation-mediated process suppresses autophagy and worsens SAKI. Importantly, the research showed that Sirt1-mediated p53 deacetylation can partially reverse this detrimental effect [56].

In contrast, other studies have demonstrated that protein acetylation can promote cellular autophagy. During sepsis-associated acute lung injury (SALI), pulmonary macrophages exhibit increased acetylation of histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27), which enhances autophagy flux by upregulating ATG5, thereby mitigating alveolar damage [57]. Furthermore, zinc ions (Zn²⁺) increased parkin acetylation by inhibiting SIRT7 activity. This acetylation boosts mitophagy (selective autophagy of mitochondria) and helps alleviate SAKI [58].

Other functions

Acetylation also plays an important role in the regulation of mitochondrial function. Research has shown that EP300-mediated acetylation of H3K27 increases the expression of Methylenetetrahydrofolate Dehydrogenase 2(MTHFD2), a key factor in maintaining mitochondrial respiratory chain function and preventing mitochondrial damage [59]. Acetylation also affects cellular pyroptosis. In SALI, EP300 mediates histone H3/H4 acetylation of the NLRP3 promoter. This epigenetic modification promotes pyroptosis in alveolar macrophages and exacerbates lung tissue damage. These findings provide new insights into the regulation of pyroptosis during sepsis [60].

Recent studies have identified major acetylation targets (histones, transcription factors, signaling molecules, and metabolic enzymes) and their regulatory enzymes in sepsis. Early interventions, such as HDAC modulation, have shown therapeutic promise. However, key challenges remain: Acetylation’s dual role (both harmful and protective) in sepsis lacks full explanation, the complete regulatory network remains unclear, dynamic spatiotemporal changes in acetylation during sepsis require further study, and how these changes precisely affect cellular function requires further investigation.

Methylation and sepsis

Protein methylation, first reported in 1964, plays an important role in the regulation of protein function [61, 62]. Methyltransferases catalyze the addition of methyl groups primarily to arginine and lysine residues, although histidine and glutamic acid can also be modified [63]. The functional consequences of methylation vary depending on the specific protein site, degree of methylation, and cellular context, leading to either upregulation or downregulation of gene transcription [64]. Two major methyltransferase families, lysine methyltransferases (PKMTs) and arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs), mediate these modifications [65, 66], targeting both histone and non-histone proteins [67, 68]. Among histone modifications, methylation at the H3K and H4K sites has been the most extensively studied [69, 70]. Non-histone methylation has also gradually become a common PTM and an important signal transduction factor related to signal transduction pathways such as Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase(MAPK) and NF-KB [71].

Methylation and inflammation

Recent studies on methylation in sepsis have revealed its long-term effects, including effects on immune cell function and inflammatory responses [72]. Methylation exerts either pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory effects in sepsis by regulating inflammatory signaling pathways [2]. Methylation and immune cell function

Methylation drives immune cell differentiation. PRMT1 catalyzes H4R3me2a methylation at Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma(PPARγ) promoters, driving M2 polarization to protect against sepsis [79]. H3K4me1 modification of Interferon Gamma (IFNG) enhancers in Natural Killer Cells (NK) is essential for memory NK cell generation and secondary infection defense [80].

Methylation and cell death

Protein methylation regulates septic cell death. Research has shown that Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2(EZH2) induces ferroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells (AECs) by elevating H3K27me3 methylation in the Ubiquitin-Specific Peptidase 10 (USP10) promoter region [81]. Similarly, EZH2 promotes ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes by increasing H3K27 methylation at the Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2-Related Factor 2 (Nrf2) promoter [82]. Additionally, increased mtDNA in sepsis induces miR-138-5p promoter methylation, causing Alveolar Macrophages (AM) pyroptosis and aggravating organ injury [83].

Recent research has revealed that various methyltransferases are involved in the regulation of septic inflammatory signaling, highlighting the critical modulatory function of methylation. Existing evidence presents apparent discrepancies, with some studies indicating an anti-inflammatory effect of methylation, whereas others report pro-inflammatory outcomes. More importantly, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying methylation-mediated regulation of immune cell function have not been fully characterized. In the future, the mechanism of methylation in sepsis should be explored further.

Phosphorylation and sepsis

Phosphorylation is one of the most common post-translational modifications and occurs in approximately 30% of eukaryotic proteins [84]. This process involves the addition of phosphate groups to amino acid residues [85]. It occurs mainly in threonine, serine, and tyrosine but is not limited to these proteins [86]. The phosphorylation reaction is catalyzed by protein kinases [87], while phosphatases remove these phosphate groups, making phosphorylation a dynamic and reversible process [88]. More than 500 kinases and 200 phosphatase genes are encoded in the human genome [89]. These enzymes are essential for regulating the cell cycle [90], signal transduction [91], Enzyme activity [92], protein interactions [86], etc. Research has confirmed that phosphorylation plays a significant role in the regulation of sepsis.

Phosphorylation and inflammation

Phosphorylation critically modulates inflammatory signaling in sepsis. (Table 4).TLR4/Src-mediated phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 and MAPKs exacerbates renal inflammation and cell death in sepsis [93]. Conversely, reducing the phosphorylation of NF-κB P65, as well as decreasing Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase Kinase(MEK) and Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase(ERK) phosphorylation, helped alleviate oxidative stress and inflammation [94]. Macrophage NLRP3 Ser725 phosphorylation is essential for inflammasome activation and inflammatory responses [95]. The Janus Kinase (JAK)/Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (STAT) pathway also contributes to sepsis pathology, where IL-6-induced STAT phosphorylation leads to inflammation-mediated muscle wasting [96]. Studies have revealed upregulated Phosphoinositide 3-Kinase(PI3K) and AK strain Transforming (AKT) phosphorylation in the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade during sepsis-induced myocardial damage. This enhanced phosphorylation correlates with amplified inflammatory responses and increased programmed cell death, ultimately causing structural and functional damage to heart muscle cells [97]. Heat shock protein 70 (HSP70), as a classical molecular chaperone, plays a pivotal role in cellular stress protection mechanisms. Emerging evidence has established a close association between HSP70 expression/functional regulation and sepsis-related cell death and multi-organ dysfunction [98, 99]. Notably, during the pathological progression of sepsis, HSP70 undergoes phosphorylation modifications. Specifically, in LPS-induced sepsis models, researchers have observed phosphorylation of histone H3 on chromatin at the HSP70 gene locus. This epigenetic regulatory mechanism significantly enhances HSP70 transcriptional activity and extracellular secretion [100], which may potentially exacerbate disease progression in sepsis. However, current research connecting HSP70 with PTMs in sepsis remains limited and warrants further in-depth investigation in the future.

Roles of phosphorylation in sepsis

PI3K/AKT

PI3K/AKT

Phosphorylation can also negatively regulate inflammation and exert protective effects. For instance, research has investigated the therapeutic potential of ginsenoside Rg1, a natural compound, in the treatment of sepsis. Studies have demonstrated that Ginsenoside Rg1(Rg1) treatment significantly increases AKT phosphorylation levels in the lung tissue. These findings reveal that AKT phosphorylation-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling activation significantly lowers systemic pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α), thereby mitigating inflammatory cascades and providing lung-protective effects [101].

Phosphorylation and immune cell function

Phosphorylation regulates immune cell function during sepsis. In macrophages, phosphorylation influences the proinflammatory M1 phenotype. Specifically, the activation of macrophages by TLR4 is contingent upon the Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor(EGFR) phosphorylation status [102, 103], while phosphorylation of interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) further promotes M1 polarization [104]. Beyond activation, phosphorylation can impair macrophage clearance functions. STAT3 phosphorylation reduces macrophage phagocytic capacity by remodeling the actin cytoskeleton [105]. Phosphorylation also affects neutrophil activity. Programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) induces PI3K/AKT pathway activation, increasing AKT phosphorylation, and delaying neutrophil apoptosis. This leads to prolonged release of inflammatory mediators [106]. Furthermore, phosphorylation affects adaptive immunity. Transcription factor EB (TFEB) phosphorylation promotes CD4+T cell apoptosis, weakens adaptive immune responses, and increases susceptibility to infections and tissue damage [107].

However, certain phosphorylation events can reduce sepsis-related damage. Studies have demonstrated that melatonin-induced PTEN-Induced Kinase 1(PINK1)Ser495 phosphorylation promotes microglial polarization from M1 to M2 phenotype, mitigating LPS-triggered neuroinflammation in septic encephalopathy [108]. Similarly, the calcitonin family peptide, intermedin (IMD), has protective effects against sepsis. Research has demonstrated that IMD enhances adaptive immune responses through phosphorylation-mediated activation of ERK1/2, stimulating proliferation of both T and B lymphocyte populations. This mechanism helps restore impaired immune responses during sepsis, aiding infection control and recovery [109].

Phosphorylation and mitochondria

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a critical pathophysiological mechanism in sepsis-induced multiorgan failure [110]. Phosphorylation is a critical regulatory mechanism in this process. Emerging evidence has demonstrated its dual function in mitochondrial control during sepsis. On the detrimental side, phosphorylation can significantly exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction. Calmodulin-dependent Protein Kinase II(CaMKII), a universally present kinase that is principally activated by increasing intracellular Ca2+ [111], has been implicated in cardiomyocyte death during sepsis. Activated CaMKII mediates Ryanodine Receptor 2(RyR2)phosphorylation, promoting endoplasmic reticulum Ca2 + leakage. This leads to mitochondrial Ca2 + overload, ultimately triggering apoptosis and worsening myocardial injury [112].

Conversely, phosphorylation can protect mitochondrial function through specific pathways. Maintaining mitochondrial quality control is essential under septic conditions [113]. Stable expression of pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) in sepsis enables phosphorylation at Ser91 of the mitochondrial outer membrane protein Prohibitin 2(PHB2). This phosphorylation effectively prevents LPS-induced PHB2 degradation, thereby preserving mitochondrial quality control and protecting mitochondria from cellular stress [114]. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is vital for mitochondrial homeostasis [115]. Sepsis typically reduces phosphorylated AMPK (p-AMPK) levels, which correlates with mitochondrial structural damage, functional impairment, and decreased Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) production. Importantly, AMPK activator intervention reversed p-AMPK reduction and ameliorated mitochondrial damage in renal tissues. These results indicate that increasing AMPK phosphorylation could serve as a potential treatment approach to safeguard mitochondrial function and enhance survival in sepsis [116].

In summary, the function of phosphorylation in sepsis is mainly reflected in the regulation of the inflammatory response, immune response, and mitochondrial function. Current research has investigated the phosphorylation levels of specific kinases and substrates, along with their mechanisms of action, in key sepsis pathways. Studies have revealed that phosphorylation is not simply a ‘bad molecule, but can play a protective role under certain conditions. Kinases demonstrate multisubstrate specificity, as substrates accept phosphorylation from various kinases at diverse sites, yielding differential biological outcomes. This complexity suggests that the precise regulation of specific phosphorylation events remains a significant challenge.

Ubiquitinization and sepsis

The 76-amino-acid ubiquitin molecule forms covalent linkages with protein targets [117]. The enzymatic cascade mediating ubiquitin conjugation involves three essential components that operate consecutively: E1 ubiquitin-activating enzyme, E2 ubiquitin-carrier enzyme, and E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase [118, 119]. The reaction occurs in three stages: (1) E1 activates ubiquitin, (2) E2 carries and transfers activated ubiquitin, and (3) E3 catalyzes the final covalent bond between ubiquitin and lysine residues in target proteins [120]. The ubiquitin system regulates diverse cellular processes, ranging from proteostasis maintenance and genomic integrity preservation to immunological regulation and intracellular signaling cascades [121]. Ubiquitination and inflammation

Ubiquitination exerts protective effects against sepsis by facilitating the degradation of proinflammatory proteins. Research has demonstrated that ubiquitin-mediated NLRP3 degradation is crucial for limiting inflammasome activation and toxemia. Specifically, the HECT-type E3 ubiquitin ligase, WW domain containing E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1(WWP1)catalyzes K48-linked ubiquitin chain formation on NLRP3, targeting it for proteasomal degradation, thereby reducing inflammation [122]. Similarly, the E3 ligase (CBL-B) mediates K48-linked ubiquitination at a specific NLRP3 site, directing it toward proteasomal breakdown [123]. The CRL4 B complex, containing the scaffold protein Cullin 4 B (CUL4 B), promotes polyubiquitination of p53 at K164, leading to p53 degradation and subsequent reduction of apoptosis and inflammation in renal tubular cells [124]. Conversely, FBXO32-mediated ubiquitination of annexin A1 (ANXA1) enhances proteasomal degradation and worsens LPS-induced cardiomyopathy [125].

Ubiquitination can also modulate inflammation through protein stabilization. PDZ and LIM Domain Protein 2(PDLIM2) catalyzes the K63-linked ubiquitination of NF-κB p65. Unlike typical degradation signals, the modification stabilizes a p65-ubiquitin complex that undergoes Nuclear Dot Protein 52 kDa (NDP52) -mediated autophagic recognition, ultimately downregulating pro-inflammatory responses and conferring septic defense [126].

Ubiquitination and immune cell function

Ubiquitination enhances immunity by inhibiting the protein molecules that inhibit the differentiation of immune cells. In septic conditions, Myeloid-derived suppressor Cells (PMN)-MDSCs undergo marked proliferation and exert immunosuppressive effects on the innate and adaptive immune responses [127]. The long non-coding RNA Malat1 facilitates ubiquitin-mediated degradation of phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3), which reduces Polymorphonuclear Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells (PMN-MDSC) and Monocytic Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells (M-MDSC) populations while increasing CD4+T and CD8+T cell proportions, thereby alleviating late-stage sepsis immunosuppression [128]. Tripartite Motif-Containing Protein 28 (TRIM28) mediates K63-linked ubiquitination of hexokinase 2 (HK2), promoting HK2 degradation. This process attenuates hippocampal inflammation and microglial activation, thereby providing protection to the central nervous system [129].

Additionally, ubiquitination can stabilize proteins involved in macrophage differentiation, particularly toward the M1 phenotype. The E3 ubiquitin ligase PRAJA2 (Praja2) ubiquitinates Malignant Fibrous Histiocytoma Amplified Sequence 1(MFHAS1) without causing degradation. Instead, Praja2 forms a stable complex with ubiquitinated MFHAS1, which activates the JNK and p38 pathways to drive macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 state [130].

Recent years have witnessed significant advances in the understanding of the role of ubiquitination in sepsis, particularly highlighting the importance of non-degradative ubiquitination pathways. However, several key challenges remain in this field. The ubiquitination network involves multiple enzymatic components (E1, E2, and E3), diverse substrate proteins, and various ubiquitin chain linkage types, resulting in a remarkable complexity. Current limitations include an incomplete understanding of E3 ligase substrate recognition mechanisms, unclear principles governing substrate specificity, and insufficient knowledge about crosstalk between ubiquitination and other signaling pathways. These knowledge gaps require further systematic investigation.

Glycosylation and sepsis

Glycosylation is one of the most common complex PTMs. This modification significantly expands proteomic diversity beyond genetic coding capacity, while profoundly influencing protein function, stability, localization, and other critical characteristics [131]. This process involves glycosyltransferases that catalyze the transfer of sugar moieties to specific amino acid residues, forming glycosidic bonds [132]. Based on the modified amino acid type, glycosylation primarily occurs as either N-linked or O-linked modifications [133]. Current estimates suggest that over 40% of human proteins undergo glycosylation [134]. It serves as a pivotal modulator of essential cellular activities such as transcriptional regulation, signaling cascades, translational control, and metabolic adaptation [135].

Emerging research has shown that glycosylation plays a significant regulatory role in sepsis. Clinical studies have identified specific glycosylation patterns of haptoglobin (Hp) that correlate with sepsis prognosis in patients [136]. Investigations employing four distinct pathogens in murine models demonstrated the upregulated expression of evolutionarily conserved core 1/3 O-glycans during bacterial sepsis [137]. However, the functional consequences of pathogen-induced glycosylation changes in sepsis require further investigation. Glycosylation of the Fc region of immunoglobulins critically influences inflammatory and immune responses [138]. In septic mice, neutrophil depletion was found to alter IgG Fc N-glycosylation patterns, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear and warrant further investigation [139].

Glycosylation exerts protective effects against sepsis. Increased O-glycan modifications inhibit the hyperactivation of NF-κB signaling, leading to decreased secretion of inflammatory mediators (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) and ameliorated systemic inflammatory responses [140]. Additionally, glutamine (Gln) enhances Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) glycosylation, promoting intestinal mucosal barrier recovery in burn-induced septic mice [141].

In summary, research on glycosylation during sepsis remains at an early stage. The current findings indicate elevated overall glycosylation levels during sepsis, and some mechanisms and functions are beginning to be understood. However, for many observed glycosylation alterations, their precise biological roles and downstream signaling pathways require further validation. The functional impact of these glycosylation changes remains unclear. Future investigations should comprehensively characterize the sepsis-associated glycosylation landscape.

Conclusion and prospects

This review synthesizes current evidence demonstrating the central role of PTMs in sepsis pathophysiology, where they critically regulate inflammatory, metabolic, and immune pathways through dual protective and detrimental mechanisms. Recent advances have identified key modification sites, substrate proteins, and regulatory enzymes, partially elucidating their molecular underpinnings in sepsis.

Future investigations should prioritize: (1) advanced mass spectrometry and proteomics for comprehensive PTM profiling, (2) deciphering cross-talk between PTM networks (e.g., phosphorylation-acetylation-lactylation axes) to identify therapeutic nodes, and (3) clinical translation through PTM-based biomarkers, targeted modulators (e.g., HDAC inhibitors), and spatial omics for organ-specific signatures. Challenges remain in capturing dynamic PTM changes during sepsis progression and detecting low-abundance modifications clinically. Emerging technologies like single-cell proteomics and CRISPR screening may overcome these limitations, potentially enabling precision immunometabolic modulation to transform sepsis management from reactive treatment to proactive prevention.
