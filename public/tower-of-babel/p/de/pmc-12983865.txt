Louse-borne relapsing fever in Ethiopia: an urgent call for WHO recognition as a neglected tropical disease
Authors: Balew Arega, Amdemeskel Mersha, Amanuel Zeleke, Birhane Tafesse, Yonas Melaku, Alazar Regassa, Mesfin Abiyo, Enyew Liyew, Kidist Samuel, Bereket Tesfaye, Asnake Agunie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLouse-borne relapsing fever (LBRF), caused by Borrelia recurrentis and transmitted by the human body louse, remains a persistent health emergency in Ethiopia, resulting in preventable mortality among young adults. Its continued endemicity reflects systemic failures and amplifies the syndromic effects of poverty, displacement and social stigma—locally termed ‘Qmalam’—which deters care-seeking. Clinical management is further complicated by the potentially fatal Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction following antibiotic treatment. Despite these severe consequences, LBRF remains off the official WHO list of neglected tropical diseases (NTDs), hindering the mobilisation of essential resources and political will. LBRF unequivocally meets WHO criteria for NTD designation, a critical step necessary to catalyse the research, funding and coordinated action required for its elimination. In this viewpoint, we present an integrated framework for addressing transmission, treatment and prediction and issue an urgent call for formal WHO recognition.Keywords: Epidemiology; Rickettsia infections; Infections, diseases, disorders, injuries

Introduction: a disease out of time, a reality we cannot escape

We, as Ethiopian clinicians, confront a disease largely consigned to the annals of history by the global health community: louse-borne relapsing fever (LBRF). The persistence of this spirochetal infection, caused by Borrelia recurrentis and transmitted by the human body louse, represents a tragic failure of global equity.1 Wherever poverty, conflict and health system neglect converge, this so-called ‘Lazarus pathogen’ claims lives that should be saved.

The challenge of LBRF in Ethiopia and the wider East African region is threefold: its status as a relic of bygone epidemics, its reliance on obsolete diagnostic techniques and a standard treatment complicated by a potentially fatal inflammatory reaction.2 These factors are not isolated but are interconnected and mutually reinforcing, creating a stable pathocoenosis. Within this equilibrium, LBRF persists as a sensitive proxy for the magnitude of underlying societal collapse and health system fragmentation.3 In Ethiopia, this dynamic is historically entrenched and compounded by a powerful social stigma, locally known as ‘Qmalam’ (ቅማላም: In Amharic language), which associates louse infestation with destitution and shapes community responses to disease.4 5

At our tertiary referral hospital, the clinical practice site for all authors, LBRF is encountered daily across medical and intensive-care units (ICU). In a consecutive cohort of 166 patients hospitalised with LBRF over 2 years, 42 (25%) required intensive care. The overall case-fatality ratio was 19% (32/166), which rose to 36% (15/42) among patients admitted to the ICU. Preliminary analysis identified delayed presentation (≥5 days after symptom onset) and severe Jarisch-Herxheimer reactions (JHR) as key factors associated with fatal outcomes. Notably, more than three-fourths (75) of the patients were young men (over 30 years old) who were daily labourers, lived on the streets or came from detention facilities. A mortality rate of this magnitude for a treatable bacterial infection is not only a clinical concern but also an indicator of systemic inequity and a failure to uphold the right to health for marginalised populations.

The Ethiopian context: a perfect pathocoenosis

The persistence of LBRF in Ethiopia, long after its global elimination, is best understood through two complementary frameworks. It is sustained by a stable pathocoenosis, a historical disease ecology of poverty, and its impact is magnified by a modern syndemic, where biological infection interacts synergistically with adverse social conditions to worsen outcomes.2 In our country, this represents a classic poverty-associated pathocoenosis, yet its lethal synergy with stigma (‘Qmalam’), displacement and treatment complications reveals its modern nature as a syndemic.3 This is not an isolated national phenomenon but part of a broader regional pattern of endemicity in East Africa, where similar outbreaks have been documented among vulnerable populations in Sudan, South Sudan and Eritrea, often linked to conflict and displacement.6 7

LBRF’s strongest foothold is within institutional failure.8 The surge in Ethiopia’s prison population is directly fueling the spread of LBRF.9 As inmate numbers climb, facilities become dangerously overcrowded, stripping away access to bedding, clean water and ventilation. These conditions are a perfect breeding ground for the body louse, transforming prisons into epidemic epicentres where outbreaks ignite and inevitably spill over, crippling local health services.10 In addition, the ongoing humanitarian crises in Ethiopia, driven by internal displacement and persistent conflict, have created another fertile ground for LBRF transmission, with a high prevalence of active louse infestation in households.11 12

Neglect has also crippled Ethiopia’s surveillance system, obscuring the true scale of the LBRF crisis. Reliance on Giemsa-stained blood smear microscopy—the diagnostic mainstay—poses a major limitation due to its inherently low and operator-dependent sensitivity, which fluctuates with bacterial load and is lowest during afebrile periods.8 Even in experienced centres, sensitivity does not exceed 70%.13 In low-resource settings with limited expertise, this means our documented, hospitalised cases represent only the visible apex of a much larger, undetected community epidemic. This ‘iceberg phenomenon’ creates a vicious cycle: historically low and unstable reported incidence—reflecting chronic under-surveillance rather than true absence—justifies continued neglect, which in turn perpetuates the conditions for transmission.

A cascade of failures: the clinical reality of LBRF

For frontline physicians in Ethiopia, LBRF exemplifies a cascade of systemic failures. Persistent clinical, diagnostic and therapeutic breakdowns create an environment of ongoing risk and professional moral distress.

The diagnostic deficit: working without adequate tools

The clinical management of LBRF is severely hampered by diagnostic obsolescence. Unlike malaria, for which rapid diagnostic tests are ubiquitous, confirmation of the bacteria relies on the demonstration of spirochaetes on Giemsa-stained blood smears. This method has low sensitivity that is highly dependent on the timing of blood collection.14 The advanced technology to create a simple, rapid test for LBRF (like CRISPR) already exists and works for other diseases.15 However, because LBRF is a neglected disease, no one has invested the money and effort to adapt this technology, build the test and get it into the hands of doctors in affected areas. This failure to turn lab science into a practical tool is known as the ‘translational gap’, which is a common problem for diseases that primarily affect the poor.16 17

The therapeutic betrayal: the JHR

The treatment of LBRF presents a profound paradox. A single, low-cost dose of penicillin or tetracycline is curative. This same treatment risks triggering the JHR reaction, a potentially fatal systemic inflammatory cascade. Historically lethal in severe, untreated cases, this reaction now drives modern case-fatality rates. The incidence of the JHR in studies ranges from 0% to 100%.18 In our ICU cohort, the JHR was the primary cause of the 35.7% (15/42) mortality rate. This therapeutic crisis underscores five decades of stalled innovation, an example of therapeutic paralysis. While immunology has been revolutionised by biologic therapies, no targeted intervention exists to mitigate the JHR. Early research into cytokine blockade, which revealed promising results, has not advanced to definitive clinical trials or further mechanistic investigation, stymied by a profound lack of commercial interest and funding—the quintessential market failure for neglected diseases.19 20 For clinicians, the experience of presiding over a predictable, preventable death from a standard treatment inflicts significant moral distress and can foster therapeutic hesitancy, sometimes resulting in incomplete treatment.

The unacceptable infection risk: eroding provider safety

The management of LBRF presents a routine occupational risk that is almost unique in modern medicine. The human body louse, the disease vector, readily infests clinical environments, rendering standard personal protective equipment ineffective. Frontline staff are thus at direct risk of infection during patient care. This creates a cruel irony: the caregiver becomes the patient. We have witnessed colleagues, including a surgeon (Dr Tsegaye, name changed), contract LBRF during an operation on an undiagnosed case; he was later treated in the same ICU bed as his patient (online supplemental annex I). This persistent threat to provider safety, in stark contrast to the protections mobilised during outbreaks of Ebola or COVID-19, violates fundamental workplace rights and jeopardises the stability of the health workforce in endemic regions.

The financial abyss: when physicians become resource facilitators

The clinical encounter is further complicated by the extreme poverty of many patients, who cannot afford the basic components of their care, including antibiotics, investigations and nutritional support. Physicians are thus forced to assume the non-clinical roles of resource facilitators and ad hoc financiers, a duty for which most are neither trained nor resourced. This shift from evidence-based practice to improvised social work and fundraising exceeds professional remit and capacity, compromising care and inflicting moral injury on clinicians who daily confront the consequences of institutional abandonment.

The funding chasm: a measure of neglect

The neglect of LBRF is starkly quantified by its near-total absence from global health research and development funding portfolios. The 2023 G-FINDER survey, the most comprehensive analysis of its kind, tracks billions of dollars in global investment for poverty-related and neglected diseases.21 While recognised NTDs receive targeted funding, LBRF is conspicuously absent as a category, indicating dedicated research and development funding that is effectively zero. This funding chasm is the root cause of the diagnostic and therapeutic stagnation described above, creating a self-perpetuating cycle where a lack of investment leads to a lack of evidence, which in turn justifies continued neglect.22 23 A comparative overview of global initiatives for selected diseases, highlighting the disparity in coordinated support between WHO-recognised NTDs and non-listed diseases such as LBRF, is provided in online supplemental annex II.

Transformative framework for elimination

Ending LBRF calls for a transformative strategy, not mere incremental reform—a direct response to the systemic failures detailed above. We propose a coordinated, three-pillar framework targeting transmission, treatment and prediction through innovative and equitable interventions.

Pillar 1: integrated vector control and clinical safety

The first pillar reframes louse control as central to clinical and health-worker safety. Priorities include the following:

Pre-emptive screening at entry points of camps, prisons and health centres using affordable detection methods to identify and target highly infested individuals.

Pillar 2: a therapeutic ‘Moonshot’ to neutralise the JHR

The second pillar addresses treatment head-on by advocating the development of a JHR-neutralising biologic—a single-dose monoclonal antibody or engineered protein that safely interrupts the cytokine cascade during antibiotic therapy. Achieving this ‘moonshot’ requires:

Investigation into JHR immunology to identify actionable intervention points.

Equitable partnerships between Ethiopian and international researchers to establish clinical trial networks.

The third pillar breaks the cycle of reactiveness by leveraging advanced analytics. Real-time integration of climate, conflict, migration and outbreak data enables machine learning to reveal patterns invisible to traditional surveillance, permitting:

The imperative of WHO neglected tropical disease (NTD) recognition

The case for designating LBRF as an NTD is compelling, as its epidemiological profile aligns decisively with the WHO’s established criteria. The disease disproportionately affects populations in poverty, causes significant morbidity and mortality, induces social stigma and is amenable to control, yet it remains severely neglected by research and development. As detailed in table 1, the evidence from the Ethiopian frontline experience systematically demonstrates this alignment with each core NTD criterion. Its continued exclusion from the NTD list represents a critical disconnect between its documented burden and the global policy response, hindering the mobilisation of essential resources and tools. Formal recognition is the necessary catalyst to break the cycle of neglect and address this persistent threat to public health.

Conclusions: a test case for global health equity

When a readily treatable infection kills a substantial proportion of hospitalised patients— and a still higher share of those critically ill—it is not merely a clinical challenge, but a profound test of global health architecture and our collective commitment to equity. Elimination demands not only technical innovation but also bold political will to confront the systemic failures perpetuating this ancient disease. Our patients—the displaced, the imprisoned, the forgotten—have endured preventable suffering for too long. As caregivers, we must not be forced to risk our own health while witnessing avoidable deaths. The tools and knowledge for elimination exist; what has been lacking is political commitment and institutional resolve. Including LBRF on the WHO NTD list is an essential first step for health justice. Such action would demonstrate that global health equity is more than rhetoric—it is an unwavering dedication to human dignity and provider safety. The time for coordinated, compassionate and decisive action is now.
