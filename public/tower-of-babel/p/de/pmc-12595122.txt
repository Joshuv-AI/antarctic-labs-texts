Absence of BCAP in myeloid cells abrogates M2 macrophage differentiation and promotes anti-tumor immunity
Authors: Kiana Kawarizadeh, Charles N. Vallez, Sepideh Aliyazdi, Kathrynne A. Warrick, Cierra Carafice, Irene Saha, Chandrashekhar Pasare
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryB-cell adaptor for PI3K (BCAP) is a critical signaling adapter that links Toll-like receptors (TLRs) to the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-Akt pathway and negatively regulates TLR-induced inflammation. BCAP also facilitates transition of macrophages from an inflammatory (M1) to a reparatory (M2) state; however, whether BCAP functions downstream of receptors that directly drive M2 macrophage differentiation is not known. Here, we find that BCAP is essential for M2 differentiation in response to interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-33. BCAP-deficient macrophages displayed an impaired M2 gene program, marked by reduced expression of Arg1, Retnla, and Mgl2. Mechanistically, BCAP enables IL-4-driven PI3K-Akt activation by mediating interactions between p85 and Grb2 without impacting STAT6 function. Strikingly, loss of BCAP, either globally or in myeloid cells, leads to suppressed tumor growth and a shift in tumor-associated macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory state and less exhausted tumor-infiltrating CD8 T cells. These findings present BCAP as a promising therapeutic target for enhancing anti-tumor immunity.

Introduction

Macrophages are highly plastic cells of the innate immune system and play a major role in both tissue homeostasis and clearance of foreign pathogens including bacteria, viruses, and parasites.1,2,3 To perform these critical functions, macrophages express a wide range of surface receptors so as to respond to microenvironmental cues and modulate their behavior along an inflammatory spectrum.4 Depending on the nature of the cell-extrinsic stimuli, macrophages can either adopt an inflammatory or reparative phenotype, commonly referred to as M1 or M2 states, respectively.4,5,6 M1 macrophage differentiation primarily results from signaling events when macrophages sense pathogens via pattern recognition receptors, such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs).4 Additionally, Th1 cytokines, such as interferon-γ, can act directly on macrophages to potentiate M1 differentiation under inflammatory conditions. M1 macrophages exhibit potent microbicidal and tumoricidal properties necessary for the clearance of pathogens and malignant cells. However, excessive hyperinflammatory responses lead to tissue damage and have been implicated in multiple inflammatory diseases.7,8 Therefore, to avoid persistent inflammation and maintain homeostasis, M1 macrophages transition into a reparative M2 state to initiate anti-inflammatory responses.9,10

The M1 to M2 macrophage transition is characterized by the downregulation of inflammatory receptors and upregulation of surface receptors such as CD206 and CD163.11,12 These scavenger receptors function through endocytosis and phagocytosis of stress-related ligands, resulting in the clearance of inflammatory cellular debris.13 M2 macrophages also produce arginase-1 (Arg1) and reparative cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-10 to control inflammation.14,15 While the M1 to M2 transition is critical for regulating inflammation and facilitating tissue repair, macrophages can also acquire an M2 state directly in response to cytokines such as IL-416,17 and IL-13,17 as well as through novel mechanisms downstream of IL-33.18,19 While the M1 to M2 transition is advantageous to mitigate inflammation, it can be detrimental for anti-tumor immunity as M2 macrophages in the tumor microenvironment (TME) have been shown to promote tumor progression.20,21 Macrophages that infiltrate the TME adopt an alternatively activated phenotype that largely phenocopies canonical M2 macrophages22 and are referred to as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Furthermore, tumor cells can influence the differentiation of tumor-infiltrating monocytes to adopt an M2 phenotype as a means of immune evasion.23 While tumor cells secrete many factors that likely influence the biology of TAMs, we do not have a full understanding of the cell-intrinsic regulators of macrophage differentiation that promote tumor growth.

We and others have previously shown that B-cell adaptor for PI3K (BCAP), a large, multi-domain protein originally discovered in linking the B-cell receptor to phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) activation,24 is a critical adaptor that links TLRs to the PI3K-Akt axis in macrophages. The engagement of BCAP and the PI3K-Akt pathway was critical in negatively regulating inflammatory responses following TLR stimulation.25,26 We have also shown that M1 macrophages require BCAP to transition into reparative M2 macrophages as BCAP-deficient macrophages failed to upregulate a reparative M2 gene program following clearance of the TLR4 ligand, LPS.9 Specifically, BCAP contains an N-terminal TIR domain and is recruited to TLRs via TIR-TIR domain interactions to mediate these downstream signaling events.25,27 BCAP contains other domains including a proline-rich region allowing for associations with SH3-containing proteins such as Grb2,28 which has been shown to link IL-4 receptor signaling to the PI3K pathway.29,30 While BCAP is required for regulating inflammation downstream of TLR signaling and mediating M1 to M2 macrophage transition, the role of BCAP downstream of direct M2-inducing stimuli such as IL-4 (and IL-33) remains to be explored. Previous work demonstrating BCAP’s direct association with Grb2 and p85 led us to hypothesize that BCAP may play a role downstream of IL-4 signaling to activate the PI3K-Akt pathway and ultimately drive macrophages into an M2 state.

Here, we reveal a previously unknown role for BCAP in enabling the binding of the p85 subunit of PI3K to Grb2—a key molecular interaction following IL-4 signaling. Even more striking, BCAP emerged as a critical link connecting IL-33 signaling to Akt activation, permitting a downstream M2 macrophage program. In vivo, the absence of BCAP dramatically impaired tumor growth, both in globally-deficient mice and in mice lacking BCAP specifically within the myeloid compartment. TAMs from BCAP-deficient mice adopted a distinctly pro-inflammatory phenotype, in sharp contrast to their BCAP-sufficient counterparts. These results position BCAP as a central, cell-intrinsic regulator of M2 macrophage differentiation and a powerful driver of tumor progression. While targeting the diverse external signals that shape tumor-supportive macrophages remains a major challenge, BCAP stands out as a promising and targetable molecular switch offering a therapeutic avenue for the treatment of solid tumors.

Results

BCAP-deficient macrophages exhibit a defective M2 gene program in response to IL-4 stimulation

BCAP is a large adaptor protein characterized by several domains28 that functions downstream of multiple immune receptors.25,26,31 Previous work has shed light on the direct binding of adaptor proteins including Grb2 and p85 to BCAP in resting HEK293T cells.28 Notably, Grb2 and p85 are also recruited downstream of the IL-4 receptor (IL-4R) to drive PI3K-Akt signaling and promote an M2 macrophage transcriptional program.29 Given BCAP’s ability to interact with these critical signaling components, we investigated whether BCAP contributes to IL-4-driven M2 macrophage differentiation.

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) from wild-type (WT) and BCAP knockout (KO) mice were generated (Figure S1A), and the induction of M2 genes was assessed via RT-qPCR following IL-4 stimulation. We found that the upregulation of several key M2 genes, Arg1, Retnla, and Mgl2, was defective in BCAP KO BMDMs when compared to WT BMDMs (Figure 1A). To ascertain the requirement for BCAP in regulating M2 gene expression in primary ex vivo-derived macrophages, we evaluated macrophages resident in the peritoneal cavity. Peritoneal cavity macrophages were enriched by magnetic selection (Figure S1B) and stimulated with IL-4 in a similar fashion. We found that peritoneal cavity-resident macrophages isolated from BCAP KO mice also had reduced induction of M2 genes when compared to their WT counterparts (Figure 1B). Protein expression analysis for arginase 1 (Arg1) corroborated the gene expression data, supporting the role of BCAP in the induction of Arg1 following IL-4 stimulation (Figure 1C; Figure S1C). Additionally, intracellular staining by flow cytometry before and after IL-4 stimulation further confirmed the need for BCAP for Arg1 expression (Figure 1D). Importantly, baseline Arg1 expression in peritoneal macrophages was unaltered in WT versus BCAP KO mice (Figure S1D). Additionally, baseline Arg1 and CD206 expression in macrophages collected from the visceral adipose tissue were comparable between WT and BCAP KO mice (Figures S1E and S1F), indicating that BCAP is required for M2 gene expression only following sensing of IL-4. IL-4-induced STAT6 activation is intact but PI3K-Akt signaling is compromised in BCAP-deficient macrophages

The type I IL-4R, composed of IL-4Rα chain and the common γ chain (γC), initiates two simultaneous, yet independent pathways29 following binding of IL-4. Janus-associated kinase (JAK) 1 is recruited to the IL-4Rα chain where it phosphorylates tyrosine residues critical for the docking and activation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6), permitting translocation to the nucleus and transcription of anti-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors.32,33 STAT6 is known to be the master transcriptional regulator of M2 gene expression, as STAT6-deficient BMDMs exhibit a complete abrogation of M2 genes and phenocopy many of the defects seen in IL-4-deficient mice.32,34 Simultaneously, JAK3 phosphorylates tyrosine residues on the common γC, leading to the recruitment of insulin receptor substrate-2 (IRS2), an adaptor molecule known to link IL-4 signaling to the PI3K-Akt axis, driving M2 gene expression.29,32 In this sense, activation of the PI3K-Akt axis refines the transcriptional program for optimal M2 differentiation.

To explore the mechanisms governing BCAP-mediated M2 gene programs, we evaluated signal transduction downstream of IL-4R. We found that STAT6 phosphorylation was comparable in WT and BCAP KO BMDMs following IL-4 stimulation (Figure 2A). To confirm that BCAP KO BMDMs have functional STAT6 signaling downstream of IL-4, expression of Akt-independent genes, Fabp4 and Chil3,34 were measured via RT-qPCR. The induction of Fabp4 and Chil3 gene expression in BCAP KO BMDMs was comparable to WT BMDMs (Figure S2A). Notably, recent work has defined a subset of M2 genes exclusively regulated by the PI3K-AKT axis including Arg1, Retnla, and Mgl2.34,35 As we found preferential defects in the induction of these genes in BCAP KO macrophages upon IL-4 stimulation (Figure 1), we hypothesized that although STAT6 signaling was unaltered, BCAP KO macrophages may have defective PI3K-Akt signaling. Indeed, we found that upon IL-4 stimulation, BCAP KO BMDMs exhibited decreased phosphorylation of PI3K (Figure 2B) and Akt (Figure 2C). Similar results were obtained when we examined macrophages resident in the peritoneal cavity (Figures S2C and S2D). Given the established role of the PI3K-Akt pathway in promoting cell survival and suppressing apoptosis in macrophages,36,37 we next examined if BCAP KO BMDMs underwent accelerated cell death. WT and BCAP KO BMDMs were left unstimulated or stimulated with IL-4, and cell death was accessed via flow cytometry. We found that BCAP KO BMDMs do not undergo increased cell death compared to WT BMDMs as measured by uptake of Zombie Yellow dye (Figure S2E), indicating that defective signaling and gene expression was not a result of reduced cell survival.

To confirm that BCAP mediates M2 gene expression via activation of the PI3K pathway, we treated WT and BCAP KO BMDMs with IL-4 in the presence or absence of the PI3K inhibitor, Wortmannin. In WT cells, Wortmannin significantly reduced the expression of Arg1, Retnla, and Mgl2 (Figure 2D). In contrast, Wortmannin had no effect on BCAP KO cells, suggesting that impaired M2 gene expression seen in BCAP-deficient macrophages is largely a result of defective PI3K activation (Figure 2D).

IL-4-induced p85-Grb2 interactions are attenuated in macrophages in the absence of BCAP

We next sought to determine the molecular interactions that enable BCAP to influence the IL-4R-induced PI3K pathway. Upon IL-4 signaling, IRS2 binds to phosphotyrosine residues on the cytoplasmic tail of the common γC and recruits p85 via three tyrosine-binding sites to mediate activation of the PI3K pathway.29 IRS2 has also been shown to bind Grb2 via SH2 domain interactions, but the functional importance of this interaction remains unknown.29 Many reports detail an intricate protein complex that is recruited to the intracellular domain of the IL-4R upon IL-4 ligation, yet how BCAP mediates protein-protein interactions in this complex has not been elucidated. To determine the role of BCAP in facilitating downstream protein interactions upon IL-4R signaling, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays in WT and BCAP KO BMDMs stimulated with IL-4 for the indicated time points. We first pulled down BCAP in WT BMDMs and probed for its association with p85 and Grb2. We found BCAP to be constitutively bound to p85 in resting primary BMDMs and no increase in this interaction following IL-4 stimulation (Figure 3A). We, however, found that although BCAP associated with Grb2 in resting macrophages, IL-4R signaling led to increased interaction between Grb2 and BCAP (Figure 3A).Figure 3IL-4-induced p85-Grb2 interactions are attenuated in macrophages in the absence of BCAP(A) Western blotting of WT and BCAP KO BMDMs stimulated with IL-4 (20 ng/mL) and immunoprecipitated with a polyclonal BCAP antibody. While the results above suggest interaction between BCAP and p85, immunoprecipitation of p85 in resting macrophages failed to reveal its association with BCAP. However, IL-4 stimulation clearly led to signal-dependent interaction between p85 and BCAP (Figure 3B). The differences in the association between p85 and BCAP that we found here could be due to the nature of interaction between these two proteins, as well as their relative abundance in the cell. Agreeing with previous work done in HEK293T cells, association between p85 and Grb2 in macrophages was constitutive,28 and our data show that this association was not enhanced upon IL-4 signaling in WT BMDMs.29,33 Interestingly, p85 binding to Grb2 was compromised in BCAP KO BMDMs, suggesting that BCAP is required for that interaction. Thus, absence of BCAP appears to compromise the steady-state interaction between p85 and Grb2, and this translates into defective PI3K and Akt activation following IL-4 stimulation.

In addition to signaling through the type I IL-4R, IL-4 can also signal via the type II IL-4R, which is composed of the IL-4Rα chain and the IL-13 receptorα1 chain (IL-13Rα1).29 IL-13, another Th2 cytokine, also signals through this receptor. However, previous studies have shown that while IL-13 induces comparable STAT6 phosphorylation to IL-4, it leads to significantly lower phosphorylation and activation of IRS2.29 To investigate the role of BCAP in IL-13 signaling, we stimulated WT and BCAP KO BMDMs with IL-13 and assessed the expression of Arg1, Retnla, and Mgl2 using RT-qPCR. We observed no significant differences in the expression of the indicated genes between WT and BCAP KO BMDMs in response to IL-13 stimulation (Figure S3A). These findings suggest that BCAP functions specifically downstream of the type I IL-4R, which includes the common γC required for PI3K activation.

BCAP is critical for M2 macrophage gene program induction following IL-33 stimulation

While IL-4 is the most widely recognized Th2 cytokine, we sought to investigate whether other cytokines known to induce M2 differentiation rely on BCAP for optimal signaling, suggesting a conserved role for BCAP in M2 biology. IL-33 is an “alarmin cytokine” released by dying cells and has been shown to influence macrophage differentiation in the TME.38 However, recent work has identified IL-33 as a trigger for the early expression of pro-inflammatory genes followed by a subsequent M2 gene program independent of IL-4R signaling.18,19,39 IL-33 signals through its surface receptor, IL1RL1, also known as ST2.40 Previous work from our laboratory has uncovered that BCAP’s N-terminal TIR domain allows for interaction with other TIR-domain containing proteins to mediate the PI3K-Akt axis.25,31 As the ST2 receptor contains an intracellular TIR domain,40 we hypothesized that BCAP may play a role downstream of IL-33R. WT and BCAP KO BMDMs were stimulated with IL-33, and gene expression was assessed via RT-qPCR. Similar to IL-4 treatment, BCAP KO BMDMs were defective in their ability to up-regulate Akt-dependent genes, Arg1, Retnla, and Mgl2 upon IL-33 stimulation (Figure 4A). IL-33 has been previously described to mediate PI3K-Akt signaling41; therefore, we next asked whether BCAP KO BMDMs exhibited a defective M2 gene program due to dampened PI3K-Akt signaling. In accordance with its ability to initiate nuclear factor-κB signaling, IL-33-stimulated BMDMs displayed characteristics of pro-inflammatory macrophages as depicted by the upregulation of Tnf, Il1b, and Il6 transcripts.18 To identify whether BCAP played a role in facilitating IL-33-mediated inflammatory responses, WT and BCAP KO BMDMs were stimulated with IL-33 and the expression of inflammatory genes was assessed via RT-qPCR. We found that BCAP KO macrophages displayed higher expression of Tnf, Il1b, and Il6 transcripts (Figure 4C), suggesting that concomitant with a defective M2 phenotype, BCAP KO BMDMs exhibit a more M1 phenotype downstream of IL-33 signaling. To determine whether BCAP plays a global role in M2 induction, we stimulated BMDMs with IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine known to drive Arg1 expression.42 Interestingly, WT and BCAP KO BMDMs displayed comparable levels of Arg1 gene expression (Figure 4D), suggesting a stimulus-dependent role for BCAP in M2 macrophage differentiation. Collectively, our data demonstrate a broad, but not global, role for BCAP in facilitating an M2 gene program via the PI3K-Akt axis.

Tumor growth is compromised in BCAP-deficient mice

The TME is composed of a diverse and dynamic network of cancer cells, leukocytes, and stromal cells. The TME also contains numerous cytokines and chemotactic proteins that drive the differentiation of infiltrating monocytes into TAMs.43,44 TAMs typically adopt an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and have been shown to play a significant role in promoting the establishment of primary tumors and facilitating metastasis.45,46,47,48 IL-4 and IL-33 are important prognostic biomarkers that are released by both tumor cells and tumor-infiltrating leukocytes.49,50 Since we found BCAP to be a critical node in directly mediating M2 macrophage differentiation downstream of these cytokine receptors, we next asked whether BCAP mediated the inflammatory status of macrophages in the TME and, in turn, tumor progression.

Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells were implanted into WT and BCAP KO mice, and tumor growth was measured over a period of time. Tumors implanted into BCAP KO mice were smaller from the time they were measurable, and their growth over time was hampered when compared to tumors implanted into WT mice (Figure 5A). Importantly, tumor cells themselves did not express BCAP (Figure S4A), suggesting that tumor rejection in BCAP KO mice was not attributable to T cells expressing T cell receptors specific to BCAP-derived peptides. To characterize the immunophenotype of infiltrating cells, tumors were harvested, and cells were analyzed via flow cytometry (Figure 5B). We found that TAMs (live, CD11b+, F4/80+, Ly6C−) collected from BCAP KO mice expressed higher major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC II) on their surface as compared to TAMs from tumors taken from WT mice (Figure 5C). This is particularly significant as lower expression of MHC II on TAMs has been linked with tumor progression51 and classically activated (M1) macrophages, known for their pro-inflammatory traits, have been shown to upregulate MHC II as compared to their M2 macrophage counterparts.52 Furthermore, TAMs (Figure 5D) and infiltrating inflammatory monocytes (live, CD11b+, F4/80lo, Ly6C+) (Figure 5E) from BCAP KO mice had less CD206 expression on their surface, a hallmark immunosuppressive M2 TAM marker.53 Interestingly, peritoneal cavity macrophages collected from mice bearing tumors exhibited similar surface MHC II expression and similar intracellular Arg1 expression (Figure S4B), suggesting that the differences in gene programs between WT and BCAP KO TAMs were driven by specific cues within the TME. To determine whether our findings were specific to the LLC tumor growth or could be generalized across tumor types, we next employed the MC-38 adenocarcinoma tumor model. As with LLCs, we observed that MC-38 tumors implanted in BCAP KO mice were smaller when compared to WT control mice (Figure S4C) and their growth curve was significantly altered. TAMs isolated from the tumors of BCAP KO mice exhibited lower CD206 expression (Figure S4D). Although TAMs constitute the majority of tumor-infiltrating immune cells early on in disease progression, MC-38 is an immunogenic tumor that relies on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes for controlling tumor growth.54 CD8 T cells isolated from tumors of BCAP KO mice (Figure S4E) exhibited a more activated phenotype, distinguished by higher ICOS (Figure S4F) expression and more CD44+ PD1− cells (Figure S4G). Collectively, these data suggest that the tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells in BCAP-deficient mice exhibit a more pro-inflammatory phenotype and as a result, enhance anti-tumor immunity.

Absence of BCAP in myeloid cells impairs growth of implanted tumors

BCAP plays an important role in various immune cells, including B cells,24 T cells,31 and natural killer cells.55 To delineate the specific role of myeloid-intrinsic BCAP in mediating M2 TAM differentiation and tumor progression, we generated mice lacking BCAP in all myeloid cells. BCAPflox/flox (BCAPfl/fl) mice were crossed to mice expressing Cre under the control of the colony-stimulating factor receptor 1 (CSF1Rcre) promoter.56 These transgenic mice have a conditional deletion of BCAP only in the myeloid cell compartment (Figure 6A) resulting in a conditional deletion of BCAP restricted to the myeloid compartment, including macrophages, dendritic cells, and critically tumor-infiltrating monocytes. LLC cells were implanted into BCAPfl/fl and BCAPfl/fl CSF1Rcre mice, and tumors were monitored over time. When compared to BCAPfl/fl littermate controls, BCAPfl/fl CSF1Rcre mice exhibited smaller tumors (Figure 6B), suggesting that myeloid-intrinsic BCAP is the driver of tumor progression observed in total body BCAP KO mice. Flow cytometry of tumor-infiltrating macrophages revealed that TAMs from BCAPfl/fl CSF1Rcre mice expressed elevated levels of MHC II (Figure 6C) and markedly reduced expression of the M2 marker CD206 (Figure 6D), mirroring the phenotype seen in global BCAP KO tumors (Figure 5). Taken together, our data show that myeloid-intrinsic BCAP is responsible for mediating an immunosuppressive M2 TAM phenotype in vivo. Discussion

Here, we report a previously unknown role for BCAP in regulating M2 and TAM differentiation in response to several canonical M2 stimuli, such as IL-4 and IL-33, and identify BCAP as a key driver of TAM differentiation and subsequent tumor progression. Our previous work predicted the existence of a cryptic N-terminal TIR domain in BCAP and also demonstrated that truncation of BCAP’s TIR domain inhibited its ability to interact with upstream adapters such as MyD88.25,27 These TIR domain interactions are critical for the ability of BCAP to downregulate inflammatory responses. While BCAP also harbors several other domains known to regulate protein-protein interactions and signaling, their specific roles in macrophage biology have remained largely unexplored until now.

Our investigation into the role of BCAP downstream of IL-4R was prompted by a recent study that suggested interaction of BCAP with Grb228 that is known to function downstream of IL-4R. Consistent with our prediction that BCAP might be influencing IL-4R-mediated outcomes, we observed a striking defect in a specific M2 gene program following IL-4 stimulation in BCAP-deficient macrophages. Notably, the expression of Akt-dependent M2 genes Arg1, Mgl2, and Retnla was significantly compromised, while Akt-independent M2 genes such as Fabp4 and Chil334 remained unaffected. This points to a selective defect in BCAP-driven signaling. Alongside these findings, BCAP KO macrophages exhibited normal STAT6 activation but failed to trigger robust PI3K-Akt signaling downstream of IL-4, further highlighting the crucial role of BCAP in this pathway. In resting primary BMDMs, BCAP directly interacts with p85 and Grb2. However, upon IL-4 sensing, p85 interaction with BCAP was enriched in a signal-dependent manner. More importantly, we found that this association was critical for binding of p85 to other adaptor molecules such as Grb2. This is in line with the study that identified Grb2 to be constitutively bound to BCAP in resting HEK293T cells.28 The interaction of Grb2 to BCAP was solely dependent on its SH3 domain, presumably leaving its two SH2 domains free to bind phosphotyrosine residues on activated receptors.28 Previous work has shown that the adaptor protein IRS2 binds to the common γC of the IL-4R and binds to Grb2 via SH2 domain interactions29; however, whether this interaction is required for p85 activation and downstream PI3K signaling remained unclear.29 Our data suggest that BCAP binds to Grb2 via SH3 domain interactions, allowing for IRS2-Grb2 binding to occur via SH2 domain interactions upon IL-4 stimulation. This p85-Grb2 complex, which requires BCAP, identifies BCAP as a central node that rapidly orchestrates the PI3K-Akt signaling axis by facilitating p85 interactions with downstream adaptor molecules upon type I IL-4R signaling.

Furthermore, we found that BCAP does not play a role downstream of IL-13Rα1 signaling. Although IL-13 signals through the type II IL-4R, and activates STAT6 signaling, it does not utilize the common γC resulting in inefficient tyrosine phosphorylation of IRS2. As a result, previous work had also identified that BMDMs treated with IL-13 exhibited substantially less IRS2 interactions with p85 and Grb2 as compared to when treated with IL-4.29 Together, these findings suggest that BCAP might be required downstream of the γC signaling arm of the type I IL-4R to play a specific role in initiating the PI3K-Akt pathway.

Recent studies have also identified a role for IL-33 in driving the differentiation of M2 macrophages in an IL-4-independent manner.18,39,57 We found that upon IL-33 stimulation, expression of Arg1, Retnla, and Mgl2 were defective in BCAP KO macrophages. This is consistent with our previous demonstration of the importance of BCAP’s TIR domain that facilitates its participation in signaling downstream of TLR and IL-R.25,31 In contrast to previous studies,18 we found that BMDMs, either WT or BCAP KO, do not transcriptionally upregulate either Fabp4 or Chil3 in response to IL-33 (data not shown). The observed discrepancies between this study and others are likely due to primary versus secondary effects of IL-33 signaling. It has been reported that IL-33 signaling itself can induce production of IL-13 and IL-4,58 both leading to the activation of STAT6-dependent genes. Therefore, it is likely that the absence of Fabp4 and Chil3 induction is due to failure of IL-33 to directly activate STAT6-mediated gene transcription. To examine the potential role of BCAP downstream of other signals that drive M2 differentiation, we also conducted experiments using IL-10 as the upstream stimuli. We found Arg1 expression following IL-10 stimulation to be similar between WT and BCAP-deficient macrophages. IL-10 drives Arg1 expression via STAT3 signaling,59 suggesting a distinct mechanism by which IL-10 drives M2 gene expression. While our data make a compelling case for BCAP as a common signaling node that integrates signals from multiple upstream receptors such as IL-4R and IL-33R, there appear to be BCAP-independent mechanisms that drive M2 gene expression downstream of IL-13Rα1 and IL-10R that require further investigation.

While M2 differentiation is critical for tissue repair, TAMs also adopt an M2-like phenotype to promote tumor progression and impair anti-tumor immunity.60,61 As essential mediators, cytokines and chemokines released in the TME have been thoroughly implicated in driving the differentiation of tumor-infiltrating macrophages into TAMs and dictating the outcome of tumor progression.57,62,63 Among these cytokines, IL-33 has been implicated in the development of various cancers.49,64,65 A recent study identified that IL-33 promoted the differentiation of TAMs via activating the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, subsequently leading to accelerated NSCLC progression.57 Tumor sites are also well known to have upregulated expression of IL-4.66,67 Given the critical role of BCAP in regulating macrophage biology downstream of both IL-4 and IL-33 receptors, we posited that myeloid cell-intrinsic BCAP would be a critical driver of tumor progression. In line with our prediction, we found that in the absence of BCAP, tumor growth was significantly impaired. TAMs in BCAP-deficient mice had reduced expression of M2 genes but, more interestingly, exhibited a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Similar results were observed when BCAP was specifically deleted within the myeloid compartment, further implicating BCAP as a major driver of M2-like TAMs thus aiding in tumor progression. We intentionally used a Cre driver expressed in myeloid progenitor cells (CSF1R cre) so that BCAP would already be deleted in monocytes that would enter the tumor sites before differentiation into macrophages. Absence of BCAP in infiltrating myeloid cells inhibited the progression into typical TAMs and promoted macrophages to express pro-inflammatory genes. Future experiments testing the nature of the cues that drive pro-inflammatory gene expression in the absence of BCAP in the TME are required. Similarly, transcriptional profiling of TAMs in BCAP-sufficient and -deficient microenvironments is likely to lead to deeper insights into the role of BCAP in regulating macrophage biology.

Coinciding with a more inflammatory myeloid compartment, tumor-infiltrating CD8 T cells isolated from BCAP KO mice also exhibited increased activation status, as demonstrated by higher levels of CD44 and ICOS and lower levels of PD-1. PD-1 expression is a hallmark of T cell exhaustion,68 and expression of this molecule hampers tumor eradication by the immune system.69 Recent studies have shown that TAM-T cell interactions are critical in the progression of exhaustion.70,71,72 TAM-CD8 T cell interactions facilitate unique immune synapse interactions that promote exhaustion, and TAMs were primarily composed of CD206+ cells.72 While more sophisticated studies are required to profile the chemokines and cytokines by which BCAP-deficient TAMs drive CD8 T cell activation, it is tempting to speculate that prevention, or reversion, of M2 TAM differentiation, by deletion of myeloid-intrinsic BCAP, may mitigate T cell dysfunction and improve tumor control by tumor-infiltrating leukocytes. Future studies will aim to determine the precise mechanisms through which BCAP-deficient macrophages promote anti-tumor responses with potentially insightful implications for anti-cancer therapeutics. Furthermore, validating BCAP expression and its correlation with M2 markers in human tumors will be critical to establishing the clinical relevance of targeting BCAP to strengthen anti-tumor immune responses in patients.

Together, our studies show that BCAP is a critical adaptor molecule that links diverse upstream stimuli to the PI3K-Akt axis to drive M2 macrophage differentiation. While previous studies have shown a prominent role for IL-4 signaling and subsequent STAT6 activation in promoting tumor progression,63,73 loss of this pathway results in complete abrogation of M2 functions, including those necessary for reparative functions. The ability of BCAP to exclusively modulate the PI3K-Akt axis, while leaving STAT6 signaling intact, makes BCAP a particularly attractive therapeutic target in the context of solid tumors for this reason. Therapeutic targeting of the PI3K-Akt pathway has been another focus of cancer therapy development. However, the complexity of this signaling network provides avenues of resistance to these inhibitors.74 Additionally, pan-PI3K inhibitors can have significant off-target effects and toxicities, as PI3K-Akt signaling plays a critical role in growth and survival in many cell types required for homeostasis.37 In contrast, our findings show that BCAP specifically enhances PI3K-Akt signaling in macrophages, offering a unique and potentially safer therapeutic angle. Future studies exploring small molecule inhibitors that selectively target myeloid-intrinsic BCAP or disrupt its interaction with p85 could open exciting avenues for clinical attempts to treat solid tumors.

Limitations of the study

We show in this study that BCAP is required for proper p85-Grb2 interactions downstream of IL-4. The specific mechanism and domains by which BCAP mediates these interactions, or whether this interaction is critical for PI3K-Akt activation, has not been fully explored and requires further investigation. Furthermore, molecular assays looking at IRS2-interacting partners are challenging due to low basal expression of this protein in macrophages. Demonstrating direct BCAP and IRS2 interactions requires further investigation. Although we have previously demonstrated that BCAP’s TIR domain allows it to mediate signaling downstream of TIR domain-containing receptors (TLR2/4) we have not explicitly demonstrated whether BCAP’s TIR domain is required for IL-33-mediated PI3K-Akt signaling. In our targeted deletion of myeloid-intrinsic BCAP, we chose to utilize a CSF1Rcre model over other myeloid-specific systems, such as LysMcre. While the CSF1Rcre allows for more efficient and complete deletion of target molecules, we recognize that deletion of BCAP in other myeloid cells (myeloid DCs, etc.) may occur. Macrophages represent the most abundant myeloid cells within the TME, and thus it is reasonable to assume that deletion of macrophage-intrinsic BCAP is the primary driver of our observed in vivo phenotype. Additionally, BCAP expression in other myeloid cells does not limit its feasibility as a small molecule therapy and may further enhance anti-tumor responses.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

C57BL/6 (WT) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory or from the Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center Mouse Breeding Core Facility. BCAP KO mice were previously generated and were a gift from T. Kurosaki, Laboratory for Lymphocyte Differentiation, RIKEN Center for Integrative Medical Sciences (IMS-RCAI), Kanagawa, Japan, and Laboratory for Lymphocyte Differentiation, WPI Immunology Frontier Research Center, Osaka University, Osaka, Japan. BCAPfl/fl mice were previously generated by the University of Texas Southwestern Transgenic Core. CSF1Rcre transgenic mice (obtained from Jackson Laboratory) were crossed to BCAPfl/fl mice to generate mice that specifically lack BCAP in myeloid cells. BCAPfl/fl littermates were used as controls for all experiments with BCAPfl/fl CSF1Rcre mice. Male and female mice were used between 6 and 12 weeks of age and were maintained in specific pathogen-free conditions. No influence of sex was reported for all conducted experiments. Mice were group-housed in environmentally enriched cages with humidity and temperature-controlled rooms with a 12 h light dark cycle. Mouse genotypes were determined using PCR with transgene specific probes (Integrated DNA Technologies). All mouse experiments were done following approved protocols (#2024-0012) by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center.

Cell lines

MC-38 (female C57B/6), L929 (male C57B/6), and LLC (male C57B/6) cells were tested and found negative for mycoplasma contamination. Cells were authenticated by growth curve analysis and morphology check by microscope. All cells were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FCS in standard conditions (5% CO2, 37°C).

Method details

Preparation of macrophages and cell culture

For BMDM generation, mice were euthanized, and femur and tibia were harvested. Bones were crushed and filtered using a mortar and pestle and a 70 μm filter. Cells were pelleted and red blood cells were lysed. Cells were washed in 10% complete RPMI prior to being plated in a tissue-culture treated Petri dish overnight at 37°C in 10% complete RPMI containing 30% L929 culture supernatant. The following day, non-adherent cells were collected, spun down, and plated on a non-treated Petri dish at 4 × 106 cells/8 mL of media for 5 days. After differentiation, supernatant was removed and adhered BMDMs were removed off the plate with a brief incubation with ice-cold PBS containing 2 mM EDTA. BMDMs were brought to desired concentration in 10% complete RPMI and re-plated for the following experiments.

For resident peritoneal cavity macrophage isolation, mice were euthanized, and the peritoneal cavity was exposed. PBS was carefully injected into the peritoneal cavity using a 23-gauge needle. Using the same needle, fluid was aspirated from the peritoneum and fluid was dispensed into a 50 mL conical tube and kept on ice. Cells were spun down at 1300 RPM for 10 min. Cells were resuspended in PBS containing FCS and incubated with FC Shield (#70-0161-U500; Tonbo biosciences) for 10 min on ice before cells were magnetically sorted by negative selection. The following biotinylated antibodies were added (1:100): anti-CD19 (#115504; BioLegend), anti-CD90.2 (#140314; BioLegend), anti-Ly6G (#127603; BioLegend), anti-Ly6C (#128003; BioLegend) and anti-NK1.1 (#108703; BioLegend). Cells were incubated with antibodies on ice for 15 min before MojoSort Streptavidin nanobeads (#480016; BioLegend) were added. Cells were incubated on ice again for 15 min prior to magnetic separation. The negative fraction was taken for the following experiments.

For visceral adipose tissue macrophages isolation, adipose tissue was harvested from the abdomen of WT and BCAP KO mice through direct excision. Tissue was placed into a conical containing 10 mL of DMEM + HEPES and immediately minced. Tubes were spun down at 1000 g for 5 min at 4°C. Media and pellet were removed, leaving behind “wafer” at the top. Digestion buffer (7mL/sample DMEM +HEPES containing 2% BSA, 0.03 mg/mL Liberase blendzyme 3 collagenase cocktail, and 50 U/mL DNAse) was added to “wafer”. Samples were incubated at 37°C for 45 min shaking at 225 rpm. After incubation, samples were filtered and spun down. Cell pellet was RBC lysed before being washed with PBS prior to flow cytometry.

Macrophages were plated at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL in a 12- or 24-well tissue culture treated plate. Where indicated, cells were stimulated with IL-4 (20 ng/mL) (#214-14; Peprotech), IL-33 (10 ng/mL) (#580502; Biolegend), Wortmannin (1 μM) (#9951; Cell Signaling), IL-13 (20 ng/mL) (#210-13; Peprotech) or IL-10 (10 ng/mL) (#210-10-10UG; ThermoFischer scientific) for the indicated times. Cells were treated with Wortmannin 30 min prior to IL-4 stimulation where indicated. To remove cells after culturing, supernatant was removed from well and wells were carefully washed 2x with PBS. PBS was removed and lysis buffer was directly added to the wells.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

RNA was isolated using Qiagen RNA extraction kit (#74104; Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesized using a Verso cDNA synthesis kit (AB1453A; ThermoFischer scientific). The QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (ThermoFisher Scientific) was used to measure SYBR green (#4309155; ThermoFisher Scientific) incorporation. All cDNA was normalized to Hprt1.

Immunoblotting

Cells were lysed in 1x RIPA buffer (#37576; ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with protease (#1169748001; Roche) and phosphatase (#4906845001; Roche) inhibitors. Protein concentrations were quantified and equalized using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (#23225; ThermoFisher Scientific). Cell lysates were boiled in 1x Lamelli buffer (#EC-887; National Diagnostics) at 70°C for 10 min before lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Blots were blocked in 5% BSA in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and probed for the following antibodies (1:1000) overnight at 4°C: anti-Grb2 (#3972S; CST), anti-p85 (#4292S; Cell Signaling), anti-Pp85 (#4228; Cell Signaling), anti-Akt (#4691; Cell Signaling), anti-pAkt (#4058; Cell Signaling), anti-Arg1 (#93668; Cell Signaling), anti-BCAP (#AF4857; R&D), anti-H3 (#9715; Cell Signaling) and anti-βtubulin (#5568; Cell Signaling). The following day, blots were washed in TBST before being probed with appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to HRP (1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein was visualized by incubating blots with West Pico Plus ECL Substrate (#34577; ThermoFischer Scientific) for 30 s then enhanced chemiluminescence was recorded on autoradiography films using a Kodak developer.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Cells were pelleted, washed once with PBS, then lysed in 400 μL Mammalian Cell Lysis Buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl pH 8, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, 50 mM sodium fluoride, 5 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 10 mM sodium-B-glycerophosphate, 270 mM sucrose, 1% v/v Triton X-100, 0.1% v/v 2-Mercaptoethanol, cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail) for 2 h on ice and then cleared of debris by centrifugation at 15,000 RPM for 10 min at 4°C. Protein was quantified by Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit and a small portion of lysate was collected to use as input controls. The rest of the lysate was incubated with anti-p85 (#4292S; Cell Signaling) (1:50) or anti-BCAP (#AF4857; R&D) (1:100) antibodies where indicated and rotated overnight at 4°C. Dynabeads Protein G (#10003D; Invitrogen) were added to lysates and rotated for 1 h at 4°C followed by 3 washes with Mammalian Cell Lysis Buffer. Protein was eluted by boiling in reduced 1X NuPAGE LDS buffer (#NP0007; Invitrogen) at 70°C for 10 min. Protein was visualized via immunoblotting as described above.

Flow cytometry

For surface marker staining, cells were washed 2x with PBS then stained with Zombie Yellow (#423103; BioLegend) (1:1000) at room temperature for 10 min. Cells were then blocked with FC Shield (1:1000) on ice for 10 min and then incubated with antibodies of interest for 30 min. The following antibodies were used at 1:400 unless noted otherwise: anti-Arg1 (#25-3697-82; Invitrogen), anti-CD124 (IL-4R) (#144807; BioLegend), anti-CD11b (#101243; BioLegend), anti-F4/80 (#123116; BioLegend), anti-Ly6C (#128037; BioLegend), anti-CD11c (#117339; BioLegend), anti-MHCII (1:200) (#107-621; BioLegend, anti-CD206 (1:200) (#141717; BioLegend), anti-CD279 (PD1) (#135225; BioLegend), anti-CD44 (#103007; BioLegend), and anti-CD278 (ICOS) (#313523; BioLegend). Cells were washed with FACS buffer (PBS, 2% FCS, and 2 mM EDTA) 2x before being analyzed using Novocyte 3001 (ACEA Biosciences). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (BD).

Tumor experiments

MC-38 or LLC cells in PBS were subcutaneously injected into the left flank of 6 to 12-week-old WT, BCAP KO, BCAPfl/fl, or BCAPfl/fl CSF1Rcre mice. Mice were sacrificed when indicated, tumors were harvested, and tumor weights were recorded. To isolate tumor-infiltrating leukocytes, 1 mL of serum-free media was added, and tumors were cut into small pieces using scissors. Tumor lysates were transferred into a 15 mL tube and volume was adjusted to 4 mL with PBS. To each tube, 80 μL of Liberase TL (14 U/mL stock solution) (#5401020001; Roche) and 40 μL of DNAse (15 mg/mL stock solution 100x) (#D8515; Sigma-Aldrich) were added. Tubes were mixed using a vortex and incubated for 45 min at 37°C under continuous rotation. To end enzymatic reaction, 10 mL of complete media was added, and samples were filtered before cells were spun down for 7 min at 500 rcf at 4°. Supernatant was discarded and cell pellet was resuspended in 3 mL of RBC lysis buffer and left on ice for 10 min. PBS supplemented with FCS was added to samples before cells were spun down and subject to flow cytometry as described above.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Number of biological replicates and independent experiments as well as statistical tests performed are reported in each figure legend. Throughout the manuscript, independent biological replicates are defined as independently performed experiments from different animals. All statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism v10. Error bars shown as ± SEM. Statistical significance was assessed by the test depicted in the respective figure legends (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001 and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001; n.s., not significant.)
