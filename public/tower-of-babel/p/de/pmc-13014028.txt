Relations between impulsivity and drinking in adults with alcohol use disorder during recovery: A longitudinal observational cohort study
Authors: Molly L. Garber, Kyla L. Belisario, Amanda Doggett, Allan Clifton, John F. Kelly, James MacKillop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImpulsivity is a multifaceted construct robustly associated with alcohol use disorder (AUD). Few studies, however, have examined its relations with drinking during a recovery attempt, and those that have typically used coarse measurements with few follow‐up periods. This study examined prospective and reciprocal relations between multiple indices of impulsivity and drinking over a 1‐year period among adults with AUD making a significant recovery attempt.MethodsParticipants (N = 501, M age = 41.4, 42.5% Male, 79% White) were enrolled at two sites. Longitudinal trajectories of alcohol use and impulsivity were examined using mixed effects and random‐intercept cross‐lagged panel models. Drinking variables included drinks per week (DpW), percent drinking days (%DD), and heavy drinking days (%HDD). Impulsivity variables included Negative/Positive Urgency, Sensation Seeking, Lack of Premeditation, Lack of Perseverance, and a delay discounting task.ResultsCompared with baseline, significant decreases in multiple facets of impulsivity were observed during the follow‐up. The most robust relations were for Negative Urgency, with bidirectional cross‐lagged effects observed for all drinking outcomes (βs = 0.09–0.18, ps < 0.05) from months 3–12. Similarly, Positive Urgency forecasted subsequent %DD months 3–12 (βs = 0.06–0.08, ps < 0.05), and Lack of Perseverance predicted %HDD (βs = 0.062–0.066, ps < 0.05) from months 3–12. In other cases, drinking forecasted changes in impulsivity over time.ConclusionsFindings highlight the malleability and predictive validity of impulsivity during AUD recovery, but reveal potential specificity by indicator and timeframe. The consistent later‐stage bidirectional associations between Negative Urgency and drinking suggest the importance of continuing to address negative emotion regulation during the AUD recovery process.

INTRODUCTION

Alcohol is among one of the most widely used substances worldwide, and while many who drink do so without problems, a substantial minority will go on to develop an alcohol use disorder (AUD). Estimations of the past‐year prevalence of AUD in the United States are around 13%, and around one‐third of the population will develop an AUD at some point throughout the course of their lives (Grant et al., 2015, 2017). There are well‐documented associations between AUD and negative effects on health, well‐being, and morbidity and mortality, with an enormous economic cost, with estimates of nearly 250 billion in the United States (Carr et al., 2024; MacKillop et al., 2022; Roerecke & Rehm, 2014; Sacks et al., 2015). Prognosis of AUD varies, with some exhibiting a chronic, relapsing pattern while others experience stable remission (Tucker et al., 2020). Given the significant harms associated with AUD and variations in its clinical course, understanding factors which contribute to AUD development, maintenance, and recovery remains a priority.

AUD can be understood broadly as a disorder of impulsivity and self‐regulation deficits, where individuals repeatedly fail to inhibit the urge to drink despite significant negative consequences (de Wit, 2009; Dick et al., 2010). Impulsivity is considered as a key risk factor to the onset and development of AUD, and a key process involved in its maintenance. For example, higher impulsivity in adolescence predicts future alcohol problems, and those with higher impulsivity are more likely to go on to develop an AUD and to drop out of treatment or relapse (Charney et al., 2010; Daigre et al., 2019; Pepe et al., 2023; Rubio et al., 2008; Tschorn et al., 2021). However, few studies examining relationships between impulsivity and drinking outcomes specifically during the recovery process have been conducted. Those that do, use broad, nonspecific measurements of impulsivity that may cloud interpretation (Blonigen et al., 2011, 2013). Studies suggest that impulsivity is a multidimensional construct consisting of several correlated but distinct dimensions, such as a constellation of impulsive personality traits, impulsive choice (delay discounting: a preference for smaller sooner rewards over larger later ones), and impulsive behavior (difficulties inhibiting a prepotent motor response) (MacKillop et al., 2016).

Studies that use global, nonspecific measures of impulsivity may overlook important differences in the relationships between specific facets of the construct in relation to AUD and drinking outcomes. Indeed, studies that utilize finer‐grained measurements of impulsivity have documented differential relations between impulsivity and drinking/AUD outcomes (e.g., Dick et al., 2010; Littlefield et al., 2014; Shin et al., 2012). For example, one meta‐analysis focusing on impulsive personality and alcohol use found that while all facets of impulsive personality were related to some alcohol outcome, effects varied by impulsive personality dimension and alcohol outcome. All traits were equally associated with drinking frequency, Lack of Perseverance was most strongly related to quantity, and Urgency (i.e., acting rashly in response to strong affect) was most robustly related to problems/AUD (Coskunpinar et al., 2013). Longitudinal studies also tend to reveal significant, positive associations between various facets of impulsivity and alcohol use (Juchem et al., 2024; Kaiser et al., 2016; Kearns et al., 2022; Woods‐Gonzalez et al., 2024). However, the use of nonclinical samples, limited number of timepoints, and lack of research examining these associations through the context of recovery significantly limits inferences which can be drawn from these studies. More broadly, intervention research suggests that personality traits may change during treatment. One systematic review found that emotional stability and extraversion increased following psychological intervention (Roberts et al., 2017), although impulsivity was not directly examined. Another systematic review focused on personality changes in substance use disorders (SUDs) and over substance use treatment specifically, found equivocal evidence of impulsivity changes during substance use treatment, although only four studies met the review's inclusion criteria (Juchem et al., 2024).

Research focused on delay discounting also suggests that those with SUDs discount delayed rewards at a steeper rate than those without SUDs (Amlung et al., 2017; MacKillop et al., 2011). As a predictor of treatment outcome, results are more mixed and limited. Some tobacco cessation research has revealed that it is a negative prognostic factor, while cannabis‐focused research revealed no effects (Peters et al., 2013; Syan et al., 2021). One small study on alcohol use found trend associations between baseline delay discounting and treatment dropout/relapse (Rupp et al., 2016). Research also suggests that delay discounting may be a malleable component of impulsivity in response to specific, targeted intervention (Bickel et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2022). However, it remains unclear how delay discounting relates to drinking in the context of AUD recovery more broadly without specific, targeted intervention. Collectively, longitudinal studies that use fine‐grained measurements of impulsivity and multiple dimensions of alcohol consumption during the recovery period are needed to more effectively triangulate the relationship between alcohol use and impulsivity during recovery.

Longitudinal models within a structural equation modeling (SEM) framework are particularly well positioned to examine dynamic relationships between impulsivity and drinking over time. Specifically, random‐intercept cross‐lagged panel models (RI‐CLPMs) allow for the examination of the associations between drinking and impulsivity outcomes longitudinally by separating between‐ and within‐person effects. The former can be interpreted as stable inter‐individual associations of alcohol and impulsivity over time, while the latter can be interpreted as the intra‐individual associations whereby drinking outcomes at a previous time point can influence impulsivity measures at a later time point, and vice versa (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021; Usami et al., 2019). Additionally, RI‐CLPMs allow for the examination of whether there are changes in the within‐person associations over time during the recovery process (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021), which is expected given the rapid changes that occur during the first year of recovery (Hagman et al., 2022).

Using this RI‐CLPM analytic framework, this study sought to extend the literature by conducting a longitudinal examination of multiple facets of impulsive personality and impulsive choice in a sample of community adults who self‐identified as starting a significant recovery attempt. Specifically, the primary aims of the current study were to examine (1) trajectories of impulsivity over time, and (2) cross‐lagged relationships between impulsivity variables and drinking quantity, frequency, and intensity. Because research suggests that drinking patterns differ by sex and impulsivity differs by age (Harden & Tucker‐Drob, 2011; White, 2020), the secondary aim was to examine whether anticipated relationships were moderated by sex (male and female) or age (dichotomized via median split as <35 and 35+). Although changes in impulsivity were broadly predicted to forecast changes in drinking, specific hypotheses were not made for each impulsivity variable.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

Participants (N = 501) were enrolled in a longitudinal study at two sites, one in Hamilton, Ontario, Canada, and one in Boston, Massachusetts, USA. To be eligible, participants were required to be between 21 and 65 years old, identify alcohol as a substance of choice, meet criteria for AUD, report high‐risk drinking in the 30 days prior to their recovery attempt (defined as 7 or more drinks per week for females, 14 or more drinks per week for males), and have initiated a significant recovery attempt (i.e., begun either formal AUD treatment or undertaken an informal attempt during the past 90 days or the next 14 days). Participants were excluded if they screened positive for symptoms of psychosis or suicidality. Recruitment originally took place at local inpatient and outpatient alcohol treatment centers, however, following the onset of COVID‐19 lockdowns, participants were also recruited from the community using online advertising strategies. The protocol consisted of six assessments: the baseline assessment (T1) was completed between May 2019 and September 2021, T2 occurred 4–6 weeks after baseline, and all subsequent timepoints (T3–T6) occurred at 3‐month intervals, with T6 representing 1‐year post recovery attempt. Both T2 and T3 are considered to have captured participants' initial recovery period, while T4 through T6 are considered a part of participants' early recovery period (Hagman et al., 2022). Overall, 68.7% had data at all assessments, 95.2% of participants had data for at least one follow‐up assessment, and 79.8% were retained at the final assessment. The average number of assessments completed was 5.2 (SD = 1.5), and the median and modal completion was 6. See Table 1 for participant characteristics.

Assessments were initially conducted in‐person and were adapted to a virtual format following the onset of COVID‐19. Participants completed a battery of clinical interviews, self‐report instruments, and cognitive assessments. All participants provided their informed consent, and ethics approval was obtained from the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (protocol no.: 3825) and the Partners Human Research Committee at Massachusetts General Hospital (protocol no.: 2017P002345).

Measures

Three dimensions of alcohol use were examined in this study: drinking frequency (percent drinking days), quantity (drinks per week), and intensity (heavy drinking days). These measures were captured by the Timeline Follow‐Back Interview (TLFB; Sobell & Sobell, 1992) at T1 and any subsequent in‐person assessments. The Daily Drinking Questionnaire (DDQ) was employed for virtual assessments in later waves where participants would have otherwise been lost to follow‐up due to an inability to complete the TLFB interview with study staff. The TLFB captures the number of standard drinks consumed on each calendar day of the past 90 days (45 days for T2 and T3), while the DDQ captures the typical number of standard drinks consumed for each day of the week. Both provide measures of percent drinking days (%DD), average number of drinks consumed per week (DpW), and percent heavy drinking days (%HDD).

Impulsive personality was measured at each assessment wave via the 5 subscales of the Short UPPS Personality Scale (Cyders et al., 2014). Negative and Positive Urgency refer to the tendency to act out under conditions of strong negative or positive affect, respectively. Sensation Seeking refers to a tendency to seek out novel experiences. Lack of Perseverance refers to the tendency to have difficulty completing tasks. Lack of Premeditation refers to a tendency to act without foresight into consequences of an action. Internal consistency across waves and impulsive personality scales ranged 0.79–0.93; see Data S1 for reliability coefficients by impulsive personality subscale.

Delay discounting was measured through the 5‐trial adjusting delay discounting task (Koffarnus & Bickel, 2014). Participants' calculated k‐values were only included for a given magnitude if they passed a quality control item, which asked participants whether they would prefer the now or delayed monetary reward now (i.e., no delay in time) where the participant if paying adequate attention would choose the larger monetary reward. The average of the two k‐values for $100 and $1000 were subsequently log transformed. Longitudinally, they were highly correlated, average r = 0.84 (range = 0.83–0.87).

The covariates included in this study were site location (Canada vs. United States), sex assigned at birth (male vs. female), race (White vs. racialized), education (Bachelor's degree or higher vs. less than a Bachelors), and a 4‐point Likert scale measure of subjective income (Najdzionek et al., 2023).

Data analysis

Linear mixed effects models were used to contrast the pre‐recovery time point from intra‐recovery time points. They were also used to contrast a pooled initial recovery time point (combined T2 and T3) from the early recovery time points, to examine the overall declines in drinking‐related outcomes and changes in impulsivity between recovery phases. Mixed effects models were constructed using the “lme4” (Bates et al., 2015) R package and post hoc contrasts were examined using the “emmeans” (Lenth, 2023) R package.

Random‐intercept cross‐lagged panel models were used to examine the reciprocity of drinking‐related outcomes and impulsivity within persons. RI‐CLPMs disaggregate stable trait factors (between‐person effects) from within‐person effects, allowing for the examination of whether time‐specific deviations in drinking are related to deviations in impulsivity or vice versa (Usami, 2021). The RI‐CLPMs were run using the “MplusAutomation” package in R (Hallquist & Wiley, 2018), using Mplus version 8.10 (Muthen & Muthen, 1998–2017). A robust maximum likelihood estimator was used to account for multivariate non‐normality. Specifically, this approach incorporates skewness and kurtosis in a weighted matrix, adjusting the standard errors and model fit statistics. This required the use of the Satorra–Bentler Scaled (SBS) chi‐squared difference test using the “SBSDiff” R package (Mann, 2018) to compare nested models. Full Information Maximum Likelihood handled missingness in the data. For all statistical significance, an alpha of 0.05 was used. Effect sizes for the standardized within‐person effects were defined as 0.03–0.06 as small, 0.07–0.11 as medium, and 0.12+ as large (Orth et al., 2024).

To account for the cross‐lagged effects that occur within the initial recovery stage (<3 months) and the early recovery stage (3 to 12 months), constraints on the auto‐ and cross‐lagged effects were tested separately from one another. For example, if testing whether the auto‐lagged effects could be constrained equal over time, the auto‐lagged effects from T1 to T3 would be constrained the same, and subsequent auto‐lagged effects from T3 to T6 would be constrained the same. A full breakdown of the models tested can be found in Table S1. Of these models, the SBS chi‐squared difference test was used to test each constrained model to the base (fully unconstrained) model, retaining the most constrained model. The best fitting models were retained for adjusted RI‐CLPMs which added covariates to the model by regressing them on the observed variables in the models. See Figure 1 for the path diagram. Presence of contemporaneous effects between drinking and impulsivity for within‐person effects were also tested by adding endogenous reciprocal effects of drinking on impulsivity and vice versa at the same time point. Endogenous reciprocal effects took the place of the covariance of drinking and impulsivity, and were constrained equal across all time points. When regressions are not statistically significant and the estimates are not zero (i.e., did not terminate at the border of parameter space), they can be eliminated from the model (Muthén & Asparouhov, 2024).

RI‐CLPM path diagram. ALC, alcohol variable; IMP, impulsivity variable.

To examine whether there was a presence of moderators of the cross‐lagged effects in the models above, two models were run: one whereby the model effects are constrained equal across the moderator groups and one whereby the effects are allowed to vary. SBS chi‐square difference testing was then used to inform whether there were differences between moderator groups (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021). Moderators tested were sex assigned at birth and age (< 35 versus 35+, based on median split). Across analyses, drinking variables were modeled as continuous variables. Though, because alcohol variables could also be considered as count variables, to compare fit statistics, alcohol variables were rounded to the nearest integer and then subsequently tested as count indicators using a negative binomial distribution. However, this approach resulted in fatal modeling errors and thus this approach was not further pursued.

Negative Urgency

Adjusted models revealed four positive, large magnitude cross‐lagged coefficients from %DD to Negative Urgency: one in the initial recovery phase (β %DD = 0.19, p < 0.05) and three in the early recovery phase (β %DD = 0.17–0.18, ps <0.05). Additionally, all three cross‐lagged coefficients from Negative Urgency to %DD in the early recovery phase revealed medium effects (βs%DD = 0.10, ps <0.05), suggesting reciprocal effects between Negative Urgency and %DD across time, whereby decreases in Negative Urgency were associated with decreases in %DD, and vice versa. For DpW and %HDD, a similar pattern of bidirectional results emerged where significant and medium‐to‐large effects were observed from DpW and %HDD to Negative Urgency (β DpW = 0.12–0.14, β%HDD = 0.09–0.10, ps <0.05) and Negative Urgency to DpW and %HDD (β DpW = 0.09–0.10 β%HDD = 0.10, ps <0.05) in the early recovery, but not initial recovery stage. Significant random‐intercept effects were observed for DpW and %HDD (β DpW = 0.23 and β %HDD = 0.24, ps <0.05), but not %DD. No contemporaneous effects were observed.

Positive Urgency

Adjusted models revealed three cross‐lagged small‐to‐medium magnitude effects from Positive Urgency to later %DD in the early recovery phase (βs%DD = 0.06–0.08, ps <0.05). For DpW, two positive medium‐sized cross‐lagged effects from DpW to later Positive Urgency in the early recovery phase were observed (β DpW = 0.10 and 0.11, ps <0.05). There were no significant cross‐lagged effects between Positive Urgency and drinking intensity observed in the adjusted model. Random‐intercept effects were not observed between Positive Urgency and any drinking variables. No contemporaneous effects were observed.

Sensation Seeking

One positive, large magnitude cross‐lagged effect from Sensation Seeking to later %DD was observed in the initial recovery stage (β %DD = 0.19, p < 0.05). There were no significant cross‐lagged effects observed between Sensation Seeking and DpW. For %HDD, the three medium‐to‐large magnitude negative cross‐lagged effects from %HDD to Sensation Seeking were observed in the early recovery period (β %HDD = −0.11 to −0.12, ps <0.05). Contemporaneous effects between %HDD and Sensation Seeking were also observed and rendered these cross‐lagged effects nonsignificant (see Table S7). Random‐intercept effects were not observed between Sensation Seeking and any drinking variables.

Lack of Premeditation

No cross‐lagged effects were observed between Lack of Premeditation and %DD. For DpW, two large cross‐lagged effects from DpW to later Lack of Premeditation were observed in the early recovery stage (β DpW = 0.13 and 0.15, ps <0.05). For %HDD, three large positive cross‐lagged effects were observed from %HDD to later Lack of Premeditation in the early recovery stage (β %HDD = 0.13–0.14, ps <0.05). Random‐intercept effects were observed between Lack of Premeditation and DpW (β DpW = 0.16, p < 0.05) and %HDD (β%HDD = 0.13, p < 0.05), but not %DD. No contemporaneous effects were observed.

Lack of Perseverance

No significant cross‐lagged effects were observed between Lack of Perseverance and %DD. Regarding DpW, one significant cross‐lagged effect in the initial recovery stage was observed whereby Lack of Perseverance predicted later DpW (β DpW = 0.15, p < 0.05). For %HDD, three positive cross‐lagged effects of small‐to‐medium magnitude were observed from Lack of Perseverance to later %HDD in the early recovery stage (β %HDD = 0.06–0.07, ps <0.05). A random‐intercept effect was observed between Lack of Perseverance and DpW (β DpW = 0.15, p < 0.019), and %DD (β %DD = 0.13, p < 0.05) but not %HDD. No contemporaneous effects were observed.

Delay Discounting

No significant cross‐lagged effects were observed for %DD or DpW. For %HDD, one cross‐lagged effect was observed from %HDD to subsequent Delay Discounting in the early recovery stage (β %HDD = 0.13, p < 0.05). A large negative random‐intercept effect was observed between Delay Discounting and %DD (β %DD = −0.19, p < 0.05), but not DpW or %HDD. No contemporaneous effects were observed.

Moderation effects

SBS chi‐squared differences tested whether the models by sex (male vs. female) and age (<35 vs. 35+) could be constrained the same. The results generally supported constraining the models, indicating no differences by age or sex for any of the impulsive personality variables. However, age was revealed as a significant moderator between Delay Discounting and %DD and %HDD. Upon further examination, however, these differences were restricted to auto‐lagged, not cross‐lagged, effects and thus did not speak to leading or lagging relationships over time. See Tables S6 and S7 for a comparison of model differences.

DISCUSSION

This study focused on longitudinally examining relationships between multiple facets of impulsivity and alcohol consumption throughout the first year following the start of a significant AUD recovery attempt. It characterized trajectories of impulsive personality, impulsive choice, and alcohol consumption, including drinking quantity (DpW), frequency (%DD), and intensity (%HDD). Most importantly, it used RI‐CLPMs to separate within‐ and between‐person relationships to examine associations between impulsivity and drinking, and whether these relationships were moderated by sex or age. Generally, results from this study support the malleability of impulsive personality during recovery and highlight the interconnected and bidirectional nature of the relationship between Negative Urgency and drinking behavior throughout recovery, regardless of sex or age.

Trajectory analyses revealed several impulsivity characteristics which decreased during the recovery period. Evidence generally suggests that impulsivity is a relatively stable trait that increases during adolescence and young adulthood, stabilizes across adulthood, and reduces in older adulthood (Harden & Tucker‐Drob, 2011; Liu et al., 2020). Findings from this study add nuance to this general trajectory and suggest that certain facets of impulsivity are subject to change within the context of recovery. Broadly, there were significant reductions in most facets of impulsivity, except for Lack of Perseverance and Delay Discounting. Most reductions in impulsive personality were delayed 3 months into the recovery period (Positive Urgency, Sensation Seeking, and Lack of Premeditation), but for Negative Urgency, these reductions were present at the 6‐week assessment. Furthermore, during the recovery timeframe Negative Urgency continued to decrease, with 6‐, 9‐ and 12‐month follow‐ups significantly lower than the average Negative Urgency scores reported at the initial recovery phase (6‐week and 3‐month assessments). It was surprising that Delay Discounting did not exhibit a downward trajectory, although a previous study of changes within an inpatient setting reported nonsignificant changes over the admission (Levitt et al., 2023). Treatment studies which specifically target delay discounting (i.e., episodic future thinking) convincingly suggest that it is a mutable individual characteristic (Ye et al., 2022), but it is possible that explicit targeting is necessary for these changes to be observed.

The RI‐CLPM results illuminate Negative Urgency as potentially important in the context of drinking and recovery. While impulsivity at one timepoint predicted drinking in subsequent timepoints (or vice versa) for many impulsive personality variables, Negative Urgency was the only impulsivity facet to exhibit bidirectional effects, whereby elevations in Negative Urgency predicted increases in drinking at the subsequent timepoint, and increases in drinking at one timepoint predicted increases in Negative Urgency at the next timepoint. This bidirectional pattern was consistent across all timepoints in the early recovery phase and across all drinking variables. These findings are consistent with accounts of addiction as a disorder of dysfunctional negative reinforcement motivation, where individuals use substances to alleviate or escape negative emotional experiences, and highlight the cyclical nature of these relationships that serve to maintain drinking behavior over time (Khantzian, 2003; Koob & Volkow, 2016; Kwako et al., 2016). They highlight this mechanism as being especially important to target during treatment. These findings also have implications for further research focused on investigating the mechanisms through which treatment confers benefits. For example, it has been hypothesized that changes in impulsivity are one of the therapeutic mechanisms through which individuals benefit from treatment and continuing care. One study of individuals attending Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) following treatment revealed associations between reductions in impulsivity, longer duration of AA involvement, and improvements in drinking and other psychosocial outcomes, particularly for younger participants (Blonigen et al., 2011, 2013). However, the measurement of impulsivity in that study was not multifaceted. Results from the present study suggest that further interrogation of Negative Urgency as a treatment mechanism may be warranted.

Although not bidirectional, Positive Urgency and Lack of Perseverance prospectively predicted drinking frequency and intensity, respectively. Specifically, elevations in Positive Urgency were associated with more frequent drinking (higher reported %DD) at later timepoints throughout the early recovery period (6, 9, and 12 months post recovery attempt). Other studies have also revealed associations between Positive Urgency and alcohol consumption, where those with higher reported Positive Urgency had increased alcohol consumption under positive mood induction (Cyders et al., 2010; Dinc & Cooper, 2015). In the present study, elevation in Lack of Perseverance was associated with later increases in %HDD throughout the early recovery period, suggesting that those with elevations in Positive Urgency and Lack of Perseverance may be more vulnerable to risky drinking, and that these facets may be useful treatment targets. For example, helping patients plan for celebrations, brainstorm alternative rewards in situations that are likely to elicit heightened positive affect, or discussions which draw attention to and crystallize the negative consequences of drinking and positive effects of reductions or abstinence all may be fruitful activities to focus on during treatment.

While it was surprising that results did not reveal associations between delay discounting and any drinking indicators over time, other studies also have not found support for bidirectional associations. One study of a similar population (problem drinkers who had recently stopped drinking without treatment) also found limited, inconsistent support for cross‐lagged relationships between drinking and delay discounting, and no support for bidirectional associations (Cheong et al., 2023). Although there is consistent evidence for impulsive discounting as a negative prognostic factor for smoking cessation in adults (Syan et al., 2021), studies in other populations have not revealed significant associations, and one systematic review of delay discounting and associations with treatment outcomes also found mixed evidence across the literature (Exum et al., 2023). The tool used to measure delay discounting in this study was a relatively low‐resolution, 5‐item measure. Although growing research suggests that delay reward discounting can be measured with a limited number of items (Dwyer et al., 2023; Koffarnus & Bickel, 2014; Miranda et al., 2018), this may be nonetheless a contributing factor to the lack of findings observed in the current study. On balance, these inconsistent findings suggest that the relationship between delay discounting and SUD recovery may be more nuanced, possibly systematically varying by substance, subject to important unmeasured confounders, or, possibly, related to the tool used to measure delay discounting.

This study also revealed specificity with regards to the timing of cross‐lagged effects. Those which persisted most consistently across multiple subsequent timepoints occurred exclusively in the early recovery stage, highlighting this as a particularly important period where relationships between impulsivity and drinking crystallize. Results from this study suggest that future studies focused on impulsivity and drinking within the context of recovery should also focus on examining this early recovery period. While moderation analyses in the present study did not reveal differences in cross‐lagged effects by sex or age, other recent research has revealed sex differences. Specifically, one cross‐sectional study of individuals with severe AUD who have attended AA meetings found that negative urgency was related to increased alcohol consumption only among females (Lannoy et al., 2024). Sex‐specific correlates warrant further research attention, particularly in the context of AUD recovery.

This study must be considered within the context of its strengths and limitations. This study had a relatively large sample size which followed participants 1 year into their recovery attempt. This longitudinal design allowed the study to observe effects and make inferences about the relationship between impulsivity and drinking that would have remained unobserved had the study not lasted 1 year. From an analytical perspective, testing for contemporaneous effects (i.e., the association between drinking/impulsivity at a single timepoint) allows for increased confidence in the robustness of the observed and retained cross‐lagged effects. Additionally, the rate of completion of all six assessments in this study was high, at almost 70%, with participants completing on average 5.2 of 6 assessments. This study also examined impulsivity using a relatively high‐resolution multidimensional measurement (multiple personality facets and delay discounting). This high‐resolution measurement allowed for a more nuanced examination of relationships between impulsivity facets and alcohol consumption. Although this study was initially intended to comprise only participants in clinical care (inpatient/outpatient treatment clinics), it had to be adapted due to COVID‐19 restrictions and thus expanded its eligibility criteria to those in the community reporting that they were planning on making a “significant recovery attempt.” As a result, the nature of the recovery attempt in this sample is more difficult to characterize. However, it may also be more generalizable to those not necessarily involved in clinical care, which is important given that many people recover from AUD without formal treatment (Kelly et al., 2017; Tucker et al., 2020). Furthermore, the drinking trajectory analyses support that a sample who attempted recovery and made significant, sustained changes to their drinking was indeed recruited. This study was also not able to examine several potentially relevant covariates or moderator variables, including primary mode of recovery attempt after baseline (e.g., outpatient, inpatient, without professional support), number of previous recovery attempts, or presence of psychiatric conditions relevant to impulsivity (e.g., attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, borderline personality disorder, bipolar disorder). Finally, a lack of racial diversity within the recruited sample limits generalizability about individuals who identify as racial minorities.

In conclusion, this study elucidates relationships between drinking and impulsivity during the year following a significant AUD recovery attempt. It suggests that facets of impulsive personality are mutable and subject to change during the recovery process, and revealed significant cross‐lagged associations between drinking and several facets of impulsive personality, which were robust to differences in sex and age. In particular, the study revealed consistent, robust, and bidirectional associations between Negative Urgency and drinking quantity, frequency, and intensity. This implicates regulation of negative emotions a potentially critical mechanism to target during treatment to support those making an AUD recovery attempt. Consistent specificity of effects occurring in the early recovery period also suggest that impulsivity and drinking associations crystallize during this initial recovery phase.
