Prevalence of BRCA1 Promoter Methylation in Cohorts of Individuals With and Without Cancer Assessed by Circulating Cell-Free DNA
Authors: Edison T Liu, Margaret Antonio, Tracy Nance, Francesca Menghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEBRCA1 promoter methylation (BRCA1meth) affects 20%-27% of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and ovarian cancer (OvCa) and has therapeutic predictive value in both cancer types. BRCA1meth is established during early embryonic development as a constitutional event, and its detection in the peripheral blood cells of unaffected women associates with increased risk for breast cancer and OvCa. Thus, facile and sensitive assessment of BRCA1meth is an important component for both risk assessment and the therapeutic management of these cancer types.METHODSWe used the GRAIL circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA)–based targeted methylation platform to quantify BRCA1meth in cfDNA (cfBRCA1meth) from plasma samples of individuals with and without cancer.RESULTSDetection of cfBRCA1meth was accurate and sensitive, with an empirical LoD95 of 0.0081. cfBRCA1meth was found in 4.1% (113/2,790) of individuals without cancer, and it was significantly more prevalent in females compared with males (4.8% v 2.9%, P = .016), suggesting that the dynamics of BRCA1 promoter methylation and its consequences for cancer development may differ by sex. In a pan-cancer cohort comprising 2,849 patients and representing 15 tissue types, cfBRCA1meth was enriched only in TNBC (15.2%, P = .001) and OvCa (13.9%, P = .014). Importantly, in these cfBRCA1meth-positive cases, the fraction of cfBRCA1meth was correlated with a methylation-based estimate of tumor burden.CONCLUSIONOur findings demonstrate that BRCA1meth can be robustly detected using cfDNA assessment both as a constitutional event in noncancer samples and as a metric of BRCA1meth tumor burden in individuals with BRCA1meth cancers. Therefore, the GRAIL assay provides a single quantitative platform that can be used to explore the role of BRCA1meth in cancer risk and therapeutic response.

BACKGROUND

BRCA1 deficiency induced by BRCA1 promoter methylation (BRCA1meth) is found in the tumor tissue of 20%-27% of patients with ovarian cancer (OvCa) and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)1-4 and is considered causative in the development of these cancer types.5 Although BRCA1meth tumors share similar genomic signatures with BRCA1-mutant tumors,2,6 our previous work demonstrated that BRCA1meth associates with worse treatment response. This reduced sensitivity arises because BRCA1meth is frequently lost after chemotherapy exposure, leading to BRCA1 re-expression, restoration of homologous recombination proficiency, and acquired treatment resistance.2

Recently, BRCA1meth was observed constitutionally in the peripheral blood cells (PBCs) of 5%-9% of women without cancer.5,7 This is generally recognized as a primary epimutation event, where an epigenetic change occurs in the absence of a DNA mutation, as opposed to a secondary epimutation, where an epigenetic change is triggered by a cis- or trans-acting genetic mutation.8 Constitutional BRCA1meth typically affects a single allele and has a mosaic presentation, affecting a small fraction of somatic cells: the median BRCA1meth allelic fraction ranges between 0.01 and 0.05 (on a 0-1 scale), rarely reaching up to 0.3.5,7,9-12 Since BRCA1meth is detected in cord blood samples and in adult tissues from all germinal layers, and because of the intraindividual consistency of the affected allele, it is proposed that constitutional BRCA1meth occurs as a single primary epimutation event during early embryonic development and that it is maintained into adulthood, with affected individuals carrying at least a 2.5- and 1.8-fold increased risk of incident TNBC and OvCa, respectively.5,11,13 A recent epidemiologic study described two unique cases of familial breast cancer and OvCa, where BRCA1meth occurred as a secondary epimutation, due to an inherited DNA mutation in the 5′ untranslated region (5′UTR) of the BRCA1 gene.14 The pedigrees of the two families examined showed a clear inheritance pattern with the 5′UTR mutation and its associated BRCA1meth status segregating with cancer.14 Although these cases are extremely rare, they support a driving role for constitutional BRCA1meth in tumorigenesis.

Given the potential impact of BRCA1meth on cancer risk assessment and prediction of therapeutic outcomes, a facile and quantitative detection of BRCA1meth that does not require tissue biopsy is likely to be an important biomarker in clinical cancer medicine.

The GRAIL circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA)–based targeted methylation platform is a robust, biopsy-free, scalable assay derived from a previously validated next-generation sequencing (NGS) platform.15-17 Herein, we show that this assay can be used for sensitive and quantitative cell-free BRCA1meth (cfBRCA1meth) detection in large cohorts of individuals with and without cancer, with potential utility both as a predictive biomarker and as a cancer risk assessment tool in precision oncology.

METHODS

CCGA Study Cohort

The Circulating Cell-Free Genome Atlas (CCGA, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02889978) is a previously reported multicenter, observational, longitudinal, case-control study.16 Participants with cancer had a confirmed diagnosis of invasive cancer, whereas noncancer participants had no previous or current cancer diagnosis and no acute illness at enrollment. In total, 5,639 cfDNA samples from CCGA Substudy 2 and 316 were analyzed for cfBRCA1meth, representing 2,849 cancer and 2,790 noncancer individuals.

TCGA Study Cohort

Methylation beta-values from the Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation27 and HumanMethylation450 arrays were downloaded from the UCSC Xena Browser in January 2024. Only tumor types found in the CCGA cohort were included in the analysis, accounting for 7,789 unique samples across multiple tumor types. For each tumor sample, the average beta value of the probes mapping to the BRCA1 promoter region (chr17:41277134-41277486; GRCh37) was computed and the threshold of 0.2 was applied to identify BRCA1meth cancers.

Determining BRCA1meth in Circulating cfDNA (cfBRCA1meth)

An existing targeted methylation assay that was originally developed to detect cancer methylation signatures for multicancer early detection (MCED) was used.17 Although the MCED assay captures over a million CpG sites genome-wide, this analysis focuses only on sequencing reads overlapping the BRCA1 promoter region (chr17:41277134-41277486; GRCh37) and spanning at least four CpG sites (ie, informative reads). Informative reads were required to have at least two PCR duplicates to ensure robustness; however, they were then deduplicated so that each read represents a unique molecule of cfDNA in the sample. Across the cohort, the number of informative fragments per sample ranged from 21 to 237 (median = 92). A fragment was considered methylated if at least four of its CpGs were methylated. Fragments contained a median of 11 CpGs (range, 4-20) per sample with similar distributions for methylated and nonmethylated samples. Fragments tended to be either predominantly methylated (>75% CpGs are methylated) or predominantly unmethylated (<25%). The threshold of 4 methylated CpG sites was chosen such that the theoretical probability of erroneously calling a sample methylated due to sequencing or other technical error is <0.0001. A sample was considered methylated (ie, cfBRCA1meth) if at least one informative fragment was methylated (Data Supplement, Fig S1). For each sample, the cfBRCA1meth fraction was computed as the ratio of the number of methylated fragments over the number of informative fragments (scale: 0-1). To avoid confusion, we use percentages when discussing the prevalence of cfBRCA1meth within groups of samples (scale: 0%-100%, Data Supplement, Fig S1).

Empirical Limit of Detection Analysis

To assess the limit of cfBRCA1meth detection, seven serial dilutions of commercially available material with defined global methylation levels were analyzed as proxies for expected BRCA1meth fractions. At contrived methylation levels of 0, 0.1, 0.9, and 1.0, samples were prepared by mixing biochemically methylated or unmethylated, sheared, and size-selected genomic DNA samples from HCT116 DKO cell lines, with 7-8 replicates per level. For lower contrived methylation levels (0.003-0.0243), Seraseq ctDNA reference material (SeraCare) was spiked into a background noncancer sample, with 19-26 replicates per level. In all mixtures, the reported contrived methylation reflects only the spiked-in material and excludes background methylation. Linearity was assessed for samples with contrived methylation levels >0.001. Sensitivity was evaluated using the empirical limit of detection 95 (LoD95), defined as the minimum cfBRCA1meth fraction (estimated from the contrived methylation level) at which 95% of replicates are classified as positive.

Statistical Analyses

All plotting and statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.2. Where prevalence was measured within a group (eg, on the basis of cancer type, sex, etc), the prevalence and a 95% exact binomial confidence interval were reported (using the Hmisc function of the binconf R package, method = “exact”). For comparisons across groups, Fisher's exact test for count data was applied if at least one of the cell counts was ≤5 or if the total count was ≤1,000. Otherwise, the chi-square test was used.

This study used data from the CCGA study,17 a prospective, multicenter, observational study sponsored by GRAIL, LLC. The CCGA study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT02889978. All participants were required to provide written informed consent. RESULTS

cfBRCA1meth Can Be Detected in a Sensitive and Quantitative Manner

In this study, we used data from a blood-based cfDNA assay originally designed as a targeted bisulfite sequencing approach to detect differences in methylation patterns between cancer and noncancer individuals.17 Although the original assay queries over a million CpGs, we focused on the assessment of the BRCA1 promoter locus, as described in Methods and in the Data Supplement (Fig S1).

To determine the linearity of cfBRCA1meth fraction and our limit of cfBRCA1meth detection, we assessed a dilution series of biochemically methylated contrived samples with expected cfBRCA1meth fractions ranging from 0.0003 to 1 (where 1 = 100% methylated). The assay showed a strong linear correlation between observed and expected cfBRCA1meth fractions across the full range tested (R = 0.97, P < .001, Fig 1A). The empirical limit of detection 95 (LoD95),18 determined from the dilution series, corresponded to a cfBRCA1meth allelic fraction of 0.0081 (Fig 1B), indicating that samples with fractions ≥0.0081 would be classified as positive in 95% of replicates. Thus, our approach is both sensitive and accurate in detecting and quantifying BRCA1meth in circulating cfDNA.

Constitutional cfBRCA1meth Is Detected in Individuals Without Cancer

We analyzed cfBRCA1meth status in 2,790 individuals without cancer, originally collected as part of the CCGA, a previously reported multicenter, observational, longitudinal, case-control study.16 In this noncancer cohort, 4.1% (113/2,790) of individuals harbored evidence of cfBRCA1meth (Table 1 and Data Supplement, Table S1), a percentage comparable to previous assessments of constitutional BRCA1meth detected by ultradeep targeted bisulfite sequencing of PBCs from adults.5,11 The cfBRCA1meth fractions in cfBRCA1meth-positive samples ranged from 0.004 to 0.32 (median = 0.015, Data Supplement, Fig S2A), with most cfBRCA1meth-positive individuals (92/113, 81%) having a cfBRCA1meth fraction <0.05 (Data Supplement, Fig S2A). Again, the fractional range of cfBRCA1meth is comparable to what found in previous surveys using PBC DNA,5 confirming that constitutional BRCA1meth can be sensitively and accurately detected using cfDNA. Interestingly, we observed a significantly lower incidence of cfBRCA1meth in males compared with females (31/1,080 [2.9%] cfBRCA1meth-positive cases in males v 82/1,710 [4.8%] in females, P = .016, Table 1), although positive cases from both sexes shared similar distributions of cfBRCA1meth fractions (Data Supplement, Fig S2A). A recent study of DNA obtained from the umbilical cord blood of newborns and from PBCs of their parents also showed a significant sex bias for constitutional BRCA1meth, with 9% of newborn girls but only 4.5% newborn boys, and 8% of mothers versus 3% of fathers showing evidence of constitutional BRCA1meth.7 Intriguingly, this study also suggests a potential age bias in the rates of constitutional BRCA1meth. We therefore explored the possibility that cfBRCA1meth may be more prevalent in younger adults. However, we did not observe any significant age-related change in the rate of cfBRCA1meth within our noncancer cohort that includes individuals ranging from age 19 to 86 years (median = 57 years, Table 1). This was true even when the analysis was restricted to each sex (Data Supplement, Table S2). Moreover, we did not detect any correlation between age and cfBRCA1meth fraction (Data Supplement, Fig S2B). Finally, we did not find any associations between cfBRCA1meth prevalence and additional demographic features, including self-reported race/ethnicity and smoking history (Table 1).

cfBRCA1meth Is Enriched in Triple-Negative Breast and Ovarian Cancers

We then quantified cfBRCA1meth in 2,849 patients with cancer from the CCGA cohort (Data Supplement, Table S1). We found that the prevalence of cfBRCA1meth was significantly higher in women with cancer compared with those without cancer (6.4% v 4.8%, P = .052, Fig 2), while the opposite trend was true for the male cohort (1.4% with cancer v 2.9% without cancer, P = .014, Fig 2). When we assessed each tumor type individually, cfBRCA1meth was found to be significantly enriched only in female patients with TNBC (17/112 [15.2%], P < .001, Fig 2) and OvCa (14/101 [13.9%], P < .001, Fig 2). Furthermore, upon histologic subclassification, we observed that cfBRCA1meth was specifically enriched in the high-grade serous subtype of OvCa (high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma: 6/41 [14.6%], P = .015), as well as in the indeterminate OvCa subset (5/26 [19.2%], P = .008, Data Supplement, Fig S3), for which there was insufficient pathologic data for subtype assignment. No other cancer types showed a significant enrichment of cfBRCA1meth patients over noncancer controls (Fig 2 and Data Supplement, Fig S3). Conversely, we noted lower cfBRCA1meth rates in prostate cancer (4/484 or 0.8%, Fig 2), which contributes to the reduced cfBRCA1meth rates in the male all-cancer cohort. The distribution of cfBRCA1meth prevalences across different tumor types in the CCGA cohort is comparable to that of BRCA1meth assessed in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data set directly from tumor tissue DNA samples, using Illumina methylation arrays (Data Supplement, Fig S4).

The median cfBRCA1meth fractions in patients with TNBC and OvCa were approximately 3-fold higher than those observed in individuals without cancer (P < .001, Fig 3A). This is expected, as unlike the more traditional analysis of PBC DNA, the cfBRCA1meth signal detected in patients with cancer captures both the potential presence of constitutional BRCA1meth and the BRCA1meth signal originating from BRCA1meth-driven cancer cells. To deconvolve these two components, we explored cfBRCA1meth levels as a function of the tumor methylation fraction (TMeF), a metric that aggregates the GRAIL genome-wide signal to provide an estimate of tumor burden in cfDNA, which generally correlates with tumor mass.19 The cfBRCA1meth fraction positively correlated with TMeF in both TNBC (R = 0.68, P = .005) and OvCa (R = 0.65, P = .03; Fig 3B), suggesting that, in these tumor types, the bulk of cfBRCA1meth originates from BRCA1meth cancer cells that release BRCA1meth DNA into the plasma. When all female cancers other than TNBC and OvCa were analyzed separately (ie, other cancers), there was no significant association between cfBRCA1meth fraction and TMeF (Data Supplement, Fig S5). However, the median cfBRCA1meth fraction in the other-cancer female subset was 1.5-fold higher than that in the noncancer female subset (P = .04, Fig 3A), indicating that some women with cancers other than TNBC and OvCa either have higher constitutional cfBRCA1meth levels than noncancer individuals or have occasional tumors driven by BRCA1meth.

DISCUSSION

BRCA1meth is a highly consequential epigenetic state, which manifests constitutionally and associates with an increased risk of incident breast cancer and OvCa in otherwise healthy individuals.5 In patients with established tumors, BRCA1meth associates with poor therapeutic outcomes.2 Accurate, reproducible, and quantitative tests for BRCA1meth therefore will have clinical utility in both cancer risk assessment and therapeutic guidance.

Although several methods have been developed to assess locus-specific methylation over the years, including methylation-specific PCR,20 methylation-sensitive high-resolution melting,21 and clonal bisulfite Sanger sequencing,22 these strategies are limited in their sensitivity and throughput and do not allow the quantification of low methylation frequencies, such as those associated with constitutional BRCA1meth. NGS methodologies address these limitations by allowing ultradeep coverage combined with CpG phasing, and generate highly sensitive and granular CpG-level information.23 Our approach, based on a noninvasive cfDNA NGS assay, is agnostic to the cellular origin of the cfBRCA1meth signal, enabling the assessment of BRCA1meth across the entire body rather than within specific tissues. Furthermore, being built on a clinically validated targeted-methylation platform for comprehensive cancer-signal assessment, it allows interpretation of BRCA1meth signals alongside other metrics, such as a tumor burden estimate.19

Across 2,790 individuals without cancer, we detected cfBRCA1meth in 4.8% and 2.9% of females and males, respectively, with most patients having a cfBRCA1meth fraction <0.05, in alignment with findings using BRCA1meth from PBC DNA.5,7 We acknowledge that with an LoD95 of 0.0081, we may be missing a small proportion of individuals with even lower cfBRCA1meth frequencies down to 0.001, which has been established as the detection limit in other ultradeep NGS studies that directly sequence blood or buccal DNA.5,7 To overcome this potential limitation, future studies should directly compare the sensitivity of NGS approaches based on cfDNA versus PBC DNA from the same donors.

The intriguing observation of an almost two-fold increased prevalence of cfBRCA1meth in females over males confirms the findings of a recent study in newborns and their parents,7 but with larger numbers. Although it has been speculated that constitutional BRCA1meth is more common in younger individuals, our study, comprising the largest cohort ever assessed across a wide age range (19-86 years), showed no association between age and cfBRCA1meth prevalence, nor cfBRCAmeth fraction, at least within this age range. Although our observations point to a consistent rate of cfBRCA1meth throughout an individual lifespan, we cannot exclude modest age-associated effects, which, ideally, should be investigated in longitudinal studies with repeated intraindividual samplings over the years.

We found that patients with BRCA1meth-linked cancers (TNBC and OvCa) have generally higher cfBRCA1meth fractions, suggesting that cfDNA can be used to accurately and quantitatively detect clinically meaningful BRCA1meth from cancer tissues, thus bypassing the need for invasive biopsies. Although a high fraction of cfBRCA1meth alone is not indicative of the presence of cancer, as we did observe sporadic instances of noncancer samples with cfBRCA1meth fractions in the range of 0.10-0.37 that are comparable to those observed in patients with BRCA1meth-linked cancers, the cfBRCA1meth fraction is a useful quantitative experimental metric, especially when coupled with cancer detection tools that quantify tumor burden.

Interestingly, we observed that women with other cancers (ie, neither TNBC nor OvCa) had a statistically higher cfBRCA1meth fraction than women without cancer. Similarly, concordant tumor and blood BRCA1meth signals have been observed in a small percentage of estrogen receptor–positive breast cancers.7 Thus, female cancers that are not typically associated with loss of BRCA1 can also show tumor BRCA1meth, possibly from a constitutional origin, which has critical implications for risk assessment and clinical outcomes. Indeed, as BRCA1 disruptions appear to drive a small fraction of other malignancies such as biliary tract, gastric, lung, and pancreatic cancers,24 assessing the frequency of cfBRCA1meth may identify a subset of these refractory cancers where BRCA1 meth drives tumorigenesis, which may be used to guide treatment planning.

An unexpected observation from our study was the depletion of cfBRCA1meth cases among patients with prostate cancer, which aligned with no BRCA1meth cases detected across the 502 TCGA prostate cancer cases examined. This suggests that not only BRCA1meth is exceedingly rare in this tumor type—as are genetic mutations in the BRCA1 gene (although not BRCA2 mutations25)—but that even constitutional BRCA1meth is underrepresented in patients with prostate cancer. Whether these patterns are reflective of a selection process at play in male patients during the development of prostate cancer or whether it is the result of a developmental peculiarity remains to be elucidated.

In summary, we present a new approach for the detection of constitutional BRCA1meth in the cfDNA of individuals with and without cancer. We show that the prevalence and fraction of cfBRCA1meth align with previous NGS studies using PBC and tissue DNA, and provide novel insights into demographic and clinical associations. Future studies in larger prospective cohorts of noncancer individuals, including longitudinal sampling, will elucidate dynamic changes in cfBRCA1meth fraction as a function of normal aging and/or tumorigenesis, validating the predictive utility of cfBRCA1meth for cancer risk and therapeutic outcomes. As constitutional and somatic DNA methylation has been observed for several cancer-relevant genes other than BRCA1 (eg, MLH1, MLH2, and MGMT26), assays like the one presented here, which detect cfDNA methylation at extremely low frequencies and in association with tumor status and tumor burden, represent a powerful tool to explore the prevalence and dynamic evolution of pathogenic methylation signals in epidemiological studies.
