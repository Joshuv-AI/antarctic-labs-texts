Clarifying physiology-guided subphenotyping of hypotension in early sepsis
Authors: Carlos Sanchez-Escalante, Jean-Louis Teboul
Published: 2026

We thank Dr. Ghataka for his insightful and constructive comments [1] about our editorial on physiology-guided subphenotyping of hypotension in early sepsis [2]. We appreciate his recognition of the pragmatic and bedside-oriented intent of our framework and welcome the opportunity to clarify several conceptual points raised in his letter [1].

We fully agree that sepsis-induced hypotension is rarely attributable to a single dominant mechanism. As explicitly stated in our editorial [2], mixed and dynamically evolving hemodynamic states are common, particularly during the early phase of shock, with varying contributions from hypovolemia, vasoplegia, and cardiac dysfunction over time. The proposed subphenotypes were not intended as rigid diagnostic entities, but rather as simplified physiological constructs designed to facilitate early therapeutic orientation at the bedside, rather than static classifications or definitive diagnostic labels. Importantly, we emphasized that these mechanisms frequently coexist and that repeated reassessment is mandatory as the clinical course evolves [2].

We agree that the diastolic arterial pressure (DAP) and the diastolic shock index (DSI) are not pure markers of vascular tone [3,4], and we acknowledge some of the limitations highlighted by Dr. Ghataka [1]. However, physiologically speaking, the main determinants are vascular tone, arterial stiffness and heart rate (HR) [5] and a low DAP such as <45−50 mmHg can only be explained by a low vascular tone, except in cases of extreme bradycardia [5]. Nevertheless, variables such as DAP or DSI are intended as early warning signals suggesting impaired vascular tone, to be interpreted within the broader clinical and hemodynamic context.

We also agree that point-of-care echocardiography is a powerful tool for the early identification of sepsis-related myocardial dysfunction and preload-dependence. In our editorial [2], cardiac ultrasound was positioned after universally available bedside variables in the conceptual diagnostic sequence, to ensure broad applicability across diverse clinical settings, including environments where immediate echocardiographic expertise or equipment may not be readily available. When accessible, early focused cardiac ultrasound remains a decisive and complementary tool, and its prompt integration can substantially enhance diagnostic precision in septic hypotension.

Finally, we agree that while capillary refill time–targeted resuscitation has demonstrated outcome benefits [6], the broader concept of physiology-guided subphenotyping requires prospective validation before widespread implementation. Our editorial was not intended to claim superiority over contemporary standard-of-care strategies, but rather to promote mechanistic bedside reasoning and to provide a conceptual framework for hypothesis generation and future research [2].

We are grateful to Dr. Ghataka for his thoughtful comments, which reinforce the central message of our editorial [2]: early management of septic-induced hypotension should prioritize bedside physiology, continuous reassessment, and individualized therapeutic decisions rather than reliance on rigid, uniform algorithms.
