Signatures of the Anthropocene: Population Genomic Structure Detected in Pennsylvania Coyotes
Authors: Craig A. Marshall, Julia Halo, Kyle Van Why, Yeraldi Loera, Stavi R. Tennenbaum, Casey Burton, Ariana Di Landro, Dominic Dominguez, Daniel Duncan, Madison Machado, Catalina Posada, Stephen J. Gaughran, Madison Lichak, Kristin Brzeski, Elizabeth Heppenheimer, Bridgett vonHoldt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCoyotes ( Canis latrans ) expanded across eastern North America in the last century and are ecological generalists capable of thriving across diverse habitats. Broad genetic surveillance has reported little spatial genetic patterning for this highly dispersive species. Here, we explore the genome‐wide signatures of spatial patterns found in a 10‐year study of 1199 coyotes from northeastern United States, with a temporal analysis of Pennsylvania coyotes. Despite their broad dispersal capability, we detected subtle but significant population structure, with two genetic clusters that have a weak clinal transition zone. This partitioning aligned qualitatively with patterns of human population density and activity. We inferred that gene flow between these genetic groups was associated with two different demographic expansions of coyotes eastward, south along the Great Lakes and separately along the northern Lakes towards northeastern United States. We identify Pennsylvania as a recent contact zone. Morphometric analyses revealed only modest differentiation between clusters and no robust temporal shifts, though a weak trend of increased body mass was noted in southwestern males. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that anthropogenic features likely influence fine‐scale cryptic population structure, even in a highly dispersing and widespread mesopredator.

Introduction

The impact of increased and significant human activity in the past several centuries, a period referred to as the Anthropocene, is a result of human domination of the landscape with consequences found across all evolving life due to habitat destruction and degradation, among other human activities that impact species demography, history, and evolutionary patterns (Crutzen 2002; Dirzo et al. 2014; Waters et al. 2023; Edgeworth et al. 2024; Zalasiewicz et al. 2015, 2024). Spatial genetic variation reflects the combined effects of species' life histories, landscape features, and ecological processes that shape movement and reproduction (Manel et al. 2003; McRae 2006; Bragg et al. 2015; Storfer et al. 2018; Paz et al. 2015; Fenderson et al. 2020). Consequently, population genetic structure emerges from the balance among gene flow, genetic drift, and local adaptation (Slatkin 1987, 1989). In species with limited dispersal or found in fragmented habitats, population structure may arise due to genetic isolation and local selection landscapes (Wright 1943, 1978; Barton 1996; Whitlock 2004; Crisci et al. 2016). When genetically distinct populations interbreed in contact zones, evolutionary outcomes depend on the interaction between gene flow, selection, and the genetic architecture of adaptive traits. Gene flow can counteract local adaptation through admixture, while selection may favor or eliminate introgressed genotypes depending on their fitness consequences (Wright 1943, 1978; Lynch 1987; Bowcock et al. 1991; Hedrick 2013).

Ecological gradients and patchy selective pressures across contact zones can either restrict or promote gene flow (Lavergne et al. 2010; Bonte et al. 2012; Larson et al. 2014; Ravinet et al. 2017). When conspecific populations meet following range expansion, their genetic, ecological, and behavioral divergence can produce a genomic cline—a gradual shift in allele frequencies shaped by selection, life history, quantitative phenotypes, and movement ecology (Endler 1977; Barton and Hewitt 1985; Szymura and Barton 1986, 1991; Rieseberg et al. 1999; Coltman et al. 2003; Lexer et al. 2007; Gompert and Buerkle 2009; Nolte et al. 2009). Species management activities that include hunting can profoundly alter genetic variation beyond immediate reductions in population size. Such anthropogenic activities can modify gene flow, effective population size, and admixture. Human‐caused mortality can cause bottlenecks, genetic erosion, and spatial restructuring of genetic variation (Harris et al. 2002; Allendorf et al. 2008; Coltman 2008; Rodríguez‐Muñoz et al. 2015; Kardos et al. 2018; Stoffel et al. 2018). Selective removal of dominant or wide‐ranging individuals further disrupts social cohesion and territoriality, leading to cascading effects on dispersal and reproduction (Haber 1996; Brainerd et al. 2008; Rutledge et al. 2010).

Coyote ( Canis latrans ) hunting as one option for species management is widespread across North America and among the most pervasive forces shaping their population dynamics, particularly for this adaptable mesopredator. With bounties first established in the 1600s, wild canids have experienced centuries of lethal management practices, including recreational hunting, bounty programs, and government control efforts (DeCalesta 1976; Dunlap 1988; Reynolds and Tapper 1996; Knowlton et al. 1999; Gompper 2002a; Kays et al. 2010; Bergstrom et al. 2014; Bruskotter et al. 2014; Treves et al. 2016). More recently, regulated control efforts focused at site‐specific management and recreational harvest have supplanted efforts conducted in the past. These pressures can drive complex evolutionary responses by altering demographic structure, disrupting social organization, and reshaping the landscape of gene flow (Knowlton et al. 1999; Gese 2005; Rutledge et al. 2010; Benson et al. 2017; Poessel et al. 2017). Coyotes have also rapidly expanded their range over the past century, colonizing much of eastern North America where they were historically absent (Parker 1995; Heppenheimer, Brzeski, et al. 2018; Heppenheimer, Cosio, et al. 2018; Hody and Kays 2018). This expansion has been accompanied by repeated hybridization events with both wolves ( C. lupus , C. lycaon , and C. rufus ) and domestic dogs ( C. familiaris ), resulting in admixed populations that vary in their ancestry composition and genomic architecture (Wayne and Jenks 1991; Bohling and Waits 2011; vonHoldt et al. 2011; Monzón et al. 2014; Heppenheimer, Brzeski, et al. 2018; Heppenheimer, Cosio, et al. 2018; Heppenheimer, Harrigan, et al. 2018).

However, coyotes are notable for their ecological flexibility and extensive dispersal capabilities, often spanning wide‐ranging territories across diverse habitats and human‐modified landscapes (Gese et al. 1988; Bekoff and Gese 2003; Riley et al. 2006; Gehrt et al. 2009; Hody and Kays 2018; Bird et al. 2023). Despite their mobility, patterns of genetic structure have been detected at large geographic scales, and in some cases at local scales (Sacks et al. 2004, 2005, 2008; Rashleigh et al. 2008; Heppenheimer, Cosio, et al. 2018; Adducci et al. 2020; Kreling et al. 2024). Such structure has been attributed to their recent demographic expansion, federal and state targeted elimination programs, and landscape permeability (Parker 1995; Gompper 2002b; Gehrt et al. 2009; Weckel et al. 2015; Heppenheimer, Cosio, et al. 2018; Hody and Kays 2018). In this context, assessing the degree of genetic structure within a highly mobile canid provides insights into the effectiveness of dispersal in maintaining connectivity, the influence of geographic and ecological barriers, and the implications for conservation strategies under changing environmental conditions.

Here, we investigate the landscape genetic structure in a long‐term surveillance of coyotes in Pennsylvania in the eastern United States using genome‐wide SNP data. By integrating genome‐wide SNP data, we test the hypothesis that high mobility corresponds to weak or absent genetic structure and explore possible explanations of such patterns as it pertains to indicators of human activity and landscape features. Our findings have important implications for understanding how mobility shapes population connectivity, which traverses both natural and anthropogenic boundaries.

Methods

Sample Collection

We obtained tissue samples from 901 “focal” coyotes across Pennsylvania between 2014 and 2024 from hunters, state management programs, and the US Department of Agriculture to explore temporal and spatial trends in genomic variation and morphology. To determine the source of gene flow within this population, we compared these focal samples with canids from geographically “adjacent” regions that had a less dense sampling across time and space (Coyotes: Michigan = 19, New Jersey = 11, New York = 114, Ohio = 2, Ontario = 5, West Virginia = 45, eastern wolves = 9) (Table S1).

Genomic DNA Extraction and Library Preparation

We collected genomic DNA using DNeasy blood and tissue extraction kits (Qiagen) following the manufacturer's protocol, and we quantified the DNA on a Fluorometer Qubit system. We prepared DNA for restriction‐site associated DNA sequencing capture (bestRADseq) following Ali et al. (2015) by first digesting the DNA with the SbfI restriction enzyme. We ligated a unique 8‐bp barcoded biotinylated adapter to each digested DNA to allow pooling of equimolar amounts of up to 48 DNA samples for library processing and pooled sequencing. We used a Covaris LE220 for randomly shearing pooled libraries to an average of 400 nucleotides in length that we then enriched for adapter‐ligated fragments using a Dynabeads M‐280 streptavidin binding assay. We used Agencourt AMPure XP magnetic beads for library cleaning and size selection of fragments that were 300–400 nucleotides in size. We prepared the enriched pools using the NEBnext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina NovaSeq 6000 paired‐end (2 × 150 nt) sequencing at Yale's Center for Genome Analysis, requesting approximately 192 million reads per pool.

Sequence Data Processing and Variant Genotyping

We retained sequence read pairs that contained the unique barcode and the remnant SbfI recognition site using STACKS v2.6 process_radtags module (up to 2 nucleotide mismatches permitted) and retained reads that had a quality score ≥ 10 (Catchen et al. 2013; Rochette et al. 2019). We then removed PCR duplicates with the clone_filter module before mapping to the reference dog genome CanFam3.1 assembly (Lindblad‐Toh et al. 2005) with the Y chromosome included (KP081776.1; Li et al. 2013) using bwa‐mem (Li 2013). We excluded reads with a low mapping quality score (MAPQ < 20) before we converted the SAM files to BAM format using Samtools v0.1.18 (Li et al. 2009).

When building the full catalog of variant sites in STACKS, we included 275 canids with previously published BAM files (dog = 1, coyotes: Alabama = 3, California = 3, Florida = 1, Georgia = 2, Idaho = 3, Kentucky = 1, Maine = 3, Minnesota = 8, Missouri = 1, North Carolina = 1, New Jersey = 4, Nevada = 1, New York = 4, Ontario = 36, Pennsylvania = 34, South Carolina = 1, Virginia = 51, Washington = 1, Wisconsin = 2, West Virginia = 5, gray wolves: Michigan = 4, Minnesota = 4, Wisconsin = 1, Wyoming = 10; Eastern wolf = 81; ex situ Red wolf = 8) (see SRA accession numbers in Table S1), which were previously aligned to the same reference genome using the same methods reported here. We constructed a catalog of all polymorphic sites in the gstacks and populations modules in STACKS v2.6 (‐‐vt‐alpha, ‐‐gt‐alpha, p = 0.01). We filtered the genotype dataset using VCFtools v0.1.17 (Danecek et al. 2011) to exclude singleton and private doubleton alleles, remove loci with more than 60% missing data across all samples, and remove loci with < 10× sequencing depth.

We constructed two datasets. The first was the minimally filtered dataset where we excluded loci that had a minor allele frequency < 3% (‐‐‐maf 0.03), loci with > 20% missing genotype data (‐‐geno 0.2), and removed individuals with > 20% missing genotype data (‐‐mind 0.2) in PLINK v1.90b3i (Chang et al. 2015). For demographic analyses that assume neutral and unlinked loci, we constructed a second dataset after we excluded loci that exceeded genotype correlations of r = 0.5 in 50‐SNP windows across the genome (‐‐indep‐pairwise 50 5 0.5) as a proxy for linkage disequilibrium (LD). We excluded loci that significantly deviated from Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) at a more stringent threshold of p < 0.001. We conducted a non‐model principal component analysis (PCA) in FlashPCA v2.1 (Abraham et al. 2017). We also employed the supervized maximum likelihood framework in the program Admixture to explore the number of genetic groups (K = 1–10) across time and geography space (Alexander et al. 2009). We used the ‐‐het flag to estimate observed and expected heterozygosity per sample, and the ‐‐fst flag to estimate inter‐group differentiation in PLINK.

Bioinformatic Inference of Sex

We bioinformatically inferred the sex of individuals that lacked such information. We utilized the number of nucleotides sequenced (VCFtools ‐‐depth) that mapped to the Y chromosome (KP081776.1) per individual. We conducted a simple paired t‐test between the number of nucleotides sequenced for samples with field‐based sex records and used this as a threshold to assign inferred sex for individuals with unknown sex.

Spatial and Temporal Analysis With Morphometrics

For each Pennsylvania focal coyote, the county of origin was obtained from the hunter's permit, and we used the county's centroid for a latitude and longitude location per sample. For spatially explicit analyses, we excluded samples that did not have either a recorded county or latitude and longitude.

We used TreeMix v.1.13 (Pickrell and Pritchard 2012) to show the historical relationships between coyote populations and genetic clusters, with the scope of inferring possible migration (i.e., admixture due to gene flow) events that would explain geographic patterns of genetic variation. We explored these relationships as branching patterns under m = 0, 1, 2, and 3 migration events for autosomal genotypes of the focal and adjacent coyote populations.

For the focal coyotes of Pennsylvania, we collected morphometrics that included total body weight (lbs), distance between ears (cm), head length as the distance from nose tip to the midpoint between the ears (cm), the distance between left and right eye tear ducts (cm), and circumference of chest (cm) measured at the axilla. Coyotes display sexually dimorphic traits, predominantly noted in skull morphology or body mass with males larger than females (Kennedy et al. 1986; Gittleman and Van Valkenburgh 1997; Hinton et al. 2019; Bidau and Martinez 2016). Thus, we assessed sex‐specific trait distribution using a PCA and correlation across traits with the corr, FactoMineR, factoextra, ggcorrplot, and Hmisc packages in R (Lê et al. 2008; Makowski et al. 2020, 2022; Kassambara and Mundt 2020; Kassambara and Patil 2023; Harrell and Dupont 2025). We omitted entries with missing data prior to z‐score normalization and visualized the results as a PCA biplot of the contributions for each of the morphological traits to a given dimension as the square cosine (cos2) with the fviz_cos2 function. We also explored the correlation between traits and visualized by a correlogram. We investigated the degree to which weight showed any sex‐specific temporal shifts. We selected this single morphological trait as it had no missing data for our focal population of coyotes. The temporal shift in weight was determined by a Kendall's Tau rank correlation test to determine the strength and direction of a monotonic relationship. Kendall's Tau ranges from −1 to +1, with either end of the range indicative of a strong monotonic relationship (negative or positive, respectively); a Tau ~ 0 suggests a weak relationship exists if any. We visualized sex‐specific trends over time using a linear regression model (lm function) in base R.

Genetic Cline and Geographic Projection Analysis in Focal Coyotes of Pennsylvania

We characterized the geographic boundaries between genetic clusters with a geographic cline analysis (Derryberry et al. 2014). As foundational to cluster assessment in genotypic data, a cline analysis describes the transition in allele frequencies and genotypic compositions along a one dimensional geographic transect. The resulting characterization of the putative contact zone between distinct genotypes is a sigmoidal shaped curve with exponential decay curves on either end (i.e., tails) (Szymura and Barton 1986, 1991). Two of the key mathematical parameters that characterize a cline are the inflection point or center of the clinal curve and the width. The inflection point provides a geographic indication of where, along the geographic transect, a significant change in trait frequency is most rapid. Cline width (i.e., the inverse of the maximum slope) describes the geographic distance over which the transition occurs. The transition can be compared to the frequency of the trait at each end of the transect with the two additional parameters, pMin and pMax. We established multiple transects perpendicular to the putative cline as the shortest straight line distance (Heppenheimer, Cosio, et al. 2018).

We fit a total of eight cline models as represented by eight linear transects extending from southwestern or central starting points to a common northeastern endpoint in Pennsylvania. Individual samples were projected onto each transect by calculating their fractional position along the transect vector. Distances along transects were then estimated in kilometers by converting differences in latitude and longitude to Euclidean distances (1° ≈ 111 km). Logistic clines were fit to Q‐values projected along each transect using non‐linear least squares, and cline centers, defined as the position where Q = 0.5, were estimated in kilometers and converted to geographic coordinates for mapping.

We overlaid the spatial genetic clusters and cline results with the human population density. We obtained per county resident data from the Pennsylvania 2020 census reported on April 1, 2020 (the United States Census Bureau for Pennsylvania n.d.). We divided the census per county by the county's total square mile area to obtain a population density per square mile (Table S2). We used a linear regression to assess the correlation between genetic signatures of geographic structure and human population density.

Results

We collected bestRADseq genotype data for 1381 canids across North America (Table S1), retaining 96.6% of the primary alignments, and built 9,367,977 loci with a mean insert length of 361.7 ± 108 nucleotides. We obtained an effective per‐sample sequence depth of coverage mean of 12.1 ± 7.7 prior to any filtering based on depth. After filtering for depth of coverage, we retained 315,998 loci with at least 10‐fold sequence coverage for the complete set of 1381 samples. We next excluded 38 samples with > 20% missing data (Table S1) for a total genotyping rate of 94.5% and an average rate of missing 6% of the genotype data from 1343 canids. We excluded 13,325 variants due to missing genotype data and 199,730 variants due to the minor allele threshold. This “minimally filtered” SNP set contained 102,943 loci genotyped across 1343 canids. We further filtered genotype data to obtain a “statistically unlinked and neutral” set of 67,645 loci for demographic analyses. For bioinformatic inference of sex identity, we assessed the number of loci sequenced on the Y chromosome for 551 known females and 568 known males to assign sex to 262 unknown individuals. We found that field‐reported males had, on average, significantly more sites on the Y chromosome sequenced than females (362.9 and 31.9, respectively; Welch Two Sample t‐test t = 54.2, df = 1078.3, p < 2.2 × 10−16) (Figure S1). Of the 262 individuals with unknown sex, we assigned 144 as males and 118 as females.

Coyotes, regardless of their geographic location, carried significantly higher average observed heterozygosity (H O = 0.23) than any wolf species (H O gray = 0.16, Red = 0.17, and eastern = 0.22; 1‐tailed t‐test of unequal variances p = 2.831 × 10−14, p = 7.595 × 10−7, and p = 4.316 × 10−6, respectively) (Figure 1A; Tables S1 and S3). Although sample sizes were not equivalent, only two states had observed heterozygosity estimates higher than the expected: Pennsylvania (H O = 0.229, H E = 0.227) and Virginia (H O = 0.233, H E = 0.217) (Table S3). Caution is suggested for interpretation given the notable correlation between sample size and observed heterozygosity (r = 0.281, r 2 = 0.08), with a lesser degree with expected heterozygosity (r = 0.073, r 2 = 0.005).

Coyotes Are Genetically Structured at the Regional and Intra‐Regional Level

We conducted an initial cluster analysis with PCA of all canid samples and found an expected intra‐specific clustering on component 2 (1.4% variation explained), albeit a preponderance of focal coyote samples dominated the principal component space (PC1, 1.6% variation explained) (Figure S2). These clusters were better resolved across the geographic eastern United States with an unsupervized maximum likelihood approach, with six genetic clusters that broadly distinguish Ontario (Canada), northeastern United States, Great Lakes and mid‐Atlantic, and southeastern United States regions from the American west (Figure 1A; Tables S1 and S4). We further assessed coyotes from focal and adjacent regions and found support for four maximum likelihood clusters (K = 4) that best captured intra‐specific genetic variation with the lowest minimum cross‐validation error (Figure 1B; Tables S1 and S4). We qualitatively noted a shift in the genetic signatures that distinguish Pennsylvania, New Jersey, New York, and Ontario from Ohio, Michigan, Virginia, and West Virginia (Figure 1B). When we restrict the findings to include only coyotes with a known geographic location of sample collection, the distinction between these two groups revealed a potential boundary in southwestern Pennsylvania, dividing the state diagonally into essentially southwestern and northeastern genetic groups (Figures 1C and S3). This relationship was further supported by autosomal genetic differentiation estimates, whereas Pennsylvania's two genetic groups were significantly more diverged from each other (southwestern versus northeastern F ST = 0.012) than either was to their adjacent neighbors (southwestern Pennsylvania versus West Virginia, F ST = 0.004, Welch Two Sample t‐test t = 91.9, df = 130,479, p < 10−16; northeastern Pennsylvania versus New York, F ST = 0.005, t = 83.9, df = 113,094, p < 10−16).

We next inferred historical relationships between the southwestern and northeastern genetic groups in Pennsylvania and the adjacent coyote populations. The former is indeed more aligned with the genetic variation found in the Great Lakes, while the latter is with the northeastern United States (Figure 1D). When we allowed for two migration events, there is 100% genetic admixture between the two Pennsylvania genetic clusters, suggesting they represent a recent contact zone between two previously diverged genetic lineages.

Weak Evidence of Morphologic Changes Over Time and Space

We assessed sex‐specific trends in the five morphometric traits and found that females were significantly smaller or shorter than males on all but one trait: weight (females and males, 32.1 and 36.9 lbs., 1‐tailed t‐test of unequal variance p = 1.32 × 10−61), distance between ears (8.9 and 12.7 cm, p = 0.1107), head length (22.1 and 23.1 cm, p = 5.269 × 10−8), distance between eyes (5.1 and 5.3 cm, p = 2.347 × 10−6), and chest circumference (54.9 and 57.2 cm, p = 1.109 × 10−8). From the PCA biplots, we found that male head length and weight had highest loadings on dimension 1 and were positively correlated (Figure 2A,B; Table S5). Male chest circumference also influenced this dimension but to a lesser degree. Dimension 2 was most explained by the distance between ears in males, which appeared to be uncorrelated with the other morphologic traits (Figure 2A,B). Female traits had a different pattern of loadings and correlations, with dimension 1 influenced by chest circumference and weight, which were positively correlated while dimension 2 generally explained by distance between ears and head length (Figure 2A,B).

We partitioned the focal coyotes based on their highest probability of membership to two predominant genetic groups in Pennsylvania (Table S1). Collectively, sex‐specific body weight was significantly heavier in the northeast cluster than the southwest cluster (Males: NE = 37.2, SW = 36.2 lbs., 1‐tailed t‐test unequal variance p = 0.0081; Females: NE = 32.3, SW = 31.7 lbs., p = 0.0115). We found no significant evidence that body weight has changed over time in either of the genetic groups (Males NE: z = 0.195, τ = 0.008, p = 0.8452; Females NE: z = 0.241, τ = 0.011, p = 0.8097; Females SW: z = −0.172, τ = −0.009, p = 0.8632), albeit weak suggestive evidence of an increase in weight over time for southwestern Pennsylvania males (Males SW: z = 1.819, τ = 0.101, p = 0.06895) (Figure 2C).

Genotypic Transition Across Pennsylvania Is Broad and Gradual

Cline analyses revealed a consistent genetic transition across Pennsylvania with a very weak slope (Figure 3A). We estimated the inflection points for each of the 8 transects, which ranged from approximately 13.8 to 164.5 km east from their respective transect origins. The southwestern and northeastern genetic clusters are largely separated along the transects and are coincidentally split between the state's remote interior and peripheral urban landscapes (Figure 3B). The inflection point reflects the geographic differences in the midpoint of the transition from southwestern to northeastern ancestry. Across all transects, transitions were generally gradual, reflecting broad zones of admixture rather than abrupt steep shifts in the genotypic identity. We assessed the relationship between genetic cluster probability (Q value at K = 4 for the focal and adjacent coyotes) and the per‐county population density (people/mi2) using a linear regression and fitting a loess trend line with a 95% confidence interval. We excluded two counties (Allegheny = 1678.6, Lehigh = 1073.2) that had > 2 standard deviations above the mean population densities (mean ± SD = 204.7 ± 268.7 people/mi2). We found significantly higher northeastern genetic cluster probabilities at very low densities (as per non‐overlapping 95% confidence intervals) until approximately > 50 people/mi2 when differences disappeared (Figure S4).

Discussion

Our genome‐wide analysis of 1199 coyotes in the eastern United States revealed clear yet shallow regional population structure, particularly for the focal region of our study in Pennsylvania, a state characterized by heterogeneous landscape. Coyotes were first documented in Pennsylvania during the 1930s and 1940s, following their eastward expansion from the Great Lakes region after wolf extirpation (Kays et al. 2010). The Pennsylvania Game Commission reported that the first coyote killed in the state was in the north (Tioga County) in 1940. Despite their high dispersal capacity (Gese et al. 1988; Bekoff and Gese 2003), coyotes in Pennsylvania are not panmictic. However, we report here two distinct genetic signatures, one for northeastern and southwestern coyotes separately in the state, separated by a clinal transition zone centered in southwestern Pennsylvania. This geographic partitioning coincides with spatial patterns in human population density. The northeastern genetic cluster overlaps with the Allegheny Plateau, also known as the “Big Woods” forested public land located in north‐central Pennsylvania (Diefenbach et al. 2005). With their expansion east out of the Great Lakes region, we believe our findings highlight two routes eastward, southern and northern, with a secondary contact zone in Pennsylvania.

Subtle Structure in the Highly Mobile Coyote

The discovery of regional genetic structure in the focal coyotes of Pennsylvania aligns with other reports of cryptic population differentiation in canids with high dispersal capabilities, such as gray wolves and eastern wolves (Geffen et al. 2004; Carmichael et al. 2007; Pilot et al. 2006, 2012; Muñoz‐Fuentes et al. 2009; Musiani et al. 2007). Though mobility tends to homogenize genetic differences over large distances (Wright 1943; Barton 1996), structure may persist or emerge when selective pressures, natural or anthropogenic such as habitat fragmentation or lethal control, limit connectivity and shift demographic signatures. In our study, the cline analyses suggest gene flow is not uniform across Pennsylvania and is shaped by ongoing processes, likely from both demographic history of expansion and localized events (Endler 1977; Barton and Hewitt 1985; Gompert and Buerkle 2009; Heppenheimer, Cosio, et al. 2018). Thus, although dispersal in western coyotes can exceed hundreds of kilometers (Gese et al. 1988; Bekoff and Gese 2003), our analyses indicate that movements are not translated into complete genetic homogenization. The shallow cline separating northeastern and southwestern genotypes is consistent with other examples of cryptic population subdivision in mobile canids such as gray and eastern wolves (Geffen et al. 2004; Carmichael et al. 2007; Pilot et al. 2006, 2012; Musiani et al. 2007). In these systems, structure often persists when dispersal is counterbalanced by selection, habitat discontinuities, or demographic instability.

For the focal coyotes, the weak yet discernible genetic discontinuity aligns with the Allegheny Plateau, possibly a consequence of a recent secondary contact zone, anthropogenic landscape features (e.g., population density per square mile), recreational or management hunting, or a combination thereof as primary filters of gene flow. We find that coyotes carrying northeastern genetic variation dominate areas of low population densities (< 50 people/mi2), but this pattern disappears as densities increase. Density is one of many metrics that state governments use to classify localities, alongside socioeconomic status or abundance, although such metric assessment is not a nation‐wide requirement for reclassification from rural to urban (Jiang et al. 2020). For example, in 2010, urbanized areas or cities can be defined as > 1000 people/mi2 and a total population size greater than 50,000 people (Jiang et al. 2020). During the intercensal period of 2000–2010, land area that remained as “rural” had an average of 6.4 people//mi2 whereas rural areas that experienced growth (and reclassified as urban) had 294.7 people/mi2 while urban areas reclassified as rural had 84.9 people/mi2 (Jiang et al. 2020).

Genetic partitioning, though, is likely an ongoing interplay between current events and past demography. The genomic patterns also likely reflect the legacy of historical range expansion. Coyotes colonized the eastern United States through multiple routes, north of the Great Lakes and south through the Ohio River Valley, which carry distinct admixture histories with other canid species (vonHoldt et al. 2011; Monzón et al. 2014; Heppenheimer, Brzeski, et al. 2018; Heppenheimer, Cosio, et al. 2018). It is possible that Pennsylvania represents a secondary contact zone between these eastward‐moving lineages, where incomplete lineage sorting and admixture maintain a gradient in genetic variation, especially where northeastern populations overlap with those from the Great Lakes or Appalachian regions (Heppenheimer, Cosio, et al. 2018; Heppenheimer, Harrigan, et al. 2018). Or perhaps we are simply documenting genetic mixing, which will soon homogenize genetic variation across the state, or even the larger region. The shallow genetic cline between northeastern and southwestern clusters suggests ongoing gene flow, though insufficient to eliminate structure. In systems of continuous distribution, such weak clines may arise from subtle ecological gradients or behavioral differences rather than strict barriers (Ravinet et al. 2017). Indeed, we hypothesize that the observed structure may reflect a combination of demographic inertia from range expansion (Heppenheimer, Cosio, et al. 2018), historical admixture (vonHoldt et al. 2011), and differential hunting intensity that disproportionately affects one population more than the other.

Species Management and Genetic Structure

Coyote management programs may act as a spatially variable evolutionary force shaping population structure, either by creating demographic sinks or by altering social and dispersal behaviors (Brainerd et al. 2008; Rutledge et al. 2010; Kardos et al. 2018). In Pennsylvania, species management activities that include hunting likely create a zone of highly variable demography as a driver of constant influx of coyotes to fill ecological vacancies. Such dynamics may inflate effective population sizes and increase genetic diversity estimates yet may also reduce social cohesion when socially dominant individuals are removed, which can promote compensatory reproduction and skew sex‐ or age‐based dispersal (Gese 2005; Rodríguez‐Muñoz et al. 2015). Our landscape‐level analysis shows that the state's interior regions coincide with areas of high genetic turnover. These findings echo recent work showing that anthropogenic activities can impose shifts in population structure even in species with high dispersal potential (Musiani et al. 2007; Stoffel et al. 2018).

Morphology and Genetic Structure

Though morphology can be a proxy for ecological adaptation or hybrid ancestry in wild canids (Hinton et al. 2019; Kays et al. 2010), we found only modest differentiation in body weight between genetic clusters in focal coyotes and no strong temporal trends. The lack of significant change in weight over time suggests that morphologic traits in focal coyotes may be relatively stable or under weak selective pressure. However, the slight increase in body weight among southwestern males, though not statistically robust, warrants further monitoring and may reflect responses to harvest regimes, a demographic history of admixture, or ecological conditions. Morphometric traits, particularly head length and chest circumference, differed by sex as expected (Gittleman and Van Valkenburgh 1997), but did not track across genetic clusters. This uncoupling suggests that morphology and genetic variation may be evolving under different selective or demographic pressures, or that admixture has homogenized phenotypic variation.

Implications for an Understanding of the Species

Our findings challenge the notion that coyotes in the eastern United States form a single, genetically homogeneous population. Instead, we document fine‐scale genetic structure likely shaped by historically species range expansion eastward combined with anthropogenic activities and landscape features. These patterns have important implications on coyote genetic connectivity and evolutionary potential. As coyotes continue to expand into urban and peri‐urban landscapes (Adducci et al. 2020; Kreling et al. 2024), understanding how gene flow operates in the face of human disturbance will be essential for predicting their ecological roles and evolutionary trajectories.
