Single-Cell Microfluidics: A Primer for Microbiologists
Authors: Remy A.
A. Ripandelli, Antoine M. van Oijen, Andrew Robinson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Recent advances in microfluidic technology have made it possible to image live bacterial cells with a high degree of precision and control. In particular, single-cell microfluidic designs have created new opportunities to study phenotypic variation in bacterial populations. However, the development and use of microfluidic devices require specialized resources, and these can be practical barriers to entry for microbiologists. With this review, our intentions are to help demystify the design, construction, and application of microfluidics. Our approach is to present design elements as building blocks from which a multitude of microfluidics applications can be imagined by the microbiologist.

Introduction

Phenotypic variation abounds in the bacterial world, and this variation can have important ramifications. A familiar example is the spontaneous appearance of novel variants within bacterial populations due to mutations, which can give rise to altered phenotypes such as pathogenesis1 and antibiotic resistance.2 A wide range of variable phenotypes have now been described for bacteria, and these have been traced to variation at the multiple levels: genetic, epigenetic, and metabolic variation.3,4 Remarkably, much of this progress has been made using relatively blunt instruments: culture-based methods that select for one variant over others or are insensitive to variation.5,6 Despite these challenges, decades of insightful research have started to reveal key mechanisms responsible for antibiotic-induced resistance. Stochastic changes in populations increase the chance of survival of subsets, which can lead to “super bugs” or recurrent infections. Additionally, antibiotic-induced mutagenesis gives rise to a pool of increased genetic diversity. Competition among the subpopulation eventually results in a dominant mutation entirely resistant to treatment.7 Alternatively, the cause of resistance could be purely epigenetic. In the case of persistence, redundant toxin–antitoxin modules can stochastically reduce cellular activities, thereby compromising the efficiency of antibiotic targeting.8 Gene amplification can cause resistance by the overexpression of genes responsible for protein product targeted by antibiotics.9 Phase variation is another phenomenon in which these targeted gene functions are switched on or off due to environmental pressures.10 All of these phenomena present a common challenge: they are rare and are based on the heterogeneity of the population. Therefore, the progression of this research calls for a quantitative single-cell approach to understand the subpopulation dynamics behind these phenomena.

Over the past 20 years, advances in microscope technology and fluorescence-based molecular probes have provided new opportunities to study phenotypic variation in bacteria.11,12 Single-cell imaging of live bacteria has generated unprecedented new insight into population heterogeneity, rare events, and rapid phenotypic changes within cell populations.13 More recently, the capacity to carry out single-cell imaging with single-molecule sensitivity has provided even more detail, allowing the roots of variable phenotypes to be linked to the behaviors of individual molecules within bacterial cells.13−15

There is now an opportunity to push the analysis of variable phenotypes one step further. Recent advances in the development of microfluidic devices are enabling researchers to image bacterial populations within increasingly sophisticated microecologies.16,17 Devices now exist that enable individual bacterial cells to be separated, captured, sorted, exposed to stressors, and selectively extracted, all while being imaged at single-cell resolution on a microscope.18,19 Unsurprisingly, these diverse functionalities introduce significant complexity, and this may pose a barrier to entry for microbiologists who wish to study phenotypic variation but are unsure of how to get started in single-cell microfluidics.

The aim of this review is to provide a primer in single-cell microfluidics for the broader microbiology community. We first provide some examples of variable phenotypes in bacteria that would be well-suited for study at the single-cell level. For the sake of brevity, we limit the discussion to phenotypes associated with antibiotics. We then explain two microfluidic concepts to study single bacteria, which have made a large impact in the past few decades, and discuss examples of how these are integrated in microfluidic systems. After that, we present the design elements of single-cell microfluidics devices as “building blocks” that can be combined to create new systems. Finally, we give an overview of the physical steps involved in turning a design into a microfluidic chip.

Examples of Variable Phenotypes That Are Challenging to Detect

Antibiotic-Induced Mutagenesis

The notion that antibiotic-resistant variants appear spontaneously in bacterial populations is well accepted and should form the null hypothesis in any study examining the source of antibiotic-resistant mutations.20 In the 1980s, however, it was discovered that certain antibiotics induce elevated rates of mutation in bacteria.7,21 From this, an “increased evolvability” hypothesis was proposed: antibiotic-induced mutagenesis increases the genetic diversity of bacterial populations, thereby increasing the rate at which bacteria develop antibiotic resistance.7 There is ample evidence that antibiotic-induced mutagenesis occurs. The primary question facing the field is does it matter? Does the potential increase in mutation supply brought on by antibiotic-induced mutagenesis ever outweigh the loss of diversity brought on by the antibiotic killing the cells? These simple questions are surprisingly difficult to answer using traditional culture-based techniques.

In a recent review12 we hypothesized that addressing the relevance of antibiotic-induced mutagenesis will require simultaneous, real-time measurements of mutagenesis and cell-growth parameters. A combination of single-cell microfluidic devices and single-molecule imaging are ideally suited to this task, enabling long-term tracking of cell phenotypes in populations exposed to antibiotics.16,17 Direct observations made using single-cell microfluidics would allow researchers to circumvent many of the factors that have traditionally confounded the study of antibiotic-induced mutagenesis. In particular, the existence of spontaneous resistance mutants in the starting culture could be accounted for directly if all cells can be monitored by microscopy.

Tolerance Caused by Persistence

Antibiotic persistence is a series of nonheritable phenomena in which small subsets of bacterial populations avoid external stress effects by entering a pseudodormant state.22 These bacteria stochastically reduce the number of cellular activities that would otherwise be targeted by antibiotics. After removal of the stress factor, the dormant subpopulation is resuscitated and repopulates to form a population that is genetically identical with the original one. It is thought that in many cases persisters are responsible for the recalcitrance of chronic infections.6,8,22−24 Furthermore, in a recent study persistence was correlated with the evolution of antibiotic resistance.25

Persistence arises through several independent parallel mechanisms. This redundancy may lead to multiple coexisting persistent subpopulations, making a bacterial population tolerant to a range of stress factors and antibiotics.6 Persisters occur at relatively high frequencies in bacterial populations. In Escherichia coli cultures, approximately one in 104 to 106 cells are persisters.6,22,26 There are several triggers for persister formation, including nutrition depletion in midexponential and stationary phases, pH levels, temperature, and bactericidal antibiotics. This makes this a highly versatile survival strategy that poses another large risk for infection treatments.

Molecular pathways regulating entry into persister states include, but are not limited to, (i) the stringent response, which is induced as a consequence of starvation and is associated with the alarmone (p)ppGpp, guanosine tetra/pentaphosphate; (ii) the general stress response, associated with the RpoS protein; and (iii) the SOS response, associated with DNA damage.8,22,23,27,28 Type I and II toxin–antitoxin modules are implicated in some persistence pathways. In these systems, a stable toxin inhibits cellular functions and can be inactivated by a readily degraded antitoxin. When the ratio of toxin:antitoxin increases beyond a certain threshold, the cell will enter a dormant state.28,29

The challenges associated with the study of unstable persistence-associated phenomena are significant and have been discussed in several recent publications.6,26 There are still many aspects of persistence that are poorly understood.30 In particular, the mechanisms responsible for resuscitation are largely unknown.22,31 The role of the host-associated stress factors in persister formation is of great importance to our understanding of chronic infections.32

Tolerance Caused by Gene Amplification

Like persistence, antibiotic tolerance is a bistable form of antibiotic resistance in which the resistant genotypes and their corresponding phenotypes are rapidly reversible. The phenomenon was discovered in the 1970s and has been linked to gene amplification.9

Some genes, often those whose protein products are targeted by antibiotics, afford antibiotic resistance when they are expressed at higher-than-wild-type levels. This increased expression can occur if the region of the genome containing such a gene becomes duplicated, doubling the number of copies of the gene in the cell. When a population of bacterial cells is exposed to a relevant antibiotic, any duplication mutants that are present may be selected for.9,33 Further amplification of the genomic locus can lead to additional increases in the resistance. In contrast to persistence, amplification mutants keep growing in the presence of antibiotics, albeit at a reduced rate.9 Reversion to the original single-copy genotype can occur at very high frequencies and is typically mediated by RecA-dependent homologous recombination.34 Once antibiotics are removed from the system, any cells that revert to the original genotype grow faster than the amplification mutants and rapidly become the dominant genotype in the population. Thus, gene amplification affords antibiotic tolerance by enabling a temporary antibiotic-resistant low-fitness phenotype during antibiotic exposure, which readily reverts to a low-resistance, high-fitness phenotype in the absence of antibiotics.

Gene amplification has been observed in clinically isolated pathogenic bacteria including E. coli,35Acinetobacter baumannii,36Klebsiella pneumonia,37Salmonella enterica,38 and Streptococcus agalactiae.39 It clearly has the capacity to affect antibiotic-based therapies, yet the phenomenon remains poorly understood. This is mainly because it is difficult to identify and study gene amplification mutants.33

Phase Variation

Phase variation is a phenomenon in which certain gene expressions can be switched ON and OFF under specific environmental pressures. This process is stochastic and leads to proteome heterogeneity in the population. As a consequence, phenotypic subpopulations can arise with different stress resistances.10

There are different kinds of phase variations that can contribute to antibiotic resistance. These include DNA inversions,40 transient frame shifting,41 and DNA methylation.42 The latter is epigenetic, with the enzyme deoxyadenosine methylase (DAM) adding a methyl residue to the N-6 position of adenine in the DNA sequence GATC. Certain proteins that can bind to this sequence block the expression of the nearby gene. Methylation or under-methylation of the sequence prevents the binding of the protein, thereby making the process act as an ON/OFF switch.43 This phenomenon has gained attention not only for newly discovered phenotypic heterogeneities but also for possible new antibiotic drug targets.10

As with the other phenomena, the study of phase variation using traditional techniques can be tedious and difficult due to its sensitivity to environmental pressures. Microfluidics can offer advantages in these studies in terms of single-cell isolation and environmental control. Furthermore, single-cell fluorescence microscopy offers unprecedented possibilities to study the ON/OFF switching at the chosen loci of interest. For example, in a recent study flow cytometry and single-cell fluorescence microscopy were used to study loci harboring methylated and undermethylated GATC DNA sequences. Using GFP and either a mCherry or a mOrange transcriptional fusion, it was determined that the switching of these loci occurred independently, as opposed to being coregulated.44

Microfluidic Systems Composed of Standard Building Blocks

The examples presented above highlight the diversity of antibiotic-associated bacterial phenotypes. There are many more examples of variable phenotypes in the microbial world, and there are many unanswered questions. Properly applied, microfluidics could play a significant role in expanding our understanding of variable phenotypes in microbiology. In this section, we introduce key concepts in the design of single-cell microfluidic systems. We first discuss two examples of commonly used designs and then present the general design principles as “building blocks”.

Example Systems: Mother Machines and Droplets

Within the past 20 years, two microfluidic designs have had a particularly large impact on single-cell experimentation and have now been adapted for many different uses: the mother machine and the droplet system.

Within so-called mother machines, bacteria are loaded into microchannels that are only wide enough to accommodate cells in a single file (Figure 1A). A larger “main” channel connects to the microchannels, allowing nutrients to be delivered to the microchannels by diffusion. Each microchannel serves as a microexperiment in which a single bacterium is allowed to grow and divide for many generations without competing against other cells. Throughout the course of the experiment, the daughter cells are pushed out of the microchannels and into the main channel.45 A contemporary application of the mother machine design, which incorporates pressure-modulated gates known as Quake valves (Figure 1B) and allows cells to be caught and moved using an optical trap, is illustrated in Figure 1C.19 In this application, bacterial cells derived from a library of color-coded variants are passively loaded into the microchannels. Using a microscope, cell lines have been observed for many generations. Cells displaying an interesting phenotype can be selected and moved to a separate sterile area containing the collection channels. The inlet and outlet valves are closed to stop the flow of the medium, and a second valve separating the collection channels is opened. The bacterial cell of interest is trapped by an optical tweezer and moved to a collection channel. The second valve is closed again to isolate the collection channels. The selected bacteria can now be grown in isolation and exposed to different growth conditions if desired. Cells derived from the isolated cell can be collected from the outlet of the chip for offline analysis.

In droplet systems, reagents and/or cells are encapsulated in microsized aqueous spheres that flow in an immiscible oil carrier medium and are processed in microfluidic channels (Figure 2A). This technique is very promising for high-throughput picoliter experiments in chemistry and biology.47−50 In addition to providing high-throughput analyses and precise cell isolation, droplets offer other attractive possibilities such as barcoding,51 sorting,52 genome sequencing,51 and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) techniques.53 Due to the spherical shape of the droplets, cells are usually detected and studied with indirect (fluorescence) techniques. An example of a droplet system is depicted in Figure 2B. In this system, bacteria are selected (sorting module) to be isolated (encapsulation in droplets), moved to, and trapped into addressable compartments (using pneumatic valves), exposed to reagents (by merging droplets), and if needed extracted from the chip (by droplet elution).54 Within the device, a selected bacterium is loaded into a droplet and flowed to a selected compartment. The route to this compartment is opened by programmable pneumatic valves. At a reagent-metering module, a droplet is prepared that contains a metered amount of medium and reagents. The reagent-containing droplet is then moved to the address containing the bacteria-loaded droplet, allowing the droplets to merge. The phenotypic response of the cell can be monitored by using fluorescence probes. If extraction is needed, the compartment is flushed with a continuous, oil-sheathed stream of buffer. The droplet content is now moved to the elution channel and exits the chip.

The Building Blocks of a Single-Cell Microfluidic System

In most single-cell studies, cells are manipulated in the same order. During loading, they are separated from each other and are captured. After being loaded, they are interrogated by measuring them while exposing them to a stressor. Cells showing interesting phenomena can then be sorted and extracted for offline analysis. In the past two decades, various techniques have been developed for these tasks. We have organized many of these techniques in building blocks (Table 1, Key Table), which we will discuss for each task.

The previously described droplet system separates single bacteria in an even stricter manner, by isolating them from the outside world completely (Figure 2A). Reagents can be added by merging cell-containing droplets with reagent-containing droplets. This technique is very effective if one wants to isolate bacteria and be able to mobilize these picoliter experiments through the chip.47

Separating cells can also be achieved without the use of compartments. Inertial focusing is a technique that uses inertial forces of the flow to separate particles along a streamline without the use of external forces (Figure 3B). It can be used for bioparticle separation and separation and ordering of bacteria prior to being encapsulated in droplets.60,61 This technique is one of the most elegant ways of using laminar-flow physics to manipulate single cells and has become an important field of study.62

Capturing the Bacteria

In order to eliminate competition and signaling between cells, the cells need to be isolated. Since these living organisms tend to migrate or even swim toward a more preferable environment, they need to be trapped.63

The most straightforward approach is to sandwich the cells between the glass slide and a membrane, thereby immobilizing them.64 In a more advanced example, submicrometer passages in a PDMS chip were inflated to reach dimensions such that bacteria could pass through them. After deflation, the bacteria became trapped in the compartment (Figure 3C).65 This technique captures micropopulations. Capturing single bacteria requires more sophisticated techniques.

The mother machine has different variations of capturing. In the original design the channels had one dead end, causing the bacterium to stay there, while pushing its offspring out of the channel.45 A later variant introduced a fluid passage at the dead end to allow active flow of medium through the channel and assist with loading and exposure (Figure 3A).18,57,58 The concept has also been used with each microchannel having two free ends to allow offspring to be pushed away from the center of the channel.55,56

In another example, yeast cells were captured in a wide monolayer channel containing micro fish-trap-like geometries, small enough to catch only one cell. The result was a whole micro area with captured single cells.66 In more complex systems, cells can be captured in compartments using pneumatic Quake valves (Figure 1B).46,67 Multiple Quake vales can be combined to build fully addressable compartment systems (Figure 2B, Figure 3D).54,68

Loading the Cells

Loading the cells in passive systems can be done in various ways. In the microcompartment example that was mentioned earlier, the passages to the compartments were smaller than the cells. Using the elasticity of the PDMS, the cells were loaded by increasing the pressure in the chip, thereby enlarging the passages through which the cells could pass through. After the pressure was released, the cells were trapped, and the experiment could begin.65 The mother-machine concepts were first loaded passively by flowing the cells in the main channels, and then they were let to diffuse into the microchannels.45 Alternatively, the chip was loaded in a centrifuge to gravitate the cells into the channels.17 In the versions with open ends, the cells were simply flown into the channels by creating a temporary pressure difference along the channel (Figure 3A).18,56−58 In a more complex example, a single cell could be loaded by using a clever channel resistance design (Figure 3E). After flowing one cell in the experiment channel, the resistance becomes high enough such that the rest of the cells will simply bypass via the channel with less resistance.69

Droplet systems are loaded in two steps. First the cells in the medium are separated, either by diluting the population or by using the inertial techniques that were mentioned earlier. Then the laminar medium stream is injected into an immiscible carrier fluid, where it forms aqueous drops. The flow velocities can be tuned such that each aqueous drop contains one cell (Figure 3F).49,61

Exposure

With bacterial cells isolated and captured, it is often desirable to provide nutrients and to expose the cells to other reagents. Mother machine designs without a pressure difference in the microchannels (dead end or completely open ends) expose bacteria by diffusion (Figure 1A).17,19,45,56 Designs with tapered ends allow an active flow through the microchannels (Figure 3A).18,57,58 In mother machine variants made using agarose rather than PDMS, the medium can be delivered directly via diffusion through the agarose.55

In droplets, bacteria are completely isolated from the outside world. Exposure to other reagents is achieved by fusing a droplet with another droplet containing the chemicals.48 In the most passive technique, a local change in hydrodynamic resistance slows the droplet down, enabling the second droplet to catch up. The two will then merge (Figure 3G).70

In many experiments, it is important to expose the bacteria to a compound at a variety of concentrations. These can be prepared off-chip or on-chip. Due to the laminar flow regime, on-chip chemical mixing requires specific mixing designs.71 In the simplest approach, it is done passively by diffusion and smart channel design. Different mixtures can be prepared on-chip using a branched system to allow different fractions to diffuse (Figure 3H).72 It is even possible to expose a population to a fine-tuned gradient using a source and sink for the reagents.73 In another approach, the fractions could be modulated manually by varying the pressure of one of the two inlet channels.74−76 Overall, these techniques require very precise channel designs, and the validation is done using fluorescent dyes with diffusion coefficients that are similar to the chemical used in the actual experiment.

Measurement

In the past two decades, time-lapse microscopy has been increasingly used for single-cell studies. It offers the possibility to track the phenotype of individual cells over time directly with bright-field or fluorescence microscopy.

It is often desirable to measure cell-growth rates.79 This can be accomplished by monitoring cell morphology, either by bright-field microscopy or by fluorescence microscopy of cells expressing fluorescent proteins.45 Using other probes, it is possible to monitor cell physiology in more detail. Examples that have been paired with single-cell microfluidics have included monitoring rRNA to measure growth rate,80 monitoring ATP synthase activity to measure energy metabolism,81 and mismatch repair machinery to detect point mutations (Figure 3I).14,17,82 Many other applications have been published.83 Recently, probes have been developed that can be attached to antibiotic molecules84−86 and glucose87 to monitor antibiotic uptake and glucose accumulation, respectively. Fluorescence has also been used to monitor electron receptors used in cellular metabolic activity, such as NADH and FADH, using the fluorescent viability indicator alamarBlue.88 Fluorescent techniques have visualized many intracellular processes that were previously out of reach.

In addition to these very successful fluorescence techniques, various techniques have also been used to measure mechanical properties of the cell. These include measurements using funnel-like channels89 or magnetic beads in channels90 and measurements of cell diameter and cell presence by collapsing a Quake-valve-like ceiling.91 In more complex techniques, electrodes are integrated to measure the number of cells in a microchannel as a function of the electrical resistance in the channel (Figure 3J).58,92 Single-cell interrogation techniques have rapidly evolved and are one of the core advantages of microfluidic approaches.4,31

Sorting

Sorting cells can be necessary for sample purification or postexperiment cell selection. Typical techniques use inertia to separate pathogen types62 or the switch of flow trajectory. In the latter case, cells can be sorted based on fluorescence read out in a system called microfluorescence activated cell sorting (μFACS). The cells that have an activated fluorescence probe are recognized by the microscope, which controls a component in a Y-channel junction responsible for the deviation of the cell trajectory. Some components can be fully pneumatic. The trajectory is changed by applying a pressure difference across the junction93 or by mechanically changing the channel resistance of one of the output channels (Figure 3K).77,94 Other components are electrical. The trajectory is changed by electro-osmotic flow,95 by using dielectrophoresis (DEP) (Figure 3L),78,96 or by using a combination of channel resistances and DEP.52 Electrodes are usually patterned on a glass slide. Alternatively, agarose gel electrodes can be injected into channels adjacent to the cell channels. These electrodes are made of 1% agarose in 1 M NaCl as an electrolyte. The generated electrostatic repulsion force then guides the selected cells in a collection reservoir.97 However, when using electric fields, it is important to assess potentially unwanted effects on the cells.98 Sorting is a very sophisticated building block. Many more techniques have been proposed and have been summarized in a dedicated review.99

Extraction

In experiments where rare phenomena are induced, it might be of interest to extract a single bacterium for further off-chip analysis. In high minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) experiments, most of the population is inhibited or killed. A single mutated bacterium can be allowed to populate the rest of the chip. This culture was then flown out of the chip into a new container. It becomes challenging when bacteria are not selected by cell death. In this case, extraction techniques require active components like valves to select the cells. The same group that invented the pneumatic Quake valve (Figure 1B) constructed a system with 1024 microchannels that could be individually opened and closed with 20 control channels using Boolean logic.100 These channels could be as narrow as 6 μm.67 In a similar approach, bacteria were grown in addressable compartments controlled by pneumatic valves68 (Figure 3D).

Droplets were extracted from a chip by draining them into a microcentrifuge tube. The emulsion was then broken by vortexing the tube in a vigorous manner and extracting the supernatant containing the bacteria.52 One limitation of these techniques is the number of addressable components that one can control and fit on a chip for selection.

To date, the most straightforward approach is by using optical tweezers, where bacteria can be moved using a highly focused laser beam (Figure 1C). Due to the strong electric field gradient in the waist of the beam, the dielectric bacteria are attracted and captured at the center of the beam. This allows one to move a bacterium of interest to a separate cultivation site on the chip, before being captured at the outlet of the chip.19,101 Despite these sophisticated approaches, the extraction building block remains the most challenging of all.

Designing and Preparing a Microfluidic System

How do we design and fabricate a microfluidic chip, and how is it used in a live-cell microscopy system? A microfluidic system is essentially a microscopic channel system in which cell movement is controlled by a precise flow design. Such a system is designed by using the electrical circuit analogy. Here, the Hagen–Poiseuille equation describes the relation between the pressure difference, flow rate, and channel dimensions and is the equivalent of Ohm’s law. Together with the equivalent of Kirchhoff’s law, it is used to carefully design all channel dimensions to achieve the required flows. There are excellent books with mathematical derivations and examples of this very important theoretical tool, three of which can be found in the references.74,104

In this review, we focus on single-cell microfluidic devices, which are almost exclusively made with polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS). The elastomeric properties, the biocompatibility, and the ease of chip preparation make it an ideal material for research platforms.105 The negative side of a chip design is written or etched on a silicon wafer. This forms the master mold, with which many PDMS chips can be casted. The production of the master mold requires special tools and expertise and can be outsourced to microfabrication facilities. Once produced, the actual microchips can be copied in-house, by casting and baking PDMS on the master (Figure 4). The copy is then stripped off, and inlet and outlet holes are punctured with a biopsy punch. Because PDMS is toxic to bacterial cells, the chip must be washed thoroughly for several hours in a sonicator. Once dried, it is sealed on a microscope glass slide using a plasma treatment. The tubes are attached, and the chip is then loaded with a bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution to coat the glass slide surface. This coating prevents cells from sticking to the glass slide so that they can flow easily through the microstructures. A description and protocol can be found in refs (17, 74, and 106).

With an initial investment to fabricate the master mold, many low-cost chip copies can be made. The process of making a working copy of the microfluidic system takes about a single day with the crucial washing steps taking up most of that time. This relatively low cost and fast process makes microfluidics for single-cell studies accessible for every research group.

Next, we describe the hardware required to run a microfluidic experiment that is not based on droplets. In a complete setup, media and stressors are drawn through the microfluidic chip with a pump, while timelapse images are taken with a microscope (see Figure 5A). The microfluidic chip is sealed on a glass slide, which is mounted on a thermally controlled, automated microscope stage (Figure 5B and D). For bacterial imaging, each PDMS chip is used only once and is connected to new and sterile tubes and containers (see Figure 5C).

The pump is an important part of the setup. As described, the microfluidic circuit can be designed and understood by using an electrical circuit analogy. Pumps act as power sources that move the media, which act as the electrons. In this analogy, a traditional syringe pump acts as a voltage source and controls the flow indirectly. Latencies due to lagging pressure differences can be caused by the elasticity of the PDMS or capacity effects of potential air bubbles trapped in the syringe. It took a few minutes for the syringe pump to have reached a steady flow. However, syringe pumps are easy to use and ideal for assays that simply need a somewhat steady flow. Typically flows of 5 to 30 μL/min are used for sufficient exposure to fresh media. When more rapid and precise control is required, more advanced pumps are used. These pumps control the flow directly by regulating the pressure with a feedback loop. In the analogy, this pump acts as a current source.

Before the timelapse is started, the chip circuit is flooded with media until it is free from air bubbles. Vacuum channels, patterned close to the fluid circuit, help in removing air bubbles due to the lower pressure in these channels and the permeability of PDMS (Figure 5B and D). Cells are loaded by drawing them into the circuit by temporarily switching to a cell-culture container. After a sufficient cell concentration is loaded, the inlet is switched back to the media container. For live cell imaging the laser angle in the objective is set somewhere between total internal reflection (TIRF) and normal incidence. Laser excitation is used to excite the fluorophores used to outline the cell shape and, if needed, specific proteins of interest. Exposures of 50 to 100 ms and timelapse intervals of several minutes are used to minimize phototoxicity. A high-resolution CCD or CMOS camera records the images of each stage position until all essential parts of the circuit are captured (Figure 5A). The interval with which this cycle is repeated depends on the required time resolution for the assay. To monitor the growth of E. coli cells, a 5 min time lapse interval results in about 6 to 12 data points per cell cycle.

The recorded images are corrected with the excitation beam profile to make the intensities in the fluorescence image independent of the position in the field of view. The images are further corrected by subtracting the camera noise, which needed to be recorded prior to the time-lapse recording. During image processing, the cells and cell features are detected, segmented, and tracked. This can be done with traditional techniques or with deep learning techniques. The latter has proven to be highly effective in analyzing complex circumstances, depending on the quality and quantity of the training data sets (see Figure 6A–C).

The cell cycles are logged by tracking and measuring the segmented cells during the timelapse. A cell length graph then reveals the full cell cycles that can be used for phenotype analysis. For example, labeled proteins of interest can be analyzed as a function of cell cycle (see Figure 6C and D).

This includes the design and fabrication of the chip-master mold and an introduction to deep learning image analysis techniques used for quantitative single-cell analysis.200

Discussion

Bacteria are among the oldest organisms and have evolved to endure every event that our planet has gone through. Spontaneous and induced mutations have allowed them to adapt to evolutionary selection. Centuries of study of these small organisms have revealed not only the pathogenicity of bacteria to humans and animals but also the resilience of these organisms displayed by processes such as antimicrobial resistance.1,2 The more we understand, the more we appreciate that the molecular and cellular mechanisms that underlay the behavior of bacteria need to be understood in the context of the organism’s environment. Traditional population-averaged assays have been a key approach for about a century and are the backbone of microbiology. But as we progress, the need for single-cell quantitative data grows, to understand the complex interactions of intra- and intercellular processes that give rise to the resilience of these powerful organisms.

This review is intended to invite microbiologists to be creative in the use of single-cell techniques to obtain quantitative data. We described state-of-the-art microfluidic techniques to interrogate bacteria organized along categories of building blocks. These impressive advancements in microtechnology have resulted in novel tools for the study of microbial systems, but tools alone are not enough. The second challenge in the strategy to address the need for quantitative data is the design of effective assays. We now discuss two important types of assays to inspire the reader to participate in these developments.

Induced Resistance

Many genetic changes caused by single-nucleotide point mutations or insertion and deletions come with a high fitness cost. In the absence of antibiotics, they will be outcompeted by low fitness cost mutations unless secondary compensatory mutations occur which will increase their fitness.33,38 When these effects were studied with traditional culture broths, the only resistant mutant that presents itself is the strongest mutant that outcompeted the rest of the population, but this is only the tip of the iceberg. What happened during the growth of the culture? The details of all of the mutations that occurred are largely unknown. Yet, it is of great importance to understand the evolutionary dynamics of these variations in fitness and resistance as a function of time and drug concentration. These dynamics were shown in the iconic experiment of the Kishony lab, for which they built the Microbial Evolution and Growth Arena (MEGA) agar plate. This was a 120 cm × 60 cm agar plate with lanes containing various drug concentrations used to induce and reveal serial mutators.107 The experiment visualized the competition among these mutants. Due to this competition, only the mutations with a fitness advantage could reach the zone with the highest drug concentration.

How can we use the microfluidic techniques described in this review to study this relationship more quantitatively? Despite not having billions of microchannels in a mother machine, we can still image thousands of cells while they are being exposed to various drug concentrations. The concentrations between 0.5 and 1.5 times the MIC are of particular interest. By analyzing the fitness of all monitored cells, we can build a fitness landscape that reveals the many variations of fitness and resistance as a function of drug concentration. The fitness parameter can be the growth rate, as it is considered to be a reasonable predictor for fitness.22,31 Alternatively, it can be any protein of interest that can be marked by fluorescent probes. This approach could be used for a series of experiments in which various drug concentrations are tested with gyrA, parC, marRAB, and soxRS mutant strains. In this example, the resulting fitness landscapes would reveal crucial fitness details in fluoroquinolone-resistant pathways.

We can take this even one step further. Recently a droplet technology called BacDrop was introduced, capable of performing bacterial single-cell RNA sequencing on millions of cells.108 The high throughput is mainly credited to a 2-step barcoding technique to allow multiple cells in each droplet. Three antibiotics (Meropenem, ciprofloxacin, and gentamicin) were used to successfully demonstrate the induction of the corresponding key genes in the resulting sequencing data. Subpopulations were identified that showed a diverse range of stress responses, indicating a heterogeneity in phenotypic outcomes, rather than a homogeneous response of the entire culture. Furthermore, a subpopulation of a population treated with Meropenem showed a high-level expression of cspD. This gene encodes a toxin of a TA module, which inhibits DNA replication. Indeed, after examination, a higher survival rate of this subculture was observed, indicating a persistence mechanism.

Another important technique that found its way to applications in single cells is Raman spectroscopy. Raman spectroscopy applied to single cells is a promising development to gain insight into biomolecular changes during and after exposure to stressors. In a recent study, E. coli bacteria were analyzed in treatments with sublethal concentrations of bacteriostatic and bactericidal antibiotics with distinct targets. The study revealed the ability of Raman spectroscopy to differentiate between resistant and sensitive bacteria. Furthermore, it could identify the emergence of antimicrobial resistance in cells that were previously susceptible.109

Induced Persistence

The persistence phenomenon has gained a lot of attention in the past decades.22,24 As described before, it is associated with multiple redundant toxin–antitoxin (TA) modules. This redundancy makes it challenging to study persisters due to the high dependency on the conditions of the surroundings. Bulk assays have revealed important characteristics to identify persisters.6 The biphasic killing curve should show differences in kill rate among the bacterial subcultures. A time scale parameter has been suggested to measure the minimum duration for killing a certain percentage of the population (MDK).110 This parameter should show a similar killing rate up to a percentage. Above this percentage, multiple different killing rates should appear, indicating persistence. When using drug concentration far above the MIC, the size of the persistent subpopulation should only depend weakly on the concentration. This subculture should not be able to replicate in the presence of the drug. After the removal of the stressor, the surviving subculture should give an offspring that is as susceptible to the stressor as the original parental population.

Growth is an important phenotype to monitor, but what exactly are the characteristics? This is where single-cell experiments can provide quantitative details about growth parameters and the heterogeneity in a population. In these assays, growth is monitored in combination with various fluorescent markers.

In 1983, the hipA7 mutant (high persistence) was identified which makes E. coli populations less vulnerable to the inhibition of murein synthesis.111 This mutation causes a thousand-fold increase in persistence rate, compared to an occurrence of less than 0.1% in wild-type strains, which makes it useful to study workings of the hipBA toxin–antitoxin module.112 This mutant has been used to study the heterogeneity in growth rates using a YFP113 and later to study the growth arrest duration for which a transcriptional fusion of HipA to the mCherry fluorescent protein was constructed.114

Persistence can be governed by many TA modules and is not always caused by the hipBA TA module.115 In one experiment in which wild-type E. coli was used, the apparent dormancy of persisters was studied with the expectation that dormant cells would have a low rate of protein synthesis. An E. coli strain expressing an unstable GFP from a ribosomal promoter was used. This GFP had a half-life of less than an hour. This time-dependent fluorescent assay was used to sort the dim appearing dormant cells from the population.103 In another study, E. coli cells were exposed to an antibiotic that targets the DNA-gyrase. Here, persisters were studied by monitoring the nucleoid dynamics by using the hupA-mCherry reporter that binds to DNA in a nonspecific manner.102 After removal of the antibiotic, persisters were identified by the difference in mCherry fluorescent intensity. This technique revealed the difference in the ability to restart the replication of the chromosomes.116 In most of these studies, single cells were monitored while allowing them to grow in close contact. Repeating these assays with the application of a mother machine to isolate the cells could reveal possible effects of cell signaling during the exposure to the stressor.

Understanding drug-induced persistence is of great importance. An example of an important, but largely unknown, mechanism that deserves study is the one responsible for the resuscitation of the persistent cells after the removal of the stressor.22,31 The challenge is to distinguish drug-induced persistence from stationary-phase-induced persistence. Therefore, in these assays cells need to be kept in a steady state because the trigger is the antibiotic.6 Trapping cells in mother-machine channels gives the possibility of constant exposure to fresh media throughout the experiment, thereby keeping them in a steady state.

Alternatively, in recent developments of the application of Raman spectroscopy on live single cells, Single Cell Raman Spectroscopy (SCRS), metabolic activities during the formation, and resuscitation of persister cells were analyzed.117 As H2O is essential for metabolism, the transition of hydrogen to deuterium was used as a probe to assess the metabolic activity, using D2O-Ramanometry. The study revealed that compared to nonpersisters ampicillin-induced E. coli persisters have higher metabolic activities, indicating a change in metabolism needed for survival, while at the same time appearing to show growth arrest. Furthermore, when resuscitated, the cells showed lower metabolic activities during and after the awakening period.

Concluding Remarks

Microfluidics has come a long way in the past two decades. In this review, we have laid out the current state of antimicrobial resistance studies and a variety of easy-to-implement microfluidic single-cell techniques to expand this very important research. So far, the design and use of these systems has been a prestige and maybe even “exotic” for many research groups. With this review we hope to have standardized this technology, by taking away the “exoticness” and the barrier to inclusion of these techniques in the portfolio. We discussed examples of microfluidic single-bacterium systems and categorized the vast variety of microfluidic techniques for single-cell loading, separating, capturing, exposing, measuring, sorting, and, if possible, extracting into the building blocks of a microfluidic platform.

We followed with a brief explanation of how these systems can be designed in-house. The production of the master-molds requires sophisticated clean-room facilities and experienced engineers. Fortunately, nowadays, these tasks can be outsourced to specialized microfabrication foundries, often affiliated with universities. These options make these next-generation research techniques accessible to any research group. Finally, we discussed examples of how researchers apply these single-cell microfluidic techniques to address some of the most urgent research questions in the areas of antimicrobial resistance and persistence. The reader is invited to advance this development by designing novel single-cell microfluidic systems and the novel bacteriology assays they might enable.
