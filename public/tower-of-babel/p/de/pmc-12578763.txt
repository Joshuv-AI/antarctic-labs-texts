Physicochemical, Structural, and Proteomic Insights into Drinking Water-Isolated Acinetobacter calcoaceticus Aggregation and Biofilm Dynamics
Authors: Ana C. Afonso, Manuel Simões, Maria José Saavedra, Lúcia Simões, Juan M. Lema, Alba Trueba-Santiso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acinetobacter calcoaceticus, a ubiquitous Gram-negative bacterium, exhibits remarkable adaptability across diverse environments, including drinking water distribution systems (DWDS), where its biofilm-forming and coaggregation capabilities pose significant public health challenges. This study integrates physicochemical, structural, and proteomic analyses to elucidate the mechanisms underlying A. calcoaceticus aggregation and biofilm dynamics. Surface characterization through contact angle measurements, zeta potential, and co-adhesion energy assessments revealed a predominantly hydrophilic surface with strong electron donor properties and a highly negative charge, promoting intercellular adhesion. Transmission electron microscopy unveiled dense cellular aggregates with extracellular filamentous structures, indicative of enhanced cell-to-cell interactions and potential extracellular polymeric substance involvement. Proteomic profiling identified 2593 differentially expressed proteins between aggregation stages, highlighting metabolic shifts, stress response activation, and upregulation of biofilm-associated proteins, including chaperones and quorum-sensing regulators. Our multidisciplinary approach emphasizes the importance of surface characterization in understanding bacterial community and underscores the critical role of physicochemical properties and proteomic flexibility in A. calcoaceticus biofilm and aggregation ability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-025-02623-z.

Introduction

Acinetobacter calcoaceticus, a ubiquitous Gram-negative bacterium, is recognized for its adaptability to diverse environments, including soil, water, and healthcare settings [1, 2]. In drinking water distribution systems (DWDS), A. calcoaceticus can persist and proliferate, potentially influencing water quality and safety [3, 4]. While typically considered non-pathogenic, certain strains of Acinetobacter spp. have been associated with opportunistic infections, particularly in immunocompromised individuals, making their presence in DW of interest from a public health perspective [1].

A. calcoaceticus’ ability to form biofilms poses challenges not only for water treatment but also for other environments [5, 6]. Biofilms serve as reservoirs for microbial contaminants, providing protection against disinfection and facilitating the proliferation of potentially harmful bacteria [7]. In clinical settings, Acinetobacter species, including A. calcoaceticus, have demonstrated reduced susceptibility to various disinfectants and a tendency to develop multidrug resistance [6]. In aquatic systems, the diversity of bacterial strains, including A. calcoaceticus, has been shown to influence biofilm resistance to disinfection, with more diverse biofilm communities exhibiting increased tolerance to disinfection [5]. Notably, while A. calcoaceticus forms single-species biofilms that are susceptible to sodium hypochlorite, its presence in multispecies biofilms significantly enhances their resistance to disinfection. In fact, multispecies biofilms containing A. calcoaceticus displayed the highest resistance to sodium hypochlorite, whereas multispecies biofilms without this species were the most susceptible. This suggests that A. calcoaceticus plays a critical role in strengthening the resilience of complex biofilm communities against disinfection. Moreover, the coaggregation ability of A. calcoaceticus within biofilms presents additional complexities for DWDS. Coaggregation facilitates the formation of structured microbial communities, where interactions between different species can influence community composition, stability, and function [8, 9]. Specifically, A. calcoaceticus has been identified as a bridging bacterium, promoting intergeneric coaggregation among DW bacteria and enhancing biofilm development by connecting otherwise non-coaggregating species, thereby contributing to the persistence and resilience of biofilms in DWDS [3]. This highlights the role of A. calcoaceticus not only in biofilm formation but also in enhancing the resilience of these microbial communities against standard water treatment practices.

Understanding the mechanisms underlying coaggregation in A. calcoaceticus is therefore essential for elucidating the dynamics of biofilm formation and persistence in DW environments. To date, research on how physicochemical surface properties affect coaggregating bacteria has been limited, with most studies concentrating on the oral microbiome [10–12]. However, recent work has expanded this focus by employing physicochemical analyses and advanced imaging techniques to characterize bacterial coaggregation beyond the oral environment, providing new insights into the role of surface charge, hydrophobicity, and structural properties in mediating coaggregation of the DW Delftia acidovorans [13].

This study explores the physicochemical, structural, and proteomic characteristics of A. calcoaceticus to better understand its aggregation and biofilm-forming behavior. By elucidating the surface characteristics of A. calcoaceticus, we aim to unravel the underlying mechanisms driving its coaggregation ability and its implications for biofilm community dynamics in DW systems. Furthermore, our study underscores the importance of comprehensive surface characterization in understanding the ecological significance of bacterial interactions and their impact on water quality and public health. Through this multidisciplinary approach, we contribute to advancing our understanding of microbial ecology in aquatic environments.

Methods

Bacterial Cell Growth and Preparation

A. calcoaceticus isolated from a DW system [14] was grown overnight in R2A broth (peptone 0.5 g/L, glucose 0.5 g/L, magnesium sulfate heptahydrate 0.1 g/L, sodium pyruvate 0.3 g/L, yeast extract 0.5 g/L, casein hydrolysate 0.5 g/L, starch soluble 0.5 g/L, and di-potassium phosphate trihydrate 0.4 g/L), at room temperature (23 °C ± 2), under agitation (150 rpm). Then, cells were harvested by centrifugation (10 min, 3100 × g), washed three times in saline solution (NaCl 8.5 g/L), and resuspended in ultrapure water at a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL.

Surface Contact Angle Measurements

Bacterial suspensions were filtered through a 0.45-mm membrane to ensure uniformity. Surface tension was evaluated using the sessile drop contact angle method, following Simões et al. [14]. Measurements were performed at room temperature with water, formamide, and α-bromonaphthale to assess polar and apolar properties. A model OCA 15 Plus was used, with at least 25 readings across three independent experiments. Surface tension values for the reference liquids were obtained from the literature [15].

The measurements of the zeta potentials (ζ) of bacterial cells were performed using a Zetasizer PRO Blue electrophoretic light scattering spectrophotometer. The electrophoretic mobilities (µe) at an applied voltage of 150 V and at a temperature of 22 °C were then converted to zeta potential using the Helmoltz Von Smoluchowski relation (Eq. (6)) [20]. Transmission Electron Microscopy

To analyze cellular appendages and potential surface structures, negative staining was performed. Samples were adsorbed onto glow-discharged, carbon-coated collodion films on 400-mesh copper grids. The grids were then rinsed with deionized water and stained with 1% uranyl acetate. Imaging was conducted at 80 kV using a JEOL JEM 1400 transmission electron microscope, and digital images were captured with an Orius 1100 W CCD camera. The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis was performed at the HEMS core facility of i3S, University of Porto, Portugal.

Proteomics

Protein Extraction

Samples were collected at two different time points (0 h and 24 h). They consisted of A. calcoaceticus suspensions prepared according to the aggregation assay protocol by Afonso et al. [21], which establishes optimal conditions for studying bacterial coaggregation. The time points correspond to the moment when the samples were taken from these suspensions: immediately after preparation and after 24 h of cell contact (when a high level of aggregation is anticipated) [22]. Since cells remained in suspension throughout the 24-h period, the proteomic data reflect aggregation-related responses mediated by cell–cell interactions. At each time point, 5 mL of sample was collected and centrifuged (10 min, 3700 × g, 4 °C). The resulting pellet was used for cellular proteome extraction, while the supernatant was reserved for exoproteome analysis. The pellet was first washed with PBS (pH 7.4) and then resuspended in an extraction buffer containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.5) and 1% SDS. Samples were incubated at 90 °C for 20 min at 1000 rpm in a thermoshaker, followed by mechanical cell disruption using bead beating (four cycles of 3 min with 1 min on ice). After a final centrifugation step (20 min, 3700 × g, 4 °C), the supernatant containing the extracted proteins was collected. Protein concentration and purification involved at least two rounds of ice-cold acetone precipitation. Following centrifugation (10 min, 9000 × g, 4 °C), the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in molecular-grade water. Three independent extractions were performed.

Protein Quantification

Protein quantification was conducted using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit with a bovine serum albumin (BSA) standard curve. After incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer.

SDS-PAGE Electrophoresis

To assess sample quality, a sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis in denaturing conditions (SDS-PAGE) was performed. Aliquots (10 µg protein/well) were mixed with loading buffer and a reducing agent before electrophoresis on a 4–12% Bis-Tris gel at 200 V. A molecular weight marker (10–250 kDa) was included, and protein bands were visualized using standard blue Coomassie staining.

Proteomic Analysis by Mass Spectrometry

For proteomic analysis, proteins were digested with trypsin, reduced, alkylated, and desalted using ZipTip-μC18. Peptides (0.2 µg) were analyzed by shotgun proteomics on a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer coupled to a nanoELUTE chromatograph. Chromatographic separation was performed on a C18 analytical column with a gradient elution over 105 min. Blanks were run between samples to ensure no carry-over. Mass spectrometry acquisition utilized collision-induced dissociation (CID) fragmentation in nanoESI positive ionization mode, with PASEF-MSMS scanning (100–1700 m/z). Data were processed using PEAKS Studio software against an NCBI protein database for the A. calcoaceticus species, with the database version specified. Only proteins with at least two unique peptides were considered, and ribosomal proteins were excluded to refine the dataset.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to calculate the mean and standard deviation (SD) of contact angles and zeta potential results.

Proteomic data analysis aimed to identify differentially expressed proteins between time points and was performed using PEAKS Studio software. Protein abundances were derived from three independent biological replicates per condition. The software calculated average abundance values for each protein and used internal statistical models to estimate Log₂ Fold Change and p-values, based on replicate-level variability. The results exported for further analysis include only the mean abundance per condition, the Log₂ Fold Change, and the associated p-values.

Discussion

The physicochemical characterization revealed that A. calcoaceticus exhibits a hydrophilic surface with a net negative charge, as demonstrated by the measured water contact angle and zeta potential values. Similar hydrophilic characteristics have been documented in previous studies [14], reinforcing the notion that hydrophilic surfaces enhance bacterial adhesion in aqueous environments, thereby promoting biofilm formation in DWDS. The strain also displayed a strong electron donor character (γ−) and low electron acceptor ability (γ+), a pattern consistent with prior reports on members of the same species [14]. These surface properties suggest a high affinity for aqueous environments, facilitating bacterial adhesion and persistence within water distribution systems. Regarding surface charge, this interpretation is supported by previous results on the same A. calcoaceticus strain, which reported a highly negative zeta potential (~ –30 mV) [23]. Despite this strong surface negativity, this bacterium was shown to form biofilms on DWDS-relevant materials such as high-density polyethylene (HDPE), polypropylene (PPL), and polyvinyl chloride (PVC), all of which also possess net negative surface charges [24]. This suggests that A. calcoaceticus can overcome electrostatic repulsion through alternative adhesion mechanisms such as cation bridging, EPS, or surface appendages. Moreover, biofilm formation with another negatively charged species (Stenotrophomonas maltophilia) was observed, reinforcing the notion that electrostatic compatibility is not the unique determinant of bacterial interactions [24]. Together, these findings are consistent with the observed surface charge properties and support their ecological relevance in driving interactions with both abiotic surfaces and other microorganisms.

The calculated co-adhesion energy further supports the hypothesis that A. calcoaceticus engages in robust cell–cell interactions, promoting aggregation and biofilm development. The high SE value corresponds to more negative co-adhesion energy, indicating enhanced bacterial aggregation via stronger cell–cell interactions. These findings align with Afonso et al. [13], who proposed co-adhesion energy as a key indicator of bacterial coaggregation, as both processes are closely linked. While co-adhesion involves interactions between suspended cells and those adhered to a surface, aggregation occurs between freely suspended bacterial cells or between suspended cells and bridging bacteria within a biofilm [8, 25–27]. A similar study has reported an SE value of 125.7 mJ/m2 and a ∆Fco-adh of −127.4 mJ/m2 for the coaggregating strain D. acidovorans 005P [13]. Although we identified physicochemical traits that are compatible with cell aggregation, we recognize that this study did not directly test the causal role of these features. Future work should compare aggregating and non-aggregating strains or experimentally modulate surface properties to validate their functional relevance in aggregation.

Microscopic analysis via TEM provided direct structural evidence of bacterial aggregation and the presence of extracellular filamentous appendages, compatible with surface structures such as pili. These appendages are commonly known to mediate interbacterial interactions and surface attachment and may therefore contribute to the coaggregation process observed in A. calcoaceticus. However, functional validation using gene knockouts would be required to definitively confirm their role. Additionally, the detection of EPS-like material surrounding the aggregates suggests that the production of biofilm matrix enhances bacterial cohesion and stability. Such extracellular structures have been reported in A. calcoaceticus and other Acinetobacter species, highlighting their importance in surface colonization and biofilm resilience in DWDS environments [28–30]. Importantly, the presence of such polar surface structures and matrix material may also explain the hydrophilic character observed for this strain.

Proteomic analysis reinforced these observations by identifying significant changes in protein expression associated with aggregation and biofilm maturation. The differential expression of proteins linked to metabolism, stress response, and membrane transport suggests that A. calcoaceticus undergoes metabolic adaptation in response to aggregation conditions. Notably, the upregulation of DNA-binding response regulators and biofilm-associated proteins suggests a role in QS and biofilm regulatory pathways [31]. Additionally, the increased expression of a protein disaggregation chaperone underscores the role of stress response mechanisms, potentially enhancing bacterial resilience against environmental stress [32, 33]. The strong upregulation of ribosomal proteins, such as the 50S and LSU subunits, reflects an increased demand for protein synthesis to support bacterial growth and rapid adaptation. This active translational machinery is essential for producing stress-related proteins required for survival under adverse conditions [34]. Alongside, enzymes involved in key metabolic pathways, including malate dehydrogenase and ATP synthase subunits, were significantly upregulated, indicating metabolic reprogramming aimed at enhancing energy production, amino acid biosynthesis, and redox balance. This metabolic shift likely meets the heightened demands associated with biofilm development and maintenance [35]. Transcription-related proteins, such as transcription termination factor NusA and DNA-binding transcriptional regulators, were also upregulated, suggesting enhanced transcriptional activity to regulate genes critical for stress adaptation, metabolism, and survival [36]. The pronounced upregulation of chaperone proteins (ClpB, DnaK, and GrpE) highlights the activation of protein quality control mechanisms to counteract stress-induced protein misfolding and aggregation, which is vital for maintaining cellular homeostasis within biofilms [37, 38]. Proteins involved in membrane structure and transport, such as BamA and YaeT, were significantly upregulated, pointing to membrane remodeling processes that may enhance nutrient uptake, efflux of toxic compounds, or membrane integrity. This is crucial for maintaining biofilm resilience in fluctuating environments [39]. Interestingly, several hypothetical proteins were among the top upregulated, suggesting they may play uncharacterized roles in bacterial adaptation, stress response, or biofilm regulation, warranting further investigation. Conversely, selective downregulation of ribosomal proteins suggests a strategic reduction in protein synthesis and metabolic activity under certain stress conditions. This may reflect a resource-conserving mechanism during unfavorable conditions, allowing the bacteria to prioritize essential survival functions [40]. The downregulation of some stress response proteins implies either a transition to a recovery phase or a suppression of specific stress pathways, potentially modulating bacterial vulnerability to environmental challenges [41, 42]. The coordinated upregulation of two-component regulatory systems (OmpR, RstA) and QS regulators (LuxR family) underscores the bacteria’s ability to sense and respond to environmental cues, fine-tuning gene expression to optimize biofilm dynamics [43, 44]. Furthermore, the upregulation of twitching motility-associated proteins, such as PilG, supports functions such as surface adhesion, DNA uptake, aggregation, and motility [45]. Recently, a similar study also described the overexpression of pilus-related proteins in a coaggregating strain [22]. Notably, pili and other surface appendages have been implicated not only in surface-associated motility but also in mediating intercellular interactions that drive aggregation. These structures may act as physical bridges between cells, facilitating the formation and stabilization of bacterial aggregates. Such suspended aggregates can also exhibit features of early-stage biofilms, often referred to as free-floating or planktonic biofilms [46]. Together, these proteomic insights reveal a complex, dynamic bacterial response involving metabolic reprogramming, stress adaptation, enhanced biofilm formation, aggregation, environmental sensing, and communication, ultimately supporting bacterial survival and persistence in challenging environments.

In sum, our study provides a comprehensive analysis of the physicochemical, structural, and proteomic characteristics of A. calcoaceticus and their implications for bacterial aggregation. By integrating surface characterization, microscopy, and proteomics, we identified traits compatible with aggregation behavior, including hydrophilic surface properties, negative surface charge, extracellular structures, and the differential expression of proteins associated with adhesion, motility, metabolism, and stress response. While these findings expand our understanding of aggregation in A. calcoaceticus, the study was conducted in monoculture and under static suspension conditions. As such, the results do not directly reflect aggregation or biofilm development in multispecies or surface-attached systems. Further investigations using isogenic mutants, modulation of physicochemical conditions, and in situ or multispecies models will be essential to validate the functional roles of the observed features and their ecological relevance. These future efforts will help clarify the contribution of A. calcoaceticus to biofilm dynamics in more complex environments, such as DWDS.
