Association of propofol vs. sevoflurane maintenance anesthesia with postoperative delirium, cognitive decline, and mortality in older adults: a global comparative-effectiveness study
Authors: Mingyang Sun, Mengrong Miao, Zhongyuan Lu, Yangyang Wang, Shige Xue, Yuqin Tang, Tian Mao, Liang Zhao, Jiao Wang, Saihao Fu, Wan-Ming Chen, Szu-Yuan Wu, Jiaqiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative delirium and longer-term neurocognitive disorders are major complications of surgery in older adults. As surgical volumes rise in ageing populations, identifying modifiable intraoperative factors—particularly anesthetic choice—has become increasingly important. Propofol is often favoured for its recovery profile, whereas sevoflurane offers organ-protective preconditioning that may benefit vulnerable patients. Comparative evidence in high-risk real-world populations remains limited.MethodsWe performed a multicentre retrospective comparative-effectiveness study using a global federated health network. Adults aged 60–80 year undergoing surgery with general anesthesia were included if they received propofol or sevoflurane for maintenance. Propensity-score matching (1:1) and doubly robust Cox models estimated adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs). The primary outcome was postoperative delirium; secondary outcomes were 30-day mortality, long-term postoperative cognitive dysfunction (180–365 days), and long-term mortality. Sensitivity analyses incorporated antipsychotic-based delirium ascertainment and time-stratified hazards.ResultsAfter matching, 91,046 patients were included (propofol 45,545; sevoflurane 45,545). Propofol was associated with higher risks of postoperative delirium (3.36% vs. 2.70%; aHR 1.37, 95% CI 1.25–1.48) and 30-day mortality (0.66% vs. 0.53%; aHR 1.25, 95% CI 1.05–1.48), with the greatest excess on postoperative day 1 (aHR 2.58). Long-term cognitive dysfunction (aHR 1.16, 95% CI 1.09–1.24) and long-term mortality (aHR 1.23, 95% CI 1.17–1.29) were also increased.ConclusionsIn this large real-world cohort, propofol maintenance anesthesia was associated with higher risks of postoperative delirium, long-term cognitive decline, and mortality compared with sevoflurane, particularly in physiologically vulnerable patients. These findings generate hypotheses regarding anesthetic choice in high-risk older adults and support the need for adequately powered randomized trials. Limitations include potential outcome under-ascertainment, lack of intraoperative physiologic data, and residual confounding inherent to electronic health records.Keywords: Sevoflurane, Propofol, Postoperative delirium, Neurocognitive outcomes, Geriatric anesthesia

Introduction

Postoperative delirium (POD) and longer-term neurocognitive disorders are among the most consequential complications faced by older adults undergoing major surgery [1–7]. These conditions are common and strongly associated with functional decline, prolonged institutional care, and increased long-term mortality, creating a substantial and growing burden on patients and healthcare systems worldwide [8–11]. As surgical volumes rise in aging populations—including within the United Kingdom and other European health systems—preservation of perioperative brain health has become a public health priority [5]. Identifying modifiable intraoperative factors, including the choice of maintenance anesthetic strategy, is therefore essential to reducing neurocognitive morbidity in this vulnerable group [5, 12, 13].

Propofol is frequently favored for maintenance anesthesia because of its rapid recovery profile and presumed cognitive benefits [14–17]. Yet this preference coexists with a well-recognized physiological paradox: volatile anesthetics such as sevoflurane possess organ-protective properties, including anesthetic preconditioning, that enhance cellular resistance to ischemia and oxidative stress [14–17]. These effects are mediated through mitochondrial KATP channel activation, ROS-dependent prosurvival signaling, and anti-inflammatory modulation—mechanisms not shared by propofol and in some instances attenuated by its mitochondrial complex I inhibition and antioxidant properties [14–23]. Such pharmacologic differences may become especially relevant in older adults with limited physiologic reserve, who are more susceptible to intraoperative hypotension, microvascular hypoperfusion, and heightened systemic inflammation [2, 15, 17, 24]. Evidence comparing propofol and volatile agents, however, remains inconsistent: small randomized trials have generally shown little difference in short-term neurocognitive outcomes, whereas mechanistic studies suggest that volatile agents may confer superior protection under physiologic stress [15–17, 24–26]. Importantly, most prior trials systematically excluded frail adults, ASA class ≥ 3 patients, individuals undergoing cardiac or major vascular surgery, and those with elevated inflammatory burden—precisely the phenotypes in whom anesthetic-related organ protection is biologically most relevant [16, 17, 24, 26].

These uncertainties underscore several critical knowledge gaps. The comparative effectiveness of propofol versus sevoflurane in real-world older adults—particularly those aged 60 to 80 years with multimorbidity—remains poorly defined because this population has been systematically underrepresented in randomized trials [16, 17, 24, 26]. It is also unknown whether anesthetic choice differentially affects patients exposed to substantial physiologic stress, such as those undergoing cardiac surgery or those with elevated systemic inflammation. Furthermore, the long-term consequences of anesthetic maintenance strategy—including persistent cognitive decline and mortality—have not been adequately evaluated, as prior studies were underpowered and focused largely on short-term postoperative endpoints [1, 17, 24, 25]. Addressing these questions requires large, multicenter datasets with sufficient statistical power and longitudinal follow-up to capture meaningful postoperative trajectories in high-risk geriatric patients.

To address these gaps, we conducted a large multicenter comparative-effectiveness study using a global federated real-world data network. Leveraging routinely collected clinical information across diverse health systems, we assembled a well-balanced cohort of older adults undergoing major surgery and compared outcomes following maintenance anesthesia with propofol versus sevoflurane. We hypothesized that in high-risk geriatric patients—particularly those exposed to ischemia or systemic inflammation—sevoflurane would be associated with lower risks of postoperative delirium, long-term cognitive decline, and mortality than propofol, reflecting potential differences in organ-protective properties not detectable in prior randomized trials.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study using the TriNetX Global Health Research Network (data version 2024-Q3; analyses performed on November 25, 2025). This federated platform aggregates de-identified electronic health records (EHRs) from more than 120 million patients across diverse healthcare organizations (HCOs) worldwide, including academic medical centers, community hospitals, and specialty clinics spanning the United States, Europe, the Middle East, and the Asia-Pacific region. Within the TriNetX network, local EHR data are mapped to standardized clinical terminologies (ICD-10, CPT, LOINC, and RxNorm) through centralized data harmonization and quality assurance processes, supporting semantic consistency across contributing sites. Because of data privacy agreements and the federated architecture of the network, specific hospital identities are not disclosed, and patient-level data are not pooled across HCOs. From the investigator perspective, all analyses were conducted within the TriNetX Analytics Platform using standardized analytic workflows, and only aggregated, de-identified results were accessible. We acknowledge that variability in clinical documentation, coding practices, and diagnostic thresholds across HCOs introduces inherent heterogeneity in outcome ascertainment, which is an unavoidable limitation of multinational federated EHR analyses. The study followed STROBE guidelines. The analysis adhered to a pre-specified statistical analysis plan (SAP) developed and approved by the core investigators prior to data extraction; primary and secondary outcomes, as well as key subgroup analyses, were defined a priori, with all subgroup analyses explicitly labeled as exploratory. All data were de-identified in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), and institutional review board approval was therefore not required. Detailed cohort definitions (ICD-10, CPT, LOINC, and RxNorm) and analytic specifications are available from the authors upon reasonable request.

Study population and exposure definition

The study population consisted of adults aged 60 to 80 years who underwent surgical procedures requiring general anesthesia. The date of surgery served as the index time point for exposure assignment, and follow-up began immediately after completion of anesthesia to avoid immortal-time bias. Patients were required to have sufficient preoperative observation time to ascertain baseline comorbidities.

To ensure that postoperative neurocognitive outcomes reflected incident conditions rather than pre-existing disorders, we excluded individuals with prior diagnoses of dementia, mild cognitive impairment, chronic cognitive disorders, recent delirium, stroke, traumatic brain injury, Parkinsonian syndromes, or any major neurologic condition documented within the preceding 12 months. We also excluded patients undergoing transplant, bariatric, obstetric, or major trauma procedures, as well as those with missing demographic information or inadequate follow-up.

Exposure classification relied on medication-administration records captured within TriNetX. Because granular intraoperative dosing and timing data were unavailable, maintenance anesthetic assignment was inferred using a pre-specified day-of-surgery medication exclusivity algorithm. Patients were assigned to the propofol cohort if propofol (RxNorm 8704) was administered on the day of surgery without any recorded administration of volatile anesthetics (sevoflurane, isoflurane, or desflurane). Patients were assigned to the sevoflurane cohort if sevoflurane (RxNorm 3616) was administered and no propofol was documented beyond the presumed induction window. Consistent with standard clinical practice, short-acting propofol records within the sevoflurane cohort were attributed to anesthetic induction. To mitigate potential exposure misclassification and confounding by indication, patients receiving mixed or nonstandard anesthetic regimens were strictly excluded.

Covariates

Baseline characteristics included demographic factors, vital signs, anthropometrics, comorbidities, surgical complexity, and preoperative medication use (including opioids, benzodiazepines, corticosteroids, and antipsychotics). To account for potential non-linear relationships with postoperative delirium, key continuous variables (e.g., age and body mass index) were categorized into clinically meaningful groups rather than modeled as linear continuous terms. Variables with < 20% missingness were imputed using the median (continuous variables) or mode (categorical variables). For variables with > 20% missingness—such as C-reactive protein at certain HCOs—we retained missingness as a binary indicator (“measured” vs. “not measured”) rather than applying model-based imputation.

Outcomes and outcome validation

The primary outcome was postoperative delirium (POD), defined as any ICD-10 code F05 recorded within 7 days after surgery, while a 30-day capture window was retained to ensure comprehensive ascertainment of clinically documented delirium episodes. Prior validation studies, including recent reports by Sheehan et al. and Chuen et al., [27, 28] have shown high specificity (> 90%) but moderate sensitivity (~ 60%) for delirium coding in EHR data; administrative data may therefore under-capture cases, particularly hypoactive delirium. Consequently, effect estimates likely reflect conservative associations. From a pharmacoepidemiologic perspective, such under-ascertainment primarily affects incidence estimation and, assuming it is non-differential with respect to anesthetic exposure, would be expected to bias relative effect estimates toward the null rather than generate spurious associations.

To address potential under-coding, we conducted a separate sensitivity analysis incorporating new initiation of antipsychotic therapy as a proxy marker. To mitigate misclassification arising from alternative indications (e.g., insomnia, agitation, or chronic psychiatric conditions), we strictly defined this proxy outcome as the issuance of a new antipsychotic prescription within 48 h postoperatively in patients with no record of antipsychotic medication use in the preceding 3 months. Results from this proxy-based analysis are reported separately in eTable 2. Additionally, consistency in outcome definitions across participating healthcare organizations is supported by the network’s standardized terminology mappings (described in Study Design and Data Source), although residual inter-site variability in delirium recognition cannot be fully eliminated.

Secondary outcomes included 30-day all-cause mortality; long-term postoperative newly diagnosed neurocognitive disorders, defined by new ICD-10 codes G31.84, R41.x, or F06.7 recorded 180–365 days after surgery; and long-term mortality (> 180 days).

For analyses of POD and 30-day mortality, patients who underwent a second surgical procedure within 30 days of the index operation were excluded. Early repeat operations in this age range are infrequent and usually indicate an acute postoperative complication rather than elective reintervention [29]. Excluding these patients reduces the likelihood of exposure misclassification—because a subsequent procedure may involve a different maintenance anesthetic—and does not materially alter cohort size or event rates. For long-term outcomes (incident neurocognitive diagnoses and mortality from 180 to 365 days), patients who had any additional surgery during this interval were similarly excluded to avoid redefinition of anesthetic exposure and to minimize confounding from new perioperative neurological or systemic insults.

Statistical analysis

We applied propensity-score matching (PSM) to reduce confounding by indication. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression incorporating all baseline demographic, clinical, and medication covariates. Potential multicollinearity among covariates included in the propensity score model was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with no evidence of severe multicollinearity observed. To improve covariate overlap, we trimmed observations below the 5th and above the 95th percentile of the propensity-score distribution before matching. Matching was performed 1:1 using nearest-neighbor algorithms without replacement. Covariate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences, with values < 0.10 indicating adequate balance. Missing data were handled as described in the Covariates section, using categorical indicators for variables with substantial missingness to minimize imputation-related bias.

Because inverse-probability weighting can become unstable in the presence of extreme weights, the primary analyses used PSM combined with doubly robust estimation. Adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using Cox proportional-hazards models including all baseline covariates, regardless of minor residual imbalance post-matching.

TriNetX does not support competing-risk modeling or provide patient-level event-time data required for Fine–Gray models. Given platform constraints for residual-based diagnostics (e.g., Schoenfeld residuals), the proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using prespecified time-stratified analyses across clinically relevant postoperative intervals (days 1, 7, 15, 30, 60, 90, 180, and ≥ 180). This approach explicitly allows hazard ratios to vary over time and provides an empirical assessment of potential non-proportionality. In addition, consistency of effect estimates across truncated follow-up periods was interpreted as evidence that differential early postoperative mortality was unlikely to substantially bias the observed associations. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery rate.

Regarding potential site-level effects, formal adjustment for clustering (e.g., mixed-effects modeling) was not feasible due to the federated architecture of the network, which precludes pooling of patient-level data across healthcare organizations. However, the TriNetX platform enforces rigorous data harmonization and standardized analytic pipelines across all participating sites to minimize methodological heterogeneity. Furthermore, the calculated E-values indicate that unmeasured confounding (including potential site-level variations) would need to be implausibly strong to explain away the observed associations.

To evaluate robustness to unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values for all primary outcomes. All analyses were performed using the TriNetX Advanced Analytics Platform. A two-sided P value < 0.05 (after correction when applicable) was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 91,090 patients between 60 and 80 years of age were included after propensity-score matching, with 45,545 assigned to the propofol cohort and 45,545 to the sevoflurane cohort. Baseline demographic, clinical, laboratory, and medication characteristics were well balanced across groups, with all standardized mean differences below 0.10 (Table 1 & eTable 1). The absolute incidence of delirium in this real-world cohort was lower than rates reported in prospective studies, a pattern consistent with the known under-ascertainment of delirium in electronic health records; however, this under-capture is expected to be non-differential and therefore would bias estimates toward the null rather than create a spurious association. Moreover, the delirium events captured in EHRs represent episodes judged by clinicians to be sufficiently severe to warrant documentation, reflecting a phenotype of clinically significant delirium rather than transient or borderline confusion; as such, the outcome likely identifies the most consequential cases.

Thirty-day all-cause mortality occurred in 300 patients (0.66%) in the propofol cohort and 242 patients (0.53%) in the sevoflurane cohort (aHR, 1.25; 95% CI, 1.05 to 1.48) (Table 2). Time-specific analyses showed that excess mortality was greatest immediately after surgery; on postoperative day 1, the aHR was 2.58 (95% CI, 1.32 to 5.03), with attenuation thereafter, although estimates remained elevated across the 7-day interval (eFigure 1).

The increased risk of delirium associated with propofol persisted across postoperative time points. The aHR was 1.21 (95% CI, 1.03 to 1.41) on day 1, rose to 1.40 (95% CI, 1.26 to 1.54) on day 7, and remained significant at 180 days (aHR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.19 to 1.32) (Fig. 2).

Long-term outcomes from postoperative days 180 to 365 also differed between groups. Postoperative cognitive dysfunction occurred in 2,581 patients (5.67%) in the propofol cohort and 1,712 patients (3.76%) in the sevoflurane cohort (aHR, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.09 to 1.24) (Table 3). Long-term delirium was diagnosed in 25.24% of patients receiving propofol and 18.53% of those receiving sevoflurane (aHR, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.13 to 1.20). Long-term all-cause mortality (> 180 days) was also higher among patients in the propofol cohort (9.40% vs. 6.21%; aHR, 1.23; 95% CI, 1.17 to 1.29).

Subgroup analyses demonstrated heterogeneity in the association between anesthetic type and delirium (Fig. 3). These stratified assessments were conducted to identify potential effect modification and should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. The strongest associations were observed among patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery (aHR, 2.02; 95% CI, 1.82 to 2.23) and respiratory procedures (aHR, 1.75; 95% CI, 1.47 to 2.08), with no significant differences among patients undergoing skin or musculoskeletal procedures. Systemic inflammation further modified the association: among patients with a baseline C-reactive protein level ≥ 10 mg/L, propofol was associated with higher risks of delirium (aHR, 1.93; 95% CI, 1.35 to 2.73) and mortality (aHR, 2.29; 95% CI, 1.15 to 4.51). Patterns of effect modification were similar for mortality (eFigure 2). Across all subgroup evaluations, point estimates favored sevoflurane.

To identify patient populations most susceptible to the differential effects of anesthesia, we performed stratified analyses. These analyses are exploratory and intended for hypothesis generation. The increased risk of delirium associated with Propofol was not uniform across all groups. Most notably, the type of surgery significantly modified the risk. Patients undergoing cardiovascular operations exhibited the highest risk elevation in the Propofol group (aHR, 2.02; 95% CI, 1.82–2.23), whereas no significant difference was observed in skin or musculoskeletal procedures. Furthermore, a strong interaction was observed with systemic inflammation; patients with CRP levels ≥ 10 mg/L faced a nearly two-fold increase in risk with Propofol (aHR, 1.93), while those with normal CRP levels showed no significant difference. Vulnerable populations, such as those on preoperative antipsychotics (aHR, 1.75) or opioids (aHR, 1.40), also showed a pronounced benefit from Sevoflurane. Discussion

Our study showed consistent associations between the choice of maintenance anesthesia and major postoperative outcomes in older adults undergoing surgery. Across a PSM cohort of more than 91,000 patients, propofol maintenance was associated with higher risks of POD (Table 2), 30-day mortality (Table 2), long-term cognitive dysfunction, and long-term mortality (Table 3) compared with sevoflurane. These differences were not transient: early separation in delirium incidence emerged on postoperative day 1 and continued to widen through 30 days (Fig. 1), and elevated delirium risk remained detectable up to 180 days (Fig. 2). The magnitude of association was greater among physiologically vulnerable subgroups—including patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery and those with systemic inflammation—where adjusted hazard ratios exceeded 2.0 (Fig. 3). These real-world findings contrast with several small randomized trials that reported minimal differences in short-term neurocognitive outcomes between anesthetic agents, yet those trials were underpowered for rare or long-term endpoints and often excluded high-risk surgical populations [1, 15–17, 24, 25, 30]. The large sample size, inclusion of “hard” outcomes such as mortality, and broad real-world representation in our analysis help address these limitations and allow a more comprehensive evaluation of the consequences of anesthetic choice in routine practice.

The observed differences in POD and mortality carry important clinical implications. Delirium is widely regarded as an acute marker of limited cognitive reserve and accelerated neurodegenerative vulnerability in older adults; [31–34] thus, the consistently higher delirium incidence with propofol across postoperative days 1 through 30 (Figs. 1 and 2) suggests that anesthetic choice may interact with underlying brain susceptibility rather than merely reflect transient postoperative fluctuations. The early divergence in 30-day mortality—most prominent within the first 24 h after surgery (Table 2)—further indicates that these findings are unlikely to represent coding variability alone, but instead reflect differential tolerance to acute physiologic stress. Importantly, excess early postoperative mortality may have introduced a survivor-depletion effect. The disproportionate removal of the most vulnerable patients from the propofol cohort would be expected to leave a comparatively hardier remaining population, rendering subsequent estimates of long-term neurocognitive risk conservative and biasing the association toward the null. In this context, the observation that mortality—a hard, objectively ascertained endpoint not subject to diagnostic under-capture—demonstrated the same directional disadvantage for propofol strengthens the inference that the parallel excess in postoperative delirium is unlikely to reflect a coding artifact alone, but instead supports a genuine difference in perioperative risk. Early postoperative mortality is often interpreted as an indicator of global organ vulnerability, and the parallel elevation in delirium, cognitive decline, and death strengthens the biological plausibility of a true drug-related difference [10, 11, 31, 33, 35–37]. The immediate risk signal associated with propofol, especially among patients undergoing cardiovascular procedures or those with systemic inflammation (Fig. 3), therefore warrants attention: these high-risk groups may be disproportionately affected by anesthetic strategies that provide less endothelial, inflammatory, or neuroprotective stability during the perioperative period [16, 24, 38, 39].

Several features of our findings align with established physiologic and clinical evidence and therefore reinforce their plausibility. Volatile anesthetics have long been recognized to exert organ-protective effects under ischemic and inflammatory stress; experimental and clinical studies have shown that agents such as sevoflurane activate preconditioning pathways that mitigate myocardial and cerebral injury during hypoperfusion [14, 16, 20, 40, 41]. These mechanisms provide a coherent biologic framework for the stronger advantage of sevoflurane that we observed in cardiovascular and thoracic surgery (Fig. 3), settings characterized by marked hemodynamic fluctuation and ischemia–reperfusion stress [14, 16, 20, 40–44]. The close concordance between excess delirium and excess mortality in the propofol group (Tables 2 and 3; eFigure 1) is also consistent with the established epidemiology of POD, which is not only an acute manifestation of brain vulnerability but also a strong predictor of long-term cognitive decline and death in older adults [35–37]. The stronger associations among patients with elevated preoperative inflammatory markers (CRP ≥ 10 mg/L) further align with evidence that aging brains are disproportionately susceptible to inflammation-mediated neuronal dysfunction [15], and that systemic inflammatory loads amplify the neurocognitive impact of perioperative stress [2, 4, 15]. Although CRP is a nonspecific marker, it serves here as a pragmatic surrogate of systemic inflammatory burden rather than a direct measure of neuroinflammation.

The divergence between our results and several prior randomized trials warrants careful consideration [1, 15, 17]. First, most trials enrolled highly selected [1, 15, 17], lower-risk patients and systematically excluded many of the populations in whom we observed the strongest associations—older adults with substantial comorbidity, elevated inflammatory markers, or undergoing high-risk cardiac and thoracic procedures (Fig. 3). These selection criteria sharply limit the applicability of previous findings to the real-world population represented in our cohort [1, 15, 17]. Second, earlier trials were generally too small to detect the modest but clinically important differences observed here [16, 17, 24, 26]. Detecting a 20–25% excess mortality or a 15–20% increase in long-term cognitive decline (Tables 2 and 3) requires sample sizes far exceeding the 100–400 participants typical of prior studies [16, 17, 24, 26, 39, 45]. Third, trial follow-up windows were short; most evaluated delirium or early cognitive recovery within 3–7 days [1, 17, 24, 25], whereas the excess risks associated with propofol in our study accumulated over 6–12 months (Fig. 2). Short-term assessments alone would not have captured these durable disadvantages [1, 17, 24, 25]. Finally, trial environments minimize the hemodynamic variability and physiologic stress that characterize routine perioperative care. Continuous titration of anesthetic depth, narrow blood-pressure targets, and reduced episodes of cerebral desaturation in protocolized trials may attenuate the conditions under which volatile anesthetics exert ischemic-preconditioning or anti-inflammatory effects [14, 16, 20, 39, 41, 46, 47]. In real-world practice, where intraoperative fluctuations are more common, these protective pathways may be more relevant, potentially magnifying the risk differences we observed [48–50]. Taken together, these methodological distinctions suggest that prior trials and our study examined fundamentally different clinical realities; [15, 17, 24, 25] as highlighted in recent commentaries, distinguishing efficacy under controlled protocols from effectiveness in routine practice is essential when interpreting apparently discordant evidence [51]. The findings here therefore complement rather than contradict the existing literature.

Specifically, our results diverge from the MYRIAD trial [52], which reported comparable mortality between volatile and intravenous anesthesia in cardiac surgery. A key distinction lies in exposure definition: the MYRIAD protocol was pragmatic and permitted concurrent propofol use in the volatile group, which may have diluted the specific organ-protective effects of volatile anesthetics. By strictly excluding mixed maintenance regimens, our real-world analysis allowed a more isolated assessment of the independent associations of each anesthetic class. Similarly, the recent SESAR trial reported adverse outcomes with inhaled sedation in critically ill patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome [53]. However, this discrepancy likely reflects a fundamental difference in physiologic context. SESAR evaluated prolonged inhaled sedation over several days in patients with established lung injury, a setting in which fluoride accumulation and impaired gas exchange may predominate. In contrast, our study focused on discrete intraoperative exposure lasting hours, a timeframe in which anesthetic preconditioning mechanisms are most biologically relevant and toxicity is minimized. Together, these findings may reflect different points along a temporal spectrum, distinguishing short-term protective preconditioning from potential toxicity associated with prolonged exposure in injured organs.

A biologically plausible framework may help explain why the excess risks associated with propofol were most pronounced in frail older adults and in physiologic states characterized by ischemia or inflammation. Volatile anesthetics such as sevoflurane have long been recognized to induce anesthetic preconditioning, engaging cytoprotective pathways that enhance cellular tolerance to metabolic stress [14, 16, 20, 41]. Three mechanisms are particularly relevant: activation of mitochondrial K_ATP channels, which stabilizes mitochondrial membrane potential during ischemia; low-grade ROS–mediated signaling that initiates anti-apoptotic cascades; and modulation of innate inflammatory responses [14, 18, 20, 41]. Experimental and clinical models suggest that these pathways can limit neuronal injury during hypoperfusion, cerebral desaturation, or systemic inflammatory activation [14, 18, 20, 41]—conditions that were common in the surgical contexts represented in our cohort. Propofol lacks these preconditioning properties and may, in some settings, counteract them [14, 16, 19]. Its potent antioxidant activity, while beneficial in reducing oxidative injury, may suppress the ROS-dependent signaling required to initiate ischemic tolerance [18, 42, 54, 55]. Propofol has also been shown to inhibit components of mitochondrial complex I, potentially diminishing cellular resilience during metabolic crisis [14, 16]. These pharmacologic features may become clinically relevant when hemodynamic instability or inflammatory surges occur—events that are tightly constrained in controlled trials but unavoidable in routine perioperative practice [1, 46, 49]. The elderly brain is particularly vulnerable to these perturbations [2, 56–58]. Age-related microvascular disease, impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation, diminished cardiac reserve, and heightened neuroinflammatory reactivity reduce the physiologic margin for error [2, 56–58]. In such contexts, even modest differences in an anesthetic’s capacity to maintain mitochondrial integrity or to attenuate inflammatory activation may translate into marked differences in postoperative trajectories [2, 56–58]. This mechanistic paradigm is consistent with our observation of disproportionate early mortality (day-1 aHR, 2.58; eFigure 1) and substantially elevated delirium risk among patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery (aHR, 2.02; Fig. 3).These findings suggest that the interaction between anesthetic choice and physiological frailty may be a critical determinant of perioperative brain vulnerability in older adults. Because direct intraoperative physiologic and molecular measurements were unavailable, these mechanistic interpretations should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than as empirically established causal pathways.

This study has limitations. First, intraoperative hemodynamic data and specific physiologic biomarkers (e.g., lactate, inflammatory cytokines) were unavailable, a constraint inherent to federated EHR networks. We addressed this by using broad clinical endpoints—delirium, long-term cognitive decline, and mortality—that integrate the cumulative effects of any hemodynamic variability and are not dependent on minute-to-minute physiologic recordings. Second, exposure classification relied on medication administration records without precise intraoperative timestamps, requiring the assumption that propofol administration within the sevoflurane cohort represented induction-only use. While this assumption is clinically plausible, absolute verification is not possible in federated EHR data lacking dose, duration, or depth-of-anesthesia information. To mitigate this limitation, we strictly excluded patients receiving mixed or nonstandard maintenance anesthetic regimens to minimize exposure ambiguity. Any resulting exposure misclassification is therefore expected to be predominantly non-differential, which would bias effect estimates toward the null rather than generate spurious associations. Third, POD is under-coded; we treated this as non-differential misclassification and performed a sensitivity analysis incorporating new antipsychotic initiation, which increased event detection but left the effect size unchanged, indicating that the association is likely attenuated rather than exaggerated. Additionally, the use of standardized terminology mappings (ICD-10) across the TriNetX network helps mitigate inter-site variability in outcome definitions. This antipsychotic-based definition was prespecified as a sensitivity analysis and reported separately, rather than serving as the primary outcome definition. Fourth, as with all observational studies, causality cannot be inferred. We therefore interpreted our findings as associative, while using consistency across delirium, incident cognitive diagnoses, and mortality to assess biological coherence. Fifth, residual confounding by indication remains possible despite extensive propensity-score matching and covariate balancing. Specifically, propofol (TIVA) may be preferentially selected for patients with airway concerns or hemodynamic instability that are not fully captured by diagnostic or procedural codes. Alternative explanations unrelated to direct drug toxicity, including unmeasured differences in intraoperative hemodynamic management, depth of anesthesia, or ICU admission patterns, could plausibly bias estimates in either direction and cannot be assumed a priori to favor one anesthetic strategy. However, the E-value for delirium (1.82) suggests that a single unmeasured confounder of sufficient strength to fully account for the observed association is unlikely. Sixth, we lacked a validated frailty index, such as the Hospital Frailty Risk Score [59, 60]. Chronological age and comorbidity burden do not perfectly capture biological vulnerability, and frailty is a known predictor of delirium and poor recovery. Due to the federated nature of the dataset, consistent longitudinal data required to reliably calculate such scores across all sites were not available. To mitigate this, we adjusted for extensive comorbidity profiles, polypharmacy, and physiological risk factors (e.g., renal function, systemic inflammation) that serve as proxies for physiological reserve. Although residual confounding by frailty remains possible, the robustness of our estimates across high-risk subgroups suggests that the findings are not driven solely by unmeasured vulnerability.

Taken together, these limitations reflect the trade-offs inherent to real-world evidence rather than analytic shortcomings. Although real-world data inevitably carry imperfections, they also capture large numbers of very old, high-risk patients who are rarely enrolled in randomized trials. This trade-off underscores—not undermines—the value of our findings, and the concordant pattern across delirium, incident cognitive diagnoses, and mortality supports a genuine difference in postoperative risk.

This study has several strengths. It is, to our knowledge, the largest real-world cohort to examine anesthetic choice and postoperative neurocognitive outcomes, providing statistical power well beyond that of prior randomized trials. The multinational, multi–health system design enhances generalizability by aggregating data across diverse clinical settings and practice environments. The evaluation of long-term neurocognitive outcomes—a domain rarely assessed in anesthesia trials—allowed us to characterize risk trajectories extending to one year after surgery. Time-stratified analyses provided granular resolution of early versus late hazards and helped disentangle the contribution of early excess mortality to downstream cognitive estimates. The results were directionally consistent across multiple clinically important endpoints, including 30-day mortality, long-term incident cognitive diagnoses, and long-term all-cause mortality, strengthening internal validity through concordance across hard and soft outcomes. Finally, the associations were observed across diverse subgroups and persisted in patients at elevated physiologic risk, supporting the robustness of the findings across varying clinical contexts.

The clinical implications of these findings are most relevant for older adults, particularly those with limited physiologic reserve or heightened vulnerability to ischemia and inflammation. In this population, even modest differences in organ stress responses may translate into meaningful differences in postoperative neurocognitive outcomes and survival. The consistent pattern of early mortality, delirium, and long-term cognitive decline observed with propofol suggests that anesthetic choice may play a more consequential role in high-risk geriatric patients than previously appreciated. Because volatile anesthetics have demonstrated preconditioning properties that attenuate ischemic and inflammatory injury, sevoflurane may offer relative advantages in settings where organ protection is clinically paramount—such as cardiovascular surgery or procedures performed in the context of systemic inflammation. Although these results do not establish causality nor define prescriptive practice, they may reasonably inform risk–benefit discussions when selecting maintenance anesthesia in older adults, especially those facing elevated ischemic or inflammatory stress. Finally, these clinical considerations must be balanced against environmental stewardship. While sevoflurane may offer potential neuroprotective advantages in selected high-risk patients, it is a potent greenhouse gas with a substantially larger carbon footprint than propofol. Future research should aim to identify specific patient phenotypes in whom the potential clinical benefit of volatile anesthesia justifies its environmental cost.

Conclusion

In this large real-world cohort, propofol maintenance anesthesia was associated with higher risks of postoperative delirium, long-term cognitive decline, and mortality in older adults, with the greatest divergence observed in patients facing ischemic or inflammatory vulnerability. These findings generate hypotheses regarding anesthetic choice in vulnerable older adults and underscore the need for adequately powered randomized trials incorporating long-term neurocognitive and survival endpoints to determine whether anesthetic strategy meaningfully alters outcomes in high-risk surgical populations.
