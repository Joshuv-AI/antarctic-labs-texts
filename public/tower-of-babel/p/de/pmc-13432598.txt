Intravascular Ultrasound in Chronic Limb‐Threatening Ischemia Endovascular Revascularization: A Multidisciplinary Survey of Clinical Practices and Operator Perceptions
Authors: Marc‐André d'Entremont, Eric A. Secemsky, Graham R. McClure, Krzysztof Kowalik, Laura M. Drudi, Samuel Lemaire‐Paquette, Andrew D. Brown, Elisa Greco, Andrew Benko, Anirudh Mirakhur, Elie Girsowicz, Jane Newman, Jonathan Misskey, Simon Bérubé, John Harlock, Sanjit S. Jolly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIntravascular ultrasound (IVUS) may be beneficial for guiding endovascular revascularization in patients with chronic limb‐threatening ischemia (CLTI); however, its use remains limited.MethodsWe surveyed interventional radiologists, vascular surgeons, and interventional cardiologists in the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom using a 13‐question, anonymous online questionnaire to evaluate clinical equipoise, willingness to randomize, and opinions and perceptions regarding IVUS use in patients with CLTI undergoing endovascular revascularization.ResultsA total of 105 operators (5.7% response rate) participated in the survey: 42 interventional radiologists, 40 vascular surgeons and 23 interventional cardiologists. Operators indicated the main obstacles to adopting or expanding IVUS for endovascular revascularization were cost or lack of reimbursement (69.5%), concerns about longer procedural time (47.6%), and the absence of randomized data supporting its benefit (39.0%). Overall, 80.0% of operators agreed that a large, multicenter, randomized trial was required, and 77.1% were willing to randomize patients in such a trial.ConclusionIn this multidisciplinary survey, the identified barriers for IVUS use in CLTI revascularization were cost/non‐reimbursement, increased procedural time, and lack of randomized evidence. Given respondents' clinical equipoise and willingness to randomize, large randomized controlled trials are needed to inform clinical practice.

Introduction

Chronic limb‐threatening ischemia (CLTI) involves ischemic rest pain or tissue loss lasting over 2 weeks in patients with confirmed peripheral artery disease (PAD) [1]. CLTI affects about 11% of patients with PAD and is associated with poor outcomes, including 1‐year mortality and amputation rates of 25% and 30%, respectively [2, 3, 4]. Endovascular revascularization can be considered as a first‐line therapy for lower‐extremity CLTI to prevent major adverse limb events (MALE) and to improve quality of life [1, 5, 6, 7]. However, restenosis limits the durability of endovascular revascularization [8]. Contemporary data from the Best Endovascular versus Best Surgical Therapy in Patients with Critical Limb Ischemia (BEST‐CLI) trial suggest a 25% major reintervention rate for the index limb at a median follow‐up of 2.7 years after endovascular revascularization in a CLTI population [5].

Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) has emerged as a key adjunct to enhance endovascular outcomes in PAD patients. Allan et al. randomized 150 patients with CLTI or claudication undergoing femoropopliteal revascularization and found lower 12‐month binary restenosis with IVUS guidance (55.4% vs. 72.4%, p < 0.01), favouring IVUS guidance over angiography guidance [9]. Ko et al. randomized 237 patients, most with claudication, to receive drug‐coated balloons with IVUS versus angiography guidance and observed a higher 12‐month primary patency rate with IVUS (83.8% vs. 70.1%, p = 0.01) [10]. Both trials were underpowered for hard clinical outcomes, limiting definitive conclusions.

The primary objective of the current study is to characterize operators' clinical equipoise and willingness to randomize patients in a large, definitive, randomized controlled trial of IVUS‐guidance versus angiography‐guidance for endovascular revascularization in patients with CLTI using a self‐administered online questionnaire. Our secondary objective is to determine operators' perceptions and opinions of IVUS use in patients with CLTI undergoing revascularization to inform future trial design.

Methods

Questionnaire Development

We created a multidisciplinary focus group of lower‐extremity endovascular operators composed of 2 vascular surgeons (J.H., G.M.), 1 interventional cardiologist (S.J.), and 1 interventional cardiology fellow (M.D.). Through discussion, five main domains of interest were generated [1]: Demographics [2], Procedural volume [3], Expertise criteria [4], Perceptions/Opinions [5], Equipoise/Willingness to randomize. Leveraging content and method experts in our focus groups, we then generated potential items within each domain. We then categorized and combined items, and subsequently eliminated redundancies. Items were then formatted using binary yes/no answers, categories, or 7‐point Likert scales. We then conducted pilot testing by sending the questionnaire to two vascular surgeons (L.D. and E.G.), one interventional cardiologist (E.S.), and one interventional radiologist (A.B.), with subsequent item reduction and modification to improve administrative ease and optimize comprehensibility [11]. The final self‐administered online English 13‐item questionnaire is available in the Supporting Information Materials.

Sampling Frame and Questionnaire Administration

The questionnaire was sent using three emails at 2‐week intervals to the following organizations and societies (total of 1832 email addresses): the Outpatient Endovascular and Interventional Society (OEIS) (580 email addresses), the Canadian Society for Vascular Surgery (CSVS) (220 email addresses), the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions (SCAI) targeting only those who reported an endovascular practice (593 email addresses), the Canadian Association for Interventional Radiology (CAIR) (204 email addresses), and the British Society of Interventional Radiology (BSIR) (580 email addresses). The survey period was between July 13, 2024, and November 6, 2024. There was no compensation for participation. The data for this survey were collected using SurveyMonkey Inc. (San Mateo, California, USA). The Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (HiREB) approved the current study.

Statistical Analysis

The individual respondent was the unit of analysis. Categorical data were reported as counts and percentages, whereas continuous data were presented as means with standard deviations or medians with 25th and 75th percentiles (interquartile range, IQR), depending on data normality. The respondents were separated into three groups [1]: interventional radiology [2], vascular surgery, and [3] interventional cardiology. Statistical testing was performed between the three groups using the non‐parametric Kruskal‐Wallis test for categorical variables and the chi‐squared test for categorical variables. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We did not adjust for multiplicity. All analyses were performed using R, version 4.2.3.

Results

Baseline Demographics of Operators

A total of 105 endovascular operators (5.7% response rate) from 5 countries participated in the survey: 42 interventional radiologists, 40 vascular surgeons, and 23 interventional cardiologists (Table 1). Most operators were aged 40–49 (36.5%), and 93.2% were male. Approximately 50% of respondents originated from the United States, with the most frequently cited work environment being a university‐affiliated hospital (48.6%). Operators estimated performing a median of 80 (IQR 40.0–150.0) CLTI endovascular revascularizations annually, with interventional cardiologists (35%, IQR 10.0–70.0) reporting a higher percentage of cases performed using IVUS compared to vascular surgeons (0%, IQR 0–31.3) or interventional radiologists (10.0%, IQR 0–50.0) (p < 0.01).

Operators' Opinions of IVUS Use in CLTI Revascularization

To achieve baseline competency, interventional cardiologists estimated that 25 (IQR 17.5–50.0) lifetime IVUS‐guided endovascular PAD revascularizations were required, compared with 10 for vascular surgeons (IQR 5.0–20.0) and 10 for interventional radiologists (5.0–25.0) (p = 0.01) (Table 2). Operators signalled that the leading barriers to initiating or increasing IVUS use were cost/non‐reimbursement issues (69.5%), potential increases in procedural time (47.6%), and lack of randomized trial data supporting its use (39.0%) (Table 3). Despite differences in health care systems, concerns regarding cost/non‐reimbursement issues were reported by 84.6%, 81.1%, and 57.7% of operators in the United Kingdom, Canada, and the United States, respectively. Of note, more vascular surgeons (35%) reported unfamiliarity with the equipment as a significant barrier to IVUS use than interventional radiologists (11.9%) or interventional cardiologists (0.0%) (p < 0.01) (Table 4).

Operator Equipoise and Willingness to Randomize

Overall, 80% of operators (87.0% in interventional cardiology, 80.0% in vascular surgery, and 76.2% in interventional radiology) agreed that a definitive large multicenter randomized controlled trial with relevant clinical outcomes was required to determine the potential benefit of IVUS in CLTI endovascular revascularization. In addition, 77.1% of operators (87.0% in interventional cardiology, 75.0% in vascular surgery, and 73.8% in interventional radiology) were willing to randomize patients in such a trial.

Discussion

Key Findings

In this international multidisciplinary survey of endovascular operators with expertise in PAD regarding IVUS use, we found that [1]: the vast majority (80.0%) had clinical equipoise when comparing IVUS versus angiography to guide procedures [2], most (77.1%) were willing to randomize patients in a large randomized controlled trial and that [3] the leading barriers of IVUS use were cost/non‐reimbursement, increased procedural time and lack of randomized data supporting its use. There also appears to be differential use of IVUS amongst the different specialties performing these procedures. Although the response rate was low, respondents were predominantly high‐volume operators, performing a median of 80 CLTI endovascular revascularization procedures annually. As such, the findings likely reflect the perspectives of experienced key opinion leaders, rather than those of practitioners in lower‐volume settings.

Implications for Clinical Practice and Research

There has been a growing use of IVUS for endovascular revascularization in patients with PAD [12]. This was initially supported by expert consensus, with infrainguinal IVUS use for procedural optimization deemed appropriate, and then by two subsequent small randomized controlled trials demonstrated promising results [9, 10, 13]. However, reported overall use was relatively low, with operators utilizing IVUS in only 11.7% of cases between 2016 and 2019 [12].

As demonstrated by our survey, IVUS use still remains low, even in our sample of high‐volume endovascular operators who performed a median of 80 CLTI endovascular revascularizations per year. Cost and non‐reimbursement are complex and vary by healthcare systems. There were numerical differences in how cost/non‐reimbursement was perceived as a barrier among operators from the USA, the UK, and Canada. This regional variation may reflect differences in healthcare financing models, with cost and reimbursement barriers more frequently reported in publicly funded systems (e.g., the UK and Canada) compared with the more heterogeneous, multi‐payer environment in the United States. However, economic analyses of both Japanese and German healthcare systems have demonstrated that IVUS use is associated with lower long‐term overall costs and improved outcomes [14, 15]. Whether IVUS truly adds to the total procedural time remains unclear in the literature. While the two randomized trials did not report procedural time, observational data from the Vascular Quality Initiative (n = 65,038) suggest an increase in fluoroscopy time by about 4 min (24.3 min vs. 21.9 min, p = 0.002, but a decrease in contrast use by 5 ml (80 vs. 85, p < 0.001) [9, 10, 16]. However, these data may be confounded by indication bias, whereas operators are more likely to use IVUS for more complex cases.

There was also a significant discrepancy in the perceived minimum number of IVUS‐guided endovascular revascularizations required to achieve baseline competency among interventional radiologists, vascular surgeons, and interventional cardiologists. This discrepancy may reflect differences in familiarity with IVUS, whereby operators with greater routine use—such as interventional cardiologists—have a more nuanced appreciation of its technical and interpretative complexities and therefore perceive a higher threshold for baseline competency.

A large randomized controlled trial is required to provide definitive evidence regarding the potential benefits of IVUS. The IVUS for PAD Revascularization (CLARITY) trial with the Population Health Research Institute as the coordinating center (Clinicaltrials.gov NCT06979284) will randomize CLTI Rutherford class 4–6 patients undergoing endovascular revascularization to an IVUS‐guided versus angiography‐guided revascularization strategy. The primary composite outcome will be time‐to‐first occurrence of MALE, defined as unplanned above‐ankle amputation of the index limb or clinically driven target‐lesion revascularization, or target‐lesion binary restenosis, defined as > 50% restenosis on vascular imaging. As demonstrated by our survey, the lack of high‐quality randomized evidence is a barrier to increasing IVUS use, and most operators have both clinical equipoise and willingness to randomize in such a trial. Complementary information from our survey will be helpful in planning and executing the CLARITY trial and subsequent trials. Notably, a substantial proportion of vascular surgeons reported limited familiarity with IVUS equipment, suggesting that education and knowledge translation may be important considerations for successful trial implementation.

Limitations

Our study's results should be interpreted considering the following limitations. First, our sample size is modest and may not be well‐representative given the low response rate. However, not all vascular surgeons or interventional radiologists on our email lists perform lower‐extremity CLTI revascularizations and therefore had no incentive to respond. Second, while our survey is international, it is mainly limited to the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom and therefore provides a limited view of practices in developed, English‐speaking countries. Third, given that the interventional cardiology cohort was almost exclusively based in the United States, comparisons across specialties may be partially confounded by geographic differences, as observed variations may reflect both specialty‐ and region‐specific practice patterns. Lastly, our survey was not statistically validated and may be unintentionally biased by its developers' own viewpoints.

Conclusions

In this international, multidisciplinary survey, the main perceived barriers to IVUS use in patients with CLTI undergoing revascularization were cost/non‐reimbursement, potential increase in procedural time, and lack of randomized evidence. Most operators had clinical equipoise and willingness to randomize. Large definitive randomized controlled trials are needed to provide evidence to guide clinical practice.
