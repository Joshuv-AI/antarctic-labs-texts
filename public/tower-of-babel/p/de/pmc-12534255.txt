Remark

It is worth noting that, as pointed out to me by the reviewer, if a positive answer to any of the questions above were to be found, it cannot be a monoid since by virtue of Lemma 2.4 below, a monoid which is universally rigid is necessarily a group, hence Cauchy complete and also stably universally rigid.

Remark

Neither [8, Cor. 3.3] nor the result presented here is more general. There seems nonetheless to be a common theme, as the split epimorphisms also play an important role in the current paper.

Remark 2.6

In a previous version of the paper, I wrongly assumed that the set of retracts of an object suffices to recover that object as the “maximal” element. If this were true, the argument to show Cauchy completeness at the end of the previous proof would only need universal rigidity instead of stable universal rigidity, since it would be impossible to have two non-isomorphic objects which are retracts of each other. My mistake was pointed out by the reviewer with a counter-example: in the category freely generated by two objects which are retracts of each other, these two objects are not isomorphic. Many examples can be found in usual concrete categories. In the category of topological spaces, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {N}\times [0,1]+\{ 0 \}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {N}\times [0,1]$$\end{document} are retracts of each other. In the category of posets, the infinite binary tree and the infinite ternary tree are retracts of each other.

Concluding Remarks

One could wonder whether the conditions (SUR3)(i) and (SUR3)(ii) are enough to deduce that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{C}$$\end{document} is universally rigid. Let us see why this is a natural question. The functor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X \mapsto \mathbf{Poset}(\mathbf{C}_{/X})^\textrm{op}$$\end{document} sending each object \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X$$\end{document} to the opposite of the poset reflection of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{C}_{/X}$$\end{document} is a polyadic poset as defined in [1, Dfn. 4.21] or [6, § 2.2, p. 69]. Viewing each poset as an Alexandroff locale, the functor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X \mapsto \mathbf{Poset}(\mathbf{C}_{/X})^\textrm{op}$$\end{document} is also a polyadic locale, more commonly called a geometric doctrine [9, Ch. III]. Its classifying topos is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[\mathbf{C}^\textrm{op},\mathbf{Set}]$$\end{document}, so the subtoposes of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[\mathbf{C}^\textrm{op},\mathbf{Set}]$$\end{document} are in bijection with its polyadic sublocales. If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{Poset}(\mathbf{C}_{/X})$$\end{document} is Artinian for each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X$$\end{document}, we know by [5, Thm. 2.12] that each polyadic sublocale is actually a polyadic subposet. This seems to be an important step towards universally rigidity, but the following counter-example shows that this is not enough. It would nonetheless be interesting to understand how to combine this remark with (SUR3)(iii) to deduce (stable) universal rigidity.

We defined stable universal rigidity by requiring that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{C}_{/X}$$\end{document} be universally rigid for every \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X \in \mathbf{C}$$\end{document}.
