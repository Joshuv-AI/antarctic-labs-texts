ReLink-PyB: an adapted REINVENT-based framework for non-DSM PfDHODH inhibitor discovery
Authors: Thejus Varghese Thomas, Amrita Thakur, S Anil Kumar, Andrew Tom, Aravind Krishnan, Sreeharsha Nagaraja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReLink-PyB, an integrated computational framework that synergistically combines reinforcement learning (RL), molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and quantum-level analyses, was developed to discover next-generation Plasmodium falciparum dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (PfDHODH) inhibitors. This study proposes a new series of non-DSM compounds the PyB Series having Pyrazole-benzene core, generated by this approach. PyB-318, a novel lead molecule from this series, shows compelling in silico evidence of drug-like behavior and target engagement, and proposes it as a promising antimalarial candidate against drug-resistant parasites. The lack of effective PfDHODH inhibitors, particularly the clinical trial failure of triazolopyrimidine-based (DSM) scaffolds, highlights the urgent need for novel antimalarial drug discovery. Using the REINVENT 4.0 platform and its LinkINVENT model, two rationally selected warheads, pyrazole and benzene, were employed to guide scaffold innovation, generating a user-defined library of 20,000 compounds. Following Rigorous RDKit-based cheminformatics filtering integrating synthetic accessibility (SynA), quantitative drug-likeness (QED ≥ 0.7), and lipophilicity (logP: −1 to 4) yielded 625 drug-like candidates. Molecular docking against PfDHODH (PDB ID: 6I4B) identified 264 compounds with binding affinities ranging from − 8.7 to − 8.5 kcal/mol, significantly exceeding the co-crystallized ligand E2N (− 7.8 kcal/mol). The top four docked complexes underwent rigorous 100-nanosecond molecular dynamics simulations, revealing PyB-318 as the optimal lead compound with exceptional stability (ligand RMSD: 2.5 Å, RMSF: 0.5 Å plateau by 10 ns) and superior conformational compactness (radius of gyration: 0.48 Å). MM-GBSA binding free energy calculations confirmed PyB-318’s thermodynamic superiority (ΔG_bind = − 52.3 kcal/mol), representing a 10.2 kcal/mol advantage over E2N and demonstrating remarkable resistance to single-point mutations through distributed secondary interactions. Quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM) analysis with DFT (6-31G (d, p)) affirmed binding interactions via bond critical point (BCP) evaluation, identifying five high-electron-density BCPs (ρ > 0.1 au) distributed across diverse residue pairs, further validating the superior thermodynamic and electronic landscape. PyB-318 exhibits exceptional drug-like properties with a QED of 0.71, optimal logP of 3.54, and most favourable synthetic accessibility (SynA: 2.51) superior to DSM265 and the reference ligand positioning it as a readily synthesizable lead candidate. ReLink-PyB thus establishes a systematic, transferable paradigm for accelerated de novo antimalarial drug discovery against drug-resistant parasites while providing a generalizable template for neglected-disease therapeutic development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47293-0.Subject terms: Chemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

Malaria remains a severe global infectious disease, with Plasmodium falciparum causing significant morbidity and mortality1,2. Despite advances in antimalarials, the rise of multidrug-resistant parasites continues to undermine frontline therapies, highlighting an urgent need for innovative drug discovery strategies3,4. A particularly promising target is the P. falciparum dihydroorotate dehydrogenase () enzyme, which is critical for the parasite’s de novo pyrimidine biosynthesis and, consequently, its survival5,6. While several inhibitors targeting PfDHODH have been developed, many, such as those based on the triazolopyrimidine (DSM) scaffold like DSM265, have shown promise but ultimately failed in clinical trials7,8. These hindrances are often attributed to issues like toxicity, poor metabolic stability, and unfavourable pharmacokinetic profiles9,10, underscoring the need for novel, non-DSM scaffolds with improved drug-like properties.

Pyrazole and benzene scaffolds possess favourable pharmacological properties and are widely found in approved drugs. Pyrazole is associated with diverse biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antiviral, and anticancer effects, antiparasitic, and antiviral11,12. Benzene derivatives promote π–π stacking and hydrophobic interactions, contributing to their frequent use in clinically approved antiparasitic and antiviral agents13,14. Together, these scaffolds represent privileged fragments and promising candidates for novel drug development.

Traditional drug discovery methods are often resource-intensive and limited in their ability to explore the vastness of chemical space for truly novel structures15,16. In contrast, computational approaches, particularly those driven by artificial intelligence (AI), offer a powerful alternative. Reinforcement Learning (RL), a subfield of AI, is exceptionally well-suited for de novo molecular generation. RL-based models can autonomously explore immense chemical space and perform “scaffold hopping” to design innovative molecules optimized against multiple pharmacological objectives, such as binding affinity, synthesizability, and lipophilicity, thereby surpassing the limitations of conventional methods17–24.

REINVENT 425, an RL platform, has established itself as a robust, open-source tool for de novo molecular design. It integrates advanced neural network architectures, including recurrent neural networks and transformers, within the RL frameworks. The LinkINVENT, one of its molecular generator model, identifies a linker between two selected fragments. It has been pre-trained on ChEMBL version 27 through drug-like compound filtering, SMIRKS-based reaction slicing to generate training tuples of (linker, warheads pair, full molecule), removal of unrealistic linkers (MW > 500 Da), SMILES randomization for data augmentation, and validation against 287 Bemis–Murcko scaffolds26. The prior features an encoder-decoder RNN architecture with embedding dimension 256 and three LSTM layers of 512 cells each, trained via teacher-forcing to learn the conditional probability distribution that generates chemically valid and biologically plausible linkers. LinkINVENT employs a policy-gradient reinforcement learning framework based on the “Difference between Augmented and Posterior” (DAP) loss strategy. Here an agent model initialized with prior parameters iteratively updates its policy through reward-scaled augmented log-likelihood, the agent samples 128 linked molecules per epoch, evaluates their desirability via a composite Scoring Function. Parameters are updated via stochastic gradient descent (Adam optimizer) to steer Sampling toward high-scoring linkers while maintaining prior constraints. The Scoring Function aggregates multiple objectives through a weighted geometric mean approach, integrating linker-specific properties. The properties include effective length, length ratio, molecular weight < 200 Da, rotatable bond ratio, ring count and physicochemical properties like QED, logP, TPSA, molecular weight, hydrogen bond donors/acceptors, rotatable bonds, Fsp3 for ADME prediction and oral bioavailability27.

This study, therefore, employs LinkINVENT for novel non-DSM scaffold design, and integrate PfDHODH specific computational validation framework. The workflow has been designated as ReLink-PyB framework, wherein PyB refers to Pyrazole–Benzene warheads. In this framework the assessment of LinkINVENT generated molecules’ target specificity will be done by docking against PfDHODH, stability of docked complex by MD simulations and the electronic-level mechanistic insight of protein drug interaction by QTAIM with density functional theory (DFT) analyses. Methodology

This study demonstrates an integrative de novo drug design pipeline where reinforcement learning-guided scaffold innovation, combined with rigorous atomistic and quantum validations, identifies non-DSM PfDHODH inhibitors. The workflow, illustrated in Fig. 2, combines reinforcement learning-guided molecular generation with detailed structural and electronic analyses methods used to ensure the scientific validity of our findings.

Detailed ReLink-PyB Methodology Workflow.

Ligand generation using reinforcement learning (RL)

Novel PfDHODH inhibitor candidates were generated using the REINVENT 4.0 platform, specifically its LinkINVENT module28, based on a specialized encoder-decoder RNN-based generator optimized for warhead-linker guided scaffold design. Two rationally selected warheads, pyrazole29,30 and benzene31,32(Figs. 3 and 4 respectively) were employed to guide scaffold innovation. These fragments serve as structural anchors to explore diverse linker topologies while maintaining pharmacophoric relevance to the PfDHODH target and deliberately circumventing the triazolopyrimidine-based (DSM) scaffold class that encountered clinical trial failures.

Benzene structure with the linker positions represented as *.

The training protocol operated over 50–100 epochs with a batch size of 128 molecules per epoch, generating a total library of user defined 20,000 unique compounds with retention of full SMILES strings, scoring components, and epoch tracking. The raw set of generated molecules including duplicates and invalid chemical structures were discarded based on SMILES syntax validation and fundamental valence rules.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)33 was employed on the initially generated molecules to analyse the dimensionality and inter-relationships among the six predicted molecular descriptors: estimation of drug-likeness (QED), partition coefficient (logP/SlogP), molecular weight, synthetic accessibility (SynA) score, Lipinski hydrogen bond acceptors/donors. Prior to PCA, all descriptors were standardized using Z-score normalization (auto scaling) in Origin 2024, transforming each variable to a mean of zero and unit standard deviation to ensure equal weighting regardless of their original measurement scales.

PCA was performed by computing the Pearson correlation matrix from the standardized descriptor data, followed by eigenvalue decomposition to extract principal components. The correlation matrix was employed given the heterogeneous scales of the original descriptors.

The standardized data matrix had dimensions of designed molecules × 6 (descriptors). Eigenvalue decomposition yielded a complete set of orthogonal eigenvectors and their corresponding eigenvalues (λ), each quantifying the variance captured by successive principal components. Principal component loadings were computed to identify which descriptors most strongly contribute to each principal component. The cumulative variance explained threshold of 80% was used to determine the number of retained principal components. A two-dimensional score plot visualizing Principal Component 1 (PC1) versus Principal Component 2 (PC2) was generated to display the distribution of all molecules in reduced dimensionality space. The score plot was color-coded by a score to simultaneously reveal clustering patterns in physicochemical property space and correlate them with scoring performance.

Cheminformatics-based filtering

A multi-parameter filtering mechanism to ensure drug-likeness and practicality for all produced compounds. SynA scores assessed chemical synthesis feasibility, excluding substances scoring above 6 due to difficult synthetic methods. To eliminate candidates with poor drug-like profiles, molecules with QED < 0.7 were removed. QED34 values are derived by integrating molecular weight, lipophilicity, solubility, topological polar surface area, hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, rotatable bonds, and aromatic rings. Additionally, logP values were limited to −1 to 4 to optimize pharmacokinetics and preserve balanced lipophilicity. These strict criteria screened only promising candidates with sound physicochemical and pharmacological features for further assessment. Information related to the calculation of filtering criteria and their limiting values is provided in supplementary material as Tables ST1 and ST2, respectively. RDKit (Python 3.9), coupled with in-house Python scripts36 used for the post-processing and filtering, has been attached in the supplementary materials.

Molecular docking-based screening

Molecular docking was performed using Autodock Vina software36 against the PfDHODH crystal structure (PDB ID: 6I4B, resolution 2.8 Å)37. The protein was prepared by removing crystallographic water molecules and non-essential ions while retaining the co-crystallized ligand (E2N: 3-hydroxy-1-methyl-5-[3-trifluoromethyl] phenoxymethyl-1 H-pyrazole-4-carboxylic acid) for binding site reference. The docking grid was defined as a cubic box centered on the co-crystallized ligand’s centroid (coordinates: X = −3.165 Å, Y = − 17.08 Å, Z = 25.2 Å) with dimensions of 50 × 22 × 54 Å³ to encompass the ubiquinone-binding pocket and accommodate conformational flexibility of generated ligands. A secondary empirical scoring function (London dG) was applied to validate pose ranking; this function estimates binding affinity based on hydrogen bonding, ionic interactions, and hydrophobic contacts, providing a complementary ranking metric. All generated ligands were evaluated using identical docking parameters to ensure consistent comparison. To validate the reliability of the docking setup, E2N was re-docked using identical parameters.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

The top compounds from molecular docking underwent molecular dynamics simulations using GROMACS 22.4.3738and the CHARMM36 force field39. The protein was prepared at pH 7.4 using a standard protein preparation workflow, which included assigning protonation states to titratable residues (Asp/Glu deprotonated, Lys/Arg protonated) and manually setting histidines (HID/HIE/HIP) according to their local hydrogen‑bonding environment. The docked ligands were protonated for their dominant microspecies at pH 7.4 and parametrized with CGenFF, with penalty scores inspected and high‑penalty torsions adjusted to ensure compatibility with the CHARMM36mm force field. Other parameters include explicit TIP3P water, with each complex solvated in a cubic box (≥ 10 Å padding), neutralized with Na⁺/Cl⁻. Further the system was energy minimized, equilibrated for 100 ps in the NVT ensemble at 300 K (velocity‑rescale thermostat, τ = 0.1 ps) and 100 ps in the NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 bar (same thermostat plus Parrinello-Rahman barostat, τ = 2.0 ps), followed by 100 ns production using a 2 fs time step. Other settings for MD include Particle‑mesh Ewald electrostatics, a 1.0 nm real‑space cut-off, LINCS constraints on bonds to hydrogens. The trajectory frames saved every 10 ps for analysis.

Trajectory analysis included RMSD of protein and ligand components, RMSF for local flexibility measurement, radius of gyration for protein compactness, and total energy profiles for system stability assessment. The stability was quantified by the RMSD of the backbone atoms (N, Cα, C) after least‑squares fitting of each trajectory frame to the minimized starting structures over the backbone of the entire protein. Ligand RMSD was computed separately using only the ligand heavy atoms (all non‑hydrogen atoms) while keeping the protein alignment fixed, thereby reporting pure conformational and positional fluctuations of the ligand within the binding site rather than overall complex motion40..

Molecular mechanics–generalized born surface area (MM-GBSA)

Binding free energy (ΔG_bind) was calculated using the MM-GBSA approach to quantitatively assess thermodynamic stability and rank the top compounds. ΔG_bind values were calculated for the top 4 compounds (based on docking scores) and the E2N to enable quantitative ranking and cross-validation against docking scores and MD stability metrics (RMSD, RMSF, hydrogen bond occupancy).

Single-point energy evaluations were conducted without re-equilibration to minimize computational cost while capturing ensemble-averaged energetics. Entropic contributions are reported separately, given computational expense and known approximations in normal mode analysis; ΔG_bind values are presented both with and without the −TΔS term to allow independent assessment.

Quantum mechanical validation (QTAIM and DFT analyses)

The complex showing the most stable interactions was subjected to quantum mechanical analysis via QTAIM41. Molecular structures were optimized using DFT with the 6-31G (d, p) basis set42, and electron density was calculated at the optimized geometry. Bond critical points (BCPs) were identified using the AIMALL package (Version 19.10.12)43, which locates local minima of electron density along interatomic paths. For each BCP, the electron density ρ and Laplacian ∇²ρ were computed. The Laplacian sign distinguishes interaction types: ∇²ρ > 0 indicates non-covalent interactions (hydrogen bonds, van der Waals contacts), while ∇²ρ < 0 suggests covalent character. A total of 85 BCPs corresponding to direct protein-ligand contacts were identified and analysed. Five key interactions with the highest electron density and greatest impact on binding were selected for detailed chemical interpretation.

Results and discussions

Molecular generation and initial validation

REINVENT 4.0’s LinkINVENT model generated 20,000 novel molecular candidates incorporating pyrazole and benzene scaffolds. The selection of attachment positions on these core structures was critical44. Suboptimal choices led to chemically invalid molecules, which were systematically removed. Initial validation and duplicate removal reduced the library to 17,000 unique, chemically valid molecules. The core-specific generation approach preserved essential drug like features and introduced structural diversity through systematic variation of linker regions and peripheral substituents. These molecules confirmed successful exploration of structurally related yet distinct molecular entities within the chemical space around the target scaffolds. SMILES generated are attached in supplementary materials (Smile.csv).

PCA analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of these 17,000-compound revealed a concentrated distribution of molecules near the PCA centre (score ≈ 0) (Fig. 5), indicating successful RL-guided constraint of the chemical space to drug-like regions. The majority of molecules (> 70%) clustered within ± 3 standard deviations of the origin, while peripheral molecules at extreme PC1/PC2 coordinates (± 5 to ± 8). The peripheral molecules represented property outliers with distinct scaffold types and higher descriptor variance. This non-random distribution validates that the RL Scoring Function effectively optimized for drug like attributes with moderate, balanced descriptor values rather than extreme property combinations.

Two-dimensional PCA score plot illustrating the distribution and clustering of molecules based on the first two principal components, demonstrating the variance captured by key molecular descriptors and the relative positioning of the PyB-series compounds in descriptor space.

Descriptor correlation analysis (Table 1) revealed generally low to moderate inter-relationships, with the moderate correlations observed between SynA score and HBD (r = 0.394), MW and HBA (r = 0.346), MW and SynA score (r = 0.248) and MW and HBD (r = 0.377). The weak correlations between QED and SlogP (r = − 0.034), QED and MW (r = − 0.042), and most other descriptor pairs demonstrated substantial independence among variables. The moderate negative correlation between SlogP and HBA (r = − 0.354) reflects the expected lipophilic-versus-polar trade-off in medicinal chemistry. The low-to moderate overall correlation confirmed their use as complementary state variable in the RL framework, enabling balanced multi-objective optimization while avoiding redundant feature learning and chemically implausible reward exploitation.

PCA analysis (Fig. 5), thus, demonstrated that generated molecules exhibited continuous, balanced coverage of drug-like property space with chemically diverse scaffold architectures spanning a continuous distribution rather than discrete clusters. High-scoring (yellow) molecules concentrated in central-to-upper PCA regions, correspond to moderate, balanced descriptor values: intermediate logP (~ 1–3), MW (~ 300–450 Da), QED (~ 0.6–0.8), and low synthetic difficulty (SynA ~ 3–5) while peripheral low-scoring (purple) molecules represented property extremes (highly lipophilic, large, or synthetically inaccessible scaffolds). The analysis points to a potential reduction in the number of molecules with desired drug-like features relative to the designed compounds.

Comprehensive molecular filtering

A refined RDKit based filtering pipeline was applied to the 17,000 valid molecules. The filtering protocol utilized QED and SynA scores as primary selection criteria. The screening yielded 625 compounds (3.5% of the initial library) with optimized pharmaceutical properties were obtained. These compounds exhibited superior drug-likeness (mean QED > 0.7) and synthesizability (mean SYNA score < 4.0), molecular weight below 500, and the LogP between − 1 and 5. These values indicate successful optimization toward oral bioavailability while retaining essential structural features.

Visual inspection of filtered molecules showed promising results. Their heterocyclic scaffolds include pyrroles, furans, thiazoles, pyrazoles, thiophenes along with the inputted benzene and pyrazole. (Attached with supplementary materials-(molecule_grid)). It is well known that heterocyclic cores in molecules contribute to metabolic stability and interactions with biological targets through hydrogen bonding and π-π interactions45,46. Designed linker demonstrated presence of simple alkyl chains, ether groups, and amide linkers provide flexibility and spatial orientation in the molecules47,48. The molecular structure also demonstrated presence of polar functional groups like hydroxyls, amines and amides. These groups ensure solubility and hydrogen bonding property while aromatic rings and substituents in structure help with the hydrophobic and stacking interactions with the protein49,50. Overall, our generated compounds are expected to show pharmacophoric relevance and potential biological activity while balancing structural variety, synthetic accessibility, and drug-like features.

Some of the representative molecules are shown in Fig. 6. Most frequent functional groups in these molecules include amines, amides, carboxyl groups, and esters. These are linked by short alkyl chains, usually one carbon atom long. Short linkers has the right blend of flexibility and stiffness to preserve optimal interaction, spatial orientation and reduced entropic penalties upon binding. In medicinal chemistry, short alkyl linkers allow the molecule to evolve but limit conformational freedom that could reduce binding affinity51,52. Molecular docking studies

The PfDHODH crystal structure (PDB 6I4B) contains a conserved binding site network critical for substrate recognition and catalytic activity. Two key residues, HIS185 and ARG265, are essential for coenzyme Q (CoQ) binding and are conserved across PfDHODH orthologs. HIS185’s imidazole ring serves as a hydrogen bond donor/acceptor, while ARG265’s guanidinium group provides strong electrostatic interaction for ligand binding37.

Our docking studies reveal a binding mechanism with multiple complementary interaction networks. The designed ligands form conventional hydrogen bonds with HIS185 and ARG265 within the CoQ binding pocket, anchoring the pyrazole core and carbonyl and hydroxyl functional groups through the conserved electrostatic interactions. Beyond these primary anchoring points, the ligands engage an extensive secondary hydrophobic network comprising PHE188, PHE227, and PHE71, which facilitate strong π–π stacking interactions. Additionally, π-sulfur interactions between the aromatic rings of the designed molecules and CYS175 contribute to hydrophobic stabilization.

The binding pocket also accommodates the non-polar linker of molecules through van der Waals contacts with TYR528 and VAL532, distributing interactions across a broader surface area. Critically, a deep hydrophobic pocket formed by an isoleucine and leucine cluster (ILE179, ILE263, ILE272, LEU172, LEU176, LEU187) provides a selectivity-determining microenvironment. This region preferentially accommodates aliphatic and heterocyclic linker. . Also, GLY181 contributes carbon-hydrogen bonds with polar functional groups (ketones, esters, amides) in the linker region, enabling bulky ligand scaffolds to bind without steric hindrances. Molecular dynamics simulation analysis

Structural stability of the docked complex of the top hit molecules (Table 4), and E2N, was assessed by MD simulations.

It is evident that amongst the top four PyB-series compounds, PyB-318 (Rank4) emerged as the optimal lead compound. RMSD analysis showed that compound PyB-318 (Rank4) achieved the most stable binding configuration ligand RMSD stabilizing at 2.5 Å (Fig. 7) after an initial equilibration period of 10 nanoseconds. These values were much lower than those of the other compounds, indicating better conformational stability throughout simulation. Finally binding free energy calculations were performed to assess protein-ligand complex’s thermodynamic stability. The RMSF study showed that PyB-318 had an impact on the protein-ligand complex binding site, with typical RMSF values of 0.05 Å (Fig. 8). The number of hydrogen bonds in the complex is two (Fig. 9), which exactly matches the docking results. Radius of gyration calculations demonstrated that the PfDHODH-PyB-318 complex maintained compact, stable conformations 0.48 Å (Fig. 10).

The competitive low RMSD values for rank 1 (PyB-338) and rank 4 (PyB-318) molecules can be attributed to the presence of thio groups (–S–) in the structures of these ligands. The sulphur, with a distinctive capacity for diverse noncovalent interactions, such as sulphur–aromatic (S–π) and sulphur–hydrogen bonds significantly enhance binding stability and rigidity in the protein-ligand complex53,54. This unique electronic property of sulphur contributes to better RMSD and RMSF results during MD simulations. These interactions help maintain molecular orientation and restrict unnecessary movement within the binding pocket, explaining the superior stability observed with these molecules. In contrast, although both rank 2 (PyB-514) and rank 3 (PyB-184) have pyrimidine and piperidine rings, rank 2 fails to form any hydrogen bonds and is limited in overall interaction diversity, while rank 3 forms only sparse hydrogen bonds.

The absence or limitation of such strong stabilizing bonds in rank 2 (and the only moderate interaction capacity in rank 3) results in less effective binding and thus reduced MD simulation performance compared to thio-containing molecules. Therefore, the synergy of thio-mediated interactions with other functional groups explains the similarity in dynamic results of rank 1 and rank 3 (Fig. 7), and highlights that interaction diversity, rather than ring similarity alone, is critical for superior protein-ligand stability.

Rank 4 performed best in MD simulations because it forms a complex network of interactions with the protein binding site. The two conventional hydrogen bonds with HIS185 and ARG265 stabilize and specificize ligands, and several pi-alkyl, carbon-hydrogen, alkyl, and pi-sulphur bonds strengthen and retain orientation. Presence of thiophene and piperidine rings, ester, amine, and ketone functional groups, which are both hydrophobic and polar, in the molecular scaffold, helped in efficient interaction with a wide range of residues. These structural features also helped in improved stability by fitting perfectly into hydrophobic pockets and allowing multiple hydrogen bonding and dipole-driven contacts. This mix of contact types and functional group diversity gives rank 4 the most dynamic flexibility and durable binding throughout simulation, as compared to the more rigid or interaction-limited architectures of the other top molecules. Ligand-induced stabilization: mechanistic validation via Apo protein control

To quantify PyB-318-driven conformational stabilization5556, an Apo PfDHODH (PDBID:6I4B) simulation of 100 ns production, in identical conditions to holo runs established a protein backbone RMSD baseline of 2.25 Å (Fig. 11) and binding site RMSF of 2.0 Å (Fig. 12), with a dramatic peak at residue ~ 180 (HIS185 region) reaching 6.7 Å revealing an inherently dynamic, disordered unliganded pocket. Although PyB-338 (Rank 1) achieved a marginally superior docking score (− 8.71 kcal/mol vs. −8.62 kcal/mol for PyB-318), it was deprioritized due to inferior RMSD stability (1.5 Å vs. 1.25 Å) and radius of gyration (5 Å vs. 4 Å). These residues are catalytically critical for the PfDHODH. This convergence of structural, thermodynamic, and interaction-based criteria uniquely identified PyB-318 as the optimal lead.

Quantum mechanical validation through QTAIM analysis

The lead compound, PyB-318’s mechanistic insights into the protein-ligand interaction was analysed using QTAIM Analysis. Its predicted binding mode was investigated utilizing quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM) analysis of the PfDHODH–PyB-318 complex using DFT geometry optimization (6-31G (d, p) basis set) and bond critical point (BCP) evaluation. QTAIM classifies interactions using electron density ρ(r_BCP) and Laplacian ∇²ρ(r_BCP), with ∇²ρ > 0 indicating non-covalent interactions (hydrogen bonds, van der Waals) and < 0 indicating covalent interactions.

The Laplacian of the electron density, which is another strong indicator of the nature of the interaction between any two atoms joined by BCP. Additional discussion: scientific novelty and comparative advantages

This study introduces a reinforcement learning guided, warhead-constrained molecular design strategy tailored to antimalarial drug discovery, addressing a critical gap in the application of deep generative models to neglected- disease targets. While generative frameworks have been widely explored for oncology and GPCR targets, their adaptation to PfDHODH, particularly with explicit preservation of pharmacophoric warheads, has not been previously demonstrated. By applying the LinkINVENT framework to PfDHODH, this work establishes a novel use case in which linker optimization is leveraged to generate chemically valid, non-DSM scaffolds with favorable drug-like properties.

A key innovation lies in the multi-objective reinforcement learning formulation, which simultaneously optimizes predicted drug-likeness, synthetic accessibility, and lipophilicity. This contrasts with conventional pipelines that either optimize a single objective or apply filters sequentially, often leading to high attrition rates. The integrated strategy employed here results in a substantially enriched hit set, demonstrating that reinforcement learning can be used not only for molecule generation but also as an effective prioritization engine.

Compared with traditional virtual screening and standard SMILES-based generative approaches, the proposed workflow offers several advantages: markedly higher chemical validity, improved hit enrichment, and access to structurally diverse, non-DSM scaffolds that are difficult to obtain through analogue-based design. Importantly the identified PyB series introduces heterocyclic cores distinct from clinically failed DSM scaffolds, addressing a known limitation in PfDHODH inhibitor development. The observed improvements in predicted binding affinity over the benchmark inhibitor further support the practical relevance of the approach.

Beyond the immediate application to PfDHODH, the framework presented here is scalable and transferable to other antimalarial and antiparasitic targets with available structural information. As such, this work provides not only specific candidate molecules but also a generalizable, resource-efficient paradigm for AI-assisted drug discovery in settings where experimental high-throughput screening is limited.

Conclusion

In summary, this study presents ReLink-PyB, a systematic and transferable computational framework that integrates reinforcement learning-guided de novo molecular generation, rigorous cheminformatics filtering, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, MM-GBSA thermodynamic analysis, and quantum mechanical validation to identify novel non-DSM PfDHODH inhibitors. From a user-defined library of 20,000 LinkINVENT-generated compounds incorporating pyrazole and benzene warheads, a multi-stage filtering and screening pipeline culminated in the identification of PyB-318 as the optimal lead candidate. PyB-318 demonstrated exceptional binding stability (ligand RMSD: 1.25 Å; RMSF: 0.5 Å), the strongest thermodynamic affinity among all candidates (MM-GBSA ΔG_bind: −52.3 kcal/mol), dual hydrogen bond engagement with the two conserved catalytic residues HIS185 and ARG265, and superior drug-like properties (QED: 0.71; SynA: 2.51) compared to both the co-crystallised reference ligand E2N and the clinically trialled DSM265. QTAIM analysis further validated the electronic robustness of PyB-318’s binding interactions through five high-electron-density bond critical points distributed across diverse residue pairs, suggesting resilience against single-point mutations, a critical advantage in the context of drug-resistant Pf. Beyond the identification of PyB-318, the broader significance of this work lies in the ReLink-PyB framework itself, which establishes a generalizable, AI-driven paradigm for accelerated antimalarial drug discovery that can be readily adapted to other neglected tropical diseases and emerging drug-resistant pathogens. By deliberately circumventing the failed DSM scaffold class and introducing the novel PyB series as a structurally distinct chemotype, this framework addresses a critical gap in the current antimalarial drug discovery landscape. Future experimental validation through enzymatic inhibition assays, parasite culture studies, and in vivo pharmacokinetic profiling will be essential to advance PyB-318 toward preclinical development, with the ultimate goal of delivering effective therapeutics against drug-resistant malaria.

​Limitations and future scope

While the ReLink-PyB framework demonstrates a rigorous and multi-layered computational pipeline, several inherent limitations of the in-silico approach must be acknowledged. First, molecular docking using AutoDock Vina, though validated against the co-crystallised ligand E2N, relies on an empirical scoring function that approximates binding affinity and may not fully capture the complexity of protein–ligand interactions. Particularly provides limited inderstanding of the entropic contributions and induced-fit conformational changes upon binding. Second, although 100 ns MD simulations provided meaningful insights into binding stability, longer simulation timescales or enhanced sampling methods such as replica exchange MD may be required to capture rare conformational events and slow binding/unbinding kinetics. Third, MM-GBSA binding free energy calculations, while more rigorous than docking scores, involve approximations in solvation modelling and entropic estimation via normal mode analysis, which can introduce systematic errors. Fourth, QTAIM analysis was performed on a truncated model of the binding site rather than the full protein–ligand complex, which may not fully represent the electronic environment in the physiological context. Fifth, the predicted ADMET properties of PyB-318, though favourable in silico, require experimental validation through cell-based permeability assays, metabolic stability studies, and in vivo pharmacokinetic profiling. Accordingly, future work should prioritise the chemical synthesis of PyB-318, followed by in vitro enzymatic inhibition assays against recombinant PfDHODH, antimalarial activity testing in Plasmodium falciparum culture models, and ultimately in vivo efficacy and toxicity evaluation in rodent malaria models. Such experimental validation will be essential to confirm the translational potential of PyB-318 as a next-generation antimalarial candidate.
