Evaluating failure to rescue after esophagectomy: The esophagectomy failure to rescue assessment, trends, and Evaluation (E-FATE) study
Authors: Christina S Boutros, Philip A Linden, Omkar Pawar, Boxiang Jiang, Jillian Sinopoli, Leonidas Tapias, Christopher W Towe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFailure to rescue, defined as death after a postoperative complication, is a key metric for evaluating hospital performance and surgical quality. We hypothesized that both patient characteristics and hospital performance are associated with failure to rescue risk after esophagectomy.MethodsWe analyzed esophagectomy cases from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons General Thoracic Surgery Database (2012-2023), excluding emergency surgeries and colonic interpositions. The primary outcome was failure to rescue, defined as death within 30 days or during the index hospitalization after a complication. Hospitals were grouped into quintiles based on their risk-adjusted standardized mortality ratio using hierarchical logistic regression. We compared failure to rescue rates between hospitals in the top (very low standardized mortality ratio) and bottom (very high standardized mortality ratio) quintiles using generalized estimating equations to account for clustering. Failure to rescue rates were further stratified across patient risk categories using established Society of Thoracic Surgeons risk metrics.ResultsA total of 28,626 patients from 318 hospitals were included. Top-tier hospitals performed more procedures than bottom-tier hospitals (10,431 vs 3527, P < .001) and had significantly lower unadjusted mortality (1.2% vs 6.6%, P < .001). Adjusted odds of failure to rescue were 4.4 times higher in bottom-tier hospitals (95% CI, 3.6-5.3), with similar trends after major complications (odds ratio, 3.83) and esophagectomy-specific complications (odds ratio, 7.27). Across all patient risk strata, bottom-tier hospitals had higher failure to rescue rates; high-risk patients had an odds ratio of 5.94 (95% CI, 3.6-9.6).ConclusionsHospital performance is strongly associated with failure to rescue after esophagectomy, even after adjusting for patient risk. System-level interventions are needed to reduce variation in rescue capacity.Key Words: esophagectomy, failure to rescue, hospital performance, surgical outcomes

Hospital tier impacts FTR across all patient risk groups after esophagectomy.

Central Message

Hospital performance, not just patient risk, drives variation in FTR after esophagectomy, highlighting the need for system-level improvements to reduce preventable mortality.

Perspective

Using national STS data, we show that high-performing hospitals have significantly lower FTR rates after esophagectomy. These differences persist across all patient risk groups and reflect institutional ability to recognize and manage complications early.

Esophagectomy remains a high-risk surgical procedure associated with significant morbidity and mortality.1,2 Despite advances in surgical techniques and perioperative care, complication rates after esophagectomy remain high, with up to 65% of patients experiencing some form of postoperative complication.3 In recent years, failure to rescue (FTR), defined as death after a postoperative complication, has emerged as a critical quality metric in surgical care, particularly for complex procedures like esophagectomy because it acknowledges the inevitability of complications while evaluating a hospital's proficiency in managing and mitigating adverse outcomes.4,5 Unlike raw complication rates, which may not fully capture institutional capabilities in patient rescue, FTR focuses on the effectiveness of systems in place to address clinical deterioration, thereby offering a more nuanced assessment of hospital performance.5

Although the overall rate of complications remains high across institutions, recent data from large national databases have revealed significant variation in the incidence and severity of complications, particularly between high- and low-volume centers.3,6 These variations in complication rates persist across hospital volume despite adjusting for patient age and comorbities.7 Although previous studies have identified associations between hospital volume and outcomes,6,7 volume alone does not fully explain the observed variation in FTR rates.8 Additionally, the relative contributions of patient factors and hospital performance to FTR risk have not been described previously.

The objectives of this study are (1) to evaluate FTR rates after esophagectomy across a large cohort of hospitals and (2) to assess the impact of both patient characteristics and hospital performance on FTR risk, hypothesizing that both patient characteristics and hospital performance are associated with an FTR risk after esophagectomy. By addressing these objectives, we aim to provide insights that can inform quality improvement initiatives and guide efforts to optimize outcomes for patients undergoing esophagectomy.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Data Source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons General Thoracic Surgery Database (STS-GTSD) from 2012 to 2023. The data were acquired through the STS Participant User File (PUF) request process. The STS-GTSD is a national clinical registry that captures detailed information on patient characteristics, surgical procedures, and outcomes for cardiothoracic surgeries performed across participating institutions in the United States.

To access the STS PUF, researchers are required to submit a formal application to the STS Research Center, outlining the study's objectives, methodology, and proposed statistical analyses. The application undergoes review by the STS Research and Analytic Center to ensure compliance with STS policies and ethical standards. Upon approval, the dataset is deidentified in accordance with Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act regulations before being released for analysis. The PUF provides patient-level data while omitting institutional and surgeon identifiers to uphold confidentiality.

Study Population

All adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent esophagectomy during the study period were considered for inclusion. We excluded emergency surgeries and cases involving colonic interposition due to their increased risk profile. We included patients who received elective esophagectomies using standard reconstruction techniques. Patients with missing data on key variables or outcomes were also excluded from the analysis. Esophagectomy-specific complications were defined a priori based on those reliably captured in the STS database: anastomotic leak, conduit necrosis requiring surgery, and postoperative esophageal dilation. Other complications, such as chyle leak or tracheoesophageal fistula, were not included due to inconsistent coding.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome of interest was FTR, defined as death occurring within 30 days of surgery or during the index hospitalization after a postoperative complication.

Hospital Performance Categorization

To assess hospital performance, we calculated risk-adjusted standardized mortality ratios (SMRs) for each participating hospital using hierarchical logistic regression models. These models accounted for patient-level risk factors (adjusting for age, sex, body mass index [BMI], smoking, comorbidities, prior treatment, and pathologic staging) and the clustering of patients within hospitals. Based on their SMRs, hospitals were grouped into quintiles, with the top quintile representing very low-risk (top-tier) hospitals and the bottom quintile representing very high-risk (bottom-tier) hospitals.

Patient Risk Stratification

To evaluate the impact of hospital performance across different patient risk profiles, we stratified patients into risk categories for operative mortality using established STS risk metrics.8 We then compared FTR rates between top-tier and bottom-tier hospitals within each patient risk group using generalized estimating equations models.

Statistical Analysis

We compared baseline characteristics and unadjusted outcomes between patients treated across all 5 quintiles of hospitals using appropriate statistical tests (eg, chi-square for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank-sum for continuous variables). To assess the association between hospital performance tier and FTR, we used generalized estimating equation models to account for clustering of patients within hospitals. We calculated adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs for FTR in bottom-tier hospitals compared with top-tier hospitals. This analysis was performed for FTR after any complication, major complications, and esophagectomy-specific complications. Data are shown as OR/hazard ratio and 95% CI. StataSE v16.1 (StataCorp LLC) was used for statistical analyses.

Institutional Assurances

This research was determined to be exempt research with a waiver of informed consent from Advarra Institutional Review Board (Mod01760092, Version 1.1, Approval date July 17, 2023).

Results

A total of 28,626 patients from 318 hospitals were included in the analysis. Hospitals were grouped by SMRs into quintiles (very low risk N = 64, low risk N = 64, medium risk N = 63, high risk N = 64, very high risk N = 63). Hospitals in the top tier performed significantly more procedures than those in the bottom tier (10,431 top tier vs 3527 bottom tier, P < .001). Unadjusted hospital mortality was significantly lower in the top tier compared with the bottom tier (1.2% vs 6.6%, P < .001). Low-tier hospitals performed more operations on patients with high patient risk profiles compared with high-tier hospitals (31.5% vs 40.4%, P < .001). The patient gender distribution was the same across all quintiles (P = .14). High-tier hospitals performed esophagectomies on patients with higher BMI than low-tier hospitals (27.1 vs 26.7, P < .001). Lower tier hospitals performed operations on patients with higher incidence of hypertension, congestive heart failure, coronary artery disease, and diabetes when compared with high-tier hospitals (50.6% vs 44.6%; 3.3% vs 1.4%; 16.0% vs 13.1%; 19.2% vs 16.4%, P < .001 for all). There is an increased number of patients who are current smokers receiving esophagectomy compared with high-tier hospitals (16.7% vs 11.8%, P < .001) (Table 1). Median annual esophagectomy volume at top-tier hospitals was 15.2 cases/year (interquartile range, 10.1-22.3) compared with 4.8 cases/year (interquartile range, 2.9-7.6) at bottom-tier hospitals.

When stratifying by hospital performance tier, hospitals with higher SMRs exhibited progressively higher FTR rates, with a clear stepwise increase across quintiles. The overall rate of complications was similar across hospital tiers, with any complication occurring in 19.5% of patients at very low-risk hospitals and increasing slightly to 27.3% at very high-risk hospitals. Major complication rates followed a similar pattern, increasing from 17.5% at very low-risk hospitals to 29.2% at very high-risk hospitals (Figure 2).

There was substantial variation in FTR rates across hospital tiers. Among patients who experienced any complication, FTR was lowest in very low-risk hospitals at 4.7%, increasing to 12.6% in very high-risk hospitals. The disparity was even more pronounced among patients experiencing major complications, with an FTR rate of 4.7% in very low-risk hospitals compared with 19.0% in very high-risk hospitals. Esophagectomy-specific complications, including anastomotic leaks, conduit necrosis, and need for postoperative dilation, also showed consistent trends, with FTR rates rising from 2.5% in very low-risk hospitals to 15.5% in very high-risk hospitals (Figure 2). These findings demonstrate that although complication rates are relatively similar across hospitals, the ability to rescue patients from complications varies significantly. The progressively increasing FTR rates across hospital tiers highlight the critical impact of hospital performance on postcomplication survival, suggesting that system-level factors such as perioperative care protocols, early complication recognition, and access to rescue interventions may be key determinants of patient outcomes after esophagectomy.

Hospitals demonstrated a wide range of SMRs, highlighting significant variability in postesophagectomy outcomes across institutions. The caterpillar plot shows a continuous gradient in hospital performance, with SMRs ranging from well below 1.0 in the highest performing hospitals to greater than 2.5 in the lowest performing hospitals. The majority of hospitals clustered around the reference line (SMR = 1.0), but a subset of hospitals at the higher end of the spectrum exhibited significantly worse than expected mortality rates, as indicated by their SMRs exceeding the upper CIs of the distribution (Figure 3). The increasing SMR trend across hospitals suggests that a proportion of institutions consistently perform below national benchmarks, with higher than expected mortality rates after esophagectomy. These findings reinforce the notion that hospital-level factors, including perioperative care quality, early recognition and management of complications, and resource availability, play a crucial role in determining patient outcomes after esophagectomy.

The distribution of patient mortality risk, adjusted for patient-specific factors, varied across hospital SMR quintiles. Most patients, regardless of hospital SMR category, had relatively low predicted mortality risks, with a right-skewed distribution indicating that high-risk patients were less frequent overall. Discussion

Esophagectomy remains a high-risk surgical procedure with significant postoperative morbidity and mortality. Understanding factors contributing to FTR is crucial for improving patient outcomes.9,10 In this study, we observed that hospitals with higher SMRs had progressively higher FTR rates, despite similar overall complication rates across hospital tiers. Notably, patients experiencing major complications or esophagectomy-specific complications in higher SMR hospitals had significantly worse survival outcomes compared with those in lower SMR hospitals.

The concept of FTR emphasizes the importance of a hospital's ability to effectively manage complications once they occur.11 Prior research indicates that differences in mortality after inpatient surgery are largely related to death after a complication, highlighting the critical role of timely recognition and management of postoperative complications. Ghaferi and colleagues12 first described FTR as a key driver of surgical mortality, showing that hospitals with lower mortality rates did not necessarily have fewer complications but were more effective in rescuing patients from life-threatening events postoperatively. Subsequent research has reinforced this concept, identifying that hospitals with robust perioperative monitoring systems, early warning protocols, and multidisciplinary critical care teams are more successful in mitigating the consequences of major complications.13,14 Moreover, such centers typically have dedicated thoracic intensive care units, facilitating prompt recognition and intervention. Access to interventional radiology, therapeutic endoscopy, and operating room availability around-the-clock further supports timely rescue.13,14 Institutional adherence to standardized postoperative pathways, including routine leak surveillance protocols, may also play a role. Together, these system-level capabilities contribute to improved outcomes and should be the focus of dissemination to lower-performing centers. Our findings reinforce this, showing that higher-SMR hospitals have higher FTR rates, suggesting that mortality outcomes after esophagectomy are not solely dictated by patient-related risk factors but are also heavily influenced by hospital-level processes that enable early complication detection and timely intervention.

Multiple studies have demonstrated a significant association between hospital surgical volume and postoperative mortality in esophageal cancer surgeries. High-volume centers often report lower mortality rates, a phenomenon likely due to more standardized care protocols and the presence of experienced multidisciplinary teams. A meta-analysis examined the relationship between hospital volume and postoperative mortality in esophageal cancer surgeries revealed that high-volume providers had a significantly lower postoperative mortality rate.15 Likewise, a previous study investigated the impact of hospital volume on long-term survival after esophageal cancer surgery. Although the study found no significant difference in long-term survival between high-volume and low-volume hospitals, it did note that high-volume hospitals had lower short-term postoperative mortality rates.16

Farjah17 analyzed the role of FTR in thoracic surgery and found that complication rates remained relatively consistent across hospitals, but institutions with lower mortality rates exhibited superior rescue capabilities. This suggests that hospital infrastructure, care escalation protocols, and perioperative monitoring systems play a more crucial role in determining patient survival after complications than merely preventing complications themselves. Likewise, another study by Farjah and colleagues18 examined pulmonary resection outcomes and found a 4-fold variation in mortality rates across hospitals, despite only a modest difference in complication rates. Notably, FTR rates varied significantly (6.8% vs 1.7%, P < .001), reinforcing the idea that hospitals with better postoperative surveillance and early intervention strategies can dramatically improve survival in patients experiencing complications. These studies support our findings that hospitals with higher SMRs have disproportionately higher FTR rates, highlighting the need for quality improvement measures that prioritize early detection and rapid management of postoperative complications to enhance rescue. The findings suggest that centralization of esophageal cancer surgeries to high-volume centers may improve patient outcomes.19,20 Although centralization may reduce FTR through concentrated expertise, it must be balanced against potential negative effects on access to care, particularly in underserved or rural communities. Travel burdens, delays in diagnosis or surgery, and financial toxicity could widen existing disparities.

Patient-related factors also play a role in FTR. Studies have identified advanced age, frailty, and comorbidities as predictors of increased postoperative mortality.21,22 Our study observed that hospitals in higher SMR quintiles treated a higher concentration of patients with elevated adjusted mortality risks. However, the persistent disparities in FTR rates, even after adjusting for these patient-level risk factors, underscore the significant impact of hospital-level factors on patient outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective nature and reliance on registry data from the STS-GTSD may introduce selection bias and coding inaccuracies. Complications and mortality events are subject to variability in reporting practices across institutions, which could influence our estimates of FTR rates. Second, although we adjusted for known patient-level risk factors (eg, comorbidities, BMI, smoking status), there remains the potential for unmeasured confounding variables that could impact FTR outcomes, such as hospital staffing ratios, intensivist availability, or postdischarge care quality. Third, individual surgeon volume and experience, which have been independently linked to better surgical outcomes,21 were not accounted for in our analysis, potentially limiting our ability to isolate the effects of hospital-level factors on FTR rates. Because of limitations in the STS-GTSD dataset, we could not assess institutional characteristics such as geographic location (urban vs rural), catchment area, or socioeconomic markers, which may contribute to the observed referral patterns and risk distribution. The STS-PUF dataset is deidentified and does not include variables such as total institutional surgical volume, number of surgeons, or intensive care unit structure. These characteristics could be meaningful contributors to variation in FTR. Surgeon specialty (general thoracic vs mixed practice) is not captured in the STS-GTSD, and we could not evaluate how this influenced FTR rates. Our analysis is limited to patients who underwent esophagectomy. We cannot account for case selection bias or assess whether high-tier hospitals are more risk averse.

Finally, the definition of FTR in this study was based on in-hospital mortality after a complication, and we were unable to capture long-term outcomes or deaths occurring after discharge, which may underestimate the true impact of hospital performance on patient survival. We acknowledge the potential overlap between hospital SMR and FTR, because both involve mortality events. However, SMR captures all-cause operative mortality, whereas FTR isolates failure to survive after a complication, making it a distinct and more actionable metric. Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable insight into institutional disparities in FTR rates and highlight opportunities for quality improvement initiatives to enhance postoperative care in patients undergoing esophagectomy. Future work should incorporate structure and process measures, such as intensive care unit staffing ratios or time-to-intervention protocols, to better define hospital rescue capacity. Although hospitals cannot directly benchmark SMR or FTR using the STS-GTSD, internal audit of complication-related deaths, paired with tracking rescue process metrics (eg, time to intervention), may provide actionable insight.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that FTR rates after esophagectomy vary significantly across hospitals, with higher SMR hospitals exhibiting disproportionately worse rescue outcomes despite similar complication rates. These findings highlight the critical role of hospital-level factors in determining postoperative survival. Moving forward, quality improvement initiatives and centralization of high-risk surgeries to experienced centers may help reduce FTR rates and improve outcomes for patients undergoing esophagectomy.
