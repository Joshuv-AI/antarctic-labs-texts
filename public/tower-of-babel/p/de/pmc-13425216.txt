Clinical outcomes of lentiviral gene therapy for SCD
Authors: Claire Booth, David Jacobsohn
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Prueksapraopong et al1 report results from a single site study of lentiviral gene therapy for sickle cell disease and outline a practical, responsive approach to protocol development with the use of real time learnings to refine study design to maximize patient benefit. Despite not advancing further, such studies provide important learning opportunities and contribute critical knowledge on vector performance and protocol improvement, not just for hemoglobinopathies but also for transfer to the wider field. Early studies, like this, played a key role in accelerating progress to support the development of effective advanced therapies for sickle cell disease that are now licensed across several territories.

The first trial patient was treated in 2015 using a lentiviral vector containing the antisickling βAS3-globin gene under control of the human β-globin promoter, together with elements from the intrinsic locus control region to improve transgene expression. Additional FB insulator elements were included to reduce the risk for insertional mutagenesis and mitigate position effects, a serious complication previously seen in integrating vector trials, particularly for inherited immunodeficiencies. Bone marrow was initially the starting material for drug product manufacture and reduced intensity busulfan conditioning was used as conditioning. The first patient received a low cell dose of 2 × 106 CD34+ per kilogram with a vector copy number of 0.2. Subsequently, very low gene marking was seen in peripheral blood in the first patient with undetectable HbAS3 protein expression, leading to no clinical benefit.

The team then redesigned the vector using the optimized GLOBE backbone, incorporated transduction enhancers, and switched to plerixafor-mobilized peripheral blood stem cells as the starting material. To improve patients’ condition before treatment, they also introduced a transfusion protocol to reduce hemoglobin S (HbS), informed by experience from bluebird bio studies. Together with raising the minimum cell dose threshold to 3 × 106 CD34+ per kilogram, these changes led to improved clinical outcomes in the next 3 patients, including a reduction in the frequency of vaso-occlusive crises and transfusion independence in 2 patients.

It has now been >2 years since the US Food and Drug Administration–approval of exagamglogene autotemcel and lovotibeglogene autotemcel, the 2 gene therapy products for sickle cell disease. The former uses electroporation, followed by CRISPR/Cas9 to target the BCL11A gene and decrease its expression, which leads to increased hemoglobin F levels, whereas the latter uses a lentiviral vector that enables the production of a functional hemoglobin. Both products lead to resolution of vaso-occlusive events in >95% of patients with improvements in quality of life.2, 3, 4 These products are amazing additions to the armamentarium of curative therapies for sickle cell disease, which, up to now, comprised only allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation that was accompanied by unique complications, such as graft-versus-host disease and a serious risk of infections.5,6 There are even more gene therapies on the horizon, such as ristoglogene autogetemcel,7 which increase our options and make use of even more advanced technologies.

Significant issues remain, however, and these hinder increased uptake of these therapies. Given the massive 1-time expense, complex single case agreements need to be obtained from the insurer, which sometimes add a few months of delay. Peripheral blood stem cell collections from patients with sickle cell disease are complex in that filgrastim cannot be used and we rely on plerixafor, leading to less CD34+ cells per collection when compared with patients with β-thalassemia who undergo similar collections (in which filgrastim is given). Sometimes there is significant loss of cells during the manufacturing process. These issues translate into frequently needing >1 collection for each patient. Lastly, the side effects of high-dose busulfan are not trivial, including significant mucositis and tangible rates of sinusoidal obstruction syndrome in the short term and infertility in the long term. All these issues impact access because they require patients to be treated in hospitals that offer comprehensive treatment, including apheresis, transplant, sickle cell care, sophisticated pain treatments, and mental health support (reviewed in Butt et al8).

Therefore, novel curative therapies for sickle cell disease continue to be desperately needed. Although we have learned a great deal about optimizing ex vivo therapies through shared experience, there remains significant scope for improvement, particularly when it comes to making these treatments more accessible. Reducing manufacturing costs, including reducing the number of cell collections required, could make a real difference in improving accessibility, especially in low- and middle-income settings where the need is greatest. Of course, it would be in vivo therapies that do not require stem cell collection or manufacturing that would truly advance care and potentially make a cure available to every patient. Although there are in vivo therapies already approved for neuromuscular disorders (eg, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, spinal muscular atrophy) and hemophilia, the ability to gene-edit stem cells in the body without off-target effects would be revolutionary. Let us get there for our sickle cell warriors!
