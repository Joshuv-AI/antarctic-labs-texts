MegaEye: Applying multiple machine learning approaches to identify oral compounds with ocular bioactivity
Authors: Fabio Urbina, Scott H. Greenwald, Patricia A. Vignaux, Thomas R. Lane, Joshua S. Harris, Mayssa Attar, Keith Luhrs, Sean Ekins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The eye is a complex organ with the critical role of mediating the optical and initial signal processing steps of vision. As such, the eye has multiple physiological and dynamic barriers to protect ocular tissues and compartments. Oral administration of pharmacological agents to treat ocular diseases have often failed to demonstrate efficacy in clinical trials. The ability of a molecule to reach a specific target in the eye (e.g. cells in the anterior versus posterior segment) is largely determined by whether its physicochemical properties permit passage across the various ocular barriers (e.g. cornea, sclera, tear dilution, blood-retinal barrier, lymphatic outflow) that are relevant to the route of administration and the target location. The use of machine learning to predict ocular bioactivity of molecules is underexplored. We now describe the curation of several datasets, generated by a wide array of computational approaches, that are used to identify drugs predicted to reach the eye following oral delivery. These datasets included simple molecular properties (e.g. molecular weight), using the blood-brain barrier MPO score, and machine learning models as a proxy for the blood-retinal barrier using transporter and other relevant literature datasets. FDA approved drugs with reported ocular activity were used to validate the models’ ability to identify additional molecules not in the models. Finally, we used a large language model, to rank over 400,000 natural compounds by potential activity in the eye. In summary, we illustrate machine learning model applications that can be expanded for ocular applications in future to repurpose molecules.

Introduction

Disease processes affecting the eye are etiologically and mechanistically diverse [1]. Likewise, interventions must be tailored to the underlying cause, whether it be genetic, aging, infectious, traumatic, an environmental factor (e.g. seasonal allergies), or a combination thereof, as well as to the physical location of the target structure within the eye [2]. Eye diseases are commonly treated with locally delivered pharmaceuticals that are noninvasively delivered via topical application. As examples, depending on the type, keratitis can be resolved using antibacterial, antiviral, or antifungal eye drops [3]; glaucoma can be managed in some patients by lowering intraocular pressure with eye drops or, in acute cases, orally administered carbonic anhydrase inhibitors [4]; and uveitis can be managed with topically or orally delivered corticosteroids [5,6]. In contrast, some medications require an in office or surgical procedure to inject the pharmacological agent to a location in the eye proximal to the target (e.g. intravitreal injection of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor drugs, subretinal delivery of retinal gene therapies) [7–9]. Frequent delivery by this route has a high burden and productivity loss [10]. It can be inferred that there are eye conditions which remain untreatable or require a medical procedure either because an effective drug has not yet been identified or because noninvasive administration of an otherwise effective drug results in insufficient bioavailability at the target. Although more invasive intraocular treatments such as intravitreal injections have become common clinical practice, the chronic nature of many retinal diseases creates a need for less-invasive approaches to improve the risk/benefit ratio and to reduce patient and caregiver burden. Moreover, if therapeutic intervention is intended earlier in the disease process prior to loss of vision then less invasive therapeutics are necessary since the benefit:risk of regular intraocular injections may not justify the treatment burden.

Addressing disorders at the ocular surface, an externally accessible set of tissues, is relatively straightforward. For example, bacterial conjunctivitis can be resolved pharmacologically with topical antibiotics [11]. However, predicting the likelihood that a topical compound can reach an intraocular target, such as the retina, is complicated by dynamic and static barriers. Blinking and tear dilution can greatly reduce the effective dose of topically delivered drugs, and permeation into the eye may be impeded by the tear film, cornea, sclera, the blood-aqueous barrier (BAB), and clearance via conjunctival blood flow and lymphatics [2]. The ability of molecules to diffuse through the hydrophobic corneal epithelium to reach the aqueous humor is limited to 1 – 5 % for lipophilic molecules and <0.5 % for hydrophilic molecules [12,13]. Topical delivery also has the issue of poor medical adherence [14]. Aqueous vehicles often used for eye drops require additives and preservatives that can cause ocular irritation [15], and hydrophilic emulsions that enhance penetration of the corneal stroma may also temporarily cause blurred vision [16]. Topical administration is not typically considered effective for posterior segment targets due to low drug penetration [17]. To reach the retina, a therapeutic would need to overcome ocular surface drainage and tear film dynamics, pass through the cornea, avoid expulsion from the eye by the ocular outflow pathway in the anterior chamber, circumvent the lens, diffuse the long distance though the vitreous humor, and penetrate the basement membrane at the vitreoretinal interface (internal limiting membrane) [2,18] An alternative route of entry to the ocular posterior is to diffuse initially through the conjunctiva, sclera, and choroid, avoid sequestration through melanin binding or clearance by blood flow or lymphatic drainage, to cross Bruch’s membrane, and the retinal pigmented epithelium, a component of the blood retinal barrier, before entering the retina.

Oral administration of pharmacological agents to treat ocular diseases (such as retinal disease [19]) is an active area but has had limited success in obtaining efficacy in clinical trials [20–24]. Oral dosing of medication for ophthalmic indications, as an alternative to topical administration, faces the usual absorption and metabolism inherent to oral dosing plus blockage by the blood-retinal barrier (BRB), BAB, and choroid, as well as rapid clearance by the choroidal blood flow [2]. The BRB, comprised of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) at the outer retina and endothelial cells surrounding retinal blood vessels at the inner retina, challenges access of orally delivered drugs to the retina because it serves as a filter that restricts which blood-borne molecules are carried into the eye from the systemic circulation [25]. Similarly, the BAB selectively permits diffusion or transport of bloodborne molecules and across the ciliary process nonpigmented epithelium, inner wall endothelium of Schlemm’s canal, and endothelium cells of the iris vasculature to protect the anterior segment of the eye [26]. Together, the BRB and BAB may lead to drug bioavailability of <2 % [2]. The choroid is a dynamic barrier to drug delivery owing to the high blood flow rate of the choriocapillaris, and the structure of this vascular bed also makes it a static barrier; both mechanisms especially prevent entry of hydrophilic molecules [17]. Computational modeling suggests that the majority of drug that reaches the choroid is removed by choroidal blood flow [27].

Absorption, metabolism, excretion, and toxicology (ADME/Tox) properties, as well as bioactivity data across disease targets, are available for millions of compounds through publicly available repositories, like PubChem and ChEMBL which can be used as a starting point for machine learning [28]. Development and validation of a machine learning algorithm, based on complementary machine learning techniques (e.g. Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, and deep learning), that rapidly predicts and ranks the ocular bioavailability of compounds across existing datasets using molecule descriptors or properties could help shortlist therapeutically viable drug candidates. Such an algorithm may be able to deliver molecules ready for empirical laboratory testing. This approach has the potential to greatly expand the therapeutic landscape in ophthalmology and largely eliminate the need for time-intensive high throughput screening [28]. Computational approaches that use artificial intelligence have been reviewed in areas such as neurological disease drug discovery [29] but there are few such assessments for ocular diseases [30]. Recent ocular publications have focused on quantitative structure property relationships (QSPR) [31] computational simulations [32], AI endophenotypes [33], combinations of approaches for drug discovery [34] machine learning for toxicity prediction [35] and drug transport [36].

We now describe using a wide array of computational approaches and datasets to initially score a library of US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved drugs to discover which ones can pass the cornea and BRB. These scoring strategies included models for the blood brain barrier (BBB, as a surrogate for the BRB), simple molecule property cutoffs, and organic cation transporters (OCT1, OCT2, OCT3) to predict substrates. As a proxy for the interaction of candidate molecules with the BRB, we used data on toxicity in a human RPE cell line (ARPE-19 cells) challenged by exposure to a set of compounds. We also used published data from the parallel artificial membrane permeability assay (PAMPA) which has previously been used to predict whether a compound can cross the cornea [37] and may be another surrogate for crossing biological membranes. Finally, we explored the development of MegaEye, a large language model comprising the earlier described models, that was used to predict and rank more than 400,000 natural compounds and other molecules. Such combinations of models and large language modeling may overcome the reliance on a single model and could compliment other predictive approaches considering ocular pharmacokinetics and adverse effects [38], predictive models for the BRB [39,40] or disease models [41].

Experimental section

Datasets and curation

Training datasets were downloaded from ChEMBL 34 as described [42]. Half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) or other data were downloaded for the ChEMBL targets. These data were rigorously curated using our in-house automated curation pipeline e-Clean [43–46]. The following steps were performed to standardize the Simplified Molecule-input Line-entry System (SMILES): standardize isotopes, remove stereoisomer information, disconnect metals, remove common salts and solvents, identify parent molecule, discard molecules with undesirable elements (inorganic elements), neutralize charges and canonicalize SMILES. After standardizing SMILES, samples were grouped together by molecular structure and measurement values are converted to common units. Any samples with incomplete data were discarded first.

For classification models, the measurements on the same molecule are combined to a single binary value by the following steps: 1) discard exact duplicates, 2) binarize values using a binarization threshold (e.g. if all values <1 μM are assigned a binarized value of 1, all others are assigned 0), 3) if binarization was ambiguous (≤60 % agreement), the sample was discarded. This process allows data with different qualifiers (“<”, “>”, “=”) to be utilized and condensed to a single classification for each molecule.

For regression model training data, any samples with qualifiers other than “=” are discarded. Measurement values are converted to common units (negative log-molar). Measurements on the same molecule are combined to one value by the following steps: 1) discard exact duplicates (e.g. the same data point reported ≥2 times), 2) discard outliers (z-score >1.5), 3) discard molecule if remaining measurements are inconsistent (coefficient of variation >2), 4) take the arithmetic mean of remaining measurement values (since units are on the log scale, this is equivalent to the geometric mean of the values in nM).

Machine learning software

We generated multiple classification and regression models as have been described in detail previously in our proprietary Assay Central® software [47]. Models were generated with extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFP6), which are well-known for their application to drug targets as well as ADME/Tox models [48]. The algorithms used included Bernoulli naïve Bayes, Linear Logistic Regression, AdaBoost Decision Tree, Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, Deep Neural Networks and XGBoost. Assay Central® provides a probability-like score and an applicability score for individual chemicals. Due to the nature of the prediction scores, typically a threshold of 0.5 is used to determine if a compound is classified as active, but higher scores suggest a higher likelihood of activity. Applicability scores are based on the reliability-density neighborhood (RDN) method which considers the model overlap, individual bias and precision of the overlapping fingerprints used as measures for the representation of the predicted molecule in the training data [49]. There is not a defined threshold for an acceptable applicability score but higher is generally better.

Machine learning model validation was performed using a nested 5-fold cross validation. Nested 5-fold cross validation initially selects a random, stratified 20 % hold-out set that is removed from the training set prior to model building. The model is then built with the other 80 % of the training data and the hyperparameters (if applicable) are optimized using a grid search using 5-fold dataset splits (20 % validation sets). This optimized model is then used to predict the initial 20 % hold out set and then repeated until all compounds have been in a hold-out set (total 20 models trained). The final nested 5-fold cross validation scores are an average of each of the hold-out set metrics. Models were again built using ECFP6 descriptors only and metrics generated as described previously.

Model building and analysis

BBB models

We used Pfizer’s multiparameter optimization score (MPO) score that is used to predict BBB activity and that uses six distinct chemical properties calculated with ChemAxon (LogP, MW, LogD, TPSA, pKa, and HBD) as described previously [50]. The settings on this score can be set to increase stringency. We also used our own BBB machine learning model for making predictions as has also been described previously [50].

Molecule properties

We investigated molecules less than 150 Da as an unbiased screening method for finding small molecules which have a high chance of crossing the BRB.

Transporter models

As organic cation transporters have been suggested to be important in the eye [51,52], they may also represent a set of physicochemical properties desirable for entry. Curation for transporter substrate data proceeded in three steps. First, all bioactivity data for OCT1 (CHEMBL5685), OCT2 (CHEMBL1743122), OCT3 (CHEMBL2073684) were downloaded from ChEMBL. Substrate data were separated from inhibition data, removing entries labeled IC50, Ki, Inh, or Inhibition and retaining those labeled Clint, Drug Transport, Drug Uptake, FC, Km, and Vmax. Entries labeled Activity or Ratio were removed. Non-human transporter data were also removed. Data from PubChem Bioassays and BindingDB were also searched for substrate data that were absent from ChEMBL. Second, we proceeded with a literature search for new substrate data in the UNC Library Search Catalog and PubMed Central. The majority of the new data came from four publications for OCT1 [53–55], OCT2 [54–56] or OCT3 [54,55]. The “uptake ratio” of a substrate for a transporter (e.g. OCT1) was calculated by measuring the amount of substrate accumulated in a cell line overexpressing OCT1 versus passive uptake of that substrate by the identical cell line not overexpressing OCT1. Accumulation of substrate was measured by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. Third, as one of the articles [57] specifically mentioned DrugBank as a source of substrate data, we accessed this repository for each transporter and copied the lists of known substrates. The datasets for each transporter are shown in Table 1.

Substrate activity at human OCT1, 2, 3 was assessed as uptake ratio. We used the Assay Central software to build 8 classification models for each dataset, which we evaluated with nested 5-fold cross validation. We selected the highest performing models from the set to predict the FDA approved drug library to identify ‘eye-active’ drugs. For the OCT1 substrate models, we then set a threshold of ≧1.5 and ≧3.0 uptake ratio of drug over control to define our positive class for an OCT substrate (Table 1).

Additional surrogate models for uptake in the eye

Leveraging data available in ChEMBL, we built models based on toxicity experiments performed in ARPE-19 cells challenged with exposure to a set of compounds [58]. ARPE-19 cells serve as a model for RPE cells which are a component of the BRB and are implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple retinal diseases, including age-related macular degeneration [25,59]. We set an IC50 threshold of 10 μM for ARPE-19 toxicity and built classification models for the 60 drugs. Finally, we used the PAMPA dataset [60] with 189 drugs that has been previously used to create an in silico model for predicting corneal permeability [37]. PAMPA is also widely used as a surrogate for passive membrane permeability (e.g. skin, intestine, BBB) [61]. We built machine learning models for these datasets and compared ECFP6 and ChemAxon descriptors. These models were then used to predict likely ‘eye-active’ FDA drugs.

Mining PubMed

In addition, we have used simple search terms in PubMed (e.g. [drug name] and human eye and clinical trial; [drug name] and human eye and toxicity; [drug name] and human eye and clinical and oral) to identify molecules that when used orally had activity or toxicity in the human eye.

FDA approved drugs

FDA-approved compounds were compiled from the FDA’s “Purple Book” [62] and the list of “novel drug approvals” up to 2023 [63]. SMILES for each compound (by name) were found using a batch search from the CompTox dashboard [64]. SMILES not found with this approach were identified using other public databases (ChEMBL or PubChem). Compounds were then standardized using the same protocol to clean the training sets for model building (canonicalizing SMILES, removing salts, removing duplicates, etc.).

COCONUT database screen

Besides searching the FDA approved drugs dataset with our models we have also used the COCONUT database, a large database of over 400,000 natural products and related molecules [65]. We first curated the database to reduce the number of compounds with structural errors. Our cleanup process included using eClean, our proprietary molecular error detection, correction, and standardization software. Briefly, eClean verifies that the molecular structure is feasible, flagging molecules which are not realistic. eClean then attempts to fix flagged molecules through procedures such as charge neutralization and the removal of molecules which cannot be corrected. Finally, all molecules are standardized for modeling, having salts and metals removed.

Our initial screen with the different approaches suggested that many fragments were able to pass most filters, in particular the MPO, BBB, and MW filters. Further, many natural products in the initial screen were analogs of each other, giving less chemical diversity than desired. t-SNE plots

t-SNE plots embed data into a lower-dimensional space [66]. 1024 ECFP6 fingerprints were generated for all compounds. The ECFP6 fingerprints were then embedded into a 2-dimensional vector using t-SNE. All t-SNE values were generated using the scikit-learn library in python with default hyperparameters (n_components =2, perplexity = 30, early exaggeration = 12.0, learning rate = 200, n_iter = 1000).

Molecular shape analysis

Molecules were analyzed using a Principal Moment of Inertia (PMI) plot [67]. A PMI plot is generated by first computing the three principal moments of inertia from the 3D shapes of the molecules and normalizing by the largest value for each molecule. The two smaller principal moments of inertia are then plotted against each other. Due to the mathematical relationship between principal moments of inertia, the values on a PMI plot always fall within a triangular region on the plot; the corners of the triangle correspond to mass distributed along a line, within a flat disc, or within a sphere (rod-shaped, flat, or round molecules). Points between the corners of the PMI plot represent shapes interpolated across these three extremes (e.g. a point between the “rod” and “flat” corners represents a long, wide molecular structure).

Developing a rule for eye-active molecules

We set out to find the optimal chemical property or feature space similar to Lipinski’s Rule of 5 [68] for orally acting eye-active drugs. Rules like this have been used to guide the drug discovery process previously. We generated a set of 11 molecular descriptors (logP, logD, pKa, HBD, TPSA, MW, sp3, number of aromatic rings, number of rotational bonds, HBA, and number of aliphatic rings) using ChemAxon software and explored two approaches to find heuristic rulesets for these descriptors. We used a Fast Interpretable Greedy-Tree Sums (FIGS) [69] model to generate a predictive model trained to identify FDA approved oral drugs that are active in the eye. FIGS is a model which learns an interpretable decision tree using the input features while retaining the predictive power of typical classification and regression trees.

Large language model

Recently, state-of-the-art large language models (LLMs) have dominated the field of Machine Learning, showing great promise in language generation tasks. In practice, a base language model is often trained on a large corpus of data to learn the structure of language. These base models are then fine-tuned downstream on individual tasks such as question answering or code-completion to great effect, needing only a few training epochs on small data to achieve excellent results. As we can represent molecules as a language using SMILES, these model types offered an attractive option for model-building. We utilized a Bidirectional Autoregressive Transformer model (MolBART) [70] pretrained on millions of SMILES strings which we have used previously [71]. MolBART can learn to predict the next token in the SMILES sequence and thereby learn the language of assembling molecular structures. We fine-tuned the pre-trained MolBART model on all datasets to simultaneously predict activity for OCT1/2/3, toxicity in ARPE-19 cells, and corneal PAMPA results. We dubbed this model “MegaEye”. We then performed a stratified split on each dataset 70/15/15, train/validation/test, respectively. We then trained the model for 75 epochs.

Results

Results of in silico screens

Filter FDA approved drugs using the Pfizer BBB rule

The MPO desirability score predicts the likelihood of a compound penetrating the BBB within a range of 0 – 6, and a score of ≥4 is considered desirable. For greater stringency, we selected compounds which attained the maximal possible score of 6. Examples of compounds predicted to cross the BBB with this approach are shown, and we were able to identify publications in PubMed for some of the drugs with human eye activity data (Table S1). Using the MPO score resulted in a precision of ~0.19, discovering 7 FDA drugs with activity in the eye (Table 2).

Filter FDA approved drugs with BBB machine learning models

We also used our previously published BBB machine learning model [50] as a surrogate to predict FDA approved drugs which cross the BRB and to identify additional publications in PubMed for drugs with human ‘eye activity’ data (Table 2, Table S2).

Machine learning models for OCT1, 2, 3 substrates

We curated data on OCT1, OCT2, and OCT3, as these transporters are expressed in the human eye [72] and may be used to predict which FDA approved drugs act as substrates and are able to cross the BRB. The distribution of several molecular descriptors showed that these broadly displayed similar distributions for numbers of hydrogen bond acceptors, numbers of hydrogen bond donors, logP, molecular weight and polar surface area (PSA, Figures S1–3). Our best performing models all used a threshold of ≧3.0, and thus we used them to predict which FDA drugs are OCT substrates and have eye activity. For OCT3, the XGB and Laplace Modified Bayes models were tied for best, so both were used together for predicting substrates for this transporter (Table S3). The models were used to search FDA drugs and identify publications in PubMed for drugs with human eye activity data (Table S4). Having a ‘hit rate’ of over 30 %, these models were generally excellent for identifying molecules with eye activity (Table 2).

Filter with other constraints such as MW ≦ 150 Da

There were several hundred molecules with MW <350 Da so we selected MW ≦150 Da as a stringent size threshold for the molecules in the FDA approved drugs dataset (Table S5). Many of the molecules identified also were shown to have bioactivity in the eye based on one or more papers identified in PubMed (Table 2).

Searching PubMed for data on ocular toxicity or ocular efficacy for compounds

We searched PubMed for evidence of ocular toxicity or efficacy through oral dosing. Using a combination of machine learning models and rules, as described above, alongside data mining in PubMed we discovered a total of 115 FDA approved oral drugs which had various effects in the human eye (Table S6).

ARPE-19 toxicity dataset and modeling

In addition to the OCT transporters, we built a model for eye toxicity as a proxy for predicting BRB interaction. We searched ChEMBL and identified data for 60 molecules with ARPE-19 cells and the molecular properties of this dataset was skewed towards larger and more hydrophobic molecules based on the logP (Figure S4). The model statistics indicated that the Random Forest model performed the best in terms of 5-fold cross validation (Table S7). This model was therefore selected to predict the FDA approved library and to identify further drug molecules with ocular activity based on published papers (Table S8).

Corneal PAMPA dataset and models

We identified a corneal PAMPA [60] data set with 189 drugs that has been previously been used to create an in silico model for predicting corneal permeability [37]. The molecular properties of this dataset are more closely aligned to those of the OCTs with a lower logP and PSA (Figure S5). In a preliminary assessment, classification models had better statistics over regression models, and the former are shown (Table S9). We discovered that while our traditional ECFP6 descriptors did not perform well (data not shown), a set of ChemAxon-derived physicochemical properties were used and produced models with reasonable predictive power. This model was used to identify additional compounds in the FDA drug set (Table S10), and it was also used to suggest additional compounds (Table 2).

Demonstration of an integrated approach to identify drugs with activity in the eye.

Using this combined approach for predictions, we extracted all FDA approved drugs which passed the combined model and physicochemical filter criteria. Of the 1960 compounds in the FDA library, 518 were predicted and investigated by the sets of criteria outlined (Table S6 summarizes the findings). Of the 101 compounds predicted to have some therapeutic activity in the eye by the combined OCT models, 35 were discovered to have activity in the eye. The OCT models individually had precision ranging from 0.36 (~1/3 correct predictions) to 0.49 (~1 in 2 drugs predicted correct), giving a high confidence in the model predictive abilities. ARPE-19 by comparison had a lower precision (0.26), as expected from a toxicity model used as a proxy, and the PAMPA model had the lowest precision (0.14), which may be likely due to these smaller dataset or the fact that this artificial system is not a good model of the cornea However, the ARPE-19 and PAMPA models independently predicted ‘eye active’ FDA drugs that were not captured by any other model, respectively indicating that other approaches to identify molecules that likely cross the BRB and cornea have utility.

Identification of additional FDA approved drugs with eye data in PubMed.

By combining all data from the model searches, we created an updated ensemble of models/ algorithms that could be used to search for additional FDA approved drugs. Data for compounds that the models predicted correctly were ultimately incorporated back into the training set for each model. As lack of evidence of activity is not evidence of an inability to cross the cornea or BRB, no negative data were introduced into the model, only the positively identified drugs. With this additional round of model building, the statistics for all 3 OCT models improved. The updated ensemble approach identified 18 additional molecules that are used topically, intravenous, intravitreal, etc., (based on literature information) as well as 4 further molecules that were delivered orally and had ocular activity (Table S11).

Molecular properties of compounds that reach the eye and t-SNE analysis.

To evaluate the chemical similarity of the findings, we performed t-SNE cluster analysis. The lower dimensional space generated by t-SNE [66] preserves distance in higher dimensions: molecules that are closer together in t-SNE space have chemical structure more similar than to those further away. These t-SNE plots show how well the oral drugs that are active in the eye are distributed, as they cover most of the drug space (Fig. 1) and overlap all drugs active in the eye (Figs. 2 and 3). Discriminating molecules based on properties alone may be difficult (Figs. 4 and 5), and it appears oral drugs with specific mechanisms cluster together in some cases (Fig. 6).

COCONUT screens.

After using the filtering method as described above, a total of 396 compounds were predicted to be eye-active and these resulting molecules from this predominantly natural products database appeared much more diverse than the FDA approved drugs (Table S12). We reviewed the molecules and visually predicted the likely class or type of molecule based on our pharmacology knowledge. Ebelactone A, Solwaric Acid B, and other compounds of interest that likely represent antibacterials, central nervous system-active compounds, and smaller ‘anaesthetic-like’ natural products were found. Twenty-nine molecules had literature evidence of their bioactivity. We also performed a t-SNE analysis to visualize the structural similarity of the top hits from the COCONUT database list to known eye-active compounds with specific known functions (Fig. 7). These molecules unsurprisingly tended to occupy a distinct chemical space from most eye-active compounds identified earlier and were surrounded primarily by likely retina-active compounds followed by similar molecules used to manage pain, IOP, dry-eye, macular degeneration, and infection. However, several molecules from the COCONUT database appeared close in chemical structure to some of the known ‘eye-active’ molecules. We performed a nearest-neighbor search in the t-SNE space to find the closest ones to each ‘eye-active’ compound. Investigating these molecules for similar activity in the literature, we found structurally similar compounds that were proximal to those with known eye activity (Fig. 7).

Molecular shape.

The PMI plot [67] in Figure S6 demonstrates that the orally acting FDA drugs tend to be distributed between the “flat” and “rod” corners of the PMI plot, with a higher density in the rod-shaped corner. Very few of them even come close to the “round” corner of the plot, indicating that most of the orally acting FDA drugs are linear, flat, or somewhere in between, with little extent into the third spatial dimension. This pattern could be the result of a bias of FDA drugs, or it could relate to a molecular shape that improves BRB penetration. The distribution of molecules predicted active by MegaEye matches the distribution of orally acting FDA drugs (mostly rod shaped, some flat, and very few round). By contrast, the molecules predicted active by the original set of models are evenly distributed between rod-shaped and flat, with a much larger tendency toward round (i.e. more of a 3-D shape, as opposed to flat or linear). This result suggests that MegaEye is selecting molecules closer in shape to known, FDA-approved orally acting drugs than the original set of models did.

Developing a “Rule of 5″ for eye-active molecules.

We next set out to find optimal chemical space features similar to Lipinski’s Rule of 5 for orally acting eye-active drugs [68]. This heuristic describes how most drugs tend to fall into a range of molecular properties that predicts oral absorption. These rules have been used to guide the drug discovery process for several decades. We generated a set of 11 molecular descriptors and explored a FIGS model to generate a predictive model trained to identify FDA approved oral drugs that are active in the eye. We used 5-fold cross validation to train the FIGS classifier. The final model had a high precision (0.71), indicating that the tree has a high positive identification rate for finding eye-active compounds. However, the recall of the final model was low (0.12), suggesting that it would also tend to miss finding ‘actives’ (Table 3). The FIGS classifier (Fig. 8) also suggested that TPSA, logP, pKa, and sp3 were important for finding ‘eye-active’ compounds. This model might be potentially improved with additional descriptors (outside of the interpretable ones used) or other algorithms (that may be more black-box in nature).

Training a large language model to predict eye activity of molecules.

We initially curated datasets and built machine learning models to predict toxicity in ARPE-19 cells, OCT/1/2/3 transport uptake, corneal permeability via PAMPA, and BBB permeability as surrogates to predict likely activity in the eye. Although the statistics for the individual models suggested good predictive capabilities, the datasets curated for eye-specific models were small individually, containing low-hundreds of datapoints (or less). We next employed a modeling strategy that combined all the datasets and modeling tasks to fine tune an LLM trained on molecule structures.

After ~2500 training steps, the MegaEye LLM began to overfit the data (Figure S7), and thus we kept for final evaluation the model checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. The large language eye model outperformed all previously built individual models, except for the ARPE-19 cell line, to which it was comparable, suggesting the pre-training and multi-task learning improved predictive power (Table 4). The precision improved dramatically for OCT1, 2, and 3, while maintaining good recall. The model predicted toxicity in ARPE-19 cells perfectly, although this effect was likely due to the small dataset size; a larger test set would likely be needed to reflect true accuracy. The model has improved precision for corneal PAMPA with a small drop in recall.

The distinct feature input of the large language model, which is SMILES versus the ECFP6 for the rest of the individual models, provides another approach from which to make predictions. We therefore also screened the COCONUT database using MegaEye to capture potentially ‘new’ molecules predicted to have activity in the eye that might not have been identified with the individual models used earlier. We kept molecules with a prediction score >0.75 in at least one of the MegaEye output predictions. These results are included as a separate file (Table S13).

The MegaEye large language model identified 2047 compounds that achieved an MPO desirability score of ≥4, warranting further investigation. Two hundred twenty-two of these “hits” are predicted to be active in the eye, among which 114 were identified as being capable of circumventing the BBB upon oral delivery.

MegaEye predicted a different subset of active molecules compared to the original models, identifying additional natural products to investigate (Table S13) that cover a different chemical property space (Fig. 9). Similarly, we can use this second subset of compounds from COCONUT for target prediction, as we did for the earlier molecule selections performed with the t-SNE plot. The hits from the MegaEye predictions for the COCONUT database suggest a concentration around molecules identified for pain and retinal disease, but less so for IOP management and miosis (Fig. 10).

In summary, we have illustrated with multiple machine learning models how they can be used to select hundreds of molecules with potential activity in the eye from a database of hundreds of thousands of prospective molecules, including natural products, for future in vitro testing and model validation.

Discussion

For eye disease conditions that are known or predicted to be responsive to pharmacological drugs, a key consideration is the route of administration. Of the numerous approaches available to deliver a drug to the eye, topical application is considered the easiest and safest [17,73], and similarly, oral dosing represents a non-invasive method that does not require sterile conditions [17]. Predicting molecules and their entry into the eye using computational approaches has to date been a niche area. Virtually all computational modeling for predicting entry of molecules into the eye has focused on corneal permeability and used small training sets that are usually comprised of congeneric molecules. As an example, computational approaches that aim to replace animal models have been used to predict ocular irritants [74]. However, employing QSAR models [75–78] for this purpose does not completely correlate with ocular permeability [79]. Ocular absorption of drugs has been compared in the porcine cornea model [80]. Twenty-five small molecules showed a 52-fold range in permeability in this study, and the most important molecular descriptors identified were the hydrogen bond donor (HBD), topological polar surface area (TPSA), and halogen ratio [80]. A comparison of conjunctival drug permeability values indicated that the loss of drug via the conjunctiva restricts drug bioavailability such that absorption into the eye does not increase in proportion to the corneal permeability. In vitro data from the porcine model has also been used to generate quantitative structure property relationship (QSPR) models [81]. The in vivo permeability coefficients of fluoroquinolones in the rabbit eye model have been used to derive a QSPR for prediction of corneal permeability of β-blockers and fluoroquinolones [82]. Biopartitioning micellar chromatography has been used to simulate the cornea and provide data to generate quantitative structure retention relationships (QSRR) between 17β-carboxamide steroids and molecular descriptors. These models indicate that lipophilicity and number of short C–C fragments of tested compounds have the strongest influence on drug retention [83]. Early work used artificial neural networks (ANNs) with a set of 45 molecules and experimental corneal permeability data. The resulting model required only 4 descriptors and had a high correlation coefficient [84]. Strategies for sustained drug delivery to the eye have also leveraged machine learning to design multifunctional peptides conjugated to drugs that bind to melanin and thereby resist metabolic turnover [85].

In this study, we applied numerous computational models and filters to identify molecules that might have activity or toxicity in the eye when delivered orally, and the results in turn suggest ideal physicochemical properties for such molecules. We have used several strategies to identify molecules with biological activity in the human eye. The search included simple rule-based approaches for BBB penetration using the MPO [50] and a BBB machine learning model [50] (as surrogates for assessing BRB permeability), a conservative MW cutoff of 150, OCT 1, 2, 3 machine learning models, as well as ARPE-19 cell line and corneal PAMPA machine learning models. In general, each approach ranked different molecules as high scoring and there was a relatively low degree of overlap in the molecules suggested by each method. The ARPE-19 cell line is extensively used in ophthalmology research; however, we acknowledge that it has limitations. Given that ARPE-19 cells have a transcriptome that does not fully represent that of native human RPE cells [86], have tight junctions which maintain a transepithelial resistance lower than that of the RPE (indicates “leakiness”) [87], and are a mixture of mortal and immortalized cells [88], the effect of a drug candidate observed in ARPE-19 cells may not accurately reflect that which would occur in vivo. Toxicity results from ARPE-19 cells cannot necessarily be extrapolated to cell types in the eye other than retinal pigment epithelium cells and do not directly test drug permeation through the BRB. Alternative approaches in future could generate and model data from organoids whether retinal [89] or corneal [90].

Combining our machine learning and simple PubMed searches resulted in 115 molecules (Table S6) which, when delivered orally in humans, have bioactivity in the eye. Most of these candidates are not approved for ophthalmic use or perhaps are not even widely known to have ocular activity. These molecules therefore may represent repurposing opportunities or indicate that we could search for structurally similar compounds that also have activity but have not yet been tested clinically. For this analysis, we excluded ‘in vitro eye’ and ‘non-human species’ related work and instead focused solely on ‘in vivo human data’.

Using t-SNE plots of oral drugs versus the FDA approved drugs, oral drugs appear widely distributed across the plot (Fig. 1). This result suggests that oral acting compounds that cross the BRB can be achieved with numerous core structures that are not restricted to a single scaffold or family. Another way to reframe this problem is that entry into the eye may not be dependent on one molecule structure property, or class alone. For example, this entry could be via transporters like OCTs, which may be promiscuous in the wide variety of structures that are substrates [91–93]. As the OCT substrate models perform well at predicting molecules that have eye activity, it would be worth identifying additional OCT substrates through literature testing or in vitro testing of the FDA approved drug library. Other transporter models could also be used. A similar pattern emerged when we examined the distribution of compounds found to have activity in the eye; activity is not restricted to a single core or scaffold (Fig. 4). The distribution of orally available compounds also distributes across the many scaffolds of ‘eye active’ compounds (Fig. 5), with a slight concentration on steroidal structures like prednisone and eplerenone.

Challenges exist for formulating topical and oral medications for ophthalmic indications. For topical delivery of medication to the eye, it has been suggested molecules larger than 500 Da resist absorption through cornea [94], as do those that are hydrophilic or highly charged/polar [15,95]; molecules must be amphipathic to permeate the corneal epithelium and stroma [2]. Bioavailability of a topically applied compound at the target can be inconsistent for many reasons [13,96,97]. More than half of the locally dosed drugs enter the blood stream through the conjunctiva and nasopharyngeal mucosa which can lead to systemic effects [98,99]. Oral drugs must be provided at a sufficiently high dose that the material is available to reach the eye after much of it has been lost to the liver, gastrointestinal tract, vasculature, and lungs via the first-pass effect [100,101]. Compounds administered orally should be able to traverse the BRB, and experiments in bovine indicate the ability to do so is inversely related to size and directly related to lipophilicity [102]. As the oral route of administration is inherently systemic, the risk of off-target effects must also be considered [103–105] for new medications and those repurposed for the eye (e.g., β-blockers affecting the cardiovascular system). Both topical and oral delivery often rely on patient compliance, which can negatively affect outcomes in at least two ways. First, only 50 % of patients with chronic illness are estimated to take prescription drugs as directed by their healthcare professional [106,107]. This problem may be exacerbated with eye drops, which can be difficult for some patients to instill independently or can cause a burning sensation that deters compliance. Second, patients who self-administer medication for slowly progressing diseases that are not associated with overt physical symptoms are likely to skip routine visits to their eye care professional [108].

Drugs that pass the BBB, and therefore likely pass the BRB, have distinctive properties compared to the remaining FDA approved drugs (Fig. 4). Drugs that are active in the eye generally have a slightly lower log-TPSA on average, and likewise a slightly lower MW (Figs. 4 and 5). Drugs that are orally delivered and reach the eye must pass the BRB and these show similar physicochemical properties as the drugs identified as eye active. The molecules identified with ‘eye-activity’ indicate numerous core structures confer the ability to cross the BRB following oral dosing. Oral drugs that are active in the eye also have significantly reduced HBDs (Fig. 5), and this pattern is more pronounced in these drugs than in those which are active in the eye but are not known to be orally active.

We used assigned categories for the oral drugs that are active in the eye, and this analysis revealed that clustering was tight for some groups and diffuse for others (Fig. 6). These classes may be useful for scoring other drugs to determine if they cluster near these already classified drugs – a type of nearest neighbor approach. We could use this method to search for analogs (or very similar structures) of all the molecules that were shown to have eye activity when delivered orally. We then searched these analogs in PubMed for eye related data. We could also use our ‘integrated approach’ to search other libraries of molecules (e.g. drugs approved outside the US, food ingredients, excipients and natural products) to potentially identify other molecules that are potentially active in the eye. Further work could include building a machine learning model for orally active molecules in the eye versus those delivered topically or by other routes. Having identified the corneal PAMPA model and its capabilities, such a machine learning model could be useful for validating the predictions experimentally. This in silico validation could be followed by screening compounds in ARPE-19 cells, 661 W cells [109,110], retinal organoids [111,112], or other ocular cell lines, as appropriate to the indication being investigated. Alternatively, we could perform target-based and phenotypic screens in zebrafish, as permeation of compounds across the BRB upon systemic delivery can be evaluated in this animal model following oral gavage [113,114] or injection into the sinus venosus [115]. Data collected from these experiments could then be used to develop additional machine learning models to predict drug efficacy and toxicity for a variety of specific ocular diseases, such as glaucoma [116].

Our interest in natural products is focused on identifying molecules that could be made available over the counter to patients for ocular diseases. However, there are advantages and disadvantages to this drug distribution model (easy access, inexpensive, widespread access to treatment; possible patient compliance issues, possible reduction in return visits to clinic, etc.) and to suggest such molecules may have utility without a deeper assessment of drug-drug interaction potential could ultimately be dangerous.

Examples of natural product hits in the COCONUT database identified with MegaEye include anti-inflammatory molecules like mangicol A [117], neurotrophic factors such as mastigophorene A [118], the antibacterial merobatzelladine A [119], the fatty acid activator of soluble guanylate cyclase (lowers IOP [120]) 12-hydroperoxyeicosatetraenoic acid [121], carotenoids with antimicrobial and antioxidant properties like 2-(3,7,12,16,20,24-hexamethylpentacosa-1,3,5,9,11,13,15,17,19,23-decaen-1-yl)-1,3,3-trimethylcyclohex-1-ene (alpha-Zeacarotene) [122], polyene antibiotics like rimocidin [123], the polyketide antibiotic aurafuron A [124] and neuroprotective molecules such as 4′-O-methylbavachalcone [125] (Table S13) that may hold promise for novel ocular treatments.

In summary, our multiple various computational approaches (Figure S8) used a combination of various property filters and machine learning that has the potential to identify otherwise overlooked applications of drugs and natural products in ophthalmology following topical or oral delivery. Future directions of this work could involve the exploration of additional machine learning algorithms and molecular descriptors for use with these datasets used to create the models, as well as in vitro and in vivo experimental testing of such compounds to further validate the predictions from MegaEye.
