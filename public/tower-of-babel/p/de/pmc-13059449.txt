Treatment patterns, and outcomes in elderly patients with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer: a real-world analysis from a single center in China
Authors: Xuexing Wang, Xingxing Tang, Yufeng Wang, Guozhong Zhou, Kai Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis retrospective cohort study aims to describe the treatment patterns and evaluate survival outcomes in patients aged 70 years or older with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (mNSCLC), and to identify independent prognostic factors for overall survival (OS).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed medical records of 298 patients aged ≥70 years with mNSCLC diagnosed between June 2021 and December 2024. Patients were categorized into an active anti-tumor therapy (ATT) group and a best supportive care (BSC) group for descriptive purposes. Key clinical data, including molecular biomarker status (EGFR, ALK, PD-L1) and Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI), were collected for a subset of patients. A multivariate Cox proportional-hazards model was used to identify independent prognostic factors for OS across the entire cohort.ResultsOf the 298 patients, 93 (31.2%) received ATT and 205 (68.8%) received BSC. The decision for ATT was significantly associated with better baseline ECOG performance status (ECOG-PS) and fewer comorbidities, indicating profound selection bias. The median OS was 18.0 months in the ATT group and 6.0 months in the BSC group. This difference is descriptive and cannot be interpreted as a treatment effect due to the baseline imbalances. Within the ATT group, treatment modalities included chemotherapy (n = 54), targeted therapy (n = 11), and combination therapy (n = 28). In the multivariate analysis of the entire cohort, better ECOG PS (0–1 vs. 4: HR = 0.28, P < 0.01), fewer comorbidities (0 vs. ≥3: HR = 0.17, P < 0.001), higher GNRI (≥ 98 vs. <92: HR = 0.55, P = 0.02), and adenocarcinoma histology (vs. non-adenocarcinoma: HR = 0.68, P = 0.04) were independent predictors of longer OS.ConclusionIn this real-world cohort of elderly mNSCLC patients, treatment selection was heavily influenced by baseline health status. Independent prognostic factors for longer survival were better performance status, lower comorbidity burden, better nutritional status (higher GNRI), and adenocarcinoma histology. These findings underscore the importance of comprehensive geriatric and nutritional assessment in this population and highlight the need for prospective trials to guide treatment decisions.

Introduction

China is facing a significant challenge due to its rapidly aging population, a concern that has garnered the attention of policymakers, healthcare providers, and academic researchers alike. According to prevailing clinical guidelines, individuals aged 70 years and older are conventionally classified as elderly in China. This age-based categorization takes into account the increased prevalence of various physiological changes and diseases commonly experienced after age 70, such as malignant tumors, cognitive impairment, chronic pain, and cardiovascular diseases [1]. Lung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among the elderly, both in China and worldwide [2]. More than half of lung cancer diagnoses occur in individuals 65 years of age or older, with about 30% of cases identified in those aged 70 and up [3, 4]. Notably, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 80% of all lung cancer cases [5]. Recent advancements in treatment modalities—including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and combination therapies—have led to significant improvements in the response rates and overall survival (OS) of NSCLC patients [6].

However, the application of active anti-tumor therapy (ATT) in elderly patients is often restricted due to multiple considerations. These include declining physical functionality, compromised hepatic and renal functions, pulmonary issues, hematopoietic disorders, as well as apprehensions related to surgical trauma and the adverse effects of chemo-radiotherapy [4, 7]. Economic and social factors also come into play. As a result, treatment strategies with minimal side effects or targeted immunotherapies are often preferred for these patients. While numerous studies have explored the efficacy of ATT in elderly patients with NSCLC, there remains a dearth of phase III clinical trials that focus exclusively on-stage IV NSCLC patients receiving ATT alongside Best Supportive Care (BSC) [8]. Existing research primarily includes patients with high-performance status, often determined by an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score between 0 and 2 and mostly younger patients [9]. Furthermore, implementing targeted immunotherapies in a real-world setting poses challenges due to barriers such as limited access to immunotherapeutic drugs and difficulties in obtaining pathological histology [10].

To address these gaps, our retrospective cohort study aims to describe the real-world treatment patterns, evaluate toxicity and OS, and identify key prognostic factors in a sample of 298 elderly patients with stage IV NSCLC, diagnosed between June 2021 and December 2024.

Patients and methods

Study population

We conducted a retrospective review of hospital records and written archive files for patients with stage IV metastatic Non-Small-Cell Lung Cancer (mNSCLC) diagnosed and treated at the First People’s Hospital of Anning, affiliated with Kunming University of Science and Technology, between June 2021 and December 2024. Patients were staged according to the 8th Edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and the International Union for Cancer Control Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) staging system. All included patients were diagnosed through histopathological examination, with staging confirmed by 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18 F-FDG-PET/CT) scans and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) prior to treatment initiation.

The inclusion criterion was patients aged 70 years or older. Exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of a second primary malignancy; (2) an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG-PS) of 5; and (3) substantial missing data for key variables required for prognostic analysis, specifically ECOG-PS, comorbidity count, or the date of diagnosis. After applying these criteria, a total of 298 eligible patients were included for the final analysis (See Fig. 1 for details).

Data collection and explanatory notes

In this comprehensive study, we meticulously gathered clinical and demographic data for each patient. This included age, gender, occupation, and smoking intensity quantified using the Brinkman Index(BI) (categorized as 0, 1-200, 201–399, or ≥ 400). Additionally, pathological subtypes and concurrent diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, coronary artery disease, chronic bronchitis, gout, cerebral infarction, and hepatitis were documented. The Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG-PS) was employed to assess functional status. We also collected detailed information on treatment modalities and responses.In addition to standard clinicopathological features, we collected data on nutritional status, calculated using the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) based on serum albumin and body weight [11]. For the non-squamous NSCLC subgroup, we collected data on molecular biomarker status, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) mutations, and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression levels, where these assessments were part of standard clinical practice.

Patients were stratified into two cohorts based on treatment approaches: those receiving BSC and those undergoing ATT. Patients were stratified into two cohorts based on the primary treatment strategy. The active anti-tumor treatment (ATT) group included patients who received at least one cycle/course of systemic therapy (chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy), radiotherapy (regardless of curative or palliative intent), or palliative-intent surgery. The best supportive care (BSC) group consisted of patients who received only symptom management without any of the aforementioned anti-tumor modalities.ATT encompassed curative-intent chemotherapy, radiation therapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, or combinations thereof, aimed at eradicating malignant cells, prolonging survival, and facilitating recovery. The chemotherapy protocol included intravenous paclitaxel analogs (e.g., paclitaxel, its analogs), albumin-bound paclitaxel, cisplatin, carboplatin, pemetrexed, docetaxel, and oral agents like etoposide and capecitabine, dosed based on body surface area. These protocols were administered for a minimum of three cycles or extended over a month. In patients with NSCLC harboring ALK fusion-positive tumors undergoing antineoplastic therapy, targeted treatments predominantly involved oral ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as crizotinib, lorlatinib, and alectinib. These drugs impede the activity of ALK fusion proteins, curtailing tumor progression and metastasis. For NSCLC with other molecular traits, like ROS1 fusion-positive or BRAF-mutant tumors, targeted therapies included Lorlatinib or Dabrafenib combined with Trametinib. In some elderly NSCLC cases, clinicians might opt for immunotherapy, based on initial treatment tolerance, financial capability, and patient preference. The choice of immunotherapeutics, such as nivolumab, pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, was guided by anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibody expression and tumor mutation burden. Combination therapy was defined as the concurrent or sequential use of at least two different modalities (e.g., chemo-immunotherapy, chemo-radiotherapy).These antitumor treatments conformed to the 2019–2024 Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) guidelines, which evolved during the study period to increasingly incorporate immunotherapy and targeted therapy as first-line options for selected patients [12]. BSC focused on symptom management, patient well-being, and personalized care, including pain management, infection prevention, nutritional support, psychological counseling, and rehabilitation. The distinction between ATT and BSC is crucial for categorizing patients in studies. Treatment plans were devised through multidisciplinary team discussions, incorporating clinical judgment and patient input, and considering factors like age, health status, medical history, and current medications. The primary study objective was to evaluate survival outcomes in metastatic NSCLC aging patients post-active anti-tumor therapy. OS was the interval from initial treatment to death from any cause or the last follow-up, with diagnosis established via chest X-ray or CT and confirmed by lung biopsy or cytology. OS was defined as the time from confirmed diagnosis to the date of death or data cutoff in January 2025. The assessment of ECOG-PS and age was performed at the time of initial diagnosis of metastatic disease, prior to the first-line treatment decision. Patients who initiated ATT but discontinued after fewer than three cycles were analyzed within the ATT group based on the intention-to-treat principle.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 23.0. Descriptive statistics were used to calculate frequencies, medians, and interquartile ranges (25th-75th percentiles). We evaluated OS and median survival time (MST) against several variables: gender; age categories (<76, and ≥ 76 years); histological type (adenocarcinoma versus non-adenocarcinoma); and tumor site(left side versus right side). Survival Log-rank tests were used for descriptive comparisons within the ATT group, with the understanding that these subgroups are not randomized and comparisons should be interpreted with caution. For the multivariate analysis, a Cox proportional hazards model was constructed for the entire cohort (N = 298) to identify independent baseline predictors of survival. Variables included in the model were age, gender, ECOG-PS, number of major comorbidities, pathological type, and GNRI. Treatment type (ATT vs. BSC) was not included as a covariate due to its nature as an outcome of baseline patient status, which would introduce significant confounding. The influence of each factor was presented as a hazard ratio (HR) accompanied by its 95% confidence interval (CI). A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed indicative of statistical significance. A formal sample size calculation was not performed due to the retrospective nature of the study. Propensity score matching (PSM) was not performed to balance baseline characteristics between the ATT and BSC groups. Due to limitations in the dataset, such as unmeasured confounders critical for matching (e.g., detailed frailty scores, social support), a PSM analysis was not performed as it could introduce new biases; this represents a significant limitation, as detailed in the Discussion section.

Ethical approval

The study, designed as a single-center retrospective clinical evaluation, sourced its participants solely from The First People’s Hospital of Anning, affiliated with Kunming University of Science and Technology in Yunnan Province, China. Ethical clearance was duly obtained from the Ethics Committee of the aforementioned institution. All participants were assured that their data would be exclusive to this research initiative. Stringent measures ensured the confidentiality of the data; any analyses performed utilized de-identified information to uphold patient privacy. The ethical approval reference for this study is 2023-019.

Result

Comparison of basic patient characteristics between ATT group and BSC group

In our retrospective cohort analysis, patients were categorized into two groups: 93 (31.2%) received ATT and 205 (68.8%) received BSC.Of the 298 patients in the entire cohort, only 45 (15.1%) underwent EGFR/ALK gene testing, and 41 (13.8%) underwent PD-L1 expression testing. Due to the low testing rates, a meaningful statistical analysis of these biomarkers could not be performed. The foundational characteristics of these cohorts are delineated in Table 1. Crucially, significant baseline differences were observed between the two groups. Patients in the ATT group had a significantly better ECOG-PS (P < 0.001), a lower burden of major comorbidities (P = 0.002), and a better nutritional status as indicated by a higher GNRI (P < 0.001). These imbalances highlight a strong selection bias, where clinically fitter patients were preferentially selected for ATT. No significant differences were noted in age, gender, or pathological classification between the groups (P > 0.05). Within the ATT cohort (n = 93), treatment modalities included chemotherapy (n = 54), targeted therapy (n = 11), and combination therapy (n = 28).Our examination revealed that, among geriatric patients with advanced neoplastic diseases in the ATT cohort, monotherapeutic chemotherapy and combined therapeutic strategies were predominant. Within the ATT group, no significant variations in age or ECOG-PS were discerned across the varied therapeutic strategies. Nevertheless, pronounced statistical discrepancies were observed in gender, tumor location, BI, the prevalence of major severe comorbidities, and pathological categorization.Within the ATT group (n = 93), treatment modalities were heterogeneous. The most common approaches were chemotherapy alone (n = 54, 58.1%) and combination therapy (n = 28, 30.1%). A smaller number of patients received targeted therapy alone (n = 11, 11.8%). Due to the small sample sizes and non-randomized nature of these subgroups, no formal statistical comparisons of their baseline characteristics or outcomes were performed, as such analyses would be underpowered and potentially misleading.

The survival analysis for the cohort of 298 patients

Descriptive survival analysis was performed for the entire cohort. As shown in Fig. 2A, a significant difference in overall survival was observed between the treatment groups, with a median OS of 18.0 months (95% CI: 14.5–21.5) in the ATT group and 6.0 months (95% CI: 4.8–7.2) in the BSC group (Log-rank P < 0.001). The 1-year survival rate was 60.9% in the ATT group versus 0% in the BSC group. This stark difference is interpreted as a reflection of profound baseline prognostic disparities and selection bias, rather than a true treatment effect. A subgroup analysis was conducted to explore survival outcomes across different age strata. A statistically significant survival difference between the ATT and BSC groups was noted in patients aged 70–75 years [ATT: 19.0 months (95% CI: 15.2–22.8); BSC: 6.5 months (95% CI: 5.1–7.9); Log-rank P < 0.001] (Fig. 2B) and ≥ 76 years [ATT: 16.0 months (95% CI: 12.3–19.7); BSC: 5.5 months (95% CI: 4.2–6.8); Log-rank P = 0.036] (Fig. 2C).

Survival analysis of patients in ATT group

Gender-related differences in overall survival were not statistically significant, with males having a median survival of 13.0 months (95% CI: 8.93–17.08) and females 37.0 months (95% CI: 11.12–62.88) (Log-rank P = 0.236) (Fig. 3A). Tumor location, whether left or right, did not significantly influence survival outcomes: 13.0 months (95% CI: 8.58–17.42) and 18.0 months (95% CI: 7.45–28.56), respectively (Log-rank P = 0.893) (Fig. 3B). Pathological subtypes displayed contrasting survival rates. Patients with adenocarcinoma had a median survival of 37.0 months (95% CI: 25.3–48.7) as opposed to those with non-adenocarcinoma, who had 11.0 months (95% CI: 6.6–15.4). This difference was statistically significant (Log-rank P = 0.024) (Fig. 3C). These observations are purely descriptive and should not be interpreted as a direct comparison of treatment efficacy due to significant baseline differences and the non-randomized nature of treatment allocation.

Analysis of prognostic factors in elderly patients with metastatic NSCLC

To identify independent baseline predictors of survival, a multivariate Cox proportional-hazards model was constructed for the entire cohort (N = 298). The model included key clinical and demographic variables: age, gender, ECOG-PS, number of major comorbidities, pathological type, and GNRI. Notably, treatment type (ATT vs. BSC) was correctly excluded from this model to avoid confounding by indication. The analysis identified four factors as independent predictors of overall survival. As detailed in Table 2, poorer ECOG PS (PS 4 vs. 0–1: HR = 3.55, 95% CI 1.98–6.36, P < 0.01), a greater number of comorbidities (≥ 2 vs. 0: HR = 2.54, 95% CI 1.70–3.79, P < 0.001), poorer nutritional status (GNRI < 98 vs. ≥98: HR = 1.82, 95% CI 1.28–2.59, P = 0.02), and non-adenocarcinoma histology (vs. adenocarcinoma: HR = 1.47, 95% CI 1.02–2.12, P = 0.04) were all significantly associated with an increased risk of death.

Adverse drug reactions in cancer patients undergoing different types of treatment

An analysis of treatment-related adverse events (AEs) was conducted for patients in the ATT subgroups.In the chemotherapy-alone group (n = 54), hematologic toxicities were most prominent. Any-grade myelosuppression was observed in 26 patients (48.1%), with clinically significant Grade 3–4 events occurring in 4 patients (7.4%). The most frequent gastrointestinal AEs were nausea (12/54, 22.2%) and vomiting (6/54, 11.1%).Among patients receiving radiotherapy alone (n = 18), the most common toxicities were dermatological (8/18, 44.4%), including erythema and pruritus, followed by gastrointestinal events such as nausea (4/18, 22.2%).For the targeted therapy group (n = 11), dermatological reactions were predominant, reported in 8 patients (72.7%). Digestive complaints were noted in 5 patients (45.5%), and elevated transaminases occurred in 2 patients (18.2%).The combination therapy group (n = 28) exhibited a diverse AE profile. The most common events were dermatological and digestive, each affecting 7 patients (25.0%). Hematologic toxicity was observed in 6 patients (21.4%).Beyond these manageable toxicities, infrequent but severe complications (e.g., pulmonary, renal, neurologic, cardiac) were occasionally observed, particularly with integrated treatments. Due to their rarity, these events were not subjected to statistical analysis but underscore the need for vigilant monitoring in this population.

Discussion

Recent advances have marked a significant surge in the life expectancy of the elderly, propelling the expansion of this demographic [1, 13]. Data from various Asian territories underscores an average life expectancy of 80 years for men and 87 years for women. This demographic swell amplifies the fraction of elderly patients diagnosed with neoplasms [14]. Theoretically, these patients may face challenges with ATT adherence, primarily attributed to age-induced organ function decrement and extant comorbidities. For instance, attenuated renal and hepatic functionalities can decelerate drug metabolism, escalating the risk of drug-induced toxicity. Moreover, prevalent ailments in elderly patients, such as diabetes, hypertension, and cardiac diseases, often necessitate concurrent medications that might adversely intersect with ATT [15].

Notably, the elderly, irrespective of their cancer subtype, find themselves underrepresented in clinical trials [9, 16, 17]. This paucity casts shadows on the therapeutic efficacies and potential adverse responses in this cohort, rendering clinical decisions challenging in the absence of robust empirical evidence. Emphasizing the depth of this knowledge gap, it is essential to augment research efforts tailored for elderly patients with cancer.

Our analysis did not find a statistically significant difference in survival between male and female patients receiving ATT (P = 0.20). This finding aligns with some studies suggesting gender may not be a primary independent prognostic factor for survival in this population [18]. Nevertheless, the influence of gender on lung cancer prognosis remains contested. Some large-scale analyses have suggested that females may have a survival advantage. The precise mechanisms underpinning potential gender-linked discrepancies warrant deeper exploration, and our study did not have sufficient power to definitively resolve this question.

Presently, the prognostic gravity of histological subtypes in NSCLC remains under debate. We assessed the predominant NSCLC categories, including adenocarcinoma (subtypes: lepidic, acinar, papillary) and non-adenocarcinoma (subtypes: squamous cell, large cell carcinoma), which collectively represent 80% of lung cancer incidences. Although prior research [5]. has spotlighted histological subtyping as pivotal in lung cancer prognosis, emerging targeted therapies, such as epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs), and specific chemotherapy agents like pemetrexed have reinvigorated interest in discerning the interplay between histological subtypes and therapeutic outcomes.Our rigorous evaluation of tumor sites revealed no convincing evidence supporting their role as independent prognostic markers for survival. Recent literature also converges on the inconclusive impact of tumor locations on survival trajectories, potentially stemming from the intricate interplay between tumor attributes, patient health profiles, and treatment efficacy. Based on our data, tumor location might not warrant prioritization in guiding aggressive anti-tumor treatments for advanced non-small cell lung cancer in elderly patients. This further underscores the pressing need for pioneering research to craft bespoke therapeutic modalities, optimizing survival prospects for this demographic.

Consistent with prior research [19–21]. our findings underscore the pressing challenge of therapy-induced toxicities in oncologic management. This issue is particularly accentuated in elderly patients, whose heightened vulnerability to adverse events arises from physiological senescence and consequent functional diminution. Our investigation pinpointed prevalent hematologic and gastrointestinal toxicities stemming from chemotherapy and radiation, mild cutaneous reactions linked to targeted therapies, hepatic perturbations, and an encompassing gamut of toxicities affiliated with combination regimens, spanning cutaneous, hematologic, hepatic, and cardiovascular domains. Notably, while comprehensive treatment regimens elicited sporadic yet severe events like pulmonary, renal, neurologic, and cardiac toxicities, their infrequent manifestation rendered them statistically non-analyzable in our dataset. Given this toxicity profile, we advocate for a calibrated therapeutic strategy in geriatric oncology, favoring less cytotoxic chemotherapeutic agents or molecularly-targeted interventions during the maintenance phase.

Our multivariate analysis, focused on baseline characteristics across the entire cohort, identified four independent predictors of survival: better ECOG-PS, lower comorbidity burden, higher GNRI, and adenocarcinoma histology. The strong prognostic significance of ECOG-PS and comorbidities is well-established in geriatric oncology, confirming that functional status and physiological resilience are paramount in this population [4, 22–24].

Crucially, our findings highlight the independent prognostic value of nutritional status, as measured by the GNRI. This suggests that in elderly patients, a triad of factors-functional capacity (ECOG), underlying health (comorbidities), and nutritional reserve (GNRI)-collectively provides a more comprehensive prognostic picture than any single factor alone. This reinforces the importance of integrating nutritional assessment into the routine evaluation of older patients with cancer [25, 26].

The finding that adenocarcinoma was associated with better survival must be interpreted with caution. As noted in our limitations, this study lacks systematic molecular data. It is highly plausible that this observed survival advantage is not due to the histology itself, but is instead confounded by a higher, unmeasured prevalence of targetable driver mutations (e.g., EGFR) within the adenocarcinoma subgroup, which are known to have a better prognosis.

Therefore, these results underscore the necessity of a multi-dimensional baseline assessment to accurately stratify risk and inform prognostic discussions with elderly mNSCLC patients and their families, rather than to guide specific therapeutic choices between ATT and BSC.

This study has several critical limitations which we have integrated into our interpretation.First and foremost is the profound selection bias inherent to its retrospective, non-randomized design. The finding that the BSC group had 0% 1-year survival confirms this cohort had an exceptionally poor prognosis at baseline, rendering any direct survival comparison to the ATT group scientifically invalid and uninterpretable as a treatment effect.Second, the absence of systematic data on key molecular biomarkers (e.g., EGFR, ALK, PD-L1) is a critical omission. This lack of molecular data is a major unmeasured confounder that likely explains a significant portion of the observed survival advantage in the adenocarcinoma subgroup, given that targetable mutations are more prevalent in this histology. Future prospective studies must include these biomarkers.Third, the ATT group was extremely heterogeneous. The “combination therapy” group, in particular, was ill-defined, mixing various modalities with different efficacy profiles. Consequently, the subgroup analyses comparing different ATT regimens were severely underpowered and should be considered purely descriptive and hypothesis-generating at best.Fourth, our analysis relied on ECOG-PS and did not incorporate a comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA), which is the gold standard for evaluating older cancer patients [25, 26]. This prevents a deeper analysis of frailty and its impact onoutcomes. Furthermore, data on second-line and subsequent lines of therapy were not collected, limiting our understanding of the complete patient treatment journey.The relatively small number of patients aged ≥ 80 years may reflect local referral patterns or a higher rate of treatment refusal in this very elderly population at our center.Finally, as a single-center study, the generalizability of our findings to other settings may be limited.

Our decision to remove the treatment variable (ATT vs. BSC) from the final multivariate prognostic model was a direct and necessary response to these limitations to ensure our conclusions remain scientifically sound. Notwithstanding these constraints, this study provides a descriptive snapshot of real-world treatment patterns. More importantly, it confirms the prognostic significance of key baseline factors (ECOG-PS, comorbidity burden, and nutritional status), thereby establishing a crucial baseline that underscores the urgent need for prospective, biomarker-driven research. This trend is already exemplified by ongoing efforts such as the prospective real-world study NCT06422546, which aims to characterize molecular marker changes in EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients following targeted therapy [27]. Our findings, therefore, not only describe current clinical realities but also highlight the necessity and provide a foundation for these essential future investigations.

Conclusion

In this real-world descriptive study of elderly mNSCLC patients, treatment selection was heavily biased by baseline performance status, comorbidity, and nutritional status. The analysis identified better ECOG PS, lower comorbidity burden, higher GNRI, and adenocarcinoma histology as independent prognostic factors for longer survival. The profound confounding by indication makes it impossible to draw any conclusions regarding the effectiveness of ATT versus BSC. These findings highlight that baseline health status, rather than treatment itself, was the primary determinant of survival in this cohort and underscore the urgent need for prospective trials incorporating comprehensive geriatric assessments to guide treatment for this vulnerable population.
