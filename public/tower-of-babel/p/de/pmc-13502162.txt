V-safe: Summary of findings reported after COVID-19 vaccination to a US CDC active safety surveillance system through June 2023
Authors: Tanya R Myers, Lauren H Zauche, Paige L Marquez, Isaac McCullum, Anne M Hause, David K Shay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractV-safe is an active surveillance system developed during the pandemic to monitor COVID-19 vaccine safety. After vaccination, US residents could enroll to receive text messages linked to health check-in surveys. Questions about symptoms and health impacts, including inability to complete daily activities, attend work or school, or receipt of medical care were asked at daily, weekly, and monthly intervals after vaccination. We describe demographics of participants compared to the vaccinated population, trends in enrollment, participation over time, and self-reported health impacts in the V-safe population during the COVID-19 pandemic. During December 14, 2020 – June 30, 2023, 9,609,282 persons completed at least one V-safe survey. Most were female (62.2%), aged 25–64 years (69.2%), and received an mRNA vaccine (95.0%). Compared to the US vaccinated population, a higher proportion of participants were non-Hispanic White or Asian and ≥ 75 years. Most registered for V-safe within the first few months after vaccination was recommended for their age group. Most completed an initial health survey within the first week after vaccination. Participation decreased for successive doses, especially among persons aged <18 years. Health impacts were reported more frequently during the first week post-vaccination, after dose 2 compared to dose 1, and among recipients of mRNA-1273 vaccine compared to recipients of BNT162b2 vaccine. Health impacts among children <12 years were reported slightly less frequently compared to adults. Seeking medical care was reported in the first week after vaccination by 0.8–1.2% of adults after doses 1–6 of an mRNA vaccine. Findings were similar to those in previous V-safe analyses and data from clinical trials. Rapidly decreasing participation during the course of the pandemic suggests that incentives encouraging survey completion in V-safe could be useful for improving safety monitoring for future emergency vaccines. V-safe continues to collect data about COVID-19 vaccines, with an emphasis on new products.Keywords: COVID-19 vaccines, Vaccination, Active surveillance, Health surveys, Safety

Introduction

The United States launched a mass immunization program in December 2020 to reduce morbidity and mortality associated with the COVID-19 pandemic [1]. In preparation, several government agencies coordinated efforts for timely collection of vaccine safety data from their respective surveillance systems [2]. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) determined that surveillance efforts could be strengthened with the addition of an active surveillance system to collect health data from vaccine recipients during early days of the campaign and to identify potential participants for a COVID-19 vaccine pregnancy registry [3,4]. In response, V-safe was developed as the first active safety surveillance system for vaccines administered to U.S. residents. V-safe supplemented existing U.S. vaccine safety monitoring systems, including the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) and the Vaccine Safety Datalink (VSD) [2].

The primary goal of this new system was to characterize the reactogenicity of COVID-19 vaccines among a large sample of the U.S. vaccinated population. An additional goal was to facilitate reporting of potentially serious adverse events to VAERS. Detailed descriptions of V-safe and its role in vaccine safety surveillance during the COVID-19 pandemic have been published [2,3]. The original V-safe platform was supported by services donated to the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services by Oracle as a component of the Oracle Health Management System. This original version of V-safe used Oracle services and collected data from December 14, 2020, through June 30, 2023. Deidentified data collected during this time is available for public access at data.cdc.gov. In fall of 2023, CDC launched a new V-safe system built using software more integrated with existing CDC information technology platforms (vsafe.cdc.gov).

This report describes demographic characteristics of participants who received a COVID-19 vaccine from December 14, 2020—May 19, 2023 and completed surveys during the pandemic response (December 14, 2020 – June 30, 2023), trends in enrollment and survey participation over time, and reports of specific health impacts among recipients of COVID-19 vaccines authorized for administration in the United States.

Methods

Data sources

Any person who received a COVID-19 vaccine in the United States or its territories from December 14, 2020, through May 19, 2023, was eligible to participate in V-safe [3]. Ability to register in V-safe was restricted to those of an age eligible for vaccination according to the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) recommendations at the time of enrollment as summarized in Supplemental Table 1 [5]. Beginning June 12, 2021, parents (or guardians) could create their own accounts to register dependent children aged 12–15 years; the lower age limit for dependent accounts changed over time based on current recommendations [5]. CDC promoted participation in V-safe through social media channels, posting information about V-safe on COVID-19 vaccine resource pages, and encouraging vaccine providers to inform patients of V-safe [3]. Methods used to inform vaccinated persons about the opportunity to participate in V-safe included mounting information posters in waiting areas, inclusion of a section describing V-safe in the Vaccine Information Statements provided to vaccinated persons, and communications from vaccine providers including email or text messaging [3]. These notifications included the V-safe Uniform Resource Locator or a Quick Response code directing participants to the V-safe landing page at vsafe.cdc.gov.

Fig. 1 shows actions completed by V-safe participants. Participants were asked to provide first and last names, date of birth, ZIP code, sex, and a mobile telephone number [3]. After completing a two-factor authentication process, they were asked to enter information about COVID-19 vaccines received to date [3]. Available vaccines in the United States during the study period were mRNA vaccines (BNT162b2, BNT162b2 bivalent, mRNA-1273, and mRNA-1273.222), an adenoviral-vector vaccine (Ad26.COV2.S), and a protein subunit vaccine (NVX-CoV2373). Participants were invited by text message to complete brief health surveys. During the first week after vaccination, daily text messages provided links to surveys. After the first week, surveys were available at weekly intervals up to 6 weeks post-vaccination and then at 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months (weeks 13, 26, and 52 postvaccination) [3].

Progression of activities for participants contributing post-vaccination surveys to V-safe, December 14, 2020 – June 30, 2023.

Survey questions varied depending on length of time since vaccination. Each survey included three health impact questions, designed to identify health events that were disruptive for the participant or warranted solicitation of a VAERS report. These three questions asked participants to indicate whether symptoms or health conditions after vaccination had caused them to 1) be unable to work or attend school, 2) be unable to do normal daily activities, or 3) seek care from a physician or other healthcare professional. Starting at week 2 after vaccination, health impact questions were asked only if a participant reported new symptoms or worsening conditions since their last check-in. Surveys at select timepoints included questions related to pregnancy, general health, and COVID-19 diagnosis after vaccination as described previously [3].

Because promotion of V-safe was at the discretion of each COVID-19 vaccination site and vaccinated persons had to complete an opt-in registration process, the cohort of participants in V-safe is best described as a convenience (i.e., non-probability) sample of all vaccinated persons in the United States and territories. Specific populations were not targeted or recruited for enrollment.

Analysis inclusion criteria

To be eligible for inclusion in this summary, participants needed to have registered in V-safe, recorded receipt of a COVID-19 vaccine by May 19, 2023, and completed at least one health survey by June 30, 2023. Surveys for doses recorded as received before the authorization date for each vaccine were not included, as we could not determine whether the recorded date was an entry error or represented vaccines received during clinical trials.

Data analyses

We described sex, age, and race and ethnicity of participants in V-safe. We compared distributions of these characteristics to those among the vaccinated U.S. population, defined as persons reported by U.S. jurisdictions to CDC as having received at least one dose of COVID-19 vaccine through May 10, 2023 [6]. We used 5-digit ZIP code to define geographic residence by state. ZIP codes were mapped to ZIP code tabulation areas (ZCTAs), geographic boundaries produced by the U.S. Census Bureau as standardized geographic representations of 5-digit ZIP codes. Because ZIP codes are designed for mail delivery rather than statistical reporting, associated ZCTAs vary widely in terms of population and geometry and do not always follow administrative boundaries. We used the Housing and Urban Development ZIP-CBSA crosswalk (2nd quarter 2023) to confirm participant location when state could not be confirmed by mapping to ZCTAs; for example, where a given ZCTA crossed state boundaries [7,8]. The number of persons who received at least one dose of COVID-19 vaccine by state or territory, as reported by U.S. jurisdictions, were used to estimate the proportion of vaccinated persons in each state or territory who participated in V-safe [9].

We described trends in enrollment and survey participation over time. Proportions of surveys completed by participants were calculated based on the number of surveys each was eligible to complete, which varied by vaccine doses received. When additional doses were reported, participants stopped receiving invitations to complete surveys corresponding to the previous dose received and began receiving invitations to complete surveys that corresponded to the most recent dose reported.

We summarized health impacts (defined as inability to work or attend school, inability to participate in normal daily activities, and seeking healthcare) and the types of healthcare sought (telehealth, virtual health or e-mail consultation; outpatient clinic or urgent care clinic visit; emergency room or department visit; hospitalization). Health impacts and types of healthcare sought were summarized as the proportion of total surveys (by age group, vaccine, and dose) reporting an event at the following timepoints: at least once in the first week of surveys, and at weeks 2–6, 13, 26, and 52. Self-report of general health (excellent, good, fair, poor) at 6 weeks, comparison of health to that before vaccination (better, about the same, worse) at 6, 13, 26, and 52 weeks, and belief that health problems might be related to vaccination at 6, 13, 26, and 52 weeks were also summarized. Data for monovalent and bivalent formulations from the same manufacturer were combined in this analysis and described using the shorter, non-brand specific acronym (BNT162b2 and mRNA-1273). Demographics, health impacts, and types of healthcare sought were also summarized for participants aged 18–54 who reported vaccination during pregnancy. Survey collection and data management have been described previously [3]. All analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute).

Ethics review

The V-safe platform used for data collection supported active surveillance for public health purposes. Registration in V-safe was voluntary, and registered participants opted-in to receive health surveys. This activity was reviewed by CDC, deemed not research, and was conducted consistent with applicable federal law and CDC policy (45C.F.R. part 46.102(1)(2), 21C.F.R. part 56; 42 U.S.C. §241(d); 5 U.S.C. §552a; 44 U.S.C. §3501 et seq.). V-safe participants were informed that their information is protected by administrative, technical, and physical measures that safeguard the confidentiality, integrity, and privacy of personal information, and that to the extent V-safe uses existing information systems managed by CDC, FDA, and other federal agencies, the systems employ strict security measures appropriate for the level of sensitivity of the data.

Results

Demographic characteristics

During December 14, 2020 - June 30, 2023, 9,609,282 persons or their proxy completed at least one V-safe survey (Table 1). A majority reported that they were female (62.2%) and were aged 25–64 years (69.2%) when they received their first vaccine dose. Only 3.2% of participants were children aged 0–17 years. Most participants reported receiving an mRNA vaccine as their first dose (95.0%). Of those not receiving an mRNA vaccine, most received the adenoviral-vector vaccine; few (<0.1%, n = 230) received the protein subunit vaccine. Among participants receiving a second dose for a vaccine that required a two-dose primary series, 99.8% received a vaccine from the same manufacturer as reported for dose 1. Among those younger than 18 years, 93.3% received BNT162b2 vaccine for their first dose. Non-Hispanic White (60.9%), Hispanic or Latino (11.2%), and non-Hispanic-Asian (6.9%) were the most common reported race and ethnicity categories for participants (Table 1).

Compared to the U.S. population who received at least one COVID-19 vaccine through May 10, 2023, demographics of the V-safe population differed in several ways. A lower proportion of V-safe participants were male (36.9%) compared with U.S. vaccinated population (47.4%). V-safe had a lower proportion of people aged less than 25 years (9.5% vs 21.1%) and aged 75 years or older (5.5% vs 8.8%). V-safe participants were more homogenous by race and ethnicity, with a larger proportion of non-Hispanic-White (60.9% vs 41.6%) and non-Hispanic-Asian (6.9% vs 5.2%) compared with the US vaccinated population.

Based on ZIP codes provided by V-safe participants, all U.S. states and territories were represented. Fig. 2 shows participation in V-safe as a percentage of vaccinated persons by state or territory. Participation ranged from 7.9% in New Hampshire to <0.1% in American Samoa, with a median of 3.7% of those vaccinated participating in V-safe (interquartile range, 2.8–4.4%).

Registrations during the study period

The number of V-safe registrations by month for active participants, defined as those submitting at least one health survey after reporting a vaccine dose, is shown by age group in Fig. 3. Most participants registered for V-safe within the first few months after vaccination was recommended for their age group [5]. For example, an increase in registrations was observed in March 2021, when COVID-19 vaccine recommendations were expanded to include all persons 16 years and older (Fig. 3A) [10]. Registrations for children aged 12–17 years peaked in May 2021, after all U.S. residents aged 12 years and older were eligible for vaccination (Fig. 3B).

Registrations in V-safe from December 14, 2020 through May 19, 2023, presented by month-year (blue bars) and % of vaccinated population (orange trendlines) for persons A) 18 years and older, B) 12–17 years, C) 5–11 years, and D) 6 months-4 years. Asterisks (*) indicate months during which a COVID-19 vaccine was first recommended for ages within each group (see Supplemental Table 1).

From December 2020 through January 2021, 3,039,110 participants registered in V-safe, about 10% of the 29,886,805 persons aged 16 and older in the United States who had received at least one COVID-19 vaccination [6]. Registrations decreased over time, with most occurring during the first months after vaccines were recommended for each age group. Registrations for younger age groups showed similar temporal trends. When the registration period ended on May 19, 2023, V-safe had enrolled 3.9% of the vaccinated adult population aged 18 and older in the United States (9,293,473 of 238,239,640 vaccinated) and 1.0% of the vaccinated pediatric population aged <18 years (315,809 of 31,779,895 vaccinated) [6].

Participation during the study period

Most V-safe participants completed their first self-reported survey after dose 1 (85.3%) or dose 2 (12.2%) (Supplemental Table 2). Overall survey participation dropped with each successive dose, especially after completion of the primary series of each vaccine. Variability in participation by race/ethnicity and age decreased with subsequent doses (Supplemental Table 3). Over 80% of V-safe participants reporting doses 5–7 identified their race and ethnicity as non-Hispanic White. For dose 1, 21.6% of the V-safe population was aged 65 years or older compared to doses 5–7, for which over half of V-safe participants were aged 65 years or older.

Most participants completed initial health surveys for each dose within the first week (days 0–7) after vaccination (Supplemental Table 4). The proportion of participants initiating surveys in the first week was highest for doses 1 (92.0%) and 2 (88.1), and lowest after dose 4 (56.6%).

Median numbers and median percents of surveys completed by dose and vaccine are shown in Supplemental Table 5. Median percent completion of daily surveys was 75.0% after receipt of each primary series dose of mRNA vaccines, 62.5% after receipt of each primary series dose of protein-subunit vaccine, and 75% after the single primary series dose of adenoviral-vector vaccine. A median of 50.0% of weekly surveys after dose 1 were completed for BNT162b2, 66.7% for mRNA-1273, 50% for NVX-CoV2373, and 62.5% for Ad26.CoV2.S vaccine. Greater proportions of dose 2 weekly surveys were completed for each vaccine.

Patterns of survey completion varied by dose number (Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5). Survey completion for vaccines with a 2-dose primary series was consistently lower in all age groups after dose 1. By week 3 after dose 1 of BNT162b2 and NVX-CoV2373 vaccines and by week 4 after dose 1 of mRNA-1273, survey completion was less than half of that observed at week 1. This drop-off in survey completion coincided with the recommended interval for receiving the second dose (at day 21 after dose 1 for BNT162b2 and NVX-CoV2373 vaccines, at day 28 after dose 1 for mRNA-1273 vaccine). Only participants who did not report another vaccine dose remained eligible for additional weekly surveys. Participant attrition was less pronounced among those who reported subsequent doses of mRNA or NVX-CoV2373 vaccines.

Health impacts reported after vaccination

Health impacts are shown in Tables 2 through 5 by age group. Findings are presented by vaccine and dose, together with numbers of surveys submitted by participants and proportions of surveys submitted among those eligible.

The proportion of participants reporting new symptoms or worsening medical conditions varied by week after the first dose of an mRNA vaccine by age group; however, this proportion was similar by weeks 2–6 for subsequent doses of mRNA vaccines and for the adenoviral-vector and protein subunit vaccine doses. The proportion of participants reporting new symptoms or worsening conditions increased at weeks 13, 26, and 52 (survey timepoints that asked the participant to report on a multi-week period of time) for all vaccines.

Most health impacts reported were associated with inability to perform normal daily activities or inability to attend work or school. These effects were reported most frequently during the first week after vaccination compared with later timepoints; they were consistent by age group, dose, and vaccine. Among vaccines requiring a 2-dose primary series (i.e. mRNA vaccines and the protein subunit vaccine), health impacts generally were reported more frequently following the second dose. Health impacts were reported less frequently after dose 2 of the adenoviral-vector vaccine compared to doses 1 and 3. Among adults, proportions of persons reporting health impacts decreased with each subsequent dose of an mRNA vaccine. Among children aged <18 years, health impacts generally increased with the second dose.

V-safe participants reported seeking healthcare infrequently by age group, dose, and vaccine. Among adult participants, 0.8–1.2% reported seeking medical care in week 1 post-vaccination after dose 1–6 of an mRNA vaccine, <0.1–1.4% after dose 1–5 of the adenoviral-vector vaccine, and 2.2–5.9% after dose 1–3 of the protein subunit vaccine. Healthcare received was most frequently telehealth/virtual health/email or outpatient or urgent care visits; seeking care in an emergency setting or hospitalization was rare. Emergency care was reported in week 1 post-vaccination by <1% of adult participants, excepting dose 3 of NVX-CoV2373 (5.9%) for which there were few reports (n = 34). Hospitalization in week 1 post-vaccination was reported by 0.1% or less for adult V-safe participants, excepting dose 7 of BNT162b2 (1.2%) for which few surveys were submitted (n = 82).

The proportion of participants seeking healthcare was similar among children and adults. Less than 0.3% were reported as receiving emergency care and 0.1% or less were hospitalized in week 1 post-vaccination. Children in the youngest age group (6 months to <5 years for BNT162b2, 6 months to <6 years for mRNA-1273) reported seeking healthcare during the first week after vaccination more frequently than other age groups consistently, regardless of dose number.

Additional information reported in V-safe surveys

Maternal vaccination and pregnancies reported after vaccination

There were 118,829 participants aged 18–54 years who reported receipt of a COVID-19 vaccine dose during pregnancy (Supplemental Table 6). Most participants reported a pregnancy at the time of dose 1 (n = 82,973) or dose 2 (80,594). Persons reporting pregnancy at the time of vaccination were predominantly aged 25–44 years (93.7%), and non-Hispanic White (69.8%). Most (97.0%) doses reported as received during pregnancy were mRNA vaccines.

Patterns of health impacts among pregnant V-safe participants were similar to those reported by the general adult V-safe population (Table 6). Health impacts were most commonly reported during the first week after vaccination for all doses. A greater proportion of pregnant women reported health impacts following a second dose of an mRNA vaccine compared with the first. Among participants receiving mRNA vaccines, health impacts were lower for weeks 2 and 3 post-vaccination compared to weeks 4–6 post-vaccination for the first dose. Proportions of pregnant women reporting a health impact were generally similar by weeks 2–6 for doses 2, 3, and 4. For each dose, health impacts were reported more frequently by pregnant women receiving the mRNA-1273 vaccine compared to the BNT162b2 vaccine. In week 1 after vaccination for each mRNA vaccine, 2.6% or less of pregnant women sought healthcare.

Other health characteristics reported after vaccination

A majority of participants (≥75%) responded that their current health was good or excellent at 6 weeks after vaccination (Supplemental Table 7). Reports of poor current health status were rare and more common after the first dose of an mRNA vaccine. When participants were asked to compare their current health to that before vaccination, a majority indicated that health was better or about the same (Supplemental Table 8). The proportion of persons who reported their health was worse compared to before vaccination and believed that their health problems may be related to vaccination was higher among adults at week 6 after dose 1 of an mRNA vaccine (17.9% of those completing a week 6 survey after dose 1 of BNT162b2, 10.5% after dose 1 of mRNA-1273) than after subsequent doses of mRNA vaccines. In persons <18 years of age, reporting of this belief was more similar by dose.

Discussion

To conduct active safety surveillance during the initial rollout of COVID-19 vaccines, the U.S. CDC developed V-safe to solicit self-reported information about post-vaccination symptoms and health impacts from a vaccinated population. More than 9.6 million people (3.6% of the U.S. COVID-19 vaccinated population) completed at least one V-safe survey. Compared to the U.S. COVID-19 vaccinated population, V-safe participants were more homogenous in respect to race and ethnicity; 61% identified as non-Hispanic White. Most participants were female (62%), adults (97%), and received an mRNA vaccine (95%).

The successful enrollment of a large sample and representation from all U.S. states and territories reflect efforts made to reach immunization program partners and the general public through promotion during CDC update calls, online COVID-19 resource pages, and social media channels. The proportion of vaccinated residents enrolled did vary substantially by geographic area of residence, suggesting that future outreach efforts may need to target underrepresented geographic areas. Participation in V-safe was highest at the beginning of the U.S. COVID-19 vaccination program, when over 10% of the U.S. vaccinated population registered for V-safe. Most participants (85%) completed their first survey after receipt of their first vaccine dose and completed the initial survey for each dose within a few days of vaccine receipt. The provision of V-safe promotional materials by vaccine administrators preferentially during early COVID-19 vaccination efforts may have influenced enrollment. Robust participation early in the COVID-19 vaccine rollout let CDC rapidly publish post-vaccination findings when little was known about reactogenicity in large populations [11-15]. Lower participation rates in later surveys could be associated with survey fatigue or a perception that reporting no longer provided useful data.

Health impacts were commonly reported after vaccination and were similar to those published previously. In the initial 6 months of V-safe, 12% and 32% of participants ≥16 years of age reported a health impact in the week following first and second doses of mRNA vaccines, respectively [16]. In the entire V-safe population through June 2023, health impacts were reported by 11% and 26% of adults following first and second doses of BNT162b2 vaccine and by 14% and 39% of adults following first and second doses of mRNA-1273 vaccine. Reactogenicity and health impacts remained more common following second than first doses and were more frequent among participants receiving mRNA-1273 compared with BNT162b2 vaccines. Health impacts were most commonly reported during the first week after vaccination, decreased from weeks 2 through 6, and then increased again after week 13. An increase in health impact reports in the 13-, 26-, and 52-week surveys was expected as these surveys assessed health over longer periods (6–26 weeks versus 1 week).

As in previous analyses of V-safe data [17-20], health impacts among children were relatively similar or slightly higher after receipt of an initial booster dose compared to the primary series. Health impacts were reported slightly less frequently among children <12 years old compared to those 12–17 years old and adults. The proportion of children reporting seeking healthcare following vaccination increased with decreasing age. A small number of V-safe participants reported hospitalization after vaccination. Because V-safe does not record reasons for health impacts or medical care and did not ask participants to only report those impacts or medical care related to vaccination, we cannot evaluate what proportion of reported health impacts or medical care (such as hospitalization) were related to vaccination. Overall, no unexpected findings were noted in analyses using the entire V-safe cohort compared with those using smaller, interim populations.

After the first dose of an mRNA vaccine, the proportions of individuals reporting that their health was worse than before COVID-19 vaccination or that their worsened health at 6 weeks after receipt of vaccine might be related to the vaccine was higher than after a second dose. This finding seems unusual, as previous studies have found that vaccinated persons reported more reactogenicity in the first week after receiving a second compared with a first dose of an mRNA vaccine [13,16]. However, it must be interpreted in the context of low survey completion rates after dose 1 among those who did not record another dose. For example, week 6 surveys were completed by fewer than 3% of eligible adult participants after a first dose versus 69% after a second dose of an mRNA vaccine.

Other groups have collected data similar to V-safe. AusVaxSafety (www.ausvaxsafety.org.au) is a national active vaccine safety surveillance system in Australia using post-vaccination surveys sent through text messages. Patterns of health impacts among the vaccinated Australian population (i.e. impact on work, study, or routine duties or receipt of medical care) are similar to those observed in V-safe. For example, higher proportions of participants reported health impacts following second and third doses compared to a first dose and among those receiving mRNA-1273 compared to BNT162b2 vaccine [21-23]. The majority of AusVaxSafety participants who reported an impact on work, study, or routine duties noted it for 1 day or less and attributed this impact to lethargy, headache, or joint pain. Reports of medical care following NVX-CoV2373 vaccine were more common compared to mRNA vaccines and receipt of medical care was reported infrequently, similar to patterns reported in V-safe. AusVaxSafety does not specifically ask participants for the reason why they accessed medical care; therefore, similar to V-safe, reported medical care may not be related to vaccine reactions. The consistency of major findings in similarly designed national surveillance systems operating in two countries suggests that emergency care or hospitalization after COVID-19 vaccination occurs infrequently.

Monitoring for post-vaccination symptoms among pregnant women was an important objective of V-safe, especially as pregnant women were not included in prelicensure clinical trials [24]. Assessment of pregnancy status in V-safe facilitated enrollment in CDC's COVID-19 vaccine pregnancy registry [4] and enabled CDC to monitor for post-vaccination symptoms and health impacts among the pregnant population. Local and systemic reactions reported to V-safe were similar between pregnant women and nonpregnant adults, with greater reactogenicity following second dose compared to first dose, consistent with a previous summary of preliminary V-safe data [24].

The V-safe surveillance system was designed to collect timely reactogenicity and health impact data from vaccinated U.S. residents. A strength of the V-safe implementation for COVID-19 vaccines is gathering data from millions of vaccinated persons. In contrast to the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS), V-safe includes data from persons who do not experience any post-vaccination symptoms or health impacts and the solicitation of specific common side effects. With V-safe data, CDC was able to assess the effects of a vaccination program on daily life activities among vaccine recipients for the first time.

V-safe has several important limitations. Although it included a large sample of COVID-19-vaccinated persons, participants in V-safe are not nationally representative of the US vaccinated population. Thus, the ability to generalize findings from a fairly homogenous population to underrepresented US groups is limited. Second, data are participant-reported, not verified by medical record review, and subject to reporting bias. For example, misclassification of vaccine manufacturer or pregnancy status may occur. Third, completion rates of surveys decreased with increasing weeks after vaccination. Fourth, participants may have been more likely to complete surveys if they experienced bothersome symptoms or health impacts after vaccination.

Conclusions

Descriptive findings from 9.6 million persons who provided post-COVID-19-vaccination data to V-safe from December 14, 2020, through June 30, 2023, are similar to those from earlier reports. Health impacts were reported more frequently after second compared with first vaccine doses and were most common in the week after vaccination. Health impacts in children <12 years were similar or slightly less common than in children 12–17 years old and adults. Emergency care and hospitalization post-vaccination were reported infrequently.

V-safe continues to collect post-vaccination symptoms and health impacts following vaccination for recently introduced vaccines, including COVID-19 and RSV. V-safe data may be particularly important for monitoring other vaccines introduced in emergency use situations, including future pandemics and uncommon but severe infections. We found that participation in V-safe was greatest at the onset of the COVID-19 vaccination program, when public interest in pandemic vaccines was high. Most participants registered and completed their initial health survey after receipt of their first COVID-19 vaccine dose. Participation decreased with time since vaccination and by number of doses received. The patterns suggest that survey data from future vaccination programs may accrue most quickly early during initial implementation, when data are most useful for policy makers and of greatest interest for the public. New strategies to maintain high levels of participation over time, such as social media campaigns and engagement with community partners and local and state health departments, should be explored, particularly when indications for or compositions of vaccines change.
