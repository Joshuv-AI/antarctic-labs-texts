Clusters of vascular aging manifestations predict incident cardiovascular events in the community
Authors: T. van Sloten, P. Boutouyrie, M. Abouqateb, RM Bruno, RE Climie, F. Zhu, Shuyue Yang, Ali Farzaneh, O. Guedj, N. Danchin, B. Pannier, S. Laurent, M. Kamran Ikram, F. Mattace-Raso, M. Kavousi, X. Jouven, JP Empana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vascular aging is the accumulation of functional and structural changes of vessels throughout life. While earlier studies have shown that single manifestation of vascular aging is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), it is unknown how the different manifestations of vascular aging cluster at the individual level, and whether individuals with different vascular aging patterns have different risk of progression to overt CVD. Here, we identify three patterns of vascular aging including healthy vascular aging (HVA), arteriosclerosis and atherosclerosis. Vascular aging manifestations were evaluated non-invasively by carotid ultrasound methods. The main and the validation analysis were conducted on 8360 and 2086 participants in two separate prospective cohorts. We find that arteriosclerosis and atherosclerosis compared to HVA clusters are independent predictors of CVD, and improve risk stratification for stroke among those at intermediate risk. Vascular aging evaluation may help identify those at increased risk of CVD.

Introduction

Vascular aging is the accumulation of functional and structural changes of vessels throughout life1. Vascular aging results from individual susceptibility and exposure to multiple risk factors across the lifespan1. Manifestations of vascular aging are heterogeneous and include structural and functional alterations reflecting atherosclerosis, arteriosclerosis, increased arterial wall stress, and arterial diameter enlargement2,3. Earlier studies have shown that these manifestations are associated with incident coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke4 Some of these manifestations share individual risk factors and pathophysiological mechanisms and co-exist in individuals1,5.

However, it is unknown how the different manifestations of vascular aging cluster at the individual level, and whether individuals with different vascular aging patterns have different risk of progression to overt CVD. Most previous studies focused on single manifestations of vascular aging, ignoring the heterogeneity of vascular aging manifestations5. High-resolution ultrasound of the carotid artery enables the measurement of the various vascular aging manifestations at the same time in large vessels non-invasively, offering opportunities to explore these parameters in large populations. These manifestations include atherosclerosis parameters, i.e., presence of plaques and higher carotid intima-media thickness (IMT), (2) arterial stiffening parameters, i.e., lower distensibility coefficient (DC), and higher Young’s elastic modulus (YEM), local pulse wave velocity, beta stiffness index and local pulse pressure, and (3) arterial remodeling parameters, i.e., larger arterial diameter2–4.

Therefore, the main objectives of the study were: (1) to identify the patterns of vascular aging, as quantified by carotid ultrasound, at population level and (2) to examine how these clusters are associated with incident CVD and all-cause mortality. These questions were addressed in the Paris Prospective Study III (PPS3), and externally validated in the Rotterdam study. In an exploratory analysis, we further evaluated the potential added value of the identified clusters of vascular aging to predict CHD or stroke in addition to traditional risk factors, in individuals at moderate risk of CVD.

Results

In PPS3, after excluding participants with prevalent CVD (n = 315), missing data on carotid ultrasound (n = 838) and covariates (n = 171) or follow-up status (n = 473), a total of 8360 participants were included in the analytic sample (Fig. 1). We identified three clusters of vascular aging (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Cluster 1, labelled as healthy vascular aging, included 3995 (47.8%) participants and was characterized by relatively low prevalence of carotid plaque, low carotid IMT, low carotid artery stiffness (i.e., high DC, low YEM, low local pulse wave velocity, low beta stiffness index and low pulse pressure) and small carotid diameter. Cluster 2, labelled as vascular aging characterized by increased arteriosclerosis, included 2109 (25.2%) participants and was characterized by high carotid artery stiffness (i.e., low DC, high YEM, high local pulse wave velocity, high beta stiffness index, and high pulse pressure). After an average follow-up of 8.75 years (95% CI: 8.58–9.08), 108 incident stroke events, 218 CHD events, and 192 deaths occured, respectively. The unadjusted Kaplan–Meier curves show heterogeneity in the survival rates free of stroke, CHD, stroke and CHD combined and all-cause mortality according to the clusters (Fig. 2A–D). The unadjusted and adjusted HRs for the study outcomes by cluster status are reported in Table 3. In multivariable analysis and compared with cluster 1, cluster 2 (HR 1.76, [95% CI, 1.06–2.94]), but not cluster 3 (HR 1.57, [95% CI, 0.94–2.62]), was associated with a higher risk of stroke. In addition, cluster 3 (HR 1.53, [95% CI, 1.08–2.16]), but not cluster 2 (HR 1.38, [95% CI, 0.96–1.98]), was associated with higher risk of CHD. Cluster 2 (HR 1.51, [95% CI, 1.12–2.04]) and cluster 3 (HR 1.48, [95% CI, 1.11–1.98]) were equally related to a higher risk of CHD and stroke combined. Similarly, both cluster 2 (HR 1.50, [95% CI, 1.02–2.21]) and cluster 3 (HR 1.48, [95% CI, 1.01–2.17]) were equally associated with higher all-cause mortality. In these models and as shown in supplementary Table S3, being male, age, smoking status, LDL and HDL cholesterol, and antihypertensive medications were related to the different outcomes. Internal validation

Bootstrapping analysis indicated that the 3 clusters were highly stable with a bootstrapped distance of 0.995, 0.991, and 0.997 between original cluster 1, 2, and 3 and their corresponding generated clusters. Further, after bootstrapping, in multivariable analysis, cluster 2 and cluster 3 were significantly associated with each outcome, with effect size similar to those found in main analysis (Table S4).

Sensitivity analyses

In PPS3, accounting for multiple imputations or for competing risk by death provided consistent associations between clusters of vascular aging and incident cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality (Table S4). The results of the analyses further adjusted for blood pressure values are reported in the supplementary material (Table S5).

External validation

In the Rotterdam study, 2086 participants free of CHD and stroke and with all available carotid ultrasound data were analyzed. Every Rotterdam participant was assigned to one of the three clusters of vascular aging identified in PPS3, suggesting their generalisability across populations (Supplementary Fig. 3). The distribution of the participants characteristics overall and across the clusters is reported in Table S6. The mean age was 64 (SD 7.5) and 60.6% were women. Overall, participants from the Rotterdam study were older, had more comorbidities and risk factors at higher level than in PPS3. However, the distribution of risk factors and comorbidities across the clusters of vascular aging were similar to what was observed in PPS3. After a median follow-up of 18.9 [12.4–19.5] years, 234 CHD, 183 stroke, and 718 deaths occurred, respectively. As in PPS3, the unadjusted Kaplan–Meier curves show that survival free of stroke, CHD, stroke, and CHD combined and all-cause mortality was lower among participants from cluster 2 or cluster 3 as compared to participants from cluster1 (Supplementary Fig. 4A–D). Compared to cluster 1, cluster 2, and cluster 3 were related to incident stroke, CHD, CHD and stroke combined, and to all-cause mortality (Table S7).

Exploratory analyses in PPS3

In those at moderate risk of CVD, addition of the clusters of vascular aging significantly improved the discrimination for stroke by 7.8% (95% CI: 0.031–0.17) using the published SCORE2 risk equation and by 2.2% (95% CI: 0.007–0.061) using the published US pooled risk equation, respectively (Table 4). No significant improvements were seen for CHD or all-cause mortality. Discussion

In a community-based study involving 8360 adults with a median follow-up of 8.5 years, three clusters of vascular aging, determined by carotid artery ultrasound, were identified: (1) a cluster that predominantly reflected healthy vascular aging; (2) a cluster that predominantly reflected arteriosclerosis; and (3) a cluster that predominantly reflected atherosclerosis. Compared with the healthy vascular aging cluster, the cluster characterized by arterial stiffening was associated with higher risk of incident stroke, and the cluster characterized by atherosclerosis with higher risk of CHD. External validation in the Rotterdam study showed consistent findings.

Previous community-based studies have investigated the co-occurrence of some manifestations of vascular aging. In the Framingham Heart Study cohort, the concomitant presence of atherosclerosis (presence of coronary artery calcification), high arterial stiffness (high carotid-femoral PWV), and poor cardiovascular health was associated with a higher risk of CVD, whereas their absence was associated with a lower risk6. Other studies evaluated the joint contribution of arterial diameter and arterial stiffness7, and the relative contribution of central pulse pressure and arterial stiffness with incident CVD8. We expand the results of previous findings by conducting a simultaneous analysis of 10 carotid-echotracking parameters reflecting several manifestations of vascular aging of the large vessels. The differential distribution of established risk factors across these clusters together with the independent associations of the clusters with incident stroke or CHD events and all-cause mortality, support the clinical relevance of the identified clusters of vascular aging. This is further supported by the external validation analysis showing consistent findings between PPS3 and the Rotterdam study III despite differences in the population characteristics between the two studies. The Rotterdam study III took place 10 years before PPS3, comprised of older participants (6 years older on average) with more frequent comorbidities, medication use and higher value of vascular aging parameters.

There are several mechanisms supporting the independent association between the identified clusters and the outcomes. We speculate that stiffening of elastic arteries (for which the carotid artery may serve as a proxy) may lead to CVD via reduced coronary perfusion (contributing to heart damage) and increased pulsatile pressure and flow transmitted to the microcirculation (contributing to brain damage)9. Carotid atherosclerosis may contribute to athero-tromboembolic stroke on the one hand, and on the other hand can be viewed as a marker of coronary atherosclerosis explaining its association with CHD10,11.

The current findings may help clinicians to identify group of patients who are at higher risk of CHD, stroke, or mortality according to their patterns of vascular ageing as assessed non-invasively. The clinician may adapt the monitoring and the management of the cardiovascular health of the patients assigned to the at risk clusters. For the patient, the knowledge of his assignment may help to support or change his health behaviors. The current findings may also have implications for individualized CVD risk prediction. Additional imaging testing, such as coronary artery calcium score or carotid plaque assessment by ultrasound can be considered for those at moderate CVD risk, according to current primary prevention risk stratification guidelines12. However, these are atherosclerosis-specific biomarkers, ignoring the potential relevance of arterial stiffness biomarkers. Previously, addition of biomarkers of atherosclerosis, such as coronary calcium score, carotid IMT, or ankle brachial index failed or only modestly improved the discrimination of the revised Framingham risk score for future stroke prediction13. In the present study, however, among those at low to moderate risk, adding the clusters of vascular aging representing arterial stiffness in addition to traditional risk factors, significantly improved the discrimination capacity of the risk prediction for stroke by almost 8%. In those individuals, intensive anti-hypertensive treatment, notably high dose renin angiotensin system blockers (i.e., [angiotensin-converting enzyme] inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker) or calcium channel blockers, or even new antidiabetics such as sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors may be considered2,14,15. These drugs may reduce arterial stiffness not only through blood pressure lowering, but also independently of blood pressure reduction, that is, likely through long-term arterial remodeling16. Still, larger and longer-term RCTs targeting vascular aging are needed. Importantly, this multidimensional carotid imaging should not be interpreted as replacing routinely measured risk factors and primarily blood pressure, but as a complementary approach for CVD risk evaluation.

Strengths of this study include the state-of-the-art carotid artery imaging, prospective design, use of a large community-based study population, and external replication of the main findings in a different community-based prospective cohort. The present study also has several limitations. First, without employing methods of causal inference and their accompanying assumptions, we cannot derive conclusions about causality. Second, only data on hospital CVD events were available in PPS3, which may have led to some missed events. This is particularly true for stroke events that do not necessarily lead to hospitalization. Additionally, the relatively small number of CVD events in PPS3 limited the ability to test for real difference in the association of clusters 2 and 3 with the study outcomes. Third, carotid artery parameters of vascular aging were only available at baseline, precluding exploring the patterns of change in vascular aging over time and their potential association with CVD. Fourth, the clustering analysis did not standardize for age although it differed between the healthy vascular aging cluster and the remaining two clusters. This standardization could identify people of similar biological age with distinct vascular aging. That could be of value for future studies, particularly those with longitudinal carotid artery measurements, to identify people with distinct patterns of carotid artery aging independent of biological aging. Fifth, ultrasound features of plaque vulnerability, such as plaque composition including plaque echodensity, total plaque burden, and thickness, were not examined. Sixth, carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) was not measured in the PPS3 study, so the possible added value of the carotid parameters measured on top of cfPWV could not be examined. Seventh, we acknowledge that IMT is not a marker of atherosclerosis per se17. However, the coexistence of higher levels of carotid IMT and higher prevalence of carotid plaques in cluster 3 as compared to cluster 2 in the present study suggest this is likely characteristic of atherosclerosis rather than arteriosclerosis. Eighth, the results of the sensitivity analyses further adjusting for blood pressure measures, should be interpreted with caution due to the collinearity with pulse pressure already included in the calculation of the clusters of vascular ageing manifestations. Nineth, using SCORE2 or the US pooled risk equation as single input variables, limits model flexibility and may overestimate the contribution of the vascular ageing clusters, although analyses based on locally fitted equations yielded consistent findings. Contrary to association analysis (e.g., HRs estimates), discrimination analysis (e.g., C-index estimate) is not sensitive to collinearity. This explains the apparent discordance between the unsignificant HRs estimates and the improved C-index when further adjusting for blood pressure. Tenth, we acknowledged that the SCORE2 and the US pooled risk equation were not designed to predict stroke risk. Still, a recent analysis indicates that specific stroke risk algorithms did not perform better than the US pooled risk equation in white populations (the vast majority of the PPS3 study)18. Finally, the main and validation study populations included participants who were mostly of Caucasian origin, thus the results may not be generalizable to other ethnicities.

In conclusion, we have identified three clusters of vascular aging, including one cluster that predominantly reflects healthy vascular aging, another that reflects arteriosclerosis, and a third that reflects atherosclerosis. These clusters were independently associated with incident stroke and CHD events and all-cause mortality. Awaiting for additional validation studies, these findings open opportunities for developing more specific CVD preventive strategies.

Methods

Study populations

PPS3 is a longitudinal cohort study on novel markers for phenotypes of CVD that included 10,157 participants (NCT00741728). The design and main objectives of PPS3 have been described previously19. In brief, individuals aged between 50 and 75 years were recruited at the Centre d’Investigations Préventives et Cliniques (IPC), a large preventive medical center in Paris (France), between June 2008 and June 2012. The IPC is one of the largest preventive medical centers in France and conducts free medical examinations (20,000–25,000 examinations/year) among all working and retired employees and their families. The IPC is subsidized by the French National Insurance System for salaried workers. All participants underwent a complete clinical examination coupled with standard biological tests. A self-administered questionnaire was obtained regarding socioeconomic status, lifestyle, personal and family medical history, including history of CVD, current health status, and use of medication. Sex was self-reported in the questionnaire. The Ethics Committee of the Cochin Hospital (Paris, France) approved the study.

Carotid artery parameters of vascular aging

Carotid artery parameters of vascular aging were determined using a high-resolution echotracking system (Artlab, Esaote, Maastricht, the Netherlands), as described previously19,20. All measurements were obtained by trained vascular technicians unaware of the participants’ clinical status, and centrally analyzed in a core-lab (Supplementary material). Briefly, we used a 10 MHz 128 transducer linear array probe and performed the measurements on a 4 cm segment of the right common carotid artery (1 cm proximal to the bifurcation) throughout the cardiac cycle for 6 s while plaque screening was performed bilaterally on the whole common, internal, and external carotids. The system allows real-time radiofrequency signal analysis with operator-independent determination of carotid diameter, distension, and mean IMT.

We determined the following carotid artery parameters according to consensus guidelines21: (1) atherosclerosis parameters, i.e., presence of plaques and higher IMT, (2) arterial stiffening parameters, i.e., lower DC, and higher YEM, local pulse wave velocity, beta stiffness index and local pulse pressure, and (3) arterial remodeling parameters, i.e., larger arterial diameter. Carotid DC represents arterial stiffness (the lower the DC, the greater the stiffness); a higher YEM represents greater stiffness of the arterial wall material; local pulse wave velocity is the velocity with which the arterial pulse wave travels at the level of the carotid artery (a higher pulse wave velocity reflects greater arterial stiffness); and a higher SI represents greater stiffness and takes into account the nonlinear relation between pressure and carotid artery diameter21.

Carotid plaque was defined as a focal thickening encroaching into the carotid lumen of more than 1.5 mm (as measured from the intima-lumen interface to the media-adventitia interface) or at least 50% of the surrounding IMT. Carotid IMT was measured along a plaque-free segment of the common carotid artery during end-diastole. IMT was defined as the distance between the lumen-intima and media adventitia interfaces of the far (posterior) wall. The carotid DC was calculated according to the following equation: ΔLCSA/LCSA ∙ PP, where LCSA is diastolic lumen cross-sectional area, ΔLCSA is change in lumen area, and PP is local pulse pressure estimated from the carotid distension waveform. YEM was calculated as 3 ∙ (1 + LCSA/WCSA)/DC, where WCSA is wall cross-sectional area. Local carotid pulse wave velocity was estimated from the Bramwell and Hill equation22. Beta stiffness index was calculated as Ln(Ps/Pd)/(Ds-Dd)/Dd, where Ps is local systolic pressure, Pd local diastolic pressure, Ds systolic diameter, and Dd diastolic diameter. From IMT and carotid diameter, lumen diameter (LD) was calculated using the following formula: LD = carotid diameter-(2 ∙ IMT) in mm. Circumferential wall stress was then calculated according to Laplace’s law as P*(r/w), where P is transmural mean arterial pressure, r is lumen radius, and w is wall thickness. The reproducibility of carotid artery stiffness measurements has been reported previously23.

Study outcome

Information on any hospitalization for CVD events during follow-up was collected on a biennial questionnaire and was validated by an event validation team who was blinded to baseline data and who critically reviewed hospital discharge records, as previously described in ref. 24. Non-fatal CHD events included acute coronary syndrome (i.e., unstable angina, non-ST and ST-elevation myocardial infarction), hospitalized angina pectoris requiring coronary revascularization procedures, including coronary artery bypass graft or percutaneous coronary interventions. Hospitalized stroke included ischemic, hemorrhagic, transient ischemic attack, and undetermined stroke. All stroke events had brain imaging data available (magnetic resonance imaging and cerebral tomodensitometry). Incident CVD and all-cause mortality data were obtained until September 30th, 2020. All-cause mortality status was obtained from the mortality records at the National Institute of Statistics and Economic Studies (INSEE).

Covariates

Self-administered questionnaires were used to obtain information on sex, education level, smoking behavior (catergorised as never, former, and current), and medical history. We divided education level into three categories: (1) low (no or primary education); (2) intermediate (secondary education), and (3) high (higher education or university). Fasting glucose and lipid levels were measured as part of the IPC visit, as described previously19. Diabetes was defined by use of glucose-lowering medication and/or a fasting blood glucose ≥7 mmol/L. Blood pressure was recorded using an Omron 705 C oscillometric device and an appropriate sized cuff after 10 min of supine rest. Hypertension was defined by a blood pressure of ≥140/90 mmHg and/or use of antihypertensive medication. Prior CVD was defined by a self-reported history of stroke, myocardial infarction, and/or angina pectoris.

Validation cohort study: the Rotterdam study

The Rotterdam Study (RS) is an ongoing prospective, population-based cohort study comprising 17931 men and women aged ≥40 years25. The study includes 4 different cohorts (RS I-IV), who are examined in-person every 3–5 years in a dedicated research facility, and who are followed-up continuously through automated linkage with health care providers, both regionally and nationally. The overall response rate of RS is 65%. For this study, we used data from the third examination of the original RS cohort (RS-I-3; 1997–1999) during which carotid ultrasound measures were obtained. Carotid artery parameters of vascular aging were determined using a duplex scanner (ATL Ultramark IV, Advanced Technology Laboratories, Bethel, Washington, operating frequency 7.5 MHz) connected to a vessel wall movement detector system and an automatic device (Complior; Artech Medical, Pantin, France) that measures the time delay between the rapid upstroke of the feet of simultaneously recorded pulse waves in the carotid and femoral arteries, as has been described elsewhere26. Carotid IMT, carotid plaque presence, carotid PWV, carotid distensibility, and mean pulse pressure were the carotid parameters available for this analysis. Their measurements are detailed in the Supplementary material. Incident CVD and all-cause mortality data were obtained until 01.01.2015. The Rotterdam Study has been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Erasmus MC (registration number MEC 02.1015) and by the Dutch Ministry of Health, Welfare and Sport (Population Screening Act WBO, license number 1071272-159521-PG). The Rotterdam Study Personal Registration Data collection is filed with the Erasmus MC Data Protection Officer under registration number EMC1712001. The Rotterdam Study has been entered into the Netherlands National Trial Register (NTR; www.trialregister.nl) and into the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP; https://www.who.int/clinicaltrials-registry-platform) under shared catalogue number NTR6831. All participants provided written informed consent to participate in the study and to have their information obtained from treating physicians.

Statistical analysis

Clusters of vascular aging

Hierarchical clustering using Ward distance was employed to identify patterns of vascular aging. All the vascular aging parameters described above were used to determine the clusters. We aimed to maximize the intra-cluster similarities while minimizing inter-cluster similarities. The vascular aging parameters were first processed by dimensionality reduction (principal component analysis) to correct for multicollinearity. In total six dimensions were retained, explaining 98.37% of the total variance (see supplementary material). The resulting dimensions were used as input for hierarchical agglomerative clustering via an algorithm with Euclidean distance measures and Ward’s linkage criterion27 The optimal number of clusters (see supplementary materials and Table S7) was chosen on the basis of clinical (i.e., the number of participants and events by cluster) and statistical (i.e., we allowed the number of clusters to vary between 2 and 6 and the selected number of clusters corresponded to the one with the highest rates of clustering validity indices among the 26 calculated using the NbClust R package, NbClust Version: 3.0.1 and R 3.6.3 for Mac OS.

Main analysis

The baseline characteristics of the population by clusters of vascular aging were compared using Pearson chi-square test and ANOVA for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Unadjusted incidence rates of stroke, CHD, stroke and CHD combined and all-cause mortality by cluster of vascular aging were quantified using Poisson regression model and reported per 1000 person- year and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). In addition, the incidence of stroke, CHD, stroke and CHD combined and all-cause mortality by cluster of vascular aging were plotted on Kaplan–Meier curves using time-to-event as the time scale and compared using the log-rank test. The associations between the clusters of vascular aging and incident stroke, CHD, and stroke combined, and all-cause mortality were examined in separate Cox proportional hazards model using time-to-event as the time scale, using cluster 1 as the reference category. The proportional hazards assumption was checked using Schoenfeld residuals test and graphical inspection of the residuals.

All analyses were adjusted for age, sex, education level, smoking, diabetes mellitus, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, use of lipid-modifying and antihypertensive medication and pulse rate measured at study recruitment.

Internal validation

In PPS3, we tested the stability of the generated clusters and their respective association with the outcomes using bootstrapping. A total of 1000 samples were generated using sampling with replacement. In each generated sample, 3 clusters were built using the same vascular aging parameters as in the main analysis; the distance between the 3 generated clusters and the 3 initial clusters from the main analysis was used to label the generated clusters, and this distance was averaged across the 1000 iterations (Jackard similarity coefficient). This coefficient ranges from 0 to 1, and an average score above 0.85 classically indicates the stability of the clusters28. Then, we estimated bootstrapped hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs by averaging the HRs calculated in each of the 1000 generated samples.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses tested the robustness of the association between the identified clusters of vascular aging manifestations and study outcomes in PPS3. To evaluate the potential impact of missing covariates and missing ultrasound parameters, Cox analysis were repeated using multiple imputations (number of sets = 5). We used predictive mean matching for quantitative data, logistic regression imputation for binary variables, polytomous regression imputation for unordered categorical data (factor > 2 levels), and proportional odds model for ordered categorical data. Competing risk by death was assessed using Fine and Gray model and sub-distribution HRs were estimated29 Lastly, we repeated the Cox analyses after further adjustment for blood pressure measures, including systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and mean arterial blood pressure.

External validation

The aim of the external validation was to test the transportability of PPS3 clusters of vascular aging in a population with different characteristics. Participants from the Rotterdam study were, therefore, assigned to the PPS3 clusters of vascular aging manifestations using the following steps. After scaling the Rotterdam data (i.e., each variable has the same unit), we calculated the Euclidean distance between each Rotterdam participant and the center of each of the three PPS3 clusters. We used the shortest distance to assign each Rotterdam participant to a cluster. We then evaluated the associations between these clusters and incident stroke, CHD, stroke and CHD combined as well as all-cause mortality in Cox proportional hazards model using time-to-event as the time scale, and adjusting for the same covariates as in PPS3.

Exploratory analyses

In PPS3, we explored the possible clinical utility of the clusters of vascular aging and estimated their added value for CVD risk stratification on top of established risk factors contained in the SCORE2 risk30 and the US recalibrated pooled risk equation31 among individuals at moderate risk. We considered the 2 risk equations as published (e.g., as single pre-computed variables) and as locally fitted (e.g., reconstructed in PPS3 using the individual components of each risk equation). In both approaches, the clusters of vascular aging were incorporated as two dummy variables, including cluster 2 (arteriosclerosis) and cluster 3 (atherosclerosis) as compared to cluster 1 (healthy vascular aging). Difference in discrimination capacity (Harell’s C index)32 was evaluated on nested models (e.g., SCORE 2 or pooled risk equation vs. SCORE2 or pooled risk equation+vascular ageing clusters in dummy variables). The average difference and its 95% CI were computed using boostrapping (n = 1000) on paired difference among the same individuals.
