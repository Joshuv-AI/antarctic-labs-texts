Disruption and recovery in physical and mental health, body mass index and smoking during the COVID-19 pandemic: a trend analysis of US BRFSS data from 2016 to 2022
Authors: Khushbu Balsara, Varun Kotharkar, Panagis Galiatsatos, Norma Kanarek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe COVID-19 pandemic caused widespread disruptions to population health, affecting key risk factors such as physical and mental health, smoking status and body weight. While short-term impacts have been studied extensively, long-term trends and recovery patterns remain unclear.MethodsThis study used data from the Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System covering 2016–2022. Weighted proportions for health outcomes were calculated, and trends were analysed using locally weighted scatterplot smoothing and Kendall’s tau correlation. Bootstrapping was used to estimate CIs.ResultsDuring the pandemic, individuals reporting 14+not good physical health days increased significantly (τ=0.7238, p<0.0001). Mental health also deteriorated, with 14+not good mental health days increasing (τ=0.7263, p<0.0001). Smoking prevalence declined (τ=−0.5536, p<0.0001) while those who ‘never smoked’ increased (τ=0.6024, p<0.0001). Obesity prevalence rose significantly postpandemic (τ=0.6632, p<0.0001).ConclusionsThe findings highlight the pandemic’s profound and differential impact on physical health and persistent worsening of not good mental health days and body mass index indicating obesity. The decline in smoking prevalence suggests a positive behavioural shift during the early years of the pandemic. Targeted public health interventions are needed to address these challenges and promote recovery.

Introduction

The SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) pandemic afflicted countries worldwide, causing overwhelming health consequences for the global populace and disruptions in everyday life, healthcare and health behaviours.1 In addition to a surge in COVID-19 attributable deaths, research has identified that the pandemic has played a leading role in disruption of population health, especially in key health outcomes such as smoking and obesity by body mass index (BMI), which are the leading primary prevention risk factors for chronic disease and physical health.2 As with any new disease, research has highlighted the importance of monitoring trends over time to better understand the broader and long-range implications on population health.3 Such trends can assist in learning further about the disease’s characteristics, including its consequences, the vulnerable populations most affected and its spread and extinction.4

Studies conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic have shown additional behavioural changes in population health,3 which include increases in sedentary lifestyle,5 social isolation,6 disrupted sleep patterns7 and declines in mental health.8 While the short-term effects of the COVID-19 pandemic are well documented, the long-term trends and consequences of these risk factors remain largely unexamined. Existing studies provide a baseline for tracking significant shifts and placing disease patterns in perspective. While such studies have promoted the generation of hypotheses and filled a knowledge gap regarding the change in longer trends, we still need to examine recovery of health indicators post-COVID-19. Health indicators, such as obesity indicated by BMI and not good mental health days (MHDs) (eg, categories being 0 days, 1–14 days and 14+ days), have been worsening since 2001.9 10

Not good physical health days (PHDs) and not good MHDs are key measures of health-related quality of life (HRQOL), both physical and psychological health.11 For several chronic diseases, including lung cancer, coronary heart disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, smoking status continues to be the primary risk factor,12 13 while weight measured by BMI is frequently regarded as a crucial metric for assessing the lack of exercise and poor diet profiles. Obesity and its consequences are closely linked to higher rates of morbidity and mortality and lower HRQOL.14 Moreover, more than one-third of premature, preventable deaths are attributed to these two leading risk factors.15 These are impactful surveillance markers of population health that have shown variation over time.

These indicators16 were selected because they capture physical, mental and health behaviour domains that are especially sensitive to public health stressors such as COVID-19. The pandemic introduced widespread disruption in daily routines7, healthcare access and health behaviours3 5, all of which have direct implications for perceived physical and mental health2, smoking behaviour and BMI fluctuations5. As such, these measures offer a comprehensive lens through which to assess both the disruption and early signs of recovery in the US population health.

This study aims to examine temporal trends using Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) data17 in population-level mental and physical health, smoking behaviour and BMI across pandemic, a population stressor,16 periods: prepandemic (2016–2019) and postpandemic periods (2020–2022) a population stressor. Time trend analysis will let us document both incremental changes and longer-term effects, which make these outcomes especially appropriate for evaluating the evolving public health landscape of the COVID-19 pandemic. In summary, our analysis will focus on the nature of prior and concomitant trends exploring the disruption and recovery for not good PHDs and not good MHDs, and two major risk factors, smoking status and BMI categories, including overweight and obesity.

Methods

Data collection

Data used in this study were obtained from the BRFSS, a large-scale survey conducted by states and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The dataset includes weighted responses from individuals across each state in the USA, covering the period from 2016 to 2022.

BRFSS is a nationally representative, state-based telephone survey system that collects detailed information on US adults’ health behaviours, chronic conditions and access to care. Each year, over 400 000 interviews are completed across all 50 states, the District of Columbia and participating territories, enabling robust, population-level analyses.

Data preprocessing

To ensure data consistency and prepare it for analysis, we performed several preprocessing steps. First, we reduced the data to include only health outcomes, demographics and survey weights. The outcome variables of interest included self-reported physical health (_PHYS14D), mental health (_MENT14D), smoking status (_SMOKER3) and body mass index (_BMI5CAT). The dataset also included weights (_LLCPWT), which account for the complex survey design and ensure representativeness of state populations and ultimately the nation.

In preprocessing the BRFSS data, approximately 10.8% of records were removed due to missing or non-informative responses across several key variables. Specifically, responses such as 7 (refused), 9 (don’t know/missing) and NA were considered analytically uninformative and could bias results if included. The largest proportion of removed data was due to missing BMI category (9.24%), followed by smoking status (5.06%), race (2.24%), state (2.23%), PHDs (2.21%), MHDs (1.82%) and sex (0.07%). After excluding these responses, the final analytical dataset comprised 2 766 352 observations.

We derived new variables for race (combining categories from _RACEGR3 and _RACEGR4) and sex (combining categories from SEX, BIRTHSEX and SEXVAR), handling missing values by removing records with non-informative responses (eg, ‘refused’, ‘don’t know’). Body mass index (_BMICAT) originally consisted of four categories: underweight, normal weight, overweight and obese. However, due to the low number of underweight cases each year and to streamline the analysis, the underweight and normal weight categories have been combined into a single group.

Underweight individuals (category 1) represent a very small proportion of the BRFSS sample, often <2%, leading to unstable estimates. Merging with normal weight is a conceptually coherent choice—both groups represent individuals not classified as overweight or obese. The combination enhances statistical power, ensures adequate cell size and avoids overfitting or misleading patterns due to rare categories. We acknowledge that this may limit the ability to detect distinct trends or health risks specific to underweight individuals. Nonetheless, this simplification is both methodologically sound and practically helpful for interpreting weight-related trends.

BRFSS surveys have used the same questions from year to year, assuring comparability in annual prevalence rates.

Weighted and proportion calculations

To account for the survey design and derive representative estimates, we calculated demographically weighted proportions of the population for each health variable: not good PHDs, not good MHDs, smoking status and BMI (_MENT14D, _PHYS14D, _SMOKER3, _BMI5CAT). The BRFSS variables include categories for self-reported physical and mental health (0 days, 1–14 days, 14+ days), smoking status (current smoker every day, current smoker some days, former smoker, never smoked) and BMI status (under/normal weight, overweight, obese). This was achieved by grouping interview data by month and year and applying the survey weight (_LLCPWT) to calculate weighted proportions for each health variable category. For example, the weighted proportion for smoking status (_SMOKER3) was calculated by summing the weights for each smoking category, divided by the total weight for each month. Weighted prevalences assure sex-age-race/ethnicity adjustment.

Additionally, to quantify uncertainty, bootstrap CIs were computed for each monthly proportion. A bootstrapping procedure with 1000 resamples was used to estimate 95% CIs for each category within the variables, allowing us to measure the variation in our weighted proportion estimates.

Statistical analysis

To assess trends over time, we applied a smoothed curve using the locally weighted scatterplot smoothing technique to the weighted proportions of each health outcome. This approach helped smooth monthly fluctuations and highlight broader trends, particularly surrounding the onset and recovery phases of the COVID-19 pandemic. The smoothing parameter (frac=0.1) was guided by the need to balance local trend detection with overall curve stability. Given that the dataset spanned 84 monthly time points (2016–2022), a frac of 0.1 ensured that each local regression incorporated approximately 8 months of data capturing short-term to medium-term fluctuations while avoiding overfitting. We conducted a sensitivity analysis using a range of frac values (0.05–0.7). The results confirmed that frac=0.1 preserved key trend features such as inflection points around 2020–2021 while maintaining a smooth and interpretable trajectory. In contrast, higher values oversmoothed meaningful variation, and lower values introduced visual instability. We also evaluated candidate smoothing parameters (0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7) using leave-one-out cross-validated squared prediction error and complementary visual inspection to select the optimal level of smoothing. Thus, frac=0.1 represented an optimal trade-off between bias and variance for our analysis.

Further, to evaluate changes in trends specifically related to the pandemic period, we performed a Kendall’s tau rank correlation test. This non-parametric test was used to measure the strength and direction of association between the time periods and the proportion of individuals in each health category. Kendall’s tau is particularly suited for detecting monotonic (ie, consistently increasing or decreasing) trends over time and does not assume a linear or normal distribution, making it appropriate for time series of survey-based proportions. Separate tests were conducted for the periods before 2020, 2020 and later, and for the full period from 2016 to 2022. This allowed assessment of whether the pandemic had any statistically significant impact on the trends in physical health, mental health, smoking and BMI status categories.

All data preprocessing and statistical analyses were conducted using Python (V.3.11.5).

Visualisation

The trends in health outcomes were visualised using line plots for each variable of interest (_MENT14D, _PHYS14D, _SMOKER3, _BMI5CAT) with weighted proportions plotted over time. These plots provided insights into the disruption and recovery patterns in population health during the pandemic. In addition to the trend line of monthly averages, the CIs from the bootstrapped proportions described below were generated to estimate the uncertainty in our estimates.

Bootstrapping and Kendall’s tau in the analysis

Bootstrapping for weighted proportions and CIs

Bootstrapping is a resampling technique used to estimate the distribution of a statistic by sampling with replacement from the original dataset.18 19 In this analysis, bootstrapping was employed to estimate the CIs for weighted proportions of various health indicator categories (ie, physical health, mental health, smoking status and BMI status). Given the complex survey design of the BRFSS, bootstrapping provides a robust way to derive empirical estimates of uncertainty around population-level proportions. For each year, the dataset was split, and weighted proportions for the categories within each health indicator were calculated. The key steps in the bootstrapping process were as follows:

Resampling: For each bootstrap iteration, we randomly sampled 5000 observations with replacement from the data for that year.

Resampling: For each bootstrap iteration, we randomly sampled 5000 observations with replacement from the data for that year.

Weighted proportion calculation: In each resampled dataset, the weighted proportions were computed by summing the survey weights for each category (eg, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0 for _MENT14D) and dividing by the total sum of weights in the resampled dataset. This step has the advantage of controlling for demographic factors of age, race/ethnicity and sex with the intent of reducing potential confounding with time.

Weighted proportion calculation: In each resampled dataset, the weighted proportions were computed by summing the survey weights for each category (eg, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0 for _MENT14D) and dividing by the total sum of weights in the resampled dataset. This step has the advantage of controlling for demographic factors of age, race/ethnicity and sex with the intent of reducing potential confounding with time.

CI construction: After 1000 bootstrap iterations, we calculated the 95% CIs by taking the 2.5th percentile and the 97.5th percentile from the distribution of bootstrap estimates.

These non-parametric, bootstrapped CIs provide insight into the variability of our weighted proportion estimates, accounting for potential uncertainties introduced by the survey’s complex design. The use of bootstrapping in this context ensures robust CIs even when there may be non-normality or heteroskedasticity in the data.

Kendall’s tau for temporal trend analysis

Kendall’s tau is a non-parametric correlation coefficient used to measure the ordinal association between two variables.20 In our analysis, we used Kendall’s tau to assess the monotonic relationships between month-based time (in terms of the survey date) and the weighted proportions for each health category (eg, _MENT14D, _SMOKER3, _PHYS14D and _BMI5CAT). To assess temporal trends in health outcomes and behaviours across survey months and years, we used Kendall’s tau, a non-parametric rank correlation coefficient. Unlike Pearson’s correlation, Kendall’s tau does not assume linearity or normality and is particularly well-suited for detecting monotonic relationships in time series data, including those that may exhibit non-linear or plateauing patterns. This makes it ideal for evaluating changes in population-level proportions across discrete survey waves. We applied Kendall’s tau separately for the full study period (2016–2022), as well as for the pre-2020 and post-2020 periods to capture potential shifts associated with the COVID-19 pandemic. Specifically, Kendall’s tau was applied to detect significant trends over the following periods: Study Period (2016–2022): The full study period encompassing both the prepandemic and postpandemic years, used to assess overall trends in each health indicator. Pre-COVID-19 (2016–2019): The years preceding the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, used to evaluate baseline trends and patterns in health indicators before pandemic-related disruptions. During COVID-19 and later (2020–2022): The years spanning the pandemic and beyond, used to analyse the impact of COVID-19 on health indicators, including whether trends worsened or showed signs of recovery. To compare the pretrend (2016–2019) and posttrend (2020–2022) periods using Kendall’s tau, we can examine both the direction (positive or negative) and magnitude of the tau statistic for each category. A stronger (higher magnitude) tau value indicates a more pronounced increasing monotonic trend in that period. Comparing the early and later tau values can help us determine whether trends strengthened, weakened or reversed post-2020. For example, a shift from a weak trend in the earlier period to a stronger trend in the later period (eg, ‘_MENT14D, 14+ days’) indicates an intensification of the trend. Similarly, trends that were not significant in the earlier period but became significant later highlight the impact of events like COVID-19.

The steps involved in the Kendall’s tau analysis were as follows:

Ordinal mapping: To evaluate trends, monthly dates were graphed to their ordinal form, enabling temporal comparison. Weighted proportions for each health category were calculated for each time point (month-year).

Ordinal mapping: To evaluate trends, monthly dates were graphed to their ordinal form, enabling temporal comparison. Weighted proportions for each health category were calculated for each time point (month-year).

Kendall’s tau calculation: For each health category, the correlation between time (as an ordinal variable) and the weighted proportion of individuals in each category was computed. This analysis was done for the full period, as well as separately for pre-2020 and 2020 and later periods to capture shifts in trends.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study, as it used publicly available, anonymised data from the BRFSS.

The majority of respondents were aged 35–64 years (48.0%); of female sex (54.8%); and of non-Hispanic, white race/ethnicity. Trends in not good PHDs

Figure 1 depicts temporal trends in not good PHDs across the study periods. Pre-COVID-19 (2016–2019), the prevalence of 14 or more not good PHDs rose significantly (τ=0.2145, p=0.0315), while no significant trends were detected for zero not good PHDs. During COVID-19 and later (2020–2022), the proportion of individuals reporting 14 or more not good PHDs increased sharply (τ=0.7238, p<0.0001), while those reporting zero not good PHDs declined significantly (τ=−0.7619, p<0.0001). Across the entire study period (2016–2022), despite each time segment showing significant positive trends, the overall period did not display a statistically significant trend.

Trends in not good MHDs

As shown in figure 2, the proportion of individuals reporting zero not good MHDs declined steadily across all periods. Pre-COVID-19 (2016–2019), this decline was already significant (τ=−0.6765, p<0.0000), and it continued during COVID-19 and later (2020–2022) (τ=−0.6515, p<0.0000). Across the study period (2016–2022), Kendall’s tau revealed an overall significant negative trend for zero not good MHDs (τ=−0.7625, p<0.0001) indicating a steady decline in the proportion of individuals reporting not good MHDs. Meanwhile, the proportion of individuals reporting 1–14 and 14+ not good MHDs increased significantly across all periods (τ=0.6713, p<0.0000; τ=0.7263, p<0.0000, respectively).

Trends in smoking status

Figure 3 illustrates trends in smoking status across all study periods. The proportion of individuals classified as non-smokers in the past 30 days increased significantly across pre-COVID-19 (2016–2019) (τ=0.2791, p=0.0076), during COVID-19 and later (2020–2022) (τ=0.2500, p=0.0417), and for the study period (2016–2022) (τ=0.6024, p<0.0001). Conversely, daily smoking prevalence declined significantly only during COVID-19 and later (2020–2022) (τ=−0.4736, p=0.0001). Smoking on some days also declined significantly pre-COVID-19 (τ=−0.3277, p=0.017) and during COVID-19 and later (τ=−0.3371, p=0.0056).

Trends in BMI

Trends in BMI status categories (figure 4) show a significant increase in obesity prevalence across the study period (2016–2022) (τ=0.6632, p=0.0000) reflecting increasing levels of obesity across the population across time segments. Pre-COVID-19 (2016–2019), obesity prevalence rose significantly (τ=0.4255, p=0.0000), accompanied by a reduction in the normal/underweight category. During the COVID-19 and later (2020–2022) period, this trend persisted, with a sharp increase in obesity prevalence (τ=0.4889, p<0.0001), a decline in the normal/underweight (τ=−0.6661, p=0.0000) and overweight categories (τ=−0.3902, p=0.0012).

Discussion

The findings from this study illustrate significant changes in population health over the 2016–2022 period, particularly during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. The sharp deterioration in both mental and physical health following the onset of the pandemic in 2020 reflects the broad public health consequences of the pandemic crisis. Increased stress, reduced healthcare access,21 social isolation6 and lifestyle changes2 5 22 23 likely contributed to these declines since COVID-19 for each study indicator, though some 2019–2022 effects were in progress earlier.

Physical health

The postpandemic increase in either category of any not good PHDs is alarming. This trend can likely be attributed in addition to COVID-19 itself, to limited access to healthcare services, reduced physical activity during lockdowns and the potential long-term effects of COVID-19 infections. The pandemic disrupted preventive care and chronic disease management, further straining healthcare systems and leaving many individuals unable to access necessary services for any disease management. A recent study in Germany highlighted that such lifestyle changes during the pandemic had prolonged impacts on physical health, contributing to a steady rise in poor health days.5 22 These findings are consistent with other studies,5 23 emphasising the combined effects of inactivity, stress and delayed care in worsening physical health outcomes.

In hindsight, 14 or more not good PHDs were already on the increase from 2016 to 2020, a trend that the pandemic neither mitigated nor reversed. Additionally, the moderate category of not good PHDs (1–14 days) has also increased since 2020, reflecting a broader population-wide impact rather than a decline isolated to only the most severe category. This persistence suggests that the pandemic amplified existing challenges in health systems and lifestyle patterns, leaving perhaps a lasting mark on physical health.

Mental health

The decline in zero not good MHDs was profound and worsened since 2016 and through the onset of the pandemic into 2022. This finding aligns with reports from the WHO, which identified a 25% global increase in anxiety and depression during the first year of the pandemic.24 Similarly, studies indicate that even mild COVID-19 infections can result in long-term cognitive impairments, such as memory loss and attention deficits, lasting up to 18 months postinfection.25 A study of cigarette and electronic cigarette users found a portion increased their motivation to quit and successful quitting of both products due to concerns about health effects of smoking compounded by COVID-19. However, the amount smoked for non-quitters increased during COVID-19, potentially an effect of heightened pandemic stress addressed by increased exposure to nicotine. A major source of stress for Americans reported by the American Psychological Association26 27 accounts for worsening mental health as measured by reported stress. Rising trends are found in younger adults, those of non-white race/ethnicity and females.

The concurrent increase in poor MHDs (both 1–14 and 14+ days) highlights the pandemic’s substantial toll on mental well-being. Stressors such as political elections,2628 social isolation,6 economic disruptions29 and reduced access to mental health services9 are key contributors to these trends.30 31 Upwards trends in depression from 2005 to 2015 were noted around employment and income declines brought about by the 2008 global recession, concomitant with deaths due to suicide.29 A study of cigarette and electronic cigarette users found a portion increased their motivation to quit and successful quitting of both products due to concerns about health effects of smoking compounded by COVID-19.24 However, the amount smoked for non-quitters increased during COVID-19, potentially an effect of heightened pandemic stress addressed by increased exposure to nicotine.32

Furthermore, the prepandemic increase in mental health indicators suggests that societal factors like economic instability and healthcare inequities were already negatively impacting mental well-being before the pandemic as long ago as 2011–2012.9 21

Often, public health interventions target particular diseases, but mental health is multifactorial and may require more generalised population-based interventions. One example is stress reduction through exercise, irrespective of cause, which the study concluded has a variety of indications and wide applicability.33

Smoking status

In positive public health news, the pandemic period was associated with a decline in smoking prevalence, with a significant increase in individuals classified as ‘never smokers’. This trend aligns with findings from studies in England, which observed an increase in quit attempts during the early stages of the pandemic, driven by heightened awareness of respiratory health risks.34 35 Public health campaigns emphasising the dangers of smoking, particularly in the context of COVID-19, may have further encouraged cessation efforts.36

Additionally, changes in daily routines, such as remote work, job loss and the closing of schools, may have reduced social triggers for smoking or fostered greater consideration for others in shared indoor spaces. These factors likely contributed to the acceleration in smoking decline observed after 2020 (τ=−0.4735, p<0.0056). Despite the known association of smoking with stress management, the pandemic appeared to highlight the recognition of smoking and dire COVID-19-related outcomes, highlighting the complex interplay between health awareness and behavioural responses during a global health crisis. The long-term consequences of this should be explored.

Body mass index

The increasing prevalence of obesity during the pandemic highlights the substantial impact of widespread lifestyle changes. Even before the onset of COVID-19,37 obesity rates were steadily rising,37 but pandemic-related factors such as reduced physical activity38 due to lockdowns, stress-induced overeating and disruptions in food systems39 appear to have accelerated this trend. Early pandemic reports estimated an average weight gain of approximately 15 pounds in many populations, illustrating the challenges of maintaining healthy habits during prolonged periods of restricted movement and heightened stress. 40 41 Further, food insecurity was heightened for some population segments such as the newly unemployed,36 though not enough to significantly change reported BMI in individuals of normal and underweight in this study.

The association between obesity and severe COVID-19 outcomes further underscores the public health implications of these findings. Research by the CDC has established that individuals with obesity face a higher risk of hospitalisation, severe illness and mortality from COVID-19.42 This reinforces the urgency of addressing obesity not just as a long-term health concern, but also as a critical risk factor during respiratory infectious disease outbreaks.

Data from 2022 indicate a slight improvement in the prevalence of individuals classified as underweight or within a normal weight range, though the trend is not statistically significant. While this is encouraging, it remains unclear whether this represents a sustained positive shift or a short-term adjustment. These trends call for tailored interventions that combine weight management programmes with strategies to promote physical activity and healthier dietary habits,37 particularly in a postpandemic context where new challenges to public health may emerge in response to quarantine measures.43

The lack of attenuation in associations after adjusting for obesity and smoking suggests that these risk factors do not fully explain the observed differences in PHDs and MHDs. This may indicate that other determinants such as persistent socioeconomic stressors, reduced access to healthcare, pandemic-related social isolation and exacerbation of pre-existing mental or physical health conditions play a more substantial role in driving these outcomes during and after the pandemic. It is also possible that the temporal worsening of PHDs and MHDs reflects broader systemic effects, including disruptions in preventive care and shifts in social support networks, which are not captured by obesity and smoking measures alone. Future research incorporating a wider set of behavioural, psychosocial and environmental variables may help clarify these pathways.

Limitations

Through a cross-sectional survey, time trends were calculated, tobacco and obesity may affect outcomes of physical or mental health. In addition to adjustment for race, age and sex, adjustment for obesity and smoking had no further effect on not good PHDs and not good MHDs (data not shown). Additionally, the underweight category (<2% of the BRFSS sample) was merged with normal weight to improve statistical power and avoid unstable estimates. While this limits our ability to detect trends specific to underweight individuals, it is a methodologically sound approach for interpreting weight-related trends.

BRFSS surveys are state-based and thus there are differences between methodologies or local circumstances which may advocate for not combining state data, though common protocols for data collection and actual questions attempt to hold the effects of administration to small state-to-state differences.

Overall, these findings suggest that the COVID-19 pandemic significantly disrupted both not good PHDs and MHDs, while also influencing lifestyle behaviours such as smoking and BMI. Public health efforts aimed at recovery should prioritise mental health recovery and resilience as suggested by the surgeon general.44 Additional chronic disease management and lifestyle interventions before and during an epidemic, with attention to concurrently maintaining or improving mental health, may mitigate the short-term and long-term effects of the pandemic.

Conclusions

Indicators of both not good MHDs and obesity worsened postpandemic and had not recovered by 2022. These findings suggest that beyond the direct effects of the virus, COVID-19 has had lasting implications on the physical and mental health of the US population. To mitigate these effects, targeted public health interventions such as expanding access to community-based mental health services, promoting healthy lifestyle programmes and reinforcing preventive care should be prioritised. Continued surveillance is warranted to monitor progress in physical and mental health recovery and to sustain the positive trends observed in smoking cessation.
