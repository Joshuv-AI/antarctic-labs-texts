Associations of complement proteins and immunoglobulins with cognitive impairment in type 2 diabetes mellitus: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yujing Mi, Huimin Yan, Shanshan Dong, Zining Liu, Meiying Zhang, Jing Zhao, Guochao Yan, Yanmin Li, Shichao Gao, Yan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a global health concern associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment. While the complement system and immunoglobulins, crucial components of the immune system, have been implicated in the pathogenesis of both T2DM and cognitive decline, their specific clinical associations remain unclear. This study aims to investigate the association between inflammatory markers, specific complement proteins, immunoglobulins, and cognitive impairment in T2DM patients.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted involving 242 participants aged over 40 years from the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University between March 2022 and April 2023. Participants were divided into the control (n = 120), T2DM (n = 45), and T2DM with cognitive impairment (T2DM&CI) (n = 77) groups. Cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Blood samples were analyzed for fasting blood glucose, lipid profiles, complement proteins, and immunoglobulins. Statistical analyses included non-parametric tests, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and linear and logistic regressions adjusted for covariates (age and gender).ResultsSignificant differences were observed among the groups in cognitive function scores, blood glucose, lipid levels, and inflammatory markers, complement proteins, and immunoglobulins. The T2DM & CI group exhibited lower MoCA scores and higher total cholesterol (TC), complement factor B (CFB), and immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels compared to the T2DM group. Correlation analyses revealed that elevated CFB and IgA were negatively correlated with cognitive function, whereas IgM showed a positive correlation. Following covariate adjustment, CFB remained independently associated with the presence of cognitive impairment in T2DM patients.ConclusionCognitive impairment in T2DM is significantly associated with altered immune-inflammatory profiles, specifically alternative complement pathway activation (elevated CFB) and specific immunoglobulin shifts (increased IgA, decreased IgM). CFB represents a promising potential biomarker for the early identification of cognitive risk in T2DM patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02341-8.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), Cognitive impairment, Inflammatory markers, Complement proteins, Immunoglobulins

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), the most prevalent form of diabetes, is a significant global health concern, affecting over 90% of diabetes patients worldwide [1]. T2DM is not only characterized by insulin resistance and hyperglycemia but also by its profound impact on cognitive function. T2DM has been identified as a significant risk factor for cognitive impairment, with diabetic patients facing a notably elevated risk of mild cognitive impairment, ranging from 1.5 to 2.5 times higher than that of non-diabetic individuals [2, 3]. The cognitive decline associated with T2DM manifests in various domains, including memory, learning, orientation, judgment, and understanding, leading to a diminished capacity for daily living activities, social interactions, and work capabilities. In severe cases, this cognitive impairment may necessitate professional care, thereby imposing substantial economic burdens on both families and society [4]. Consequently, identifying novel biomarkers for early diagnosis and intervention in cognitive impairment among T2DM patients is of paramount importance for improving their quality of life.

The complement system, a critical component of the innate immune system, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of both T2DM and cognitive impairment. The activation of complement pathways is observed in a variety of diseases, including cognitive impairment and T2DM [5]. The classical pathway, which involves proteins such as C1q, C3, and C4, has been linked to neuroinflammation and synapse loss in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [6, 7]. Furthermore, the levels of complement proteins have been correlated with the incidence of T2DM, suggesting that complement-mediated inflammation may contribute to the pathophysiology of T2DM [8].

In addition to the complement system, the broader context of immune system activation and inflammation is increasingly recognized as a common denominator in cognitive impairment. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers indicative of immune activation have been demonstrated to predict the progression of cognitive impairment over time [9]. Immunoglobulins, particularly immunoglobulin A (IgA) and immunoglobulin G (IgG), are also intricately involved in the pathogenesis and progression of T2DM and its associated complications, including cognitive dysfunction. These immunoglobulins contribute to the inflammatory milieu, influence insulin resistance and metabolic dysregulation, and may even exert protective effects against certain complications [10, 11].

This study aims to investigate the associations between inflammatory markers, complement proteins (C1q, C3, C4, and the B factor), immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, IgM, IgE), and cognitive impairment in T2DM patients. By conducting a comprehensive analysis of a cross-sectional cohort, we aim to delineate the potential of these biomarkers in diagnosing and predicting cognitive impairment in T2DM. Our findings could provide valuable insights for the development of early intervention strategies, thereby potentially mitigating the cognitive decline associated with T2DM and improving the overall prognosis for affected individuals.

Method

Study participants

This study was a cross-sectional research project conducted from March 2022 to April 2023. It included type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients admitted to the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University, comprising both inpatients and outpatients aged over 40 years. Diabetes was diagnosed according to the American Diabetes Association criteria [12], specifically defined as meeting at least one of the following thresholds: fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L during a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%. To ensure the homogeneity of the study population, patients with Type 1 diabetes mellitus, gestational diabetes, or other specific types of diabetes were strictly excluded based on a comprehensive evaluation of their clinical history, age of onset, and routine clinical and laboratory assessments. Furthermore, the general exclusion criteria for the study were: (i) thyroid dysfunction, parathyroid gland or other endocrine-related diseases, or autoimmune diseases; (ii) major medical illness, such as severe anemia, serious heart disease, liver, and kidney dysfunction or other systemic diseases; (iii) acute infection or acute complications of diabetes; (iv) malignant tumors and autoimmune diseases; (v) a serious history of neuropsychology, mental illness, alcohol and drug abuse; (vi) inability to complete the neuropsychological evaluation. The T2DM patients were divided into two groups based on cognitive function: the group with cognitive impairment and the group without cognitive impairment. Health examiners from the physical examination center of our hospital during the same period served as the healthy control group. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University (No. 2023S00423). Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants. This study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Clinical and biochemical measurements

Patient clinical information, including gender, age, education, and cognitive function, was collected by professional physicians. Cognitive function was specifically evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA, version 8.1) [13]. Blood samples were drawn by an experienced nurse after a 12-hour overnight fast. The fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C) were measured using enzymatic methods. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (HCRP), serum amyloid A (SAA), complement C1q, complement factor B (CFB), immunoglobulin A (IgA), immunoglobulin G (IgG), immunoglobulin M (IgM), immunoglobulin E (IgE) were determined using immunoturbidimetry with a biochemical analyzer and corresponding assay kits (US Beckman AU5800 automatic chemistry analyzer, Beckman Coulter, USA). Platelet (PLT), neutrophil count (N#), lymphocyte count (L#) were measured using the sheath flow impedance method with a BC-6800 Automated Hematology Analyzer and corresponding assay kits (Beckman Coulter, USA). Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography with an ADAMS A1c HA-8380 Automated Hemoglobin A1c analyzer (Arkray, China). The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) was calculated using the formula (N# × PLT)/L#. The triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) was calculated using the formula Ln [TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2].

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics software (version 25.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), MedCalc software (version 17.0.4, Ostend, Belgium), and GraphPad Prism (version 8.1.0, GraphPad Software, Bethesda, MD, USA). Continuous variables with normal distributions were expressed as the mean ± SD, and comparisons among groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by the Dunnett test. For variables with skewed distributions, data were presented as the interquartile range [M (P25, P75)], and comparisons among groups were made using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables were expressed as n (%), and comparisons among groups were performed using the χ2-test. Linear regression analyses were used for continuous outcome variables (complement proteins, immunoglobulins, and cognitive function scores) to calculate the regression coefficient (β) and standard error (SE). Logistic regression analyses were employed for binary outcomes (T2DM with or without cognitive impairment) to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). The regression analyses with gender and age included as covariates. Receiver operating curves (ROC) analyses were conducted to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of the influencing factors. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics and cognitive function of study participants

A total of 242 participants were included in the study: 120 in the control group, 45 in the T2DM group, and 77 in the T2DM with cognitive impairment (T2DM&CI) group. Demographics and clinical characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. Significant differences were observed among the groups in terms of age, gender, education, and MoCA scores (P < 0.05). Clinical biochemical parameters, including HbA1c, FBG, TG, TC, LDL-C, N#, L#, TyG, SII, HCRP, and SAA, also exhibited significant differences across the groups (P < 0.05). Moreover, significant variations were found in complement proteins (C1q, C3, C4, CFB) and immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, IgM, IgE) among the groups (P < 0.05). The serum levels of complement proteins and immunoglobulins in control, T2DM and T2DM&CI groups are depicted in Fig. 1.

Compared to the control group, both the T2DM and T2DM&CI groups exhibited significantly higher age and levels of HbA1c, FBG, TG, LDL-C, N#, TyG, SII, HCRP, C3, C4, CFB, and IgA (P < 0.05), while showing lower MoCA score and levels of L#, C1q, and IgM (P < 0.05). Additionally, the T2DM group had increased SAA and decreased IgG levels, whereas the T2DM&CI group demonstrated increased TC and IgE levels (P < 0.05). Compared to the T2DM group, the T2DM&CI group showed significantly lower MoCA scores and higher TC, CFB, and IgE levels (P < 0.05).

Supplementary Table S1 further details the clinical characteristics of patients in the T2DM and T2DM&CI groups. The analysis revealed significant differences in age and gender distribution, with the T2DM&CI group being significantly older and comprising a lower proportion of males compared to the T2DM group (P < 0.05). However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding the proportion of patients with a university-level education or above, diabetes duration, or body mass index. Furthermore, the utilization profiles of various glucose-lowering therapies were highly comparable between the cohorts, indicating no significant differences (P > 0.05).

Associations of complement proteins and Immunoglobulins with clinical parameters

We hypothesized that levels of complement proteins and immunoglobulins would be associated with the indicators of hyperglycemia and hyperlipemia, and the univariate linear regression was utilized to examine our hypothesis. Detailed statistical results are presented in Tables 2 and 3. As shown in Table 2, C1q was positively correlated with LDL-C [β(SE) = 0.324(5.284), P = 0.021] and negatively correlated with HbA1c [β(SE) = -0.260(1.730), P = 0.013]. C3 showed positive correlations with TC [β(SE) = 0.351(0.027), P = 0.009], TyG [β(SE) = 0.273(0.024), P = 0.003], and HCRP [β(SE) = 0.298(0.005), P = 0.002]. C4 was positively correlated with TC [β(SE) = 0.407(0.008), P = 0.002], TyG [β(SE) = 0.256(0.007), P = 0.004] and HCRP [β(SE) = 0.390(0.002), P < 0.001], but negatively correlated with LDL-C [β(SE) = -0.263(0.012), P = 0.047]. CFB exhibited positive correlations with HbA1c [β(SE) = 0.369(1.910), P < 0.001], TC [β(SE) = 0.284(4.201), P = 0.024], TyG [β(SE) = 0.411(3.761), P < 0.001], and negative correlations with FBG [β(SE) = -0.285(1.303), P = 0.009] and LDL-C [β(SE) = -0.255(5.834), P = 0.046]. These associations remained statistically significant after adjusting for age and gender (P < 0.05).

Associations of complement proteins and immunoglobulins with cognitive function

To investigate the associations of complement proteins and immunoglobulins with cognitive function, as measured by MoCA scores, we estimated the relationships between these biomarkers and cognitive function. As shown in Table 4, IgM was positively correlated with the MoCA score [β(SE) = 0.181(0.857), P = 0.047], whereas CFB [β(SE) = -0.257(0.008), P < 0.001] and IgA [β(SE) = -0.148(0.251), P = 0.013] were negatively correlated with the MoCA score. No other significant associations were observed between complement proteins and immunoglobulins with cognitive function.

Univariate logistic regression analyses of clinical parameters in T2DM with cognitive impairment patients

Univariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify the factors influencing cognitive impairment in T2DM patients. As shown in Table 5, CFB was found to be a contributing factor to cognitive impairment (Model 1, OR = 1.017, 95% CI = 1.003–1.031, P = 0.018). This association remained significant after adjusting for age and gender (Model 2, OR = 1.023, 95% CI = 1.007–1.039, P = 0.005). The ROC analyses revealed that the area under the curve (AUC) of CFB for predicting cognitive impairment in T2DM patients was 0.625 (95% CI = 0.560–0.736, P < 0.05), as shown in Fig. 2. The sensitivity and specificity of CFB were 68.8% and 62.2%, respectively, at the optimal cut-off value of 224.4 mg/L.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we observed significant associations between complement proteins, immunoglobulins, and cognitive impairment in T2DM patients. While multiple inflammatory and immunological markers were found to differ across the patient groups, our findings lead to a clear conclusion: the specific activation of the alternative complement pathway (characterized by significantly elevated CFB) and distinct shifts in immunoglobulins (increased IgA and decreased IgM) are predominantly and independently associated with cognitive decline in T2DM. Notably, CFB was independently associated with cognitive impairment, suggesting its potential as an observational biomarker for early clinical identification.

Our findings revealed that FBG, TG, LDL-C, and the TyG Index were elevated in both the T2DM group and the T2DM&CI group compared to the healthy control group (Table 1). Several studies have highlighted the diagnostic value of the TyG index, which correlates significantly with other markers of insulin resistance [14], such as the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and the euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic clamp test [15]. The TyG index is also an effective screening tool for predicting glycemic control and insulin resistance in T2DM patients [16].

The levels of SII and HCRP were significantly elevated in both the T2DM group and the T2DM&CI group compared to the healthy control group (Table 1). This finding is consistent with previous research indicating higher inflammatory marker levels in T2DM patients, particularly those with cognitive dysfunction. The elevated SII in T2DM patients with cognitive dysfunction suggests a significant role for inflammation in both T2DM and its associated cognitive impairments [17]. Additionally, the presence of cognitive impairment in T2DM patients further exacerbates the inflammatory state, evidenced by elevated levels of other inflammatory markers such as interleukin-2 (IL-2), C-reactive protein (CRP), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [18].

Importantly, the relationship between cognitive impairment, diabetes management, and inflammation is likely bidirectional. On the one hand, systemic inflammation is a critical factor linking T2DM to cognitive impairment and dementia. The immune system, particularly microglia, plays a crucial role in cognitive decline. Pro-inflammatory cytokines released from microglia contribute to neuronal death and cognitive decline [19, 20]. In addition, chronic low-grade systemic inflammation is linked to domain-specific cognitive performance and cognitive decline in older adults [21]. On the other hand, cognitive decline can conversely compromise a patient’s ability to self-manage their diabetes. Impaired memory and executive function often lead to poor medication adherence, suboptimal dietary choices, and inadequate lifestyle control, which subsequently exacerbate hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and systemic inflammation [22, 23]. This vicious cycle highlights the necessity of early cognitive screening to prevent further deterioration of metabolic control.

Sustained increases in CRP can activate the complement system, enhancing the phagocytic capacity of phagocytes. In the context of neuropsychiatric diseases, the complement system may serve as a significant mediator of inflammation-related changes [24]. The complement system, composed of more than 30 proteins, participates in the host defense by regulating inflammation and can be activated through the classical, lectin, and alternative pathways [25]. We observed significant differences of C1q, C3, C4, and CFB among different groups (Table 1; Fig. 1). Specifically, serum levels of C1q were significantly reduced in both the T2DM group and the T2DM&CI group relative to the healthy control group. This reduction suggests an inhibition of the classical complement pathway in these patient cohorts. These findings align with previous studies indicating elevated serum C1q levels in individuals with cognitive impairment alone compared to those with T2DM alone or T2DM&CI [5].

C1q is the initiating factor that activates the classical pathway of complement and plays a role in synapse formation and pruning during central nervous system (CNS) development [26]. However, uncontrolled activation of C1q can lead to inflammatory responses and progressive damage within the CNS [27]. Although there is limited direct evidence implicating reduced serum C1q levels in the pathogenesis of T2DM or cognitive impairments, further research is warranted to establish a definitive link.

Our study also indicates that serum levels of CFB, C3, and C4 in the T2DM and T2DM&CI groups were significantly higher than in the control group (Table 1; Fig. 1). The serum CFB levels in the T2DM&CI group were significantly higher than those in the T2DM group, suggesting that activation of the alternative pathway may play a key role in T2DM-related cognitive disorders. Additionally, CFB demonstrated an inverse relationship with MoCA (Table 4), indicating that the activation of the alternative pathway may be critical in T2DM-related cognitive disorders.

CFB is critical in the activation of the alternative pathway, helping convert C3 into C3b, which contributes to opsonization and the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) [28]. C3b, CFB, and C5b-9 are involved in the release of inflammatory factors and the inflammatory response of the body, promoting target cell transport and facilitating the removal of immune complexes and cell damage or lysis [29]. C3, a pivotal component within the complement system, serves as a critical juncture for all three activation pathways, leading to the formation of C3 convertase, which catalyzes the cleavage of C3 into C3a and C3b. Subsequently, C3b undergoes further degradation into iC3b, C3c, and C3d, each with distinct immunological functions and roles in the inflammatory response [30]. However, our discussion on these complement pathways is somewhat speculative. It must be framed cautiously, as there is no direct mechanistic evidence linking CFB to cognitive impairment in this specific cross-sectional cohort. The elevated CFB levels observed might be a secondary reflection of the broader metabolic dysregulation rather than the direct causative agent of neurodegeneration. Therefore, while our data suggest that the alternative pathway might be implicated in T2DM-related cognitive disorders, further longitudinal and rigorous experimental studies are required to establish a definitive mechanistic link.

Our findings also suggest that hyperlipidemia may cause activation of the alternative pathway, as evidenced by positive correlations between C3, C4, CFB, and TC and the TyG index (Table 2). Research indicates that the complement system plays an essential role in the pathogenesis of diabetes mellitus and its complications. Elevated levels of C3 in circulation are linked to insulin resistance (IR), a critical factor in the development of metabolic diseases such as T2DM [31]. C3 can induce IR by promoting glucose uptake and triglyceride synthesis in adipose tissue [32] and is associated with increased macrophage infiltration into adipose tissue, exacerbating insulin resistance [33].

In the present study, we observed that patients with a level of CFB also exhibited greater cognitive dysfunction, elevated HbA1c, TC, TyG index, alongside lower levels of LDL-C (Tables 2 and 4). These findings suggest that dysregulated glucose and lipid metabolism may influence serum complement protein levels and activate the complement pathway. Previous research has established associations between adipsin, CFB, and metabolic disorders such as obesity and diabetes [34]. The activation of the alternative pathway, mediated by adipsin and CFB, contributes to an environment that promotes IR and leads to increased glucose production and reduced glucose uptake, reflecting the broader metabolic dysregulation observed in obesity and diabetes.

Through logistic regression analysis adjusted for gender and age, our study demonstrated that CFB remains a significant risk factor associated with the occurrence and progression of T2DM complicated by cognitive impairment (Table 5; Fig. 2). This finding suggests that serum CFB levels may be closely linked to the pathogenesis and development of T2DM and associated cognitive decline. T2DM is known to contribute to cognitive dysfunction through various mechanisms, including insulin resistance, β-cell dysfunction, and chronic inflammation, all of which are implicated in neuroinflammation and cognitive deterioration [35]. Additionally, individuals with T2DM face an increased risk of dementia, with certain treatments, such as metformin, showing potential in mitigating cognitive decline, thereby reinforcing the connection between neuropsychiatric disorders and metabolic dysregulation [36].

In this study, we found elevated levels of IgA in both the T2DM group and the T2DM&CI group compared to the control group (Table 1; Fig. 1). This finding is supported by evidence indicating that patients with T2DM may experience increased IgA levels, which are biomarkers for inflammation [37]. IgA levels were highly correlated with markers of inflammation and fibrinolysis in drug-naive individuals with T2DM [38]. IgA levels were inversely correlated with MoCA scores (Table 4), suggesting that IgA levels increase with the severity of cognitive impairment. This aligns with previous studies showing higher blood IgA levels in AD patients with cognitive dysfunctions [39].

Conversely, IgM levels were significantly reduced in both the T2DM group and the T2DM&CI group compared to the control group (Table 1; Fig. 1). Moreover, IgM exhibited a positive correlation with MoCA. IgM is the first immunoglobulin to be produced during B-cell ontogeny, followed by class-switching to IgG or formation of T-cell memory cells. Due to their pentameric structure, IgM antibodies have high avidity for binding to antigens such as Aβ or tau, facilitating their clearance [40].

We also found that the T2DM&CI group displayed significantly higher levels of IgE compared to both the T2DM and control groups (Table 1; Fig. 1). This is supported by evidence suggesting that T2DM can lead to systemic changes, including immune system alterations that might manifest as changes in immunoglobulin levels. While specific evidence linking T2DM directly to elevated IgE levels is limited, the overall context supports the notion that T2DM can lead to various systemic changes, including potential immune system alterations [41]. IgA, IgG, and IgE all exhibited positive correlations with HCRP. IgG demonstrated an inverse relationship with TC and a positive correlation with LDL-C, while IgE showed the opposite pattern (Table 3).

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing causal relationships between immune markers and cognitive impairment, and the interactions may be bidirectional. Second, the lack of neuroimaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers limits our ability to definitively attribute the observed cognitive deficits exclusively to diabetes. Third, while we rigorously accounted for key confounders, including age, gender, education, BMI, diabetes duration, and medication use, residual confounding from unmeasured lifestyle factors cannot be entirely ruled out. Finally, the single-center, predominantly Han Chinese cohort may restrict the generalizability of our findings. Future longitudinal, multi-center studies are needed to validate these results.

Conclusion

Our study identifies that altered immune-inflammatory profiles, specifically the activation of the alternative complement pathway and distinct immunoglobulin shifts, are significantly associated with cognitive impairment in T2DM patients. Elevated CFB and IgA, alongside decreased IgM, are closely linked to cognitive decline. Notably, CFB emerges as a potential independent biomarker for the early identification of cognitive risks. Future longitudinal and experimental studies are warranted to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms and explore potential therapeutic interventions.
