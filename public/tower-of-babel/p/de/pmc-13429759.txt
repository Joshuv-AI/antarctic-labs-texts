Treatment-resistant Depression in the Post-ketamine Era: Unmet Needs beyond Rapid Response
Authors: Alessandro Serretti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The introduction of ketamine and its S-enantiomer, esketamine, has been one of the most consequential advances in treatment-resistant depression (TRD) in recent decades, demonstrating that rapid and clinically meaningful antidepressant effects can occur within hours, even in patients who have not responded to conventional treatments. However, this advance has not resolved the clinical problem of TRD. Rather, it has shifted the field’s most important unmet needs from the induction of short-term symptom improvement to the management of what follows. The central questions now concern the post-response trajectory: how durable the initial benefit is, how relapse should be prevented, how maintenance treatment should be structured and eventually tapered, whether symptomatic improvement translates into functional recovery, which patients are most likely to benefit, why clinically useful predictive biomarkers remain elusive, and what the long-term safety and abuse-liability profile of repeated glutamatergic treatment will prove to be. This clinically oriented review examines these unresolved problems in the post-ketamine era, considers whether emerging rapid-acting antidepressants may address some of them, and argues that durability, functional recovery, patient selection, long-term safety, and implementation value, rather than acute efficacy alone, should now guide research priorities and bedside decision-making in TRD.

INTRODUCTION

Major depressive disorder is among the leading causes of disability worldwide, and a substantial fraction of patients do not achieve remission despite adequate treatment. The clinical importance of sequential non-response was made visible by Sequenced Treatment Alternatives to Relieve Depression, in which remission became progressively less likely across successive treatment steps and relapse remained common even among remitters [1]. Treatment-resistant depression (TRD), most commonly defined as failure to respond to at least two antidepressant trials of adequate dose and duration within a depressive episode, carries the highest disability burden of all depressive conditions and a disproportionate share of the health-care, productivity, and mortality costs of mood disorders [2-8]. The boundaries of the construct remain contested, and the estimated prevalence varies widely with the operational definition and setting. In a nationwide cohort of 176,132 adults with major depression, only 2.6% met strict TRD criteria over one year, whereas 26.4% failed at least their first antidepressant. Resistance was consistently associated with comorbid anxiety (odds ratio 1.88) and substance use disorders (odds ratio 1.73) [7,9].

These numbers conceal a deeper definitional problem that has shadowed the field for decades. “Treatment resistance” is not a unitary biological entity. It is an operational label, and what it captures depends on how many failed trials are required, how adequacy of dose and duration is judged, whether adherence has been verified, whether bipolarity and comorbidity have been actively excluded or treated, and whether psychotherapy and neuromodulation count. Some authors therefore prefer the broader notion of difficult-to-treat depression, which captures patients who may technically respond yet never achieve sustained, functional recovery [2,3,10]. Staging models grade resistance by the number and type of failed interventions, but they were built mainly to describe populations, not to predict the next best step for an individual, and they map only loosely onto the heterogeneous biology, chronicity, trauma exposure, medical burden, and comorbidity that drive poor outcome [7]. This conceptual looseness matters for everything that follows, because patients enrolled in rapid-acting-treatment trials, and those seen in clinic, are defined by exactly this contested label.

Against this long-standing therapeutic impasse, the demonstration that a single subanaesthetic infusion of the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist ketamine could produce antidepressant effects within hours, rather than over the weeks required by monoaminergic agents, was transformative [11-15]. The effect was first shown in a small proof-of-concept crossover study and then replicated in randomized trials in unipolar and bipolar treatment-resistant depression; in one two-site trial a single infusion produced a response in 64% of patients versus 28% with active placebo at 24 hours [13]. This overturned the prevailing assumption that antidepressant action must be slow, and the benefit extended to rapid reduction of suicidal ideation [12,16,17]. When intranasal esketamine, given with an oral antidepressant for TRD, was approved in 2019, a rapid-acting glutamatergic treatment entered routine practice for the first time [18,19]. For a field that had spent decades refining variations on monoamine reuptake inhibition, ketamine reframed what was biologically and clinically possible and catalysed an entire pipeline of rapid-acting candidates [20,21]. The esketamine development programme was deliberately built around the resistant population, but its evidence is more mixed than the approvals alone suggest. In a phase 2 dose-finding study, esketamine added to the patient’s antidepressant produced a significant ascending dose-response, with least-squares mean Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) differences versus placebo of −4.2, −6.3, and −9.0 points for the 28-, 56-, and 84-mg doses [18]. The pivotal phase 3 trial of flexibly dosed nasal spray added to a newly initiated oral antidepressant (TRANSFORM-2) then confirmed superiority over the oral antidepressant alone [22]. The programme was not, however, uniformly positive. The fixed-dose (TRANSFORM-1) and elderly (TRANSFORM-3) pivotal short-term trials did not meet their primary endpoints [23,24]. A recent systematic review judged esketamine’s adjunctive effect modest, comparable in magnitude to atypical-antipsychotic augmentation. The same review noted that most individual short-term randomized trials were negative or failed and that the long-term and abuse-liability picture remains incompletely characterized [25-27]. A further, cross-cutting caveat applies to the whole ketamine literature: the vivid psychoactive and dissociative effects of these drugs are difficult to mask, so functional unblinding and expectancy may inflate apparent efficacy. The field has tried to mitigate this problem with active-placebo comparators such as midazolam, but has not eliminated it [13,27]. A systematic review of placebo response in ketamine and esketamine trials likewise found that placebo improvement accounts for a substantial share of total observed symptom change [28]. These caveats qualify the magnitude and generalizability of the benefit; they do not erase it. More recently, a placebo-controlled trial in 378 adults showed esketamine retains efficacy as monotherapy, with least-squares mean MADRS differences versus placebo at day 28 of −5.1 and −6.8 points for the 56- and 84-mg doses. This decouples the rapid-acting benefit from an obligatory oral antidepressant and broadens its potential clinical positioning [29]. A second approved indication, granted in 2020 for depressive symptoms in adults with major depression and acute suicidal ideation or behaviour, rests on the ASPIRE trials and reflects the same rapid mechanism applied to a psychiatric emergency, where the speed of effect is itself the therapeutic point [30-32]. What this body of work establishes is narrower than is sometimes claimed but still consequential: a rapid, clinically meaningful acute response is reproducibly achievable for a substantial subset of patients who had exhausted conventional options. What it conspicuously does not settle is everything that happens after the induction phase.

The thesis of this review is therefore specific: ketamine did not solve TRD, but it changed the dominant clinical question from whether a response can be induced quickly to whether it can be made durable, safe, equitable, and functionally meaningful. Acute efficacy is no longer the only rate-limiting step; durability, relapse prevention and maintenance, functional recovery, patient selection, biomarkers, and long-term safety are. This is a clinically oriented narrative review rather than a systematic review or treatment guideline. It emphasizes pivotal trials, meta-analyses, long-term extension studies, real-world evidence, and recent methodologically informative work, while treating effect estimates as context-bound because definitions, concomitant treatments, comparators, and follow-up periods vary substantially across studies. DURABILITY OF RESPONSE

The main clinical limitation of ketamine is that its benefit, however rapid, is often transient. After a single intravenous infusion, antidepressant effects typically peak within 24−72 hours and then fade over days to a few weeks in most responders. In a randomized dose-ranging study, 48% of 60 patients responded and 34% remitted three days after a single infusion, but among those who responded to the standard 0.5 mg/kg dose a large proportion had relapsed within two weeks [33]. This transience drives every subsequent unmet need: a treatment that works within hours but loses effect within weeks forces the question of how to sustain benefit.

Repeated dosing prolongs benefit but introduces its own uncertainties. Serial infusions extend response for many patients, yet the effect of each dose can attenuate over a course, and the optimal number and spacing of treatments to consolidate remission are not established. A systematic review of 22 studies found no single strategy that reliably extended benefit beyond the typical median of two to four weeks to relapse [34,35]. Age and baseline severity modulate the temporal pattern of response without necessarily changing its ultimate durability [36]. Importantly, repeated-dose efficacy should not be inferred simply by extrapolating from single-infusion trials. In the pragmatic KARMA-Dep 2 randomized trial of hospitalized patients with depression, serial adjunctive ketamine infusions were not significantly superior to serial midazolam on depressive symptoms, cognition, cost-effectiveness, or quality-of-life outcomes, and most patients and raters guessed allocation correctly [37]. Although that trial was not a pure outpatient TRD trial, it is methodologically important because it places repeated ketamine in a real-world treatment context and underscores how active comparators and blinding integrity can change the interpretation of effect. Esketamine and racemic ketamine differ in pharmacokinetics, potency at the NMDA receptor, and route, and although both are effective (a meta-analysis of 36 trials found a pooled standardized mean difference versus placebo of −0.63 for depression severity; [38]), the comparative durability of intranasal esketamine versus intravenous racemic ketamine remains an open and clinically important question [14]. In short, the field can often induce a response; it cannot yet reliably keep one.

This transience also bears on how ketamine compares with the older standard for severe and resistant depression, electroconvulsive therapy (ECT). In the pivotal head-to-head ELEKT-D (electroconvulsive therapy versus ketamine in patients with treatment-resistant depression) trial ketamine was non-inferior to ECT for non-psychotic treatment-resistant depression (55.4% versus 41.2% response), and wider comparisons suggest broadly comparable acute efficacy for many non-psychotic patients [39]. This finding should not be read as a general replacement claim: ELEKT-D excluded psychotic depression, and the result is not directly transferable to psychotic, catatonic, nutritionally or medically urgent, or otherwise life-threatening depressive presentations in which ECT retains a distinct clinical role. The more clinically decisive question is durability after the acute course, and here the evidence is unsettled. In the same trial’s naturalistic six-month follow-up, fewer ketamine responders than ECT responders had relapsed (34.5% versus 56.3%). Taken at face value this favours ketamine, but continuation treatment was left to the treating clinician rather than standardized, so the comparison cannot be read as a durability head-to-head [39]. A recent systematic review, by contrast, found no study that directly compares time to relapse after the two modalities and concluded that, if anything, ECT may be associated with longer remission [40]. The two sources thus point in different directions, and relative durability is best regarded as undetermined pending trials that standardize continuation treatment. Mechanistically, the leading account holds that ketamine triggers a burst of synaptic plasticity (glutamate release, AMPA-receptor activation, and downstream synaptogenesis in prefrontal circuits) that lifts mood rapidly but is not, by itself, self-sustaining; keeping the new synaptic state stable is precisely what current maintenance strategies are trying, empirically, to achieve [14,41]. Durability, then, is not a peripheral matter of tolerability; it is the central therapeutic problem of the era.

RELAPSE PREVENTION AND MAINTENANCE STRATEGIES

The most rigorous evidence on sustaining benefit comes from the esketamine relapse-prevention programme. In a randomized-withdrawal design, patients with TRD who reached stable remission or response on esketamine plus an oral antidepressant and were then continued on esketamine had a significantly longer time to relapse than those switched to placebo nasal spray. Among stable remitters, 26.7% versus 45.3% relapsed, a 51% reduction in relapse risk (hazard ratio 0.49). These data establish maintenance esketamine as the clearest evidence-based relapse-prevention strategy for rapid-acting treatment in TRD [42]. This design speaks directly to the clinical question of whether responders should continue treatment, but it also enriches for patients who both tolerate and benefit from esketamine; its absolute relapse-prevention estimates should therefore not be generalized uncritically to all patients starting induction. Long-term extension data indicate that response can be maintained over many months with individualized dosing frequency and an acceptable tolerability profile [43,44].

For racemic ketamine, maintenance evidence is thinner and more heterogeneous. A systematic review identified only 3 randomized controlled trials alongside 8 open-label trials and 30 case series, and concluded that intravenous, intranasal, oral, and subcutaneous maintenance ketamine can sustain antidepressant effect with seemingly uncommon serious harms, while emphasizing how limited the controlled long-term data remain [45]. Real-world cohorts broadly support continued benefit but are vulnerable to selection, survivorship bias, non-standardized concomitant treatment, and attrition [46].

Several questions of maintenance design are unresolved. The optimal dosing interval is typically individualized empirically rather than guided by evidence; there is no validated rule for when to lengthen intervals, attempt deprescribing, or stop. Whether esketamine is best combined with a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor or serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, with lithium, or with structured psychotherapy to prolong benefit is only beginning to be tested [47,48]. Combination with neuromodulation has so far been explored only in small case series [49]. Esketamine has also been compared head-to-head against a conventional augmentation strategy, extended-release quetiapine, with esketamine achieving higher remission at week 8 (27.1% versus 17.6%) and, among those who remitted, more patients remaining relapse-free through week 32 (21.7% versus 14.1%); this is useful evidence for sequencing decisions, though it does not resolve how long maintenance should continue [50]. The practical consequence is that many patients are placed on open-ended treatment whose necessary duration is unknown. A higher-quality maintenance paradigm would prospectively specify the target duration, relapse criteria, rescue plan, and tapering conditions rather than allowing continuation to become indefinite by default.

Guidelines and consensus statements have moved faster than the maintenance evidence. The Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments positioned esketamine and intravenous ketamine within the TRD algorithm and offered pragmatic guidance on continuation, while explicitly flagging the thinness of long-term data [19]. An American Psychiatric Association Council of Research consensus statement similarly emphasized careful patient selection, informed consent, monitoring, and the limits of long-term evidence for ketamine in mood disorders [51]. Real-world access and utilization studies reveal a further gap between the controlled trial setting and practice: dosing intervals, concomitant medications, and treatment persistence vary substantially, and many patients discontinue earlier than trial protocols would predict, for reasons ranging from logistical burden and the requirement for on-site monitoring to cost and incomplete benefit [46,52]. A maintenance strategy that works in a clinical trial but cannot be sustained in a patient’s life is not, in practice, a durable one, and this unmet need is as much organizational as pharmacological.

FUNCTIONAL RECOVERY BEYOND SYMPTOM RESPONSE

Symptom response and remission, the endpoints of almost all ketamine trials, are not the same as functional recovery: the restoration of work, relationships, cognition, reward engagement, autonomy, and quality of life that patients actually seek. Patients with TRD show pronounced deficits in daily functioning and health-related quality of life that exceed those of non-resistant depression, and these deficits do not necessarily normalize when depressive scores fall [44,53]. Where patient-reported and quality-of-life outcomes have been measured directly, esketamine does improve them alongside symptoms: in a pooled analysis of the ASPIRE trials, quality-of-life scores improved more with esketamine than placebo (a change of −14.4 versus −12.2 points on the Quality of Life in Depression Scale, a modest between-group difference of −3.1), although a parallel hopelessness measure did not separate from placebo. Even so, such endpoints are reported far less often than rating-scale change and seldom as the primary outcome [54,55]. Comorbidity, quality of life, and psychosocial outcomes remain under-investigated in depression treatment generally, and the gap is conspicuous for rapid-acting agents evaluated primarily on rating-scale change [53,56]. For ketamine there are no controlled studies [57].

Two residual domains deserve particular attention. Anhedonia, the diminished capacity to anticipate, pursue, and experience reward, frequently persists after mood and neurovegetative symptoms improve and is a strong driver of functional impairment; ketamine appears to exert relatively specific anti-anhedonic effects, and in a 36-patient crossover trial a single infusion reduced anhedonia independently of its effect on overall depressive symptoms, while preclinical work has begun to map the cell-type-specific reward adaptations that may underlie these effects [58-61]. Cognition is the second: cognitive dysfunction is a major contributor to disability in TRD, and although ketamine and esketamine do not appear to impair cognition over the medium term and may modestly improve some domains (a pooled analysis of 4 phase-3 esketamine studies, n ≈ 884, found cognition unchanged or slightly improved versus comparator), dedicated cognitive and functional endpoints are still rarely primary outcomes [62,63]. Embedding patient-reported, cognitive, occupational, and social-functioning measures as primary or key secondary endpoints, rather than afterthoughts, is one of the clearest methodological needs of the field.

The distinction between symptomatic remission and lived recovery is therefore quite relevant. A patient whose rating-scale score has halved but who cannot return to work, sustain relationships, or experience pleasure has not, by any patient-centred standard, recovered. Evidence from conventional antidepressants is cautionary here: even where symptoms improve, gains in quality of life can be more modest than the symptomatic change implies, a dissociation that should be expected to apply to rapid-acting agents as well unless specifically demonstrated otherwise [53,56]. Because TRD by definition selects for chronicity and accumulated psychosocial damage, the work of recovery (reversing deconditioning, re-engaging with employment and relationships, rebuilding reward responsiveness, restoring sleep-wake structure, and regaining confidence after years of non-response) may outlast and outscale the work of symptom reduction. Rapid-acting treatments create an opportunity to begin that work sooner, but realizing it requires that maintenance be paired with psychosocial and functional rehabilitation, and that trials measure whether function actually follows symptoms [44].

PATIENT SELECTION AND PREDICTORS

Deciding who should receive rapid-acting treatment, and when, remains largely a matter of clinical judgement. A first step is distinguishing true pharmacological resistance from pseudo-resistance arising from inadequate dosing or duration, poor adherence, undetected bipolarity, unrecognized psychotic features, persistent substance use, sleep disorders, medical contributors, or unaddressed anxiety and trauma-related comorbidity; misclassification inflates apparent resistance and exposes patients to escalation they may not need [7,9]. Staging and definition frameworks help structure this judgement but are not yet predictive at the level of the individual patient, and the definition of resistance itself differs across unipolar and bipolar depression [2,64].

Clinical predictors of ketamine response have been reported: greater baseline severity, family history of alcohol use disorder, higher body mass index, certain symptom profiles such as hypersomnia, and the intensity of acute dissociative experience. Few of these replicate robustly enough to guide selection, however, and effect sizes are modest [65-67]. Dissociation is a particularly instructive example: it has been proposed both as a side effect and as a possible marker of engagement of the therapeutic mechanism, and a case-based comparison of ketamine and esketamine has suggested that the magnitude of dissociative features may track differential efficacy between the two molecules [66]. However, a side effect that complicates blinding and tolerability should not be treated as a selection biomarker until tested prospectively. Bipolar depression, comorbid substance use, and late-life depression each raise distinct selection considerations, with the evidence base in bipolar and geriatric TRD considerably thinner than in mid-life unipolar disease [68,69]. Personalized, prospectively validated selection algorithms remain aspirational [67].

Special populations further complicate the selection problem. In bipolar depression, the same glutamatergic strategy must be weighed against the risk of affective switching and the comparative scarcity of controlled data. What is an established rapid-acting option in unipolar TRD becomes a more cautious, case-by-case decision in bipolar TRD, for which controlled efficacy was shown early but the very definition of resistance has only recently been formalized [16,64,68,70]. In late-life depression, which is often chronic, medically comorbid, vascularly burdened, and historically under-represented in trials, the balance of benefit against haemodynamic, dissociative, cognitive, and logistical risk is different again, and the evidence base is correspondingly thinner [69]. At the other end of the age range, the demonstrated efficacy of intravenous ketamine in adolescent TRD shows the reach of the approach but also how far ahead of the safety and selection evidence enthusiasm can run in vulnerable groups [71]. Across all of these populations, the recurring issue is that efficacy demonstrated in mid-life unipolar TRD does not transfer automatically, and that selection must be individualized precisely where the data are weakest.

BIOMARKER LIMITATIONS

A recurring promise of the rapid-acting era has been that the speed and discreteness of ketamine’s effect would finally make antidepressant response tractable to biomarkers. That promise is largely unfulfilled: no blood, genetic, electrophysiological, or imaging marker is validated for routine prediction of who will respond, remit, relapse, or develop problematic adverse effects. Candidate markers have been pursued along several lines. Peripheral inflammatory indices are altered in subgroups of depressed patients and have been linked to ketamine’s therapeutic profile, but they lack the specificity and prospective validation needed for clinical use [72]. Electroencephalographic measures, including frontal gamma activity and vigilance indices, show association with response in small samples but await replication [73,74]. Neuroimaging and measures of synaptic density speak to mechanism, since synaptic plasticity is central to current models of rapid antidepressant action, yet they do not currently inform bedside decisions [41,75].

The reasons for this translational stall are likely the same as for other antidepressant treatments. Sample sizes are small and heterogeneous; candidate markers are rarely tested prospectively against pre-specified thresholds; the outcomes themselves (response, remission, relapse) are coarse; and the biology is multifactorial, so any single marker captures only a small part of variance. The broader programme of fluid and physiological biomarkers in depression has repeatedly shown initial promise followed by failure to replicate, and rapid-acting treatments have not escaped this pattern [73]. Progress will require biomarker studies embedded in adequately powered randomized or pragmatic cohorts, with external validation, repeated measurement across induction and maintenance, pre-specified clinical decision thresholds, and outcomes that include relapse and function rather than symptom response alone. Until then, patient selection will continue to rest on clinical features and measurement-based care rather than on mechanism.

LONG-TERM SAFETY AND ABUSE LIABILITY

The acute tolerability of ketamine and esketamine is reasonably well characterized: dissociation, transient blood-pressure and heart-rate increases, nausea, dizziness, and sedation are common but generally short-lived and self-limited at therapeutic doses [38,76]. The first systematic review of ketamine’s safety in depression, screening 288 articles and including 60, of which 20 were randomized trials totalling 430 ketamine-exposed participants, reached a more sobering conclusion: assessment of long-term and repeated-dose safety has been strikingly limited, with selective reporting and short follow-up relative to what is known from chronic pain populations and recreational users [77]. Consensus guidance has therefore emphasized structured screening, informed consent, vital-sign and mental-status monitoring, attention to substance-use risk, and caution against casual off-label expansion beyond settings capable of managing acute adverse effects [51]. The chief long-term concerns are urological: lower urinary tract symptoms and, with heavy chronic exposure, ketamine-associated uropathy, though severe bladder pathology has not been reported at the doses and frequencies used for depression under prescribing guidance [76].

Abuse liability is the second unresolved concern, and it has a mechanistic basis. The observation that pretreatment with the opioid antagonist naltrexone can attenuate ketamine’s antidepressant effect first suggested that its action may depend in part on the endogenous opioid system, beyond NMDA-receptor antagonism. This finding has since been both extended and contested. In a randomized crossover study in 26 patients, naltrexone blunted both ketamine’s acute prefrontal glutamatergic surge and its day-one antidepressant effect, with direct implications for dependence risk and for how the drug should be monitored over time [78-80]. Spontaneous and sometimes delayed dissociative phenomena have been documented with intranasal esketamine in clinical use, underscoring the need for structured observation and for distinguishing therapeutic from problematic exposure [81]. Long-term cognitive safety under years of maintenance dosing is essentially uncharacterized. Reassuringly, the largest real-world pharmacovigilance analysis to date, covering almost five years of post-approval use and more than a million esketamine treatment sessions in the United States, identified no new safety signals, with serious adverse events, suicide, and abuse or misuse all rare and the suicide rate below population background [82]. Yet surveillance of this kind captures acute and subacute events far better than the slow, cumulative outcomes (cognitive change, dependence, uropathy, tolerance, and functional consequences of repeated dissociation) that years of maintenance dosing might produce. Taken together, the safety database is adequate for supervised, time-limited use but thin precisely where the post-ketamine era is heading: chronic, repeated, increasingly community-based administration.

These uncertainties have shaped how the treatments are delivered. Esketamine is administered under risk-management controls requiring on-site observation for sedation and dissociation and a period of monitoring after each dose, a model that improves acute safety but adds the logistical burden implicated in the maintenance attrition noted above [52]. As provision expands toward office-based and, in some systems, less intensively supervised settings, the tension between access and safeguards sharpens: the same features that make rapid-acting treatment attractive (speed, potency, a subjectively salient experience) are those that demand vigilance against misuse and diversion. The responsible path lies between two errors: treating ketamine as an ordinary antidepressant, and walling it off behind impractical restrictions. What it requires is proportionate, evidence-based monitoring that scales with the duration, dose, comorbidity, and setting of use [51,76,77].

THE POST-KETAMINE PIPELINE AS PARTIAL ANSWERS

If ketamine changed the problem, a pipeline of newer rapid-acting and novel-mechanism agents is now being asked to answer the downstream questions it exposed, above all durability, tolerability, scalability, and route of administration [20,21,83,84]. Each candidate maps imperfectly onto the unmet needs catalogued above. Serotonergic psychedelics, principally psilocybin, are the most striking example of a treatment that aspires to one- or few-session durability: in a phase 2b dose-finding trial in TRD, a single 25 mg dose with psychological support produced substantially greater symptom reduction than a 1 mg control at week 3 (least-squares mean MADRS change −12.0 versus −5.4; a between-group difference of −6.6 points). The durability promise, however, was only partly borne out in that trial: the advantage over the low-dose control was no longer statistically significant at week 12. The safety profile also tempers enthusiasm, since adverse events were reported in roughly three-quarters of participants, with suicidal ideation or self-injurious behaviour occurring across all dose groups [85,86]. Whether durable benefit holds at scale, how far it depends on the resource-intensive psychological-support component, and how expectancy and functional unblinding can be managed are the field’s next questions [87].

Other strategies target tolerability and route rather than mechanism alone. Extended-release oral ketamine formulations aim to retain efficacy while smoothing the pharmacokinetic peaks that drive dissociation and, potentially, abuse liability, but they do not by themselves answer the maintenance, monitoring, and long-term safety questions [88]. Glutamatergic modulators beyond ketamine, and rapid-acting agents acting through GABAergic and other systems, are advancing through development with the explicit goal of combining speed with a cleaner long-term profile [83,84]. Recently approved oral agents illustrate the trend toward speed without parenteral administration or scheduled observation, but their relationship to TRD specifically remains uneven. The dextromethorphan-bupropion combination, approved for major depression in 2022 rather than specifically for TRD, couples an NMDA-receptor antagonist and sigma-1 agonist with a pharmacokinetic booster. It produces a rapid oral antidepressant effect (remission 46.5% versus 16.2% for bupropion at week 6) with a tolerability profile that lends itself to maintenance, and shows preliminary signals in more resistant presentations [89-92]. Neuroactive steroids that positively modulate GABA-A receptors (the intravenous brexanolone and the oral zuranolone, both approved for postpartum depression) offer a different rapid mechanism, delivered over a short, defined course rather than as continuous exposure, which reframes the durability question as how long a discrete treatment course holds; their role in non-postpartum and resistant depression is still being defined [93,94]. Esmethadone and other glutamatergic modulators extend the same logic of oral, repeatable, rapid-acting treatment [83]. A useful way to compare these agents is by their clinical-specificity profile, the particular combination of speed, durability, tolerability, scalability, and target symptom domain each offers, rather than by acute efficacy alone [95]. On present evidence no single successor resolves all of the gaps ketamine exposed; each trades among them, which is itself an argument for matching agent to patient.

Viewed against the catalogue of unmet needs, the pipeline’s contributions are partial and complementary rather than comprehensive (Fig. 3). Oral neuroactive steroids and dextromethorphan-bupropion chiefly address route and tolerability, easing maintenance and reducing monitoring burden; psychedelics target durability and the reward and existential dimensions of recovery, at the cost of a resource-intensive psychological intervention and unresolved questions about blinding and scalability; extended-release ketamine formulations attack the pharmacokinetic drivers of dissociation and possibly abuse liability [85,88,90,93]. None convincingly closes the biomarker or patient-selection gap, which is mechanism-agnostic. The realistic near-term future is therefore not a single rapid-acting cure but a differentiated set of options whose rational deployment depends on the same downstream judgements about durability, function, access, and safety that define the era [20,84].

FURTHER PERSPECTIVES AND CONCLUSION

The cumulative message of the post-ketamine era is that the decisive problems in TRD have become problems of management over time rather than of acute pharmacology alone (Table 1). This reframing has concrete implications. For research, the priorities are adequately powered maintenance and relapse-prevention trials with pre-specified deprescribing rules; pragmatic comparisons against active and clinically relevant comparators; functional and patient-reported outcomes as primary or key secondary endpoints; designs that protect and report blinding integrity, including active comparators and treatment-guess rates, so that the efficacy of psychoactive agents is not overestimated; prospective, decision-focused biomarker designs; and long-term safety surveillance that matches the chronic use the treatments are receiving [27,37,45,53,77]. For drug development, the bar has moved from showing rapid efficacy to demonstrating durable, tolerable, function-restoring, and implementable benefit [20,21].

For the treating clinician, several practical positions follow from the current evidence. Rapid-acting treatment should be offered after genuine resistance is confirmed and pseudo-resistance excluded, not as a reflex; an induction response should be consolidated with a planned maintenance strategy rather than left to chance; functional and cognitive recovery, and residual anhedonia, should be assessed and targeted even after symptomatic response; patients should be told explicitly that maintenance duration and tapering rules remain uncertain; and the still-incomplete long-term safety and abuse-liability profile warrants structured monitoring, particularly as administration moves into community settings [42,44,51,67]. Within this context, the expanding pipeline is best understood not as a search for a single successor to ketamine but as a toolkit to be matched, on a precision-psychiatry logic, to the specific unmet need a given patient presents [67,95]. Figure 4 draws these positions together as a pragmatic care-pathway, with the unresolved question attached to each step.

A final, often understated unmet need is organizational and economic. Rapid-acting treatments are more resource-intensive than oral antidepressants: they require monitored administration, repeated clinic visits, staff trained to manage dissociation and haemodynamic changes, and, for psychedelic-assisted approaches, trained therapists for prolonged sessions. These demands influence who can actually access the treatments and for how long they can sustain them, and they risk widening rather than narrowing disparities in TRD care if maintenance is available only to those who can bear its logistical and financial burden [52,53]. Economic evidence is context-dependent and still evolving: modelling studies have produced different conclusions depending on comparator, jurisdiction, drug price, delivery assumptions, and whether productivity gains are included [96,97]. A recent multi-armed Hong Kong model, for example, found esketamine more economically favourable than some neurostimulation strategies but not cost-effective versus several commonly used third-line pharmacological or psychological strategies at the chosen willingness-to-pay threshold [98]. Cost-effectiveness comparisons with established augmentation strategies are therefore part of the evidence clinicians and systems need; the durability question is inseparable from what durable treatment costs and who can reach it. Building delivery models that make maintenance feasible in ordinary practice is as much a part of meeting the post-ketamine unmet needs as any molecule in development.

In conclusion, ketamine and esketamine have changed the treatment landscape for TRD by proving that rapid, clinically meaningful improvement is possible in patients who had few fast options. They have not ended TRD. Instead, they have revealed the next generation of problems: durability, relapse prevention and maintenance, functional recovery, patient selection, biomarkers, long-term safety, and equitable implementation. Meeting these needs will require a shift of research and clinical attention from how quickly depression can be improved to how durably, how fully, how safely, and how fairly it can be kept that way.
