Typical Atrioventricular Nodal Re-Entry Tachycardia Masquerading as Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Vijay Soorampally, Yash Lokhandwala, Vaibhav R Vaidya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report 3 cases of irregular, narrow complex tachycardia misdiagnosed and treated for atrial fibrillation. The adenosine response, detection of recurring triple cycle length variation patterns, and pseudo-R-wave in lead V1 during tachycardia made us suspect typical atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia. The electrophysiology study confirmed atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia, and symptoms were resolved by slow pathway modification.Key Words: complex arrhythmias, RF ablation, supraventricular tachycardia

Typical atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT) is the most common supraventricular tachycardia, with conduction occurring antegradely over a slow pathway and retrogradely over a fast pathway. Presence of multiple antegrade atrioventricular node pathways is not rare in patients with AVNRT. The involvement of 2 or more antegrade slow pathways during AVNRT can result in an irregular narrow complex tachycardia (NCT) that can easily be misdiagnosed as atrial fibrillation (AF), leading to major therapeutic implications.

Case 1

An 81-year-old woman without any known comorbidities presented with recurrent episodes of paroxysmal palpitations that were sudden in onset and offset. The electrocardiography (ECG) during the tachycardia showed irregular NCT (Figure 1), which was diagnosed as AF, and she was started on amiodarone and apixaban. Given her recurrent episodes despite amiodarone, the patient was taken up for an electrophysiology (EP) study.

Irregularly Irregular Narrow Complex Tachycardia

On EP study, NCT was easily inducible with atrial burst pacing. Tachycardia was irregular, but there were alternative short and long cycle lengths with a constant and short HA interval of 60 ms (Figure 2). The tachycardia repeatedly terminated with an atrial electrogram, and ventricular overdrive pacing during the tachycardia showed a VAV response that ruled out atrial tachycardia. Radiofrequency (RF) energy in the Koch triangle at the M1 to M2 region resulted in successful slow pathway modification. Postprocedure, amiodarone and anticoagulants were discontinued.

Follow-Up

After 12 months of follow-up, a 30-day monitor demonstrated no evidence of AF or NCT, and the patient was symptom-free.

Case 2

A 51-year-old woman with hypertension reported recurrent episodes of palpitations for 5 years. The episodes were sudden in onset, nonexertional, and well tolerated, and would subside within 10 to 15 minutes after taking rest. A few episodes required hospitalization, and the episodes were terminated with adenosine. The recent episode was diagnosed as AF (Figure 3) and was terminated a few hours after starting intravenous amiodarone. The patient was discharged on amiodarone, dabigatran, and amlodipine. She was referred to us for an EP study.

Irregular Tachycardia

The ECG during the episode showed irregular NCT without an obvious P-wave, making AF the obvious choice. On careful analysis, there was a rhythmic sequence of 3 cycle lengths (Figure 4A) and the presence of a small but distinct r’ in lead V1, which was absent in sinus rhythm (Figure 4B), which made us think of tachycardia as AVNRT involving multiple slow pathways.

Electrocardiographic Features Suggestive of Atrioventricular Nodal Re-Entrant Tachycardia

(A) Rhythm strip (lead II) electrocardiogram showed a repetitive rhythmic sequence of 3 cycle lengths (400 ms, 320 ms, and 280 ms). (B) The red arrow indicates a distinct r’ (pseudo-r’) in V1 during the tachycardia, which was absent during sinus rhythm.

An EP study was done after stopping amiodarone for 4 weeks. During the study, typical AVNRT was easily and repeatedly induced at a cycle length of 360 ms (Figure 5). However, we could not demonstrate the irregular tachycardia, as in the clinical ECG. A single application of RF energy just anterior to the coronary sinus ostium in the triangle of Koch was effective. After this, despite administering isoprenaline and atropine, no AVNRT was inducible.

Typical (Slow-Fast) Atrioventricular Nodal Re-entrant Tachycardia Induced After an AH Jump With Programmed Atrial Stimulation

Follow-Up

In a 1-year follow-up period, the patient had no further episodes of palpitations, and he continues to receive amlodipine for hypertension.

Case 3

A 22-year-old woman with a structurally normal heart presented with paroxysmal palpitations due to irregular NCT. The patient was started on flecainide and referred for an EP study because of her persistent symptoms.

An incessant, irregular, short RP tachycardia with an HA interval of 60 ms was easily inducible. The irregularity was due to variations in the AH interval, and the HA interval remained constant (Figure 6). Ventricular overdrive pacing demonstrated a V-A-V response. Premature atrial and ventricular extrastimuli were delivered during tachycardia, but the response was challenging to interpret due to the irregularity of the tachycardia cycle length. Tachycardia would terminate spontaneously with an atrial electrogram but would recur due to any premature stimulus. However, a premature ventricular extrastimulus delivered during a brief episode of sinus rhythm demonstrated retrograde right bundle branch block with delay in the atrial activation, without a change in atrial activation pattern, ruling out retrograde conducting accessory pathways. The findings were most consistent with AVNRT. RF ablation was performed in the usual region of the slow pathway within the triangle of Koch. Junctional complexes were observed, and some had a chevron-shaped coronary sinus activation, suggestive of retrograde conduction through a left-sided slow pathway (Figure 7). Following ablation, tachycardia was no longer inducible.

Junctional Beats With Chevron-Shaped Coronary Sinus Activation, Suggestive of Retrograde Conduction Through a Left-Sided Slow Pathway

Follow-Up

Flecainide was discontinued post–RF ablation, and the patient was asymptomatic at a 1-year follow-up.

Discussion

Our cases had a unique variant of typical AVNRT in which there were likely multiple slow pathways manifesting as an irregular NCT that made the clinician misdiagnose it as AF.

Multiple antegrade atrioventricular node pathways are seen in 5% of patients with AVNRT.1 However, in most of these patients, during an episode of tachycardia, only one of the antegrade slow pathways is active. This may be because most additional slow pathways have different conduction properties and cannot establish a circuit with the retrograde fast pathway in a way that allows for re-entry and, hence, sustained tachycardia.

We could not demonstrate multiple AH jumps on EP study in our patients. The definition of an AH jump (50 ms increment for a 10-ms S1-S2 decrement), though universal, is surprisingly somewhat arbitrary. It has been accepted for decades without rigorous scientific validation. Moreover, the atrioventricular node is highly susceptible to autonomic tone, and disparities in conduction within it can vary from time to time. These could be the underlying reasons why we were unable to demonstrate “textbook” multiple slow pathways in our cases during the EP study, despite such obvious manifestations in the clinical ECG.

In all our cases, the initial misdiagnosis of AF was made due to the irregular tachycardia and no obvious P-wave on the ECG, which led to the inappropriate initiation of amiodarone and oral anticoagulation. These could have gone on long term with potential major adverse consequences if the correct diagnosis was not made. The history of termination of tachycardia with adenosine was a red flag in case 2, and subsequent, meticulous analysis of the ECG unearthed the amazing recurrent triple cycle length variation (in case 2) pattern during tachycardia, along with the identification of a pseudo-r' in lead V1, which made the suspicion of AVNRT strong. In patients with irregular NCT, a repetitive sequence of cycle length variation, presence of pseudo-r’ in lead V1, absence of fibrillatory waves, and termination with adenosine should alert clinicians to a possible diagnosis of AVNRT as opposed to AF.

Another unique feature of our cases is the simultaneous elimination of all the evidence of slow pathway conduction following an application of RF energy in Koch's triangle, just anterior to the coronary sinus ostium. This implies that the arrhythmic substrate can be formed by either functional variations in a specified circuit based on nonuniform anisotropy or 3 distinct anatomic slow pathways with closely spaced insertion sites in the triangle of Koch and an upper common pathway.

Richter et al2 reported 2 similar cases with typical AVNRT disguised as AF due to the involvement of 3 different anterograde slow atrioventricular nodal pathways, which resulted in a unique sequence of the QRS complexes, giving rise to an image of group beating triplets on the ECG similar to our case 2. In both cases, an application of RF energy to the posterior aspect of Koch’s triangle eliminated all the slow pathway conduction simultaneously.2

AVNRT can act as a trigger for true AF, especially in younger patients. Studies have shown that induced AVNRT was found in 4% of patients referred for AF ablation, and 9.7% of patients with AVNRT had concomitant AF. A detailed EP study before AF ablation may uncover previously undetected AVNRT, which triggers AF. When AVNRT is ablated after it has been detected, it is more successful and less likely to cause complications than when the pulmonary veins are targeted in those with paroxysmal AF. Furthermore, in certain cases, AVNRT ablation may be the sole treatment required to cure AF.3 Paroxysmal AF is thus sometimes misdiagnosed, and such patients may erroneously be considered for pulmonary vein isolation, while just slow pathway ablation may prevent further episodes of AF.4

Conclusions

Typical AVNRT with multiple antegrade slow pathways can present as an irregular NCT that can mimic AF. Young age, the absence of atrial fibrillatory waves on ECG during the longer RR interval of tachycardia, presence of pseudo-r' in V1 during the tachycardia, rhythmic changes in the cycle length of tachycardia, and a structurally normal heart are all factors that help prevent the misdiagnosis of AF, which can prevent the need for needless oral anticoagulation and antiarrhythmic medications.
