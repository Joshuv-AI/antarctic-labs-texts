Latent class analysis of multimorbidity patterns and associated factors among older adult fallers in the hospital
Authors: Xinyun Wang, Lili Sun, Lijuan Quan, Minglu Fan, Zhaodi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFalls among older adults in hospitals remain a frequent occurrence, and multimorbidity is significantly associated with this adverse event. However, research on multimorbidity patterns specifically among older hospitalized patients who experienced falls remains limited. This study aimed to identify multimorbidity classes and their associated factors in this population.MethodsData were extracted from the nursing management information system of a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou, China, covering fall incidents recorded between January 2015 and August 2025. Information on patient characteristics, fall event features, and outcomes was collected. Latent class analysis was performed using 12 chronic conditions to delineate multimorbidity classes among older adult fallers in the hospital. Multinomial logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with each multimorbidity class.ResultsAmong the 705 older adult fallers, cancer was the most prevalent chronic condition. Four distinct multimorbidity patterns were identified: the Stroke group (11.9%), the Cancer group (22.8%), the Multisystem multimorbidity group (22.4%), and the Relatively healthy group (42.8%). Logistic regression analysis revealed that age, ward, accompaniment status, pre-fall status, staff years of service, risk assessment score, and the number of fall-risk-increasing drugs were significantly associated with latent multimorbidity classes among older hospitalized fallers (p < 0.05).ConclusionMultimorbidity among older adult fallers in the hospital was common, underscoring the urgent need to incorporate multimorbidity into current fall prevention guidelines. Distinct multimorbidity classes can be identified among older hospitalized fallers, each characterized by specific comorbidity patterns and associated with different factors.Based on these findings, clinical practitioners can develop targeted nursing intervention strategies to reduce fall risk in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07997-4.Keywords: Older adults, Fall, Fallers, Hospital, Multimorbidity, Latent class analysis

Introduction

Hospital patient falls represent a major concern for both the global healthcare sector and society at large [1, 2]. The daily incidence of falls among hospitalized patients worldwide ranges from 0.36% to 1.26%, and in China, from 0.14% to 1.82% [3, 4]. Approximately 30–50% of these falls result in physical injuries ranging from bruises and lacerations to fractures and even death, significantly increasing mortality, length of hospital stay, resource utilization, and healthcare costs among hospitalized patients [5, 6]. Older inpatients, characterized by complex medical conditions, polypharmacy, and reduced self-care ability, are particularly vulnerable to falls in hospital settings [7, 8]. As the Chinese population ages, the number of older hospitalized patients at risk of falls and fall-related injuries will continue to increase.

Multimorbidity is a significant factor influencing falls, as the accumulation of multiple chronic conditions often leads to sensory and mobility impairments, thereby increasing fall risk [9]. Multimorbidity is defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions in an individual without a defined index disease [10]. Furthermore, previous research has clearly demonstrated that older adults with multimorbidity have a higher risk of falls than those with a single chronic condition, with each one-unit increase in the multimorbidity count associated with a 16% increase in the risk of fall-related hospitalization [11]. Due to individual variability, multimorbidity is not merely a random combination of conditions but rather involves specific physiological and pathological interconnections [12]. Identifying distinct combinations of conditions within multimorbidity can facilitate a more person-centered approach to medical care and improve clinical outcomes for patients with multimorbidity [13].

Latent class analysis (LCA) is a probabilistic modeling algorithm used to identify distinct subgroups or subtypes within multivariate categorical data, and it has emerged as a promising statistical method for exploring patterns of multimorbidity in populations [14]. In recent years, the application of LCA to investigate the relationship between multimorbidity patterns and falls has garnered considerable research attention. However, most relevant studies have been conducted in community settings [15], with limited research on hospitalized patients at higher risk of falls. Moreover, existing studies have primarily identified multimorbidity patterns in general older-adult chronic disease populations [16, 17]; to our knowledge, no prior study has specifically employed LCA to examine multimorbidity patterns among older adult fallers in the hospital, which is crucial for fall risk assessment and management. A systematic review evaluating the effectiveness of interventions for multimorbidity management concluded that interventions targeting risk factors in specific populations with multimorbidity-related impairments may be more effective [18]. Therefore, this study aims to identify latent multimorbidity classes among older adult fallers in the hospital and to explore associated factors, leveraging these characteristics to identify potential targets for prevention and provide new insights for the implementation of inpatient fall prevention and management strategies.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective study enrolled older hospitalized patients who experienced falls, as documented in the Fall and Bedside Safety Incident Report within the Nursing Management Information System of a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou, China, from January 2015 to August 2025. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) occurrence of a fall during hospitalization, with the definition of a fall based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes for falls (W00-W19) [19]; and (3) cases reported according to the nursing adverse event reporting procedure. The exclusion criteria were: (1) missing or obviously incorrect information in the reported data that could not be verified or supplemented through other means; (2) non-inpatients (e.g., outpatient or emergency department cases); and (3) immobilized patients. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No. IIT20250631B-R1) and was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample size

To determine an appropriate sample size for this study, we consulted Nylund‑Gibson et al. [20], who suggest that a minimum of 300 participants is required for latent class analysis to reliably identify the optimal number of classes. Assuming that 10% of the samples would be invalid, the lower requirement was set at 330 cases. A total of 729 patients were enrolled in this study. Among them, 20 were excluded due to missing multiple key variables, including age, sex, and medical history, and another 4 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 705 patients were included in the final analysis, a number that is more than adequate for latent class analysis. The detailed inclusion and exclusion process is shown in Fig. 1.

Data collection

Immediately after each fall event, nurses verified and assessed the data in the fall incident reporting system by interviewing patients, family members, or caregivers who were the first eyewitnesses. Subsequently, the data were entered into the electronic medical record system in accordance with standardized documentation requirements. Drawing on relevant literature and clinical records, we developed the Data Collection Form for Fall Events in Older Adult Fallers in the Hospital. A two‑person verification method was used for data collection: two staff members independently entered the data, and after completing the entry, they cross‑checked each other’s work to ensure data accuracy.

Patient information: hospital admission number, age, sex, education level, history of falls, and length of hospital stay. History of falls refers to whether the patient had experienced any fall within the six months prior to the current fall event during the same hospital admission.

In this study, the primary nurse is defined as a registered nurse who has passed the national licensure examination and provides comprehensive, coordinated, and individualized care to patients throughout their entire hospital stay from admission to discharge. Accompaniment status refers to whether a family member or caregiver was present at the time of the fall. According to the National Database of Nursing Quality Indicators of the American Nurses Association and prior studies, the severity of fall‑related injuries is classified into three levels: no injury, minor injury, and serious injury [21, 22]. No injury indicates no signs or symptoms of injury; minor injuries include bruises, abrasions, and pain requiring basic treatment; serious injuries manifest as muscle or joint injuries, fractures, nerve or visceral damage, and necessitate medical interventions such as surgery or casting.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 29.0 and Mplus 8.3 software. Latent class analysis (LCA) was applied to identify distinguishable multimorbidity classes, with twelve chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, cancer, stroke, heart disease, liver disease, lung disease, kidney disease, gastric or digestive disease, arthritis or bone disease, Parkinson’s or neurological disease, and anemia or blood disease) used as observed indicators. To ensure proper convergence and avoid local optima, 500 random initial stage starts were specified for each class solution. All models converged successfully, and the global maximum-likelihood solution was confirmed by replicating log-likelihood values across multiple random starts.

The optimal number of latent classes was determined based on multiple fit indices: (1) Information evaluation indices: The Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC) were used, with smaller values indicating better model fit. (2) Classification accuracy index: Entropy was used to evaluate classification precision, with values closer to 1 indicating more accurate classification; an entropy value of 0.8 suggests classification accuracy exceeding 90%. (3) Likelihood ratio tests: The Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMRT) and the bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT) were employed to compare the goodness-of-fit between models with k-1 and k classes, with a P-value < 0.05 indicating that the k-class model provided a superior fit to the k-1 class model.

Given that the smallest class (Stroke group) comprised 11.3% of the sample, a parametric bootstrap sensitivity analysis with 200 resamples was conducted to assess the stability of the results. The four-class model was refitted for each bootstrap sample, and the class proportions and entropy values were recorded.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. For missing data, mean imputation was used for the nurse-to-patient ratio at the time of the fall (missingness < 5%), and mode imputation for the risk assessment score (missingness < 5%). For the univariate analysis, the normality of the continuous variables was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were analyzed using one-way ANOVA; nonnormally distributed variables were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons. Variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis were included as independent variables, with latent class membership as the dependent variable. Prior to conducting the multinomial logistic regression analysis, all continuous variables were standardized using Z-scores, and multicollinearity diagnostics were performed for all independent variables. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Identification and naming of latent classes

As shown in Fig. 2, Class 1 had the highest stroke prevalence, accounting for 11.9% of the total sample; accordingly, it was designated the “Stroke group”. Class 2 demonstrated the highest prevalence of cancer, comprising 22.8% of the total sample, and was consequently named the “Cancer group”. Class 3, which exhibited elevated prevalence across multiple chronic conditions spanning multiple domains, accounted for 22.4% of the total sample and was therefore labeled the “Multisystem multimorbidity group”. Class 4 comprised 42.8% of the total sample and was characterized by relatively low prevalence rates of diabetes, cancer, and various conditions involving organ dysfunction (e.g., heart disease, pulmonary disease, and kidney disease); accordingly, it was designated the “Relatively healthy group”. A parametric bootstrap sensitivity analysis with 200 resamples confirmed the stability of the smallest class (Stroke group, 11.3%), with a bootstrap mean proportion of 14.8% (95% CI: 5.6%–26.1%) and acceptable classification accuracy (bootstrap mean entropy = 0.820, 95% CI: 0.730–0.928).

Table 2 presents demographic features for the entire sample and each identified group. In the final analysis, 705 valid data points were collected. Within the total sample, the majority of patients were male, the overall educational level was relatively low, and cancer was the most prevalent chronic condition. Patients in the Multisystem multimorbidity group were significantly older than those in the Relatively healthy group. The number of chronic conditions was significantly higher in the Multisystem multimorbidity group. The proportion of serious fall-related injuries was 33.5% in the Multisystem multimorbidity group, 29.2% in the Cancer group, 25.5% in the Relatively healthy group, and 19.0% in the Stroke group, with no significant differences among the groups (p = 0.297). Table 4 presents the results of the multinomial logistic regression analysis. Using the Relatively healthy group as the reference, older age was associated with lower odds of belonging to the Cancer group (OR = 0.968, 95% CI: 0.941–0.995) and higher odds of belonging to the Multisystem multimorbidity group (OR = 1.056, 95% CI: 1.029–1.084). Compared to medical wards, patients in surgical wards had lower odds of belonging to the Stroke group (OR = 0.458, 95% CI: 0.212–0.988), and patients in other wards had lower odds of belonging to the Multisystem multimorbidity group (OR = 0.201, 95% CI: 0.116–0.348). Nurses with 3–6 years of service were associated with lower odds of patients belonging to the Multisystem multimorbidity group (OR = 0.377, 95% CI: 0.183–0.777). Relative to the Relatively healthy group, bedridden status was marginally associated with higher odds of belonging to the Stroke group (OR = 1.748, 95% CI: 0.998–3.061).

Discussion

In this study, cancer was the most prevalent chronic condition (29.6%) among older adult inpatients who experienced falls. Using LCA, we explored, for the first time, multimorbidity patterns among older hospitalized fall patients and identified four distinct classes: the Stroke group, Cancer group, Multisystem multimorbidity group, and Relatively healthy group. Among these, the Multisystem multimorbidity group had a serious fall-related injury rate as high as 33.5%. Furthermore, we identified factors associated with different multimorbidity classes, including patients' age, ward type, accompaniment status, pre-fall status, risk assessment score, number of all-risk-increasing drugs, and staff years of service.

Using similar classification criteria for chronic diseases, this study observed that cancer was the most prevalent condition among older inpatients who experienced falls. This finding aligns with Lyu et al.'s report in hospitalized older adults [23], but contrasts with the cardiometabolic pattern (e.g., hypertension) documented by Nie et al. [15] and Yan et al. [24]in community-dwelling populations. A plausible explanation for this discrepancy lies in the distinct risk profiles between inpatient and community settings. Inpatient falls represent multifactorial events influenced not only by underlying chronic conditions but also by acute illness trajectories, polypharmacy, and environmental hazards inherent to the hospital setting [25], which collectively amplify fall vulnerability in this population. Furthermore, unlike community-dwelling older adults with relatively stable, outpatient-managed hypertension, hospitalized cancer patients are typically in an acute treatment phase involving chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or postoperative recovery. These patients frequently experience impaired proprioception and compromised balance due to chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy [26], while cancer-related fatigue, generalized muscle weakness, and anemia further compromise muscle strength and postural stability, increasing the likelihood of balance loss during positional changes [27]. Additionally, reduced bone mineral density secondary to bone metastases or endocrine therapy increases the risk of severe injury should a fall occur. Therefore, fall prevention strategies for hospitalized older adults with cancer should extend beyond routine assessments to include systematic screening for chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy, anemia, and nutritional status, alongside medication reconciliation and environmental safety modifications. Future large-scale, multi-center prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings.

Consistent with previous research [24], this study identified a close relationship between multimorbidity and falls, further stratifying older adult fallers in the hospital into four distinct multimorbidity classes, each characterized by significant heterogeneity. A 100% prevalence of stroke characterized the Stroke group. It is well-established that stroke survivors are at a heightened risk of falling due to associated motor deficits, sensory impairments, and poor balance, as corroborated by previous studies [28]. The cancer group was defined by the presence of cancer among all fallers. Older adults with cancer represent a common yet unique population, whose disease mechanisms and treatment regimens may lead to cancer-related fatigue, cognitive impairment, and electrolyte disturbances—all of which contribute to an increased risk of falling [29]. The coexistence of multiple systemic conditions, such as hypertension and diabetes, characterized the Multisystem multimorbidity group. This may be attributed to polypharmacy, functional decline, and increased symptom burden resulting from multisystem involvement [30], underscoring the need for healthcare providers to remain vigilant regarding fall risk in this subgroup. The Relatively healthy group, primarily composed of less frequently occurring multisystem conditions, represented the largest proportion of the sample, further confirming the cumulative effect of chronic diseases on the occurrence of falls [11].

Age is widely recognized as a shared risk factor for both falls and multimorbidity [31, 32]. Using the Relatively healthy group as the reference, our study found that older adult fallers in the hospital were more likely to be classified into the Multisystem multimorbidity group, which makes sense given that age is a risk factor for many of the conditions in that class. However, contrary to previous studies suggesting that older cancer patients are more susceptible to age-related factors [27], our results showed that older fallers were relatively less likely to belong to the Cancer latent class. A possible explanation is that the Cancer group was characterized by a predominant cancer diagnosis with minimal comorbidities, a pattern more commonly observed in younger patients. Older patients with cancer often accumulate additional chronic conditions, leading to their classification into the Multisystem multimorbidity group (which included 19.0% of patients with cancer) rather than the isolated Cancer group. Moreover, cancer represents a highly heterogeneous group of malignant diseases, with fall risk varying considerably across different cancer types and stages [33]. The crude categorization of all cancers as a single group in this study may have masked distinct multimorbidity patterns across cancer subtypes. Future research should further subclassify cancer categories among fallers.

Examining the relationship between multimorbidity classes and hospital units in this study was challenging due to a "centralized bed management" policy that admitted patients across units to optimize bed utilization. Nurses with 3–6 years of work experience, considered to be in the mature stage, were more commonly observed in the Relatively healthy group, as expected. Nurses at this career stage are in a phase of accumulating experience and professional growth, often caring for patients with less complex conditions. They may lack well-developed clinical judgment regarding fall risk and proficient emergency response capabilities [34]. Therefore, nursing managers should allocate nursing staff rationally based on patient complexity, nurse competency assessments, and actual workload to ensure patient safety.

Stroke survivors often present with limb motor impairments such as hemiplegia and spasticity, which may lead to prolonged bed rest or wheelchair dependence. As demonstrated by Smith et al. [35], the risk of falls increases when patients who have been immobilized for prolonged periods transition to an active state. In other words, discomfort from muscle soreness and pressure injuries associated with long-term bed rest may heighten patients’ desire to mobilize [36]. However, muscle atrophy and impaired balance resulting from immobilization can further increase fall risk, creating a vicious cycle of "reduced activity—worsening frailty—elevated fall risk." Therefore, to prevent falls, clinicians should assess stroke patients’ mobilization needs, improve their limb balance, and facilitate a timely transition from supine to upright positions. Furthermore, this study found that fallers in the low-risk category on the JHFRAT were more likely to be classified into the Stroke group, suggesting that the JHFRAT may inadequately capture the multimorbidity-related fall-risk profile in stroke patients, consistent with the findings of Hong et al. [37]. A possible explanation is that the JHFRAT does not directly capture the increased fall risk associated with motor, sensory, and visual deficits in stroke patients. However, as this study did not calculate calibration or discrimination statistics for the JHFRAT in the stroke group, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating only. Future research should formally evaluate the diagnostic performance of the JHFRAT in stroke patients with multiple comorbidities.

Our findings corroborate previous evidence that both polypharmacy and FRIDs constitute significant contributors to falls among hospitalized older adults [38, 39], particularly among those with complex multisystem conditions. Although this study lacked granular data to identify which specific FRID class was most prevalent, available evidence has demonstrated that the most common FRIDs in acute care settings are central nervous system active medications, including opioids, benzodiazepines, and antipsychotics, followed by diuretics and antiepileptics [40, 41]. While the former are associated with dizziness, impaired balance, and postural instability, the latter elevate fall risk through orthostatic hypotension and sleep disruption induced by nocturia [42]. Nevertheless, a systematic review by Namiko et al. found no association between FRID discontinuation and the occurrence of falls [43]. This discrepancy likely reflects the multifactorial nature of falls; accordingly, deprescribing alone, without concurrent management of intrinsic and extrinsic risk factors, may prove insufficient [42]. Consequently, at hospital admission, benzodiazepines should be deprescribed or tapered where clinically feasible; opioid and antipsychotic regimens should be optimized with enhanced nursing monitoring during the inpatient stay; and diuretic administration timing should be adjusted to reduce nocturnal voiding and orthostatic loading, particularly before mobilization.

Notably, this study was conducted within a clinical environment where standardized fall risk assessments were routinely performed, and multifactorial fall prevention interventions were regularly implemented; nevertheless, falls still occurred in some patients. This phenomenon may be partially attributable to the incomplete implementation of proposed interventions. Wyss-Hänecke et al. [44]reported that even in well-managed hospitals, the implementation fidelity of multifactorial in-hospital fall prevention programs was suboptimal, with a median of merely 80.6% (range: 63.9–91.7%), accompanied by marked ward-level variations. Similarly, AlGhareeb et al. [45]found that fall prevention measures were not fully executed and their effectiveness varied considerably, influenced by heavy nursing workloads and patient clinical complexity. Furthermore, the latest Cochrane systematic review confirmed [46]that fall prevention in hospitals remains exceedingly difficult. However, multifactorial interventions may reduce fall rates and fall risk; the confidence intervals encompass the possibility of null effects or even slight increases in falls. Consequently, given the incomplete implementation and uncertain effectiveness of these interventions, the observed associations between multimorbidity patterns and falls in the present study should be interpreted as residual risks that may persist within the context of routine fall prevention, rather than as effects under intervention-free conditions. These results underscore that implementing fall prevention programs for older adults in hospital settings poses unique challenges for healthcare professionals. For older inpatients with multimorbidity, the exploration of personalized fall prevention strategies is urgently needed in future research.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to employ LCA to identify multimorbidity patterns and explore their associated factors among older adult fallers in the hospital. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this retrospective single-center study limits causal inference and generalizability. Hospital-specific organizational factors may yield divergent results across institutions. For example, centralized bed management policies determine patient proximity to nursing stations and nursing time per patient, which prior research has linked to unit-level fall incidence [47]. Nurse staffing levels, rounding frequency, and bed alarm protocols may also influence fall rates [48]. Consequently, the observed multimorbidity patterns may partly reflect the care organization at this single center rather than universal disease clustering among hospitalized fallers. Future multicenter, large-scale cohort studies with long-term follow-up are recommended to validate these findings. Second, the reason for admission among older adult fallers in the hospital was not captured, yet this is a critical contextual factor for distinguishing inpatient falls from those occurring in community settings. Existing evidence indicates that malnutrition upon admission, specific admission diagnoses such as stroke, and acute episodes of diseases including pneumonia and infection are significantly associated with the risk of inpatient falls [49, 50]. Consequently, the relative contributions of acute admission causes versus chronic conditions themselves could not be disentangled. Future studies should incorporate both acute admission diagnoses and chronic comorbidity factors to elucidate their respective roles in precipitating falls during hospitalization. Third, although we excluded immobilized patients to minimize the bias of low fall probability, this may selectively exclude the most severely affected stroke patients, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to a wider stroke population with different functional levels. Fourth, hepatic and renal diseases were broadly classified by organ system, which may have obscured heterogeneous associations between specific diseases within the same system and fall risk. Future research could adopt a more fine-grained analysis based on specific disease diagnoses. Furthermore, the data were derived from an electronic medical record system; given that information was documented after the fall event and no follow-up was conducted, underreporting of events and reporting bias were inevitable.

Conclusions

This study found that cancer was the most prevalent disease among older adult fallers in the hospital. Using LCA, four distinct multimorbidity patterns were identified in this population: the Stroke group, the Cancer group, the Multisystem multimorbidity group, and the Relatively healthy group. The distribution of these classes was significantly associated with factors such as age, risk assessment scores, and the number of high fall-risk medications. These findings provide a reference for identifying fall risk in older inpatients with multimorbidity, optimizing individualized intervention strategies, and rationally allocating healthcare resources. This study further emphasizes that fall prevention strategies should be tailored to different multimorbidity patterns, with particular attention given to patients in the Multisystem multimorbidity group. Furthermore, there is an urgent need to strengthen the consideration of multimorbidity in current fall prevention guidelines. Future research employing multicenter, large-sample prospective cohort designs is recommended to validate these findings and further explore the underlying mechanisms.
