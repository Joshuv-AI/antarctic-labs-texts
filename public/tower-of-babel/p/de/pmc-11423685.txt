Ctr9 promotes virulence of Candida albicans by regulating methionine metabolism
Authors: Jiyeon Park, Shinae Park, Jueun Kim, Yong-Joon Cho, Jung-Shin Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCandida albicans, a part of normal flora, is an opportunistic fungal pathogen and causes severe health issues in immunocompromised patients. Its pathogenicity is intricately linked to the transcriptional regulation of its metabolic pathways. Paf1 complex (Paf1C) is a crucial transcriptional regulator that is highly conserved in eukaryotes. The objective of this study was to explore the role of Paf1C in the metabolic pathways and how it influences the pathogenicity of C. albicans. Paf1C knockout mutant strains of C. albicans (ctr9Δ/Δ, leo1Δ/Δ, and cdc73Δ/Δ) were generated using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. To investigate the effect of Paf1C on pathogenicity, macrophage interaction assays and mouse survival tests were conducted. The growth patterns of the Paf1C knockout mutants were analyzed through spotting assays and growth curve measurements. Transcriptome analysis was conducted under yeast conditions (30°C without serum) and hyphal conditions (37°C with 10% FBS), to further elucidate the role of Paf1C in the pathogenicity of C. albicans. CTR9 deletion resulted in the attenuation of C. albicans virulence, in macrophage and mouse models. Furthermore, we confirmed that the reduced virulence of the ctr9Δ/Δ mutant can be attributed to a decrease in C. albicans cell abundance. Moreover, transcriptome analysis revealed that metabolic processes required for cell proliferation are impaired in ctr9Δ/Δ mutant. Notably, CTR9 deletion led to the downregulation of methionine biosynthetic genes and the cAMP-PKA signaling pathway-related hypha essential genes, which are pivotal for virulence. Our results suggest that Ctr9-regulated methionine metabolism is a crucial factor for determining C. albicans pathogenicity.KEYWORDS: Paf1 complex, Ctr9, Candida albicans, Pathogenicity, Methionine metabolism

Introduction

Candida albicans is an opportunistic pathogenic yeast that colonizes the skin and human gut as normal flora, typically maintaining a commensal relationship with its host. However, pathogenic traits emerge in response to external stimuli such as low pH, starvation, serum, N-acetylglucosamine, and CO2 [1]. The metabolic adaptability and flexibility are essential for infecting the host cells and the survival of the yeast. The ability of C. albicans to metabolically adapt to diverse host microenvironments is critical for its survival and pathogenicity [2].

C. albicans pathogenicity is closely related to its metabolic adaptability. Several metabolic pathways govern proliferation and the pivotal morphological shift from yeast to the hyphal form – a key factor in host cell invasion [3]. This morphogenesis is underpinned by a complex metabolic network involving nucleotide, amino acid, lipid, and iron metabolism, including specific pathways, such as arginine and methionine metabolism [4]. Although the roles of these metabolic pathways in virulence have been extensively studied, the contribution of transcriptional regulation of these metabolic pathways for virulence has remained underexplored [5–7].

The genomes of pathogenic organisms reflect the infection processes required for survival in the host niche. For example, they comprise genes encoding cell wall proteins required for initial adhesion, colonization, and resistance to immunological attacks [4]. Additionally, they comprise genes encoding various transcription factors, including RBF1, EFG1, and TUP1, serve as positive or negative regulators of virulence, and genes encoding secreted aspartyl proteases that participate in the invasion of the host endothelial barrier and macrophage phagocytosis [4]. Therefore, for successful survival in the host and infection of the host cells, transcription in C. albicans should be tightly controlled via transcriptional regulators.

RNA polymerase-associated factor 1 complex (Paf1C) has emerged as a key transcriptional regulator in the eukaryotes [8]. Consisting of core subunits (Paf1, Leo1, Cdc73, Rtf1, and Ctr9) and an additional subunit in humans (Ski8), Paf1C influences chromatin structure and transcriptional fidelity through epigenetic modifications [9,10]. Paf1C is indispensable for transcriptional regulation and gene expression in various organisms, affecting cell proliferation, tissue development, differentiation, and even tumorigenesis in mammalian cells [10–16]. The roles of Paf1C in various cancers and developmental processes underscore its biological significance; however, its function in fungal pathogenesis, particularly in driving the morphological differentiation critical for C. albicans virulence, remains unclear.

In this study, we investigated the role of Paf1C in C. albicans pathogenicity, with a particular focus on Ctr9-mediated regulation of fungal virulence. By constructing knockout mutants of the Paf1C complex components, including Ctr9, we analyzed their impact on the ability of C. albicans to proliferate in host cell environments and infect host cells. Using transcriptome analysis, we aimed to reveal how Ctr9-mediated transcriptional regulation of metabolic pathways affects pathogenic processes such as hyphal formation in fungi.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture condition

C. albicans wild-type SC5314 and all derived knockout mutants in this study were shown in Table S1. For spotting assay, YPD (1% yeast extracts, 2% peptone, 2% glucose), SD (0.67% yeast nitrogen base without amino acids, 2% glucose, and 2% agar), and SC (0.67% yeast nitrogen base without amino acids, 2% glucose, 2% agar, and 0.2% drop-out mix) were used. The drop-out mix was prepared as described in [17].

CRISPR-Cas9 system

The CTR9, LEO1, and CDC73 deletion stains (ctr9Δ/Δ, leo1Δ/Δ, and cdc73Δ/Δ) were constructed using CRISPR-Cas9 system as previously described [18]. pADH99 plasmid (HIS/FLP CAS9 expression plasmid) was used to get Cas9 Cassette, and it was purified through MSSI digestion. A fragment including promoter of conserved guide RNA is derived from pADH110 (universal plasmid template for A fragment) and B fragment including conserved guide RNA sequence and downstream sequence of HIS1 is derived from pADH147 (plasmid template for B fragment) by PCR purification. Purified A fragment and B fragment are linked to each other by stitching PCR to make C fragment. DNA materials, including Cas9 Cassette, purified C fragment and donor DNA, were transformed into SC5314 by electroporation. Cas9 Cassette and C fragment have Nourseothricin sequence, causing homologous recombination. This linked DNA fragment can be replaced with HIS1 sequence. Expressed Cas9 protein causes double-stranded breaks at target genes and donor DNA was inserted into that region. Table S2 represents used oligonucleotides in this study.

Macrophage infection

In the macrophage interaction test, RAW 264.7 macrophage cell line was used (1 × 105 cells per well) and cultured as previously described [19]. C. albicans was diluted as 1/4 × 105 CFU, 1/8 × 105 CFU, 1/16 × 105 CFU, and 1/32 × 105 CFU and incubated within ice with macrophage cells for 30 min. After 16 h at 37°C with 5% CO2, the fungal colonies were counted under a microscope in the 96 well plates where fungal cells and macrophages were cultured.

Mouse survival test

Treatment of all animals was conducted in compliance with Arrive Guidelines for Reporting Animal Research [20]. Female BALB/c mice were purchased from ORIENT BIO Inc. The mice were acclimated for 1 week before injection and were used at an age of 6 weeks (18 ± 2 g). They were housed in an environment with controlled temperature and humidity. For injection, the mice were randomly divided into four groups, each consisting of five individuals and placed in polycarbonate containers. Mice were infected via tail-vein injection with 106 CFU of C. albicans strains (SC5314, ctr9Δ/Δ, leo1Δ/Δ, and cdc73Δ/Δ) resuspended in 100 μL PBS. Condition of the mice was monitored every day, and the survival rate was observed for 16 days after injection. Mice exhibiting severe weight loss (more than 20% of body weight) during the monitoring period were euthanized with CO2 to reduce suffering and recorded as dead the next day.

Animal experiments were performed at the Kangwon National University Animal Laboratory Center and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Kangwon National University (Approval Number KW-210317-9).

Spotting assay

Wild-type and mutant strains were cultured in YPD at 30°C until OD600 = 1.0 and diluted from 2 × 105 cells/ml to 1.25 × 104 cells/ml. Diluted cells were spotted on agar plates (SD, SC, and YPD), and the result was observed after 16 h incubation.

RNA sequencing analysis

For RNA sequencing, yeast condition samples were cultured on YPD at 30°C and harvested at OD600 = 1.0. Hyphae condition samples were cultured on yeast condition until OD600 = 0.9, transferred to YPD with 10% FBS (Gibco 26,140–079) and cultured at 37°C, and harvested after 30 min induction.

Total RNA was extracted at OD600 = 1.0 by using NucleoSpin RNA kit (MN, Cat. #MN740955) and cDNA library was synthesized by using NEBNext® UltraTM Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, Cat. #E7420). RNA sequencing analysis of duplicated samples was performed as previously described [19]. Analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed with DEseq2 normalization on R studio. Heatmap was generated by the Pheatmap R package and Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) genome browser [21,22]. Gene Ontology (GO) annotation in this study is based on DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) functional annotation tools and PANTHER classification system (https://www.pantherdb.org/).

Sequencing data sets generated and/or analyzed during this study have been deposited to the Gene Expression Omnibus Database (GEO) under the accession number: GSE244665 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE244665).

Results

Ctr9 plays a central role in pathogenesis in vitro and in vivo

To examine the correlation between transcription elongation factor of C. albicans and its pathogenicity, we constructed knockout mutants of Paf1C components using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. We successfully generated CTR9, LEO1, and CDC73 knockout mutant strains. However, we were unable to construct PAF1 and RTF1 knockout mutant strains. Multiple attempts to disrupt the PAF1 and RTF1 genes did not yield viable knockout strains, suggesting that these genes may be important for C. albicans survival.

Two independent experiments – a macrophage interaction test and a mouse survival test – were performed using wild-type (WT), ctr9Δ/Δ, leo1Δ/Δ, and cdc73Δ/Δ mutant C. albicans strains. As C. albicans invades the surface of host cells, lyses them, and subsequently escapes, it forms colonies around the dead infected macrophages [23]. To determine the colony formation of C. albicans that survived exposure to macrophages, appropriately diluted strains of C. albicans were co-cultured with the mouse macrophage cell line Raw264.7. The number of colonies formed by three knockout mutant strains was lower compared to that formed by WT strain (Figure 1a). Specifically, the number of colonies formed by ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain was significantly lower (<50%) than that formed by the wild-type (Figure 1b).

Furthermore, after intravenous infection of the wild-type strain and three knockout mutants into BALB/c mice, the survival rate of the mice was observed for 16 days. Wild-type infected mice began dying on the 4th day after injection, and all mice died within 9 days. However, mice infected with the knockout mutant survived longer than those infected with the wild type (Figure 1c). This indicates that all knockout mutants of Paf1C components do not completely alter the virulence of C. albicans; however, it slows the rate of infection and killing of host cells. Notably, mice infected with ctr9Δ/Δ mutant survived the longest (Figure 1c), showing the most attenuated virulence, confirming in vitro macrophage test results (Figure 1a,b). Thus, Ctr9 May be an essential Paf1C component regulating C. albicans infection process and virulence.

Deletion of CTR9 affects growth of C. albicans

To confirm the effect of Paf1C subunits on the growth of C. albicans, we studied the growth rate of WT and knockout mutant strains in conditions conducive for yeast growth (YPD, 30°C). Although ctr9Δ/Δ mutant showed a slower growth phenotype than WT in the spotting assay, the growth of leo1Δ/Δ and cdc73Δ/Δ mutants was unaffected and similar to that of the WT strain (Figure 2a). Furthermore, ctr9Δ/Δ mutant showed a slight defect in growth rate in the exponential phase in contrast to leo1Δ/Δ and cdc73Δ/Δ mutants (Figure 2b). This suggests that the reduced abundance of C. albicans resulting from the deletion of CTR9 may impede preparation for infection. However, the growth rate mediated by Ctr9 alone does not account for the virulence of C. albicans, indicating that the role of Ctr9 in the pathogenicity requires further investigation.

Pathogenesis-related genes are sophisticatedly regulated by the transcription machinery during infection. Therefore, we hypothesized that the defective growth rate of ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain may be associated with the downregulation of an unknown gene group.

To clarify the genes involved in growth rate regulation, we analyzed transcriptomes of WT and ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strains. Therefore, the two strains were cultured under yeast (30°C and no serum) and hyphal (37°C and 10% FBS) growth conditions. RNA sequencing was performed in duplicates for each strain, and the results were clustered together for principal component analysis (PCA) (Figure 2c). First, we analyzed that the transcriptome of strains (n = 2 samples each) cultured under yeast growth conditions (YPD, 30°C), in which C. albicans proliferates. Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis revealed that 406 genes were downregulated in the ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain by more than twofold compared to that in WT (Figure 2d and Table S3), and 396 genes were aligned in the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID). Furthermore, numerous genes related to cellular processes and metabolism such as methionine biosynthetic process (GO:0009086), glycolytic process (GO:0006096), sulfate assimilation (GO:0006096), gluconeogenesis (GO:0006096), carbohydrate transport (GO:0008643), and leucine biosynthetic process (GO:0008643) were significantly and negatively regulated in the ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain (Figure 2e). Therefore, DEG analysis demonstrated that Ctr9 regulates cellular processes and metabolism at the transcriptional level and affects C. albicans abundance.

Ctr9 involves sulfate/methionine metabolism

To understand how Ctr9-mediated metabolism and virulence are correlated, we further analyzed the genes downregulated in the ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed that the majority of the downregulated genes belonged to metabolic pathways (Figure 3a). Moreover, a majority of these metabolic pathway genes were categorized under the metabolic interconversion enzyme group (PC00262), according to the Protein Analysis Through Evolutionary Relationships (PANTHER) database (Figure 3b). Using the Candida Genome Database (http://www.candidagenome.org/), we functionally annotated these metabolic pathway genes and found that 30 genes were greatly associated with sulfate and methionine biosynthetic functions (Figure 3c). Additionally, we identified eight genes involved in sulfur and methionine metabolism that were downregulated by more than twofold in the ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain compared with the WT strain. These genes were not classified under any metabolic pathway in the KEGG pathway database. Finally, we found 18 sulfate/methionine metabolism genes, 3 transporter genes, and MET4 among the downregulated genes. We defined them as SM22 genes and analyzed their expression levels and roles in sulfur/methionine metabolism (Figure 3d and Table 1). SM22 genes include sulfur metabolic genes (MET15, MET3, MET14, MET16, ECM17, MET10, HAL21, and HAL22), trans-sulfuration genes (CYS1, CYS3, and STR3), methionine biosynthetic genes (MET1, MET2, MET6, MET13, SAM2, MXR1, and MEU1), transporter genes (AGP3, MUP3, and SUL2), and an upstream regulator of methionine biosynthesis (MET4) [25]. Therefore, it can be suggested that in C. albicans, Ctr9 regulates the transcription of most SM22 genes.

Ctr9-mediated methionine metabolism positively regulates hyphal formation

Methionine synthesis and assimilation are crucial for the growth and virulence of various pathogens, including Salmonella enterica Typhimurium, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Aspergillus fumigatus [26–28]. In addition, methionine synthase was reported as an effective target for fungal infection, and methionine permease was confirmed to alter hyphal formation in C. albicans [29,30]. Therefore, we investigated how Ctr9-mediated methionine metabolism affects the proliferation and hyphal formation of in C. albicans.

The spotting assay confirmed that the growth of both WT and ctr9Δ/Δ mutant stains slightly decreases on methionine-depleted synthetic complete (SC) media compared to normal SC media. Furthermore, the ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain was not more affected by methionine depletion than WT (Figure 4a), indicating that Ctr9-mediated sulfate/methionine metabolism does not influence C. albicans cell abundance. Additionally, we examined that growth phenotype of ctr9Δ/Δ mutant in methionine-supplemented media by adding methionine to synthetic dropout (SD) media. Although supplementing methionine led to a slight increase in growth of both WT and ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strains, the growth of ctr9Δ/Δ mutant did not recover to WT levels (Figure 4a). Despite the unclear effects of excess methionine on cellular metabolism, we added excessive methionine to SC media. When methionine was added at ten times the normal concentration in regular SC medium, the growth of both WT and ctr9Δ/Δ mutants recovered (Figure S1a). However, even with the tenfold increase in methionine concentration, the growth of the ctr9Δ/Δ mutant did not fully return to the levels observed in the WT. These results suggest that Ctr9 either directly regulates C. albicans growth or uses a metabolic pathway other than methionine biosynthesis.

By responding to environmental signals, C. albicans enters hyphal formation stage via activation of signal transduction pathways such as the cAMP-PKA, amino acid-induced signal transduction, and MAPK pathways [31]. Thus, through morphological change, C. albicans acquires the ability to invade the cell membranes of host cells. Hyphal formation is directly related to infection and virulence. Moreover, methionine serves as a primary inducer of morphogenesis, initiating a switch from yeast to hyphal growth [32].

To test whether defective methionine metabolism in the ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain affects C. albicans hyphal formation, we stimulated WT and ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strains with hyphal induction signals (37°C and 10% FBS) and observed hyphal elongation. In SC media, WT and ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strains showed increased hyphal formation by hyphal induction signals (Figure 4b). However, in methionine-depletion media, hyphal formation of ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain in response to hyphal induction was reduced compared to that of WT (Figure 4b). We also confirmed that the filamentation capacity of ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain increases in the presence of excess of methionine (Figure S1b). This indicates that Ctr9-regulated methionine metabolism is required for hyphal formation during the infective stage. Notably, the deletion of other components of Paf1C (leo1Δ/Δ and cdc73Δ/Δ) did not inhibit hyphal formation in methionine-depleted media.

To identify the correlation between Ctr9, methionine metabolism, and hyphal formation, we performed transcriptome analysis using samples extracted after hyphal induction. Under the hyphal induction signals, the SM22 genes were downregulated in the ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain compared with the WT strain (Figure 4c). Although several SM22 genes were also downregulated in the leo1Δ/Δ and cdc73Δ/Δ mutants, the reduction in their expression was not as pronounced as that observed in the ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain. The expression of all SM22 genes, except CYS3, MEU1, and SUL2, was significantly (padj < 0.05) downregulated in the ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain compared to that in the WT (Table S4). Notably, inhibiting SPE2, which encodes S-adenosyl methionine (SAM) decarboxylase, markedly impairs methionine-induced morphogenesis in certain media and substantially attenuates virulence in mouse models [33]. SPE2 was downregulated in ctr9Δ/Δ mutant strain cultured under the hyphal growth conditions (Figure 4d). Thus, Ctr9 is required for the methionine metabolism-mediated hypha formation in C. albicans.

C. albicans morphogenesis, and consequent virulence, induced by activation of the cAMP-PKA pathway requires methionine [34,35]. A notable target of this pathway is the transcription factor Efg1, which stimulates the expression of various hyphal-specific genes by activating the transcription factor Ume6 [31]. To determine which genes related to hyphal formation are affected by Ctr9-mediated methionine metabolism, we focused on the transcriptional levels of hypha-essential genes [36]. Although the expression of hypha-associated genes, including ECE1, HWP1, and ALS3, was increased in the ctr9Δ/Δ mutant due to signaling pathways activated by 37°C and serum (Figure S2a and S2b), we confirmed a significant downregulation of EFG1 and UME6 under hypha-induced conditions (37°C and 10% FBS) (Figure 4e). Thus, we propose that Ctr9 affects hyphal formation by controlling the expression of EFG1 and UME6 through the regulation of methionine metabolism.

In summary, Ctr9 plays a crucial role in the pathogenicity of C. albicans. This study highlights that Ctr9 controls sulfur and methionine biosynthesis pathways. While Ctr9-regulated methionine metabolism does not markedly influence the growth of yeast cells, it plays a significant role in the infective hyphal formation. This was achieved through the impact of Ctr9 on the expression of genes essential for hyphal development, highlighting the intricate relationship between methionine metabolism and the regulation of virulence factors in C. albicans (Figure 5).

Suggested model.

Ctr9 is required for the regulation of cell proliferation and hyphal formation of C. albicans. It is essential for the transcription of genes associated with cellular metabolic processes. Consequently, this regulation impacts the cell growth rate before the infection. Notably, among various metabolic pathways, sulfate/methionine metabolism involves hyphal formation during the infection, and the transcription of genes related to sulfate/methionine metabolism is dependent on Ctr9. As a result, we propose that Ctr9-mediated regulation of methionine metabolism controls hyphal formation, thereby significantly influencing the virulence of C. albicans during the infection process.

Discussion

Methionine plays a pivotal role in the metabolism and regulation of growth in fungi, being produced through the methionine biosynthesis pathway [37]. This pathway is very complex and is an important cellular metabolism pathway in most eukaryotes. The gene encoding methionine synthase is crucial for the viability and virulence of A. fumigatus, making methionine synthase a potential target for antifungal agents [28,38]. In C. albicans, methionine is required for cAMP-PKA-mediated morphogenesis and virulence [33]. Inhibition of the methionine permease Mup1 causes defective hyphal growth and biofilm formation, leading to attenuated virulence [30].

The transcriptional regulation of genes within the methionine metabolic pathway has been investigated in diverse filamentous fungi and Saccharomyces cerevisiae. In S. cerevisiae, methionine metabolic pathway is governed by a network of five transcription factors (Met4, Cbf1, Met28, and Met31/Met32) [39]. In Alternaria alternata, the methionine biosynthesis regulator, AaMetR, significantly contributes to oxidative stress tolerance and virulence [40]. Although Met4 and Cbf1 are known to control this pathway in C. albicans [33], the precise upstream transcriptional regulators of the genes encoding these two proteins remain unclear. In this study, we identified that Ctr9 is essential for the expression of methionine biosynthetic pathway genes and their transcriptional regulator Met4. Ctr9 is required for virulence by regulating cell proliferation and methionine metabolism-mediated hyphal formation in C. albicans.

We have confirmed that Ctr9 plays a crucial role in cell proliferation and hyphal formation of C. albicans by modulating the expression of genes involved in metabolic processes, such as methionine biosynthesis. Recent studies have established that Ctr9 in Drosophila orchestrates the expression of genes associated with lipid metabolism and nucleotide biosynthesis during nervous system development [41]. Downregulation of CTR9 also causes morphological defects in germ cell nuclei [42]. This underscores the essential role of Ctr9 in cell proliferation and differentiation. Taken together with previous studies, our results confirm the role of Ctr9 in cellular metabolism, cell proliferation, and differentiation. However, further studies are required to determine how Ctr9 specifically regulates methionine biosynthetic genes.

We have demonstrated that in contrast to LEO1 and CDC73 deletions, deletion of CTR9 causes reduced hyphal formation when plated on methionine-depleted media. The Paf1C is vital for histone H2B ubiquitination (H2Bub) and H3K4 methylation by Set1 [43,44]. Paf1, Ctr9, and Rtf1 are indispensable for these methylation processes. However, the absence of Leo1 and Cdc73 does not markedly affect H2Bub-dependent H3K4 methylation compared with the absence of Paf1, Ctr9, and Rtf1 [45,46]. This observation necessitates a deeper exploration into how Paf1C-regulated histone modifications affect methionine metabolism. Research on the influence of chromatin regulators that modulate histone modification on the pathogenicity of C. albicans remains insufficient. Prior studies have identified that methionine is particularly susceptible to oxidation by reactive oxygen species (ROS) [47] and the H3K4 methyltransferase Set1 plays a crucial role in C. albicans pathogenicity by managing the transcriptional levels of genes related to intracellular ROS [19]. Through a deeper understanding of the complex interactions among histone modification, Paf1C, and C. albicans virulence, we can potentially uncover new strategies for developing antifungal agents.

Our findings show that essential genes such as MET4 and SPE2 are downregulated in CTR9 knockout mutants further elucidate the intricate link between Ctr9 function, methionine metabolism, and the pathogen’s virulent capabilities. Furthermore, our analysis sheds light on how Ctr9-mediated methionine metabolism is critical for the activation of the cAMP-PKA pathway, through which it regulates the expression of hyphae-specific transcription factors like EFG1 and UME6, thereby orchestrating the fungal pathogen’s morphogenesis and virulence.

Conclusion

In this study, we highlight the critical role of Ctr9 in the pathogenesis of C. albicans, elucidating its essential function in cell proliferation, hyphal formation, and methionine metabolism. We found that the absence of Ctr9 significantly impairs C. albicans virulence in in vitro and in vivo models. Notably, the ctr9Δ/Δ mutant displayed reduced macrophage lethality and extended survival in mouse models, underscoring the pivotal role of Ctr9 in fungal virulence.

Our research further delves into the transcriptional landscape altered by CTR9 deletion, revealing a profound downregulation of genes within the methionine metabolic pathway. Furthermore, our findings demonstrate that Ctr9-mediated methionine metabolism directly influences hyphal formation, which is a key factor in the infection process. The attenuation of hyphal formation in ctr9Δ/Δ mutants, particularly under methionine-depleted conditions, highlights the importance of Ctr9 in the morphological transitions of C. albicans. Additionally, the observed downregulation of key genes (such as MET4 and SPE2) in ctr9Δ/Δ mutants highlights the intricate relationship between Ctr9, methionine metabolism, and virulent capabilities. Moreover, the downregulation of EGF1 and UME6 reveals the necessity of Ctr9-mediated methionine metabolism for hyphal induction via the cAMP-PKA signaling pathway.

However, future research is needed to uncover specific interactions and regulatory networks involving Ctr9. This exploration could deepen our understanding of the complex interplay between metabolism and C. albicans virulence, opening up new possibilities for the effective control and treatment of fungal infections.
