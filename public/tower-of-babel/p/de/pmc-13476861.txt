A Bilateral Primary Non-refluxing Non-obstructed Megaureter Presenting as Chronic Flank Pain in an Adult Female Patient: A Case Report
Authors: Hasan F Buali, Tarek Abushloa, Khaled Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A primary megaureter is a congenital ureteral anomaly most commonly diagnosed in the pediatric population. Bilateral presentation in adults is exceptionally rare and poses a diagnostic challenge given the broad differential diagnosis of hydroureteronephrosis. A 56-year-old woman presented to the urology clinic with chronic intermittent bilateral flank pain. Computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis confirmed bilateral hydroureteronephrosis with ureteral dilatation measuring 16 mm on the right and 11 mm on the left, with no obstructing lesion identified. Cystoscopy with bilateral retrograde pyelograms demonstrated bilateral ureteral and pelvicalyceal dilatation with free contrast drainage and no filling defects, excluding obstruction. A cystogram excluded vesicoureteral reflux. The diagnosis of a bilateral primary non-refluxing non-obstructed megaureter was established by exclusion. The patient was managed conservatively with six-monthly renal ultrasound surveillance and remained asymptomatic at follow-up. A bilateral primary non-refluxing non-obstructed megaureter is a rare but important diagnosis in adults presenting with hydroureteronephrosis. A systematic workup excluding obstruction and reflux is essential. Conservative management is appropriate in patients with preserved renal function and absent complications.

Introduction

A megaureter is defined as a ureter exceeding 7 mm in diameter and represents one of the most common congenital anomalies of the upper urinary tract [1]. Almost one quarter of children referred to a pediatric urologist for obstructive uropathy suffer from an obstructive megaureter [2]. Not all megaureters are due to obstruction. Some result from vesicoureteral reflux, and many represent a developmental variant that resolves spontaneously with observation [2].

The condition is classified into primary and secondary forms. Primary megaureters arise from an intrinsic ureteral abnormality, most commonly an adynamic juxtavesical segment at the ureterovesical junction. They are further subdivided into obstructed, refluxing, non-refluxing non-obstructed, and mixed subtypes [3]. The pathophysiology of primary megaureters is multifactorial. Histological studies have demonstrated excess collagen deposition and abnormal interstitial cell populations in the obstructed ureteral segment, which impair peristaltic conduction and lead to progressive ureteral dilatation [4]. Secondary megaureters result from extrinsic causes including bladder outlet obstruction, neurogenic bladder, or vesicoureteral reflux [3].

The Pfister-Hendren grading system classifies primary megaureters into three grades based on the degree of ureteral and pelvicalyceal dilatation. This grading guides management and predicts prognosis [5]. Grades I and II carry a recognized potential for spontaneous resolution, particularly in the pediatric population. Grade III carries a poor prognosis for conservative management [5,6].

Primary megaureters are predominantly a pediatric diagnosis. The widespread use of fetal ultrasonography has allowed earlier identification, and physicians are increasingly faced with the task of distinguishing which patients require intervention and which do not [2]. Adult presentation is uncommon, and bilateral adult presentation is exceptionally rare, with only a small number of cases reported in the literature [1,7,8]. When identified in adults, primary megaureters often represent a long-standing congenital anomaly that remained clinically silent for decades. It may then manifest with nonspecific symptoms such as flank pain or be discovered incidentally on imaging [3,7].

We report a case of a bilateral primary non-refluxing non-obstructed megaureter in a 56-year-old woman presenting with chronic bilateral flank pain. The diagnosis was established through systematic exclusion of secondary causes, and the patient was managed conservatively.

Case presentation

A 56-year-old woman with a background of type 2 diabetes mellitus presented to the urology outpatient clinic with chronic intermittent bilateral flank pain. She reported no lower urinary tract symptoms, no macroscopic hematuria, and no prior urinary tract infections. There was no previous urological history.

Physical examination was unremarkable. Serum creatinine was 52.9 µmol/L (reference range 49-90 µmol/L) and serum urea was 3.8 mmol/L (reference range 3-7 mmol/L). These values confirmed preserved renal function. Urinalysis demonstrated no white blood cells or red blood cells. Urine culture was sterile.

Computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis confirmed bilateral hydroureteronephrosis. The right ureter measured 16 mm in diameter, and the left ureter measured 11 mm in diameter. No obstructing hyperdense calculi were identified. Bilateral extra-renal pelvis configuration was noted. The right side demonstrated moderate hydroureteronephrosis, and the left side demonstrated mild changes (Figures 1, 2).

Cystoscopy was subsequently performed. The urethra was normal. Systematic cystoscopic examination demonstrated a normal bladder mucosa with no tumors, stones, trabeculations, or diverticula. Both ureteric orifices were identified in their normal positions and appeared normal. A cystogram was then performed. The bladder was filled with approximately 700 mL of contrast. Imaging demonstrated a smooth-walled bladder with a well-defined outline. No vesicoureteral reflux was observed during filling or drainage phases (Figure 3). A formal voiding phase was not performed

Bilateral retrograde pyelograms were subsequently performed. Contrast injection into both ureters demonstrated dilatation of the ureters and pelvicalyceal systems bilaterally (Figures 4-7). No filling defects were identified. Contrast drained freely from both collecting systems without evidence of obstruction.

The absence of vesicoureteral reflux on cystogram and the absence of obstruction on retrograde pyelogram established the diagnosis of a bilateral primary non-refluxing non-obstructed megaureter. Given preserved bilateral renal function, absence of complications, and the non-obstructed, non-refluxing classification, the patient was managed conservatively with six-monthly renal ultrasound surveillance. At the time of reporting, the patient remained asymptomatic.

Discussion

A primary non-refluxing non-obstructed megaureter is the rarest subtype of primary megaureter and is considered a diagnosis of exclusion [1]. The pathophysiology involves an adynamic ureteral segment at the ureterovesical junction that impairs peristaltic propagation without causing true obstruction or reflux [3]. Histological studies have shown that the obstructed ureteral segment contains excess collagen intervening between smooth muscle fibers and abnormal interstitial cell populations, all of which contribute to impaired ureteral peristalsis [4]. In the pediatric population, this subtype frequently resolves spontaneously during early childhood, with reported spontaneous resolution rates of up to 40% in some series [6].

Adult presentation of primary megaureters is uncommon. The condition may remain clinically silent for decades, with ureteral dilatation identified during evaluation of nonspecific flank pain or incidentally on imaging performed for unrelated indications [7]. Ogunfusika and Adewoye reported an 80-year-old female patient with incidentally discovered bilateral megaureters and no urological symptoms, illustrating the potential for lifelong subclinical disease [7]. Shrateh et al. reported a 43-year-old male patient with a bilateral primary non-refluxing non-obstructed megaureter presenting with dull loin pain, closely mirroring the clinical presentation in our case [1]. Yoshinaga et al. reported a series of seven adult patients with megaureters, all female patients, ranging in age from 46 to 67 years, suggesting a possible female predominance in adult presentation, though the series was small [8].

Bilateral presentation in adults is exceptionally rare. The cases reported by Shrateh et al. and Ogunfusika and Adewoye represent the closest parallels in the current literature. Both were managed conservatively in the absence of functional impairment [1,7]. Our case adds to this limited body of evidence and supports conservative management as a safe and appropriate strategy in adults with bilateral primary non-refluxing non-obstructed megaureters and preserved renal function.

The diagnostic workup in this case followed a systematic approach. Computed tomography excluded obstructing calculi and defined the degree and laterality of ureteral dilatation. Primary megaureters complicated by urolithiasis have been reported in adults and should be excluded during the initial workup, as stones may be the presenting feature of an underlying congenital ureteral anomaly [9]. Cystoscopy with cystogram excluded vesicoureteral reflux. Bilateral retrograde pyelograms excluded obstruction and filling defects. This stepwise approach allowed definitive classification without the need for nuclear medicine functional imaging [1,7].

The absence of diuretic renography represents a significant limitation of this workup. MAG-3 renal scintigraphy is the key investigation for conclusively distinguishing obstructed from non-obstructed megaureters, as it provides objective data on differential renal function and drainage kinetics for each renal unit [1,5]. A baseline renal scan would additionally serve as a reference point should the patient subsequently develop complications such as urinary tract infections, pyelonephritis, or progressive functional deterioration. In the present case, the non-obstructed classification was based on indirect evidence - normal serum creatinine and free contrast drainage on bilateral retrograde pyelograms. The decision to proceed without nuclear medicine imaging was based on these findings and the clinical decision to manage conservatively. This pragmatic approach is supported by the preserved renal function observed in comparable adult cases managed without scintigraphy [7]. Furthermore, the cystogram was performed under filling and drainage phases only. A formal voiding cystourethrogram was not obtained, which represents an additional limitation in the conclusive exclusion of vesicoureteral reflux. Renal ultrasound with post-void residual measurement was not performed prior to the procedure and would have provided useful baseline information regarding bladder function given the large bladder capacity observed at cystography.

Surgical intervention, including ureteral reimplantation with or without tapering, remains the standard treatment for symptomatic or functionally significant primary megaureters [8]. Endoscopic balloon dilatation of the ureterovesical junction has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative to open reimplantation, particularly in the pediatric population. A systematic review reported success rates ranging from 35% to 97% for endoscopic treatment of primary obstructive megaureters, with a post-operative complication rate of 23-60% [10]. Endoscopic treatment can be performed across a wide age range and avoids the morbidity of open ureteral reconstruction [11]. In adults with preserved renal function and absent complications, conservative surveillance is appropriate [1,7]. The present patient was placed on six-monthly renal ultrasound surveillance. This interval allows timely identification of progressive dilatation or functional deterioration that would warrant reassessment of the management strategy. Criteria for intervention would include progressive hydroureteronephrosis, deteriorating renal function, recurrent urinary tract infections, or new symptoms.

Conclusions

A bilateral primary non-refluxing non-obstructed megaureter is a rare congenital anomaly that can present in adulthood with nonspecific flank pain or be discovered incidentally. A systematic diagnostic workup excluding secondary causes, obstruction, and reflux is essential to establish the diagnosis. In adults with preserved renal function and no complications, conservative management with regular imaging surveillance is safe and appropriate. This case contributes to the limited literature on bilateral adult primary megaureters and supports a non-interventional approach in well-selected patients.
