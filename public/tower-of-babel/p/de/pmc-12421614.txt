Youth Med.Info: a case study in co-design of mental health medicines-information resources for children, young people and their parents/guardians
Authors: Caroline Hynes-Ryan, Ian Kelleher, Sophie Kathryn, Charlie Beaudelot, Aoife Carolan, David Columb, Sarah Donnelly, Nimantha Gamage, Nessa Hill, Maria Migone, Stephen McWilliams, Joseph Morning, Brian O’Donoghue, Izzie Solan, Judith Strawbridge, John Christopher Hayden, Dolores Keating
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo address the lack of accurate and accessible mental health medicines-information resources for children, young people and their parents/guardians using design thinking to co-design free-to-use, video resources tailored to this audience.DesignA multiphase qualitative case study using the Double Diamond model of Design Thinking: Discover, Define, Develop and Deliver. This included iterative prototyping, thematic analysis and public and patient involvement throughout.SettingDublin, Ireland with online distribution of the final resources internationally through a free, open-access platform.ParticipantsA multidisciplinary co-design team including two specialist mental health pharmacists, two academic pharmacists, five consultant psychiatrists, a psychiatric nurse, a youth content specialist, three youth activists and a parent representative.Results26 co-designed, medicines-information videos were created, including versions for children (voiced by children), parents/guardians and young people. Videos feature storytelling formats with Bitmoji characters. Feedback from youth and parent collaborators guided design and content. Since launch, www.youthmed.info has had over 25 000 website views and more than 30 000 video views, with engagement from over 91 countries. The resources are also linked on national and international clinical and charity platforms.ConclusionsYouth Med.Info addresses a gap in accessible, accurate mental health medicines-information by placing users – children, young people, parents/guardians and clinicians—at the centre of its design.

Introduction

Youth mental health

Mental health conditions are among the leading causes of illness and disability in adolescents and young adults.1 Their prevalence and diagnosis have been increasing over recent years, and globally it is estimated that 1 in 7 (14%) of 10–19-year-olds experience mental health difficulties.13 Similarly, we have seen an increase in the prescription for children and young people of mental health medicines.4 5 However, the mental health medicines-information resources that accompany these prescriptions require improvement. While the information in the manufacturers’ patient information leaflets is accurate, the content is dictated by regulatory requirements and has a greater emphasis on the side-effects of medicines than on benefits.6 Additionally, access to these leaflets in the medicine box occurs too late in the process—after the medicine has been prescribed and dispensed.7 The comprehension and usability of manufacturers’ patient information leaflets is in fact recognised as a problem internationally and not just within the mental health medicine space.8 9 Children, young people and their parents/guardians find that there are few reliable resources available, while those that exist are difficult to navigate.7 Many such individuals want to be able to relate the use of medicines to their everyday experiences but cannot tell what information is important. Knowing the benefits and side-effects of medicines can influence adherence positively, but similarly young people and their parents/guardians report a dearth of information on mental health medicines to be a barrier to treatment adherence.10 It is clear therefore that this group is underserved by current medicines-information resources.

The internet and social media for mental health information

The internet is the primary source of healthcare information for young people and adults alike.11 The vast majority (94%) of 8–12-year-olds and all 12–16-year-olds in Ireland own a personal smart device.12 13 These data are similar in other developed countries with 95% of US 13–17-year-olds reporting having access to a smart phone.14 From 2022 to 2024, YouTube, TikTok, Instagram and SnapChat were among the most commonly used video platforms by children, young people and adults.1214 In addition to their use for social engagement, YouTube, TikTok and Instagram are increasingly used by young people and parents/guardians as search tools for queries about mental health diagnoses and treatments.15 16 However, as health information videos on these sites are not peer-reviewed nor aligned to any specific quality standards, search results often return misleading/incorrect information.11 17 18 On TikTok, for example, 52% of all videos on ADHD have been classified as ‘misleading’.19 There is also evidence of the potential for the TikTok algorithm to exacerbate eating disorder symptoms.20 Given that the results returned from a keyword search are based on popularity, relevancy and view history, rather than content quality, it is difficult to filter out inaccurate content.17 Those with content knowledge (clinicians) may not possess the necessary skills, time nor funding to produce high-quality video resources, which represents a barrier to producing reliable content for children, young people and their parents/guardians on the platforms they engage with.21

Design Thinking

To overcome this barrier and develop accurate and accessible mental health medicines-information resources for children, young people and their parents/guardians, we employed Design Thinking. Design Thinking is a human-centred approach to innovation.22 It is an iterative, non-linear process that prioritises empathy by putting the user at the centre of every element of the design process and considers what is both technologically feasible and economically viable.23 24 Within health services research, there has been an increasing interest in the potential for human-centred design.25 Co-design methodologies are also increasingly being used to develop mental health interventions with young people (aged 10–24 years) including apps (eg, SlowMo, a blended digital cognitive-behavioural therapy tool co-designed to help people with paranoia in psychosis); websites (eg, www.MomentumHub.org.au, a free self-help platform offering tailored programmes for anxiety, depression and sleep issues co-designed by hundreds of children, adolescents parents and clinicians); and service frameworks.2629 Since 2018, co-design methodologies have been incorporated into mainstream research frameworks as principles of inclusion, empowerment and impact and have been promoted by research and funding institutions.30 With mental health medicines-information resources, the needs of children, young people and parents/guardians must be considered, as must those of clinicians prescribing and monitoring the medicines. As well as focusing on users’ needs, this approach considers issues such as technology and available resources which ensures solutions are immediately implementable.25 In this study, we aimed to develop accessible and accurate mental health medicines-information resources to support and empower children, young people and their parents/guardians using a Design Thinking approach. Here, we describe a case study in the co-design of these resources under the four core activities of the Double Diamond model.

Methodology

Framework

The Double Diamond model (figure 1) offers a framework for the design and delivery of services using four core activities—Discover, Define, Develop and Deliver.30 Through a process of exploring (converging), the full range of stakeholder needs for improvement before balancing and refining needs into a clear statement of the challenge (diverging), followed by an exploration of the wide range of possible concepts and potential solutions (converging) before committing to a specific solution and the means to deliver it into practice (diverging). The Double Diamond model is widely cited as a framework for capturing the essence of good design practice.30

Double Diamond model.

Co-design team

We formed a panel of specialist collaborators made up of two specialist mental health pharmacists (project lead and co-lead), two academic pharmacists, four consultant child and adolescent psychiatrists, a consultant general adult psychiatrist, a child and adolescent psychiatric nurse, a youth mental health content specialist, three youth activists and a parent representative from a charity organisation.

Public and patient involvement (PPI)

As our project used co-design methodology, public and patient involvement (PPI) involvement was integral to each aspect of the study. We engaged with two youth/child charity organisations who facilitated the involvement of young people and parents as key collaborators. Young people (>16) and parents were invited to be involved by a facilitator from both collaborating organisations who provided interested parties with an outline of the project and what their involvement would entail. Both organisations have their own process for volunteer recruitment which we adopted and consent was implied in the individual’s agreement to become part of the co-design team. The concept of this study was based on the experience of children, young people and their parents with mental health medicines, and they guided study design and dissemination of results as described. As ethical approval is not needed when people are involved in shaping research design, materials or dissemination (ie, co-design), and this research did not involve the collection of any patient identifiable data, research ethics committee approval was not required.

Discover

The Discover phase of the Double Diamond model explores current needs and gathers insights into the challenge. To better understand the content and quality of medicines-information resources available to children, young people and their parents/guardians, we examined existing resources beginning with those provided by the medicine manufacturers.

Patient information leaflets

We devised a list of the most used mental health medicines by children and young people which was validated by our clinical collaborators (online supplemental appendix 1). We then examined the manufacturers’ patient information leaflets which are available in hard copy in the medicine box or online via www.hpra.ie (Ireland) and https://products.mhra.gov.uk/ (UK). We reviewed the content of these leaflets, counted the number of pages and words for each leaflet and assessed their readability using a readability tool—the Flesch Reading Ease Scale (an internationally recognised test to measure a document’s readability)31

Video content on social media

As mental health medicines-information is also available on the online video platforms with which children, young people and parents/guardians engage, we examined existing medicines-information videos on YouTube, TikTok and Instagram. We searched for this content using the generic and brand names of the medicines on our pre-defined list (online supplemental appendix 1).

Digital and technology expert engagement

The other aspect of our Discover process involved engaging with several digital and technology experts to better understand the digital space. We held in-person and virtual meetings with the mental health content editor from a youth information and support platform, a digital expert from a technology company, a talent and learning partner at a digital marketing company and an operations officer at a healthcare technology company.

Define

The Define phase involves balancing the range of needs identified during the Discover process and articulating a clear statement of the challenge to be resolved. We reviewed the results of our Discover phase and refined our ideas to define the problem we were hoping to resolve.

Develop

The Develop phase is where solutions to the challenge are generated and evaluated. We determined that in order to meet the needs of children, young people and parents/guardians seeking information on mental health medicines, we needed to combine the technically accurate manufacturers’ patient information leaflets and clinician-led medicines-information videos on social media with the meaningful lived experience of individuals taking a mental health medicine.

Prototype video development

We produced three prototype medicines-information videos on fluoxetine (antidepressant), olanzapine (antipsychotic) and methylphenidate (stimulant). As information linked to a story is more easily remembered, we applied a narrative approach to our content.32 Stories make ideas and experiences familiar, predictable and comforting and increase people’s understanding and empathy.32 We determined therefore that the videos would tell the story of a child/young person’s journey while taking a mental health medicine.

We drafted three scripts outlining the most relevant clinical and practical information relating to the medicines sourced from the manufacturers’ patient information leaflets and adapted in line with the experience of the clinical collaborators. To appeal to a diverse audience, we designed characters with coloured hair (fluoxetine, green; olanzapine, purple; methylphenidate, orange) using BitMojis (cartoon avatars) from SnapChat who tell the story of their mental health medicine journey.

As the project was not externally funded, we were not able to seek the services of an animator to produce the videos or an actor to record the voiceover. As Design Thinking considers not only what is desirable but also what is financially feasible, we created the content ourselves. Using an online design platform—www.canva.com—we produced the three prototype videos with the project lead recording the voiceover on a smartphone. Each point from the scripts corresponds to a slide in the videos with associated imagery.

Initial clinical collaborator feedback

To ensure that the information in the videos was clinically appropriate and acceptable, we sought collaborator feedback and validation on the prototype videos. This was achieved through a series of in-person and virtual interviews with semistructured questions with nine clinical collaborators (online supplemental appendix 2).

Youth activist feedback

Once we had agreed on the relevant clinical information for each medicine, we evaluated the three pilot videos with a focus group of three youth activists. This was facilitated by the content editor for an online youth information and support platform and guided by semi-structured questions (online supplemental appendix 2).

Phase 2 and 3 video development

Having iterated the three pilot videos on fluoxetine, olanzapine and methylphenidate based on the collaborator feedback, we then produced another five videos under the same medicine categories, namely two antidepressants (sertraline and venlafaxine), two antipsychotics (risperidone and aripiprazole) and one stimulant (lisdexamfetamine). These five videos underwent the same rigorous development and evaluation process as the initial three (with our clinical and youth activist collaborators). Six months after the publication of this suite of eight videos, we produced another eight videos on atomoxetine, clozapine, guanfacine, lorazepam, melatonin, mirtazapine, promethazine and quetiapine using the same process.

Child and parent feedback

On foot of requests from our clinical collaborators and following the same Design Thinking principles, we produced three prototype videos for children and their parents/guardians. We drafted three scripts on methylphenidate, lisdexamfetamine and melatonin in the same format as that used for the youth videos. However, to appeal to the younger audience, we used simpler language and omitted information considered inappropriate to children (details of neurotransmitters, side-effects, advice on brand switching for methylphenidate). We adopted a comic-book style and used children for the voiceovers. We made two versions for each medicine—one targeting children (kid videos) and one for parents/guardians (parent/guardian videos) that could be watched with or without their child. The parent/guardian videos contain more details on how the medicines work as well as listing the most common side-effects associated with treatment.

Although our clinical collaborators for this suite of videos were the same, we engaged with a parent and child support group from a national charity supporting neurodivergent children. Feedback was gathered over four virtual interviews with a parent representative who was liaising with a support group of parents and children with ADHD. The interviews were guided by semistructured questions (online supplemental Appendix 2).

On agreeing a framework for the ‘kid’ and ‘parent/guardian’ videos, we produced another two videos on the non-stimulant medicines for ADHD—atomoxetine and guanfacine—following the same rigorous development and evaluation process.

Deliver

The Deliver phase is the final step in the Double Diamond model where all the work comes together, and the new service can be launched. During this phase, we identified how best to share the resources that we co-designed so that they are freely available to any children, young people, parents/guardians or clinicians who need them.

Data analysis

All feedback, comments and considerations gathered from the clinical, young person and parent/child collaborators were collated together, analysed and coded into themes. Actions were discussed by the project lead and co-lead, and where there were conflicting comments, the project lead and co-lead agreed on the outcome. Amendments were made to the videos based on this process and a second draft of each video was sent to all collaborators for final sign off.

Standardised format of manufacturers’ patient information leaflets for adults and children

Three of the manufacturers’ patient information leaflets that we reviewed—atomoxetine, lisdexamfetamine and methylphenidate—include a separate section with ‘Information for children and young people’ (table 1). While this section is shorter (approximately 600 words), more succinct and contains simpler language, it too has more of a focus on side-effects and risks and lacks information of how the medicine works, time to effect and other practical information. An equivalent section is not included in the patient information leaflets for the other medicines licensed for use in those <18 in Ireland—sertraline, risperidone, aripiprazole and guanfacine. None of the medicines indicated for adult-only use include information tailored to children and young people.

The manufacturers’ patient information leaflets are between 6 and 12 pages long and contain between 3000 and 5000 words. As the average child, adolescent and young adult reads at 80–185, 195–250 and 238 words per minute, respectively, for comprehension, it would take them 17–30 min to read these leaflets.33 The leaflets also scored poorly on the Flesch Reading Ease Scale (table 2). All leaflets scored between 30–49 making them ‘difficult’ to read. The ‘Information for Children and Young People’ sections in the atomoxetine, lisdexamfetamine and methylphenidate leaflets scored between 60 and 69—easily understood by 13–15-year-olds. However, these medicines are indicated from age 6, making them less suitable for this age group. Although the information in the manufacturers’ patient information leaflets is clinically accurate, it is lengthy, complex and jargonistic.

Information for parents/guardians of children taking medicines is also available at www.medicinesforchildren.org/uk.34 This free-to-use resource provides information in a similar style to the manufacturers’ patient information leaflets and notes the fact that many medicines are not licensed for use in children. Patient information on mental health medicines can also be obtained from another UK-based website, but as this is a paid-subscription service, it is not accessible to all.

Video content on social media

On reviewing the existing medicines-information videos available on YouTube, TikTok and Instagram, two styles of videos were identified. The first were clinician-led, where psychiatrists, pharmacists or other mental healthcare professionals filmed themselves providing a verbal summary of what they considered to be the most important information about the medicines in question. These videos mostly focused on the indications and associated side-effects. Videos averaged under 5 mintues in length and included clinically accurate information. The other style of videos identified was those made by people with lived experience of taking medicines or caring for a child taking medicines. Typically, individuals filmed themselves reflecting on their experiences (both positive and negative), with updates on how the medicines were impacting their day-to-day lives.

While SnapChat (a multimedia instant messaging application) does not support medicines-information video sharing in the same way as YouTube, TikTok and Instagram, we also reviewed its functionality given its popularity among children and young people. Of particular interest was the SnapChat BitMoji, a cartoon avatar that can be dropped into an array of settings which can be sent on the messaging platform as a form of visual communication. It is a familiar icon and something we identified as useful for our project to depict the characters telling the story of their medicine journey.

Digital and technology expert engagement

Our engagement with the digital and technology experts provided clarification around how best to develop online content and what resources were available. They provided advice and support around the format of the medicines-information videos as well as the platforms where the videos should be hosted.

Define

On foot of the results from the Discover phase, we refined our ideas in order to define the problem we were hoping to solve—how we might collaboratively and cost-efficiently design an independent and free-to-use mental health medicines-information resource that is engaging, inclusive, accurate and accessible in order to empower children, young people and their parents/guardians to make informed decisions about medicines and feel supported on their journey.

Based on the results of our Discover and Define phases, we had a clear objective on what we needed to do to address the problem at hand—produce our own medicines-information videos.

Deliver

For the final phase of our Design Thinking process, we brought everything together and developed an independent brand guided by our youth collaborators—Youth Med.Info—with a logo and website: www.youthmed.info. On this free-to-use platform, each medicine has a unique page where the information video is available to view as well as a printable summary leaflet and a list of clickable links where more detailed information may be sought. This includes the manufacturers’ patient information leaflet, the NHS.UK Medicines A-Z page (https://www.nhs.uk/medicines/) (if available), the Irish national link to a mental health medicines-information resource and the Medicines for Children website for the ‘kids’ videos (https://www.medicinesforchildren.org.uk/medicines/). www.youthmed.info also hosts printable QR code posters for clinicians to use when sharing the medicines-information resources with children, young people or their parents/guardians during clinic appointments. In the ‘About Us’ section, we share details of all collaborators along with their own BitMoji. In the ‘Testimonials’ section, we share in-use feedback from those who have used the resources.

To further promote the resource and outline the role of medicine for those navigating a mental health condition or ADHD, we developed two short trailer videos detailing the content of the videos and who made them. We set up accounts on YouTube (@YouthMedInfo), TikTok (@YouthMed.Info) and Instagram (@Youth.Med.Info) where we posted the completed medicines-information videos, trailer videos and information about the website and resources.

We officially launched the new resource in October 2023. We developed and delivered a strategic stakeholder engagement plan. This involved a stakeholder mapping exercise to engage strategically with professional bodies, service providers and advocacy groups. We found that the independence of the project (unfunded by any vested interests), the expertise used to create the content (both lived and clinical) and the motivation to do the project were key facilitators to engagement. www.youthmed.info has seen over 25 000 website views with over 30 000 video views across the three social media channels. While 50% of website views are from Ireland, over 23% are from the UK, 9% from the USA, 5% from the Netherlands, 2% from Finland and Australia, and the remainder are from 91 other countries. The website is also linked from the websites of national organisations in Ireland—https://adhdireland.ie/youth-medication-videos/— as well as internally within a number of mental health settings in Ireland and the UK.

Discussion

Summary

Youth Med.Info provides accurate and accessible information on mental health medicines to a group that is underserved by current resources. As the use of mental health medicines for children and young people is increasing, there is a clear need for reliable mental health medicines-information resources in an acceptable format. The youth activist and parent collaborators who co-designed our resources reported that they found mental health medicines-information difficult to navigate. They also found it challenging to relate the use of the medicines to their everyday experiences. Not feeling considered when it comes to medicines-information resources adds to the stigma associated with mental health conditions and treatment for children and young people; the idea of taking an ‘adult-only’ medicine can be overwhelming. Given the growing popularity of social media platforms such as YouTube, TikTok and Instagram as search engines among young people and parents/guardians, accessible and reliable mental health medicines-information resources should be compatible with them. Addressing problems like this requires an interdisciplinary, creative and collaborative approach to problem-solving—a Design Thinking approach.25 The Double Diamond model allowed us to explore current needs and solutions and gather insights into the challenge by reviewing the existing medicines-information available. We were then able to balance the range of needs and articulate a clear statement of the problem to be solved—how we might design a free-to-use, engaging, inclusive, accurate and accessible mental health medicines-information resource for children, young people and their parents/guardians. This led us to generate and evaluate solutions by engaging with and listening to our clinical, young person and parent collaborators and ultimately launch the new service (www.youthmed.info).

Clinical implications

While this new resource was developed to support children, young people and parents/guardians of those navigating a mental health recovery journey or ADHD and their treating clinicians, it will also be of benefit to other healthcare practitioners. Community pharmacists, general practitioners, social workers, psychologists, occupational therapists, teachers and others may benefit from the availability of these resources both to signpost children, young people and their parents/guardians but also for their own education and understanding of mental health medicines. Studies suggest that healthcare professionals are less confident in providing mental healthcare than physical healthcare.35 The videos could therefore facilitate a more open conversation about mental health medicines with the assurance that the language used is acceptable to this group. Our co-design approach could also be applied to other healthcare settings where information resources are not meeting the needs of service users.

Future directions

Active participation of children, young people, families and caregivers in the design of their services is recommended internationally and most recently in the WHO quality standards for child and youth mental health.3638 So too is the use of digital technologies to support child and youth mental health including to empower this group to make informed decisions about their care.37 Providing accurate information to users, in an easy-to-understand manner, about medicine side-effects, therapeutic alternatives, monitoring and what to do in case there is a concern about medicine is also recommended.38 As our resource fulfils a number of these quality standards, we are keen to add more content on mental health medicines to our platform including videos on the frequency and management of common side-effects associated with mental health medicines (weight gain from antipsychotic medicines, nausea/vomiting from antidepressants, loss of appetite from stimulants). Future research will focus on evaluating the impact of our resources on children, young people, parents/guardians and clinicians by establishing if providing accurate and accessible medicines-information improves engagement, adherence to treatment and outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

We recognise that there are limitations to our processes and resulting videos as they were not made professionally. However, our youth collaborators reported that they found the graphics acceptable and were able to relate to the Bitmoji characters. Our parent collaborators reported that their children found the comic-book style graphics engaging and benefited from hearing the voice of a child talking about the medicines which made the process less isolating and scary.

We based the content of our information videos on what we deemed to be the most relevant clinical and practical information from the manufacturers’ patient information leaflets. However, we acknowledge the limitations of not reviewing the broader literature—systematic and umbrella reviews or clinical guidelines—to ensure the comprehensiveness and evidence base of our video content. As we were keen to ensure the videos were short and succinct, the amount of information we could include was limited. Nevertheless, we aspire to adopt a broader approach to the development of future resources.

To our knowledge, our videos are the only mental health medicines-information resources co-designed by children, young people, parents/guardians and clinicians worldwide. This is corroborated by the engagement we are seeing internationally. However, they will require maintenance and updating as new medicines-information becomes available.

Conclusion

Youth Med.Info addresses an unmet need for freely available, accurate and accessible mental health medicines-information. By adopting a human-centred design approach to addressing this need, we ensured that the voice of the user—children, young people, parents/guardians and clinicians—was central to the design. In the words of one of our youth activists, ‘The voice of young people matters most in their own mental healthcare. Youth Med.Info empowers young people navigating mental health treatment with clear and accessible information’.
