Longitudinal serum metabolomics predicts therapeutic outcome in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Qing Cai, Wanying Liu, Changjian Yan, Jiazheng Li, Yan Huang, Xiang Li, Qinwen Yang, Xiaoyu Wei, Huilin Yang, Guanbin Zhang, Ting Yang, Yanxin Chen, Jianda Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Our study compares metabolic samples collected at multiple time points pre- and post-chemotherapy, performs longitudinal integrated analysis to characterize dynamic alterations, and assesses the temporal impacts of therapeutic responses.•By integrating pre- and post-treatment metabolic index with clinical indicators, we developed predictive models for treatment outcomes. The pre-treatment metabolic index model (AUC=0.9143, 95 %CI:0.816–1) and the after-treatment metabolic index (AUC=0.9136, 95 %CI:0.83–1) both demonstrated excellent predictive performance for AML therapeutic outcomes.•The dynamic metabolic reprogramming landscape serves as a robust indicator of AML treatment efficacy, offering novel directions for precision therapy in AML.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a highly heterogeneous malignant clonal disorder originating from hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells, with a five-year survival rate below 30 % [1], underscoring the urgent need to identify reliable biomarkers for predicting and improving therapeutic outcomes. Recent advances in understanding AML pathogenesis and genetic mutations (e.g., FLT3, IDH1/2) have led to targeted therapies; however, patient response rates remain highly variable, suggesting critical roles for non-genetic factors such as metabolic reprogramming in disease progression [2].

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer cells [[3], [4], [5]]. Leukemic cells and their microenvironment, frequently exposed to hostile conditions and stressors, exhibit dysregulated metabolic processes involved in energy production and biosynthesis, including alterations in glucose metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and fatty acid oxidation [6]. Perturbed metabolic pathways or fluctuations in specific metabolite concentrations may drive disease progression, immune evasion, or therapy resistance. For instance, Bradley et al. performed untargeted metabolomic profiling on seven AML cell lines exhibiting differential chemosensitivity to cytarabine (Cytarabine) and the anthracycline doxorubicin, revealing that resistance to doxorubicin was associated with extensive metabolic reprogramming involving lipid catabolism, enhanced fatty acid β-oxidation, upregulated amino acid uptake, amplified glycine‑serine one-carbon metabolism, and elevated glutathione biosynthesis [7]. Alpha-keto-glutarate dehydrogenase represents a critical metabolic dependency in Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML), where it supports leukemic maintenance by fuelling anabolic biosynthetic pathways [8]. Newly diagnosed AML patients also exhibit abnormalities in protein biosynthesis and fatty acid metabolism. Gilteritinib inhibits glutamine uptake and metabolic utilization in FLT3-ITD-positive AML. Furthermore, a significant reduction in tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) activity was observed following gilteritinib treatment [9,10]. In AML, decreased levels of PCs, LPCs, SMs, and PEs are frequently observed, primarily due to the Warburg effect and the reliance on extracellular lipid uptake for tumor growth [11].Cindy Arévalo reported a decrease in glycerophospholipids alongside an increase in glycerolipids in Acute leukemias (AL) patients [12].These findings highlight the importance of monitoring specific metabolic alterations as a strategy to enhance treatment efficacy.

Despite accumulating evidence of metabolic reprogramming in AML, systematic investigations into dynamic metabolic changes remain scarce. Most studies focus solely on diagnostic or single-treatment timepoints, failing to capture the adaptive remodeling of metabolic networks during therapy.Current clinical indicators (e.g., cytogenetic risk stratification) exhibit limited predictive power for therapeutic outcomes. The dynamic impact of AML metabolic heterogeneity on treatment response and the potential of metabolic signatures as predictive biomarkers remain unexplored. The novelty of this study lies in systematically delineating temporal metabolic profiles through longitudinal multi-phase sampling (pre-chemotherapy, during chemotherapy, and after-chemotherapy). This design enables not only the identification of baseline metabolic aberrations in AML patients compared to healthy controls but also tracks chemotherapy-induced metabolic adaptations, thereby elucidating the dynamic mechanisms by which metabolic heterogeneity influences therapeutic responsiveness. We collected 144 longitudinal samples across multiple treatment phases to characterize the heterogeneity of AML metabolic profiles at different stages. This approach facilitated the identification of key metabolites and pathways associated with treatment response.Furthermore, by integrating pre- and after-treatment metabolic biomarkers with clinical parameters (e.g., age, risk stratification), we developed predictive metabolic models for AML therapeutic outcomes. These models provide a theoretical foundation for developing metabolism-targeted combination therapies.

Serum sample collection

We collected a total of 159 serum samples derived from peripheral venous blood of 29 patients with AML and 15 healthy individuals. Among these, 15 samples were from healthy controls, and 144 samples were from AML patients. Specifically, the AML patient samples were grouped as follows: 29 samples before chemotherapy (BT group), 17 samples on days 1–6 of chemotherapy (AT1 group), 29 samples on day 7 of chemotherapy (AT2 group), 17 samples on days 8–14 (AT3 group), 23 samples on days 15–21 (AT4 group), 10 samples on days 22–28 (AT5 group), and 22 samples on days 29–51 (AT6 group).After an overnight fasting period, blood was collected using blood collection tubes and processed according to the following procedure to isolate serum: The blood was allowed to clot at room temperature for 60 min, followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then collected, aliquoted into appropriately labeled centrifuge tubes, and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 10 min. Serum samples were stored at −80 °C until metabolite extraction was performed.

Metabolite extraction

The sample, stored in a −80 °C freezer, was thawed on ice and briefly vortexed for 10 s. To 200 μL of the sample, 200 μL of an extraction solution (ACN:Methanol = 1:4, V/V) containing an internal standard was added. The mixture was vortexed for 3 min and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. A total of 350 μL of the supernatant was collected and concentrated to dryness. The residue was then reconstituted in 150 μL of a solution (Methanol:Water = 7:3, V/V), followed by vortexing for 3 min and sonication for 10 min in an ice water bath. After another centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C, 120 μL of the supernatant was transferred for LC-MS analysis.

QTOF-MS/MS

The Triple TOF mass spectrometer was selected for its capability to acquire MS/MS spectra on an information-dependent acquisition (IDA) basis during LC/MS experiments. Utilizing the acquisition software (TripleTOF 6600, AB SCIEX), the system continuously evaluates full scan survey MS data. It triggers the acquisition of MS/MS spectra based on pre-established criteria. In each cycle, twelve precursor ions with an intensity exceeding 100 were chosen for fragmentation at a collision energy (CE) of 30 eV (resulting in 12 MS/MS events, each with a product ion accumulation time of 50 milliseconds).The ESI source parameters were configured as follows: Ion Source Gas 1 and Gas 2 were both set to 50 Psi, Curtain Gas to 25 Psi, the source temperature to 500 °C, and the Ion Spray Voltage Floating (ISVF) was set to 5500 V in positive mode or −4500 V in negative mode. These settings facilitated optimal ionization and detection conditions for comprehensive metabolomic profiling.

Metabolomics data preprocessing

LC-MS ion information was processed using Compound Discovery (CD) software (Thermo, USA) to perform tasks such as peak extraction, peak alignment, detection of unknown compounds, prediction of unknown compositions, and database matching. The software also provided the final ion feature peak information. After quality control correction using the QC-RLSC (quality control-robust LOESS signal correction) model, the obtained ion information was normalized and log2-transformed. The identification of human endogenous metabolites was based on analysis using the HMDB database. Subsequent analyses were performed using the R programming language.

Data normalization

For the metabolic data from the normal group and the disease group, the normalize Between Arrays function from the limma package was used to correct for quantitative differences in the data. During this process, two boxplots were generated to display the sample distributions before and after normalization(Figure S2).

PLS-DA analysis

Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was performed using the pls package in R. The plsr function was used to construct the PLS-DA model, selecting an appropriate number of principal components (ncomp) and training the model based on the metabolic data and group labels. Subsequently, a score plot was used to visualize the distribution of samples in the principal component space, allowing for the evaluation of differences between groups and illustrating the contribution of metabolic features to the principal components.

Differential expression analysis

Intergroup differential analysis was performed using the wilcoxauc function from the presto package to screen for differentially abundant metabolites between groups, and the results were saved. To further analyze, an appropriate statistical significance threshold was set to filter significantly different genes, followed by intersection analysis using the UpSetR package to plot the intersection of metabolites across different groups. Based on the differential analysis results, a volcano plot was generated to display the log2 fold change and adjusted P-values of significant metabolites. The volcano plot was created using the scRNAtoolVis package, and metabolites with avg_|log2FC| > 0.1 and P_val < 0.05 were selected for subsequent heatmap generation.

Enrichment analysis and functional annotation

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package (we collected pathways closely related to AML, including "Amino Acid Metabolism," "Carbohydrate Metabolism," "Energy Metabolism," "Lipid Metabolism," "Metabolism of Cofactors and Vitamins," and "Nucleotide Metabolism"; see Table S2). Pathway analysis was conducted based on differentially expressed metabolites to obtain enriched pathway information, and the results were visualized.

Visualization

Boxplots of multiple metabolites were generated using the ggplot2 package to display differences in metabolic compound levels between groups. Additionally, the cowplot package was used to combine multiple plots, generating and saving four differential analysis figures for easy comparison of differences under various conditions.

Weighted scoring of metabolic compound sets

We identified eight significantly upregulated metabolites prior to treatment (log₂FC > 0.1 and p< 0.05), including "C01657", "C09816", "C05593", "C00811", "C20793", and "C22029". Using the standard deviations of these metabolites as weights, we applied a weighted scoring method to summarize the metabolite set. A weighted score was calculated for each sample based on the levels and standard deviations of the metabolites within the set (Supplementary Materials for detailed code). For the after-treatment metabolic model, we screened for significantly differential metabolites at the AT6 stage (log₂FC < 0.1 and p < 0.05), obtaining a total of 16 metabolites: "C00755", "C02376", "C17935", "C00739", "C06677", "C05646", "C00780", "C05512", "C00224", "C02419", "C02712", "C02496", "C18853", "C07148", "C11980", and "C08493". The after-treatment metabolic index was calculated using a method similar to that employed for the pre-treatment metabolic index.

Univariate and multivariate regression analysis

Univariate regression analysis was performed on pre-treatment metabolic indices and clinical information using a logistic regression model (glm function from the stats package), and significant variables were selected. Variable selection and model optimization were conducted using the autoReg package, followed by multivariate regression modeling to identify key variables associated with clinical treatment response.

Variable screening and model performance evaluation

A rigorous variable selection and analysis pipeline was implemented to enhance the model's robustness and reliability: Variables with a p-value > 0.05 were first excluded through univariable logistic regression; subsequently, variable correlations were assessed and variance inflation factor (VIF) testing was performed (excluding variables with correlation > 0.3 and VIF > 5); finally, variable selection was conducted using LASSO-regularized logistic regression with 5-fold cross-validation. A multivariable logistic regression model was ultimately developed. The performance of the model was evaluated using ROC curves. Forest plots generated by the forestmodel package were used to display the regression coefficients and confidence intervals of each variable. Furthermore, ROC curves were plotted using the pROC package, and AUC values were calculated to assess the model's predictive ability. Additionally, nomograms were created using the regplot package to illustrate the impact of each variable on model predictions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and software development in this study were performed using R 4.4.1. Data following a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For pairwise comparisons, an independent samples t-test was used, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA). For data not following a normal distribution, the median and interquartile range [P25, P75] are presented. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test (for two groups) and the Kruskal-Wallis test (for multiple groups). Categorical data are expressed as frequency and percentage ( %), and the chi-square (χ²) test was used to compare ratios between groups. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

This study included 159 serum samples derived from peripheral venous blood, comprising 29 AML patients and 15 healthy volunteers. The demographic and clinicopathological profiles of the metabolomic cohort are presented in Table 1, with detailed clinical characteristics of the 29-AML-patient cohort provided in Table S1. Results demonstrated a balanced distribution of sex and age across groups, with no significant intergroup differences. Based on the ELN2022 risk stratification criteria, the majority of AML patients were classified into unfavorable.

Metabolomic differences between AML patients and healthy controls

We performed untargeted metabolomics profiling on serum samples using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), which combines the high-resolution separation of liquid chromatography with the high sensitivity of mass spectrometry to comprehensively identify and quantify metabolites. Through differential analysis of untargeted metabolites, we aimed to uncover dynamic metabolic changes across different treatment stages and screen for potential biomarkers. PLS-DA, a supervised multivariate statistical method, was employed to maximize inter-group metabolic differences. Compared to principal component analysis (PCA), PLS-DA demonstrates superior efficiency in mining group-specific metabolic variations. As shown in Fig. 1A, the PLS-DA score plot revealed tight clustering of AML samples across timepoints, with clear separation from the healthy control (HC) group, indicating pronounced global metabolic dysregulation in AML. A scatter plot (Fig. 1B) highlighted significant differential metabolites between AML patients and HCs, including pseudouridine, O-phospho-L‑serine, l-aspartate, and 2-deoxy-d-ribose 1-phosphate. The distribution characteristics of these differential metabolites across different groups are illustrated in detail (Fig. 1C), particularly those exhibiting significant alterations in multiple sample groups, offering valuable insights into the pathogenesis of AML. To further investigate the potential role of these differential metabolites in AML, we conducted subsequent validation studies, including an assessment of the prognostic relevance of key metabolic alterations through Kaplan–Meier(K-M) survival analysis based on metabolite levels. K-M analysis demonstrated that patients with elevated concentrations of pseudouridine and O-phospho-L‑serine had a significantly poorer overall survival (OS) (Fig. 3A-B).No significant difference was observed in relapse-free survival (RFS) based on pseudouridine and O-phospho-L‑serine concentrations(Fig. 3C-D). We further investigated the effect of pseudouridine on AML cell proliferation. The results revealed that treatment with 2 μg/mL pseudouridine for 96 h enhanced the proliferation of both Molm13 and U937(Fig. 3E-F). Furthermore, to explore the potential mechanisms, we analyzed key enzyme genes regulating these differential metabolites using genome-wide CRISPR screening data from DepMap (https://depmap.org/portal/) to evaluate their effects on AML cell proliferation [15]. PUS7 encodes the key enzyme responsible for pseudouridylation [16], PSPH for serine biosynthesis [17], CAD for aspartate metabolism [[18], [19]], and TYMP for deoxyribose utilization [20]. Knockout of each gene in AML cell lines yielded gene effect values <0 (where lower values indicate greater essentiality for proliferation), with PUS7 =–0.06, PSPH = –0.12, CAD = –0.21, and TYMP = –0.18. For AT3 group, the enrichment focused on the Pentose phosphate pathway, Galactose metabolism, Steroid hormone biosynthesis, Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (Fig. 3L). In AT4 group, the main enrichments were seen in Steroid hormone biosynthesis, TCA cycle, and Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis (Fig. 3M). AT5 group's differential metabolic pathways were mainly concentrated in Phenylalanine metabolism and Cysteine and methionine metabolism (Fig. 3N). Lastly, for AT6 group, the primary enrichments were in Phenylalanine metabolism and Tryptophan metabolism (Fig. 3O). This comprehensive analysis highlights the specific metabolic pathways that are significantly affected during different stages of treatment in CR versus non-CR patients, providing insights into potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for AML.

Predicting treatment response in AML using Pre- and after-treatment metabolic indexes

To evaluate the predictive performance of the pre-treatment metabolic index for treatment response, ROC analysis and AUC calculations were performed. The pre-treatment metabolic index demonstrated excellent discriminatory power in distinguishing CR from Non-CR patients, with an AUC of 0.886 (95 % CI: 0.765–1.00)(Fig. 4a). The pre-treatment metabolic index was evaluated using K-M survival analysis. The results indicated a trend toward worse OS in the high-level group, though this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.089; Figure S7A). In contrast, a significant difference was observed in RFS(P = 0.022; Figure S7C). The final model was built based on the variables selected through this process. Two variables (Risk Classification and pre-treatment metabolic index) obtained through logistic regression analysis were evaluated for their association with treatment response (Fig. 4b). Dynamic changes in metabolic profiles during treatment may also carry important information. Therefore, we further investigated the predictive ability of the after-treatment metabolic index for treatment response. Potential predictive factors, including after-treatment metabolic index, age, gender, FLT3 mutation status, and risk stratification, were initially screened using univariate logistic regression. Three variables showing statistical significance (P < 0.05) were included in the multivariate logistic regression model(Table S10). Furthermore, significant collinearity was observed between the after-treatment metabolic index and other clinical characteristics, which would have introduced redundancy in a multivariable model (Figure S6G-I). Therefore, we did not pursue constructing a combined after-treatment metabolic model. We evaluated the discriminatory power of the after-treatment metabolic index alone, which demonstrated excellent performance in distinguishing CR from Non-CR patients, with an AUC of 0.9316 (95 % CI:0.83 –1.00) (Fig. 4g). Although not statistically significant, elevated after-treatment metabolic index levels demonstrated a positive trend correlating with both OS(P = 0.13; Figure S7B) and RFS(P = 0.1; Figure S7D) improvement. We conducted an analysis of the relationship between treatment regimens and after-treatment index, and found that patients who completed standard IA treatment had higher after-treatment metabolic indices(Figure S7E).

Discussion

AML, a highly heterogeneous malignant clonal disorder, accounts for approximately 80 % of adult acute leukemia cases [21]. Its incidence has risen steadily in recent decades, posing a significant threat to global health. Current therapeutic strategies for AML include chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation [22]. Despite notable advancements in treatment efficacy, the overall prognosis remains poor due to chemoresistance, with approximately 50 % of patients developing resistance during therapy, leading to disease progression or relapse [23]. Consequently, elucidating mechanisms underlying AML pathogenesis, progression, and therapeutic resistance is critical for improving clinical outcomes.Metabolic reprogramming, a hallmark of malignancy, is intricately linked to tumorigenesis and prognosis [24]. In AML, metabolic remodeling-driven by dysregulated energy metabolism, redox homeostasis imbalance and aberrant epigenetic modifications-serves as a pathological cornerstone for leukemic clonal expansion, differentiation arrest, and chemoresistance [[25], [26]]. This metabolic dependency offers a therapeutic vulnerability. Studies demonstrate that AML cells adaptively enhance glycolytic flux (Warburg effect), activate mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation, and remodel glutamine-dependent TCA cycle activity to meet biosynthetic demands. Concurrently, NAD+/NADH ratio dysregulation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) modulation, and metabolite-mediated DNA methylation/histone modifications collectively foster drug resistance [[27], [28]].In FLT3-ITD-mutated AML, significant alterations in glycolysis, glycerophospholipid metabolism, TCA cycle, gluconeogenesis, fatty acid metabolism, and glutamine metabolism have been observed. Inhibiting oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis, or glutamine metabolism suppresses leukemic proliferation and synergizes with cytarabine (Cytarabine) to overcome chemoresistance. Furthermore, AML cells upregulate serum insulin-like growth factor binding protein 1 (IGFBP1), inducing systemic insulin resistance and impairing glucose uptake in normal tissues [29]. Compared to normal hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells, AML cells exhibit heightened OXPHOS activity.This study innovatively employed a longitudinal multi-phase sampling strategy (from diagnosis through treatment) to dynamically monitor temporal metabolic profiles, uncovering continuous metabolic remodeling patterns in AML [30]. We further constructed pre- and after-treatment metabolic indices to predict therapeutic response. Our findings highlight potential therapeutic targets within the AML metabolome and inform strategies for precision therapy. Below, we discuss key insights into metabolic heterogeneity, critical pathway regulation, clinical translation, and future directions.

Our PLS-DA analysis revealed significant metabolic heterogeneity between AML patients and healthy controls (Fig. 1). Key upregulated metabolites in AML included pseudouridine, O-phospho-L‑serine, l-aspartate, and 2-deoxy-d-ribose 1-phosphate (adjusted P < 0.05), potentially linked to dysregulated nucleotide biosynthesis and amino acid metabolic reprogramming. Pseudouridine, a critical RNA modification molecule [31], may stabilize oncogenic mRNAs (e.g., MYC, BCL2), perpetuating leukemogenesis. The accumulation of 2-deoxy-d-ribose 1-phosphate, a deoxyribonucleotide precursor, suggests enhanced salvage synthesis pathways in AML to fuel rapid proliferation and chemoresistance. l-aspartate, acting as a signaling molecule, may activate NMDA receptors, driving aggressive phenotypes via eIF5A and TGFβ pathways—a mechanism previously implicated in metastatic breast cancer [32]. Phosphoserine is an intermediate product in serine metabolism. Serine, a crucial non-essential amino acid, serves multiple roles in tumor cells: it is utilized for protein synthesis; acts as a precursor for glycine and cysteine, participating in sphingolipid and phospholipid synthesis; and is cleaved via the folate-mediated one-carbon pathway to generate glycine and one-carbon units for the synthesis of porphyrins, thymidylate, purines, glutathione and SAM [33]. Serine metabolism not only supplies essential precursors for the synthesis of proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids in tumor cells but also provides reducing equivalents to maintain redox homeostasis. Thus, serine metabolism is critical for tumor growth. Additionally, studies indicate that serine metabolism regulates macrophage polarization and function in vivo [34]. These differential metabolites may serve as potential biomarkers for AML screening. In pre-chemotherapy AML serum, 2-amino-2-deoxy-d-gluconate and d-lysine were elevated in CR versus non-CR groups (Fig. 2), implicating amino sugar metabolism and lysine catabolism in differentiation and epigenetic normalization. Conversely, α-d-galactosamine 1-phosphate, 4-coumarate and 3-hydroxyphenylacetate were enriched in non-CR patients, suggesting glyco-lipid metabolic rewiring and antioxidant-driven resistance. ROC analysis identified these metabolites as robust predictors (AUC: 0.72–0.86), with alpha-d-Galactosamine 1-phosphate showing the highest discriminative power (AUC = 0.86, 95 % CI: 0.72–1.0). d-lysine enrichment in CR patients may enhance anti-leukemic immunity via mTOR-mediated T-cell metabolic reprogramming or histone crotonylation (Kcr) modulation—a process regulated by crotonyl-CoA. In contrast, persistent phenylalanine metabolism abnormalities in non-CR groups correlated with endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and caspase-dependent apoptosis. Phenylalanine deprivation disrupts TCA cycle flux and lipid/purine synthesis, while binding to ER-resident VAPB proteins triggers proteostatic imbalance [35]. Multi-phase PLS-DA (B1–B6 subplots) demonstrated sustained metabolic divergence between CR and non-CR groups across treatment phases (Fig. 3). Despite temporal heterogeneity in pathway enrichment, phenylalanine metabolism and lysine degradation emerged as conserved resistance hubs. Lysine catabolism suppression inversely correlates with GCDH mRNA levels and enhances NK/CD8+ T-cell infiltration, while crotonyl-CoA accumulation promotes tumor growth via ECHS1 downregulation [36].These findings nominate phenylalanine/lysine metabolic axes as dynamic biomarkers for early resistance detection and therapeutic targeting.

In this study, by constructing pre- and after-treatment metabolic indices (Fig. 4), we systematically revealed, for the first time, the predictive value of dynamic metabolic features for CR in AML patients and their potential therapeutic implications. Both the pre-treatment metabolic index (AUC=0.8857, 95 % CI: 0.7647–1) and after-treatment metabolic index (AUC=0.9316, 95 % CI: 0.83–1) demonstrated excellent predictive performance for CR status (Fig. 4a and g). The nomogram (Fig. 4c) quantified CR probability into a visual scoring system by integrating metabolic indices with clinical parameters, providing a practical tool for personalized treatment decisions. For example, high-risk patients with a after-treatment metabolic index >265 (corresponding to a CR probability <15 %) may require intensified therapies such as allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. This model overcomes the limitations of traditional static stratification and offers the possibility of real-time treatment adjustment. Although this study validated the clinical utility of metabolic indices, their molecular mechanisms require further elucidation. For instance, are these indices associated with specific driver mutations (e.g., FLT3-ITD, IDH1/2). Additionally, our study is a single-center investigation with a relatively small sample size, which may be subject to selection bias and limited the ability to perform more detailed subgroup analyses. Furthermore,the generalizability of the nomogram needs verification in prospective cohorts, and precision therapeutic strategies guided by metabolic indices (e.g., combining metabolic inhibitors with chemotherapy) warrant exploration. Future studies should integrate multi-omics data to construct metabolic-genomic integrated predictive models, advancing AML treatment into the era of metabolic precision medicine.

In conclusion,in this study, we constructed a time-specific metabolic index based on the differential metabolites before and after treatment, and further developed dynamic predictive metabolic models for pre-treatment and after-treatment. The dynamic metabolic reprogramming atlas not only serves as a biomarker for monitoring AML treatment efficacy to meet clinical testing needs, but also reveals metabolic heterogeneity that can be translated into novel targets for precision therapy. This provides a theoretical basis for constructing personalized treatment strategies based on metabolic intervention.
