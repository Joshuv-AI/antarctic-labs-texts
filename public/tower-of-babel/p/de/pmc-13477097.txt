From routine to rescue: Higher elective aortic surgery volume is associated with improved outcomes after acute type A aortic dissection repair
Authors: Kanhua Yin, Patrick J. Warner, Charley Sun, Michael Zhu, Laura M. Piechura, Prem S. Shekar, Yong Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association between case volume and outcomes in acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) repair is well established; however, the impact of elective proximal aortic surgery volume on emergent ATAAD repair outcomes remains unclear.MethodsUsing the 2017-2020 Nationwide Readmission Database, we identified 22,700 elective proximal aortic repairs and classified hospitals into high-volume (median, 58 cases/year), medium-volume (median, 22 cases/year), and low-volume (median, 5 cases/year) groups. We analyzed 10,793 emergent ATAAD repairs performed at these hospitals. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included stroke, renal failure necessitating dialysis, cardiac bleeding, respiratory failure necessitating ventilation, sepsis, vascular complications, and failure to rescue (FTR), defined as mortality occurring after any of these complications. Multivariable logistic regression assessed associations between hospital volume and outcomes.ResultsOverall, 74.4% of ATAAD repairs occurred at low- or medium-volume hospitals. High-volume hospitals had greater ATAAD case volume (median, 25 vs 12 vs 5 cases/year; P < .001). Baseline characteristics and malperfusion rates were similar across the groups. In-hospital mortality was lowest at high-volume hospitals (10.7% vs 15.7% vs 19.5%; P < .001). Compared to high-volume hospitals, low-volume (odds ratio [OR], 1.58; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.30-1.92; P < .001) and medium-volume (OR, 1.30; 95% CI, 1.08-1.56; P = .005) hospitals were independently associated with higher mortality. Low-volume hospitals were associated with higher FTR (OR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.10-1.73; P = .005).ConclusionsHigher elective proximal aortic surgery volume is independently associated with improved ATAAD outcomes. Concentrating elective volume and developing dedicated aortic teams may improve preparedness and outcomes.

Central Message

Higher elective proximal aortic surgery volume is independently associated with lower mortality and failure to rescue after acute type A aortic dissection repair.

Perspective

Most acute type A dissection repairs are performed at low- or medium-volume centers. Greater institutional experience with elective proximal aortic surgery may improve preparedness and outcomes in emergent cases. Concentrating elective volume and developing dedicated aortic teams are pragmatic strategies to enhance outcomes when full centralization is not feasible.

Acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is a true cardiovascular emergency that requires immediate surgical intervention. Despite advances in operative techniques, cerebral protection strategies, and perioperative management, mortality remains high, typically ranging between 10% and 25%.1, 2, 3, 4 Studies have demonstrated an inverse relationship between hospital or surgeon case volume and mortality following ATAAD repair.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 These findings have fueled discussions regarding the centralization or regionalization of care and the development of specialized aortic centers.10

Complete centralization of ATAAD care is difficult to achieve in practice because patients often require immediate operative treatment and may initially present to hospitals that do not routinely perform a high volume of aortic surgeries. Consequently, the majority of ATAAD repairs in the United States are performed at low- or medium-volume centers.1,11 Importantly, the expertise required to manage ATAAD successfully might not be limited to emergent dissection repair alone. Elective proximal aortic surgeries, including ascending and arch aneurysm repair, require many of the same technical skills, multidisciplinary coordination, and perioperative management strategies as needed for successful ATAAD repair. Whether institutional experience with elective proximal aortic surgery translates into improved outcomes after emergent ATAAD repair has not been well characterized at the population level.

This study aimed to evaluate whether hospital elective proximal aortic surgery volume, as a marker of institutional aortic surgery experience, is associated with outcomes after emergent ATAAD repair and to explore the implications of this relationship for ATAAD care.

Methods

Database

The study cohort was drawn from the Nationwide Readmission Database (NRD) for 2017 through 2020. The NRD is part of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project and contains deidentified hospitalization data from hospitals across the United States, including patient demographics, diagnoses, procedures, and hospital characteristics.12 It is a large, all-payer, nationally representative database accounting for approximately 60% of all hospitalizations in the United States, which enables evaluation of rare, high-risk conditions such as ATAAD across diverse practice settings. This breadth allows for robust estimation of hospital-level volume–outcome relationships that may not be fully captured in specialty registries or single-center studies. Because the NRD is publicly available and deidentified, Institutional Review Board approval was waived.

Study Population and Hospital Categorization

Figure 1 summaries the patient selection process. We first identified all adult patients (age >18 years) who had a diagnosis of aortic aneurysm or aortic dissection and underwent proximal aortic surgery. Patients with ATAAD were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), Clinical Modification diagnosis codes, and proximal aortic surgery was identified using ICD-10, Procedure Coding System codes for open ascending aortic and/or aortic arch repair or replacement (Table E1). Patients undergoing concomitant aortic valve- or root-related procedures may have been included if they also underwent ascending aortic and/or arch repair/replacement; however, aortic root operations, such as the Bentall operation or valve-sparing aortic root replacement/David procedure, were not classified separately, because they cannot be reliably distinguished using NRD procedure coding. Patients age <18 years and those with missing data were excluded.Figure 1Patient selection flow diagram. NRD, Nationwide Readmission Database.

Patient selection flow diagram. NRD, Nationwide Readmission Database.

An elective proximal aortic surgery cohort was defined based on the elective status of the procedure. Hospitals were stratified into tertiles according to their annual elective proximal aortic surgery volume.

Emergent repairs for ATAAD performed at these hospitals were subsequently identified, further excluding patients who underwent nonelective aortic aneurysm surgery. Patient demographics, comorbidities, concomitant procedures, and hospital characteristics were obtained. Comorbidity burden was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index.

Malperfusion was defined using ICD-10 diagnosis codes for malperfusion-related end-organ injury, including neurologic, renal, and mesenteric complications. Because the NRD does not include imaging findings, branch-vessel anatomy, or operative findings, these variables should be interpreted as claims-based proxies for malperfusion-related end-organ injury rather than direct anatomic measures of malperfusion. Coronary and limb malperfusion were not included, because they could not be reliably identified or distinguished using NRD coding.

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included major postoperative complications, including stroke, renal failure requiring dialysis, cardiac bleeding, respiratory failure requiring tracheostomy, sepsis, and vascular complications. Failure to rescue (FTR) was defined as mortality occurring after the presence of any of these complications.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables are presented as count and percentage and compared using the χ2 test. Univariable analyses were performed to compare outcomes across low-, medium-, and high-volume hospitals. Multivariable logistic regression was then used to evaluate the association between hospital volume and in-hospital mortality after adjusting for patient demographics, comorbidities, hospital characteristics, malperfusion, and concomitant cardiac procedures. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported. A 2-sided P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study Cohort and Hospital Volume Groups

A total of 22,700 elective proximal aortic repairs were identified in the NRD between 2017 and 2020. Hospitals were stratified by annual elective proximal aortic surgery volume into high-volume (median, 58 cases/year; IQR, 46-79), medium-volume (median, 22 cases/year; IQR, 17-27), and low-volume (median, 5 cases/year; IQR, 2-9) groups. A total of 10,793 patients who underwent emergent repair for ATAAD were identified; of these, 44.0% (n = 4748) were treated at low-volume hospitals, 30.4% (n = 3282) at medium-volume hospitals, and 25.6% (n = 2763) at high-volume hospitals.

Concomitant Procedures

High-volume hospitals performed coronary artery bypass grafting less frequently compared to medium- and low-volume hospitals (9.0% vs 10.9% vs 11.9%; P = .001), but performed mitral valve repair or replacement (1.9% vs 1.1% vs 0.8%; P < .001) and tricuspid valve repair or replacement (1.1% vs 0.9% vs 0.4%; P = .002) more frequently.

Discussion

Our study shows that most ATAAD repairs in the United States are performed at low- or medium-volume hospitals. After adjustment for patient, hospital, and procedural factors, a higher volume of elective aortic surgeries was independently associated with lower in-hospital mortality and lower complication rates. These findings suggest that greater institutional experience with elective proximal aortic surgery may translate into improved outcomes for emergent ATAAD repair.

The inverse volume–mortality relationship in ATAAD surgery has been demonstrated repeatedly across multiple healthcare systems.7, 8, 9 Our present findings are consistent with these reports and suggest that high-volume centers are associated with improved mortality and lower complication rates. Earlier analyses using the US National Inpatient Sample suggested that an institutional case volume exceeding 11 to 13 ATAAD cases per year was associated with improved early survival.5,6 More recent analyses using the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Adult Cardiac Surgery Database found higher odds of FTR in hospitals performing fewer than 10 ATAAD repairs annually.1 Larger pooled analyses using restricted cubic spline modeling have estimated substantially higher optimal thresholds, suggesting that 32 to 38 cases per year may be required to minimize early and long-term mortality.13,14 Together, these data support ongoing discussions regarding the centralization or regionalization of ATAAD care.

Interestingly, this volume–outcome association appears to be much stronger for ATAAD than for type B aortic dissection or thoracic endovascular aortic repair, for which institutional volume effects are less consistent.11,15 This difference likely reflects the technical complexity and physiologic instability associated with ATAAD repair. Open repair typically requires complex aortic reconstruction and coordinated multidisciplinary perioperative management, making outcomes more dependent on surgeon and team experience. In fact, a clear learning curve also has been described in which approximately 25 cumulative cases were required to reduce operative mortality to <10%.16

Although these findings support centralization of care, several important concerns remain. Centralization or regionalization may introduce delays in definitive surgical treatment due to interhospital transfer, which is particularly relevant for ATAAD given the rapid increase in mortality with time from symptom onset. Transfer strategies also may introduce selection bias, as patients who die prior to transfer or surgery often are not captured in surgical cohorts. For example, one study showed improved operative mortality and long-term survival among Medicare beneficiaries transferred to high-volume centers, but the analysis included only patients who survived transfer and ultimately underwent surgery.17 In contrast, a recent population-based analysis from Ontario, Canada that included patients who died during transfer or before operative treatment demonstrated a 2-fold higher mortality risk among patients transferred for surgical consideration.18 These findings highlight the potential trade-off between surgical expertise and delays associated with transfer.

It is important to recognize that the majority of ATAAD repairs continue to be performed outside of high-volume centers. In our study, more than 70% of cases were performed at hospitals with a median ATAAD volume of ≤12 cases per year. Similar patterns have been observed in the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Adult Cardiac Surgery Database analysis, in which approximately three-quarters of ATAAD repairs occurred at centers performing <16 cases annually.1 Moreover, most surgeons perform very few ATAAD repairs each year, with median surgeon volume reported as low as 1 case per year. Notably, the patient comorbidity burden in our cohort was similar across the 3 hospital volume groups, suggesting that differences in outcomes are unlikely to be explained solely by case mix.

Given this distribution of care, strategies beyond simple centralization are very necessary. Our findings suggest that increasing experience with elective proximal aortic surgery may improve preparedness for emergent ATAAD repair. Concentrating elective proximal aortic procedures among a smaller group of surgeons or establishing dedicated aortic teams may therefore represent a practical approach for many institutions. Prior institutional experiences support this concept. At the University of Michigan, patients treated by specialized aortic surgeons had significantly lower intraoperative mortality (1.2% vs 5.7%), 30-day mortality (6.5% vs 17%), and improved 10-year survival (66% vs 46%) compared with those treated by nonspecialized surgeons.19 Similarly, the implementation of a dedicated acute aortic dissection team at Duke University was associated with a dramatic reduction in ATAAD operative mortality from 33.9% to 7.7%.20,21 Although maintaining a fully dedicated ATAAD team might not be feasible for most low- or medium-volume hospitals, concentrating elective aortic surgery among selected surgeons can help maintain technical proficiency and improve outcomes when emergent dissections occur. Institutional experience also appears to be important in elective ascending aortic aneurysm repair22 and aortic root replacement.23 These observations further support the concept that concentrating elective aortic surgeries may enhance institutional readiness for complex emergent cases such as ATAAD.

One potential strategy for low- and medium-volume hospitals is to consider recruiting surgeons with specialized aortic surgery training, increase and consolidate elective proximal aortic cases among dedicated aortic surgeons, manage straightforward ATAAD cases locally, and/or selectively transfer complex or high-risk ATAAD patients to high-volume centers. Such a balanced strategy may help improve outcomes while acknowledging real-world practice patterns. Also, efforts to improve collaboration, training, and referral pathways between high- and low-volume centers may help strengthen regional preparedness.

Our findings also suggest that institutional aortic surgery expertise may be better assessed by considering both ATAAD volume and overall proximal aortic surgery experience. Although ATAAD-specific experience remains important, elective proximal aortic surgery volume may reflect a broader multidisciplinary aortic program, including surgeon expertise, anesthesiology, perfusion, critical care, and postoperative management. Given the complex referral logistics, geographic constraints, and inter-health system barriers, concentration of all ATAAD care at high-volume ATAAD centers might not always be feasible. Therefore, elective proximal aortic surgery experience may provide additional context, alongside ATAAD-specific volume, when transfer decisions and regionalization strategies are considered.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective observational analysis using an administrative database, it is subject to coding errors, misclassification bias, and unmeasured confounding. Second, the NRD lacks important operative and imaging details, including but not limited to cardiopulmonary bypass time, aortic cross-clamp time, circulatory arrest time, cerebral perfusion strategy, cannulation strategy, left ventricular ejection fraction, and aortic valve function. ICD-10 procedure codes do not reliably distinguish specific root operations, such as Bentall or valve-sparing aortic root replacement/David procedures. Similarly, the NRD does not consistently distinguish hemiarch from total arch repair or reliably capture the use of frozen elephant trunk techniques. Third, the NRD does not provide surgeon-level procedural volumes. We could not determine whether the observed association between elective aortic surgery volume and ATAAD outcomes was driven by individual surgeon experience, institutional resources, or broader multidisciplinary team factors. Recent study suggests that surgeon-specific ATAAD experience may influence operative complexity and outcomes24; however, our analysis was limited to the hospital level, and thus elective proximal aortic surgery volume should be interpreted as an institutional marker rather than a direct measure of individual surgeon expertise. Fourth, the analysis includes only patients who underwent operative repair and does not capture patients managed nonoperatively or those who died prior to surgery, potentially introducing selection bias. Finally, long-term outcomes beyond the index hospitalization could not be assessed.

Conclusions

This population-based study demonstrates that higher elective proximal aortic surgery volume is independently associated with improved outcomes after ATAAD repair. These findings suggest that institutional experience from elective aortic surgery enhances surgical preparedness for emergent aortic dissection. Developing dedicated aortic teams and concentrating elective aortic surgery case volume may offer a pragmatic strategy to improve outcomes across diverse practice settings.
