Mixed-methods evaluation of multi-sport activity days for children and adolescents with disability in Australia
Authors: Nicole Halim, Indigo Willing, Rochelle Eime, Leanne Hassett, Katherine B. Owen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildren and adolescents with disability (CAWD) face many barriers to sport participation and report lower levels of physical activity compared with children and adolescents without disability. Multi-sport activity days (MSADs) could address several barriers to sport participation faced by CAWD. This study examined the experiences of CAWD at MSADs, including both facilitators and barriers to participation that these children and adolescents faced.MethodsThis study was a mixed-methods evaluation of MSADs in New South Wales (NSW), Australia. This included surveys of the parents/carers of CAWD who attended a MSAD and interviews with CAWD who attended a MSAD. The survey assessed facilitators and barriers to participation, and the interviews explored CAWD's experiences at MSADs.ResultsThe 15 MSADs held across NSW reached a total of 1839 CAWD. Of these, 86 (5 ​%) parents/carers completed a survey, and 6 CAWD participated in an interview. The interviews revealed that CAWD who attended the MSADs reported positive experiences, highlighting social connections, a sense of commonality and belonging, increased self-esteem, confidence, skills development, and a sense of joy (interview data). Common barriers to sport participation included cost (32 ​%), not knowing where to play (28 ​%), lack of appropriate sporting competitions (28 ​%), lack of interest (28 ​%), and not knowing how to play (26 ​%) (survey data).ConclusionsMSADs can provide positive sport experiences and overcome some barriers to participation for CAWD. However, ongoing support and accessible participation pathways are required to translate initial engagement into sustained involvement in sport.

Background

Physical activity is associated with numerous physical and mental health benefits in children and adolescents [1,2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that children and adolescents, with and without disability, participate in physical activity for an average of at least 60 ​min per day to achieve these benefits [3]. However, in Australia, most children and adolescents do not meet these guidelines [4].

Being physically active may provide additional benefits for children and adolescents with disability (CAWD), including additional cognitive, emotional and social benefits [5,6]. Participation in physical activity is even lower among CAWD [7,8], reflecting a complex interplay of personal and environmental barriers [9]. The Physical Activity for people with a Disability model demonstrates the complex relationships between the determinants of physical activity, participation and health. The model identifies both personal and environmental barriers and facilitators to participation. Personal barriers to participation for CAWD commonly include limited knowledge, skills, energy, time, motivation, and transport, as well as different leisure-time preferences, and cost [10]. Environmental barriers include inadequate equipment and facilities, lack of appropriate programs, and limited provider capacity [10].

The Australian Sports Participation Strategy, Play Well, aims to foster lifelong participation in sport by prioritising equity [11] and improving access for those facing the most barriers, such as CAWD. Traditional competitive club-based sport has historically excluded many CAWD from participating with peers without disability. Alternatives such as adaptive multi-sport activity days (MSADs) could address multiple barriers. For example, MSADs promote awareness of available programs and offer opportunities for CAWD to try a variety of physical activities and sports, fostering the development of physical, psychological and social skills [12]. An evaluation of a multi-sport program for girls (n ​= ​13) with Autism Spectrum Disorder found improvements in motor skills, self-perceptions, and social skills [13]. Sampling multiple sports also enables children to identify the sports they prefer and enjoy [14], which is important given that enjoyment in sport is the strongest predictor of commitment and long-term participation [15,16]. Evaluating MSADs is essential to assess the effectiveness and inform future delivery of similar programs.

In 2022, Sport NSW implemented the Disability Inclusion Project (DIP) to address barriers to physical activity participation for CAWD and build provider capacity. The DIP included two streams: 1) MSADs which provided CAWD opportunities to be physically active, and build motivation and capability to participate; and 2) education and training for physical activity providers to support inclusive program delivery for CAWD. A complex, pragmatic evaluation was embedded within the DIP. This study examined multiple dimensions of MSAD participation, including factors that support engagement and barriers to ongoing participation in sport and physical activity that CAWD face.

Disability Inclusion Project (DIP) overview

The NSW DIP was a state-wide program, led by Sport NSW, funded by the NSW Government Office of Sport. It ran from April 2022 until December 2023. The project aimed to foster collaboration across NSW sporting communities and increase participation and inclusion of People With Disability (PWD) in sport and active recreation. A secondary aim was build capacity among providers, including State Sporting Organisations, State Sporting Organisations for PWD, local councils, and other sport and active recreation providers, for inclusion to be embedded in mainstream sport and active recreation. The DIP comprised two key streams: 1) MSADs; and 2) Education and training. This study focuses on the MSADs.

Multi-Sport Activity Days

The MSADs were one-off multi-sport event days coordinated by Sport NSW and its delivery partners (participating schools, a National Disability Sports Organisation, funding charity body). A total of 27 MSADs were delivered, with 15 of these included in this evaluation. The MSADs aimed to increase sport participation among CAWD by improving motivation and capability, and by addressing barriers to participation in local sporting and recreation opportunities. The MSADs targeted school children (aged 4.5–18 years) with disability and were cost-free, out-of-school events attended with peers. Events featured multiple adaptive sporting activity stations facilitated by sport coaches, staff and volunteers. Some examples of sporting activities included wheelchair-based and ambulant-adaptive forms of basketball, tennis, Australian football, rugby, cycling, badminton, and lawn bowls.

Participant recruitment (survey and interview)

All CAWD who attended a MSAD were eligible for inclusion in this evaluation. Parents/carers of CAWD who attended a MSAD were invited to complete the survey. After reviewing the participant information statement, the parent/carer indicated consent by completing the survey with their child present. While parents/carers completed the survey, all data reflects the children and adolescents’ perspectives to the greatest extent possible. The survey was distributed online (Qualtrics) and on paper to improve response rates. At the end of the survey, the CAWD and their parent/carer were invited to participate in an interview. Written consent was obtained from parents/carers and the CAWD. Survey participants were eligible to enter a draw to win a $100 gift voucher; interviewees received a $100 gift voucher.

Survey

Meeting physical activity guidelines was assessed using the single item: “In a typical week, how many days was the child physically active for at least 60 ​min?” [18]. Response options were 0–7 days, and children were classified as meeting guidelines if 7 days was selected. This item has been validated and found reliable in adolescents [18].

Sport participation was assessed using the single item: “Approximately, how many organized sessions of sport or physical activities has the child participated in, outside of school hours, during the last 12 months?” [19]. The parent/carer had the option to respond by entering the number of times in the last 12 months, number of times per month, or number of times per week.

Motivation and capability to participate in sport were assessed using the Task and Ego Orientation in Sport Questionnaire adapted for youth with intellectual disabilities (TEOSQ-ID) [20], which measures both task- and ego-oriented dimensions of sport motivation and perceived capability. This 12-item instrument asks participants to rate their agreement on a 5-point Likert scale (1 ​= ​totally disagree to 5 ​= ​totally agree), presented as a pictorial scale using facial expressions ranging from a frown to a smile to aid comprehension.

Barriers to sport participation were assessed using the Barriers to Physical Activity and Disability Survey (B_PADS) [21], which includes 19 questions about personal and environmental barriers to exercise. The B_PADS has shown to have construct validity and test-retest reliability [22]. We adapted the B_PADS to ask the participant about barriers to sport participation specifically as opposed to exercise [23].

Demographic information collected included date of birth, gender, postcode, Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander status, language spoken at home, and disability type (physical, sensory, social/emotional, intellectual). Categories for disability type were not mutually exclusive, and participants could select more than one type of disability. Postcode was used to categorise socioeconomic status using the Socioeconomic Index for Area (SEIFA), specifically the Index of Relative Socio-economic Disadvantage which ranks areas in Australia according to relative disadvantage [24].

Interviews

Interviews utilised a one-to-one, qualitative semi-structured design, and were led by a trained qualitative researcher, and co-facilitated by a disability support person. The interview guide included open-ended questions exploring participants’ experiences at the MSAD, including what they enjoyed, found challenging, current participation in activities, and whether MSADs influenced interest in future participation in sporting and recreation opportunities. See Supplementary File 1 for further details of the interview process and guide. Interviews were conducted online and recorded using Zoom, which has been found to be a user-friendly, cost-effective, and secure method [25].

Progress reports

Sport NSW securely shared periodic progress reports from their online registration and membership database with the research evaluation team during project delivery. Progress reporting information included the number and locations of MSADs, and the number of CAWD who attended the MSADs.

Data analysis

Survey data

Descriptive statistics (frequencies and proportions) were calculated for all demographic characteristics and outcomes. We compared the top five barriers and levels of motivation and capability to participate in sport across demographic characteristics using chi-squared tests. All analyses were conducted in SAS Enterprise Guide 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Interview data

Interviews were transcribed verbatim using a secure and accredited transcription service, Way With Words (https://waywithwords.net/). Prepared transcripts were cleaned by two members of the research team.

Once familiarised with the transcripts, the qualitative researcher for this project adopted an inductive approach, and drew on both thematic and qualitative content analysis. Thematic analysis enabled rigorous and reflexive identification of themes through repeated readings and iterative theory-building [26]. Qualitative content analysis was used to systematically code and identify patterns and themes [27] while avoiding “rash quantification” [28].

Transcripts were analysed inductively, and reviewed alongside policy and professional guidelines on sport and disability to enhance sensitivity and awareness to relevant contextual issues. For example, some participants with sensory disabilities described specific responses to environmental stimuli such as sound and temperature, highlighting the importance of accommodating sensory needs in inclusive sport settings. The qualitative analysis was conducted using NVivo software (NVivo 14.23.1). Two members of the research team iteratively discussed their interpretations of the data and emerging codes until the data were refined into a thematic scheme that comprehensively addressed the relevant research aims.

Results

Participant reach

Of the 15 DIP MSADs included in this evaluation, 9 were held in metropolitan regions and 6 in regional areas. The 15 DIP MSADs reached a total of 1839 CAWD, of whom 86 (5 ​%) completed a survey (with their parent/carer). A further 6 CAWD (including 3 CAWD and parent/carer dyads) participated in a semi-structured qualitative interview.

Participant demographics

Of CAWD who completed a survey, 58 ​% were boys (Table 2). Nearly half who completed a survey were aged 9–12 years (47 ​%), followed by 31 ​% aged 5–8 years, and 17 ​% aged 13–17 years. Twenty nine percent spoke a language other than English at home and 17 ​% identified as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander. Among CAWD who completed a survey, 14 ​% were classified in the most disadvantaged quartile (Q1), and 12 ​% in the least disadvantaged quartile (Q4). The majority (72 ​%) of attendees reported having social or emotional disability, 17 ​% reported having sensory disability, 15 ​% reported having physical disability, and 15 ​% reported having intellectual disability. Among CAWD who participated in interviews, 4 (66 ​%) were girls and 5 (83 ​%) were aged 13–17 years. Most reported having physical disability (n ​= ​4), followed by social or emotional disability (n ​= ​3), intellectual disability (n ​= ​2), and sensory disability (n ​= ​1).

Overall, 21 ​% of CAWD who completed a survey met physical activity guidelines. Most (70 ​%) of CAWD who completed a survey participated in some form of sport in the previous 12 months, averaging 1.4 sessions (SD ​= ​1.3) per week.

Motivation and capability to participate in sport

Across all participants, the mean TEOSQ-ID score was 3.6/5, indicating moderate levels of motivation and capability to participate in sport. No statistically significant differences were observed between sociodemographic subgroups. Summary results of item scores are outlined in Supplementary File 2.

Barriers to participation in sport

On average, CAWD reported 2.7 (SD ​= ​2.7) barriers to participation. The top 5 were cost (32 ​%), not knowing where to play (28 ​%), lack of appropriate sporting competitions (28 ​%), lack of interest (28 ​%), and not knowing how to play (26 ​%) (Fig. 1). Discussion

This study is the first mixed-methods evaluation in Australia that explored the experiences of CAWD at MSADs and examined the barriers to sport participation that these children faced. The MSADs reached a large number of CAWD across NSW. The qualitative component of this study found that children generally reported positive experiences participating in the days, highlighting social connections, a sense of commonality and belonging, increased self-esteem, confidence, skills development, and a sense of joy. A range of barriers to sport participation were identified including cost, not knowing where to play, lack of appropriate sporting competitions, lack of interest, and not knowing how to play.

The significant reach of the survey was one indicator of success for the MSADs, with a high proportion of CAWD identifying as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander (17 ​%) and culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD; 29 ​%). The MSADs had good reach into these two subgroups, as people who are CALD represent 28 ​% of the Australian population and Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islanders represent 4 ​% of the Australian population [29]. These two subgroups in Australia have lower physical activity and sport participation levels than their counterparts [30]. The reach of the MSADs suggests that specifically targeting areas with high proportions of inactive population groups is a successful strategy that could be adopted by future programs. Furthermore, this level of reach also suggests that the days are appealing and accessible for CALD and Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander CAWD, which is a strength and crucial for the MSAD's sustainability and success.

Social connection and sense of belonging were common themes that CAWD felt while participating in the MSADs. Participants who attended an interview reported having been able to form friendships, feeling a sense of belonging, community, camaraderie, and connections with others. This is consistent with previous studies that found sport participation can provide opportunities for CAWD to socialise and foster a sense of belonging [31,32]. However, the results of improved social connection and sense of belonging are specifically important in this study, given that most participants had a social or emotional disability. Social connection is one of the five domains of adolescent wellbeing and sport can play an important role in increasing social connections for CAWD [33]. Further, the social nature of sport promotes social connectedness and wellbeing above and beyond individual based physical activities [34]. Therefore, sport compared to individual-based physical activities may play an even more important role for these CAWD given their social and emotional disabilities.

CAWD who attended an interview reported that sport participation at the MSADs facilitated increased skills, confidence and self-esteem. Similarly, a previous inclusive physical activity program demonstrated positive effects on both motor and social skills among children with Autism Spectrum Disorder [35]. Sport provides CAWD an opportunity to learn specific sport skills, but also about themselves. Through sport, CAWD can gain a range of social, cultural, and personal experiences that improve their self-esteem and confidence [36]. Sport participation has a broad range of psychological health benefits for CAWD.

Through interviews, CAWD also reported negative experiences of participating in sport at the MSADs. These included a fear of injury or not having control over their mastery or skills required to participate in specific activities. Previous studies have found that isolation, inaccessible facilities, lack of sports aids, and limited adapted activities contribute to negative sporting experiences and exclusion [37,38]. Future MSADs and sport programs for CAWD should consider co-designing programs with CAWD to ensure that programs are engaging and meet the needs of CAWD. Programs that are co-designed with CAWD are more likely to provide positive experiences and lead to long-term positive effects on physical activity and sport participation [39].

The survey data highlighted cost as the top barrier to sport participation for CAWD. This finding is consistent with previous studies among CAWD [10,40] as well as the general population [41]. There can be additional costs associated with sport participation for CAWD compared to children and adolescents without disability (e.g., transport, specialised equipment such as sport-specific wheelchairs, and support workers). A recent study found that the cost-of-living increases are disproportionally impacting physical activity for CAWD [42]. MSADs provide a free opportunity for CAWD to come and try different sports. However, while the MSADs are free, these days do not alleviate the financial barrier to longer term participation in sport for CAWD. It is important that there are long-term and affordable opportunities for CAWD to participate in sport.

Additionally, the survey findings identified a lack of appropriate competitions and not knowing where to play were common barriers to sport participation for CAWD. It may be that many traditional models of community sport in Australia- typically club-based and competitive- may be unsuitable for many CAWD, given the nature of their disability/ies. This barrier is recognised in the WHO's Global Action Plan for Physical Activity, which calls for the provision of programmes that promote physical activity among people with mental and/or physical disabilities [43]. While many national sports organisations in Australia have inclusion policies, these are not always clearly or meaningfully embedded in national strategic and participation planning. Moreover, a disconnect often exists between inclusion policies and their implementation at the club level [44]. Although sport clubs in Australia may recognise the importance of providing opportunities for people with disability, they frequently lack the resources and capacity to do so. Most Australian sports clubs rely on volunteer workforces and the ongoing challenges of volunteer recruitment and retention must be acknowledged when considering how to translate inclusion objectives into practice [45]. While the MSADs provided opportunities for CAWD to sample a variety of sports and increased awareness of inclusive activities, sports clubs require sustained support and capacity-building to deliver inclusive sport participation pathways for CAWD.

While MSADs can help overcome some of these barriers and reduce negative experiences when playing sport for CAWD, it is unclear whether these programs lead to sustained engagement in physical activity and sport among CAWD. An evaluation of MSADs with adults found that these days had no significant impact on physical activity and sport participation six months post-event [23]. To ensure long-term, sustainable participation, CAWD need access to supported pathways that enable them to transition from introductory programs into inclusive, ongoing participation opportunities. While these may include established community sports clubs, other organisations may be better equipped to provide appropriate participation options. It would be beneficial for sports organisations to have clearer insights into the sports that individuals with varying disabilities prefer to play, as some sports are better suited to certain needs. Future research is needed to assess whether MSADs influence long-term participation for CAWD. Whilst MSADs may spark initial interest, ongoing opportunities must be available to sustain participation. Sports clubs may be a natural setting for this, given their core business of sport participation; however, other community-based organisations may be better positioned to meet the specific participation needs of CAWD.

A strength of this evaluation is the mixed-methods study design, utilising both qualitative and quantitative data to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the MSADs [17]. The limitations of this evaluation include the self-report nature of the quantitative data and the volunteer bias in the quantitative and qualitative data collection. It is likely that participants who volunteer to be part of the evaluation are different from those who do not [46]. These biases can threaten the external validity of the evaluation findings. Qualitative data was collected from a small number of CAWD due to the many challenges in recruiting CAWD. Finally, this was a cross-sectional study and provides a snapshot of the current experiences and barriers to sport participation for CAWD. Future evaluations should consider collecting data across multiple timepoints to assess the longer-term impact of MSAD's on physical activity and sport participation.

Conclusion

CAWD face a range of barriers to sport participation, including cost, not knowing where to play, lack of appropriate sporting competitions, lack of interest, and not knowing how to play. MSADs can overcome some of these barriers, and provide positive and inclusive sport experiences tailored to diverse needs. However, sustained engagement in sport requires more than one-off events. Future research should explore how CAWD can be supported in transitioning into long-term participation pathways. It is also important to consider whether MSADs can play a meaningful role in both expanding access to participation and supporting structured transitions into long-term engagement in sport.
