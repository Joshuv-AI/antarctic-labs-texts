MOFA: microbial optimization without forced altruism
Authors: Soraya Mirzaei, Mojtaba Tefagh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicroorganisms typically exist in communities, where interactions among them define the complexity of these ecosystems. Developing in silico frameworks to investigate the behavior and functionality of these communities is therefore essential for advancing our understanding of microbial ecology. In recent years, several computational modeling frameworks based on genome-scale models have been developed for the community-level analysis of microbial systems.ResultsHere, we introduce microbial optimization without forced altruism (MOFA), a bilevel optimization framework that considers both species-level and community-level fitness criteria. By imposing constraints on species biomass in the outer problem, it prevents the forced altruism observed in previous algorithms. We applied MOFA to a toy model and to community models of Desulfovibrio vulgaris and Methanococcus maripaludis, which exhibit a cross-feeding relationship that causes the community objective to override individual fitness goals by prioritizing the export of metabolites for other community members. For this microbial community, a comparison with the results of NECom, OptCom, and Joint-FBA shows that MOFA yields predictions that better match the experimental results. Additionally, for pairs with a cross-feeding relationship in which exported metabolite production associated with this mutual interaction competes with species biomass, such as D. vulgaris and M. maripaludis, NECom fails to predict the community growth rate, whereas our method succeeds.ConclusionsMOFA effectively analyzes community growth rates without relying on forced altruism. In cases where NECom fails to predict community growth, MOFA successfully predicts these growth rates. Furthermore, MOFA enhances computational efficiency by eliminating the need for the binary variables required in the NECom algorithm.Keywords: Microbial community, Genome-scale models, Forced altruism, Bilevel optimization

Introduction

Microbial communities play a fundamental role in all aspects of human health and environmental ecosystems [1–3]. Despite significant advances in metagenomic studies and technologies, our understanding of the functions and interactions of the members of these communities remains limited. These consortia contain hundreds or thousands of microorganisms; consequently, culture-dependent studies are challenging [4]. Employing computational and mathematical modeling techniques provides an effective strategy to address this problem. Genome-scale metabolic models have become an efficient framework in systems biology for simulating and analyzing microbial communities [5].

In recent years, various methods have been developed using the framework of constraint-based models, including both single-level computational methods and those based on bilevel optimization [6, 7]. The first group of optimization models include flux balance analysis (FBA) with a single objective function, which is extended to microbial community genome-scale models (GEMs) [8, 9]. Community modeling can be performed using either mixed-bag GEMs or compartmentalized GEMs [10]. In mixed-bag GEMs, the metabolic networks of all community members are combined into a single, comprehensive model. Alternatively, in compartmentalized GEMs, each species is represented in separate compartments, along with a shared external compartment, allowing for the simulation of metabolite exchanges among species within the community [10]. These compartmentalized constraint-based approaches aim to simulate the growth of microbial consortia by optimizing a community-level objective and are among the most popular FBA-based methods. Defining an appropriate objective function remains a key challenge in the development of these computational approaches [11].

In its simplest form, FBA-based modeling, known as joint FBA, was introduced by Stolyar et al. [12]. This method involves optimizing the sum of the biomass fluxes of species within a community, under steady-state and reaction irreversibility constraints. Some FBA-based methods incorporate species’ relative abundances, obtained from metagenomic data, into their community model by weighting each species’ biomass flux in the objective function [13, 14]. However, the degeneracy of optimal flux vectors in these models can lead to unrealistic growth rates among community members [10]. This issue arises because multiple flux distributions can achieve the same optimal community growth rate.

To address this issue, MICOM proposes a two-stage optimization strategy [15]. In the first stage, the community growth rate is computed by incorporating the relative abundances of species. In the second stage, L2 regularization is applied to the biomass fluxes to identify realistic individual growth rates. Many constraint-based methods address this problem by assuming that all organisms in the community grow at equal specific growth rates [16–19].

Among these methods, RedCom and SteadyCom adopt the assumption of balanced growth, and solve bilinear optimization problems to determine the relative abundances of species [16, 17]. Similarly, OptDeg uses a hierarchical optimization approach to predict optimal community compositions under the assumption that all organisms in the community grow at equal specific growth rates [19].

Another strategy used to address the challenge of defining community objective functions in static modeling approaches is the development of dynamic modeling methods [20–24]. These methods incorporate dynamic flux balance analysis (dFBA) to describe continuous changes in metabolic exchange over time. Some approaches, such as COMETS [25] and BacArena [26], integrate dFBA with physically structured environments. However, these dynamic methods are computationally intensive and are usually only tractable for communities comprising a limited number of species [27].

As previously mentioned, another group of constraint-based modeling approaches is based on bilevel optimization [28, 29]. Bilevel optimization is a mathematical framework in which one optimization problem is nested within the constraints of another. In this hierarchical structure, the outer problem represents the main optimization problem, while the optimization problems embedded within the constraints are referred to as inner problems [30]. OptCom is a multi-objective optimization framework that maximizes community growth rate at the outer level and individual growth rates at the inner problem [28]. NECom is another bilevel optimization framework that predicts the Nash equilibrium of microbial consortia by applying non-cooperative game theory to model community interactions [29].

A fundamental challenge associated with the proposed static FBA-based methods (with the exception of NECom) is that they can yield unrealistic growth rates for a limited number of fast-growing species, while many others exhibit zero growth [10]. This issue arises because some species are forced to utilize imported metabolites to produce compounds for another fast-growing species, without contributing to their own growth. This phenomenon is known as “forced altruism.” Recently, Raghu et al. [31] addressed this problem by coupling an organism’s metabolic reactions with its biomass reaction. However, flux coupling is strongly influenced by both the metabolic network and the growth medium, and this strategy may unnecessarily constrain the export of metabolites to the biomass flux rate, preventing species from producing metabolites beyond what is required for biomass. However, NECom [29] avoids the forced altruism observed in OptCom by relaxing the outer optimization constraints for exporting metabolites while bounding uptake fluxes.

Here, we propose microbial optimization without forced altruism (MOFA), an FBA-based bilevel optimization problem for predicting the maximum community growth rate in microbial communities. This approach prioritizes species biomass growth under the medium conditions imposed at the outer level. Our method prevents species from consuming the medium solely to produce metabolites for others without optimizing their own biomass production. It also ensures that the maximum community growth rate is not achieved when species produce metabolites without any biomass growth. A key feature of the proposed algorithm is that, despite defining a community-level objective function, it effectively prevents forced altruism and also predicts the growth rate of communities with cross-feeding relationships, something that NECom is unable to predict accurately in such cases.

We applied our framework to the toy model from [18], the community model of Methanococcus maripaludis and Desulfovibrio vulgaris. Like NECom, our method prevents forced altruism. However, MOFA eliminates the need for binary variables, resulting in a continuous nonlinear optimization problem rather than a mixed-integer nonlinear one, thereby improving computational efficiency. We demonstrate that, in cases where pairs have a cross-feeding relationship, MOFA successfully predicts the community growth rate, whereas NECom fails to do so (for more details, see subsection D.vulgaris & M.maripaludis community).

We developed a bilevel optimization problem to predict the maximum growth rate of a microbial community. The proposed algorithm models the community under steady-state conditions and can be used to analyze the community solution space. Results and discussion

Comparison to other methods

Commonly used methods in community modeling based on constraint-based approaches can be broadly divided into two groups: single-level based and bi-level based. In single-level based methods, the entire microbial community is treated as a single entity by merging the metabolic networks of its members, and an overall community objective (such as maximizing sum of biomass) is optimized. In contrast, in bi-level based methods, the individuality of each species is maintained by defining separate objective functions for community members, and these objectives are simultaneously optimized to capture trade-offs between individual growth and community-level biomass maximization.

As shown in Table 1, among the proposed methods, COMETS, NECom, and MOFA do not exhibit forced altruism. COMETS, being a dynamic approach, runs separate FBA simulations for each community member after determining metabolite concentrations using kinetic modeling. However, the sequence in which FBA is executed can influence the final community-level outcomes [25].

In our framework, it is assumed that each species optimizes its own growth, and thus a Nash equilibrium is obtained, similar to the NECom method. The key difference, however, lies in the type of game, complete-information versus incomplete-information [32]. We formulate the optimization problem as a complete-information game, providing a conceptual abstraction of ecological and physiological processes, such as adaptive metabolic regulation, environmental feedback, and niche construction, that govern microbial metabolic responses to local chemical conditions [33, 34]. Microbial species typically occupy distinct ecological niches and continuously interact with their local chemical environment. Relying on regulatory and signaling networks, microbes continuously sense environmental cues, such as nutrient availability, metabolic byproducts, and the activity of neighboring species, enabling them to switch between alternative metabolic pathways in a manner that is strongly dependent on community context.

In our optimization problem, both the outer and inner optimization are based on the stoichiometric matrix of the entire community. Consequently, each species has information about the metabolic networks and feasible fluxes of the others, which makes this a complete information game. In contrast, in the inner optimization problems of NECom, each species only has its own stoichiometric matrix, so it is essentially an incomplete information game, since each player lacks knowledge about the feasible fluxes of the others when it is in community. This distinction is important, as the flux distribution of a species in isolation can differ substantially from its fluxes when embedded in a community.

Within microbial communities, species typically evolve in relation to their specific ecological niches, adjusting to particular environmental conditions. This niche specialization enables them to coexist with other species by reducing competition and optimizing resource use [35]. Such adaptations suggest that each species has an understanding of its own metabolic network as well as the feasible fluxes of other community members, consistent with the concept of complete information in game theory.

Toy model

To validate the capability of MOFA in predicting community growth rate solutions without forced altruism, we first applied it to the simplified microbial toy model introduced in [18]. As shown in Fig. 2a, the two species share two metabolites and exhibit a mutualistic relationship. The growth of each species depends on consuming metabolites produced by the other; this association is known as cross-feeding. This association is obligatory, as neither microorganism can survive in the absence of the other due to the lack of an external supply of these metabolites.

The NECom algorithm avoids forced altruism by imposing constraints at the outer problem to eliminate infeasible solutions in the inner problem. In the outer problem, the constraint ensures that the uptake of a metabolite by any individual species does not exceed the total exports of that metabolite by all other species. D.vulgaris & M.maripaludis community

The second community we investigated is a two-species model consisting of D. vulgaris and M. maripaludis, which exhibits a mutualistic relationship. We used the stoichiometric model for this pair as published in [44]. The shared metabolites between these two species include acetic acid (AcOH), CO2, formate (Form), H+, hydrogen (H2), hydrogen sulfide (H2S), and ammonia (NH3). For H+ and AcOH, both species act as producers, while for the other metabolites, there is a producer–consumer relationship between the species. These interactions are illustrated in Fig. 2b.

For this community, we compare the results of four different methods: OptCom, NECom, Joint-FBA, and our proposed algorithm, MOFA, with experimental findings reported by Stolyar [12]. We ran NECom both with and without an objective function, and in both cases, it predicted a zero community growth rate. M. maripaludis can produce NH3, enabling the growth of D. vulgaris, but only if it receives CO2, Form, H2, and H2S from D. vulgaris. Conversely, D. vulgaris requires NH3 to synthesize these metabolites, establishing a mutual dependency.

The fundamental distinction between MOFA and NECom lies in their contrasting approaches to defining and preventing forced altruism. While both methods characterize forced altruism as flux vectors that yield suboptimal biomass rates for individual species given a fixed growth medium, they differ significantly in how they calculate the optimal biomass flux. In NECom, the optimal biomass flux is determined through FBA considering mass balance constraints exclusively for the individual species’ metabolic network. In contrast, each inner problem in MOFA considers mass balance constraints for the entire community metabolic network, thereby capturing the full scope of inter-species metabolic dependencies.

This distinction becomes particularly evident in scenarios where the relationships between reactions, such as coupling and inhibition patterns, differ when comparing an isolated species’ metabolic network versus the integrated community metabolic network. Consider, for example, a cross-feeding relationship where species 1 exports metabolite and imports metabolite , which subsequently enables biomass production with high yield. If the export of does not compete with biomass production for essential precursors, both methods perform adequately and produce comparable predictions.

However, consider a more complex scenario where the production of inhibits the biomass reaction due to competition for a shared essential precursor with limited uptake capacity. Furthermore, assume that if species 1 fails to provide the community with , then the import of becomes impossible, establishing a directional coupling between the import of and the export of when considering the community-level mass balance, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Cross-feeding relationships, where exported metabolite production competes with biomass reaction

In this situation, NECom fails to recognize this coupling relationship because it evaluates each species in isolation. Consequently, NECom would not permit production, effectively preventing the cross-feeding relationship from occurring despite the significant benefit that would provide to species 1’s growth (For this toy model and its implementation, see the GitHub repository Cross-feeding toymodel).

This limitation extends to a broader class of interactions: any cross-feeding relationship where the production of exported metabolites competes with biomass production will be deemed infeasible by NECom. This occurs because NECom’s species-level perspective cannot capture the emergent community-level dependencies that make such relationships viable despite the apparent local competition. MOFA’s community-level mass balance approach, however, successfully identifies these viable cross-feeding relationships by accounting for the complete metabolic context, including all inter-species dependencies and feedback loops. This comprehensive perspective enables MOFA to predict biologically realistic community behaviors that NECom would incorrectly classify as infeasible, particularly in syntrophic communities where mutual dependencies are essential for survival.

To evaluate the accuracy of each algorithm, we calculated the mean absolute error (MAE) between the predicted and experimental values. Additionally, for biomass flux predictions, we employed the one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, a non-parametric statistical method, to compare the absolute errors between predicted and experimental values for MOFA, OptCom, and Joint-FBA.

In contrast to NECom, our model is capable of predicting the community growth rate of the D. vulgaris and M. maripaludis microbial community (Fig. 2b). Consistent with [12] and [28], we performed separate runs for each case, where the lactate and hydrogen uptake rates were set to experimentally determined values and metabolite accumulation was set to zero. The cross-feeding relationship between the two species was established as illustrated in Fig. 2. Following the settings of Stolyar et al., the lower and upper bounds for metabolite exchanges were defined. For common metabolites, ammonia was assumed to be present in excess, whereas the uptake of metabolites such as formate, H2S, and CO2 from the environment was constrained to zero. Consequently, the metabolites secreted by D. vulgaris could be utilized by M. maripaludis. Acetate, methane, and carbon dioxide were allowed to accumulate. For other metabolites, such as N2, SO4, and others, the upper and lower bounds were defined according to the constraints specified in the metabolic network model.

The predicted fluxes of acetate, CO2, and methane produced by D. vulgaris and M. maripaludis were compared with corresponding experimental measurements. Unlike OptCom and Joint-FBA, which underpredicted and overpredicted the CO2 rate, respectively, MOFA demonstrated better agreement with the experimental data, achieving a mean absolute error approximately half that of both methods. While both methods predict a continuous increase in community biomass over time (without any decrease in any time interval), MOFA captures this trend more consistently, showing increases during specific time intervals with slight decreases in others; however, the decrease in predicted biomass occurs at different time points compared to the experimental observations.

Overall, for acetate, MOFA consistently underestimates flux relative to the other computational methods. During the early intervals (0–50 and 24–56 hr), all three models predict lower fluxes than those observed experimentally. In the mid-growth intervals (56–69 and 62–76 hr), Joint-FBA and OptCom tend to overestimate acetate production, whereas MOFA more closely follows the experimental trend. However, MOFA’s predictions remain lower than observed values, resulting in a higher mean absolute error compared to the other methods.

Except for acetate, MOFA achieves lower MAE values than the other two methods. Despite these differences, particularly in CO2 predictions, MOFA provides total biomass estimates that closely match the experimental data.

As shown in Fig. 4, Stolyar’s Joint-FBA predicts higher biomass values than the OptCom method. This difference arises because OptCom not only maximizes the community-level objective function but also incorporates the maximization of individual species’ objectives, making its predictions more consistent with the experimental measurements than those of Joint-FBA. On the other hand, OptCom permits metabolite uptake and secretion by community members at the outer optimization level, which can induce forced altruism and consequently result in predicted fluxes that exceed experimentally observed values. MOFA shares the same objective function as OptCom, which is to maximize both community and individual biomass, but employs a different strategy to eliminate solutions involving forced altruism. The main difference between our results and those obtained using the other two methods is associated with CO2 production. According to the simulation results reported by Stolyar et al. [12], D. vulgaris converts the consumed lactate into acetate, with a portion directed toward biomass formation and the remainder released as CO2. In contrast, under inner-level optimization in MOFA, D. vulgaris is constrained to utilize lactate primarily for its own growth, which results in reduced CO2 production. Consequently, MOFA predicts a total community biomass that is closer to experimental observations than those predicted by OptCom and Joint-FBA.

Conclusion

MOFA is capable of analyzing community-level growth rate without forced altruism, which can lead to biologically unrealistic predictions in models. We observed that commonly used methods predicted biomass yields that were higher than the experimental results, whereas our algorithm demonstrated significant improvement. NECom is the only method designed to predict community growth rates without forced altruism. However, in the case of D. vulgaris and M. maripaludis, NECom algorithm failed to produce an accurate prediction, returning a zero community growth rate. In contrast, MOFA predicted a community growth rate that closely matched the experimental data, and produced a more realistic exchange pattern.

Overall, NECom fails to capture cross-feeding relationships in which exported metabolite production competes with biomass, as its species-level perspective forced altruism overlooks the community-level dependencies that sustain such interactions. In contrast, MOFA’s community-wide mass balance approach incorporates inter-species dependencies and mutualistic feedback loops, enabling it to correctly predict viable syntrophic relationships and realistic community behaviors.

Although the MOFA algorithm can be implemented as an NLP by removing the binary variables present in the NECom algorithm and converting the problem into a continuous one, which typically makes it easier and faster to solve, the resulting NLP remains non-convex and computationally challenging. Our observations indicate that our algorithm, which eliminates binary variables, was executed faster than the NECom algorithm. As future work, a more detailed investigation into the sources of this speed-up, along with the development of efficient solution strategies for both NLP and MINLP formulations, could help determine whether continuous NLP formulations are indeed more suitable than MINLP for modeling large-scale genome-scale metabolic networks in microbial communities.
