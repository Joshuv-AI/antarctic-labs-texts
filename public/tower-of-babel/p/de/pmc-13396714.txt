Importance of methods selected for microbiome studies: State of the science and challenges
Authors: Joshua M. Lyte, Monika Proszkowiec-Weglarz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The microbiota has impact on virtually every aspect of poultry production, including performance, health, and food safety. It is therefore not surprising that considerable promise exists for the application of microbiota science in addressing current and future challenges faced across the poultry industry. Yet, methodological variation among poultry microbiota studies has had negative impact on reproducibility of findings, thereby slowing the application of effective microbiota-based solutions in a production setting. The experimental workflow in microbiota research involves many steps, ranging from sample collection to bioinformatic processing. Likewise, at each experimental stage there exists a vast methodological toolbox available to poultry microbiota researchers. As the technical decisions made at each experimental stage have demonstrable impact on each downstream step, outcomes in microbiota research are largely dependent on the methodologies employed. In order to improve translatability and reproducibility between different methodological pipelines, it is essential that a best practice framework be adopted in poultry science regarding microbiota research. The present review seeks to briefly encapsulate our symposium on microbiome methodology in poultry research. This symposium discussed the need for a robust set of evidence-based guidelines that streamline microbiota research in poultry. Attention was given to each major stage of a typical poultry microbiota study, including sample collection, storage, extraction, library preparation, sequencing, bioinformatic and data analyses, as well as data reporting and repositories. The use of positive and negative controls, improved reporting of raw data, and other tangible aspects that improve consistency and reproducibility across poultry microbiota study designs were highlighted. Ultimately, it was the goal of this symposium not to advocate a single methodology, but instead to demonstrate the need for robust practices at each experimental stage that enable cross-study comparisons of findings in poultry microbiota research.

Introduction

Beginning in the early 20th century, the microbiome was demonstrated to impact poultry physiology (Johansson et al., 1948). As the gut microbiome is often highly complex and without a defined composition, early efforts relied on the use of germ-free and gnotobiotic birds to uncover a role for the microbiome on poultry physiology and growth (Cowieson, 2022; Reyniers, 1959). With the recent advent of next-generation sequencing, bacterial marker genes such as the 16S rRNA gene have allowed global profiling of the microbiome in conventional birds. Such technological advances have accelerated our understanding of how the microbiome in poultry may be linked to outcomes ranging from performance (Kogut, 2022) and food safety (Xu et al., 2023) to behaviour (van der Eijk et al., 2020) and disease (Clavijo and Flórez, 2018). Nevertheless, the race to generate results and new findings in the poultry microbiome using next-generation sequencing has largely outpaced needed attention towards methodological considerations. Indeed, microbiome research involves many branching options during the experimental workflow that ultimately affect results and findings, including but not limited to sample storage, nucleic acid extraction kit, sequencing platform, and bioinformatic tools. The issue of methodological inconsistencies between published studies have led to general challenges in identifying consistent outcomes for the microbiome in poultry physiology and growth (Marková et al., 2024). As such, it should not be surprising that there have been recent calls in the poultry science community for the widespread adoption of best practice frameworks in microbiome research experimental design that enhance consistency and reproducibility (Lyte et al., 2025). This review seeks to briefly encapsulate why attention towards best practices in microbiome methodologies is now needed, and how addressing this issue can accelerate evidence-based practices that leverage the poultry microbiome in a production setting.

A brief overview of microbiome methodologies

While the microbiome refers to bacteria, viruses, fungi, and other microorganisms, the focus of this review is the bacterial component as the majority of poultry microbiome research focuses on bacteria. There are many ways to characterize the microbiome ranging from traditional culture-based techniques to next generation sequencing that achieve identification of microbes and functional potentials (Oladokun et al., 2026). The use of culture-based methods, or metaproteomic and metabolomic approaches can also be used to identify microbial function beyond simple fingerprinting of microbial identity and gene catalogs (Hiergeist et al., 2015). The present review focuses on the former category, specifically the use of next-generation sequencing for the identification and characterization of microbial taxa.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

The 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene is highly conserved among bacteria and is used as a taxonomic marker gene for bacterial identification. Due largely to technological advances that have reduced financial cost, the use of 16S rRNA gene sequencing is the most widely used next-generation sequencing approach in poultry microbiome research. The 16S rRNA gene in sequencing can be targeted through primer design towards hypervariable regions, or the full length of the gene (which includes all hypervariable regions) can be sequenced directly (Poudel et al., 2025). The use of the full length 16S rRNA gene likely provides superior taxonomic resolution compared to focusing on a hypervariable region; although, as discussed below there are technical challenges regarding long-read sequencing (Aja-Macaya et al., 2025).

While the major advantage of the 16S rRNA gene is that it is a marker gene of bacteria, and can be used for bacterial community characterization, it is only a single gene. Nevertheless, 16S rRNA gene sequencing can also be used for predictive functional potential of bacteria. Namely, by first identifying a specific 16S rRNA gene sequence and then using bioinformatic tools such as Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States (PICRUSt2) (Douglas et al., 2020), gene families and pathway abundances may be inferred. Nevertheless, it was shown that chicken cecal microbiota predicted functions were highly dependent on the type of primer used (Darwish et al., 2021). As such it is important to highlight here that follow-up studies are required and pathway prediction alone does not definitively indicate if a bacterial taxa does have the predicted gene or phenotype (Sun et al., 2020).

There are, however, numerous challenges with using the 16S rRNA gene for species-level resolution which are only briefly encapsulated herein. For example, the 16S rRNA gene can be transmitted between different bacterial species via horizontal gene transfer, thereby leading to problems of misidentification and over- or under-estimates of abundances as bacteria can hold numerous copies of the 16S rRNA gene (Bartoš et al., 2024). Indeed, challenges regarding amplification bias (Reysenbach et al., 1992) as well as issues related to multiple 16S rRNA gene copies within a genome have been described decades ago (Case et al., 2007).

Metagenomic sequencing

Metagenomic, which is also called shotgun, sequencing includes all genes and does include the 16S rRNA gene. It is important to appreciate that unlike 16S rRNA gene sequencing, the metagenomic approach does not inherently discriminate between bacteria and any other organism as the metagenomic approach requires sample preparation or computational methods to deplete or remove host or other non-bacterial DNA or reads, respectively (Forry et al., 2024).

There are multiple depths for which metagenomic sequencing can be performed from shallow to deep or ultra deep sequencing (La Reau et al., 2023). The depth of metagenomic sequencing is primarily defined by the number of reads generated per sample. While it is generally correct that the greater the depth, the more comprehensive the output, shallow shotgun approaches have shown an excellent compromise between the financial cost and resolution afforded by deep sequencing (Stothart et al., 2023), and has been performed in poultry microbiome research (Lyte et al., 2024). Ultimately, metagenomic sequencing ideally provides a comprehensive picture of microbial functional potential by identifying the entire gene catalog of any microbial taxa.

It would be incorrect to think that metagenomic sequencing is necessarily superior to 16S rRNA gene sequencing as both offer advantages and can be used cooperatively (Durazzi et al., 2021). For example, in areas of low bacterial biomass such as the respiratory tract, unless ultra-deep sequencing is used, 16S rRNA gene sequencing can be used to characterize the microbiome (Glendinning et al., 2017); although user caution is warranted for issues of contamination (Odendaal et al., 2025). It is important to appreciate that 16S rRNA gene and metagenomic sequencing approaches can give different pictures of the microbiome (Feng et al., 2025). Likewise, the use of metagenomics can help to counteract bias used in amplicon-based 16S rRNA approaches (Brooks et al., 2015). This means that even though metagenomic sequencing captures the 16S rRNA gene, metagenomic and 16S rRNA gene sequencing approaches do not give identical ‘snapshots’ of the microbiome.

Experimental design in microbiome research

For an in-depth discussion of experimental design in microbiome research in poultry the reader is referred to the following publication (Lyte et al., 2025). In brief, microbiome experimental design can be generally categorized into eight steps: 1) Sample collection, 2) Sample storage, 3) DNA extraction, 4) Library preparation, 5) Sequencing, 6) Bioinformatics, 7) Data integration, and 8) Data deposition. Each of these steps apply for both 16S rRNA gene and metagenomic sequencing approaches.

Sample collection

How and why a sample is collected must be informed by avian physiology, microbial abundance, the possibility of contamination, and a hypothesis (Lyte et al., 2025). From the perspective of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, microbial composition changes, as does microbial density, moving from the crop towards the colon (Glendinning et al., 2019). As such, for sample collection more sample may be required to obtain sufficient microbial mass when collecting from the duodenum as opposed to the ceca. Microbial mass is especially important regarding the distinction of 16S rRNA gene vs metagenomic sequencing as the latter approach will have to filter out host reads and in areas of low microbial abundance obtaining sufficient bacterial reads may become an issue (Forry et al., 2024; Villette et al., 2021). It is also important to consider regional variation in terms of the intersection of host physiology and microbial function within any single gross anatomical section. For example, the cecal tonsil, which is a major host lymphoid tissue region within the ceca, can have unique microbial populations (Glick et al., 1978). Likewise, luminal content versus mucosal adherent cecal microbial populations were demonstrated to differ in composition and predicted function (Miska et al., 2023). Gastrointestinal motility and the avian phenomenon of reverse motility should be also considered in terms of microbial populations sampled. This is especially important as reverse motility can move luminal content from the colon towards the upper GI tract (de Vries et al., 2022), suggesting that microbial composition in avians may be uniquely impacted by gut motility. The use of non-invasive sample collection techniques (i.e. boot sock samples, swabs, etc.…) only partially capture the complexity of the GI tract microbiome (Williams and Athrey, 2020).

Sample storage

How, and how quickly, a sample is stored after sample collection is a critical determinant of downstream success (Lyte et al., 2025). The gold standard for sample storage is immediate snap freezing following sample collection, and then storage at −80°C. Storage buffers that can help preserve nucleic acid integrity are known to impact outcome of results (Wegl et al., 2021). Sample freeze-thaw cycles should be avoided as this can negatively affect sample integrity.

DNA extraction

There are many DNA extraction kit options available to the researcher (Lyte et al., 2025). It is important to consider microbial physiology in DNA extraction kit selection. For example, bead-beating, a mechanical disruption step, is especially needed for effective lysis of Gram positive bacteria (Fidler et al., 2020). As such, DNA extraction method must consider both Gram positive and negative bacterial populations for complete lysis which would allow for accurate microbial community characterization. In addition, the potential presence and therefore removal of PCR inhibitors in a sample is important if planning for amplicon-based sequencing (Dawson et al., 2023).

Library preparation

Library preparation simply means processing a DNA sample to make it suitable for a sequencing platform (Lyte et al., 2025). Amplification library preparation uses qPCR while other types of library preparation do not utilize qPCR.

Sequencing

Many sequencing platforms exist allowing different technological approaches for the characterization of a microbial community in a biological sample. For an in depth discussion of sequencers used in 16S rRNA gene sequencing for poultry, the reader is referred to the following publication (Lyte et al., 2025). For discussion of sequencing in metagenomic approaches, the reader is directed to (Meslier et al., 2022).

Bioinformatics

Bioinformatics are necessary for the processing of raw sequencing data, removal of artifacts and contaminating reads, and other aspects discussed at length elsewhere for 16S rRNA gene (Lyte et al., 2025) and metagenomic (Kim et al., 2024) approaches. Bioinformatic pipelines and taxonomic databases are continuously updating. It is critically important that the researcher utilize up-to-date versions of taxonomic databases as curation policies, nomenclature, and other aspects differ not only between databases but also between updates. Likewise with up-to-date versions of bioinformatic pipelines, such as QIIME2 (Bolyen et al., 2019) or Mothur (Schloss et al., 2009), and tools such as PICRUSt2 as improvements are constantly being made.

Data integration

Data from a sequencing run can be valuable alone showing microbial community patterns in poultry microbiome research. However, making correlations between sequencer data and other non-sequencing datasets provides important insight into potential relationships between microbial taxa and other factors. The use of a variety of data integration methods, including correlation-based methods, ordination (e.g. Principal Component Analysis (PCA)), clustering (e.g. heatmaps), network analysis (e.g. co-occurrence) and other techniques are discussed at length in our previous publication (Lyte et al., 2025).

Data deposition

The use of raw data repositories is important for long-term archiving and accessibility of sequencing data and relevant metadata (Lyte et al., 2025). There are multiple databases that are available free-of-charge to the researcher under the umbrella International Nucleotide Sequence Database Collaboration (INSDC), including 1) National Library of Medicine Sequence Read Archive (SRA), 2) European Nucleotide Archive (ENA), and 3) DNA Databank of Japan Sequence Read Archive (DDJ). The use of these databases for the poultry researcher is discussed at length elsewhere.

Why are best practice approaches needed for microbiome research in poultry?

Each step of the experimental design in microbiome research in poultry has unique considerations that can impact consistency and reproducibility of results. Moreover, it is critical to appreciate that a choice made at any step can impact efficacy of downstream stages. For example, if sample contamination is not controlled for, it may be difficult to determine if an identified microbial taxa is a contaminant or a true member of the sample community (Bindari et al., 2021; Salter et al., 2014). Below is a brief overview of why a best practice approach is needed at each step of the experimental workflow of a microbiome study.

Sample collection

Best practice approaches in sample collection essentially entail defined sample collection region and the use of positive and negative controls to account for sample processing efficacy and contamination, respectively (Lyte et al., 2025). Specific location of sample collection and how a sample is collected is important for reproducibility. As regional variation exists between gross anatomical sections (Glendinning et al., 2019), it is necessary to always sample from a defined location within any specific intestinal region. The issue of contamination during sample collection will affect all downstream steps. As such, using sterile technique in collection is essential to avoid researcher-introduced contamination. Additionally, contamination from tools or the environment should be controlled for through the use of negative controls (Bindari et al., 2021). As such, if an experimental design lacks defined sample collection location, or forgoes the use of positive or negative controls, challenges will present in replicating the research.

Sample storage

Sample storage conditions are well appreciated to affect DNA yield, quality, and ultimately sequencing results (Lyte et al., 2025). Best practice approaches in sample storage should strive for snap freezing and then storage at −80°C. The use of different storage methodologies can lead to difficulty in comparing outcomes between studies as storage condition becomes a variable (Wegl et al., 2021). For example, the use of storage buffers can significantly impact DNA quality and microbial taxa compositions.

DNA extraction

Best practice approaches in the selection of DNA extraction kits should include those that have steps to isolate nucleic acids from all bacteria in order to comprehensively profile the microbial taxonomy of a sample. As different kits involve different mechanical and/or chemical steps in the isolation and concentration of nucleic acids, it is important that the researcher clearly indicate what kit was used and what steps were involved in extraction (Pankoke et al., 2021). Excessive bead beating can result in DNA shearing, resulting in fragmented DNA (Fidler et al., 2020). Further, the use of positive and negative controls are critical in DNA extraction (Hornung et al., 2019). Positive controls assess kit efficacy, while negative controls assess for kit-introduction of contamination. As such, the use of clearly reported methodology for the DNA extraction kit used and how it was evaluated using positive and negative controls can help enhance consistency and reproducibility between studies.

Library preparation

Protocols that process DNA for library preparation can include amplification, fragmentation, adapters, barcoding, and other steps. It is essential that library preparation methods include the use of positive and negative controls to assess library preparation efficacy and bias, as well as determine any introduction of contamination (Hornung et al., 2019). Clear use of controls at this step can likely help enable comparisons between studies that may use different library preparation methods.

Sequencing

Strengths and limitations of several common sequencing platforms have been previously described in poultry microbiome research (Lyte et al., 2025). In brief, the technology employed in each sequencer platform can lead to sequencer-specific errors and biases that ultimately affect the observed results. For example, long-read sequencing on Nanopore can exhibit errors related to repeated DNA bases called homopolymers, while amplicon-based short-read sequencing on Illumina can be affected by amplification biases (Abellan-Schneyder et al., 2021). The use of positive, known controls such as mock microbial communities can be effectively used to assess sequencer accuracy, biases, and errors.

Bioinformatics, data integration, and data deposition

Bioinformatic pipelines, plug-ins, and taxonomic databases are necessary to process raw sequencer data output. Likewise computational tools are utilized for the integration of sequencing and other datasets. The sheer number of different tools available to the computational biologist is staggering. As the choice of bioinformatic pipeline is well documented to affect, for example, microbial taxonomic classification it is easy to appreciate that bioinformatic tools are a source of bias and error that can create challenges in making cross-study comparisons (Fares et al., 2025). The preservation and depositing of raw sequencing data in a data repository allows for re-use of data using different bioinformatic toolsets. Moreover, as bioinformatic plug-ins and pipelines improve, or updates are issued, raw datasets can be re-analyzed as well as integrated into relevant datasets.

What steps can we take today to improve consistency and reproducibility in poultry microbiome research?

Many tools and steps are available to use today that have demonstrable impact on improving consistency and reproducibility in poultry microbiome research. A brief series of actionable steps and resources are listed below that researchers can immediately adopt. Future advances that are needed in this area are also briefly discussed; for more in depth discussion the reader is directed to (Lyte et al., 2025).1.Clear and robust reporting of methodologies. Methodologies are essential for the reproducibility of research. The detailed reporting of sampling site, sample collection technique, sample storage vessels and conditions, sample processing and handling procedures, and other aspects of microbiome experimental design should be viewed as mandatory reporting. A methodological checklist that accompanies manuscripts on the microbiome should be included during submission and publication in order to ensure authors report sufficient details that support reproducibility. An example checklist for poultry microbiome study design has recently been published for general use (Lyte et al., 2026).2.The use of positive and negative controls. Mock microbial communities (i.e. positive controls) and negative controls are available for microbiome researchers. While poultry-specific mock communities need to be developed, what is available today are whole cell and nucleic acid standards that can be used to assess researcher-introduced contamination, nucleic acid kit extraction efficiency and contamination, sequencer error and bias, as well as bioinformatic tool accuracy. The implementation of positive and negative controls is straight-forward and has been discussed previously for poultry microbiome research.3.Depositing of raw data and metadata into a publicly-accessible data repository. The INSDC repositories including the SRA, DDJ, and ENA are available to researchers at no-cost. The depositing of raw data in these repositories helps to ensure long-term preservation of large datasets, as well as enables re-use of datasets especially considering bioinformatic tools are continually being updated and developed.

Conclusions and future directions

Microbiome research in poultry has had immense impact on how we approach issues in production. As we seek to bridge the gap between foundational and applied aspects of microbiome-driven outcomes in poultry, it is essential to ensure that reproducible and consistent methodological approaches are routinely used in poultry microbiome experimental design. While many actionable steps are available to researchers today to bolster methodologies, future efforts should focus on the development of best practices approaches that are informed by poultry-specific challenges.
