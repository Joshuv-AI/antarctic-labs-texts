Living in the dark: self-perceived health and social impacts of nationwide electricity rationing in Ecuador
Authors: Lissette C Villacreses-Brito, Esteban Ortiz-Prado, Jorge Vásconez-González, Alice Gaibor-Pazmiño, Camila Salazar-Santoliva, María Paz Cadena-Padilla, Isaac A Suarez-Sangucho, Juan S Izquierdo-Condoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn 2024, Ecuador faced its most severe drought in over six decades, severely disrupting hydroelectric power generation and triggering prolonged nationwide energy rationing. This crisis had far-reaching consequences affecting essential services, economic activity, public safety and population health. The objective of this investigation is to assess the multidimensional self-perceived impacts of extreme energy rationing on health, economic stability and social well-being across Ecuador.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional survey across all regions of Ecuador using a self-administered online questionnaire between August 2024 and February 2025. The survey included 38 structured items and the validated Clinical Outcomes in Routine Evaluation 10 (CORE-10) scale to evaluate psychological distress. A total of 845 valid responses were analysed using descriptive statistics, χ² tests, t-tests and ordinal logistic regression (p<0.05).ResultsOf 845 respondents, 60.95% were women and the mean age was 42 years. The majority resided in the Highland region. A total of 76.86% reported mood changes during blackouts and 26.31% experienced panic or anxiety attacks. Emotional distress was reflected in high levels of frustration (48.72%), helplessness (40.32%) and irritability (35.2%). Social and family life was disrupted in 84.04% of participants. Among those with chronic illnesses, 7.59% reported health deterioration and 11.19% had difficulty accessing medication. Economic losses were significant: 18.18% lost between US$501 and US$1000, while 48.4% reported losses under US$500. Additional effects included impaired sleep (54.86%), disrupted eating habits, concerns about food spoilage and heightened insecurity.ConclusionProlonged energy rationing in Ecuador had substantial impacts on psychological well-being, chronic disease management, household stability and economic resilience. These findings underscore the urgent need for diversified energy sources, strengthened health infrastructure and targeted support for vulnerable populations to mitigate the consequences of future energy crises.

Introduction

Interruptions in electrical service can have widespread and serious consequences for public health and safety, disrupting critical systems such as public transportation, water supply infrastructure, food preservation and the operation of medical equipment.1 With rising average summer temperatures and an increasing frequency of heat waves due to climate change, power outages are expected to become more frequent and severe.1

In Ecuador, rainfall began to decline in March 2024, culminating in the most severe drought in the past 60 years. This environmental crisis critically affected the electricity sector, as approximately 90% of Ecuador’s electricity relies on hydroelectric power generation.24 Initial blackouts were reported between 15 April and 30 April 2024. By September, the country entered a prolonged phase of scheduled power rationing that extended through December. The peak of the crisis occurred in October and November, when outages lasted up to 15 hours per day.3 These events had widespread implications for households, industries and commercial activity, with estimated economic losses ranging from 1% to 1.5% of Ecuador’s GDP—or approximately US$1.188–1.7826 billion.4

At the household level, blackouts disrupted daily routines and access to essential services. In various regions, the inability to use induction stoves and electric water pumps hindered food preparation and limited access to potable water, exacerbating household vulnerability during extended outages.2 5 Educational continuity was also compromised, as students lost access to virtual classes and digital resources, limiting their ability to meet academic requirements.6 Public safety deteriorated as well, with an increase in traffic accidents; October saw the second-highest monthly number of incidents in 2024, with 352 recorded cases in the capital. Crime also surged during blackout periods, with 138 violent deaths in Quito and a nationwide tally of 5717 crimes against life by November.7 8

From a health perspective, certain groups are at elevated risk during power outages, particularly older adults, individuals dependent on electrically powered medical devices (eg, oxygen concentrators) and those with chronic conditions such as respiratory, cardiovascular or renal diseases.9 Furthermore, the widespread use of diesel generators during outages led to significant increases in air pollution: concentrations of Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) rose by 180%, carbon monoxide (CO) by 43%, nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) by 39%, and fine particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter of ≤2.5 μm (PM₂.₅) by 20%, posing heightened risks to cardiopulmonary health.10

This study aims to comprehensively assess the multifaceted consequences of energy rationing in Ecuador, focusing on its effects on healthcare systems, economic productivity and daily life disruption. Particular emphasis is placed on vulnerable populations, environmental health risks from pollutant emissions and broader implications for public safety and well-being.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional survey was conducted using an online questionnaire to assess the perceived impact of power shortages on various aspects of daily life in Ecuador, from August 2024 to February 2025.

Ecuador, a Latin American country located along the equatorial line, is nestled within the Andean mountainous region and bordered by the Pacific Ocean. Sharing its borders with Peru and Colombia, this small nation had a population of approximately 17.6 million inhabitants as of 2021.11 12 The online questionnaire was made available nationwide, covering all 24 provinces of Ecuador, with a focus on urban and rural areas. Data collection took place, ensuring broad representation across different sectors, including households, businesses and healthcare providers.

Participants

The sample size was not calculated, as this was a non-probabilistic sample that included all participants who voluntarily agreed to complete the questionnaire. Participants included members of the public, business owners and healthcare professionals, ensuring a multisectoral approach to understanding the effects of energy rationing.

The survey was open to any Ecuadorian resident aged 18 or older. The study used a snowball sampling approach via social media, targeting a convenience sample to capture diverse perspectives across different economic backgrounds. A non-probability convenience sampling method was employed to select participants within the researchers’ reach using an online survey platform. All responses included were obtained from voluntary participants who agreed to take part in the research.

Survey development and measures

This study employed a structured, 38-item anonymous online questionnaire developed by the research team to assess the multifaceted impacts of energy rationing in Ecuador. The instrument was designed to capture both quantitative and qualitative insights related to household disruptions, access to essential services, health-related concerns, economic effects and perceptions of environmental and public safety risks during the nationwide blackouts. As part of the final instrument, the Clinical Outcomes in Routine Evaluation 10 (CORE-10) scale was included to evaluate the psychological distress associated with prolonged power outages. This validated scale has demonstrated strong reliability across diverse populations.13

The initial draft was reviewed by two public health experts to ensure content validity, contextual appropriateness and clarity. Based on their feedback, a pilot test was conducted with 30 eligible participants to identify potential comprehension or design issues; their responses were excluded from the final dataset.

Following revisions, the finalised version of the questionnaire was originally written in Spanish and later translated into English for the purposes of this publication. The complete survey instrument is provided in online supplemental file 1.

Variables

The questionnaire collected a broad range of variables to address the research objectives. Demographic variables included sex, age, self-identified race or ethnicity, region of residence, marital status, education level, type of professional activity (health-related or non-health-related) and history of comorbidities.

Occupational status was classified into seven categories based on educational requirements, skill level and employment structure.1416 These included: gold-collar workers, defined as highly specialised professionals with advanced training (eg, physicians, engineers and lawyers); white-collar workers, referring to office and managerial personnel typically engaged in formal employment settings (eg, teachers, information technology specialists and administrators); blue-collar workers, comprising manual labourers employed in sectors such as construction, agriculture and transportation; grey-collar workers, representing hybrid roles that combine physical and professional tasks, particularly in healthcare and emergency services (eg, nurses, paramedics and laboratory technicians); pink-collar workers, denoting service-oriented occupations in caregiving, retail, hospitality and customer service; no-collar workers, encompassing individuals working independently or outside traditional employment frameworks, such as freelancers, entrepreneurs and homemakers; and finally, those who were retired or unemployed at the time of the survey.

Participants’ geographical location was classified into four major regions of Ecuador—Coastal, Highland, Amazon and Galápagos—to facilitate regional analyses. To ensure the accuracy and relevance of statistical comparisons, certain responses were excluded from specific analyses. For instance, in gender-based comparisons, individuals who selected ‘Prefer not to say’ were excluded. In analyses related to child-related outcomes, such as the impact of blackouts on children, only respondents with children were included. Similarly, for variables assessing the worsening of chronic conditions or difficulty accessing medication, responses marked as ‘Not applicable’ were omitted to focus only on those directly affected.

The CORE-10 psychological distress scale was used to evaluate mental health outcomes during power outages. Scores were calculated according to standard procedures by summing the responses to all ten items. Higher total scores indicated greater psychological distress, enabling a structured assessment of emotional well-being in the context of prolonged energy rationing.

Data collection and management

Data collection was conducted using the online platform SurveyMonkey, which hosted the questionnaire and generated a unique survey link. This link was disseminated through social media platforms and digital messaging applications, including Facebook, WhatsApp and Twitter, allowing for wide reach and participant-driven sharing across networks.

The survey began with a preamble outlining the study’s objectives, assuring participants of confidentiality and requesting informed consent. Only individuals who agreed to the terms and provided consent could access the questionnaire, ensuring both voluntary and anonymous participation.

To maintain data quality and integrity, all submitted responses were rigorously reviewed. Entries were excluded if they contained implausible demographic data (eg, unrealistic age), indiscriminate selection of multiple-choice options or if participants responded only to demographic questions without substantive content. Of the 918 initial responses, 845 met the inclusion criteria and were retained for analysis.

To protect participant confidentiality, no personally identifiable information—such as names, national ID numbers, email addresses, phone numbers or home addresses—was collected. SurveyMonkey’s settings were configured to block the collection of IP addresses, and the platform’s access restriction feature was enabled to further ensure anonymity. Participants who did not provide informed consent were automatically prevented from proceeding.

All data were stored in a password-protected database accessible exclusively to the principal research team. No data were shared with external parties. On completion of the study, all collected information, including responses related to mental health, will be permanently deleted.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise responses to categorical items, with frequencies and percentages reported for each variable. To assess associations involving binary categorical dependent variables—such as worsening of chronic conditions, mood changes, panic attacks and difficulties accessing medication, the χ² test was applied when compared with categorical independent variables. When comparisons involved continuous variables, such as age, independent t-tests were performed.

For nominal, non-ordinal categorical variables—such as changes in daily routines due to heat, food preservation strategies, perceived safety and the most affected aspects of daily life—the χ² test for multiple categories was used to identify significant associations. Ordinal logistic regression was applied to analyse ordinal outcomes, including levels of stress and anxiety, sadness and hopelessness, social and family impact, adaptation costs, financial losses, sleep disturbances, changes in food consumption, concerns about food quality and heat-related discomfort. These models estimated the influence of independent variables on the severity of the reported impacts.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Python (Jupyter Notebook environment) and Microsoft Excel.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 845 valid responses were included. The majority of participants were women (60.95%, n=515). Participant ages ranged from 18 to 99 years, with a mean age of 42.35 years and a modal value of 42 years. Responses were received from 19 of Ecuador’s provinces, with the Highland region accounting for the largest share (84.97%, n=717), driven largely by high response rates from Pichincha province (71.48%, n=604). Most respondents had completed university education (45.56%, n=385), and 37.63% (n=318) held a master’s or postgraduate degree. Participants represented a broad array of professional sectors, with the health sector being the most represented (29.11%, n=246) (table 1).

Frustration was the most commonly reported emotion (48.72%, n=400), followed by helplessness (40.32%, n=331), stress (38.0%, n=312), irritability (35.2%, n=289) and anxiety (32.28%, n=265). Notably, 30.45% (n=250) expressed hope that the situation would improve, indicating some resilience. Worsening of chronic conditions

A majority of respondents (64.3%, n=517) indicated that the question on chronic health condition worsening was not applicable. Among those with pre-existing conditions, 7.59% (n=61) reported deterioration during power outages, while 28.11% (n=226) stated their condition remained stable. Regarding access to medication, 11.19% (n=90) of those who required regular prescriptions encountered difficulties obtaining them during outages.

Impact on social and family life

Social and family life was significantly affected by power outages, with 84.04% of respondents reporting some level of disruption. Of these, 36.18% (n=297) described slight effects, 29.84% (n=245) moderate and 18.03% (n=148) severe disruptions. The most affected aspects of daily life included work-related activities (30.45%), household tasks (15.59%) and communication (14.62%) (table 2).

Sleep quality changes

Sleep quality was adversely affected in the majority of participants. While 27.24% (n=213) reported no change, 54.86% reported worsened sleep: 20.46% (n=160) slight, 17.52% (n=137) moderate and 16.88% (n=132) severe. Frequent awakenings were reported by 30.05% (n=235), occasional awakenings by 26.34% (n=206) and insomnia by 21.1% (n=165). Generator noise was a major factor: 51.15% (n=400) reported significant disruption, 23.02% (n=180) slight impact and 25.83% (n=202) reported no effect.

Eating habits

Changes in eating habits were widespread. Slight dietary changes were reported by 41.56% (n=320) and significant changes by 29.48% (n=227). Food freshness and quality were affected for nearly half the sample (49.35%, n=380). Concerns over food spoilage were high (41.95%) or moderate (36.1%), and 25.45% of participants regularly discarded spoiled food. Over half (57.27%, n=441) perceived a high risk of foodborne illness, while 54.29% (n=418) consumed perishables quickly to minimise spoilage. Only 9.87% (n=76) had access to alternative refrigeration, and 35.84% (n=276) took no preventive measures.

Perceived security risks

Power outages heightened perceived security risks. The most common concern was theft (21.39%, n=172), followed by insecurity in public areas (18.41%, n=148) and concern for family safety (16.54%, n=133). Communication loss (12.19%, n=98) and limited emergency service access (5.72%, n=46) were also reported. A sense of vulnerability due to lack of information affected 8.21% (n=66) (table 3).

CORE-10 psychological well-being scores

The CORE-10 scale indicated widespread psychological distress. The highest median scores were for ‘felt frequently anxious’ and ‘had strong emotional support’ (median=2, IQR=1–3). Symptoms such as hopelessness, unhappiness and distress from unwanted memories had a median of 1 (IQR=0–2). The item ‘had suicidal thoughts’ had the lowest median (0, IQR=0–0), although outliers suggest elevated distress in some individuals. Sleep disturbances (‘trouble sleeping’ and ‘panic’) were common, and coping ability was variable (median=1, IQR=0–2) (figure 2).

Demographic variability in responses to power outages

Gender differences were significantly associated with multiple disruption domains. Women more frequently reported food-related impacts (38%), while men identified productivity loss as most affected (41%) (χ² = 43.26, p<0.001). Women also reported higher rates of changes in food consumption (42% vs 27%, p<0.001) and lower financial loss than men (24% vs 36%, p<0.001). Emotional responses showed notable gender gaps: frustration (33% women vs 22% men, p=0.004), fear (29% vs 18%, p=0.013) and stress (44% vs 31%, p=0.014). Heat-related discomfort (47% vs 32%, p=0.005) and irritability (39% vs 26%, p=0.019) were also more frequently reported by women. These gendered patterns are summarised in online supplemental figure 3, which visualises the statistical significance of each variable across demographic categories, highlighting the relative magnitude of disruption by gender (online supplemental figure 3).

Age-stratified differences in psychological impact and coping strategies during power outages

Age emerged as a significant determinant of perceived disruption during power outages. One-way analysis of variance showed that the aspect of life most affected varied by age (F=18.41, p<0.001, η² = 0.137), with younger participants (18–35 years) more likely to report emotional distress (42%) and older adults (>60) citing food-related concerns (34%). Psychological distress, as measured by the CORE-10 scale, was inversely correlated with age (r = –0.269, p<0.001), indicating higher distress among younger individuals. Younger adults also reported significantly more symptoms of tiredness (48% vs 29%), laziness (38% vs 21%), stress (51% vs 33%), anxiety (46% vs 28%) and irritability (43% vs 25%) (all p<0.001). Although food preservation practices (η² = 0.012), implemented strategies (η² = 0.024) and safety perceptions (η² = 0.060) were significantly associated with age, their effect sizes were small. Older participants (55+) were more likely to express concern about food safety (39%) and engage in precautionary behaviours (34%) than younger cohorts (24% and 19%, respectively). These associations are visually summarised in online supplemental figure 4, which illustrates the strength and significance of each demographic variable’s relationship with multiple indicators of disruption, categorised by effect size (negligible, small and medium) (online supplemental figure 4).

Regional disparities in heat-related stress, sleep disturbances and resource access during power outages in Ecuador

Regional variation significantly shaped responses to power outages. Coastal residents reported the highest rates of heat-related discomfort (63%), sleep difficulties (52%) and irritability (49%) compared with Amazonian and Andean participants (all p<0.001). Sleep disturbances like night waking (46% vs 34% Amazonia, 27% Andes; p<0.001) and insomnia (p=0.003) were also more frequent in coastal areas. Food spoilage concern was highest in the coast (51%), followed by Amazonia (39%) and Andes (28%) (p=0.007). Emotional distress was more common in Amazonia (42%) and the coast (36%) than the Andes (30%) (p=0.019), with panic attacks and medication access issues also varying significantly (both p=0.047). Only CORE-10 distress scores showed no regional difference (p=0.176), suggesting comparable underlying psychological baselines. These trends are detailed in online supplemental figure 3 and online supplemental figure 4.

Occupational status significantly influenced how individuals experienced power outages

Occupational status significantly influenced perceived disruption during blackouts. Financial loss was most cited by blue-collar (38%) and pink-collar (34%) workers, while gold-collar (42%) and white-collar (39%) groups emphasised emotional or productivity-related impacts (χ² = 261.09, p<0.001). Psychological distress scores (CORE-10) were highest among unemployed (20.3) and pink-collar (19.6) respondents, and lowest among gold-collar professionals (15.2) (F=3.57, p<0.001). Emotional symptoms—including frustration (up to 47%), laziness (39%), irritability (41%), stress (45%), helplessness (36%), anxiety (33%) and tiredness (44%)—varied significantly across groups (all p<0.05), with pink-collar, blue-collar and grey-collar individuals showing greater vulnerability. Although effect sizes were small, the results reveal consistent occupational disparities in emotional and practical responses.

Coping strategies implemented

Participants employed diverse coping strategies during blackouts, with routine adjustments being the most common (26.1%), followed by use of candles/flashlights (24.3%) and power banks (23.8%). Portable generator use was reported by 17.3%. Regarding adaptation expenses, most respondents (43.1%) reported spending US$0–100, while 20.8% spent US$101–200 and 18.7% between US$201 and US$500. Only 1.5% reported adaptation costs exceeding US$5000. Financial losses due to outages were also widespread: 48.4% reported losses up to US$500, 19.7% up to US$1000 and 18.2% between US$1001 and US$5000. More severe losses were less frequent, with only 6.9% reporting losses above US$5000 (online supplemental figure 5).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the multifaceted impacts of extreme energy rationing implemented over a 4-month period in 2024, which disrupted multiple sectors across Ecuador. To our knowledge, this is the first empirical investigation to comprehensively examine the consequences of prolonged power outages on the healthcare system, economic productivity and population well-being in Ecuador and one of the few of its kind in Latin America.17 Despite widespread societal disruption during this period, the health-related consequences have received limited scholarly attention. Our findings suggest that the healthcare sector was among the most critically affected, with reported interruptions in emergency services and difficulties in operating essential medical devices, particularly for patients dependent on home-based technologies such as oxygen concentrators, home dialysis machines and nebulisers for respiratory therapy. Although establishing a direct causal link between power outages and increased morbidity or mortality is complex, the survey responses indicate potential health risks associated with prolonged electricity shortages, especially among medically vulnerable populations. Further research is needed to confirm causality and quantify the extent of these impacts. For instance, and considering prior studies in Ghana as a relatively good comparator, this type of shortage has been clearly linked to increased hospital mortality.18 According to the authors, each day with more than 2 hours without electricity was associated with a 43% increase in in-facility patient death rates.18 Additionally, we identified that economic losses were disproportionately higher among small businesses and self-employed workers.

From the participants’ perspective, power outages were perceived to have a substantial negative impact on mental health. A significant proportion reported mood disturbances, anxiety and emotional distress. Specifically, 76.96% of respondents experienced mood changes during blackouts, and approximately one-quarter reported panic or anxiety attacks. These responses may be attributed to concerns about access to essential services such as heating, food, water and medical care during outage periods.19 20 Similar findings were reported by Ibrahim et al, who documented significantly higher anxiety levels—measured using the 7-item Generalised Anxiety Disorder scale—among Ghanaian university students exposed to frequent blackouts.21 In addition, our results show that social and family life was significantly disrupted, with 84.04% of participants reporting some level of impact. This suggests that power outages interfere with daily routines, limit social interaction and mobility, and reduce opportunities for recreational activities, all of which may indirectly affect overall well-being.9

In terms of physical health, although most participants did not report chronic conditions, among those with pre-existing illnesses, 7.59% reported a worsening of their condition during power outages and 11.19% experienced difficulties accessing their medication. While these figures are not high in relative terms, they are critical from a public health perspective, as they reflect the vulnerability of specific groups to power supply interruptions—particularly those dependent on medical devices or refrigeration for their treatment, as has been reported in other international studies.22

With regard to dietary habits, the study revealed a clear pattern of concern regarding food quality and preservation, with over 49% of participants reporting food spoilage or the need to discard food. Increased frequency of power outages can lead to a higher likelihood of food degradation and waste.22 Kosa et al, in their study, reported that among those who experienced a blackout, 37% discarded food that had thawed in the freezer and 31% discarded refrigerated perishables that had been without power for at least 4 hours.23 These problems are exacerbated by the low availability of alternative refrigeration methods—only 9.87% of participants had access to generators or coolers—which reflects a structural limitation in coping with energy crises, especially in vulnerable sectors and developing countries like Ecuador.

Another significant finding was the perception of insecurity: more than 21% of participants reported fear of theft, while nearly 17% expressed concern for their family’s safety. This phenomenon has been observed in other countries, where blackouts have been shown to increase opportunities for crime by creating conditions conducive to theft and violence.22 In Ecuador, heavy vehicle drivers were particularly exposed to crime during nighttime blackouts, which may have facilitated criminal activity.24 Moreover, in several areas particularly urban zones with high crime rates the lack of electricity enabled the operation of criminal gangs.2527

Demographic variability analysis revealed that women and younger participants were the most emotionally affected by the energy crisis, reporting higher levels of anxiety, irritability and sleep disturbances. These findings underscore the importance of implementing targeted psychosocial support strategies during prolonged power outages, particularly for vulnerable populations. Although this study did not assess severe outcomes such as suicidal ideation or self-harm, the psychological strain associated with prolonged crises may elevate such risks, warranting further investigation in future research.28 Regional differences were also identified in symptoms related to heat exposure, insomnia and food preservation challenges, highlighting how climatic and geographical factors modulate the experience of energy rationing.

In the current context, it is important to note that one of the main causes of the extreme energy rationing experienced in Ecuador is the country’s dependence on hydroelectric power plants, which becomes critical during drought seasons when reservoir water levels drop. Between 2007 and 2017, 14 hydroelectric plants were built, but not all of them operate at full capacity due to various technical failures; some were never inaugurated, and Ecuador’s largest hydroelectric plant shuts down when sediment levels rise in the Coca River, as mud and debris can damage the turbines.29 30 Additionally, investments have been made in new power generation plants. Between 2020 and 2023, ten new facilities were expected to begin operations to meet rising electricity demand, as outlined in the Electricity Master Plan.30

This scenario has been further aggravated by other issues, such as deficiencies in alternative energy sources, including thermal power plants. In Ecuador, only 1.44% of electricity generation comes from non-conventional sources.29 From January to 9 October 2024, thermal generation averaged 641 megawatts, which represents only one-fifth of the country’s total installed capacity.29 30 For instance, between 2011 and 2021, energy production from sugarcane bagasse increased from 1423 kBEP to 2006 kBEP. However, data from two biogas power plants revealed that in 2021 they accounted for only 0.1% of the national energy matrix.31

It is essential to acknowledge that the energy crisis is not exclusive to Ecuador. Other developing countries have faced similar challenges. For example, the electricity supply in sub-Saharan Africa is constrained by inadequate infrastructure and limited generation capacity. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that despite these issues, countries like Kenya have made significant progress in diversifying their energy sources, investing in geothermal and wind energy to reduce dependence on hydropower, which is vulnerable to droughts.32 Ecuadorian society must begin the process of diversifying its energy sources, especially considering that, according to the International Energy Agency, energy demand in the region is expected to increase by 70% by 2030.31 33

Despite its methodological limitations, this study offers preliminary insights that may inform future policy, public health preparedness and social interventions aimed at mitigating the impacts of energy rationing. The evidence demonstrates the urgent need to diversify energy sources, strengthen the resilience of the healthcare system and develop community-based adaptation strategies—particularly for vulnerable populations and regions at higher risk.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The use of a non-probabilistic, convenience-based sampling strategy, distributed primarily through digital platforms and social media, may have excluded individuals without reliable internet access or those living in areas with limited connectivity. This likely resulted in the overrepresentation of specific demographic groups, particularly urban populations. Consequently, the findings cannot be generalised to the entire population of Ecuador and should be interpreted with caution. Although the sample included participants from geographically diverse regions, the relatively low response rate and the self-selection of participants—who were likely more digitally connected or more severely impacted by the energy crisis—may have introduced selection bias. Individuals who experienced more pronounced effects may have been more motivated to respond.

In addition, the study relied on a self-administered, cross-sectional online survey, which is inherently prone to recall bias and social desirability bias. The cross-sectional design also limits the ability to capture changes in perceptions or experiences over time, precluding the identification of longitudinal trends or adaptive responses. Participants may have either underreported or over-reported the emotional, economic or health-related impacts of power outages. Additionally, the assessment of complex variables such as mental health and food security was based on self-reported data, which carries a risk of subjective interpretation. Although the CORE-10 is a validated tool for evaluating psychological distress, the exclusive use of a single instrument limits the depth and diagnostic precision of the mental health evaluation.

Finally, while subgroup analyses by region, gender and age were performed, the uneven distribution of respondents—particularly the predominance of participants from the Highland region and from the healthcare sector—may have biased the results toward specific experiences or perceptions. This imbalance further limits the generalisability of the findings to other geographical regions and occupational groups within Ecuador.

Conclusion

The 2024 energy rationing crisis in Ecuador had far-reaching implications across health, economic and social domains. This nationwide study provides the first empirical evidence of its multifaceted impact, particularly on vulnerable populations. Prolonged power outages disrupted essential medical care, exacerbated chronic conditions and contributed to widespread psychological distress especially among women and younger individuals. Economic losses were substantial and disproportionately affected informal and service-sector workers. Furthermore, regional disparities highlighted how geography and climate modulate the health and social effects of energy shortages.

The results suggest a pressing need to diversify Ecuador’s energy sources, invest in resilient infrastructure and prioritise adaptive strategies that protect at-risk populations. Future preparedness efforts must integrate environmental, technological and psychosocial dimensions to minimise the adverse outcomes of future energy crises. The data presented here provide a critical foundation for evidence-based policy reforms aimed at ensuring energy security, public health resilience and social stability in Ecuador and other similarly vulnerable nations.
