Functional Analysis and Quality Assessment of Mobile Applications for Managing Alcohol Use Disorder
Authors: Yangmi Lee, Ji-Min Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aims:This study investigated the current mobile applications (apps) for the management of alcohol use disorder (AUD) and analyzed their functionality and quality.Design/Methods:A comprehensive search of the Apple App Store and Google Play Store was conducted between August 1 and October 31, 2023, based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, to identify apps specifically designed for patients with AUD. Two independent raters evaluated the apps using the Mobile App Rating Scale and the Institute for Healthcare Informatics functionality score.Results:This search resulted in the selection of 33 apps for assessment. The Mobile App Rating Scale quality assessment indicated that more than half of the selected apps were suitable for use, and the Institute for Healthcare Informatics functionality assessment determined that the apps had a 4.5 average for 11 possible functions. Additionally, a detailed analysis of the top three apps with the highest scores for both quality and functionality was conducted. This study highlighted the key features that apps for AUD management should incorporate and identified possible areas for improvement.Conclusions:Based on these results, we recommend that apps record and store data on the number of days to abstinence and the amount of alcohol consumed and display this information using graphs. In addition, we recommend that apps allow users to customize the timing and number of reminders and alarms and connect to a support system or professional immediately in high-risk situations.

INTRODUCTION

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is characterized by an impaired ability to stop or control alcohol use despite experiencing alcohol consumption-related physical, psychological, and social problems (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). In the Republic of Korea, the lifetime prevalence of AUD is 11.6%, making it the most prevalent among the major mental disorders (Ministry of Health and Welfare, 2021). More than 50% of patients with AUD relapse within 3 months of discharge, and over 80% relapse within 6 months (Barillot et al., 2023). Thus, AUD is a chronic relapsing condition that necessitates continuous and systematic treatment services to manage repeated relapses (Dalhousie University Department of Medicine, 2024; Oh et al., 2014).

The services provided by health care facilities and integrated addiction management support centers in the community generally include self-help group therapy, psychosocial therapy, and pharmacotherapy (Bernstein et al., 2024; Joe, 2019; National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, 2025). Long-term postdischarge treatment is associated with lower relapse rates (Lenaerts et al., 2014; McKay et al., 2004); however, the mental health services utilization rate remains as low as 2.6%, mainly due to factors such as social stigma, poor accessibility, time and economic burdens, lack of insight about the condition, insufficient treatment motivation, and inadequate services (Bearnot et al., 2018; Finn et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2018; Ministry of Health & Welfare, 2021). To address these issues, mobile applications (apps) are being utilized as a supplementary method for providing medical support because they offer the advantages of being accessible from home, as well as other locations, and the interventions are integrated within the context of daily life (Batra et al., 2017; Gustafson et al., 2014).

Studies have shown that the use of AUD management apps leads to higher postdischarge treatment maintenance and similar or lower alcohol consumption rates than traditional face-to-face follow-up treatment (Barrio et al., 2017; Farren et al., 2022; Gonzalez & Dulin, 2015; Gustafson et al., 2014; You et al., 2017). App-based services offer continuous, personalized care without temporal and spatial constraints, enabling real-time communication with support systems consisting of service providers, treatment teams, and family members at little to no cost (Batra et al., 2017; National Institute of Mental Health, 2021). Additionally, these apps can help monitor alcohol consumption and high-risk drinking situations, provide tailored management services, and suggest appropriate coping strategies (Gustafson et al., 2014; Miguel et al., 2023).

Hundreds of apps for alcohol consumption management can be found online. Most of them are aimed at reducing alcohol consumption for problematic alcohol use, and approximately 40 to 60 apps specifically target AUD (Colbert et al., 2020; Penzenstadler et al., 2016; Staiger et al., 2020; Tofighi et al., 2019). However, studies have only verified the effectiveness of approximately 10 of these apps (Colbert et al., 2020; Staiger et al., 2020). In the Republic of Korea, the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety has issued guidelines regarding the safety, performance, and clinical trial planning of digital therapeutic devices, which included mobile apps, for AUD in 2021 (Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, 2021). Despite these efforts, there is a scarcity of research on the development or quality evaluation of such apps. Consumers often choose inaccurate and low-quality apps based on information obtained from social media, web searches, or reviews (Bauer et al., 2020; Schueller et al., 2018) and consequently face challenges in selecting high-quality apps that include the desired features (Korea Health Industry Development Institute, 2020).

Thus, conducting quality assessments and functionality analyses of these apps is essential to help consumers make objective and informed choices (Aitken & Gauntlett, 2013; Stoyanov et al., 2015). Quality assessments will enable consumers to select high-quality content rationally (Bardus et al., 2016; Colbert et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2019), whereas functionality analyses will help them identify and better utilize the features they require (Choi et al., 2018; Gong et al., 2020; Myers et al., 2020; Schmeelk et al., 2022).

To address these unmet needs, this study reviewed the current mobile apps for managing AUD, analyzed their functions, and assessed their quality.

METHODS

App Selection

Between August 1 and October 31, 2023, we searched the major app stores in the Republic of Korea—specifically, the Apple App Store on iPhone Operating System (iOS) and Google Play Store on Android—for mobile apps designed for patients with AUD.

The inclusion criterion was apps designed for postdischarge relapse management of AUD. Two independent raters conducted the searches using the search terms “alcohol,” “alcoholism,” “sober,” “sobriety,” “relapse,” and “alcohol recovery” in Korean and English.

The exclusion criteria were nonavailability of the apps in Korean or English, pro and lite versions of the same app (to ensure accurate comparisons among apps), requirement of a membership code for access, and apps not available or nonfunctional in the Republic of Korea.

The apps that satisfied all the inclusion and exclusion criteria were selected for analyses. As there were no apps for AUD in Korean, we analyzed the functioning and quality of English language apps.

Assessment Methods

Quality Assessment

The Mobile Application Rating Scale (MARS) was developed by Stoyanov et al. (2015) to evaluate the quality of mobile apps. This 29-item scale consists of four objective quality subscales and two subjective quality subscales. The objective quality subscales comprise: engagement (five items: entertainment, interest, customization, interactivity, and target group appropriateness); functionality (four items: performance, ease of use, navigation, and gestural design); aesthetics (three items: layout, graphics quality, and visual appeal); and information (seven items: accuracy of app description, goals, quality of information, quantity of information, visual information, credibility, and evidence base). The subjective quality subscales comprise: satisfaction (four items: recommended to others, intended frequency of use, willingness to pay, and overall star rating); and perceived impact (six items: awareness, knowledge, attitudes, intention to change, help-seeking, and behavior change). Each item is rated on a 5-point scale (1 = inadequate, 5 = excellent), with higher scores indicating higher quality. At the time of development, the overall internal consistency of the MARS regarding Cronbach’s alpha was 0.90, and the inter-rater reliability per the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.79. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha and ICC across the 33 included apps were 0.85 and 0.85 (95% confidence interval, 0.83–0.88), respectively.

Functionality Assessment

The Institute for Healthcare Informatics (IMS) functionality score is a scale that was developed by the IMS as a tool for assessing the functionality of mobile apps (Aitken & Gauntlett, 2013) using 11 criteria: inform, instruct, record, collect data, share data, evaluate data, intervene, display, guide, remind/alert, and communicate. The four data-related features (collect data, share data, evaluate data, and intervene) are subfunctions of the “record” function. Each function is assigned a score of 1 if present and 0 if absent; a higher total score indicates a broader range of functionality. Although no prior studies have confirmed the reliability of the IMS functionality score, it has been utilized for analyzing app functionality in several studies (Choi et al., 2018; Diaz-Skeete et al., 2021; Gong et al., 2020; Masterson Creber et al., 2016; Vercell et al., 2022). In this study, its inter-rater reliability per the Cohen’s Kappa coefficient was 0.84 (95% confidence interval, 0.79–0.90, p < .001).

Assessment Process

Two raters independently analyzed the functionality and quality of the selected apps using the IMS functionality and MARS. The time required for reviewing and evaluating each app ranged from 15 to 30 minutes. Before the main evaluation, three apps were assessed in a pilot phase to enhance inter-rater concordance. In cases of disagreement, each rater provided their rationale, and discussions continued until consensus was reached. If consensus proved difficult, the app in question was reevaluated with the involvement of the principal investigator.

Data Analyses

The final IMS functionality and MARS scores were calculated as mean and standard deviation values using Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). Inter-rater reliability was assessed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). For the MARS, the ICC was calculated, whereas Cohen’s Kappa was used for the IMS functionality scores because it involves categorical data (0 = No, 1 = Yes).

RESULTS

Selection of Apps for Analyses

Using the selected search terms, 3,932 iOS and Android apps were identified. After removing 1,900 duplicate apps based on the app titles and developer names, 54 apps were downloaded. The exclusion criteria were then applied to eliminate 21 apps. Thus, 33 apps were finally selected for analyses (Figure 1).

General Characteristics of Selected Apps

A total of 26 apps (78.8%) were available for download on only one platform (iOS or Android), whereas seven (21.2%) were available on both platforms. Functionality

The average score for this subscale, which assesses performance, ease of use, navigation, and gestural design, was 3.55 ± 0.47. Users could begin using 78.8% (n = 26) of the apps immediately after download without an additional login process. Additionally, 39.4% (n = 13) of the apps provided clear usage instructions, presented mostly as text or images upon first use, and several also offered pop-up instructions for each menu. Most apps displayed icons alongside their names, which enhanced usability, and featured quick screen transitions.

Aesthetics

The average score for this subscale, which assesses layout, graphics, and visual appeal, was 3.11 ± 0.69. Images or icons were well-placed in 60.6% (n = 20) of the apps, making them easy to use, and the design and graphic quality of the main interface were high, enhancing visual appeal. Apps with lower scores (below 3 points) featured unintuitive icons or menus, inconsistent font sizes and styles, and low-resolution graphics, which diminished their visual appeal.

Information

The average score for this subscale, which assesses the accuracy of the app description in the app store, goals, quality of information, quantity of information, visual information, credibility, and evidence base, was 3.07 ± 0.56. All apps provided appropriate descriptions within the platform, and 33.3% (n = 11) allowed users to set personal goals. Three apps did not provide any information (9.1%), and 24 (72.7%) lacked visual information. The main content in the apps that provided information (91.9%, n = 30) was general information related to AA, such as terminology, meetings, locations, the 12 steps, and the Big Book. Only 48.5% (n = 16) of the apps disclosed their information sources, and Step Away: Alcohol Help was the only app developed based on evidence and shown to be effective through research.

Engagement

The average score for this subscale, which assesses entertainment, interest, customization, interactivity, and target group, was 2.65 ± 0.81. Apps with high levels of entertainment and interest (24.2%, n = 8) offered features such as rewards for achieving goals, games to help manage drinking urges, and various coping strategies depicted through images or graphs. However, 51.5% (n = 17) of the apps only provided AA-related information via audio or text or merely tracked abstinence duration, lacking features to actively engage users. Customization options, which allow users to personalize features such as sounds, content, and alarms, were available in only 12.1% (n = 4) of the apps. Only 9.1% (n = 3) allowed for sharing input data with others and provided responses or feedback; most apps were only designed for simple data input and lacked interactive functions.

The apps with the highest scores were Rehubs—Your Sober Life (4.58 ± 0.20), Step Away: Alcohol Help (4.28 ± 0.49), and Saying When: How to Quit Drinking or Cut Down (3.89 ± 0.39). The apps with the lowest scores were No Alcohol–Quit Drinking (2.15 ± 0.67), The Pocket Sponsor (2.35 ± 0.53), and AA Speakers (Alcoholics) (2.78 ± 0.66).

IMS Functionality Analysis Results

The results of the app review using the IMS functionality scores are summarized in Table 2 and Figure 2. On average, the 33 apps reviewed included 4.5 of 11 possible functions. The most included function was “inform” (n = 30, 90.9%), followed by “record” (n = 24, 72.7%), “instruct” (n = 23, 69.7%), “collect data” (n = 22, 66.7%), “display” (n = 14, 42.4%), “remind/alert” (n = 12, 36.4%), “share data” (n = 10, 30.3%), and “communicate” (n = 9, 27.3%). The remaining three functions—“intervene,” “evaluate data,” and “guide”—were each included in 6% (n = 2) of the apps. The two apps with the highest number of functions were Step Away: Alcohol Help (all 11 functions) and Saying When: How to Quit Drinking or Cut Down (10 functions, missing only the “communicate” function).

IMS functionality scores. The points indicate the total number of applications that contain the function. IMS = Intensive Care Unit Mobility Scale.

Regarding the extent to which the 33 AUD management apps meet the 11 functionality criteria, “inform” was included in 90.9% (n = 30) of the apps. These apps provided information on topics such as the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT), standard drink sizes, AA-related literature (12 steps, Big Book, and daily meditations), and various drinking coping strategies and treatments delivered through various media formats such as audio, podcasts, videos, YouTube, or websites. “Instruct” was featured in 69.7% (n = 23) of the apps, providing guidelines on how to use the app or interpret charts. “Record” was included in 72.7% (n = 24) of the apps, allowing users to enter data such as the start date of sobriety, number of drinking days, high-risk drinking situations, urges to drink, and moods.

The four subfunctions related to the recorded data—collect data, share data, evaluate data, and intervene—were analyzed according to the following criteria: “Collect data” (n = 22, 66.7%) allowed the app to store and collect user-entered data such as the start date of sobriety, drinking status, amount of alcohol consumed, high-risk drinking situations, urges to drink, and mood states; “Share data” (n = 10, 30.3%) allowed users to share the data they entered with others via email or messaging services; “Evaluate data” (n = 2, 6.1%) analyzed the collected data to assess drinking patterns or determine if preset goals were achieved; and “Intervene” (n = 2, 6.1%) provided alarms at high-risk drinking times based on the collected data to encourage behavior change or offered coping strategies with an alarm if the users exceeded their set drinking limits.

Among the remaining functions, “display” was featured in 42.4% (n = 14) of the apps, which presented various data, such as the start date of drinking, total drinking days, number of drinking urges, mood states, health recovery due to sobriety, and money saved from not drinking, visualized through graphs or images. The “guide” function was included in 6.1% (n = 2) of the apps, providing guidelines on AUD diagnostic tests (AUDIT) or low-risk drinking amounts to help users set appropriate goals. “Remind/Alert” was featured in 36.4% (n = 12) of the apps, offering alarms to encourage continuous app usage, including reminders to use the app if inactive for a certain period, warning alarms, and coping strategies during high-risk drinking times. The “communicate” function was included in 27.3% (n = 9) of the apps, allowing users to communicate with other app users, sponsors, and experts using bulletin boards, chats, or online community sites (such as Zoom or AA sites).

Rehubs—Your Sober Life

This social community app enables patients with AUD at similar stages of sobriety to communicate and support each other through a message board. Two former patients with AUD participated in the app development process.

The app requires a login and is available in English and Swedish. It achieved the highest MARS score of 4.58, with particularly high scores for the engagement, functionality, and aesthetics subscales, and had the closest score to the maximum of 5 points.

Engagement

The app offers varied content and allows users to add favorite items (such as sobriety pledges, checklists, motivational texts and images, and coping strategies) to the main screen. It allows for the visualization of user progress toward goals, which contributes to enhancing engagement. Users can set alarms for exercise or sobriety milestones and receive reminders for incomplete tasks, encouraging consistent app use.

Functionality

During testing, the app performed flawlessly and provided clear instructions. It features an intuitive interface, facilitates smooth navigation, and enhances the user experience.

Aesthetics

A well-organized layout with strategically placed menus, icons, and content, coupled with high-quality graphics contributed to a visually appealing interface that minimized user fatigue during extended use.

Information

The app provides appropriate in-platform descriptions and a sobriety calculator for tracking progress. It motivates users through measurable milestones and offers immediately applicable coping strategies for high-risk situations with visual tracking of strategy execution. However, the information sources were unclear, and the effectiveness of the app was not supported by research, resulting in a lower score on this subscale. The premium features are available for $9.49 USD monthly or $46.99 USD annually; available content includes a cognitive-behavioral therapy program aimed at helping users accept their past and change attitudes and behaviors with a new mindset, a voice-supported audio bulletin board, stress reduction and craving management tools, and access to various events.

IMS Functionality Score

This app fulfilled eight of the 11 functionality criteria. It allows for data input, collection, display, and sharing, but lacks data evaluation and intervention functions. Data primarily consists of quantitative inputs such as the number of days, mood states, and activity levels. Sobriety tracking was precise to the second and visually presented. The app also displays progress toward sobriety milestones using graphs that illustrate the number of days remaining until the next target. Users can also share the collected data at their discretion.

Step Away: Alcohol Help

This app, developed with support from the U.S. National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, provides comprehensive self-management for individuals with AUD. It is available in English and requires login credentials, with a subscription fee of $4.99 USD per month.

The app received an overall MARS score of 4.28, with high scores on the functionality, aesthetics, and information subscales, although it had a lower score for the engagement subscale.

Functionality

The app operated without any technical errors, and its intuitive design enabled easy navigation and quick familiarization with all features.

Aesthetics

The app features a well-organized layout, with strategically placed menus, icons, and content, as well as high-quality, high-resolution graphics that enhance the visual experience.

Information

The app clearly indicates its information sources, and its effectiveness is supported by research. Initially developed in 2013, the app was upgraded once to the assessed version. The original model, known as the Craving Management Intervention System (Location-Based Monitoring and Intervention System for Alcohol Use Disorders), incorporated global positioning system to trigger alerts when users approached designated high-risk areas, aimed to enhance motivation and provide cognitive-behavioral therapy (Dulin et al., 2014; Gonzalez & Dulin, 2015). A 6-week efficacy trial resulted in a reduction in overall alcohol consumption by more than 50%. However, due to frequent false alerts, the global positioning system feature was removed, which led to the Step Away: Alcohol Help version of the app. In a 6-month trial, 36% of users continued to use this app consistently, and the number of binge drinking days was significantly reduced (Malte et al., 2021). These findings confirm the usefulness, sustainability, and effectiveness of the app.

Engagement

The app allows users to set goals related to the number of drinking days and visually track progress, quantifying the benefits of sobriety, such as reduced calorie intake and money saved. The lower score for this category is attributed to the lack of a bulletin board function for user communication and the inability to personalize alarm settings.

IMS Functionality Score

This app achieved an IMS functionality score of 11, making it the only app among the 33 reviewed to fulfill all 11 functional criteria. Compared with other apps, Step Away: Alcohol Help offers users a broader range of data entry options and provides personalized feedback based on user input. It also delivers structured strategies for managing high-risk situations. The app accommodates both quantitative data (entered numerically) and qualitative data (entered as text). The quantitative data are presented as visual information in graphs or by quantifying metrics such as the amount and frequency of alcohol consumption and cravings. The qualitative data encompass different types of stressors that users experience and their corresponding stress management plans. Users input daily information about their drinking status and cravings, and the app adjusts its feedback accordingly, providing motivational or supportive messages. Users can also customize reminders, alarms, and message timings according to their preferences. For high-risk situations, the app offers a two-track approach to coping strategies. When facing a craving or high-risk situation, users can press “get help” to access a screen with three options: craving, problem, or stress/depression. If “problem” is selected, the app guides the user through identifying the issue, setting goals to address it, and developing a detailed action plan. Alternatively, users can bypass these self-help steps and directly request external assistance. If the call option is chosen, the app immediately connects the user to emergency services (911), the Veterans Crisis Line, an AA group, or another preselected support service.

Saying When: How to Quit Drinking or Cut Down

This app is based on a program developed by the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health in Canada, specifically designed for individuals with AUD. Unlike many other apps that strive for total abstinence, this app helps users reduce their alcohol consumption by allowing them to set personalized daily and weekly drinking limits. It does not require a login and is available in English.

In this study, the app received an overall MARS score of 3.89, with high scores for the functionality, aesthetics, and information subscales, although it had a relatively lower score for the engagement subscale.

Functionality

The app offers clear instructions and guidelines, making it easy for new users to navigate and understand its features.

Aesthetics

The app has a well-organized layout, with effectively positioned menus, icons, and content, and displays user progress toward their daily and weekly drinking goals with visual images.

Information

The app store description is clear and concise, and the sources of information within the app are well-documented. However, no studies are available on the app’s effectiveness.

Engagement

The lower score for this subscale is due to the lack of personalized alarm settings and the absence of interactive features, such as bulletin boards or chat functions.

IMS Functionality Score

The app achieved an IMS functionality score of 10, missing only the “communicate” function. It primarily focuses on goal setting and evaluation, with each function implemented simply. A key feature is the option to set daily and weekly drinking limits, assess the differences between the set limit and actual consumption, and display this information graphically. If users exceed their set limits, the app issues a warning alert, encouraging them to reassess their goals or coping strategies.

DISCUSSION

Mobile apps have tremendous potential for meaningfully expanding access to treatment for AUD. In this study, we assessed the current mobile apps for AUD management, with a focus on evaluating their quality and functionality. The quality of the 33 selected apps was determined using the MARS. The average score was 3.10 ± 0.54 of 5, with 57.6% of the apps scoring 3 or more; thus, given that a MARS score of 3 is considered the threshold for acceptable quality (Stoyanov et al., 2015), more than half of the apps were deemed suitable for use. However, the analysis of the IMS functionality scores revealed that the 33 apps offered only an average of 4.5 of 11 possible functions, which indicates a lack of functional diversity.

The 33 apps reviewed generally received high scores in the “functionality” and “aesthetics” subscales, which are two of the four objective quality subscales among the six domains covered by the MARS. This indicated that most commercially available apps for AUD management were user-friendly and had few functional defects, such as system errors. The high scores in the “aesthetics” subscale also suggest that the apps were visually appealing, with high-resolution graphics and well-designed interfaces. However, most apps scored below 3 in the “engagement” subscale, which evaluates five aspects: entertainment, interest, customization, interactivity, and target group. Among these, customization and interactivity are crucial for encouraging active app use because personalized strategies and user interaction through information and communication technology effectively promote sustained app engagement (Johnson et al., 2016). Many of the 33 apps included in this study lacked features, such as customization options that allow users to personalize settings and interactive features, such as options to provide feedback and setup reminders and alerts, which led to lower engagement scores.

Studies have shown that continuous use of apps designed to prevent relapse in AUD can improve abstinence rates (Farren et al., 2022; Gustafson et al., 2014; You et al., 2017). Given that AUD is a chronic, relapsing condition that requires ongoing management, enhancing engagement-related features is essential when developing these apps.

Among the 11 features assessed using the IMS functionality scores, “inform” and “record” were the features most commonly included in the apps, whereas “evaluate data,” “guide,” and “intervene” were available less frequently. The information provided by the 33 apps primarily focused on self-management of abstinence by providing access to content related to AA, such as the 12 steps and Big Book, daily meditations, various drinking coping strategies, and the benefits of abstinence. These features are ultimately intended to support sobriety maintenance and relapse prevention. The “record” feature allowed users to enter quantitative and qualitative data. The quantitative data included the sobriety start date, drinking days, and the amount of alcohol consumed. The qualitative data captured user moods or circumstances during high-risk situations, such as craving periods, which could be documented in a diary- or checklist-based format. Real-time data recording in high-risk situations, including alcohol cravings, is referred to as ecological momentary assessment, which involves participants using portable devices (smartphones and personal digital assistants) to document their experiences (emotions, thoughts, and behaviors) multiple times in their daily routines (Kim & Kwon, 2018). Ecological momentary assessment provides high ecological validity and can effectively capture the temporal characteristics of variables related to high-risk situations and their interactions with other factors, making it a valuable tool in addiction research for alcohol, drugs, and gambling (Kianersi et al., 2024; McDevitt-Murphy et al., 2018). Given the wide range of fluctuations in emotional states, such as cravings and urges, relying solely on memory to record these states can result in recall bias and memory distortion (Arend et al., 2024). Therefore, incorporating a feature that enables accurate real-time documentation of user state is essential because it enhances the accuracy of collected data and ultimately supports the delivery of personalized services based on a precise analysis of a user’s condition.

The less frequently available features “evaluate data,” “guide,” and “intervene” were implemented in only 6.1% of each app reviewed; these are also crucial for effective AUD management. To prevent relapse in individuals with AUD, it is essential to evaluate the data entered by the user to identify high-risk situations and early warning signs of relapse. When such situations occur, the app should provide guidance on coping strategies or offer immediate interventions to suppress cravings (Shin & Kim, 2022). The Step Away: Alcohol Help app includes all three features. The app initially prompts users to complete the AUDIT, evaluates the results, and uses them to provide guidance, such as raising awareness regarding the seriousness of the user’s drinking and recommending medical treatment. The app automatically analyses drinking patterns and suggests interventions by issuing alerts during high-risk times. It also assesses whether users have reached their goal milestones and offers relevant interventions (feedback) if not.

However, most of the AUD management apps reviewed in this study only included features allowing users to input mood, stress, and craving levels, without offering evaluation, guidance, or intervention based on the input data. Due to the continuous advancement of information technology, it is now possible to instantly monitor physiological metrics such as heart rate and respiration rate, which reflect a user’s craving state, using a mobile phone. Since mobile phones are the devices that users are most likely to carry with them at all times, apps in these phones could be actively used to monitor a user’s craving state and physiological signals. In other words, if the craving state input by the user and the measured physiological signals are analyzed and the app confirms a craving state, it could immediately provide craving prevention interventions, greatly enhancing the effectiveness of AUD management.

Of the top three apps with high scores in functional and quality, availability and effectiveness were evaluated only for the Step Away: Alcohol Help app. A study conducted to create a veteran version of the Step Away app (Blonigen et al., 2020) reported that customized feedback promoted app use. A subsequent study that evaluated the usability and acceptability of the app among veterans (Malte et al., 2021) found that 36% of the users used it until the 24th week and suggested that the daily and weekly feedback functions and push alarms may have contributed to the observed effects of reduced alcohol consumption. In another study that compared the Step Away: Alcohol Help app with Chatbot (Dulin et al., 2022), the Step Away: Alcohol Help app group used it more frequently and longer than those in the Chatbot group, which indicates that daily push alarms and visually superior graphs may have encouraged its use. In other words, this app has the advantages of push alarms, helpful infographics, and customized feedback, and the proper combination of these functions could be effective in increasing app usage and reducing alcohol consumption.

Based on the function and quality evaluations and analyses results of the top three apps, we propose the following four recommendations to facilitate efficient app selection by individuals with AUD. First, we recommend selecting apps with enhanced “record,” “collect data,” and “display” functions. Specifically, user input data (such as abstinence goal, number of days to abstinence, exercise time, craving time, and location) should be saved and their results accumulated and displayed onscreen visually in graphs with impactful color schemes. Colored graphs are effective tools in behavior modification therapy and can enhance motivation (Jimmy & Jose, 2011). Therefore, if users can intuitively monitor their current state through visual data, their motivation to remain abstinent is likely to increase. The Rehubs—Your Sober Life app displays progress toward sobriety milestones using a sobriety calculator and a graph that illustrates the number of days remaining until a goal is reached.

Second, we recommend selecting apps with enhanced “remind/alert” and “customization” functions. For example, it would be effective to include a customization function that allows users to set the timing and number of reminders or alarms. Although reminders and alarms are generally recognized as being able to promote behavioral change (Wiecek et al., 2020), the inability to adjust their timing or frequency could result in excessive alerts, prompting users to delete the app. A 2019 survey of nearly 2,000 mobile app users found that 12.6% of respondents had deleted apps due to excessive notifications (CleverTap, 2019). The Step Away: Alcohol Help app effectively utilizes the remind/alert functionality by allowing users to customize the timing of motivational texts or images based on their preferences. Research has shown that such features can help reduce alcohol consumption (Quanbeck et al., 2014).

Third, we recommend selecting apps with enhanced “evaluate data,” “guide,” and “intervene” functions. For example, if the amount of alcohol entered by the user or the level of desire to drink is high, an app that detects the user is in a high-risk situation and sends a message recommending treatment could be effective in preventing recurrence. Notably, systematic literature reviews on internet-based interventions (Kelders et al., 2012; Looyestyn et al., 2017) have highlighted the importance of strategies that enhance user engagement, such as personalized interventions, rewards, peer support, and feedback.

Fourth, we recommend selecting apps that have an “interactivity” function. For example, app users who can share information about coping strategies to maintain sobriety and withdrawal symptoms in a shared communication space can help each other to maintain sobriety. Additionally, including a function that allows users to make phone calls to a support system (such as family members, AA, and healers) whenever they want could be effective. A study of 210 individuals with alcohol dependence found that those who received social support from AA, family members, or peers had a better quality of life (Yoon & Kim, 2015). The Rehubs—Your Sober Life app includes a message board where users with similar sobriety durations can interact, thereby enhancing motivation for abstinence. Additionally, users can open chat windows and invite others to participate in conversations.

The number of available apps for help with mental health care is increasing, as is the proportion of young adults using these apps to manage their health. However, many older adults still have relatively negative views concerning apps and find it difficult to access or navigate them, possibly due to low digital literacy and a lack of confidence regarding using smartphones. For example, the percentage of individuals with AUD aged 50 years and older in the United Kingdom is quite high; approximately 29%, and the age of those with AUD continues to increase (UK Department of Health and Social Care, 2024). Therefore, nurses will need to help patients by specifically identifying the difficulties they face when using AUD management mobile apps. It is also important to encourage and increase app usage by providing rewards and strengthening patient confidence through educating them about app use. The findings of this study could provide nurses with information that supports them in helping patients with AUD select high-quality and functional apps, monitor app use as well as high-risk drinking situations, and provide the help they need in a timely manner.

Strengths and Limitations

Two key strengths of this study can be highlighted. First, this study was the first to systematically evaluate the quality and functionality of mobile apps for AUD management using both MARS and IMS functionality scores. Although most previous studies on AUD management apps relied solely on the MARS for qualitative analysis, we also utilized the IMS functionality score to assess app features. This dual approach allowed us to identify and analyze important features not covered by the MARS that could enable users to select high-quality apps with features that best meet their needs. Second, the evaluations were conducted by two raters with multiple rounds of consensus-seeking. When consensus could not be reached, a third expert was consulted, further enhancing the reliability of the evaluation results.

Despite these strengths, a few limitations of this study should be noted. First, some AUD management apps may have been excluded from the review because they did not have English or Korean language support, required an access code, or were unavailable or nonfunctional in the Republic of Korea. Second, because the study only included apps available during a specific period, some apps reviewed may no longer be available, and subsequent updates in the apps could have altered their content. Finally, because the quality assessment was conducted by experts using the MARS, there may be discrepancies between the experts’ evaluations and actual user experiences. To address this potential discrepancy, future studies that include quality assessments from user perspectives would be beneficial.

CONCLUSIONS

We examined 33 mobile apps for managing AUD and reviewed their quality and functionality using MARS and IMS functionality scores, respectively. Of the 33 included apps, more than 50% were suitable for use. In the functionality analysis, the 33 apps had an average of 4.5 of 11 possible functional criteria. We also conducted an in-depth analysis of the top three apps with the highest scores for both quality and functionality. Based on these results, we recommend that apps record and store data on the number of days to abstinence and the amount of alcohol consumed and display this information using graphs. In addition, we recommend that apps allow users to customize the timing and number of reminders and alarms and connect to a support system or professional immediately in high-risk situations. Nurses could benefit from the information in this study by using it to determine which high-quality and functional apps can be recommended to patients and help with using them regularly.
