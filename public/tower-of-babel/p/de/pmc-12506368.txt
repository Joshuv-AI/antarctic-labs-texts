Prediction of preeclampsia by evaluating the RI of placental second villi in the third trimester with HR-Flow ultrasound
Authors: Ye-Ping He, Jun-Lan Xu, Xiu-Feng Yin, Gang Li, Chao Xue, Yi-Feng Luo, Jian-Feng Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the value of predicting preeclampsia (PE) using high resolution flow (HR-Flow) ultrasound to evaluate the resistance index (RI) of placental second villi in the third trimester.MethodsA total of 103 singleton pregnancies with follow-up results undergoing HR-Flow imaging of the placenta during the third trimester in the affiliated Yixing Hospital of Jiangsu University were recruited in this prospective cohort study from August 2021 to July 2023. Doppler resistance index values of the placental second villi were measured. Based on the clinical manifestations, the patients were divided into three groups: control (n = 78), hypertension (n = 11), and PE (n = 14). The performance of the second villous RI in predicting PE was analyzed using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve.ResultsThe PE group had a significantly higher rate of poor placental perfusion than the control and hypertension groups (P < 0.001, 0.048). The second villous RI values of the control, hypertension, and PE groups were 0.42 ± 0.10, 0.47 ± 0.07, 0.49 ± 0.06, respectively, with statistically significant differences among the three groups (P = 0.017). The area under the ROC curve for predicting PE was 0.734, the optimal cut off value was 0.50, and the sensitivity and specificity were 0.643 and 0.809, respectively.ConclusionThe optimal cut-off value of placental second villous RI may be a potential evaluation parameter for predicting PE, and is worthy of further study.Keywords: Hypertensive disorders in pregnancy, Preeclampsia, Placenta, HR-Flow, Resistance index

Background

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), including gestational hypertension (GH), chronic hypertension, preeclampsia (PE)- eclampsia, and chronic hypertension with superimposed PE, are globally challenging to maternal and child health [1]. Among them, organ or system impairment caused by PE or eclampsia is the main cause of perinatal death and morbidity [2]. Importantly, women diagnosed with PE are at an increased risk of cardiovascular disease and diabetes in the future [2, 3].

In pathophysiology, PE is closely related to placental insufficiency, which is believed to occur before maternal organ or system impairment [4]. Incomplete maternal spiral vessel remodeling in PE leads to high resistance and low capacity vessels, resulting in reduced placental perfusion [5, 6]. However, transient gestational hypertension and white-coat hypertension are common during pregnancy [7], especially at term and during delivery. Predicting which women with HDP will develop PE is limited owing to the lack of effective imaging techniques for investigating placental microvascular characterization, and the evaluation of the resistance index (RI) of placental villous arterioles is limited [8, 9].

In contrast to traditional color Doppler, which is difficult to display low-speed blood flow in the placental villi, high resolution flow (HR-Flow), which focuses on digital flow detection and spatiotemporal summation, while collecting different ultrasound angles, improving flow representation and reducing artifacts [10, 11], is a new type of ultrasonic technique that can be used to detect low-speed blood flow. We hypothesized that HR-Flow could improve placental assessment.

The second villi remained stable from the 20th week of pregnancy, whereas the third villi reached a maximum in late pregnancy [12]. Therefore, we chose to observe the placenta of pregnant women from 28 weeks of gestation and confirmed the ability of HR-Flow to visualize the placenta. This study aimed to determine whether HR-Flow has advantages in placental hemodynamic assessment and PE prediction.

Materials and methods

This prospective study included 103 singleton pregnancies admitted to our hospital with final follow-up results between September 2021 and December 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) singleton pregnancy with a gestational age between 28 and 40 weeks; (2) planned delivery at our hospital; and (3) willingness to undergo placental HR Flow assessment. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) multiple pregnancy; (2) gestational age < 28 weeks; (3) lost to follow-up, (4) placenta accreta or placenta previa; and (5) fetal anomalies. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. This study was approved by the ethics board of the Affiliated Yixing Hospital of Jiangsu University (lunshen 2021 Technical 008) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Ultrasound scans

Mindary Resona 8 S or Resona 9R, 1–5 MHz convex probes were used in this study. Ultrasound evaluations were performed with the patients in the supine decubitus position.

Fetal growth parameters, including biparietal diameter, head circumference, abdominal circumference and femur length, amniotic fluid and umbilical cord were evaluated during routine ultrasound examination. Then the placenta was depicted using HR-Flow from the chorion to the basal plate. Each placenta was evaluated using constant parameters, including the size of the Doppler sampling window, color gain setting (65–75), sample gate (2–2.5 mm), dynamic range (5–7 cm/s), and Doppler angle of insonation (0–60º) to obtain more than three repeated similar continuous waveforms.

Referring to the ontogenesis of villi in the human placenta [13], villous arborescence include the truncus villi, ramus villi, ramulus villi, intermediate villi and terminal villi in third trimester. In our study, we consider that on the placental surface, the uteroplacental artery as the continuation of the umbilical artery, further branches to the truncus villi, then entering the placenta continues as the ramus villi, and the branches of the ramus villi, i.e. ramulus villi, are the second villi in the placenta. The RI of the second placental villus was documented in at least three regions of the placenta, and the mean value was calculated for each participant. Figure 1B shows an example of the images obtained using HR-Flow.

(1 A) The schematic diagram of the villous tree-like structure in the placenta. In this study, we consider the ramulus villi as the second villi in the placenta; (1B) The image of placenta obtained by HR-Flow and the measurement RI of the placental second villi

When the ramus villus, ramulus villus, and mature intermediate were visible, regardless of the terminal villi, we considered that the villous tree-like structure in the placenta was displayable (Fig. 1A). If only the ramus and ramulus villus (or not) were visible, we considered that the villous tree-like structure in the placenta was undisplayable.

The placenta was divided into five regions: 1 (middle region), 2 (left region), 3 (right region), 4 (upper region), and 5 (lower region). When ≥ 4 regions could show the villous tree-like structure, we considered placental perfusion to be good; when ≥ 4 regions could not show the villous tree-like structure, we considered placental perfusion to be poor. If two or three regions could show the villous tree-like structure in the placenta, the patient was excluded (Fig. 2).

The schematic diagram of the placental division.When ≥ 4 regions could show the villous tree-like structure, placental perfusion considered to be good; when ≥ 4 regions could not show the villous tree-like structure, placental perfusion considered to be poor

In this study, all ultrasound examinations were performed by a senior ultrasound doctor with over ten years of experience. The images were jointly reviewed by two ultrasound physicians who were blinded to patient information. When there was a disagreement, they referred to the opinions of the operator and reached a consensus through negotiation. If a pregnant woman had more than one examination result, the last placental HR-Flow examination result before delivery was analyzed. Randomly select 10 patients with one week interval examination data in the same region of the placenta from the final control group to analyze the intra-observer variability, and two image reviewers determined whether the tree-like structure could be displayed in 30 images that were randomly selected by the operator to analyze the inter-observer variability.

Patient grouping

After delivery, according to the participants’ clinical manifestations, they were divided into control group with normal blood pressure, hypertension group (including GH, chronic hypertension during pregnancy) and PE group (including preeclampsia, eclampsia, chronic hypertension with superimposed PE).

According to the guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy by the Chinese Medical Association [1], the classification of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy is as follows: (1) GH: primary hypertension with gestational age ≥ 20 weeks, no proteinuria or other features suggestive of PE. (2) Chronic hypertension: hypertension pre-pregnancy or gestational age < 20 weeks. (3) Preeclampsia-eclampsia: PE is defined as GH accompanied by ≥ 1 new organ or uteroplacental dysfunction, including proteinuria, and other maternal end organ dysfunction, when new onset tonic-clonic in a pregnant or postpartum patient whether or not on the basis of PE in the absence of other causative etiologies considered as eclampsia. (4) Chronic hypertension with superimposed PE: Chronic hypertension accompanied by new proteinuria and/or organ or uteroplacental dysfunction.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 19 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics are expressed as the mean and standard deviation for numerical variables (‾x ± s). Analysis of variance and the least significant difference method were used to test inter-group differences. Count data are represented as an example (%), and the differences between groups were tested using the chi-square (Χ2) test. Correlation analysis between the RI of placental second villi and severity of HDP and between RI and gestational age was conducted using Spearman correlation analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to estimate the predicted RI value of placental second villi for hypertension with organ or system impairment using the area under the binomial ROC curve (AUC). The intra-observer variability analysis was calculated by the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, < 0.50 poor, 0.50–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good, and > 0.90 excellent) and 95% confidence interval (CI). The inter-observer variability analysis was calculated using the Kappa test (κ, < 0.40 poor, 0.40–0.6 moderate, 0.60–0.80 good, and > 0.80 excellent). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

During the study period, 107 pregnant women were expected to be included in the statistics after comparing the inclusion and exclusion criteria, three patients were transferred before delivery, and one patient was only able to identify the ramous villi in two of the five regions of the placenta at the last ultrasound examination, thus 103 were included in this analysis. There were 78 women in the control group, 11 in the hypertension group (9 with GH, and 2 with chronic hypertension during pregnancy), and 14 in the PE group (10 with PE, 1 with eclampsia, and 3 with chronic hypertension with superimposed PE).

The measurement of RI of placental second villi showed excellent reliability in the third trimester (ICC = 0.904, 95% CI: 0.770–0.960). The inter-observer study showed good reproducibility in judging the display of the villous tree-like structure between the two ultrasound imaging reviewers (Kappa = 0.769, 95% CI: 0.561–0.976).

The clinical information of the study population

In our study, the only significant difference was that the average age of the PE group was higher than that of the control group (P = 0.023); however, there was no statistically significant difference compared with the hypertension group (P > 0.05). There were no statistically significant differences in the other indicators among the three groups (all P > 0.05) (Table 1).

The analysis of the RI of placental second villi among three groups of patients

In the control group of 78 cases, RI showed a slight downward trend with increasing gestational age, but the difference was not statistically significant (the Pearson correlation coefficient was − 0.191, P = 0.095). The RI in the middle (n = 62), left (n = 50), right (n = 51), upper (n = 45) and lower (n = 39) region were 0.45 ± 0.07, 0.47 ± 0.08, 0.44 ± 0.09, 0.45 ± 0.07, 0.43 ± 0.06, respectively, with a total 247 of measurements, and there were no statistically significant differences among the different regions of the placenta (P = 0.170).

The results of the determination of the RI values of second placental villi by HR-Flow were shown in Fig. 3. The mean RI values were 0.42 ± 0.10 in control group, 0.47 ± 0.07 in hypertension group, 0.49 ± 0.06 in PE group, respectively, showing a positive correlation with the severity of HDP (r=0.325, P < 0.001). There was a statistically significant difference in RI among the three groups (P = 0.017), the RI in the PE group was higher than that in the control group (P = 0.012), and the RI in the hypertension group was in between, but there was no statistically significant difference compared with the two groups (P = 0.087, 0.642). Due to the small sample size, no stratified analysis was conducted on the subgroups.

Discussion

In clinical practice, the presentation of HDP is heterogeneous, the blood pressure is highly variable, and it is difficult to predict which women will develop PE based solely on clinical characteristics. However, it is very important to distinguish PE from HDP because PE can lead to severe, life threatening outcomes, such as cerebral hemorrhage, pulmonary edema, and eclampsia within days, and the only treatment is early delivery relies on early diagnosis [14–16]. Unfortunately, the diagnostic accuracy of urine protein measurement and routine fetal ultrasonography is poor [17, 18], and many experts are exploring effective predictive indicators for PE.

In recent years, with the advancement of color Doppler applications, there have been increasing reports exploring placental function through research on placental blood flow, such as superb microvascular imaging (SMI) [19], microvascular flow (MV-Flow) [20], and three-dimensional ultrasound [21]. However, data on the prediction of placental insufficiency using HR-Flow and RI of the second placental villi are limited.

Our study found that in the third trimester, HR-Flow was good for visualizing low-speed blood flow in the villi which are placental functional units [22]. Using HR-Flow to observe the display of chorionic villi in the placenta, the perfusion status of the placenta can be determined, which may be a possible qualitative and quantitative evaluation of placental vascularization in the third trimester. In women with PE, the placenta has a higher second villous RI and a higher rate of poor placental perfusion. This may be related to placental infarction caused by PE, Furuya N reported that there was avascular villi in placental infarction by using SMI, which was expressed as absent villous blood trees [19].

There was no statistically significant difference in the blood flow perfusion of the placental villi between pregnant women with hypertension and those with normal blood pressure. However, there was an increase in the mean RI of the second villi, indicating that the placental blood vessels had changed in hypertensive pregnant women, but the degree of damage was relatively mild, and the placenta still had a compensatory function. As the condition worsens, further increases in RI and irreversible changes occur in the placental blood vessels, leading to further spasms, narrowing of the lumen, infarction, and necrosis [23], resulting in insufficient blood supply to the placenta. Dysfunction of various cytokines [24], pathways [25], or enzymes [26] in the placenta can cause or exacerbate maternal and fetal damage.

Currently, placental growth factor (PlGF) is one of the well studied and widely used factors, and its levels decrease in women with PE. The sensitivity of PlGF in predicting early and late preeclampsia in early pregnancy is 55% and 33%, respectively [27]. It has the best predictive effect on early preeclampsia in the general population during mid-term pregnancy testing [28]. However, there are wide differences in the literature regarding its clinical practicality, particularly in late pregnancy.

Another widely studied predictor of preeclampsia is the pulsatility index (PI) of maternal uterine artery Doppler ultrasonography, which usually should be measured by abdominal ultrasound examination at 11–13+6 weeks of pregnancy, and the sensitivity of uterine artery PI elevation (> 90th percentile) in predicting preeclampsia is 26.4% ~ 47.8% as reported [29].

Both PlGF and uterine artery PI mainly focused on first and second trimester detection for predicting preeclampsia, but our research was conducted during the third trimester with a moderate diagnostic value. This may be an advantage of our research, because PE can occur at any time during pregnancy (prenatal, intrapartum, postpartum).

In addition, in this study, only five women with abnormal umbilical artery Doppler findings were scattered in the PE and hypertension groups, perhaps confirming that changes in placental blood flow in pregnant women with HDP occur earlier than those in the umbilical artery. There are also several PE patients with normal uterine arteries, which may indirectly confirm the complexity of the background of HDP and maternal fetal damage. Perhaps for PE, we should also pay attention to the temporal correlation between pre-pregnancy, pregnancy, and post-pregnancy, as well as the overall correlation between mother, placenta, and fetus.

In this study, all pregnant women were third trimester pregnant women with mature placental villi. All examinations were performed by the same doctor, and multiple measurements were performed to determine the average value. The results were relatively stable and accurate. However, there were also some limitations to this study. First, there may be a selection bias because patients with two or three regions displaying villous tree-like structure were excluded. Second, the number of HDP and PE cases was relatively small, making comparisons between subgroups impossible, such as the gestational age of PE onset, PE cases whether or not complicated by fetal growth restriction, and the results may be unstable, warranting further investigation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, using HR-Flow will improve the ability of ultrasonography to display the villous tree-like structure in the placenta, and in patients with PE, there is reduced placental perfusion and a high resistance state of villous blood vessels. Consequently, HR-Flow has potential value in predicting PE, which is worthy of further study.
