The association of malnutrition with clinical and financial outcomes of traumatic injuries in older adults: A national retrospective analysis
Authors: Troy N. Coaston, Konmal Ali, Amulya Vadlakonda, Deep J. Mehta, Sara Sakowitz, Dariush Yalzadeh, Areti Tillou, Peyman Benharash
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalnutrition is an often underrecognized condition among older adults and carries significant relevance among patients hospitalized with traumatic injuries. The present study aimed to evaluate the association of malnutrition with clinical and financial outcomes among older adult patients admitted with external trauma.Study designThis was a retrospective cohort study of the 2016–2021 National Inpatient Sample including older adults (≥65 years) hospitalized with traumatic injuries. Patients were stratified by nutritional status (Malnourished and Non-Malnourished). Risk-adjusted logistic and linear regression models were constructed to evaluate the association of malnutrition with outcomes including inpatient mortality, clinical complications, and hospitalization costs.ResultsOf 6,587,907 older adults admitted with traumatic injuries, 7.5 % had malnutrition. The prevalence of malnutrition rose from 5.8 % to 8.6 % over the study period (nptrend<0.001). Patients with malnutrition were more commonly of the lowest income quartile (25.5 vs 24.5 %), non-White (19.4 vs 16.9 %), and male (41.3 vs 39.1 %, all p < 0.001). Following risk-adjustment, malnutrition was linked with increased odds of inpatient mortality (Adjusted Odds Ratio [AOR] 1.92, 95 % Confidence Interval [CI] 1.86–1.98) and infectious complications (AOR 2.30, 95 % CI 2.25–2.35) as well as greater inpatient costs (β + $7400, 95 % CI $7100-7600).ConclusionMalnutrition among older adults is associated with poorer clinical outcomes and increased financial burden. Rising prevalence and significant disparities underscore the need for increased screening and culturally relevant nutritional interventions to promote quality, equity, and sustainability in trauma care.

Introduction

By 2050, patients ≥65 years of age are projected to comprise nearly 40 % of all trauma-related hospitalizations [1,2]. While older patients often present with trauma severity comparable to that of younger individuals, they experience twice the risk of mortality as well as greater odds of other postoperative complications [[3], [4], [5]]. Such observations have previously been attributed to a myriad senescent conditions including frailty which is thought to reduce resilience to severe physiologic derangement [6].

Age-related physiological changes notwithstanding, nutritional status remains a critical determinant of outcomes among trauma patients, with nearly 50 % of hospitalized older adults thought to be at risk for malnutrition [[7], [8], [9]]. The hypermetabolism and catabolic stress response following trauma may further exacerbate malnutrition and its sequelae [9]. Indeed, previous studies have established profound associations between poor nutritional status among older trauma patients and increased risk of mortality, infectious complications, and cognitive impairment [2,8,9]. Moreover, serum albumin, an established biomarker of nutrition, has been recognized as a strong predictor of postoperative morbidity across surgical specialties for decades, underscoring its clinical utility in preoperative risk stratification [10]. Despite these implications, national-level characterization of the link between malnutrition and outcomes among older adults hospitalized with traumatic injuries remains sparse, with existing studies relying on single-institution or health system data [2,11,12].

The present study characterized the association of malnutrition with clinical and financial outcomes of hospitalization for trauma among older adults. We hypothesized malnourished status to be independently linked with increased risk-adjusted odds of mortality and complications as well as higher expenditure.

Methods

This was a retrospective cohort study of the 2016–2021 National Inpatient Sample (NIS). Maintained as part of the Healthcare Utilization Project, NIS is the largest publicly available all-payer inpatient repository in the U.S. [13]. Through survey-weighting methodology, the NIS provides accurate estimates for approximately 97 % of hospitalizations, annually. All nonelective older adult (≥65 years) hospitalizations with external traumatic injuries were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes, as previously described [14]. Records missing key data including sex, hospitalization costs, or mechanism of injury, were removed from analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1CONSORT Diagram.Fig. 1

CONSORT Diagram.

Patient (age, sex, race, income, primary payer) and facility (bed size, teaching status, region) characteristics are reported in accordance with the NIS data dictionary [13]. Importantly, income reflects the median household income of the patient's community, as defined by their ZIP code. The presence of malnutrition status was ascertained using previously reported ICD-10 codes while Subjects were categorized into Malnutrition and Non-Malnutrition cohorts, accordingly [15]. The primary mechanism of injury (fall, motor-related, penetrating, and other blunt mechanism) was identified using ICD-10 codes as reported elsewhere [14]. Frailty was defined using the 11-item Modified Frailty Index (mFI-11), as described by Subramaniam and colleagues [16]. Importantly, the MFI differs from many other indices as it does not include malnutrition as a covariate. The burden of chronic conditions was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index, which similarly does not include malnutrition in its calculation [17]. Injury severity was quantified using the Injury Severity Score (ISS), as reported by Clark et al. [18]. Clinical complications, identified through ICD-10 codes were categorized as cardiac (arrest, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, tamponade, myocardial infarction), infectious, respiratory (pneumonia, pneumothorax, acute respiratory distress, respiratory failure, prolonged ventilation, bronchopleural fistula), and thromboembolic (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism) [19]. Non-home discharge was defined as disposition to an acute, intermediate care, or nursing facility. Inpatient costs were calculated by applying hospital-specific cost-to-charge ratios to overall charges followed by inflation adjustment to the 2021 Personal Health Index [20].

The primary outcome was inpatient mortality. We further examined factors associated with malnutrition, clinical complications, and makers of resource utilization which included inpatient costs, non-home discharge, inpatient length of stay (LOS).

Patient and hospital factors are reported as proportions, means with standard deviations (SD), or medians with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. The significance of between-group differences was determined using the Pearson's Χ2 or Wilcoxon Rank Sum tests for categorical and continuous covariates, respectively. Cuzick's nonparametric test was used to ascertain the significance of temporal trends [21]. To identify independent association of malnourished status with study endpoints, multivariable logistic and linear models were constructed. Elastic net regularization, which aims to reduce collinearity and enhance generalizability, was used to guide variable selection in risk-adjusted models [22]. Goodness-of-fit was assessed using receiver-operating characteristics (C-statistic) for logistic or the coefficient of determination (R2) for linear models. Regression estimates are reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) or β coefficients (β), both with 95 % confidence intervals (CI), as appropriate.

Due to its deidentified nature, this study was deemed exempt from full review by the University of California, Los Angeles, Institutional Review Board. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Of an estimated 6,587,907 older adults admitted with traumatic injuries, 7.5 % comprised the Malnutrition cohort. Over the study period, the prevalence of malnutrition among older injured adults increased significantly, rising from 5.8 % in 2016 to 8.6 % in 2021 (nptrend<0.001; Fig. 2). Compared to others, Malnutrition was less commonly female (58.7 vs 60.9 %, p < 0.001), of White race (80.6 % vs 83.1, p < 0.001), and privately insured (5.6 vs 7.2 %, p < 0.001). Conversely, those with malnutrition were more frequently of the lowest income quartile (25.5 vs 24.5 %, p < 0.001). Furthermore, Malnutrition had a greater burden of comorbidities (Charlson: 2 [1–3] vs 2 [1–4], p < 0.001) and were found to have a marginally higher mFI-11 (2.8 ± 1.4 vs 2.7 ± 1.4, p < 0.001), compared to others. After multivariable adjustment for factors including frailty, malnutrition remained independently associated with increased odds of inpatient mortality (AOR 1.92, 95 % CI 1.86–1.98; model c-statistic = 0.72). Additionally, malnutrition increased the odds of infectious (AOR 2.30, 95 % CI 2.25–2.35), respiratory (AOR 1.87, 95 % CI 1.83–1.91), and cardiac complications (AOR 1.33, 95 % CI 1.29–1.36; Fig. 4). Notably, malnourished status was associated with greater resource utilization including hospitalization costs (β + $7400, 95 % CI $7100-7600), LOS (β +3.27 days, 95 % CI 3.19–3.35), and odds of non-home discharge (AOR 1.60, 95 % CI 1.57–1.63; Table 2). A subgroup analysis among those without fall as the primary mechanism of injury yielded a similar pattern of results (Supplemental Table 1).

Discussion

With the aging U.S. population, surgeons and emergency care providers are increasingly faced with managing older trauma patients who face distinct physiologic challenges. Recent literature has prioritized identifying predictors of poor outcomes and increased costs in this complex population. In this national analysis, we found a rising prevalence of malnutrition in older trauma patients over the six-year study period. Malnutrition was disproportionately more common among individuals of lower income, non-White race, and with Medicaid coverage. Finally, malnutrition was linked with substantially greater resource use. Several of these findings warrant further discussion.

Malnutrition was independently associated with adverse outcomes, even after adjusting for frailty. While this association aligns with previous smaller cohort studies [2,23], the present works provides a more robust, contemporary, and generalizable assessment. The mechanisms underlying this association are likely multifactorial. Malnutrition is known to impair wound healing, increase infection risk, and elevate post-discharge needs [2,23,24]. Moreover, poor nutrition contributes to reduced mobility, greater dependance, and cognitive decline, factors that interact synergistically to undermine rehabilitation and recovery [8]. In addition, the rising prevalence of malnutrition observed over the study period may reflect improved recognition, demographic shifts, or other unmeasured factors, underscoring its growing importance as a clinical and public health concern. Our findings add to the growing body of literature highlighting the critical role of malnutrition in risk stratification among older adults with traumatic injuries.

We noted significant disparities in nutritional status by race and income, echoing prior work regarding individual and structural-level determinants of malnutrition [25,26]. These findings align with previous studies that report an inverse relationship between neighborhood income and food desert severity, which has been associated with increased mortality, higher obesity rates, and a greater prevalence of cardiovascular disease [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. While policy interventions such as food delivery services and incentivizing grocers in underserved areas have shown mixed success, their impact may depend on cultural relevance [[31], [32], [33]]. In qualitative studies, patients expressed frustration with food assistance programs, stating that the initiatives failed to reflect cultural preferences [34]. In contrast, community-engaged, culturally grounded interventions have constantly improved uptake and efficiency [[35], [36], [37]]. Our findings highlight the ongoing need for nutritional equity interventions that are both structurally sound and socially informed.

In the present study, we noted malnutrition to be linked with increased inpatient costs, longer hospitalizations, and higher rates of nonhome discharge. Recent estimates suggest $30 billion in expenditures attributable to trauma care in older adults [38]. With malnutrition affecting 7–22 % of this population, and nearly half being at risk, nutritional status is a likely underscreened yet high-yield target for cost mitigation [2,8,24]. Sufficient evidence supports the cost-effectiveness of nutritional interventions. Wang et al. reported oral supplementation for malnourished hospitalized older adults to reduce per-patient costs by over $3500 annually [39]. Similarly, nutritional therapy has been demonstrated to hasten patient recovery in general. Indeed, Williams and colleagues found that early nutritional supplementation for older adults with hip or femur fractures significantly shortened LOS without increasing costs [40]. Taken together, these findings make a compelling case for routine malnutrition screening and timely nutritional support as strategies to improve patient outcomes and enhance value.

The present study has several important limitations. First, as an administrative database, the NIS is subject to variation in coding practices between centers. Additionally, there is limited granularity, with no data recorded for imaging findings or laboratory values, including albumin and pre-albumin levels. Importantly, in the NIS, we are unable to determine the timing of diagnosis, as such we cannot confirm definitively that malnutrition did not develop during hospitalization. However, in a supplemental analysis of the 2022 California State Inpatient Database, which includes data regarding the presence of a diagnosis upon admission [41], we found that 96.7 % of patients ≥65 years with malnutrition carried that diagnosis at admission. Given that the California State Inpatient Database, like the NIS, is maintained by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, we are confident that most individuals identified as having malnutrition in this study did not develop the condition during hospitalization. Furthermore, although some baseline characteristics (e.g., income quartile, Charlson score, mFI-11) were statistically different between groups, these absolute differences were small and of limited clinical significance. Lastly, malnutrition may be under-coded in this dataset, and patients identified here are likely to represent those with more severe nutritional deficiency. Prior studies have shown wide variability in reported prevalence, making it difficult to estimate the extent of under-capture [2,8,24].

In summary, this study provides a contemporary national perspective on the association of malnutrition with clinical and financial outcomes among older adults with traumatic injuries. Malnutrition was independently associated with disproportionate morbidity, mortality, and financial burden. Given the aging demographic and rising cost of trauma care, understanding and addressing nutritional deficits is crucial. Our findings reinforce the need for early identification and intervention among this vulnerable population. Targeted strategies may improve recovery trajectories while promoting equity and sustainability in trauma care delivery.
