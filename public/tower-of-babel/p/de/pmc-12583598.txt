Efficacy of external physical vibration lithecbole combined with tamsulosin in upper ureteric stones after ESWL: a prospective randomized clinical trial
Authors: He-wei Xu, Xiao Liang, Yu-hao Wang, Ya-xiong Xu, Hui-qing Li, Yun-Peng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of combining external physical vibration lithecbole (EPVL) with tamsulosin to enhance the clearance of upper ureteric stones (10–20 mm) after extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL). In a prospective, randomized clinical trial, patients with upper ureteric stones were allocated to a control group receiving ESWL followed by daily tamsulosin (0.4 mg) or a treatment group receiving both ESWL and EPVL in addition to tamsulosin. The primary endpoint, the stone-free rate (SFR), was assessed at 1, 2, and 4 weeks post-ESWL. Secondary endpoints included complication rates, with radiologic evaluations conducted at predetermined intervals to monitor stone clearance. In this randomized trial involving 211 participants, 106 received the intervention, while 105 served as controls. EPVL significantly enhanced stone fragment clearance. At week one, the SFR between the groups was similar (51.9% vs. 45.4%, P = 0.912). However, at weeks two and four, the treatment group demonstrated significantly higher SFR of 81.1% and 90.6%, respectively, compared to 64.8% and 75.2% in the control group (P = 0.020 and P = 0.011). No significant differences were observed in complication rates (P > 0.05). The treatment group underwent an average of 6.2 EPVL sessions, with a mean stone expulsion time of 20.3 ± 4.7 days. The combination of EPVL and tamsulosin following ESWL significantly increased the SFR while maintaining a favorable safety profile, suggesting that this integrated approach could offer a more effective treatment pathway for stone expulsion. Further studies in larger, multi-center cohorts are needed for validation.

Introduction

Urolithiasis, also referred to as urinary stone disease, constitutes a significant global health issue, with incidence rates ranging from 5% to 15%1– 2. This condition is notably characterized by a high recurrence rate, with approximately 50% to 75% of patients experiencing recurrent stone formation after initial episodes3– 4. Since the 1980s, extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL) has become a leading non-invasive approach for stone treatment, valued for its cost-effectiveness and superior patient tolerance compared to endourological alternatives5– 6. Despite its widespread use, ESWL demonstrates a considerable variation in stone-free rates (SFR), which range from 23.1% to 91.5%7. This variability is primarily attributed to various factors, including stone size, anatomical location, composition, and renal function8.

Residual stone fragments following ESWL continue to present a major clinical challenge, given their link to recurrent lithiasis, urinary tract infections, and the gradual decline of renal function. While adjunctive techniques, such as mechanical percussion, diuresis, and inversion (PDI)9, have been employed to improve fragment clearance, their results often lack consistency and clinical efficacy. The external physical vibration lithecbole (EPVL) device10, designed to promote fragment expulsion, has shown promising enhancements in SFR, especially when combined with ESWL.

Combined with EPVL, tamsulosin—an α1-adrenergic receptor antagonist—has been explored for its potential to enhance stone clearance11. By relaxing ureteral smooth muscle, tamsulosin reduces ureteral resistance, thereby promoting the passage of stone fragments12– 13. Numerous studies, including large-scale randomized controlled trials, have shown that the combination of tamsulosin and EPVL offers substantial clinical benefits in the management of ureteral stones, particularly those located in the distal ureter. In a randomized controlled trial by Wu et al., patients with distal ureteral calculi were allocated to three groups: EPVL plus tamsulosin, tamsulosin alone, and EPVL alone14. The combination therapy group achieved a significantly higher rate of detectable fragment expulsion at 48 h (60.1%) and a SFR of 91.1% at 7 days, compared to an approximately 50% SFR in both control groups. While the final SFR at two weeks was similar across all groups (exceeding 93%), combination therapy resulted in more rapid stone expulsion (within one week), thereby accelerating symptom relief and improving patient comfort. However, despite these findings supporting the efficacy of combined EPVL and tamsulosin for distal ureteral stones, the additional benefit of this approach following ESWL, particularly for upper ureteric stones, remains unclear and requires further investigation.

This prospective, randomized clinical trial aimed to assess the therapeutic efficacy and safety of EPVL combined with tamsulosin in patients with upper ureteric stones following ESWL. By concentrating on this specific patient group, the study seeks to optimize urolithiasis management strategies, addressing a relatively underexplored area in the literature. The results are anticipated to enhance clinical protocols and inform the development of evidence-based guidelines for the treatment of urinary stones.

Materials and methods

Trial design

This prospective, controlled, single-center study was conducted between January 1, 2024, and December 31, 2024, and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200056402). A cohort of 269 patients diagnosed with upper ureteric stones was enrolled (Fig. 1). Preoperative evaluation included demographic and clinical data such as age, BMI, sex, comorbidities, stone size and location, and prior ESWL exposure. The studies were conducted in accordance with the local legislation and institutional requirements.Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement.

Participants

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: individuals aged 18 to 65 years; radiologically confirmed upper ureteric stone with a diameter of 10–20 mm; absence of urinary obstruction, infection, or structural abnormalities; preserved renal function; and detection of the stone via KUB radiography.

The exclusion criteria included the presence of urinary tract infection, uncontrolled or severe hypertension, a history of ESWL, poorly controlled diabetes, pregnancy, menstruation, or any known coagulation disorder.

Study procedure and device mechanism

The EPVL (Friend, Kangbaijia Technology Instrument Co., Ltd, Zhengzhou, China) incorporates advanced mechanical features to enhance ureteric stone clearance. The system consists of a treatment bed with an approximately 26° inclined, rotatable surface and two oscillators (Fig. 2). Its operation is based on a multi-directional harmonic vibration mechanism, which mobilizes the stone. A sub-oscillator integrated into the bed generates horizontal harmonic oscillations, producing lateral acceleration at a frequency of 1300–1900 impulses per minute, a power output of 200 W, and a 5 mm amplitude. This lateral vibration promotes the detachment of stones. Concurrently, the hand-held primary oscillator delivers axial impulses to facilitate the movement of stones from the renal pelvis, operating at 2800–3500 impulses per minute, with 40 W power and a 5 mm amplitude.

The positioning and orientation of the stones were dynamically adjusted through EPVL to optimize ureteral clearance. Real-time ultrasound monitoring was employed throughout the procedure to track stone displacement and ensure accurate targeting for efficient extraction.

All patients underwent a single ESWL session utilizing a Dornier Medical Systems device (Weßling, Germany), with energy settings ranging from 12 to 14 kV, a shock frequency of 60–90 shocks per minute, and a total of 2000–2500 shocks. EPVL therapy commenced on the third day following ESWL, with subsequent sessions scheduled at three- to four-day intervals. Post-procedural care involved recommendations for a daily fluid intake of approximately 2000 ml and the avoidance of strenuous physical activity. Follow-up imaging, including plain abdominal radiography, ultrasound, and, when indicated, non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT), was performed at 1, 2, and 4 weeks after the initial ESWL session (Fig. 3).

In the treatment group, 106 patients received EPVL combined with tamsulosin following ESWL. Patients were positioned supine with the dorsum elevated, and the primary oscillator was applied to the ipsilateral ureter after the ingestion of 1500–2000 ml of fluid. The oscillation frequency was adjusted according to individual tolerance, ranging from 2800 to 3500 vibrations per minute. Each EPVL session lasted 12–18 min, targeting the mobilization of residual fragments from the upper to the distal ureter. Treatments were administered at three to four-day intervals, with the total treatment duration not exceeding four weeks. Real-time ultrasound guidance facilitated precise tracking of stone location and dynamic adjustment of the treatment bed’s tilt angle to optimize fragment expulsion.

In the control group, 105 patients underwent ESWL followed by daily administration of 0.4 mg tamsulosin. Patients were advised to exceed 2000 ml of fluid intake per day and use a filtration device for fragment collection. Retrieved stones were then analyzed. This protocol ensured consistency across both groups while incorporating an additional mechanical intervention in the treatment group to enhance post-ESWL clearance efficiency.

Study outcomes

The primary endpoint was the SFR at weeks 1, 2, and 4, with complications associated with EPVL serving as secondary outcomes. SFR was defined as either complete stone clearance or the presence of residual fragments smaller than 2 mm in diameter15. Postoperative complications were classified according to the Modified Clavien Classification, which categorizes adverse events based on clinical severity and required interventions. Grade I complications included symptoms such as local pain, renal colic, hematuria, fever, dysuria, and steinstrasse, all of which were managed conservatively with analgesics and hydration. Grade II included steinstrasse treated with alpha-blockers, febrile episodes or urinary tract infections requiring antibiotics, renal colic alleviated by alpha-blockers, and renal hematomas requiring transfusion. Grade IIIa involved steinstrasse requiring percutaneous nephrostomy or repeated SWL, acute urinary retention managed with Foley catheter placement, and renal abscess or urinoma necessitating aspiration under local anesthesia. Grade IIIb included steinstrasse treated via ureteroscopy, open surgery, or J-J stent placement; renal hematomas or persistent hematuria addressed by angioembolization; and urinoma requiring J-J stenting. Grade IV included severe complications such as septic shock and injury to adjacent organs, including the pancreas, liver, spleen, bowel, or lung. Grade V was assigned to fatal outcomes.

Participants underwent one to eight EPVL sessions, with treatment considered unsuccessful if residual stone fragments remained after the eighth session. A structured in-person assessment protocol was implemented at 1, 2, and 4 weeks post-ESWL to evaluate the SFR and document complications. Each visit included physical examination and imaging (KUB, ultrasound, or NCCT as indicated) to detect residual stones. SFR was defined as the absence of fragments or the presence of fragments smaller than 2 mm. Complications were identified via patient interviews and clinical examination, and classified using the Modified Clavien system. All data were recorded on standardized forms to ensure uniformity in data collection. In cases where complete stone clearance was not achieved by the end of follow-up, further management via ESWL or surgical intervention was recommended.

Sample size and statistical analysis

Sample size estimation was based on previously reported SFR following ESWL of approximately 75% (0.75), with an anticipated increase to 90% (0.90) following adjuvant EPVL therapy16,17, employing a one-sided test with α = 0.05 and β = 0.20 (power = 80%). Under these conditions, 97 participants per group were required; to mitigate potential attrition during follow-up, the sample size was adjusted to 110 per group.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v22.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and stone-related variables. Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Categorical variables (such as sex, hydronephrosis grade, stone HU, stone location, SFR, and complication rates) were compared via the chi-square test. Continuous variables (including age, BMI, and stone size) were analyzed using Student’s t-test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Randomization

Eligible patients meeting all inclusion and exclusion criteria, and providing informed consent, were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the treatment group (EPVL plus tamsulosin) or the control group (tamsulosin alone). Randomization was performed through a computer-generated sequence, with group assignments concealed in sealed envelopes to ensure allocation concealment and preserve blinding integrity.

Results

A total of 49 patients were excluded based on predefined criteria: 9 opted out due to concerns over personal data confidentiality, 13 surpassed the age limit of 65, 10 had stones smaller than 10 mm, 5 had stones larger than 20 mm, and 12 had unresolved urinary tract infections. The remaining 211 eligible patients were randomized into the treatment group (n = 106) and the control group (n = 105). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 48.4 ± 6.5 years in the treatment group and 49.1 ± 6.2 years in the control group (P = 0.347). All participants underwent a single ESWL session, while the treatment group received an additional mean of 6.2 EPVL sessions. Baseline variables—such as BMI, sex, comorbid hypertension or diabetes, mean stone size, anatomical location, Hounsfield unit values, and prior ESWL history—showed no significant differences between the groups (P > 0.05 for all comparisons).

Discussion

Upper urinary tract calculi remain a significant public health concern, primarily due to their high incidence and recurrence rates18. Currently, ESWL is extensively utilized in the management of urinary tract stones, including renal and upper ureteral calculi, particularly for patients with renal stones smaller than 2 cm in diameter and without significant obstruction or active infection. Additionally, ESWL offers a safe and effective treatment option for patients with severe comorbidities who cannot undergo surgical anesthesia or invasive procedures19. Despite its efficacy, residual stone fragments often persist after ESWL, increasing the risk of infection and urinary tract obstruction. These limitations highlight the need for more efficient approaches to clear these fragments, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes and minimizing postoperative complications.

Despite advancements in ESWL technology, achieving an optimal SFR continues to be a significant challenge, necessitating the investigation of alternative therapeutic strategies. Fragment migration and variability in stone burden often lead to increased healthcare resource consumption, including unplanned emergency visits and additional interventions20. The effectiveness of MET, involving agents such as calcium channel blockers and α1-adrenergic receptor antagonists, remains suboptimal21. A meta-analysis of 55 randomized trials evaluating α-blockers revealed considerable outcome variability, primarily determined by stone size22. While minimal therapeutic improvements are seen in patients with smaller ureteral stones, those with larger stones benefit significantly. Specifically, tamsulosin has been linked to faster stone expulsion, reduced pain frequency, lower surgical intervention rates, and decreased hospitalization durations in this group. Therefore, tamsulosin is strongly recommended to enhance clinical outcomes in patients with larger ureteral stones23.

Consequently, increasing focus has been placed on adjunctive strategies, such as PDI therapy, which utilizes gravitational forces and mechanical vibrations to aid in fragment expulsion. Ongoing investigations into emerging modalities, including ultrasound-assisted systems and experimental magnetic devices, are in their early stages, with limited clinical validation and uncertain therapeutic efficacy. Among recent innovations, EPVL, developed in China, has attracted attention. Its primary mechanism involves the application of simple harmonic motion to patients positioned on a specialized treatment bed, mobilizing residual fragments from the renal collecting system24. If fragment displacement is insufficient, a multi-axial harmonic vibration device is employed to transmit directional energy, promoting fragment dislodgment. This approach combines positional adjustments with localized mechanical stimulation of the lumbar region to enhance clearance of residual calculi from anatomically variable areas within the urinary tract.

This study sought to assess the therapeutic efficacy of combining EPVL with tamsulosin following ESWL to enhance residual stone clearance and reduce the incidence of renal colic. By promoting faster expulsion of calculi, this regimen may lead to shorter recovery times and a quicker return to normal activities. The investigation focuses on determining whether this approach accelerates residual stone elimination more effectively than standard management, thus providing a potential strategy to optimize post-ESWL outcomes.

The present study demonstrated a higher SFR in the treatment group compared to controls at one week post-ESWL (51.9% vs. 45.4%), although the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.912). This outcome may be attributed to the timing of initial EPVL administration, as prior studies indicate that day three post-procedure may represent the optimal intervention window. Statistically significant differences were observed at two and four weeks, with the treatment group achieving superior SFR (81.1% vs. 64.8%, P = 0.020; 90.6% vs. 75.2%, P = 0.011). These results suggest a sustained improvement in stone clearance over time in the intervention cohort, as outlined in Table 2. Complication rates remained similar between groups (P > 0.05), suggesting that the combination of EPVL and tamsulosin improves residual stone clearance post-ESWL without increasing the risk of adverse events. To minimize variability and enhance result accuracy, our study imposed specific restrictions on liquid intake and activity intensity. Existing literature supports that a daily water intake of 2500–3000 ml or more can increase urine output, aiding in stone elimination25. Additionally, moderate physical activity can assist in the excretion of small stones through body vibrations and gravity effects26. However, these recommendations should be individualized to prevent adverse symptoms such as renal colic, hematuria, and fever.

The results of our study are consistent with previous investigations examining the adjunctive use of EPVL following ESWL or RIRS for upper urinary tract stones. In our cohort, the combination of EPVL and tamsulosin significantly enhanced SFR at two and four weeks post-ESWL, compared to standard management, without an increase in complications. This aligns with the findings of Li et al., who reported that in obese patients with lower pole stones < 15 mm, EPVL following ESWL led to higher SFRs at 1, 2, and 4 weeks (63.2%, 84.9%, and 90.8%, respectively) compared to controls (55.1%, 70.7%, and 79.6%), with a comparable safety profile. Similarly, Wu et al. demonstrated that EPVL after RIRS significantly accelerated stone clearance, achieving SFRs of 89.7% at 5 weeks, compared to 59.3% in the control group, along with reduced rates of hematuria and urinary tract infections. Our findings also mirror those of Zhang et al., who emphasized the importance of EPVL timing, noting that earlier intervention (day 3 post-RIRS) resulted in the highest SFRs at all follow-up intervals. Moreover, studies by Tao et al. and Long et al. confirmed that EPVL promoted more rapid and complete fragment expulsion following ESWL for both lower pole and upper ureteric stones, consistently improving SFRs without a significant increase in adverse events. Notably, the early SFR at one week in our study (51.9%) was comparable to previous reports (e.g., 51.3% in Tao et al.), with more substantial differences observed at later time points, suggesting that EPVL provides a cumulative benefit over time. The safety profile in our cohort, with no significant differences in complications between groups, is consistent with prior clinical trials. Notably, this study extends previous research by incorporating tamsulosin, suggesting a potential synergistic effect when combined with EPVL.

This approach not only preserves a high safety profile and therapeutic efficacy but also improves treatment efficiency by increasing SFR and reducing procedural duration. The combined use of EPVL and tamsulosin represents an innovative and effective protocol for the outpatient management of residual calculi.

EPVL offers distinct procedural advantages. Its performance without anesthesia streamlines clinical workflows and promotes higher patient adherence, while ensuring consistent reproducibility. The non-invasive nature of the procedure avoids the need for hospitalization, promoting rapid reintegration into daily activities and expanding the therapy’s applicability to a wider patient population.

Nevertheless, potential adverse events associated with EPVL include ureteral injury and stenosis. While direct evidence specific to EPVL is limited, studies on ESWL have identified urinary tract infections, severe hydronephrosis, and repeated ESWL treatments as independent predictors of ureteral stenosis [33]. The mechanical interaction between calculi and the ureter during EPVL may heighten these risks. When combined with established high-risk factors, the likelihood of ureteral complications increases substantially. Ongoing post-treatment surveillance is essential to monitor and address these risks over time.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The sample is restricted to individuals with a normal BMI, excluding those with obesity. Additionally, the exclusion of KUB-negative stones further limits the generalizability of the findings. The single-center design and small sample size may introduce selection bias, compromising external validity. The short follow-up period, coupled with variations in vibration frequency and treatment intensity, may have influenced the SFR and hindered the assessment of long-term benefits. Diagnostic accuracy could also be affected, as not all participants underwent follow-up imaging with non-contrast CT, which may have led to an underestimation of residual fragments. Although participants were instructed to maintain a daily fluid intake of approximately 2000 ml and avoid vigorous physical activity, adherence is neither standardized nor objectively monitored, potentially contributing to outcome variability.

The primary advantage of this regimen lies in its simplicity: it is outpatient-based, eliminating the need for hospitalization or general anesthesia, thereby reducing procedural risks and enhancing patient convenience. Future research should focus on refining key treatment parameters—such as vibration frequency, session duration, and frequency of sessions— to establish the optimal protocol. Furthermore, assessing the efficacy of this approach in obese populations may contribute to the development of personalized treatment strategies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the adjunctive use of EPVL in combination with tamsulosin effectively enhances residual fragment clearance following ESWL for upper ureteric stones ranging from 1.0 to 2.0 cm, resulting in improved SFR and a lower incidence of adverse events. However, further validation through large-scale, multicenter prospective trials is required to substantiate these findings.
