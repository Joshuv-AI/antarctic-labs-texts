Optimizing intermittent preventive treatment for malaria in pregnancy: protocol for phase IV randomized controlled trial of sulphadoxine–pyrimethamine, dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine, and combination therapy in Northern Nigeria
Authors: Mansur Ramalan, Abdullahi Rabiu, Murtala Jibril, Ayyuba Rabiu, Sani Malami, Abdullahi Yaro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria during pregnancy remains an important cause of maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality in sub-Saharan Africa. Currently, intermittent preventive therapy with sulphadoxine–pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP) is the standard of care. However, increasing resistance of Plasmodium falciparum to sulphadoxine–pyrimethamine (SP) raises concerns about its effectiveness. Recently, Dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine (DP), an artemisinin-based combination therapy with prolonged post-treatment prophylactic activity, has demonstrated superior parasitological efficacy compared with SP. Despite these reductions in parasitaemia, improved pregnancy outcomes have not been consistently observed. Moreover, sulphadoxine–pyrimethamine may confer additional non-malarial benefits, including antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects. Therefore, combining SP with DP may provide additive or synergistic benefits by enhancing parasite clearance while preserving SP's ancillary advantages. This trial will evaluate the effectiveness, safety, pharmacokinetic profile, and pregnancy outcomes of IPTp-SP, IPTp-DP, and the combined IPTp-SP + DP among pregnant women in Northern Nigeria.MethodsThis study is an open-label, randomized, three-arm, parallel-group, phase IV superiority trial enrolling 1,200 HIV-negative pregnant women between 16 and 26 weeks’ gestation in Northern, Nigeria. Participants are randomized in a 1:1:1 ratio to receive monthly IPTp with SP alone, DP alone, or SP plus DP. The primary outcome is the prevalence of maternal malaria at delivery, defined as peripheral and/or placental malaria infection detected by microscopy and/or polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Secondary outcomes include maternal anaemia, placental malaria, low birth weight, preterm birth, stillbirth, neonatal morbidity, pharmacokinetic measurements of piperaquine, biomarkers of placental dysfunction, maternal immune responses, molecular markers of antimalarial resistance, and maternal and neonatal adverse events. Participants will be followed prospectively from enrolment until delivery. Data will be analysed according to the intention-to-treat principle using regression-based comparative analyses adjusted for clinically relevant covariates.DiscussionThis trial will generate robust clinical, molecular, pharmacokinetic, and immunological evidence regarding alternative IPTp strategies in an area with emerging SP resistance. By integrating parasitological, maternal, placental, and neonatal outcomes, the study seeks to determine whether DP alone or in combination with SP provides clinically meaningful advantages over the current standard of care.Trial registrationPan African Clinical Trials Registry, PACTR202406478822577 registered on 18th June 2024, http://pactr.samrc.ac.za/Researcher/TrialRegister.aspx?TrialID=28360Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09535-x.

Scientific background and rationale

Malaria remains one of the most prevalent infectious diseases worldwide, resulting in nearly one million fatalities each year, with the majority occurring in sub-Saharan Africa [1–3]. Women and particularly children under five are disproportionately affected. In 2019, approximately 229 million malaria cases were reported worldwide, with 94% occurring in Africa [3].

Malaria during pregnancy can lead to serious outcomes for both mothers and babies, such as maternal anaemia, fetal loss, premature birth, stunted growth, and low birth weight, all of which increase infant mortality risk [4]. Congenital malaria occurs in 0.3% of infants born to mothers with malaria in regions where the population has some immunity, and it affects 1–4% of infants in areas where the population lacks immunity, adding to the health challenges linked to malaria during pregnancy [4].

In areas where the transmission rates of Plasmodium falciparum are elevated, pregnant women face particular vulnerabilities, often experiencing asymptomatic infections that can lead to several adverse outcomes, including maternal and perinatal mortality [3].

The World Health Organization currently recommends intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy with sulphadoxine–pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP) for pregnant women living in areas of moderate-to-high malaria transmission [3]. While IPTp-SP has greatly reduced the adverse consequences of malaria during pregnancy over the past 20 years, rising resistance to SP, driven by mutations in the pfdhfr and pfdhps genes, poses a risk to its future effectiveness [4–7].

Dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine (DP) has become a promising option for preventing malaria during pregnancy because of its potent antimalarial activity and prolonged prophylactic effect [8]. Numerous studies indicate that IPTp-DP is more effective at reducing parasitaemia than IPTp-SP [8–11]. However, these parasitological advantages have not consistently translated into improved birth outcomes [8–11]. This discrepancy suggests that SP may provide additional non-malarial benefits, such as antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, or immunomodulatory effects [8].

A combination of IPTp-SP and DP may offer enhanced protection by combining DP's extended antimalarial prevention with the additional advantages of SP. Data supporting this approach remain scarce, especially in West Africa, where resistance trends, malaria transmission dynamics, and maternal health factors differ significantly from those in East Africa [8, 9].

Methods

Study design

This research is an open-label, randomized, parallel-group, three-arm, phase IV superiority clinical trial aimed at evaluating the effectiveness and immunological outcomes of various intermittent preventive treatment strategies for malaria during pregnancy. The strategies being compared are sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP), dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (IPTp-DP), and a combination of both sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine and dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (IPTp-SP + DP). The study will be carried out in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [12] and the International Council for Harmonisation–Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) [13], as well as following the guidelines set by the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration (NAFDAC) [14].

Study setting

The trial will be conducted at Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital (AKTH), a tertiary referral centre in Kano State, Northern Nigeria, and at an affiliated primary health care (PHC) facility in Kumbotso, where routine antenatal care services are offered to pregnant women.

Study population

The study population will consist of pregnant women attending antenatal clinics at the participating health facilities.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was determined using the World Health Organization (WHO) Manual for Surveillance of Antimalarial Drug Efficacy framework. It was based on the primary outcome, the prevalence of maternal malaria at delivery [15]. In line with WHO guidance, antimalarial preventive strategies should maintain therapeutic efficacy of ≥ 90%, corresponding to a failure rate of ≤ 10% [15].

The sample size was chosen to provide 80% power at a 5% two-sided significance level, while also providing allowance for attrition and preserving adequate power for pairwise comparisons across arms.

Based on assumptions specified in the statistical analysis plan and comparative effectiveness thresholds relevant to malaria prevention in pregnancy, the minimum required sample size was 184 participants per arm.

To improve the precision of effect estimates, allow adequately powered subgroup analyses (including gravidity and gestational age), support planned laboratory sub-studies (molecular resistance markers, immunological assays, and pharmacokinetic analyses), and to mitigate the impact of attrition and missing outcome data, the recruitment target was increased to a total of 1200 participants, with 400 participants allocated to each of the three study arms in a 1:1:1 ratio.

Recruitment

Participants will be recruited from antenatal clinics at participating primary and secondary healthcare facilities in Kano State, Nigeria. Trained research assistants and clinic staff will identify potentially eligible pregnant women during routine antenatal visits and provide study information in the local language. Women who meet the eligibility criteria will be invited to participate, and written informed consent will be obtained prior to enrolment.

To achieve the target sample size, recruitment is being supported through regular engagement with antenatal care providers, integration of screening into routine clinic workflows, community sensitization activities, and continuous monitoring of enrolment rates. Recruitment performance is reviewed monthly by the study coordination team, and corrective measures, including expansion to additional sites or extended recruitment periods, will be implemented if enrolment targets are not met due to unforeseen circumstances.

Randomization and allocation concealment

Eligible participants will be randomized in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of the three intervention arms using block randomization with variable block sizes to ensure balance across study groups. The randomization sequence will be generated by an independent statistician using a computer-based random number generator. Allocation will be concealed using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes prepared off-site and opened only after participant enrolment.

Blinding and bias mitigation

Because of differences in dosing regimens and administration schedules, blinding of participants and treating clinicians is not feasible, and the trial will be conducted in an open-label manner. To minimize the risk of performance and detection bias, laboratory personnel, pharmacokinetic analysts, molecular biologists, and outcome assessors will remain blinded to treatment allocation throughout the study. In addition, data analysts will be blinded during primary analyses. Standardized outcome definitions, pre-specified analytical plans, and blinded laboratory procedures will further minimize bias.

Data collection and follow-up

Screening, eligibility, and baseline assessments

To assess eligibility, potential participants presenting at the study sites will be screened. At screening, written informed consent will be obtained prior to enrolment. Participants will be informed in clear terms that refusal to participate, or subsequent withdrawal from the study, will not affect their access to routine antenatal care or treatment, where indicated.

Screening procedures will be conducted by trained study personnel using standardized screening forms. A screening log will be maintained at each study site to document all individuals assessed for eligibility, including reasons for exclusion or non-enrolment. Participants who are unable or unwilling to comply with the scheduled follow-up visits will not be enrolled in the study. Pregnancy status will be determined based on self-report and confirmed by an ultrasound scan done at the study site as part of routine antenatal care assessments.

Demographic and medical history assessments

At enrolment, baseline demographic, epidemiological, and other clinical information will be collected using standardized case report forms. Data collected will include age, weight, residential address, use of insecticide-treated bed nets, malaria risk factors, recent travel history, previous episodes of malaria, prior antimalarial treatment, and any previous participation in this or other clinical studies. A comprehensive medical and obstetric history will also be obtained, including current and past medical conditions relevant to pregnancy and malaria risk.

Physical examination and vital signs

A qualified member of the study team will perform a brief physical examination at baseline. Vital signs, including body temperature, respiratory rate and weight, will be measured and documented. Participants will also complete a standardized symptom questionnaire to assess the presence of malaria-related or other relevant clinical symptoms at enrolment.

Drug history and concomitant medications

A detailed drug history will be obtained at baseline. All prescribed, over the counter, and traditional medications, including antimalarial drugs, used within the seven days preceding enrolment will be recorded. Known drug allergies and previous adverse drug reactions will also be documented to ensure participant safety and eligibility for study medications.

Participant flow

Pregnant women attending antenatal clinics at Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital and participating primary health care centres will be assessed for eligibility. All women screened will be recorded in a screening log. Eligible women who provide written informed consent will undergo baseline clinical and laboratory assessments before randomization.

Participants will be randomly allocated in a 1:1:1 ratio to receive IPTp-SP, IPTp-DP, or IPTp-SP plus DP. Participants will be followed monthly until delivery. Losses to follow-up, withdrawals, protocol deviations, and discontinuations will be documented with reasons.

Participants will be followed prospectively from enrolment until delivery. Follow-up assessments will be conducted at scheduled antenatal visits following administration of study interventions. At each visit, clinical evaluation, malaria testing, haemoglobin measurement, and assessment of adherence and adverse events will be performed.

At delivery, maternal peripheral and placental samples will be collected, and pregnancy outcomes recorded. All randomized participants will be included in the intention-to-treat analysis. Participants with major protocol violations will be identified for per-protocol sensitivity analyses. Maternal haemoglobin and delivery outcomes will be recorded. Neonatal outcomes, including birth weight, gestational age, and early neonatal status, will be obtained from medical records and confirmed within two weeks postpartum. All data will be collected using standardized case report forms and entered into a secure electronic database.

Treatment administration, adherence, and pharmacovigilance

All study medications will be administered under directly observed therapy (DOT) at the health facility. For participants receiving dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine (DP), the first dose will be administered under direct supervision, and subsequent doses will be self-administered at home according to the prescribed schedule. Participants receiving SP will receive the drugs as directly observed therapy, supervised by the research assistants.

To optimize adherence for the DP, trained research assistants will conduct active follow-up through scheduled home visits and/or telephone reminders. Participants will receive standardized counselling on correct dosing, the importance of full treatment completion, potential adverse drug reactions, and actions to take in the event of missed doses or side effects.

Adherence to the multi-day DP regimen will be assessed using a composite approach that includes participant self-report, pill counts, and verification during scheduled follow-up visits. Dietary requirements and pharmacokinetic considerations for piperaquine administration

Piperaquine, administered as part of the dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine (DP) combination, exhibits food-dependent pharmacokinetics. Co-administration with food, particularly high-fat meals, significantly increases piperaquine bioavailability, potentially leading to greater systemic exposure and a higher risk of concentration-dependent adverse effects, including QT interval prolongation.

To minimize variability in drug absorption and ensure consistency across participants, all doses of DP will be administered under standardized fasting conditions. Participants will be instructed to take the medication at least 3 h after their last meal and to avoid food for at least 3 h after dosing. Intake of high-fat meals around the time of dosing will be specifically discouraged. Water will be permitted for drug administration.

Participants will receive structured counselling on these dietary requirements prior to dosing, and adherence will be reinforced through follow-up visits and monitoring by study personnel.

Safety monitoring

Given the potential for increased piperaquine exposure with food, adherence to fasting conditions will also serve as a safety measure to reduce the risk of cardiotoxicity, particularly QT interval prolongation. Any deviations from dietary instructions will be documented and considered during safety and PK analyses.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions

Participants may discontinue or modify the assigned intervention due to adverse drug reactions, clinical deterioration, withdrawal of consent, or investigator judgment for safety reasons. All such cases will be documented, and affected participants will receive appropriate standard-of-care treatment.

Concomitant care

All participants will receive standard antenatal care in accordance with Nigerian national guidelines, including iron–folate supplementation, tetanus toxoid immunization, and insecticide-treated bed nets. Any episode of symptomatic malaria during pregnancy will be treated according to national treatment guidelines and documented as a study outcome.

Participant timeline

Eligible pregnant women will be screened at enrolment (Week 1) using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, and written informed consent will be obtained before study procedures. Baseline demographic, obstetric, clinical, and laboratory assessments, including malaria testing (RDT, microscopy, PCV, and PCR), will be conducted.

At Week 2, eligible participants will be randomized to receive sulphadoxine–pyrimethamine (SP), dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine (DP), or combined SP + DP, with the first dose administered under direct observation.

Follow-up visits will occur at Weeks 4, 8, 12, and 16, during which participants will receive scheduled interventions and undergo clinical evaluation, malaria testing, PCV assessment, vital sign monitoring, adverse event surveillance.

At delivery (Week 20/close-out visit), final maternal and neonatal assessments will be performed, including malaria testing, PCR analysis, placental malaria assessment, and documentation of maternal and neonatal outcomes such as birth weight, gestational age, and Apgar scores. Statistical analysis

All analyses will be conducted according to a pre-specified Statistical Analysis Plan finalised before database lock. The primary analysis will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle and will include all randomized participants according to their assigned treatment arm, regardless of protocol adherence.

The primary outcome (prevalence of maternal malaria at delivery) will be compared across treatment groups using risk ratios and risk differences with 95% confidence intervals. Regression models will be used to adjust for key baseline covariates, including gravidity, gestational age at enrolment, and baseline malaria status. Comparative effectiveness will be interpreted in relation to the WHO therapeutic efficacy benchmark of ≥ 90%.

Binary outcomes will be compared using risk ratios or odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Continuous outcomes will be analysed using appropriate parametric or non-parametric methods.

Secondary binary outcomes will be analysed using log-binomial or Poisson regression with robust variance estimation. In contrast, continuous outcomes will be analysed using linear regression or appropriate non-parametric methods depending on data distribution. Time-to-event outcomes will be analysed using Kaplan–Meier methods and Cox proportional hazards models adjusting for relevant covariates such as gravidity, baseline parasitaemia, and gestational age at enrolment.

Pre-specified subgroup analyses will be conducted by gravidity and gestational age at enrolment. Sensitivity analyses will include per-protocol analyses to assess the robustness of findings. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputations where appropriate. No interim analyses are planned for outcome measures.

Discussion

This trial aims to address a significant evidence deficiency related to optimal malaria prevention strategies during pregnancy in regions experiencing emerging resistance to sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP). Although intermittent preventative treatment in pregnancy with SP (IPTp-SP) remains the standard recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO), the declining susceptibility of parasites raises concerns about their long-term efficacy.

Dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (DP) has demonstrated superior parasitological efficacy; however, the evidence concerning its impact on clinically relevant pregnancy outcomes has been inconsistent. Thus, the present trial will evaluate whether the combination of SP and DP may offer synergistic advantages by integrating the potent antimalarial properties of DP with the ancillary anti-inflammatory and antibacterial benefits associated with SP.

One of the advantages of this research is the combination of clinical, molecular, immunological, and pharmacokinetic endpoints within a comprehensive randomized controlled trial. By incorporating placental biomarkers, resistance genotyping, and piperaquine pharmacokinetics, the study may enhance the understanding of the mechanisms that contribute to different treatment effects.

The larger sample size enhances statistical accuracy, enables subgroup analyses, and improves the reliability of outcome estimates. The use of standardized outcome definitions, blinded laboratory evaluations, intention-to-treat methodologies, and strict quality assurance measures further bolsters the internal validity of the study.

Possible drawbacks involve the open-label study design and potential participant dropouts. Nonetheless, the outcome evaluators and lab staff are kept unaware of group assignments, and robust follow-up strategies have been established to enhance participant retention. The results from this research could significantly impact the formulation of malaria prevention policies in Nigeria and other countries where malaria is prevalent and resistance to SP is on the rise.
