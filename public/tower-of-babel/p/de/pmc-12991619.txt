Observational Study on the Effectiveness of Ventilator-Assisted Preoxygenation (VAPOX) Over Bag Valve Mask (BVM) Ventilation During Rapid Sequence Intubation in Patients with Acute Respiratory Distress
Authors: R. J. Ramu, K. V. Anand, Jithin Jacob Varghese, Jithin Suresh, R. G. Remya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurpose:To evaluate the effectiveness of Ventilator-Assisted Preoxygenation (VAPOX) compared with Bag Valve Mask (BVM) ventilation during Rapid Sequence Intubation (RSI) in patients presenting with acute respiratory distress.Materials and Methods:This observational study enrolled 60 critically ill patients requiring emergency intubation. Participants aged 40–90 years were consecutively allocated to receive preoxygenation by either VAPOX (n = 30) or BVM (n = 30). Heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation (SpO2), and PaO2/FiO2 ratios were recorded at baseline, postpreoxygenation, and at 1, 5, and 30 minutes postintubation. Statistical analysis used SPSS software; significance was defined as P < 0.05.Results:Patients in the VAPOX group reached 95% SpO2 significantly faster than the BVM group (39 seconds vs. 80 seconds, P = 0.005), despite having a lower initial mean SpO2 (85.4% vs. 88.47%). Postpreoxygenation saturation was significantly higher with VAPOX (99.37%) than with BVM (96.90%) (P = 0.001). PaO2/FiO2 ratios at 5 minutes postintubation showed significant improvement in VAPOX compared with BVM (298.53 vs. 237.67; P = 0.007). Hemodynamic parameters showed no significant differences between groups.Conclusion:VAPOX is superior to BVM for preoxygenation during RSI in critically ill patients, demonstrating faster oxygenation, better maintenance of oxygen saturation, and significantly improved PaO2/FiO2 ratios, thereby minimizing transient and critical hypoxemia.

INTRODUCTION

Rapid Sequence Intubation (RSI) is a critical procedure widely used for airway management in emergency departments, aimed at securing airway patency and ensuring optimal oxygenation while minimizing aspiration risk.[1] However, RSI carries a significant risk of complications, notably hypoxemia and cardiovascular instability, particularly in patients with acute respiratory distress.[2] Effective preoxygenation is essential in mitigating these complications by prolonging safe apnea time and improving arterial oxygenation.[3] Conventional Bag Valve Mask (BVM) ventilation has traditionally been used, yet recent evidence suggests potential limitations in achieving optimal oxygen saturation, especially in critically ill patients exhibiting shunt physiology.[4] Alternatively, Ventilator-Assisted Preoxygenation (VAPOX), using noninvasive positive pressure ventilation, has shown promising outcomes by enhancing oxygenation, increasing functional residual capacity, and reducing the incidence of transient hypoxemia.[5] Thus, comparing the effectiveness of VAPOX and BVM during RSI becomes crucial to identify superior strategies for emergency airway management.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This observational study was conducted in the emergency department of a tertiary hospital, enrolling 60 patients presenting with acute respiratory distress requiring rapid sequence intubation (RSI). Patients aged between 40 and 90 years were allocated consecutively into two groups for preoxygenation: Ventilator-Assisted Preoxygenation (VAPOX) and Bag Valve Mask (BVM). Inclusion criteria were patients presenting with hypoxemic respiratory distress characterized by a respiratory rate greater than 30 breaths/min and a PaO2/FiO2 ratio below 300 mmHg. Pregnant women, patients younger than 18 years, and those with oral/maxillofacial trauma were excluded. Vital parameters, including heart rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, oxygen saturation (SpO2), and arterial blood gases, were recorded at baseline, immediately after preoxygenation, and at intervals of 1, 5, and 30 minutes postintubation. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 17.0, applying Chi-square and Student t-tests, considering a P value < 0.05 as statistically significant.

RESULTS

Sixty patients were enrolled and divided equally into two groups: VAPOX (n = 30) and BVM (n = 30). Demographic characteristics, including age and gender, were comparable between both groups. The primary outcome assessed was the time required to achieve 95% oxygen saturation (SpO2). VAPOX significantly reduced the mean time to reach 95% saturation compared with BVM (38.67 ± 51.89 seconds vs. 80.17 ± 56.96 seconds, P = 0.005; Table 1). Despite a larger initial saturation gap in the VAPOX group, a quicker attainment of optimal saturation levels was noted, highlighting the efficiency of VAPOX over BVM [Table 1].

Secondary outcomes indicated superior performance of VAPOX in maintaining higher saturation levels postintubation compared with BVM, particularly at 1 minute postintubation (98% vs. 94%, P < 0.05). Although no significant differences in heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were observed between groups, there was an overall declining trend in these parameters postintubation in both groups. The initial PaO2/FiO2 ratios were comparable between the BVM (187.37 ± 110.01) and VAPOX (189.17 ± 97.79) groups. Five minutes postintubation, both groups showed an increase in PaO2/FiO2, with BVM reaching 237.67 ± 98.91 and VAPOX achieving a higher value of 298.53 ± 67.62. DISCUSSION

Rapid Sequence Intubation (RSI) is integral to emergency airway management, particularly in critically ill patients presenting with acute respiratory distress. However, hypoxemia remains a common complication associated with this procedure, underscoring the importance of efficient preoxygenation strategies.[6] In this study, ventilator-assisted preoxygenation (VAPOX) demonstrated superiority over the traditional Bag Valve Mask (BVM) technique, reflecting quicker attainment and maintenance of optimal oxygen saturation (SpO2 ≥ 95%) levels despite lower initial SpO2 values. This is consistent with previous studies by Wang et al.,[7] who demonstrated that preoxygenation using invasive ventilator-assisted techniques significantly reduced the time required to achieve adequate saturation compared to manual methods.

The improved efficacy of VAPOX observed in our study could be attributed to the beneficial effects of non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV), which enhances oxygenation by alveolar recruitment, thereby increasing functional residual capacity (FRC). This aligns with findings from Futier et al.,[8] indicating significant improvements in gas exchange parameters and lung recruitment when employing noninvasive ventilation techniques. Moreover, VAPOX offers distinct advantages such as maintaining consistent FiO2 delivery, reducing mask leakage through a superior two-handed mask sealing technique, and limiting inadvertent gastric insufflation and subsequent aspiration risks.[8]

Interestingly, our study found no significant differences between VAPOX and BVM concerning heart rate and blood pressure parameters postintubation. This aligns with the observations of Delay et al.,[9] who reported comparable hemodynamic stability between noninvasive ventilation and conventional methods despite better oxygenation outcomes with ventilator-assisted approaches. This suggests that while hemodynamic stability is vital, VAPOX predominantly enhances respiratory rather than cardiovascular parameters during RSI.

Shunt physiology, a condition frequently encountered in critically ill patients, was notably managed more effectively with VAPOX, preventing significant saturation drops observed with BVM. This finding corroborates the physiological principle that applying continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) or positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) aids in alveolar recruitment, enhancing functional residual capacity and thus improving oxygenation even under conditions of severe hypoxemia.[910]

Despite our positive findings, limitations of our study include its observational design and relatively small sample size, limiting generalizability. Further large-scale randomized controlled trials with diverse patient demographics and clinical settings are necessary to validate our findings and explore the broader implications of VAPOX in emergency airway management strategies.[9101112]

CONCLUSION

Ventilator-Assisted Preoxygenation (VAPOX) demonstrates superior efficacy compared to Bag Valve Mask (BVM) ventilation during rapid sequence intubation in critically ill patients with acute respiratory distress. VAPOX significantly shortens the time required to achieve optimal oxygen saturation and effectively maintains higher saturation levels during and immediately after intubation, especially beneficial in patients exhibiting shunt physiology. Although hemodynamic parameters were comparable between groups, VAPOX provided better oxygenation stability, suggesting its clinical superiority. Therefore, VAPOX should be considered as a preferred preoxygenation technique to enhance patient safety, improve clinical outcomes, and minimize the incidence of transient and critical hypoxemia during emergency airway management procedures.
