Supplements and medications for respiratory resilience against air pollution
Authors: Christopher Carlsten, Sundeep Salvi, Gary W.K. Wong, Kian Fan Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Air pollution is a major threat to respiratory health. In this article, we review the current literature on accessible interventions that could help patients build resilience against this threat. We explore the potential benefits of antioxidant-rich diets, including supplements such as fish oil, vitamins C, D and E, and prebiotics, which have shown promise in reducing inflammation and oxidative stress, thereby mitigating air pollution-related respiratory decline. Nasal washes are commonly used to clear nasal passages, which could help to clear pollutants from the nasal passages and improve mucociliary clearance. Furthermore, medications such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and intranasal corticosteroids have been reported to reduce pollutant-related airway inflammation and lung function deterioration triggered by pollutant exposure. Given the generally favourable safety profile of these interventions, they are reasonable to consider in consultation with a care provider. Further research is needed to establish optimal dosing, safety and long-term efficacy, particularly for those exposed to chronic air pollution. Healthcare professionals should work together to further identify and implement effective interventions to mitigate the impact of air pollution on respiratory health.

Introduction

The Global Burden of Disease 2021 Report identified air pollution as the second leading risk factor for death, accounting for 8.1 million deaths worldwide [1]. Moreover, emissions from fossil fuel combustion contribute to global warming and the rapidly growing burden of respiratory conditions [2].

The lung epithelium, along with the airway surface liquid, serves as the first line of defence against pollutants and relies on endogenous anti-inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms to counteract excessive oxidative stress [3]. Air pollution components, such as ozone (O3), nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and particulate matter (PM), including PM2.5 with organic and metallic components, contribute to reactive oxygen species [4], which regulate transcription factors, such as nuclear factor-κB, and activate pro-inflammatory genes [5]. Pollutants such as PM2.5 or ultrafine particles elevate production of fibrinogen or interleukin-6 whilst reducing levels of anti-inflammatory molecules such as adiponectin [6]. This cascade of reactions compromises the epithelial airway barrier and impairs lung function, increasing vulnerability to pollutants and allergens. Additionally, the protective antioxidant barrier in the lungs can be overwhelmed by the oxidative products induced by prolonged or high exposure to air pollution [7], which may be more pronounced in people with airway diseases including asthma and COPD [8].

The World Health Organization's 2024 guidelines on climate-related health risks underscore the need for urgent global awareness to address environmental threats to respiratory health [9, 10]; however, in contrast to general guidelines and expert opinion regarding strategies to reduce exposure to pollutants, there is a dearth of evidence-based recommendations for specific personal interventions for those inevitably exposed to highly polluted environments.

We previously provided a review of personal strategies to minimise effects of air pollution [11]. Therein were 10 such strategies, one of which briefly addressed diet, supplements and anti-inflammatory agents. At that time, we were unable to confidently recommend any specific agent to combat the effects of air pollution. Realising that this theme warranted an update and significantly more detailing of the evidence, in this review we evaluate the potential interventions, with particular attention to those that will build up “resiliency” to the effects of pollution. In this context, resiliency refers to one's ability to withstand threats to the homeostatic stability of our bodily systems, so that one can maintain essential health despite continued exposure to the dangers of the external environment.

This review therefore summarises evidence-based recommendations regarding diet, supplements and medications that can protect respiratory health against air pollution. For consistency with our prior review, we again assign a level-of-evidence score based on the grading used in the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) [12] and present this in a table at the end of this review.

Search strategy and selection criteria

A literature search was conducted in PubMed, with full search terms provided in table 1. The search included English language articles published up to February 2024. Eligible publication types comprised original research articles, randomised controlled trials, narrative reviews, systematic reviews and meta-analyses. To ensure comprehensive coverage of the available evidence, search outputs were also reviewed manually to identify additional relevant studies.

Screening and study selection

The screening process was conducted in two stages. First, an independent body performed an initial search and screening of titles and abstracts to identify potentially relevant publications; no authors were involved in this stage. To validate the search terms, 12 manuscripts known to be relevant were selected and expected to appear in the search outputs; eight were retrieved, which was considered sufficient to confirm the validity of the search terms. During this initial screening relevant titles and abstracts were identified and subsequently assigned by topic to each author for a secondary screening.

n the second stage, authors reviewed the assigned records. After both stages were completed, all selected references were compiled, and the authors conducted a full review to determine the final set of eligible studies. Each author then reviewed, analysed and synthesised the evidence for the studies related to their assigned topic.

Because this review was conceived as a scoping narrative review intended to map the available evidence and highlight potential intervention approaches rather than critically appraise individual studies, a formal risk-of-bias assessment was not conducted.

Potential interventions for respiratory health protection against air pollution

Diet and supplements

Fruits and vegetables

Antioxidants, either consumed through a healthy diet or supplements, have been reported to strengthen the body's defences, increase resiliency to pollution-induced oxidative stress, prevent related respiratory effects, preserve lung function, reduce inflammation and enhance detoxification pathways [13, 14]. Dietary antioxidants, such as those in fruits and vegetables, have gained attention for their potential to protect against the harmful effects of air pollutants on respiratory health (table 2).

Dietary patterns abundant in fruits, vegetables and whole foods, such as the Mediterranean diet, have shown promise in improving respiratory health in vulnerable populations [15]. Children with asthma adhering to a Mediterranean diet had higher forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), higher forced vital capacity (FVC) and reduced inflammation, as indicated by lower interleukin-8 levels [15]. Children consuming pro-inflammatory diets high in processed foods and low in fruits and vegetables experienced more severe asthma symptoms when exposed to indoor PM2.5 and PM10, compared with those with an anti-inflammatory diet rich in fruits and vegetables [16].

Specific vegetables are also of interest, including broccoli sprout-derived beverages, rich in sulforaphane and glucoraphanin. These have been shown to increase excretion of pollutants like acrolein and benzene, suggesting a potential role in supporting detoxification mechanisms and reducing health risks associated with air pollution exposure [17].

In summary, antioxidant-rich diets have been linked with better lung function, reduced inflammation and enhanced detoxification, all of which are relevant to limiting the respiratory effects caused by air pollution exposure.

Vitamins

Several vitamins are essential for maintaining cellular integrity and protecting against damage from metabolic processes and environmental threats [18].

Vitamins C, D and E and β-carotene have been examined for their potential to combat oxidative stress caused by air pollution. Evidence indicates that vitamins C and E supplementation can provide benefits for vulnerable populations, including adults and children with respiratory diseases. In adults with asthma, higher vitamin C and E intake correlated with reduced airway hyperresponsiveness and lung function decline during acute pollutant exposure [19]. In children with asthma, short-term intervention with vitamins C and E reduced the impact of O3-associated small airway dysfunction, particularly in those with moderate-to-severe asthma [20]. Similarly, another study reported that children with asthma with higher dietary intake of vitamins C and E had a lower risk of peak expiratory flow decline, particularly in areas with low PM10 levels [21].

Vitamins have also been studied for potential protective effects against prolonged exposure to occupational contaminants. Supplementation with vitamins C and E improved oxidative stress markers and antioxidant activity in individuals exposed to coal mining pollutants and products from solid residue incineration [22]. Similarly, dietary vitamin C was associated with a reduced risk of small airway dysfunction in workers exposed to carbon black [23]. These findings suggest that dietary intake of vitamin C and E bolsters airflow against chronic exposure to airborne contaminants.

Air pollution exposure is also associated with inflammatory responses [24], and some vitamins are known to possess anti-inflammatory properties that could help counter this effect [25]. In a highly polluted city in China, vitamin C supplementation significantly reduced inflammatory markers, including interleukin-6, tumour necrosis factor-α and C-reactive protein [26].

Low serum β-carotene levels in adults with chronic respiratory symptoms (e.g. chronic cough, phlegm, productive cough, wheeze with shortness of breath, shortness of breath) have been linked to peak expiratory flow decrements due to exposure to PM10 or black smoke when compared with individuals with high levels of serum β-carotene [27]. However, serum levels of vitamins or anti-pollutant components may not reflect levels in the lung, where they could be effective [28]. The potential benefits of increasing concentrations of vitamins in the lungs via supplements remain unknown.

Despite the evidence for the protective role of dietary antioxidants, studies have reported inconsistent results when assessing the benefits of antioxidant supplementation. For example, a trial in healthy individuals exposed to O3 showed no significant reduction in bronchoconstriction or lung inflammatory responses following supplementation with vitamins C and E or with vitamin E alone [29]. In a systematic review of the effects of exogenous administration of antioxidants (including vitamin C, vitamin E and β-carotene), conflicting results were found in the vitamins’ ability to ameliorate air pollution-related respiratory health effects [13].

Vitamin D has been even less studied in the context of air pollution. Using a mouse model and exposure to diesel exhaust combined with allergen, investigators showed vitamin D to protect from asthma development in this pre-clinical model [30]. However, a trial of this intervention in humans exposed to air pollution has not been performed.

In summary, vitamins C, D and E and β-carotene have been explored for their potential roles in addressing air pollution-induced oxidative stress and inflammation, and the evidence is generally supportive. Variability in published results indicates that further studies are required to clarify the possible benefits of these supplements in respiratory health. Determination of the effective dosage and usage duration is also necessary before any strong recommendations could be made.

Other dietary supplements

Fish oil is a rich source of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). Fish oil is popular due to the health benefits of polyunsaturated fatty acids [31]; but precautions are needed when interpreting health claims, as not all marketed fish oil supplements align with the recommended EPA+DHA intake ≤0.5 g·day−1 [32, 33].

Fish oil supplementation (3 g·day−1, equivalent to 410 mg EPA and 274 mg DHA) significantly decreased lung function decline caused by acute O3 exposure, reducing FEV1 and FEV1/FVC decrements by 48% and 70% respectively, compared with placebo [34]. Similarly, dietary omega-3 has been associated with improved lung function and cardiovascular responses in individuals exposed to NO2, even at NO2 levels below current air quality standards [35].

Dietary fatty acids can influence the response to indoor air pollution. Brigham et al. [36] found that higher omega-6 intake was associated with increased asthma severity and poorer lung function, whilst omega-3 intake appeared to mitigate indoor PM effects on asthma symptoms. These findings indicate that dietary patterns could serve as an adjunct consideration in asthma management and highlight the potential relevance of a balanced fatty acid intake to benefit respiratory health.

Supplementation with other antioxidants such as N-acetylcysteine (NAC, 600 mg three times daily) has been associated with a reversal of airway hyperresponsiveness in healthy individuals exposed to diesel pollutants [37]. Additionally, in workers exposed to lead, NAC supplementation reduced blood lead levels, normalised homocysteine antioxidant levels and decreased oxidative stress in a dose-dependent manner [38]. These findings support NAC as a potential antioxidant, although routine use is often limited by gastrointestinal side-effects.

Natural compounds with antioxidant properties, such as saffron, may also improve respiratory function and resilience. In individuals with mild-to-moderate allergic asthma, saffron intake improved spirometry measurements compared with placebo; however, further studies are needed to establish its specific role in mitigating air pollution-related effects [39].

Overall, the current literature suggests that dietary fatty acids (particularly omega-3) and NAC play a role in protecting lung function, reducing oxidative stress and mitigating airway effects from pollutants, although further research is needed to clearly establish these benefits on human health.

Prebiotics and probiotics

Beyond antioxidants, interplay between the gut and lung microbiome plays a vital role in immune regulation and overall health [40]. In a healthy state, gut microbiota produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) that promote anti-inflammatory responses from antigen-presenting cells, support epithelial integrity and reinforce barrier functions [41]. Bidirectional communication between the gut and lung microbiomes supports immune homeostasis in the lungs. SCFAs and the microbe-associated molecular patterns, such as Toll-like receptor interactions, also have a clear role in maintaining lung epithelial barrier function [42].

Air pollution disrupts the gut–lung axis balance by stimulating reactive oxygen species production and increasing inflammation [43]. It can also imbalance the lung microbiome, increasing abundance of potential pathogenic bacteria such as Staphylococcus, Haemophilus, Streptococcus and Moraxella [44, 45]. Perturbations in the tryptophan metabolism associated with NO2 exposure, particularly in the gut microbiome-associated indole pathway, have been reported [46]. These may influence inflammatory pathways, dysregulate gut–lung microbiome crosstalk and disrupt SCFA production, leading to hypersensitivity to respiratory and food allergens [47].

Both prebiotics and probiotics have been investigated for their potential roles in respiratory health, including reducing the risk of respiratory infections, modulating allergic airways disease and preventing lung inflammation [48, 49]. Prebiotics, like oligosaccharides, serve as substrates for host microorganisms and are associated with various physiological effects [50]. Probiotics, such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species, contribute to gastrointestinal and immune health [51] and have been used to treat conditions including infectious diarrhoea and ulcerative colitis, and to prevent and treat respiratory infections [51].

Prebiotics and probiotics have been studied for their effects on microbiota composition, epithelial barrier integrity and inflammatory responses; consequently these functions could serve against the adverse effects of air pollution [43, 52]. Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, found in many fermented foods, exhibits potent antioxidant properties and modulates the intestinal barrier function [53]. These properties may help to mitigate air pollution-induced inflammation [54].

L. plantarum KC3 strain, isolated from kimchi, exhibited anti-inflammatory effects in intestinal cells and reduced immune cell infiltration and cytokines in the lungs of mice exposed to ambient PM [55]. Similarly, combination treatment with Bacillus coagulans (probiotic) and heat-killed L. plantarum (postbiotic) synergistically reduced lung damage and immunoglobulin E levels, and restored immune balance in a PM2.5-induced lung inflammation mouse model [56]. Other promising strains include Lactobacillus paracasei 33, which reduced lung inflammation, lung immunoglobulin E levels and type 2 helper T-cell (Th2) cytokine production [57].

Additionally, maternal supplementation with Bifidobacterium breve M-16V reduced asthma susceptibility in offspring exposed to air pollution by lowering airway eosinophils and Th2 cytokines and altering gut microbiota in neonatal mice [58].

Supplementation of probiotics and prebiotics may extend beyond lung health, as data suggests they improve cognitive performance in both healthy individuals and in those with cognitive disorders [59].

Overall, current evidence indicates that exposure to air pollution can alter the lung microbiome, potentially due to disruption of the gut–lung axis. Prebiotics and probiotics have been studied as potential modulators of these effects. However, long-term clinical studies are needed to determine whether these effects translate to protection from pollution.

Nasal wash

Nasal wash, including nasal rinsing or nasal irrigation, is a simple practice used to clear mucus, debris and airborne pollutants from the nasal passages. Based on treatment guidelines and literature, nasal rinsing is recommended as an adjunctive treatment for sinonasal conditions, such as chronic rhinosinusitis and allergic rhinitis [60–62].

Secreted mucus is cleared by coordinated ciliary activity of the epithelial cell, which helps to clear pollutants trapped in the mucus [63]. As air pollutants can damage the respiratory epithelium and impair mucociliary clearance [64], nasal washes may offer a practical solution to physically remove pollutants from the nasal passages. In one study, mucociliary clearance improved from baseline after applying nasal rinses at room temperature or body temperature, with the latter showing greater improvement [65]. In another study, hypertonic saline nasal irrigation significantly restored mucociliary clearance in individuals with chronic sinusitis whilst isotonic saline improved clearance in allergic rhinitis and acute sinusitis [66]. However, hypertonic saline may induce transient slowing of ciliary movement, potentially prolonging the pollutant's contact with the respiratory epithelium. Thus, despite potential benefits, nasal rinsing efficacy in clearing debris from exposure to high levels of air pollution is yet to be confirmed.

Overall, there is plausibility for nasal washes’ ability to clear pollutants and support mucociliary clearance. Prospective interventional studies using clinically relevant outcomes are needed to evaluate the clinical benefits of clearing pollutants from the nasal epithelium.

Medications

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) relieve pain, fever and inflammation by inhibiting cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, which regulate the downstream arachidonic acid pathway, including prostaglandins synthesis [67]. Given that air pollutants alter prostaglandin levels [68, 69], studies examining the effects of NSAIDs on the inflammation response of individuals living in highly polluted environments could give some insight into their potential benefits (table 3).

In a study by Riedel et al. [70] animals treated with indomethacin and exposed to sulfur dioxide (SO2) showed reduced allergic responses and lowered specific antibody levels compared with controls, suggesting that anti-inflammatory treatment influenced SO2-related sensitisation in this model.

Whilst there is insufficient clinical evidence directly supporting the use of NSAIDs to mitigate the respiratory health effects of air pollution, some observational studies suggest potential benefits. A longitudinal study explored whether NSAIDs can mitigate lung function decline following short-term PM2.5 exposure. Results showed that NSAID use, particularly aspirin, was associated with smaller decreases in FVC and FEV1 [71]. Similarly, a small trial demonstrated that indomethacin attenuated lung function decline in young adults exposed to O3 [72].

A randomised, double-blind study investigating ibuprofen's effects on O3-induced lung function and inflammation in healthy men found that O3 caused airway neutrophilic inflammation, increased COX products in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, and decreased FVC and FEV1. Ibuprofen significantly reduced FEV1 decline and lowered prostaglandin E2 and thromboxane B2 levels in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, supporting the role of arachidonic acid products in mediating protection of lung function; however, ibuprofen did not affect neutrophilic airway inflammation or changes in specific airway resistance [73].

NSAIDs may have different effects in individuals with asthma compared with healthy individuals. A study on COX metabolites in O3-induced lung function decline found that whilst indomethacin reduced FEV1 and FVC decline in healthy individuals, it had no such effect in those with asthma, but instead, the decline of small-airway function, measured as forced expiratory flow rate at 75% of vital capacity, was attenuated. Following O3 exposure, individuals with asthma showed significantly higher prostaglandin F2α levels, suggesting that COX metabolites contribute differently to O3 effects, causing small-airway obstruction in individuals with asthma and restrictive-type changes in healthy individuals [74].

Although preliminary studies suggest that NSAIDs could be considered potential treatments to increase airway epithelium resilience against air pollutants, NSAIDs are associated with adverse effects on the gastrointestinal tract [75]. They can damage the gastrointestinal epithelium, causing peptic ulcer disease and bleeding [76]. This is particularly important if these medications are needed long term.

In summary, NSAIDs may help to mitigate air pollution-induced lung function decline and inflammation. There is one supportive randomised trial in the context of O3. Randomised controlled trials are needed to assess the potential for NSAIDs to protect lung health in the context of particulate-rich pollution and to evaluate their safety in healthy and susceptible populations.

Corticosteroids

Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) are widely used to treat asthma, and nasal corticosteroids to treat allergic rhinitis [77–79]. Despite toxicity concerns, corticosteroids effectively reduce airway inflammation and alleviate associated symptoms [80]; however, air pollution may also reduce glucocorticoid responsiveness, including alterations in the glucocorticoid receptor (e.g. phosphorylation, oxidation), cytokine repression, inflammatory pathways and epigenetic changes [81, 82].

Preclinical data have shown that anti-inflammatory treatments containing methylprednisolone can effectively inhibit SO2-induced enhancement of allergic sensitisation, reducing airway narrowing and specific immunoglobulin G antibodies [70]; however, epidemiological studies show variable effectiveness of corticosteroids in moderating asthma symptoms due to air pollution [83, 84].

A limited number of human studies have shown that ICS can control symptoms aggravated by environmental pollutants (table 3). For instance, a paediatric study reported that asthmatic children not taking ICS suffered more asthma symptoms when exposed to a range of air pollutants [85]. Furthermore, early ICS intervention has shown benefits as part of a rescue intervention strategy in patients with asthma exposed to severe air pollution. Patients were asked to self-administer budesonide/formoterol (160 μg/4.5 μg/dose, one dose twice daily), plus their usual treatment (i.e. either short-acting β-agonist inhaler alone or budesonide/formoterol inhaler) at the first signs of severe air pollution (air quality index ≥200 as provided by the Beijing Municipal Environmental Monitoring Center) until severe pollution ended. These patients experienced significantly fewer asthma exacerbations and unplanned outpatient visits compared with the control group that did not receive additional budesonide/formoterol during severe pollution [86].

Collectively, this evidence suggests that preventive ICS may help to decrease asthma exacerbations and improve patient outcomes during severe air pollution events; however, the potential benefits of ICS need to be balanced against potential risks [87]. In particular, ICS are known to increase infection risk and reduce host defence by suppressing production of antimicrobial peptides [88]. If ICS-induced suppression of host defence is enhanced by traffic air pollution, the combination of ICS with traffic air pollution may increase the risk of infection and acute COPD exacerbations [89]. This is a concern as ICS use may exceed the indicated dose for some lung conditions. For example, in Canada, ICS usage is far higher than the current COPD guidelines would recommend [90].

There is less evidence for nasal corticosteroids than for ICS. One placebo-controlled clinical study has been published in the context of pollution, which showed that intranasal budesonide (256 μg·day−1 for 10 days during a high pollution season) significantly improved total nasal symptoms and reduced itching/sneezing compared with placebo in adults with allergic rhinitis triggered or worsened by airborne pollution [85].

Given this complexity, further research is needed to explore the role of inhaled and nasal corticosteroids in mitigating the effects of air pollution on respiratory health. It is unclear whether recommendations should be tailored to specific phenotypes, such as neutrophilic or eosinophilic inflammation or Th2-high versus Th2-low endotypes. Although the ICS literature on airway diseases is evolving in this direction, recommendations outside the context of air pollution may not apply when pollution is a critical driver of adverse events. These events may involve cellular drivers different from those present at baseline, when phenotype characterisation generally occurs.

Future research should also target the use of steroids in healthy individuals or those without asthma who are exposed to high air pollution levels, rather than focusing solely on populations with existing respiratory diseases. The goal would be to prevent airway inflammation that, as known from research independent of pollution [91], could lead to long-term airway remodelling associated with intractable clinical manifestations [92]. Furthermore, since most of the existing studies were limited in the range of dosage and treatment duration, new studies should be expanded accordingly from the initial promising results noted above.

Conclusion

With globally suboptimal air quality nearly ubiquitous and unabating, there is a broad and urgent need for interventions to enhance resilience; however, it is simultaneously imperative to be clear and discerning about the evidence that exists and that is still needed in this regard. We report some supportive evidence for antioxidants, NSAIDs, and both inhaled and nasal corticosteroids, and emerging but less developed evidence to support nasal washes and pre/probiotics, as summarised in figure 1 and table 4. Each of these approaches are reasonable, in the context of relatively low risk associated with any of them, but the evidence overall is modest (either GINA grade C or D). Therefore, any of these will benefit from further prospective randomised controlled clinical trials, with particular attention to the risk–benefit balance, before strongly supportive recommendations can be made to the general public. Further research is warranted to determine the optimal dosing and long-term efficacy of potential interventions, particularly for those exposed to chronic air pollution. In the meantime, healthcare professionals should emphasise the benefits of available interventions, including dietary intake that emphasises fruits and vegetables and consistent use of inhaled and/or nasal corticosteroids prescribed for chronic conditions, to enhance resiliency and mitigate the impact of air pollution on respiratory health.
