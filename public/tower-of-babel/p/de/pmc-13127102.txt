Impact of a Routine Third Trimester Ultrasound Policy on Obstetric, Neonatal and Service Delivery Outcomes in an Australian Tertiary Centre
Authors: Praneel Kumar, Adrienne Gordon, Elizabeth Fisher, Jose Garcia Flores, Saleem Khan, Rajit Narayan, Bradley D. E. Vries, Jon Hyett, Ritu Mogra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRoutine third trimester ultrasound scans have been introduced in several maternity centres to increase detection of fetal growth disorders and improve perinatal outcomes.AimsTo assess changes in obstetric and neonatal outcomes and service delivery following implementation of a routine third trimester ultrasound policy in a tertiary hospital in Australia.Materials and MethodsA single‐centre retrospective cohort study was conducted comparing a pre‐policy cohort that received ultrasound scans measuring biometry and fetal Dopplers when deemed clinically necessary during the third trimester with a post‐policy cohort in which all pregnant women were offered an ultrasound at 35–37 weeks gestation. Results of the ultrasound were communicated to the obstetric team. Primary study outcomes were changes to service delivery, obstetric and neonatal outcomes and detection of fetal growth abnormalities. Differences in proportions were compared using the chi‐squared test.ResultsIn the pre‐policy cohort, there were 4826 singleton deliveries ≥ 35 weeks gestation and 34% of these women received a third trimester scan, compared to 4499 deliveries in the post‐policy cohort in which 81% received a scan. There was a statistically significant increase in detection of LGA fetuses from 27% to 43% but no significant change in SGA detection or preterm birth rates post policy implementation. Early term births (37–38 + 6 weeks) increased (26.9% vs. 31.2%; p < 0.001) along with induction of labour (33.5% vs. 38.2%; p < 0.001). There was no decrease in stillbirths or adverse neonatal outcomes.ConclusionThe benefits of implementing routine third trimester scans in ‘real‐world’ clinical practice remain unclear given these findings.

Introduction

Fetal growth disorders, such as small for gestational age (SGA) or large for gestational age (LGA), are associated with significant perinatal mortality and morbidity [1, 2]. Routine third trimester ultrasounds have been shown to increase detection of SGA and LGA in randomised and prospective cohort studies when compared to measurement of symphysis‐fundal height or selective ultrasonography [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. There is some evidence that antenatal detection of SGA and LGA improves perinatal outcomes [8, 9, 10]. To date, high‐quality studies and systematic reviews have not found improved perinatal outcomes with routine third trimester ultrasound in low risk pregnancies, often attributed to lack of statistical power [11, 12]. The role of third trimester ultrasound in low‐risk pregnancies therefore remains controversial.

However, routine third trimester scans also allow identification of fetal malpresentation and congenital abnormalities that were previously undetected or have developed during fetal development [13, 14]. Unlike ad hoc scans, routine scans can be booked in a specific gestational window, reducing risks of inappropriate intervention leading to preterm delivery.

Several international obstetric centres have introduced routine third trimester scans given increased detection rates of at‐risk fetuses. However, there is a paucity of evidence describing the real‐world impacts from implementing such a policy. For example, prospective cohort studies blind clinicians from ultrasound findings to improve rigour which do not reflect real world practice [3], or report on a limited set of perinatal outcomes that may not show the impacts on service delivery or miss unintended consequences [15]. Furthermore, studies assessing a wider range of perinatal outcomes still do not examine documented clinical reasons explaining these trends, thereby limiting understanding of how changes in policy impact practice [4].

In Australia, third‐trimester ultrasound is generally offered selectively to pregnant women [16]. Our centre introduced a policy offering all pregnant women a routine scan at 35–37 weeks. The objective of this study was to observe changes in service delivery, obstetric and neonatal outcomes, and detection of fetal growth abnormalities following implementation of this policy in a real‐world clinical setting.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective cohort study, conducted at a tertiary public hospital in Sydney, compared two cohorts of women managed prior to and following implementation of a routine third trimester ultrasound policy. The pre‐policy historical ‘control’ cohort consisted of deliveries between July 2016 and June 2017. The post‐policy ‘routine scan’ cohort consisted of deliveries between July 2018 and June 2019. The intervening period (July 2017–June 2018) was excluded due to the need for policy education and provision of additional ultrasound services to achieve comprehensive implementation. The study excluded women with a multiple pregnancy or delivery prior to 35 weeks gestation. Only women birthing at our hospital were included.

The pre‐policy ‘control’ cohort received ultrasound scans deemed clinically necessary during the third trimester by the treating clinician. Post policy introduction, all pregnant women were offered routine scans at 35–37 weeks, irrespective of clinical concerns. Referral forms allowed clinicians to identify clinical issues meriting specific ultrasound assessment.

Pregnancies were dated according to the hospital's standard obstetric care policy. The ultrasound protocol included measurement of fetal biometry (head circumference (HC), abdominal circumference (AC), and femur length (FL)), estimated fetal weight (EFW), fetal presentation, placental location, fetal Dopplers, amniotic fluid index (AFI) and selective examination of fetal anatomy.

Ultrasound measurements were recorded using Viewpoint software (version 6, GE Healthcare) and were based on charts from the Australasian Society for Ultrasound in Medicine for HC and AC, ‘Chitty’ for FL, ‘Hadlock 3’ for EFW (as per local guidelines) and Baschat et al. for Doppler studies [17, 18]. Scans were performed using GE Healthcare VE730/E8 and Cannon Aplio 500 ultrasound machines (GE Healthcare, Australia and Cannon, Japan respectively) by accredited sonographers and reported by obstetric ultrasound subspecialists.

Ultrasound results were communicated to the obstetric team along with recommendations for management as per local policy.

Data reporting maternal and neonatal outcomes were obtained from the hospital electronic medical record (CERNER EMR; Oracle) and from neonatal (NICUS, NSW Health) databases. Three electronic datasets (Cerner, Viewpoint and NICUS) were linked using three unique identifiers (medical record number, date of birth, last name).

Perinatal Outcomes

SGA was defined as birth weight ≤ 10th centile. LGA was defined as birth weight ≥ 90th centile. Birthweight centiles were determined using local population charts [18]. Abnormal Dopplers were defined as: umbilical artery Doppler PI > 95th centile, MCA PI < 5th centile, cerebroplacental‐ratio (CPR) < 5th centile, uterine artery Doppler mean PI > 95th centile [19, 20].

Obstetric outcomes included onset of labour (spontaneous or induced) and mode of delivery. They were divided into three clinically relevant gestational age at delivery groups: 35+0 to 36+6 weeks (preterm), 37+0 to 38+6 weeks (early term), and ≥ 39+0 weeks (term).

Neonatal outcomes included live birth or stillbirth, birth weight, APGAR less than 7 at 5 min, hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission and length of stay. Details of structural abnormalities seen after birth were also recorded.

Secondary outcomes included detection rates of SGA and LGA using our population reference birth weight charts. The sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values were calculated using a two‐by‐two table.

Service Delivery Outcomes

The impact on ultrasound services, obstetric services, clinical practice and neonatal clinics was assessed using uptake of ultrasound scans, changes in elective (planned) delivery rates, documented reasons for elective deliveries and additional babies requiring follow‐up due to identified anomalies, respectively.

Data Collection and Statistical Analysis

Data is presented as mean, standard deviation (symmetric normal data) and proportions. Differences in continuous variables between cohorts were tested using an independent samples t‐test (normal data) or Mann–Whitney U test (skewed data). Difference in proportions associated with categorical measures between cohorts were compared using the chi‐squared test/Fisher's exact test. Missing data was reported in data analysis as n/N (%) with percentages calculated against the N for which information was available. All statistical tests were performed at a significance level of 0.05 in SPSS V22.0. We did not perform multivariate regression analysis as the aim of our study was to observe changes following policy implementation rather than draw causal inferences on the impact of ultrasounds on perinatal outcomes.

Assuming prevalence of small for gestational age (< 10th centile) was 10% in our population, then a sample size of 4500 deliveries per year had an 80% chance of observing a 50% increase in prenatal detection from a baseline rate of 20% in the control group following the implementation of the policy.

Ethics

Our study received ethical approval from the local ethics committee (approval number 2019/ETH06551) and followed ethical standards.

Results

In the pre‐policy cohort, there were 4826 singleton deliveries ≥ 35 weeks gestation, 34% (1654) underwent a third trimester ultrasound scan at 35+0–37+6 weeks gestation within our institution (Figure S1). In the post‐policy cohort, there were 4499 singleton deliveries ≥ 35 weeks gestation, 81% (3683) had a third trimester ultrasound scan at 35+0–37+6 weeks gestation within our institution (Figure S1). The proportion of women with elective (planned) births increased (54.1% vs. 49.2%; p < 0.001) (Table 2). This was associated with a statistically significant increase in induction of labour (33.5% vs. 38.2%; p < 0.001), without significant change in the rate of elective caesarean section. The rate of emergency caesarean section increased (14.4% vs. 18.0%; p < 0.001), and there was an overall reduction in vaginal delivery rate (69.8% vs. 66.0%; p < 0.001).

Delivery decisions and mode of delivery were examined in detail according to three gestational subgroups (Table 3). There was no significant increase in births < 37 weeks following policy introduction. However, an increased rate of early (37–38+6 week) term birth (26.9% vs. 31.2%; p < 0.001 of births) associated with increased induction rates between 37 and 38+6 weeks was observed in the post‐policy cohort. The elective caesarean section rate did not change significantly in any gestational subgroup. Emergency caesarean section rates were slightly (< 2%) and statistically higher in the cohort of women delivered ≥ 39 weeks.

In the post‐policy cohort, a total of 52 congenital anomalies (prevalence of 1.1%) were identified during routine third trimester ultrasounds. The most prevalent anomalies were renal anomalies (41 cases), followed by cardiac anomalies [5], cerebral anomalies [2] and other anomalies [4]. Among these anomalies, 31 (60%) cases were newly detected during the routine third trimester scan. Twenty‐four (46%) of the anomalies detected during the routine third trimester ultrasound required ongoing monitoring beyond the age of 3 months.

Discussion

We observed the impact of implementing a policy of routine third trimester ultrasound scans at a tertiary Australian hospital. There were an additional 2029 scans 1 year post policy implementation. Detection of LGA fetuses significantly increased (27.0% to 43.1%), though 56.9% remained unidentified highlighting the limitations of third trimester ultrasounds. There was no significant increase in detection of SGA fetuses, and the specificity of identifying SGA decreased, reflecting the shift from scans performed for ‘clinical suspicion’ to routine scans.

A small but significant increase in the rate of labour induction (33.5% vs. 38.2%) was observed following policy implementation. This shift was larger towards ‘early term’ rather than ‘late term’ induction, with the most significant reason for ‘early term’ induction being identification of an SGA fetus.

Significant changes in neonatal outcomes were not observed post policy implementation, although there was a trend towards delivering smaller babies, with reduced rates of macrosomia and increased babies < 10th centile. The study was underpowered to detect decreases in stillbirths. There was a 1% increase in rates of admission to NICU which would equate to an additional 150 ‘bed days’ in an obstetric unit caring for 5000 women per year. However, this remained within the normal year‐to‐year variability observed in our NICU.

The poor sensitivity of routine scans for detecting SGA fetuses is disappointing in real‐world clinical settings. Interest in the value of routine third trimester scans was rekindled by Sovio et al. who found the sensitivity of EFW for predicting SGA infants increased from 20% in the selective scan group to 57% in the routine scan group, with 90% specificity and a 35% PPV [3]. Our findings differ, reflecting the difference in specificity of the test (98% in our cohort), potentially related to use of a single third trimester scan. Recent clinical trials by Henrichs et al. and Aderoba et al. have not observed an improvement in perinatal outcomes with routine ultrasounds, similar to this implementation study [5, 15].

Improvement in detection of LGA babies (27.0% pre‐policy vs. 43.1% post‐policy) was accompanied by a slight reduction in specificity (95.9% pre‐policy vs. 94.3% post‐policy). This aligns with Sovio et al. and Khan et al. who demonstrated increased LGA detection with routine third trimester ultrasounds [21, 22]. LGA fetuses are at higher risk of intrauterine fetal death and traumatic birth [23]. Though early induction at 37–38 weeks can reduce risk of shoulder dystocia, the number needed to treat is high, with 1 in 60 cases benefiting from this intervention [24]. Therefore, while routine ultrasounds may improve LGA detection, the impact on perinatal outcomes remains unclear [25].

National Australian health policies aiming to reduce stillbirth (Safer Baby Bundle) whilst simultaneously reducing late preterm and early term births (Preterm Birth Alliance) frequently present a clinical dilemma about ongoing management [26, 27]. In our study, routine ultrasound was not followed by increased preterm births; however, a higher number of planned early term deliveries was observed. Indications for induction were commonly related to perceived fetal growth anomalies (SGA or LGA) or reduced fetal movement, as anticipated.

The main limitation of this study is the lack of causal inference which can be drawn due to our study design. The lack of a comparison group or multivariate statistical analysis to adjust for confounders precludes us from attributing observed changes directly and solely to the routine ultrasound policy. For example, changes in the population prevalence rates of SGA and LGA can influence the detection rates. Moreover, temporal changes in practice due to studies like HYPITAT, DIGITAT, and ARRIVE may increase the likelihood of an obstetrician to induce labour in the early term period during this study [28, 29, 30]. The upward trend in the induction of labour mirrors statewide and national patterns, with reported annual increases of approximately 4%–5% in overall induction rates. In addition, differences in baseline maternal characteristics—particularly increasing maternal age and higher BMI (post policy cohort)—are known to further contribute to higher induction rates [31]. The escalation in inductions for small‐for‐gestational‐age (SGA) and large‐for‐gestational‐age (LGA) fetuses—ranging from 10% to 15% on average—also aligns with national trajectories, underscoring a broader shift in obstetric practice towards earlier intervention in growth‐related concerns [32]. Another limitation of this study was the inability to obtain records of third‐trimester ultrasound scans performed outside our institution. Preliminary checks indicated that only a small number of participants in either cohort underwent external imaging; therefore, we believe the potential impact of this limitation on the study findings is minimal.

This study is strengthened by relatively large sample size, high‐quality standardised scans performed by skilled sonographers and comprehensive electronic records used to assess a broad range of outcomes in an unselected population. Moreover, the value of our study arises from examining implementation outcomes when universal third trimester ultrasounds are translated from clinical trial settings into health service settings. For example, we demonstrated up to 20% of women in the ‘routine’ scan cohort had no documented scan, as they either declined or obtained scans outside the hospital systems. Limited uptake of ultrasounds in clinical practice may limit impacts observed in clinical trials. This is highly relevant given current evidence for routine ultrasounds remains limited, yet clinical practice has moved towards third trimester ultrasounds for most pregnant women [6]. Future research to improve perinatal outcomes should explore mechanisms behind late pregnancy complications, such as fetal growth restriction and stillbirth, incorporating biomarkers and fetal and placental Dopplers. A formal health‐economic evaluation would be valuable to determine the cost‐effectiveness and resource implications of implementing routine third trimester ultrasound screening for adverse pregnancy outcomes; however, this analysis was beyond the scope of the current manuscript. Additional benefits of routine third trimester ultrasound, such as the identification of malpresentation to allow timely external cephalic version or caesarean section should also be considered in such analysis.

In conclusion, our study observed improved detection of LGA fetuses, minimal improvement in detection of SGA fetuses, increased induction of labour and no observed changes in preterm births or adverse neonatal outcomes following implementation of a routine third trimester ultrasound policy. These findings have important implications for current policies recommending third trimester ultrasounds for most pregnant women.
