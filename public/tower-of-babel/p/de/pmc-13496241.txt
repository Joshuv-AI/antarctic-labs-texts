Hormonal, clinical, and survival outcomes in horses and ponies diagnosed with pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction in primary care practice
Authors: Emma Stapley, Caitrin Lowndes, Ece Inanc, Caroline Gillespie-Harmon, Sarah Waxman, Amanda Farr, Andrew van Eps, François-René Bertin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVariables associated with hormonal, clinical, and survival outcomes of equids with pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction (PPID) are incompletely investigated.Hypothesis/ObjectivesEvaluate the effect of initial hormone concentrations, clinical signs, treatment, and owner-related variables on outcomes in horses and ponies with PPID.AnimalsMedical records from 213 horses and ponies diagnosed with PPID by 2 university-based primary care practices.MethodsAnalytical observational cohort study. Variables associated with adrenocorticotropic hormone normalization, clinical improvement, and survival after diagnosis were assessed with Fisher’s exact tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. Stepwise backward multivariable logistic regression models were performed to determine variables independently associated with outcomes of interest with report of odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs.ResultsAge at diagnosis (OR: 0.90 [95% CI, 0.84-0.97]; P = .003) and number of months between tests (OR: 0.96 [95% CI, 0.92-0.99]; P = .003) were associated with hormonal normalization after diagnosis. A body condition score (BCS) between 4 and 6/9 at diagnosis (OR: 15.07 [95% CI, 2.17-238.5]; P = .004) and median household income in the county of residence/1000 (MHI, OR: 1.17 [95% CI, 1.02-1.42]; P = .02) were associated with clinical improvement after diagnosis. Age at diagnosis (OR: 0.91 [95% CI, 0.85-0.98]; P = .01) and MHI (OR: 1.04 [95% CI, 1.01-1.07]; P = .02) were associated with survival after diagnosis.Conclusions and clinical importanceDiagnosis at a younger age, frequent monitoring, a healthy BCS, and the owner’s financial means were associated with improved outcomes in horses managed for PPID.

Introduction

Pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction (PPID) is a common neurodegenerative condition in geriatric equids, affecting approximately 20% of horses over 15 years of age with equine practitioners managing a median of 5 animals with PPID.1,2 Restoration of tonic control of the pars intermedia through administration of dopamine receptor agonists improves hormone concentrations in 44-74% of cases and clinical signs in 40-100% cases.2,3 The formulation of pergolide labeled for equine use (Prascend, Boehringer Ingelheim) has been used extensively since its approval in 2011 and new equine formulations of dopamine receptor agonists, such as cabergoline, have become available more recently.4 The degenerative nature of PPID necessitates lifelong therapy.5 Careful monitoring of hormonal and clinical responses to therapy and dosage adjustments are required to keep pace with progressive pituitary disease and to minimize the effect of PPID on an animal’s quality of life and risk of euthanasia.5,6 Survival times after diagnosis range from 3.6 to 4.5 years, although much longer periods are possible.6,7 Ultimately, 47.7-73% of cases are euthanized for reasons related to PPID, although euthanasia might not occur at a younger age compared to geriatric horses without PPID.7–9 While PPID represents a common and dynamic clinical challenge for equine practitioners and treatment options have remained unchanged for over a decade, variables associated with hormonal, clinical, and survival outcomes are incompletely described, especially in primary care practice.2,6,10

Numerous variables appear to affect the course and outcome of PPID management, including horse and pony signalment, overall husbandry, and the owner’s willingness or ability to comply with treatment recommendations.2,6,10–12 Studies have found that 52% of UK owners were noncompliant with pergolide dosing recommendations, with owners reporting high levels of emotional care burden with any chronic condition in geriatric horses.10,11 In addition, financial constraints accounted for 67% of cases where PPID was untreated.2 Worse treatment compliance was documented in animals ≥ 26 years of age and in Shetland ponies.10 The same study found that hormonal control was less likely to be achieved in Shetland ponies.10 Studies investigating variables associated with survival have primarily used referral populations and found either no variables independently associated with survival or that being a pony, having a higher body condition score (BCS), and being treated with pergolide are associated with survival.7,12 Improving the current understanding of how horse, owner, and clinicopathologic variables affect treatment outcomes for PPID in primary care settings will directly benefit veterinary–client communications, client expectations and motivation for compliance, as well as the welfare of affected horses.

The objective of this study was to document variables independently associated with hormonal, clinical, and survival outcomes in horses and ponies diagnosed with PPID in 2 primary care practices with diverse clienteles. It was hypothesized that owner- and treatment-related variables would have a significant association with outcomes of interest.

Materials and methods

Animals

Medical records from 2 university equine field services were searched for horses and ponies (referred to collectively as “horses”) diagnosed with PPID by a veterinarian. Both practices were in the Northern Hemisphere with Purdue University listed as practice 1 and the New Bolton Center, University of Pennsylvania listed as practice 2. Only horses diagnosed with PPID based on seasonally adjusted adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) concentrations at baseline or after thyrotropin releasing hormone (TRH) stimulation were included; both practices used only the 10-min post-TRH time point. Donkeys and mules were excluded.

Records were reviewed and signalment (the year of birth listed in the medical record, breed, sex, and color) and location (county) were recorded. For complex coat colors, only the base coat color was recorded. Test-related data were recorded from the first test through the end of November of 2024. For each test, the date, reason for testing and clinical signs of PPID, test type, test results, BCS, test interpretation, and treatment type and amount were recorded. Reasons for testing were categorized as coat changes, insulin dysregulation, age, abnormal behavior, recurrent infection, lameness, weight loss, abnormal sweating, reproductive disorder, and presence of polyuria/polydipsia. Animals were classified as being close to an ideal BCS (4-6/9) or not.13 Based on housing location, the median annual household income of the county in which each horse was kept was determined from the United States Census Bureau (2023) records.

Hormonal normalization was defined as one or more subsequent tests with an ACTH concentration considered normal on seasonally adjusted reference ranges.14–16 Clinical improvement was defined as any record of resolution of clinical signs or subjective clinical improvement reported by or to the veterinarian.14–16 Survival was defined as a horse alive at the end of November 2024.

An Immulite 1000 chemiluminescent assay (Siemens) was used for all testing; tests were run at the practice’s diagnostic laboratory for practice 1; for practice 2, samples were shipped to an outside diagnostic laboratory before 2023 and run at the practice from 2023 onward.

Data analysis

A full description of data analysis is provided in the Supplementary material. Briefly, Shapiro–Wilk tests were used to assess normality. Univariable analysis consisted of unpaired t-tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, and Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. Multivariable analysis was performed as a backward stepwise logistic regression model with P < .2 for inclusion and P < .05 and P > .05 on a Box–Tidwell test for retention in the final model with fit evaluated via Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit tests. Practice was retained in all models. All analyses were performed using commercially available software.

Results

Animals and practice demographics

A total of 213 animals met the inclusion criteria; of these, 124/213 (58.2%) were managed at practice 1 and 89/213 (41.8%) were managed at practice 2. The median year of birth was 2000 [1996-2004]. Median age at diagnosis was 21 [18-25] years. Horses were diagnosed at an older age at practice 1 (23 [19-26] vs 20 [17-23], P = .001). Horses included in the study consisted of 123/213 (57.7%) geldings, 84/213 (39.4%) mares, and 6/213 (2.8%) stallions. There was no difference in sex distribution between the 2 practices (P = .2). Breed types included 76/213 (35.7%) stock-type breeds, 50/213 (23.5%) pony-type breeds, 21/213 (9.9%) Arabians, 20/213 (9.4%) Thoroughbreds, 19/213 (8.9%) Warmbloods, 17/213 (8.0%) American non-stock type breeds (Morgans, Tennessee Walking Horses, Paso Finos, Saddlebreds, etc.), 7/213 (3.3%) draft-type breeds, and 3/213 (1.4%) that were classified as “other.” Breeds differed significantly between the 2 practices with an overrepresentation of stock-type horses at practice 1 (P = .003, Supplementary Table 1). At diagnosis, 93/213 (43.7%) of horses had a recorded BCS, with a median BCS of 5/9 [4/9-7/9] and a median |BCS at diagnosis − 5| of 1 [1-2].

Median annual household income by county of residence was 65 345 [55 728-103 038] USD per year and was significantly lower at practice 1 (55 728 [55 728-62 014] USD/year vs 118 574 [89 390-118 574] USD/year, P < .0001).

Diagnosis

The reason for initial testing could not be determined in 59/213 (27.7%) animals. Of horses with recorded reasons for initial testing, 93/153 (60.8%) were tested for a single reason and 60/153 (39.2%) were tested for multiple reasons. Reasons for initial testing included hypertrichosis or delayed shedding (62/153, 40.5%), presence or suspicion of insulin dysregulation (including regional adiposity, recorded abnormal insulin concentrations on baseline or dynamic testing, and hyperinsulinemia-associated laminitis, 41/153, 26.8%), non-laminitic lameness (including subsolar abscessation and desmitis, 29/153, 19.0%), weight loss or muscle atrophy (27/153, 17.7%), age-related screening (25/153, 16.3%), behavioral changes (9/153, 5.9%), polyuria and polydipsia (4/153, 2.6%), reproductive abnormalities or aberrant lactation (3/153, 2.0%), and abnormal sweating (anhidrosis in both cases, 2/153, 1.3%).

Baseline ACTH concentrations were used as the only test in 112/213 (52.6%) animals; of these 51/112 (45.5%) were diagnosed from December to June, 13/112 (11.6%) were diagnosed in July and November, 14/112 (12.5%) were diagnosed in August, and 33/112 (29.5%) were diagnosed in September and October. The remaining 101/213 (47.4%) were diagnosed with a TRH stimulation test. A diagnosis was made based on the results of a TRH stimulation test collected between July and November in 23/101 (22.8%) horses. Of horses diagnosed based on the results of TRH stimulation tests, 30/101 (29.7%) had only a post-TRH sample recorded. Of animals with both pre- and post-TRH ACTH concentrations, 50/71 (70.4%) had a normal pre-TRH ACTH concentration and an abnormal ACTH concentration only on the post-TRH time point. Baseline ACTH concentrations were higher at practice 1 (81.8 [44.0-193.0] pg/mL vs 41.4 [24.8-92.9] pg/mL, P < .0001). Horses were tested a median of 3 [2-5] total times over the course of their medical record. Of these, 61/213 (28.6%) had testing before diagnosis with ACTH concentrations that were considered normal, with a median of 1 [1-2] normal tests before being diagnosed with PPID. A median of 1 [0-3] follow-up test was performed after diagnosis. The median time between diagnosis and the first follow-up test was 7 [4-12] months and the median time between each subsequent follow-up test was 11 [5-13] months. A median of 2 [1-4] years of follow-up per horse was available. The total number of tests per horse was not significantly different between the 2 practices (P = .4).

Horses had an earlier median year of diagnosis at practice 1 (2022 [2019-2023] vs 2023 [2021-2024], P = .004) with increasing frequency of testing over the course of the study (Supplementary Table 2).

Treatment

Treatment with pergolide was documented for 163/213 (76.5%) animals at the time of diagnosis and at any point in 177/213 (83.1%). Horses were less likely to be treated at the time of diagnosis at practice 1 (71.0% vs 84.3%, P = .03) but were not less likely to receive treatment at any point (79.8% vs 87.6%, P = .1). Out of the horses with documented treatment at the time of diagnosis, 156/163 (95.7%) were treated with the labeled form of pergolide and 7/163 (4.3%) received a compounded form. A compounded form of pergolide was used at any point in 20/177 (11.3%) of horses with recorded treatment. The median starting dose of pergolide was 1 [0.5-1.0] mg/day. The dose of pergolide was increased once for 41/177 (23.2%) horses, twice for 15/177 (8.5%) horses, 3 times for 6/177 (3.4%) horses, and 4 times for 1/177 (0.5%) horse. The maximum dose of pergolide administered was 3 mg/day. The dose was lowered for 17/177 (9.6%) horses. No horse was recorded as being treated with cabergoline during the included period.

Survival

A total of 41/213 (19.3%) horses were euthanized during the included period. Median age at euthanasia was 25 [22-31] years. Of these, 10/41 (24.4%) did not have recorded treatment with pergolide. Horses survived a median of 25 [12-45] months after diagnosis. Reasons for euthanasia included any form of colic (10/41, 24.4%), being found down and unable to rise with no specific diagnosis (7/41, 17.1%), neoplasia (4/41, 9.8%), hyperinsulinemia-associated laminitis (3/41, 7.3%), other non-emergent reasons or quality-of-life concerns (7/41, 17.1%), other emergent reasons (6/41, 14.6%), and reasons that could not be determined from available records (5/41, 12.2%). The independent variables associated with survival were a younger age at diagnosis and a higher median household income of the county of residence (Table 6).

Discussion

In primary care practice, a greater age at diagnosis and a longer interval between diagnosis and the first recheck of ACTH concentrations decreased the odds of hormonal normalization, a BCS between 4 and 6/9 was associated with clinical improvement and a greater age at diagnosis decreased the odds of survival. In addition, a higher median annual household income in the county of residence was independently associated with clinical improvement and survival.

A greater age at diagnosis and a longer duration between diagnosis and reevaluation of ACTH concentrations were associated with decreased odds of hormonal normalization. This could support the finding that ACTH concentrations increase with age, particularly in autumn months.17 However, due to the progressive, neurodegenerative nature of PPID, horses diagnosed at a younger age would generally be expected to have fewer dysfunctional melanotropes and a lower threshold for adequate regulation.18,19 Scaling the starting dose of pergolide to the age and likely degree of disease progression has not been recommended as dopamine D2 receptor expression increases with increasing pituitary histologic grade, likely resulting in more targets for dopamine receptor agonists.3,5,6,20 However, the current study suggests that animals diagnosed at an older age might warrant more rigorous monitoring of an individual’s hormonal response to therapy. The association between a shorter time from diagnosis to follow-up testing and hormonal normalization could simply represent a greater number of opportunities for documentation of a response to dopamine receptor agonists or more opportunities to adjust an inadequate pergolide dose.3,5,6,10,12 While not a direct aim of this study, it is clinically relevant that baseline ACTH concentrations were within seasonally adjusted reference ranges in 70.4% of horses where PPID was diagnosed based on the post-TRH ACTH concentration. This appears to be higher than previously reported in horses with overt clinical signs.21,22 This implies that dynamic testing should take precedence over baseline testing in the face of subtle clinical signs as detecting the disease at earlier stages appears associated with more successful hormonal control.

Higher BCS is associated with survival, and it is likely that the association between BCS at diagnosis and subsequent clinical improvement reflects numerous facets of adequate husbandry.12 A more direct link to body composition and outcomes might exist as comorbid obesity would potentiate reduced insulin sensitivity and increased systemic oxidative stress.23–26 Similarly, greater lean muscle mass suggests sufficient exercise to increase insulin sensitivity and to reduce the risk of soft tissue injury with appropriate exercise and overall core strength.23,26–29 While the effects of treatment could not be statistically determined as the majority of horses were treated with pergolide, these findings confirm that successful management of PPID cannot rely on dopamine receptor agonists as a monotherapy and that an evaluation and refinement of all aspects of husbandry and exercise protocols to promote healthy body composition would provide the best clinical outcomes. Neither ACTH concentration nor clinical signs at the time of diagnosis were independently associated with survival, with age as the only horse-related variable associated with survival. The median age at euthanasia was consistent with the median age at euthanasia in geriatric horses across various populations.8,9,30 Age is a risk factor for non-survival in geriatric horses, with the risk of death or euthanasia increasing substantially after 19 years of age in general equine populations and after 15 years of age with critical illness.8,30–32 While PPID might contribute to the decision to euthanize an animal in many cases, it could be less influential than the direct effects of the aging process and the owner’s perceptions of an animal’s increasing age.8,9,11,33

While median household income by county of residence was a rough proxy for actual owner income, it was independently associated with both clinical improvement and survival.34,35 The link between owner income and equid outcomes is widely recognized but poorly documented in equine practice, and limited data from small animal practice indicate that financial factors can substantially affect owners’ decisions regarding veterinary medicine.36 Extensive data in human medicine support that income is directly correlated with rates of chronic illness and unhealthy behaviors, overall life expectancy, and survival rates in critical illness.37–40 Although median household income by county is a rough indicator of owners’ likely financial means and socioeconomic environment, it represents a first step at quantifying the association between an owner’s financial status and outcomes in large animal veterinary medicine. The association with clinical improvement and income likely reflects the owner’s ability to provide adequate veterinary care, to promptly diagnose and treat PPID and any comorbid medical conditions, and to provide an overall higher standards of husbandry.2,10,12,36,41–44 The association between income and survival is consistent with surveyed equine veterinarians reporting financial constraints as a major factor when making recommendations for euthanasia.45,46 While reasons for euthanasia in the current study were similar in type and frequency to previous reports in general geriatric populations, 56.1% of euthanized horses in the current study would have required immediate, costly medical intervention while many of the non-emergent reasons for euthanasia would likely have required more expensive therapies to maintain an acceptable quality of life.8,9,30 There is also evidence that owners would be less likely to pursue emergent treatment for an animal with any existing chronic illness.30 Owner surveys rarely list finances as a significant factor when considering cost of long-term therapy or treatment for critical conditions, particularly in noncompetitive animals, but owner-reported maximum willingness-to-pay dollar amounts for emergency treatment are lower than what is likely to be feasible at most referral centers; for example, a maximum of USD 5000 for colic surgery in a 2025 study.30,45,47–49 There was marked disparity in median, maximum, and range of household income by county between the 2 practices, with practice 1 working with a narrower and lower socioeconomic range based on the counties within their catchment area. This likely created significantly different overall clinical impressions of PPID between the 2 practices and emphasizes the need for caution when generalizing the results of single-center retrospective studies, particularly when the financial means of clients and monetary value of the animals is likely to be substantially different from other practices.

An additional finding of this study is that current recommendations, including those of the Equine Endocrinology Group, are not consistently implemented in primary care practice.50 For example, testing for PPID was frequently performed in the absence of compatible clinical signs, despite guidance discouraging this approach. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in practice culture and the evolving understanding of PPID over time, and highlight the need for continued veterinary and client education to support evidence-based decision-making.

The retrospective nature of this study meant that a substantial amount of information regarding reasons for initial and follow-up diagnostics, physical examination findings, diet, and treatment was unavailable. Additionally, information regarding animals diagnosed and managed based only on clinical signs were not available due to the structure of the medical records systems used by both practices, biasing results toward owners who were willing to invest in initial hormonal testing. Documentation of changes in clinical signs was likely biased toward animals where either significant deterioration or improvement occurred, and more subtle clinical improvements or declines were likely underreported. Defining ACTH improvement by a subsequent normal ACTH concentration likely underestimated the frequency of clinically relevant hormonal improvement particularly where ACTH concentrations could not be correlated with clinical signs due to inconsistent reporting of clinical signs.51,52 The inclusion of multiple practices offers the possibility of varying attitudes toward testing and treatment as well as the clustering of cases, but mandating retention of “Practice” in the final models reduced this risk.

This study highlights the importance of early and consistent monitoring of ACTH concentrations and maintenance of systemic health in achieving clinical improvement in horses and ponies with PPID. This study demonstrates that owner finances have a significant effect on outcomes for life-threatening emergent or chronic medical conditions in geriatric horses, and that age and finances might play a greater role in treatment outcomes and euthanasia of geriatric horses than PPID status and severity.
