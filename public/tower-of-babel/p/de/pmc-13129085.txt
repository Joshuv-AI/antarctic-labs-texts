Nutrient exploration and heavy metal risk assessment of baby milk and infant formulae sold within Umuahia metropolis, Nigeria
Authors: Maryann Nkoli Mgbemena, Innocent Nwazulu Okwunodulu, Felicia Uchechukwu Okwunodulu, Mary Onuchi Mac-kalunta, Helen Okorie-Chukwuemeka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNaturally, neonates as well as infants in the society are the most susceptible health effects of malnutrition and heavy metal risks due to their inevitable rapid development of their organs and tissues for proper growth. It is therefore critical to investigate the conformity of their foods and formulae to the regulatory standard limits to avert the associated risks. This work hence focused on the evaluation of proximate, mineral and heavy metals concentrations (risk factor) in Baby and Infant Formulae sold within Umuahia, Abia State, Nigeria. Eight different Baby Milk and twelve Infant Formulae samples from different manufacturers were procured and analysed for their nutrient and heavy metal compositions using standard methods. Results of proximate (%) analysis showed the ranges as: moisture content (MC) (3.24–6.56), ash (2.42–2.65), fibre (0.00–0.00), fat (11.32–28.68), protein (1152 − 16.20) and carbohydrate (50.52–69.27) for Baby Milk. For Infant Formulae, the ranges include MC (7.89–9.29), ash (1.35–2.99), fibre (0.19–6.49), fat (0.47–9.75), protein (2.92–20.88) and carbohydrate (52.21–84.91). Carbohydrate had superior value as ranked: carbohydrate > fat > Protein > Moisture > ash > fibre in Baby Milk and carbohydrate > protein > MC > fat > ash > fibre for Infant Formulae. Baby milk minerals (mg/kg) ranged as: lead (0.07–0.14), zinc (2.80-24.03), iron (30.00-42.01), manganese (0.19–1.24), nickel (0.03–0.26), cupper (0.06–0.31), cadmium (0.01–0.21) and chromium (0.00-0.210). While Infant Milk ranged as: lead (0.00-0.015), zinc (1.38–6.87), iron (2.2–14.8), manganese (0-153-2.430), nickel (0-003-0.04), cupper (0.23–0.83), cadmium (0.01–0.04) and chromium (0.000-0.006). In Baby Milk, the macro-minerals ranked thus: P > Ca > Mg > K > Na while the Infant Formula ranked as Mg > Ca > K > P > Na. The micro-minerals of both samples ranked same as: Fe > Zn > Mn. Heavy metal risk assessment showed that only cadmium and chromium (< 0.2–0.3) posed health risk in some Baby Milk samples unlike infant Formula. Most foods contain adequate nutrients without risk, but regular checks at intervals is required during their manufacturing by the regulatory agencies to avoid possible potential heavy metal risk factor and malnutrition among babies and infants.Keywords: Baby foods, Nutrient profiles, Heavy metal levels, Risk assessmentSubject terms: Molecular biology, Physiology

Introduction

Breast milk is universally recognized as best and first for feeding babies and infants, as it provides readily bioavailable nutrients in a well-balanced supply while ensuring optimal growth and development of the child1,2. Breast milk is considered as the best natural source of nutrition for infants as it contains ideal and required balance of immunoglobulin, fats, carbohydrates, proteins, and vitamins, as well as digestive enzymes that prevent infections and diseases in infants. The importance of breast milk and breastfeeding cannot be over emphasized3 as it protects infants from common causes of infant mortality and diseases like diarrhoea and upper respiratory infections4. Adequate breast feeding lowers the risk of developing such conditions as enterocolitis or sepsis and strengthens immune systems among infants and babies2. The World Health Organization (WHO), UNICEF, and the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)5 recommended that infants should receive breast milk exclusively for at least the first six months of their lives so as to properly establish a healthy immune, nervous, digestive and reproductive systems as well as strong physical body structure6,7. Additionally, infants should receive adequate, timely, safe, and appropriate complementary foods while continuing breastfeeding.

Despite the 6 months exclusive breastfeeding recommendations, less than 40% of infants below six months are exclusively breastfed worldwide8. However, not all mothers are able to abide by this recommendation for their infants, hence the need for alternative and adequate replacement of breast milk like infant formulae. Such alternatives are beneficial to mothers who do not lactate or lactate for the short period of time after birth, mothers with infectious diseases that can be transmitted through breast milk9. Also, are mothers with latching on the breast, working mothers both in the developed and developing countries, as well as infants whose mother died after birth or those with dietary restrictions due to conditions such as lactose intolerance10,11. Other factors like rapid urbanization, advanced life style and frequency of eating by the infants can contribute to the adoption of infant formulae so as to meet the nutritional needs of infants and babies12,13.

Infant formula, baby formula or just formula (American English) or baby milk, infant milk or first milk, are manufactured foods designed and marketed for feeding babies and infants less than 12 months of age9. It is usually prepared for bottle-feeding or cup-feeding from powder (mixed with water) or liquid (with or without additional water). Infant formulae used as complement or substitute for breast milk are the best alternative for child development compared to other unprocessed food sources because they can be tailor made provide adequate nutrition. Infant formulae are primarily derived from animals or plants and are therefore mostly milk, soy or cereal-based formulations. Unquestionably, the use of formulae can be advantageous for the proper growth and development of children. However, they are rated second best to natural breast milk, and possible risks associated with the use of artificial formulae have been reported14. They are products from cow’s milk and other animals or vegetables or a mixture of these15. Most are dairy milk substitutes for the mother’s milk intended for children aged between 6 and 12 months which is the period of supplementary feeding16. Cow’s milk is the primary ingredient most often used in the manufacturing of these products. However, infant formula manufacturers seek to make cow’s milk nutritional characteristics closer to human milk by chemically adjusting their macro and micronutrient composition17–19. Infant formulae are embodiments and good sources of proteins, fats, vitamin supplements and major minerals of children and are therefore, required to be a source of essential metals20,21.

Some infant formulae are made with cereals which are introduced within the complementary period of six months, when the child’s birth weight is expected to have doubled and breast milk alone is no longer sufficient to meet the nutritional needs of the child. Some cereals used include wheat, rice, oats, barley, millets, and sorghum among others. They contain significant source of macro and micro nutrients that varies between the cereal types, processing and preparation methods. A concerted effort is being made to improve the macro and micro nutrients in cereals by groups of manufacturers of babies’ formula and other organisations like WHO and News Agency among others which in 2021 reported the need for monitoring cereal flour fortification to maximize health benefits22. The News Agency reported that flour fortification is the practice of deliberately increasing the content of one or more essential micronutrients in the flour. Fortification of wheat and maize flours with vitamins and minerals is considered a cost–effective strategy to address micronutrient malnutrition and nutrition associated with health challenges such as anaemia or the prevention of neural tube defects. In the face of this, WHO resolved to accelerate efforts on food micronutrient fortification. This resolution was adopted so as to prevent micronutrient deficiencies through safe and effective food fortification. The resolution urges member states to adopt same as micronutrient (folate, iron, vitamin A, and zinc) deficiencies affected 50% of all preschool aged children and 67% of all women of reproductive age (WRA) worldwide23.

Certain macronutrients (P, Ca, Mg, Na, K, and Cl) needed in relatively high concentrations in human diets are necessary for healthy body development, growth, metabolism, participation in many physiological processes, and maintenance of body electrolytes and tissue homeostasis. Calcium and phosphorous in particular are significant components of human bones, teeth, and connective tissues24. Infant and baby formulae continue to be major sources of energy and nutrients (micro and macro) on a global scale. Conversely, essential micronutrients (Co, Cr, Cu, F, Fe, I, Mn, Ni, Se, Si, Sn, V, and Zn) are needed in trace levels for healthy body growth and tissue regeneration. They are present in numerous metalloproteinase and crucial enzymes that regulate biological processes. Various illnesses and disorders in humans have long been linked to deficiencies of these components. As a result, they are typically consumed as food supplements or added in trace amounts to foods for humans. However, fortification above the recommended threshold (tolerable intake level) poses a health risk/challenge to infants25, who are more vulnerable group as their excretory capacity is lower than that of an adult, and their body weight is less with a weaker immune system26. Most of these micronutrients are heavy metals which are known for their high specific density (5 g/cm3) and greater atomic weight (40.04 g). They do not only have adverse effects on the ecosystems but also on living organisms especially babies and infants who are the more vulnerable groups27–29.

In Nigerian, the government is presently concerned with food-safety contamination of powdered milk/infant formula with heavy metals, mycotoxins, some microbiological and other contamination. Some heavy metal contaminants of interest (Pb, Cd, As, Hg, Al, Cr, Co, Ni), have been detected repeatedly in various infant foods based on estimated daily intakes and hazard quotients. Their concentrations have raised concern for infants’ long-term neurological and renal health among others in some surveys. Aflatoxins residues (AFM₁), an infant vulnerable carcinogen, coliforms, mesophilic bacteria, Salmonella, E. coli as well as acrylamide (industrial contaminants) had also been detected in some milk/powder and on locally repackaged ones in Cross River, Zamfara, Sokoto and other states of Nigeria through routine microbiological hazards analysis.

Hazard Quotients (HQ) is employed in risk-assessment approaches used in Nigerian. It is used in measuring the heavy metals, mycotoxins and other contaminant concentrations as well as application of deterministic exposure calculations based on infant body weight and consumption patterns to compute Estimated Daily Intake (EDI). NAFDAC, SONS, ISO among others used set standards to regulate breastfeeding and protect as well as controlling marketing of breast-milk substitutes. These notwithstanding, studies alongside review documents exposed continued violations of the WHO International Code in Nigeria in the areas of promoting practices that undermine breastfeeding and increase formula use resulting in exposure to infant health risk.

These violations result in some highlighted problem statements. The surveillance is mostly of small sizes; either fragmented, geographically, regional, small-N, or single-batch surveys without systematic, nationally representative surveillance program for baby food contaminants (heavy metals, mycotoxins, PFAS, microbiological hazards) across the formal and informal supply chains. Most survey involved limited exposure assessment rigor which stopped only at deterministic HQ. Weak compliance enforcement by NAFDAC and other enforcement agencies on market surveillance, and control of informal repackaging result in various product quality variability and marketing pressures. Poor laboratories standards and capacity with a good routine monitoring programs and standardized sampling/analysis protocols are lacking to deliver the representative, quality-assured data needed for national risk management decisions.

It becomes imperative that substantial efforts should be made to formulate foods that are rich in nutrients devoid of heavy metals for possible use as breast milk substitutes especially in developing countries like Nigeria. Therefore concerted efforts should be made, may be in form of national policies to ensure that manufacturers should produce baby foods with adequate nutrients (not above or below the RDI). This work therefore explored the proximate, minerals and heavy metal risk factors in some Babies Milk and Infant food sold within Umuahia metropolis to investigate manufacturing compliance to regulatory standards.

Materials and methods

Source of materials

Eight Baby Milk (milk based) and twelve Infant Formulae (cereal based), all locally produced, were purchased from Shopright, and groceries within Umuahia, Abia State two years before the expiration of their shelf life.

Sample collection, transportation and storage

One container sample each of both or either brand depending on availability was purchased in their sealed containers randomly at different dates from different shop locations within Umuahia metropolis. They were transported immediately to the Departmental laboratory of Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Michael Okpara university of Agriculture, Umudike, for storage in the refrigerator and subsequent analyses.

Samples preparation for laboratory analysis

In the laboratory, similar brands of the baby foods were opened, an aliquot scoped into separate air tight containers, mixed together for true representativeness, labelled, and quickly taken to the food hygiene and control laboratory for the analyses. The remaining samples were immediately closed and stored in the refrigerator. Each baby food served as a sample.

Sample size, sampling method and sampling procedure

One sample of each or both brands was purchased from each shop visited depending on availability. The sampling was made at interval of one month for three months and stored in the refrigerator, thereafter were prepared for analyses.

Study area

This study was carried out within Umuahia metropolis, strictly within Umuahia North local Government Area as shown in the map below. Umuahia is within Latitude 5.525oN and Longitude 7.492oE. It is the capital city of Abia State lying almost in the central southern part of Abia State along the railway line connecting Port Harcourt in the south and Enugu in the north. Determination of heavy minerals, micro-minerals

Micro elements were determined using the methods of30,31.

Heavy metals

All the heavy metals (Pb, Ni, Cu, Cd, Cr) were determined following the methods of32, 2.0 g of each sample (Babies’ Milk and Infant Formula) powder were weighed into a clean dry beaker in a fume cupboard. 20 mL of aqua-regia (mixture of concentrated HCl and HNO3, ratio (3:1 ratio) was added to the sample in the beaker. The beaker was covered with a clean dry watch glass and heated at 90 °C for 2 h, allowed to cool, washed together with the watch glass using de-ionized water into a volumetric flask and made-up to 100 mL solution. The solution was filtered and supernatant liquid solution was used for heavy metal analysis using Atomic Absorption Spectrometer (Perkin Elmer instrument, Analyst Model 400).

Data analysis

Data obtained from all the analyses were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA). A completely randomized design using the SPSS version 22.0 was used while the treatment means were separated using Duncan multiple range test at 95% confidence level (p < 0.05).

The moisture levels of the entire samples were generally low (< 10%) connoting better shelf stability with the BM samples having an edge provided they are packed in air-tight containers precluding oxygen and water vapour. Notwithstanding, the moisture content of the entire IF samples (7.89–9.13%) were higher than those of BM (3.24–6.56%) probably due to the raw materials used and the processing technique. Furthermore, low moisture content is an indication of high dry matter which signified nutrient density. The narrow range of moisture contents of both BM [3.24 (C) to 6.56% (B)] and IF [7.89 (I) to 9.29% (J)] could be interpreted as similarity in the choice of raw material and processing technique adopted. Besides, their values were within the safe limit (< 10%) as it will not be available for microbial spoilage of both products. Their moisture contents were within the Codex standard for BM, while those of IF were above the value of 5% (39). Higher moisture content (> 10%) may be subject to microbial degradation that will reduce the shelf-life span of the food, bad odour, unacceptable taste and rejection of the product. Consumption of such microbial degraded formulae may have pronounced health consequences on infants35.

The ash content of the entire is generally low, but most of the BM samples (2.42–3.1%) were higher than those of IF (1.35–2.99%). As ash is mineral indicator, it implied that the BM may likely have higher minerals than the IF samples. The entire samples had narrow ash contents range, [2.42 (G) to 3.17% (F)] for BM and [1.35 (M) to 2.99% (T)] for IF which is wider. The wider range could be due to variations in their processing methods and the raw materials used by different manufacturing companies. Their ash values were generally low which portend low mineral content28 and may likely affect growth in babies and infants when consumed over time. Infants’ stage is dynamic and requires a lot of appropriate functional minerals for their proper growth and development such as calcium for their strong bones and teeth, iron for blood, zinc for healing and immune booster. Bamidele et al.35 also reported similar low ash range of 0.36 to 3.09% for infant formula which also may not meet the infant mineral daily requirements.

The fat content of the BM varied widely from 11.32 (C) to 28.92% (G) compared to 0.47 (S) to 9.75% (J) from IF. The lower fat contents of the IF compared to the BM could be attributed to the variations in the raw materials used and processing methods employed. Fat is fundamental in providing energy, improving the food quality and enhancing the flavour provided the packaging precludes rancidity. Furthermore, the fat soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) are more bioavailable with fatty foods compared to low fat food counterparts36. The fat levels in the work is therefore acceptable Despite this, excess fat soluble vitamins have been reported to be toxicity and could be deleterious to human health37.

Fibre was not detected in the entire BM but was varied widely [0.19 (S) to 6.49% (J)] (p < 0.05) among the IF which may justify wide variations in the raw materials and processing methods adopted. The absence of fibre in BM may be considered as nutrient deficiency but could be deliberate as it mimics breast milk which has no fibre. Besides, BM is the first food for the baby (< 6months) which sometimes goes along with the breast milk. Also, the baby’s digestive system is under developed which may not be able to digest fibre. Therefore the absence of fibre in BM aligned with baby’s nutrient requirement.

Protein contents of the BM samples had a moderate rang of 11.52 (H) to 16.20 (B) compared to those of IF indicating similarity of the raw materials and processing methods. While infant formula with a wider range of 2.92 (M) to 20.77 (J) may mean a wider variations in the raw materials and processing methods. The higher fat content (12.62–28.68%) of the BM compared to IF (0.47–9.75%) both of which varied significantly (p < 0.05) is an indication that BM may have contained more fat soluble vitamins than IF. Variations in the fat content of the various raw materials used may contribute as well which may have resulted in their higher fat values. These variations notwithstanding, the protein, range of 8.61 to 14.59% reported by35 is within their range.

Protein is one of the most essential food components required for normal growth, body development and general repair of body tissues35. Also, it is an energy substrate for the active infants. Therefore, the protein values obtained in this study is acceptable as high amount of protein is required at infant stage for cell formation and development as well as energy. Also, the high protein is fundamental in preventing protein-energy malnutrition prevalent in most developing countries of the world like Nigeria. However, adequate caution should also be taken as too much protein is detrimental. Recent studies showed that high protein content in IF is associated with excess weight gain, which can lead to a 20% risk of obesity later in life38. Fat boosts flavour, energy content as well as fat soluble vitamins of the reconstituted products.

High protein values of both samples indicated rich protein content liable to meet the protein (9.1–10.9 g) requirements of complementary foods39 per serving. The higher protein levels (11.52–16.20%) of most BM samples than IF (2.92–20.72%) proved that BM may have contained more protein rich raw materials than IF samples. Their protein levels will no doubt help to solve the problems of protein and protein-energy deficiency of active infants more prevalence in developing country like Nigeria. Both samples will support for baby and infant growth.

The IF samples had higher carbohydrate content compared to those of BM, but all varied within a narrow range (p < 0.05) which may indicate similarity in both raw materials and processing technique. The levels of carbohydrate for BM (50.52–72.92%) and (52.21–84.91%) for IF in this work are marginally higher than 54.86–80.49% reported by35 for IF from developed and developing countries. Carbohydrate is a good source of energy, its presence in food is an indication that the food is capable of supplying the daily energy requirement of the body40 and liable to prevent protein-energy malnutrition.

The concentration of heavy metals (Table 2) in BM varied in the order: Pb > Cu > Ni > Cd > Cr. In the face of these, their entire levels were below the permissible limit (0.2–0.7 mg/kg) of World Health Organisation23 with the exception of Pb in BM. Lead is a highly toxic heavy metal liable to cause health problems even at low levels, accumulates in the body especially bones over time and affects a lot of body systems especially the blood. Children are more susceptible to lead because of their developing bodies, poor immune system and increased exposure risk. Depending on their exposure level, and individual sustainability, high lead levels result memory and learning difficulties, anaemia, constipation among others. The lead levels in this study are mild to high levels which may likely affect some children within the Umuahia metropolis with some of these health issues. Chronic exposures to heavy metals like lead can lead to respiratory and cardiovascular system damage55,56.

Higher heavy metal values in milk powders could be from contamination during handling, processing and packaging44. Use of contaminated cow’s milk for manufacturing of infant milk products57 could be a factor if not properly processed. The contamination of cow’s milk could be due to excessive exposure of lactating cows to environments contaminated with lead or other heavy metals, consumption of contaminated water and feed stuffs58.

Moreover, raw milk may be contaminated from metallic lead soldered cans59. There are several sources of contamination of infant formula with heavy metals; among which include water used to mix infant powdered formula60. Also, studies have shown that contamination from the equipment during processing, packaging, and storage may be possible. So, it is necessary to control the manufacturing process at each step, in order to minimize the sources and levels of contamination and to ensure the desired product quality61.

Conclusion and recommendation

The results of proximate analysis of the Baby Milk and Infant Formula showed high carbohydrate contents in all the samples, followed by protein, fat, ash and moisture. Fibre was not presence in the Baby Milk unlike Infant Formula. Mineral contents of the entire Baby Milk were high and varied in the order Ca > Mg > P > K > Na and P > K > Mg > Ca > Na in Infant Formula. Micro elements were found in high amounts in all the samples. Risk assessment with Target Hazard Quotient showed that Chromium (Cr) has the highest heavy metal concentration followed by Cadmium, while copper had the least in Baby Milk samples. High cadmium concentrations in samples coded A and C may likely pose health risk. The risk assessment with Hazard Index identified coded sample C with the highest heavy metal concentration among the Baby Milk samples. The overall heavy metal risk assessed with Hazard Index implicated also cadmium with the highest risk factor among all the heavy metals found in the entire samples. Only trace amounts were found in the entire samples except in some Baby Milk samples where some samples were high enough to pose health risk.

The present of these contaminants as evidence for regular checks by the regulatory agencies should be intensified for compliance to regulatory standards to avoid possible heavy metal concentrations in baby’s foods as babies are more vulnerable to them because of their poor immune systems.
