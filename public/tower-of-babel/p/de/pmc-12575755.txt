A computer vision framework for proactive anomaly detection and risk reduction in airport baggage logistics
Authors: Kalyani Vidhate, Suraj Sawant, Sohan Chavan, Bhaveshkumar Vagadiya, Debayan Talapatra, Ranjeet Bidwe, Amit Joshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This research addresses the challenges in airport baggage handling, focusing on the automated detection of key components-bags, handles, straps-and the identification of damages such as cracks. The proposed system employs the YOLOv8 algorithm for object detection and instance segmentation, trained on a self-generated dataset of 2528 images. For bag accessory detection, the model achieved precision, recall, and F1-scores of 0.92, 0.88, and 0.90 for bags; 0.89, 0.94, and 0.91 for handles; and 0.74, 0.58, and 0.65 for straps, respectively. For damage (crack) detection, YOLOv8’s instance segmentation attained a precision of 0.75, recall of 0.80, F1-score of 0.77, and mean Average Precision (mAP) of 0.76. These results indicate robust detection performance for bags and handles, with scope for improvement in strap and damage detection. A novel aspect of this work is the integration of OpenAI GPT-4 Vision into the baggage inspection pipeline. GPT-4 Vision was employed to perform higher-level reasoning on the detection outputs-such as contextual verification of detected components, natural language description of detected damages, and flagging of anomalies-thereby complementing YOLOv8’s pixel-level predictions with semantic analysis. This hybrid approach enables not only precise localization of components and damages but also contextual interpretation, making the system more adaptable to real-world operational variability. Introduction

Airport baggage handling, a critical component of aviation operations, faces challenges leading to disruptions and financial losses1. The aviation industry incurs significant expenses due to damaged bags and requires solutions to mitigate this issue. Additionally, there is a risk of bag accessories, such as handles of airbags and duffle bag straps, getting stuck in the conveyor system. Thus, it is crucial to check for such potential problems regularly with the aid of appropriate technology. Deep Learning techniques, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), are well-suited for image recognition tasks and can significantly enhance baggage handling systems2. By training CNNs on vast datasets of baggage images, the system can accurately identify and track luggage throughout the airport. Additionally, DL models can be employed for anomaly detection, identifying irregularities such as open zippers, damaged bags, or obstructions caused by bag accessories on conveyor belts. These models can continuously learn and adapt, improving their accuracy over time. The implementation of DL techniques in airport baggage handling offers significant potential to address the industry’s current challenges3. By enhancing baggage identification, anomaly detection, and real-time monitoring, DL can reduce operational costs, minimize delays, and improve overall efficiency. As air travel demand continues to rise, the adoption of innovative technologies like DL is essential for maintaining a streamlined and effective baggage handling process4.

The travel industry has seen a big increase in mishandled baggage as it recovers from the pandemic5. In 2022, the mishandled baggage rate has nearly doubled from the previous year, reaching 7.60 bags per thousand passengers (SITA-2023). The industry has faced many challenges due to the pandemic, including not having enough skilled staff, which makes handling luggage at airports difficult6. So, there is an urgent need to make baggage processing more digital.

Recent data shows that 80% of all mishandled bags in 2022 were delayed, which is a 9% increase from the previous year7. Also, the percentage of lost and stolen bags went up to 7%, while damaged and pilfered bags decreased to 13%, a 10% drop from 2021. It’s important to reduce the overall costs of managing bags, so SITA(2023) is looking into futuristic bag tracking systems that don’t need bag tags, printers, or expensive readers. This new way would let us get data on where bags are at a much lower cost and could be used in management systems.

SITA’s(2021) Bag Vision technology is trying to use computer vision technology powered by artificial intelligence to match images of a bag taken during check-in and sorting. This means we don’t need a bag tag, so we save a lot of money. The Bag Vision technology uses cameras, which are cheaper and have a smaller environmental impact than the usual barcode or RFID ATRs and the bag tag printers, and it’s just as accurate. Baggage mishandling has been a big problem for aviation authorities for a long time. But by focusing on better baggage tracking and introducing the recent IATA’s Resolution 753, which says airlines have to track bags at the four main touchpoints (make-up, loading, transfer, and arrival) throughout the journey, we have been able to reduce the number of mishandled bags in the last few years. Companies are investing in making better baggage tracking technologies to get the data they need to follow Resolution 753. These investments are going to help the market grow in the next few years. More than 40% of airlines were using AI-driven systems for baggage handling by the end of 2022, which can make baggage handling more precise and effective.

Deep learning has emerged as a transformative approach in addressing the operational challenges of airport baggage handling by enabling real-time, high-accuracy detection and classification of baggage components. Unlike traditional image processing techniques, deep learning models can automatically learn discriminative features from large datasets, allowing them to adapt to variations in lighting, orientation, and background clutter commonly encountered in airport environments. In this study, we employ the YOLOv8 architecture, which is specifically optimized for high-speed object detection without compromising accuracy, making it suitable for real-time baggage inspection on conveyor systems. YOLOv8’s ability to perform end-to-end detection in a single forward pass enables the rapid identification of multiple baggage components-such as bags, handles, and straps-alongside damage detection, thereby reducing inspection latency and minimizing the risk of operational bottlenecks. By integrating YOLOv8 into the baggage inspection pipeline, this research not only addresses industry challenges like high mishandling rates and associated financial losses but also demonstrates the potential for deploying scalable, AI-driven solutions that enhance efficiency, reduce manual workload, and improve overall passenger experience.

This research aims to address the objective of the Utilization of AI algorithms to improve baggage handling performance and propose solutions to address the identification challenges of various bag accessories. Also the Implementation of deep learning techniques and neural network models to detect the damages in the bags, with the objective of establishing handling protocols for damaged bags.

Data collection and preprocessing

The YOLOv8 algorithm underwent training using a diverse dataset obtained from reputable repositories and platforms such as Roboflow, Kaggle, YouTube, iStock, Shutterstock, and AI Image Generators. Additionally, real-time images and videos of the baggage handling system were captured from the setup at India Innovation Center Vanderlande Industries Pvt. Ltd. Furthermore, real-world data was meticulously collected through the capture of photos from various shops, ensuring a comprehensive and varied dataset for robust model training.

Prior to training the YOLOv8 algorithm, the collected dataset underwent a series of meticulous preprocessing steps to optimize the model’s performance. Initially, automatic orientation adjustment was employed to standardize the orientation of images, ensuring consistency across the dataset. Subsequently, all images were meticulously resized to a standardized dimension of 640x640 pixels, guaranteeing uniformity and improving processing efficiency during training. To further enhance the model’s capacity for generalization, a variety of augmentation techniques were implemented. These included generating three outputs for each training example to encompass diverse perspectives of bags, handles, and straps, as well as applying both horizontal and vertical flips to introduce variability in object orientation. Moreover, images were deliberately rotated by 90 degrees in both clockwise and counter-clockwise directions to simulate different viewpoints. Additionally, to fortify the model’s resilience to noise, random blur with a maximum of 2 pixels was meticulously applied, and random noise up to 0.26% of pixels was introduced to realistically emulate real-world imperfections, thereby bolstering the model’s robustness. Data labeling and annotation

This process was conducted using the Roboflow platform, the annotated dataset of 1054 original images was meticulously labeled with three distinct classes: “bag,” “handle,” and “straps”. The “bag” label encompassed 1535 instances, representing various types and orientations of baggage items, each annotated with a bounding box. Similarly, the “handle” label comprised 610 instances, identifying handles attached to baggage items, while the “straps” label included 661 instances, representing the straps or fastening mechanisms. In total, there were 2806 instances across the three classes. Each image is precisely annotated with bounding boxes, ensuring accurate identification and localization of bags, handles, and straps within the dataset represented in Fig. 5

For damage detection, instance segmentation is employed for annotating damaged areas due to its ability to precisely outline the boundaries of individual objects within an image. This approach offers several advantages over traditional bounding box annotation, particularly in scenarios where damage may vary in shape, size, and orientation. By segmenting damaged regions at the pixel level, instance segmentation provides a finer level of detail, accurately capturing the contours and extent of each defect. This granularity is crucial for training machine learning models like YOLOv8 to recognize and localize damage effectively. Around 700 images of the damaged bags are taken as a dataset and labeled using Polygon annotations which are critical to preparing datasets for training instance segmentation or semantic segmentation models and can often improve model accuracy when used instead of bounding boxes for object detection models represented in Fig. 6.

In capturing images for the self-generated dataset, particular attention was paid to replicating real-world operational conditions of airport baggage handling systems. Real-time imagery was collected under varying lighting conditions, conveyor belt speeds, and camera angles to ensure the dataset captured the natural variability of baggage presentation. One notable challenge during data collection was minimizing motion blur from moving baggage, which was addressed by adjusting shutter speeds and camera positioning. Annotation also presented difficulties, particularly for subtle or partially occluded damages and accessories, where visual ambiguity required multiple passes and cross-validation between annotators to maintain label consistency. To ensure balance across different classes and damage types, we employed targeted data collection and augmentation-actively sourcing additional examples of underrepresented categories such as straps and minor surface damages. This approach mitigated class imbalance and ensured that the final dataset provided diverse representations of both accessory variations and damage scenarios, supporting robust and generalizable model training.

Dataset splitting

The annotated dataset for handles and straps detection, consisting of 1054 original images, underwent augmentation to increase diversity and quantity, resulting in a total of 2528 images.

Training Set (87%): With 2211 images, this set was used for training the YOLOv8 model, allowing it to learn robust representations of baggage, handles, and straps on conveyor belts.

Validation Set (8%): Comprising 212 images, this set was utilized to monitor the model’s performance during training and fine-tuning processes, ensuring its generalization capability.

Test Set (4%): This set consisted of 105 images and was left apart for neutral testing of the accuracy of the trained model in actual-world situations.

The Damage detection dataset had 700 labeled images of bags with different levels of damage. To avoid class imbalance, the dataset was balanced such that there was equal representation of images with and without damage. Each image also received a proper preprocessing stage where the duplicates and corrupted files were eliminated, hence preserving the quality of the dataset. An even standard split ratio of 70-15-15 was used, dividing the images into training, testing, and validation sets, respectively. Model selection and training

In this project, the detection of baggage, handles, and straps is achieved through the implementation of the YOLOv8 (You Only Look Once version 8) algorithm. YOLOv8 is a state-of-the-art object detection algorithm that excels in real-time detection tasks, making it a suitable choice for baggage handling applications. The algorithm is trained to simultaneously identify and locate bags, handles, and straps within the airport environment.

Yolov8 architecture

The YOLOv8 model, developed by Ultralytics, represents a cutting-edge advancement in computer vision models, offering inherent support for object detection, classification, and segmentation tasks through a Python package and command line interface. Notably, YOLOv8 brings substantial architectural and developer experience enhancements over its predecessor, YOLOv537. These improvements include the integration of a novel anchor-free detection system, modifications to the model’s convolutional blocks, and the application of mosaic augmentation during training, a process that is deactivated before the model’s final 10 epochs38.

The YOLOv8 model’s foundation retains the CSP module from YOLOv5 while employing the C2f module for the extraction of visual features39–41. Additionally, YOLOv8 features a decoupled-head architecture, utilizing separate convolutions for classification and regression while integrating the concept of DFL16. A significant update present in the YOLOv8 model consists of the adoption of an anchor-free approach and the implementation of task alignment learning to align classification and regression tasks, thereby ensuring the precise positioning of aligned anchors16,43. This is achieved through the introduction of a new anchor alignment metric, obtained by the multiplication of the cls score and the Intersection over Union (IOU) between the predicted and ground truth frames16. This metric is seamlessly integrated into the sample allocation and loss function, facilitating dynamic optimization of each anchor’s prediction. Furthermore, the YOLOv8 model leverages VFL loss for classification and DFL loss + CIOU loss for regression16.

In this context, VFL represents an asymmetric weighting operation that addresses the imbalance between positive and negative samples. FL and QFL, in contrast, are both symmetrical16. As outlined in Eq. (1), p denotes the label, while q is the value computed using norm_align_metric in the case of a positive sample and p=0 for a negative sample. The pipeline begins with Data Augmentation, incorporating techniques such as mosaic augmentation, random scaling, and horizontal flipping to enhance the robustness of the model. An Anchor-Free Mechanism is utilized to eliminate the dependency on predefined anchor boxes, simplifying the detection process. The final loss computation combines DFL Loss and CIOU Loss to optimize both accuracy and localization. Bag damage detection

We employed the YOLOv8 architecture for instance segmentation to detect and localize damage on luggage images. Unlike standard object detection, which predicts bounding boxes around objects, instance segmentation predicts pixel-level masks for each detected object, enabling precise localization of irregularly shaped damage regions such as cracks, tears, or surface abrasions. This approach is advantageous in baggage inspection because damage boundaries are often non-rectangular and subtle, and accurate delineation of these areas supports more informed decision-making in repair or claims processes.

The model was trained for 200 epochs with a step-wise decay learning rate schedule, aiming to optimize for precision in damage detection while maintaining high recall. The training leveraged YOLOv8’s segmentation head, which extends its detection backbone with convolutional layers dedicated to mask prediction, enabling end-to-end learning of both class probabilities and instance masks. Data augmentation strategies such as random horizontal flips, rotations, and color jitter were applied to enhance the model’s generalizability and robustness to various damage appearances and lighting conditions. The training was conducted using NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs, ensuring efficient computation and backpropagation through the deep network layers.

The integration of the proposed YOLOv8 + GPT-based baggage risk detection system offers substantial economic value to airport operations, extending beyond the prevention of financial losses from damaged luggage claims. By enabling early detection of potential baggage handling issues, the system minimizes operational disruptions, reduces the need for manual inspections, and optimizes workforce allocation-resulting in lower labor costs and faster baggage processing times. Collectively, these factors contribute to significant cost savings and operational efficiency, strengthening the airport’s competitive advantage in a highly service-driven industry.

In addition to the quantitative results, we observed several challenging scenarios that help explain remaining error modes and guide future improvements. (1) Thin/occluded straps and straps crossing visually similar backgrounds (e.g., belt edges, seams) occasionally yield false negatives or mislocalizations-consistent with the lower strap recall-due to weak edge contrast and partial visibility. (2) Specular highlights, motion blur, and low light on moving conveyor belts can cause false positives for crack/damage segmentation, where reflections or scuffs mimic fracture patterns. **(3) Stacked or overlapping baggage introduces complex boundaries that sometimes lead to mask leakage at bag interfaces. (4) Uncommon materials and textures (e.g., patterned fabrics or glossy hard-shell cases) reduce generalization if underrepresented in the training set. To mitigate these effects, we have already employed class-specific loss weighting (for straps) and targeted augmentation (mosaic, scaling, flips, color jitter). We will further pursue hard-negative mining (e.g., adding images with scuffs/shadows labeled as non-damage), synthetic data for rare damage types (copy–paste/CutMix of annotated cracks), confidence/NMS calibration per class, mask refinement (e.g., simple CRF/GrabCut post-processing), and-when operating on video-temporal smoothing and object tracking to stabilize predictions across frames. Together, these steps are expected to reduce false positives for damage, recover thin/occluded straps, and improve robustness in operational environments.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our project has demonstrated promising results in the detection of handles, straps, and damages in bag images, achieving a mean Average Precision (mAP) of 0.92 for bags and 0.89 for handles across a dataset comprising 2,528 images. While handle detection performance is commendable, strap detection remains a challenge, with a precision of 0.74, underscoring the need for targeted dataset augmentation and model refinement. For damage detection, YOLOv8’s instance segmentation achieved a precision of 0.75, recall of 0.80, F1-score of 0.77, and an mAP of 0.76, yielding satisfactory results for crack identification in real-world baggage inspection scenarios.

Beyond the immediate results, this work lays the foundation for deploying AI-driven baggage inspection systems as part of real-time monitoring solutions within airport operations. The proposed YOLOv8-based pipeline, complemented by GPT-4 Vision for contextual verification, can be integrated into conveyor belt monitoring systems to detect anomalies instantaneously, reducing mishandling rates and operational disruptions. Such systems could also provide automated alerts to ground staff, enabling proactive interventions and enhancing passenger service quality.
