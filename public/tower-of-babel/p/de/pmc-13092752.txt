Modification of fish oil triglycerides into 1,2,3-triazole–fatty acid esters as potent antitubercular agents
Authors: Bayu Ardiansah, Lala Amanda, Erwahyuni Endang Prabandari, Tuti Wukirsari, Sri Handayani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to develop 1,2,3-triazole derivatives from fish oil triglycerides as potential antitubercular agents. The synthetic route consisted of three main steps: hydrolysis of fish oil to obtain free fatty acids (2, yield 86%), propargylation of the free fatty acids to produce propargyl esters (3, yield 59%), and azide–alkyne [3 + 2] cycloaddition of the propargyl esters to afford 1,2,3-triazole esters (5, yield 85%). Linoleic acid was identified as the predominant fatty acid. All compounds were characterized using FTIR, NMR, and HRMS analyses. The final compound (5) demonstrated moderate inhibitory activity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis shikimate kinase (47.86%), whereas the free fatty acids (2) exhibited the strongest inhibition (98.68%) against the enzyme. These findings suggest that linoleic acid and the triazole derivative of the fatty acid represent promising candidates for further development of antitubercular agents.•Commercial fish oil was successfully modified to form 1,2,3-triazole ester derivatives.•The synthetic route involved hydrolysis, propargylation, and azide–alkyne [3 + 2] cycloaddition.•The synthesized compounds showed Mycobacterium tuberculosis shikimate kinase inhibitory activity, thus, it can be considered as potential antitubercular candidates.

Background

The discovery and development of bioactive organic molecules remain an important focus in medicinal and pharmaceutical chemistry. Various synthetic strategies have been explored to construct biologically active compounds by incorporating functional groups and heterocyclic scaffolds that can enhance pharmacological properties [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. In recent years, numerous studies have reported the synthesis of biologically active molecules such as chalcones, pyrimidine derivatives, and other heterocyclic systems using catalytic and green chemistry approaches, demonstrating promising activities including antioxidant and antiviral effects [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. These findings highlight the importance of molecular modification and heterocycle incorporation as effective strategies for generating compounds with improved biological potential. In this context, the design of new heterocyclic derivatives from natural product-based precursors represents an attractive approach for the development of novel therapeutic agents.

Fish oil is a lipid-rich natural product mainly composed of triglycerides (Fig. 1A), with three fatty acids esterified to glycerol [11]. Commonly found in soft gel form, commercial fish oil contains polyunsaturated fatty acids and is widely used for managing kidney disease, obesity, and cardiovascular disorders [[12], [13], [14]]. Fish oil hydrolysis yields free fatty acids (FFAs) with reactive carboxylic groups [15], which can be potentially further transformed into propargyl esters and subsequently into 1,2,3-triazole derivatives through the reaction with azido compounds [16]. Despite its known health advantages, fish oil remains underexplored as a precursor for pharmaceutical synthesis during drug candidate exploration. 1,2,3-Triazole is a five-membered heterocycle with three nitrogen atoms, recognized for its antibacterial, antifungal, antitumor, and antituberculosis activities [17]. These biological effects arise from its ability to form non-covalent interactions and enhance solubility [18]. Triazoles are commonly synthesized via azide-alkyne [3 + 2] cycloaddition [19], and numerous derivatives, such as quinone and sulfonamide (Fig. 1B), have demonstrated potent bioactivities [20,21]. Bacterial infections remain a major global health concern [22,23], particularly with the increasing emergence of antibiotic-resistant strains that significantly reduce treatment efficacy [21,24]. Particularly, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the causative agent of tuberculosis, remains a critical health threat due to resistance to prolonged treatment [[25], [26], [27]]. Although fatty acid-based triazole derivatives have shown promise as antibacterial and antitubercular agents (Fig. 1C) [28,29], studies on modifying commercial fish oil into such bioactive compounds are limited. This study, therefore, aims to synthesize triazole ester from fish oil-derived fatty acids (Fig. 1D) and assess their antitubercular activity. Method details

Hydrolysis of commercial fish oil (triglycerides) (1) into free fatty acids (2)

We commenced the research by identifying the chemical composition of the commercial fish oil using GC–MS instrument, with the result as shown in Table 1. It was found that linoleic and oleic acids were predominate the mixture. To obtain 2, 1 (50 g) was refluxed with 1 M sodium hydroxide in 96% ethanol (250 mL) at 75 °C for 1 h. The mixture was then neutralized with 3 M hydrochloric acid (87.5 mL) and then extracted with n-hexane. The organic layer was washed with distilled water and brine, then dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate. To evaluate the physicochemical properties of both compounds 1 and 2 obtained after hydrolysis, acid value (AV), iodine value (IV), and peroxide value (PV) were measured. These parameters provide essential information regarding the extent of hydrolysis (AV), the oxidative stability (PV), and the degree of unsaturation (IV) of the fatty acid-based derivatives. The assessment was carried out on both the starting material 1 and the hydrolyzed product 2 to examine the efficiency of the process and the quality of the resulting free fatty acids.

Acid value (AV)

The acid value was determined by titrating a weighed sample (0.5 g) dissolved in neutral ethanol (50 mL) with 0.1 N NaOH solution using phenolphthalein as the indicator. The titration was conducted until a stable pale pink color was observed. The AV was determined using the following equation:AV=VNaOH×NNaOH×40msamplewhere VNaOH is the volume of NaOH (mL), NNaOH​ is the normality of the NaOH solution, and msampleis the mass of the sample (g).

Iodine value (IV)

Approximately 0.5 g of the sample was dissolved in 15 mL of cyclohexane and reacted with 25 mL of Wijs reagent. After 30 min of incubation in the dark, 10 mL of 10% KI solution was added. The liberated iodine was titrated with standardized 0.1 N sodium thiosulfate solution until the yellow color faded. Then, 1–2 mL of 1% starch solution was added, and titration continued until the blue color disappeared. A blank was prepared in parallel. The iodine value was calculated using the formula:IV=12.69×NNa2S2O3×(Vblank−Vsample)msamplewhere Vblank is the volume of sodium thiosulfate used for the blank, Vsample is the volume used for the sample, NNa2S2O3 is the normality of the thiosulfate solution, and msample is the mass (g) of the sample.

Peroxide value (PV)

A 2.5 g sample was dissolved in a 25 mL mixture of acetic acid/isooctane (3/2) in a closed Erlenmeyer flask. Saturated KI solution (0.25 mL) was added and shaken for 1 min, followed by the addition of 15 mL of distilled water. The mixture was titrated with 0.01 N sodium thiosulfate until the yellow color faded. Then, 1–2 mL of 1% starch solution was added, and titration continued until the blue color disappeared. A blank was prepared using the same procedure without addition of the sample. The PV was determined using the following formula:PV=1000×NNa2S2O3×(Vsample−Vblank)msamplewhere Vblank is the volume of Na2S2O3 used for the blank, Vsample is the volume used for the sample, NNa2S2O3 is the normality of the sodium thiosulfate solution used, and msample is the mass (g) of the sample determined. The AV of the compound 2 demonstrated a significant increase compared to the compound 1, indicating the successful cleavage of –COOR bonds forming –COOH groups during hydrolysis. The IV remained relatively unchanged, indicating that the degree of unsaturation (C = C bonds) was preserved and no significant decomposition of unsaturated fatty acids occurred during the process. Meanwhile, a marked decrease in the peroxide value after hydrolysis demonstrated the breakdown or reduction of initial oxidative products (ROOH), suggesting improved oxidative stability of the resulting FFAs. These results collectively confirmed the effectiveness of the hydrolysis treatment in converting triglycerides into stable, unsaturated free fatty acids.

Synthesis propargyl ester (3)

To synthesize compound 3, 1 (2.70 g, 10 mmol) and potassium carbonate (2.07 g, 30 mmol) were dissolved in dry DMF (20 mL) in a 50 mL round-bottom flask. The mixture was stirred at 80 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, propargyl bromide (80% in toluene, 1.12 mL, 10 mmol) was added dropwise, and the reaction was stirred for another 2 h at the same temperature. Upon completion, the mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate, and the organic phase was washed successively with water and brine. The organic layer was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, and the solvent was evaporated to obtain the crude product, which was purified by silica gel column chromatography (n-hexane/ethyl acetate = 10/1) to afford propargyl ester 3 (2 g, 59%) as a pale-yellow oil.

Synthesis 4-azidoacetophenone (4)

To synthesize compound 4, 4-aminoacetophenone (1.35 g, 10 mmol) was dissolved in a mixture of 18% hydrochloric acid (50 mL) and ethanol (10 mL) in a 100 mL round-bottom flask. The mixture was stirred at room temperature until homogeneous, then cooled in an ice bath to 0 °C. Sodium nitrite (1.04 g, 15 mmol) was added portionwise, and the mixture was stirred at 0 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, sodium azide (0.98 g, 15 mmol) was added portionwise to the reaction mixture. The reaction was stirred at room temperature for 24 h. Upon completion, the mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate. The organic phase was washed three times with saturated sodium bicarbonate solution and once with brine. The organic layer was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, and the solvent was evaporated to obtain the crude product, which was purified by silica gel column chromatography (n-hexane/ethyl acetate = 20/1) to afford 4-azidoacetophenone 4 (0.51 g, 32%) as a yellow oil.

Synthesis 1,2,3-triazole ester (5)

To synthesize compound 5, ascorbic acid (70 mg, 0.4 mmol) was dissolved in distilled water (2 mL) in a 10 mL round-bottom flask and stirred at room temperature until fully dissolved. Sequentially, propargyl ester (0.31 g, 1 mmol), 4-azidoacetophenone (0.16 g, 1 mmol), CuSO4·5H2O (50 mg, 0.2 mmol), and DMF (6 mL) were added to the mixture. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h. Upon completion, the mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate, and the organic layer was washed twice with water and once with brine. The organic layer was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, and the solvent was evaporated to afford the crude product. The crude material was purified by silica gel column chromatography. Unreacted compound 3 and less polar impurities were eluted first using n-hexane/ethyl acetate (10/1). The product began to elute at a ratio of (5/1), and fractions were collected accordingly. Further increase in eluent polarity (2/1) resulted in the elution of non-target compounds. The desired fractions were combined and concentrated to obtain the purified 1,2,3-triazole ester 5 (0.4 g, 85%) as a white solid.

Antitubercular activity assay (shikimate kinase inhibition)

Antituberculosis activity was evaluated by assessing the inhibitory effect of the test compounds on Mycobacterium tuberculosis shikimate kinase (MtSK) [30,31]. The assay was carried out in a total volume of 40 μL using a 384-well plate. Each well received 1 μL of the test compound, followed by 9.5 μL of recombinant MtSK enzyme (0.1 μM) prepared in assay buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, 50 μM MgCl2, 0.01% Triton X-100). DMSO was used as the negative control, while the enzyme-free buffer served as the positive control. Then, 9.5 μL of substrate solution containing 300 μM ATP and 200 μM shikimate in assay buffer was added. After incubation, 20 μL of coupling reagent was added. The reagent contained 100 mM Tris-HCl, 50 μM MgCl₂, 0.01% Triton X-100, 0.01% BSA, 200 μM glucose, 20 μM NADP+, 200 U/mL ADP hexokinase, 200 U/mL glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, 200 U/mL diaphorase I, 10 μM resazurin, and 2 mM N-ethylmaleimide. The mixture was further incubated for 30 min, and fluorescence was measured at an excitation wavelength of 540 nm and an emission wavelength of 590 nm using a fluorescence spectrometer. The assay was performed at a compound concentration of 250 μg/mL. At a concentration of 250 μg/mL, compound 2 showed the highest inhibition of MtSK (98.68%), followed by compound 5 (47.86%), compound 3 (44.31%), and compound 1 (36.61%) (Table 3). The high activity of compound 2 may be attributed to the presence of linoleic acid, a major component identified by GC–MS. Linoleic acid shares structural similarity with α-linolenic acid, which has been reported to inhibit shikimate kinase with an IC50 of 3.7 µg/mL [32]. Its lipophilic and flexible structure likely allows non-specific interaction with the enzyme, possibly through allosteric binding. Linoleic acid is also known to inhibit FabI, a vital enzyme in bacterial fatty acid synthesis [33]. Although FabI and MtSK are unrelated, their inhibition can suppress bacterial growth and survival. Meanwhile, the moderate activity of compound 5 provides further evidence for the pharmacological relevance of the 1,2,3-triazole moiety, which enables strong non-covalent interactions [34]. These results underscore the potential of free fatty acids 2 and 1,2,3-triazole ester of fatty acids as promising antitubercular agents via Shikimate Kinase inhibition.

Method validation

The identities of all synthesized compounds were confirmed by IR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and HRMS analyses (see Supplementary Material). For example, in the final product 5, the IR spectrum showed characteristic absorptions confirming the presence of key functional groups: a strong C = O stretching vibration of the ester at 1742 cm⁻1, C = C stretch at 1604 cm⁻1, and sp2 C–H stretching at 3134 cm⁻1. Additional peaks at 1176 cm⁻1 and 1273 cm⁻1 correspond to C–O (ester) and C–N stretches, respectively, supporting the presence of ester and triazole linkages. In the ¹H NMR spectrum, characteristic signals appeared in the aromatic region around δ 7.88–8.14 ppm, confirming the presence of the triazole moiety and substituted phenyl ring. Vinylic and methylene protons adjacent to the ester linkage appeared at δ 5.30–5.40 ppm. Signal corresponding to aliphatic chain protons were observed in the range of δ 0.88–2.75 ppm. Meanwhile, the ¹³C NMR spectrum showed signals at δ 189.59 and 166.80 ppm, corresponding to carbonyl carbon of ketone and ester, respectively. The δ 137.13–113.08 ppm region were characteristic of aromatic, triazole, and olefinic carbons. Carbon signals observed in the δ 50.21–15.55 ppm range correspond methylene and methyl groups. HRMS analysis showed [M + H]+ peak at 480.3222, corresponding to linoleic derivative. This value closely match the theoretical mass (480.3226), providing confirmation of the successful synthesis of the target molecules.

Limitations

Incomplete conversion was observed for the azide–alkyne [3 + 2] cycloaddition. Even after extended reaction times (>24 h), suggesting that reaction efficiency is influenced by experimental parameters or the complex nature of the commercial fish oil-derived precursor. Thin layer chromatography revealed a single spot; however, the 1H and 13C NMR spectra contained overlapping and unresolved signals, which may be attributed to the presence of multiple fatty acid components in the starting material. This complexity may present challenges in purification and may affect the spectral clarity and perceived purity of the final product. However, we confident that the resulted spectra were mainly associated with the derivative of linoleic and oleic acid as the main components of starting materials.
