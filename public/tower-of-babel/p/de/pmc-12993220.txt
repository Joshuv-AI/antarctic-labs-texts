Single-use optical tracking navigation for external ventricular drain placement: Preclinical 3D-Printed and cadaveric evaluation with a systematic review
Authors: Daniel De Wilde, Attill Saemann, Roman Ramseyer, Jan Stifter, Raphael Guzman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionExternal ventricular drain (EVD) placement is common in neurosurgery, yet malposition occurs in up to 38% of cases. While technical adjuncts can improve placement accuracy, simpler and more user-friendly solutions are needed. Our novel single-use optical tracking navigation (OTN) device enables real-time image-guided EVD placement. Introduction

External ventricular drain (EVD) placement is among the most frequently performed procedures in neurosurgery (Rosenbaum et al., 2014/04; Srinivasan et al., 2014). It is essential for managing acute cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage in patients with elevated ICP caused by conditions such as occlusive hydrocephalus, intracranial hemorrhage, tumors, and traumatic brain injury (TBI) (Fried et al., 2016). The current standard of placement is freehand insertion, performed in either an operating room or bedside. Typically, the catheter is introduced at Kocher's point (1-2 cm anterior to the coronal suture and 1-2 cm lateral to the cranial midline), with the trajectory determined by anatomical landmarks or perpendicular alignment to the cortical surface (Park et al., 2011). However, current literature states that freehand EVD placement is associated with misplacement rates of up to 38%, which in cases of pronounced malposition may result in shunt dysfunction and serious complications, including intracranial hemorrhage, infection, and neurological deficits (Konovalov et al., 2021a; Muralidharan, 2015; Dakson et al., 2022).

To address these challenges, a range of guidance devices has been developed, from mechanical guides for simplified trajectory assistance (Ghajar, 1985; Ann et al., 2016) to advanced technologies such as image-guided methods including fluoroscopy- or computed tomography-guidance (CT) (Fiorella et al., 2014), mixed reality (Grunert et al., 2024; Buwaider et al., 2024/07), ultrasonography (Jakola et al., 2014), neuroendoscopy (Villavicencio et al., 2003), conventional neuronavigation systems (optical and electromagnetic) (Konovalov et al., 2021b) and smartphone-assisted placement (Eisenring et al., 2020). Although these tools have been shown to enhance accuracy (Stuart et al., 2021/12), their widespread adoption in routine clinical practice has been hindered by several limitations, including prolonged procedure times, the need for head fixation, high equipment costs, restricted availability, and the additional training requirements (Jakola et al., 2014; Konovalov et al., 2021b; Stuart et al., 2021/12). Moreover, the urgency and practical constraints of EVD placement often favor methods that are time-efficient, straightforward, and easily integrated into clinical workflows.

Building upon the existing implementation of single-use optical tracking navigation (OTN) in various medical applications (Sabatini et al., 2024; Żelechowski et al., 2025/01; Kojima et al., 2025; Kanno et al., 2024; Bischofreiter et al., 2025/04), we have engineered an adaptation of this technology for cerebral catheter placement. Our novel adaptation facilitates real-time tracking of any cerebral catheter in relation to patient-specific imaging, requiring minimal equipment. It is designed to be cost-effective, user-friendly, and operable without head fixation, thereby addressing several shortcomings of currently available guidance systems. In this proof-of-concept study, we had two aims: (1) to evaluate the accuracy and usability of this OTN system for EVD placement in 3D-printed and cadaveric head models, and (2) to systematically review navigation-assisted EVD techniques to contextualize its performance within the current landscape of guidance devices.

Methods

Two sets of experiments were performed, with one experiment having EVDs placed in a 3D-printed head model with ventricles (3D Print Lab, University Hospital of Basel, Basel, Switzerland) and in an arterial and venous-injected fresh-frozen human cadaveric head without a history of TBI.

Model preparation

A custom 3D-printed head model with ventricles was developed at the 3D Print Lab, University Hospital of Basel, using a fused and modified MRI and CT scan dataset from an anonymized patient from our internal DICOM database. Our group has previously described the development of a prototype in detail (Saemann et al., 2024). The model featured bilateral pre-formed 14 mm burr holes at Kocher's point and flexible, 3D-printed ventricles with an open ceiling to facilitate easier access. A 5 mm steel ball was embedded in the third ventricle at the level of the foramen of Monro to enable radiological assessment. To mimic brain tissue, an alginate-water mixture (1:7, Alginat BHG1, Bauer Handels GmbH, Fehraltorf, Switzerland) was cast in a custom negative mold, replicating gyri and sulci. This replica was positioned atop the ventricles and stabilized using a dedicated 3D-printed support structure. Before each EVD placement, a corticotomy was performed on the brain tissue using a surgical scalpel. The brain tissue was replaced after each experiment to avoid tunneling artifacts. A fresh-frozen, arterial- and venous-injected human cadaveric head without a history of TBI was used for the cadaveric study (Science Care, Inc., Phoenix, AZ, USA).

Prior to experiments, 128-slice CT imaging (Siemens SOMATOM, Siemens Healthcare GmbH, Erlangen, Germany) with a slice thickness of 1.7 mm was performed on both head models. The imaging dataset of the 3D-printed model was fused with the initial patient CT scan to support trajectory planning. All CT scans were imported into 3D Slicer (Harvard University, Boston, Massachusetts, United States) for segmentation of the skull surface and ventricles. The expected catheter insertion depth was measured from the tabula externa at the planned burr hole to the foramen of Monro. CT scans and corresponding segmentations were exported as MRB files and uploaded to the OTN host laptop.

Navigation-assisted placement

The iVation OTN system (Medivation AG, Baden, Switzerland) is a close-range navigation platform that determines the six degrees of freedom (6DOF) of a tracked instrument relative to a camera, providing real-time navigation in reference to patient-specific imaging. While the iVation system is FDA- and CE-certified for specific orthopedic indications, this study represents its first application in neurosurgery (Sabatini et al., 2024; Żelechowski et al., 2025/01; Kojima et al., 2025; Kanno et al., 2024; Bischofreiter et al., 2025/04). The iVation system's relative RMS translation accuracy is 0.3 mm (x/y) and 0.5-0.6 mm (z), and absolute RMS translation accuracy, including instrument mounts, is 0.5 mm (x/y) and 0.7-0.8 mm (z) within defined measurement volumes. Assuming a rigid (non-bending) instrument and a verified instrument offset, iVation platform-level point targeting on the order of ca. 1 mm (RMS) is feasible (AG, 2019). Workflow-specific FRE/TRE for the present EVD setup was not measured.

For EVD placement, the system consists of a single-use camera and tracking unit, a sterile patient-side navigation kit comprising a reusable burr hole fixator, a 3D-printed shunt holder, a registration stylus, and a specialized software interface running on a host laptop (Fig. 1). Postinterventional assessment

Following EVD placement, anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopy images were acquired using a C-arm (Cios Flow, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), with standardized landmarks used for consistent alignment. DICOM files were exported and analyzed in 3D Slicer. All assessments were performed in a blinded manner by an independent investigator. Measurements were calibrated against the 5-mm steel ball. In the anteroposterior projection, mediolateral and craniocaudal deviations between the catheter tip and the steel ball were measured, while in the lateral projection, anteroposterior and craniocaudal deviations were recorded. The overall 3D tip-to-target distance (Euclidean distance) was then calculated as the square root of the sum of squared deviations:d=(Δxmediolateral)2+(Δycraniocaudal)2+(Δzanteroposterior)2

Importantly, this fluoroscopy-derived tip-to-target distance reflects the final catheter tip position relative to the selected radiographic target and therefore incorporates patient-to-image registration uncertainty, potential catheter deflection, and measurement error; it should not be interpreted as the intrinsic tracking precision of the OTN system. The mean craniocaudal deviation across projections was also calculated. To account for the deviation between the steel ball and the foramen of Monro, the real-life measured deviation between the steel ball and the ipsilateral foramen of Monro was subtracted from the corresponding deviation values.

In the 3D-printed head model, the Euclidean distance was measured from the steel ball to the catheter tip, whereas in the cadaveric study it was measured from the catheter tip to the foramen of Monro, as visualized on radiographic imaging (tip-to-target distance). Because different radiographic targets were used in the 3D-printed and cadaveric models, absolute tip-to-target distances are not directly comparable between models. Catheter tip position was graded according to the Kakarla grading system: Grade 1 indicated optimal placement in the ipsilateral frontal horn, including the tip of the third ventricle; Grade 2 indicated suboptimal placement in the contralateral frontal horn, lateral ventricle, corpus callosum, or interhemispheric fissure; and Grade 3 indicated suboptimal placement in areas such as the brainstem, cerebellum, internal capsule, basal ganglia, thalamus, occipital cortex, or basal cisterns (Kakarla et al.). Actual insertion depth of the EVD was also recorded. After all placements, participants completed a standardized Likert-scale survey to evaluate user satisfaction with the OTN system.

Literature review

A systematic review was performed to assess the current landscape of navigation devices for EVD placement. The review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The associated PRISMA checklist is available in Supplementary File 1. The review was not prospectively registered. A search strategy was built around concepts of the intervention (ventriculostomy), outcomes (accuracy, precision), and navigation aids (guided, assisted, and various technologies), incorporating a broad set of related keywords. The complete search strategy is provided in the supplementary material. PubMed and EMBASE were searched from inception to May 31, 2025.

Search results were imported into a web-based systematic review tool (Rayyan.ai) (Ouzzani et al., 2016). Title, abstract- and full-text screening were independently performed by two reviewers (D.D.W. and A.S.), with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer (R.G.). Eligible studies included preclinical or clinical research investigating any form of navigation-assisted EVD placement in adult or pediatric populations. Exclusion criteria included studies focused on Ommaya reservoirs, ventricular catheters for ventriculoperitoneal shunting, other cerebral catheters, endoscopic techniques, non-English publications, conference abstracts, and letters to the editor. Data were extracted using a standardized form that included bibliographic information, study and patient characteristics, procedural details, methods of accuracy assessment, and procedural outcomes.

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to assess the quality of the included studies (Wells et al., 2011). The results were visualized using the Risk-of-bias VISualization tool (McGuinness and Higgins, 2021).

Ethics approval

This study exclusively used anonymized patient imaging and cadaveric specimens. In accordance with institutional and national regulations, formal ethical approval and individual patient consent were not required.

Data analysis

All data were recorded in case report forms. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. The accuracy of the insertion was binarized, whereby a Kakarla grade of 1 was deemed accurate, and Kakarla grades 2 and 3 were deemed inaccurate. Binomial Clopper-Pearson 95% Confidence intervals (CI) were calculated (Clopper and Pearson, 1934). Given the small sample size, all analyses were descriptive, and any p-values reported are interpreted as exploratory. Statistical analyses and graph creation were performed using R statistical software (version 4.2.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 11 EVD placements were performed by three neurosurgeons: 9 in 3D-printed models and 2 in a cadaveric model. The participants had a mean age of 33.3 ± 4.5 years and an average of 6 ± 5 years of neurosurgical experience. EVD placement experience ranged from fewer than 5 to over 70 placements. Participants self-identified as novice, intermediate, and expert users of navigation technologies, respectively, with prior experience in systems such as ultrasound, BrainLab Cranial Navigation, and Medtronic StealthStation (both optical and electromagnetic modalities).

3D-printed head model

All placements in the de-identified 3D-printed models were optimal (9/9 (100%), Kakarla grade 1; 95% CI 0.66–1.00). The mean fluoroscopy-derived tip-to-target distance to the radiographic reference target representing the foramen of Monro was 13.3 ± 6.4 mm. By spatial axis, mean deviations were 3.9 ± 2.6 mm (mediolateral), 5.0 ± 5.9 mm (craniocaudal), and 9.5 ± 6.9 mm (anteroposterior) (Fig. 3). In this small sample, linear regression showed no significant association between neurosurgical experience and fluoroscopy-derived tip-to-target distance (p = 0.584, R2 = 0.369).Fig. 3Boxplots of mediolateral, craniocaudal, and anteroposterior deviations, and Euclidean tip-to-target distance (overall 3D spatial error; in mm) for OTN-assisted placements in the 3D-printed head model.Fig. 3

Procedure duration

The mean duration of OTN-assisted placements, including registration and calibration, was 12.1 ± 2.1 min, n = 5. Continuous measurement was only feasible in 5 of 11 cases due to procedural interruptions. Procedure duration trended downward with a mean slope of −2.3 ± 4.3 min per placement.

Procedure-related observations

No catheters required replacement during any OTN-assisted placements. An average of 2.7 ± 2.4 (sample size of n = 8) complete surface registrations were performed during the experiments. The registrations of three experiments were excluded from analysis due to investigator intervention.

Cadaveric head model

Both placements in the cadaveric model were optimal (2/2 (100%), Kakarla grade 1; 95% CI 0.16–1.00). The mean fluoroscopy-derived tip-to-target distance to the foramen of Monro was 22.2 ± 3.2 mm.

Qualitative assessment of navigation system

The OTN system received high levels of user satisfaction, with a mean Likelihood to Recommend (LTR) of 7 out of 10. Participants highlighted key features essential for clinical application, including ease of handling, operational efficiency, accuracy, and cost-effectiveness. The burr hole fixation device was praised for its stability and secure anchoring, while the registration stylus and shunt holder were commended for their ergonomic design and ease of handling. The shunt holder's adaptability for various catheter sizes was also recognized as a significant advantage for achieving secure and precise placement.

Participants noted several challenges, such as occasionally neglecting to activate the camera for patient registration and encountering potential difficulties exporting DICOM datasets from institutional imaging databases into the navigation interface. Patient registration and shunt calibration were identified as current workflow bottlenecks, sometimes leading to increased procedure times. Suggested improvements included adding a target point indicator, real-time trajectory deviation feedback, and a “probe's eye view” (a virtual CT slice along the catheter trajectory) to enhance planning capabilities.

The most frequently described navigation aid was AR/VR, most commonly using the Microsoft HoloLens 2 head-mounted display (HMD) as the visualization platform for EVD trajectories. These systems provide transparent visualization of patient anatomy, with many applications supporting preoperative trajectory planning and real-time intraoperative feedback (Buwaider et al., 2024/07; Schneider et al., 2021; Chiou et al., 2022; Chiou et al., 2023; Konovalov et al., 2025). Reported limitations included reliance on preoperative imaging and the associated risk of registration errors (Konovalov et al., 2025). Technical complexity was also cited as a barrier to routine use by surgeons (Li et al., 2019). In addition, high upfront costs for AR/VR hardware and recurring software licensing fees were noted to limit accessibility (Laspro et al., 2023).

The Thomalé guide was another navigation aid frequently reported, either as a stand-alone device or in combination with other tools (Thomale et al., 2015; Sargut et al., 2024/02; Spindler et al., 2022/03; Thomale et al., 2018; Thomale et al., 2013). This catheter guide enables orthogonal insertion relative to the sagittal plane, with an adjustable angle in the coronal plane (Spindler et al., 2022/03). The trajectory is planned preoperatively using the Thomalé Guide mobile application on a smartphone or tablet. Reported advantages included low cost and time efficiency. One study described adapting a single-use electric drill to the guide, aligning the burr hole with the planned trajectory to enhance accuracy (Sargut et al., 2024/02). Limitations include the absence of real-time feedback and advanced visualization, reducing applicability in complex cases such as slit ventricles, midline shift, or pediatric procedures. Thus, settings where navigation support might be most needed.

Neuronavigation systems were also commonly described. Although not specifically designed for EVD placement, dedicated stylets have been developed for real-time tracking of shunt devices (AlAzri et al., 2017). Multiple studies reported improved placement accuracy using neuronavigation, with electromagnetic, optical, and computer vision–based modalities demonstrating efficacy (Mahan et al., 2013; Gautschi et al., 2014; AlAzri et al., 2017). Reported limitations included high cost, the need for substantial operating room space, and decreased accuracy when anatomical changes occurred between preoperative imaging and the procedure itself. Increased setup time was also noted, although some studies suggested that the higher accuracy of catheter placement may offset this (AlAzri et al., 2017).

Across studies, navigation-assisted EVD placement generally improved accuracy and first-pass success rates compared with non-navigated techniques. AR/VR and neuronavigation systems most consistently reported mean target deviations of 1–5 mm, while mechanical guides and smartphone-based tools showed more variable results (up to 10 mm) (Supplementary Table 1). Ultrasound guidance demonstrated clear advantages in clinical settings, particularly for real-time feedback. However, direct comparison between modalities remains limited by heterogeneous models, outcome metrics, and grading systems. The marked heterogeneity in study designs, models, and outcome definitions precluded a formal meta-analysis, so accuracy data were synthesized qualitatively.

Discussion

This proof-of-concept study introduces a novel single-use optical tracking navigation (OTN) system for image-guided external ventricular drainage (EVD) placement, achieving 100% optimal placement in both 3D-printed and cadaveric models (3D-printed 9/9, 95% CI 0.66–1.00; cadaveric 2/2, 95% CI 0.16–1.00), independent of users’ neurosurgical experience (p = 0.584, R2 = 0.369). Within this small sample, we did not observe an apparent association between prior EVD experience and placement accuracy, although no definitive conclusions can be drawn. While these findings are encouraging, validation in larger cohorts, workflow improvements, and advanced visualization capabilities are required before clinical translation.

EVD placement is one of the most common neurosurgical procedures but is plagued by high misplacement rates, which may be as high as 28–45% in some contexts (Dakson et al., 2022; Nawabi et al., 2023). This, in turn, may result in clinical and economic downstream consequences. Guidance tools such as conventional neuronavigation systems and intraoperative ultrasound (iUS) have been used to address these challenges, achieving high accuracy rates in both preclinical and clinical settings (Zhang et al., 2023/07; AlAzri et al., 2017; Fisher et al., 2021). However, these systems have notable limitations: neuronavigation is costly, necessitates head fixation, and is generally restricted to the operating room, while iUS requires larger burr holes and is highly operator-dependent, posing challenges for less-experienced surgeons and in cases of complex ventricular anatomy (Jakola et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2023/07; Manfield and Yu, 2017).

Our OTN system was specifically designed to address these shortcomings. Its compact, single-use components (<5 cm) enable real-time optical tracking of surgical instruments relative to patient imaging without requiring head fixation or large burr holes. This design, combined with cost-efficiency, facilitates bedside use and broadens the scope of image-guided neurosurgery to intensive care and emergency settings.

In this first assessment of this device, all placements were graded as optimal by Kakarla grading. Quantitative analysis showed a mean tip deviation of 13.3 mm (±6.4 mm) in the 3D-printed model and 22.2 mm (±3.2 mm) in the cadaveric model. Because EVD placement clinically targets ventricular cannulation rather than point-to-point targeting, we additionally reported Kakarla grading, and all catheters were graded as Kakarla grade 1. The observed point-target distances likely reflect a combination of patient-to-image registration uncertainty, potential catheter deflection, and the limitations of estimating 3D distances from biplanar fluoroscopy, therefore, direct comparison with CT-based 3D point-target deviations reported for other guidance modalities should be interpreted with caution. In the literature, navigation-assisted approaches generally improve EVD placement outcomes compared with freehand techniques, but heterogeneous models, targets, and outcome definitions preclude direct quantitative comparison.

Interestingly, within this small cohort, we did not observe a consistent trend between operator experience and tip-to-target distance. It must however be acknowledged that the small sample size limits definitive conclusions. Nonetheless, a potential “levelling” effect driven by an easy-to-use navigation may be particularly relevant because EVD insertion is often among the first procedures performed by neurosurgical residents. In contrast, iUS, with accuracy being highly operator-dependent, may pose a challenge for less experienced surgeons.

To our knowledge, this is the first published single-use navigation system specifically developed for EVD placement. In the systematic review of the published literature, we did not identify any other single-use navigation devices for EVD. The portability and rapid setup enable accurate navigation outside the operating room, potentially extending advanced neurosurgical care to the bedside, emergency settings, and resource-limited environments. This positions the OTN system as a promising solution not only for high-volume centers but also for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where access to navigation technologies has historically been limited by prohibitive costs and logistical barriers.

A key practical advantage of the OTN system lies in its single-use design. Unlike reusable platforms, which require complex resterilization and add to operating room logistics, the sterile, disposable components simplify workflow, improve infection control, and eliminate concerns related to equipment reprocessing. However, the trade-off between reduced sterilization burden and increased medical waste warrants consideration.

From a clinical workflow perspective, the compact form factor and disposable nature of the system allow seamless integration into existing EVD placement protocols, minimizing setup and training requirements. User feedback emphasized the intuitive operation of the device. While a formal cost analysis was not performed in this preclinical study, the absence of large capital investments, licensing fees, and maintenance costs suggests potential for significant economic benefits. A comprehensive cost-effectiveness evaluation will be an important next step for clinical implementation.

System limitations

Despite its advantages, the OTN system has several limitations. One notable limitation is the registration process, which often required multiple attempts during the study, leading to prolonged procedure times. The average registration and placement time totaled 12.1 ± 2.1 min, with the placement itself accounting for only a small fraction of this duration. Algorithmic refinements have since reduced this to 5.5 ± 6.5 min, but this remains longer than registration times reported for other systems (Fan et al., 2020).

We did not quantify the fiducial registration error of the system, so we cannot disentangle the relative contributions of registration inaccuracy and catheter trajectory deviation to the observed tip-to-target errors.

Additionally, the system currently lacks advanced visualization features such as a “probe's-eye view” and real-time trajectory planning, which participants identified as critical for improving usability, particularly in complex cases. Another constraint is the device's restriction to certain registration landmarks, excluding access points such as Frasier and Keen's points that are frequently used in pediatric and ventricular-peritoneal shunt procedures (Frazier, 1928; Junaid et al., 2018). Expanding the system's compatibility with these landmarks would significantly broaden its clinical utility.

Beyond the system's technical limitations, several study-specific constraints should be considered. First, the relatively small sample size of 11 EVD placements limits the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Second, all testing was performed in an ex vivo setting using 3D-printed and cadaveric head models. Although these models provide a reasonable approximation of human anatomy, they do not replicate the full complexity of live human tissue and cerebrospinal fluid dynamics. Third, the assessment of EVD position was based on fluoroscopic imaging, which offers limited three-dimensional visualization and depends on optimal head positioning, potentially introducing variability. Accordingly, the reported 3D Euclidean tip-to-target distances should be interpreted as fluoroscopy-derived estimates and may differ from CT-based 3D measurements. Future studies should aim to validate these findings in cadaveric models with larger sample sizes, varied entry points, and pathological ventricles. Additionally, initial clinical trials in live patients will be essential to provide a comprehensive assessment of the system's clinical utility.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides preliminary evidence supporting the accuracy and usability of a single-use OTN system for EVD placement. The OTN system demonstrated a high rate of optimal placement comparable to existing guidance devices, regardless of operator experience. However, further refinements and validation in pathological ventricles and clinical trials are necessary to optimize its utility and confirm its feasibility for clinical neurosurgical use.
