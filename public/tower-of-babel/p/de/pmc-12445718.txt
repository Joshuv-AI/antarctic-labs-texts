Nonlinear Dose-Response and Calcium-Magnesium Interactions of Dietary Minerals with Cognitive Function in Older Adults
Authors: Xingchen He, Yaxuan Yan, Jiawen Wang, Yijia Lin, Xinyi Wu, Min Li, Tianyu Zhong, Xuliang Weng
Published: 2025

Introduction: Cognitive decline in older adults is a pressing public health challenge, with nutrition emerging as a modifiable factor [1]. Dietary minerals play critical roles in neuronal signaling and neuroprotection, but existing evidence on their association with cognitive function remains inconsistent, often overlooking nonlinear relationships and mineral interactions—particularly between calcium (Ca) and magnesium (Mg). Here, we present findings from a cross-sectional analysis of U.S. older adults, highlighting threshold effects and Ca-Mg synergism in relation to cognitive outcomes.

Methods: We analyzed cross-sectional data from 2,037 U.S. adults aged ≥60 years (NHANES 2011–2014). Cognitive function was assessed using standardized tests (CERAD, Animal Fluency Test, Digit Symbol Substitution Test) and combined into a global Z-score; impairment was defined as a score ≤25th percentile. Dietary intakes of sodium (Na), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), and phosphorus (P) were measured via two 24 -h recalls. Multivariable linear regression evaluated associations between mineral quartiles and cognitive Z-score, adjusting for age, sex, race, education, income, BMI, smoking, diabetes, and hypertension. Non-linear relationships were assessed using generalized additive models (GAMs) and threshold analysis. Stratified analyses to examine Ca-Mg interactions. Analyses accounted for NHANES complex survey design. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and EmpowerStats (version 5.0), accounting for the complex survey design of NHANES. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results: Significant L-shaped dose-response relationships were observed for Na, K, Ca, Mg, and P (Fig. 1). Positive associations with cognitive Z-score existed below specific inflection points: Na: 3634 mg/day (β = 0.03 per 100 mg/d, p < 0.0001), K: 2436 mg/day (β = 0.05, p < 0.0001), Ca: 443 mg/day (β = 0.27, p = 0.0013), Mg: 819 mg/day (β = 0.16, p < 0.0001), P: 1093 mg/day (β = 0.12, p < 0.0001). Associations plateaued above these points (all p > 0.05 above thresholds). Critically, Mg's cognitive benefits depended on Ca intake. Within the Mid-Ca stratum, both moderate (Mg Mid vs. Low: β = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.36–1.01, p < 0.0001) and high Mg intake (β = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.27–1.06, p = 0.0011) significantly associated cognitive scores. However, no significant associations were found for Mg within the Low-Ca (Mid Mg: β = 0.24, p = 0.16; High Mg: β = 0.47, p = 0.09) or High-Ca strata (Mid Mg: β = 0.02, p = 0.96; High Mg: β = 0.27, p = 0.60) (Table 1). Discussion: This study identifies two key findings for cognitive health in older adults: major dietary minerals show L-shaped relationships, with cognitive benefits leveling off after exceeding certain intake levels; additionally, the beneficial cognitive effects of magnesium depend on moderate calcium intake.

The neuroprotective mechanisms of Mg (e.g., NMDA receptor modulation, anti-neuroinflammation) appear synergistically enhanced within a moderate Ca window, aligning with their roles in neuronal signaling and calcium homeostasis [2,3]. High Ca intake likely antagonizes Mg absorption and bioactivity at neuronal sites [4]. The P threshold aligns with evidence linking excess intake to vascular calcification, impaired cerebral perfusion, and neuroinflammation via phosphate toxicity [5]. These findings identify mineral intake ceilings and reveal that magnesium's neuroprotection requires balanced calcium. Public health strategies should prioritize whole-food diets (e.g., leafy greens, nuts, legumes) over processed foods with isolated minerals to optimize cognitive aging. Limitations include the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference, and potential residual confounding from unmeasured factors like dietary patterns.
