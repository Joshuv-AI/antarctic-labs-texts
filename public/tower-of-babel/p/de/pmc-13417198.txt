Leveraging Morphological Profiling for Mechanistic Elucidation of Metal-Based Anticancer Compounds

ABSTRACT
Development of novel metallotherapeutics poses both significant potential and numerous challenges due to their complex reactivity in physiological systems and the ambiguity of molecular targets. Advances in target-agnostic and phenotypic-based drug profiling approaches have yielded success in anticancer therapeutic development, but their application to the development and discovery of metal-based drug candidates has been limited. This study demonstrates the use of morphological profiling on a diverse library of metal-containing compounds to elucidate mechanisms of action, providing new insights into the activities of established chemotherapeutics and guiding downstream studies of select novel compounds in the absence of predefined molecular targets. The validity of this strategy was established using a subset of clinically approved anticancer therapeutics, where morphological profiling discerned the mechanistic differences among oxaliplatin, cisplatin, carboplatin, and the clinical-stage ruthenium-based compound BOLD-100. Subsequently, a combination of cytotoxicity and selectivity assessments in conjunction with an analysis of morphological profiles of the compound library identified a novel ruthenium 2-(2-pyridyl)­benzimidazole-based compound series, which induced changes in the mitochondrial morphology of cancer cells. This association was investigated further, revealing an accompanying increase in levels of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species and a corresponding depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane. This application of morphological profiling will enable the rapid identification of promising metal-containing therapeutics, aiding in rational development strategies and mitigating persistent challenges in metallotherapeutic development.

Introduction

Metal-containing compounds have the potential to be highly efficacious anticancer therapeutics (metallotherapeutics) with diverse mechanisms of action (MOAs). Since the approval of cisplatin in 1978, platinum-containing agents have been utilized for the treatment of a range of advanced cancer malignancies including colon, ovary, lung, bladder, and cervical cancers and continue to be essential components of many current chemotherapeutic regimens. − The success of cisplatin and oxaliplatin in cancer therapy has emphasized the need for the accelerated development of novel metallotherapeutics. This can leverage unique chemical properties not available for standard organic molecules, such as diverse geometries, multiple oxidation states, and metal–ligand interactions, as well as the broad selection of metal centers available. However, this class of compounds can have multifaceted reactivity in physiological systems and ambiguity in their a priori cellular targets, which has posed challenges in understanding their MOAs and has hindered their translation into the clinic. − The number of metallotherapeutics in clinical development has consequently remained limited, and efforts to fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms of these novel therapeutics continue to be a major research focus. For example, it was only recently demonstrated that the primary MOA of oxaliplatin is dissimilar to its platinum relatives cisplatin and carboplatin, wherein oxaliplatin exerts its primary cytotoxicity via association with ribosomal biogenesis stress rather than by inducing DNA damage. − Continuous development and characterization of promising metallotherapeutics and their target landscape can significantly derisk the clinical translation of this class of promising molecules.

Phenotypic-based drug screening strategies utilize a target-agnostic approach to identify hit compounds for subsequent testing and development. In the metallotherapeutic space, the potential absence of a clear and identifiable cellular target exacerbates the difficulty of target-based approaches and, thus, makes phenotypic screening a desirable methodology. Furthermore, early insight into a potential MOA provided by phenotypic-based strategies can aid in the design of downstream validation assays for hit compounds. High-content image-based profiling methods enable the extraction of cellular morphological data, which, in turn, contain thousands of features describing the phenotypic changes conferred by the compounds of interest. This method has been used in both MOA determination and prediction, as well as bioactivity and efficacy prediction, in large screens of organic molecules. In previous studies of metallotherapeutics, the use of high-content imaging systems has focused on a limited number of specific compounds and has never been utilized in larger drug discovery screens. , A widely used application of image-based profiling is the Cell Painting assay, which uses stained images across fluorescent channels to label pertinent cellular compartments such as the nuclei, mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), cytoplasmic RNA, and the plasma membrane. − This profiling method enables the quantification of the morphological phenotype and grouping of similar perturbations, thereby revealing not only the phenotypic impact of the compound treatment but also the potential MOA based on the formed compound clusters. This transformation of complex data into compartmentalized interpretations makes this approach ideal for studying metallotherapeutics in the absence of clear molecular targets. BOLD-100, a leading clinical-stage ruthenium­(III)-based compound, exemplifies a metallotherapeutic with demonstrated therapeutic effectiveness and a unique MOA.

In the clinic, BOLD-100 is currently in development for the treatment of advanced gastrointestinal cancers with an ongoing phase 1b/2a study in combination with FOLFOX (fluorouracil, oxaliplatin, and leucovorin) (NCT04421820). Clinical results in colorectal, biliary tract, and gastric cancer patients have all demonstrated marked improvements in progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) compared to standard-of-care benchmarks in response to the BOLD-100 and FOLFOX regimen. − Mechanistically, BOLD-100 exhibits a multimodal MOA that includes a primary impact on the unfolded protein response (UPR) through the modulation of the 78 kDa glucose-regulated protein (GRP78), increased reactive oxygen species (ROS), and DNA damage. − Additional pathways such as lipid metabolism, ribosomal biogenesis, and mitochondrial alterations have also been proposed to contribute to the bioactivity of BOLD-100. − Investigations are ongoing to identify further mechanistic details of BOLD-100 and strengthen its clinical applicability. BOLD-100 highlights the complexity and multifaceted nature of metallotherapeutics and their MOAs, presenting opportunities for novel therapeutic developments.

To place mechanistic understanding at the forefront of metallotherapeutic discovery and to leverage the phenotypic-based approach to drug screening, a library of over 150 preclinical metal-containing compounds was subjected to the Cell Painting assay. Classical chemotherapeutic compounds (e.g., cisplatin, oxaliplatin, and carboplatin) as well as the clinical-stage ruthenium-based drug BOLD-100 were incorporated into the library to assess the methodology’s ability to compartmentalize and differentiate compounds based on their MOA. The results showed clustering of compounds based on their impact on cell morphology, which was used to extract their likely primary mechanistic pathways. Control compounds and a selection of discovery compounds were explored in further detail. To validate the application of the Cell Painting readout to assign potential anticancer mechanisms, the MOAs of oxaliplatin and BOLD-100 were investigated using a panel of molecular markers associated with key cellular pathways. Next, the Cell Painting result across the compound library was used to identify promising compounds with morphological evidence of a potential MOA. Cytotoxicity and selectivity in various cancer models were conducted in parallel to confirm the efficacy of the compounds. Using this combination of phenotypic profiling and bioactivity studies, a ruthenium 2-(2-pyridyl)­benzimidazole-based compound series was identified that exhibited a high degree of potency and an association with changes in mitochondrial morphology. This compound series was investigated further using the Cell Painting insights into the MOA to enable downstream assay design pertaining to specific cellular processes. This progression from unbiased exploration to targeted studies demonstrates the potential of this approach to expedite the process of hit identification and downstream assay development for novel metallotherapeutics.

Results

Library of Metal-Containing Compounds for Screening Reflects Diversity of Potential Metallotherapeutics

In total, a library of 158 metal-containing compounds and 20 organic compounds was assembled. The library contained a diverse set of metal-containing compounds, with 15 different metal centers represented across both organometallic and coordination complexes as well as polymetallic complexes (Figure A). Compounds containing metals that have shown the most clinical promise, such as ruthenium, gold, and platinum, were the most represented. Other emerging metals with potentially medicinal properties were also included, such as palladium, rhenium, osmium, and tungsten. − The library contained approved and clinical-stage metal compounds including cisplatin, oxaliplatin, carboplatin, and BOLD-100 (Figure B) but consisted primarily of discovery and preclinical compounds (88%).

Morphological Profiling Separates Metal Compounds Based on Their Potential Targeted Cellular Pathway

The total compound library (n = 178) was subjected to the Cell Painting assay in U-2 OS (osteosarcoma) cells in 8 constituent doses at 24 h. Out of 178 compounds, 14 were insufficiently soluble and were not tested in the subsequent assays. Four additional organic compounds (etoposide, monastrol, brefeldin A, and latrunculin B) were added to the study and included on each assay plate as a quality control check. In the Cell Painting assay, dyes for five major cellular compartments were used that stained the nuclei, RNA, the ER, actin, and the mitochondria. The image analysis software CellProfiler was used to extract single-cell morphological features, which were then aggregated and analyzed on a per-well basis. Using these morphological features, hierarchical clustering separated each sample into one of 26 defined cluster labels (Figure A). As the cluster labeling often varied across the constituent doses for each compound due to the continuum from cell death to limited phenotypic impact, a primary cluster label was assigned to each compound. The primary cluster for each compound was determined based on the highest dose that caused less than 50% cell death while inducing significant changes in at least 10% of morphological features compared to the DMSO controls (Figure B). Cluster assignments and IC50 values in the U-2 OS cell line for control compounds plus the compounds discussed in this manuscript are shown in Table S1.

To provide a biological interpretation of the clusters, the over-representation of phenotypic changes related to the five specific cellular compartments was assessed for each cluster label (Figure C). Specific morphological features were significantly altered in each specific cluster, and representative images from relevant clusters are in Figures S7–S12. Initially, mechanistic assignments were made by referencing clinically approved compounds in the library with known mechanisms of anticancer activity. For example, both cisplatin and carboplatin (Figure B) were labeled as cluster #10, which was over-represented by features related to changes in the morphology of the nuclei (Figure D), in accordance with their MOA of DNA chelation and subsequent DNA damage. , Alternatively, the platinum-based therapeutic oxaliplatin (Figure B) was labeled as cluster #1, which was characterized by changes in features related to the ER and the RNA (Figure D), reflecting oxaliplatin’s MOA associated with ribosome biogenesis and nucleolar stress. , Organic controls included in the library were also compartmentalized based on potentially distinct MOAs. For example, both etoposide and gemcitabine induce DNA damage, but through distinct mechanisms; etoposide acts as a topoisomerase II inhibitor, whereas gemcitabine functions as a nucleoside analog that disrupts DNA synthesis. These compounds were located in separate, defined clusters, with each cluster dominated by changes in nuclear morphology (etoposide, cluster #11; gemcitabine, cluster #10). Similarly, the clinical-stage ruthenium-based anticancer therapeutic BOLD-100 was labeled as cluster #9, which was over-represented by features related to changes in the ER and the mitochondria (Figure D). This is consistent with an MOA associated with ER stress and mitochondrial changes reported in earlier studies of BOLD-100. This compartmentalization approach also provided a foundation for understanding novel preclinical therapeutics. The viability of this exploratory approach was previously demonstrated using a single compound from this data set, with the ruthenium-based water-soluble molecule Ru-IM (Figure B) showing remarkable efficacy in a triple-negative breast cancer mouse model. Ru-IM was labeled as the mitochondrial-related cluster #8, and subsequent downstream investigations showed alterations in mitochondrial function. These results demonstrate the ability to differentially compartmentalize both related and distinct compounds and to hypothesize the potential MOA based on morphological profiles.

Although most compounds in the library were located in a defined cluster, 15.5% of the library did not obtain a cluster label assignment due to inactivity in the U-2 OS cells at up to 100 μM or due to all relevant concentrations showing significant cell death. This was associated with the cell death cluster #12. For example, the clinically approved gold-based compound auranofin had an IC50 of 0.98 μM in U-2 OS cells and was assigned to cluster #12 for three of its tested doses, with no other concentrations inducing significant changes in morphology compared to the DMSO controls.

Alterations in ER Stress Pathways in BOLD-100 Compared to Oxaliplatin

To investigate the biological pathways identified in the Cell Painting assay and to evaluate the validity of using morphological profiles as a proxy for MOAs, relevant protein markers were assessed following treatment with BOLD-100 and oxaliplatin. Analysis of the Cell Painting data assigned BOLD-100 and oxaliplatin to distinct clusters, both of which exhibited changes in the ER morphology. However, there was a distinction between the specific ER-related morphological features identified in each cluster. Cluster #9 (BOLD-100) showed numerous alterations in ER features associated with “distribution”, whereas cluster #1 (oxaliplatin) displayed changes in the ER features related to “texture” and “intensity” (Figures S7 and S9). This distinction suggests that while both compounds exert changes in the ER, the underlying processes behind such changes may be different, as reflected in the distinct morphological profiles. To assess this further, the influence of BOLD-100 and oxaliplatin on ER-related markers was investigated. As both compounds are utilized in the treatment of colorectal cancer, HT-29 cells (colon carcinoma) were used to explore the impact on the GRP78 protein. GRP78 regulates the UPR, which is a stress response closely associated with the accumulation of misfolded or unfolded proteins in the ER. BOLD-100 treatment led to a rapid, dose-dependent reduction in GRP78 levels 3 to 14 h post-treatment, followed by a return to the baseline by 24 h (Figure A). This GRP78 reduction was also observed at the transcriptional level (Figure S13). In a panel of six colon cancer cell lines, the cell lines that were most responsive to BOLD-100 treatment exhibited a greater reduction of the GRP78 protein, although there were cell-line-specific variations in kinetics and responses (Figure S14). Oxaliplatin treatment also led to a reduction in GRP78 protein levels, but the effect was observed at later time points and persisted for up to 48 h (Figure B). Negative control compounds, including cisplatin, 5-fluorouracil, and sodium arsenite (an integrated stress response (ISR) modulator), all exerted limited effects on GRP78 levels, whereas the known GRP78 inducer thapsigargin induced a significant increase (Figure B). Overall, the Cell Painting assay indicates that both BOLD-100 and oxaliplatin influence ER processes; however, their specific kinetic and functional effects are distinct.

To further elucidate the key differences between the influence of BOLD-100 and oxaliplatin on the ER, the three downstream arms of the UPR were assessed. BOLD-100 induced a limited impact on the protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) arm, as assessed by levels of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4). In contrast, BOLD-100 treatment affected the activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) arm, reflected by a large and rapid decrease in ATF6 levels. The inositol-requiring enzyme α (IRE1α) arm was also affected, demonstrated by large increases in spliced X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) levels (Figure S15). When assessed over a panel of colorectal cancer lines, the reduction in ATF6 was most pronounced in cells more responsive to BOLD-100 (Figure S14). Levels of C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP), which is a key pro-apoptotic protein induced by all three arms of the UPR, were also assessed. BOLD-100 treatment caused a dose-dependent activation of CHOP that was sustained over 24 h and led to an increase of downstream apoptotic markers such as cleaved caspase 3 (cCASP3) and cleaved poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (cPARP) at 14 and 24 h post-treatment (Figure C,E,G). CHOP, cCASP3, and cPARP levels were all heightened in cell lines responsive to BOLD-100 (Figure S14).

The effect of oxaliplatin on the constituent arms of the UPR was distinct from that of BOLD-100. Oxaliplatin exerted a limited elevation of ATF4, in contrast to the ISR and ATF4 inducer sodium arsenite. Oxaliplatin treatment led to a large reduction in ATF6 levels and an elevation in IRE1α levels at later time points (Figure S15). This resulted in limited activation of CHOP and an apoptotic response measured by cCASP3 and cPARP that was only observed after 48 h (Figure ). This dissimilar influence on the UPR pathways of BOLD-100 and oxaliplatin at the molecular level supports the interpretation of the Cell Painting assay. Both compounds were associated with the ER, but there are key molecular differences that manifest in distinct morphological profiles; oxaliplatin exerted slower alterations of the UPR, whereas BOLD-100 induced a rapid reduction in GRP78 and ATF6 levels, leading to activation of multiple arms of the UPR and subsequent CHOP-induced apoptosis.

Assessment of Nucleolar Stress and rRNAs

The Cell Painting assay also revealed that oxaliplatin induced alterations in the RNA compartment, in agreement with literature findings that suggest oxaliplatin may exert its cytotoxicity via disruptions in ribosome biogenesis. To assess this further, nucleolar stress was measured using the localization of the nucleolar stress marker nucleophosmin (NPM1) in HCT116 colorectal cancer cells. The translocation of the NPM1 marker throughout the nucleoplasm of the nucleolus was used as a proxy for nucleolar stress and nucleolar integrity disruption. This translocation of NPM1 was observed in response to oxaliplatin treatment and the positive control actinomycin D. Neither BOLD-100 nor cisplatin treatment led to distinguishable differences from the DMSO-treated cells, suggesting minimal or an absence of nucleolar stress induction (Figure S16). This further highlights the differences between cisplatin, BOLD-100, and oxaliplatin’s mode of action, as suggested by the Cell Painting assay.

Bioactivity Assays Reveal Efficacious Metal-Containing Compounds in 2D and 3D Cancer Models

To determine whether this mechanistic profiling approach can be used to gain insights into novel molecules with no prior mechanistic understanding, bioactivity screening in 2D and 3D cancer models was completed using the full compound library previously utilized in the Cell Painting assay (n = 178). Across the cytotoxicity assays in 2D models, most metal-containing compounds showed efficacy in the U-2 OS cell line (Figure A, 144/157; <100 μM), the HCT116 cell line (Figure B, 112/113; <100 μM), and the Hep-3B (hepatocellular carcinoma) cell line (Figure C, 76/77; <100 μM). Compounds determined to be inactive were periodically dropped throughout the progression of the screening. To identify compounds with a consistently high degree of cytotoxicity across the cancer types, a selection criterion of IC50 < 10 μM in the 3 cell lines tested was established, with 39 compounds passing this threshold. In the 3D spheroid model derived from the MDA-MB-231 (triple-negative breast cancer) cell line, compounds were generally less responsive (Figure D, 49/117; <100 μM). In total, 49 compounds passed the criterion of IC50 < 100 μM in the spheroid model.

Assessing selectivity in 2D models showed that a significant number of compounds were more cytotoxic in HCT116 cells than in the noncancerous MRC-5 (human fibroblast) cells (Figure E). In MRC-5 cells, the median IC50 across the panel was 9.4 μM, while the median selectivity index (SI) across the panel (MRC-5 vs HCT116) was 1.25. Compounds with greater activity in the noncancerous cell line (SI threshold of <1) were not considered for further study. In total, 69 compounds passed the selectivity criterion (Figure F).

Identification of Hit Compounds for Further Development

Using a collective bioactivity criterion of broad efficacy in 2D models (IC50 < 10 μM in the 3 cell lines), the 3D model (IC50 < 100 μM), and selectivity (SI > 1), 17 compounds were identified. This pool of compounds includes 6 different metal centers (Ru, Pd, Au, Re, W, and Cu), which were assigned 7 different clusters, with ruthenium and cluster #8 being the most represented, respectively. Therefore, we selected a ruthenium 2-(2-pyridyl)­benzimidazole-based compound series (Br-PBZ-Ru and Cl-PBZ-Ru; Figure B), which fell into cluster #8, for subsequent screening. Br-PBZ-Ru and its closely related chlorine derivative Cl-PBZ-Ru, which has a 4-chloro-2-pyridinyl instead of a 4-bromo-2-pyridinyl, were tested for cytotoxicity in additional cancer cell lines HT-29 and Hep G2 (hepatocellular carcinoma) (Table ). The cell line HEK293T (human embryonic kidney) was also used to assess levels of cytotoxicity in an additional noncancerous cell line.

Br-PBZ-Ru Causes Elevation of Mitochondrial ROS and Depolarization of the Mitochondrial Membrane

Br-PBZ-Ru and Cl-PBZ-Ru were both labeled as cluster 8 in the Cell Painting readout, with all five of the top doses of Br-PBZ-Ru labeled as part of this cluster. This cluster was associated with mitochondrial morphological changes (Figure C,D), and image analysis confirmed distinct alterations in the mitochondrial channel (Figures A and S17). Notably, the top 22 over-represented features in cluster #8 were all derived from the fluorescence channel of the mitochondrial dye (Figure S8), including features related to the texture, intensity, distribution, and granularity of the mitochondria.

The cellular impact of Br-PBZ-Ru on mitochondrial function was assessed using in vitro assays. Mitochondrial potential depolarization in HCT116 and HT-29 cells was investigated using the JC-1 dye. Br-PBZ-Ru and its analogue Cl-PBZ-Ru induced mitochondrial depolarization at 3, 6, and 14 h post-treatment, suggesting a potential impact on mitochondrial dysfunction (Figure B). In comparison, BOLD-100 also induced changes in mitochondrial morphology as per the Cell Painting result and has previously been shown to induce destabilization of mitochondrial potential, as measured by the JC-1 assay. However, BOLD-100 exhibited a minimal impact on the JC-1 signal in the HCT116 and HT-29 cell lines in the current study. This further emphasizes the distinct MOAs of the PBZ-Ru compounds and BOLD-100 and was subsequently highlighted by the apparent limited impact of Br-PBZ-Ru on the UPR response, as per molecular-based assays (Figure S18).

As metal-containing compounds can often influence redox homeostasis, cellular ROS and mitochondrial ROS (mROS) levels were assessed with respect to Br-PBZ-Ru treatment. Using the dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFDA) dye, which primarily measures hydrogen peroxide, neither Br-PBZ-Ru nor Cl-PBZ-Ru treatment led to an elevation in cellular ROS in HCT116 and HT-29 cells, in contrast to BOLD-100 and the positive control compounds (Figure C). The level of mROS was assessed using the mitoSOX Green dye, which quantifies levels of superoxides. Both Br-PBZ-Ru and Cl-PBZ-Ru treatment in HCT116 and HT-29 cells led to an elevation of mROS (Figure D). The JC-1, cellular ROS, and mROS results were confirmed in the U-2 OS cell line (Figure S19). The lack of correlation between ROS and mROS levels after treatment with Br-PBZ-Ru suggests that while this compound may cause mitochondrial dysfunction, its mode of action may differ from that of traditional ROS-inducing agents. Collectively, in conjunction with the JC-1 assay result, these observations suggest that Br-PBZ-Ru affects cellular processes related to the mitochondria in accordance with its cytotoxic effects. As such, the current screening platform was able to utilize morphological profiling and traditional screening methods to bridge hit identification and guided validation assays.

Discussion

The strength of phenotypic-based profiling, which assesses therapeutic effects in disease models with no prior biases toward a particular target, has improved with increased throughput and assay sensitivity. , Despite these advancements, high-throughput profiling assays such as cell painting have seen limited utilization in the development of anticancer metal-containing compounds. , In this study, a morphological profiling screening strategy was applied to a diverse metal-based compound library. Leveraging unsupervised clustering methodologies, the impact on cell morphology was used to identify potential pathways of importance for the library of metal-based compounds, providing direction for subsequent screening and mechanistic investigations. This work led to an enhanced understanding of the approved chemotherapeutic oxaliplatin, further distinguishing its mechanism from that of its platinum relatives and supporting evidence in the literature. , Additional mechanistic insights were obtained on the clinical-stage ruthenium compound BOLD-100, specifically the kinetics of its modulation of the UPR pathway. By the integration of phenotypic-based profiling and additional bioactivity assays, potential next-generation metal-based compounds were identified from the compound library, providing a foundation for further development. Collectively, this approach to infer MOAs based on morphological profiling can extend the understanding of metal-containing compounds with diverse chemical properties, overcoming some of the challenges in metallotherapeutic drug discovery and guiding the progression from explorative profiling studies to targeted developmental strategies.

The morphological profiling approach used here was benchmarked against the commonly used platinum therapeutics such as cisplatin, carboplatin, and oxaliplatin. While cisplatin and carboplatin are classical DNA-damaging agents, the related compound oxaliplatin has been shown to exert a distinct MOA closely associated with ribosomal biogenesis stress. , This is consistent with the Cell Painting results, which show that cisplatin and carboplatin mainly affect the nucleus morphology, whereas oxaliplatin altered the RNA compartment, in addition to the observed subnuclear localization of NPM1. − Oxaliplatin was also identified to impact the ER compartment and the UPR pathway with downregulation of GRP78, a mechanistic feature investigated in only a limited number of prior studies. , These results indicate that oxaliplatin can alter the molecular components of the UPR pathway, but this did not lead to an upregulation of the downstream pro-apoptosis marker CHOP. These findings highlight the value of unbiased phenotypic-based screening in confirming known MOAs and identifying previously unknown molecular pathways for established clinical treatments.

The understanding of BOLD-100’s MOA continues to expand throughout its clinical development, with the UPR pathway identified as a key mechanistic component. , The time course assessed in this study revealed that the molecular impact of BOLD-100 treatment on the UPR and its key modulator GRP78 is rapid, occurring within the first few hours post-treatment. This early effect potentially explains the limited impact of BOLD-100 on GRP78 levels seen in previous work that focused on later time points, , emphasizing the importance of investigating kinetic details in such studies. Furthermore, BOLD-100 affected the multiple molecular arms of the UPR with notable emphasis on the IRE1 and ATF6 components, distinguishing BOLD-100 from the classical UPR or ISR inducers that predominantly induce apoptosis through the PERK arm. The current finding that BOLD-100 and oxaliplatin both impact ER features, but were assigned different clusters, highlights the downstream results showing the two compounds had markedly different activation of ER/UPR proteins. Although the biological significance of these different clusters was not determined, cell studies of BOLD-100 suggest an alternative cellular sensitivity profile compared to oxaliplatin. Interestingly, in clinical studies for the treatment of advanced colorectal cancer, BOLD-100 has been coadministered with oxaliplatin and 5-fluorouracil as part of its chemotherapeutic regimen. − The current finding that both BOLD-100 and oxaliplatin reduce GRP78 levels and activate the UPR suggests a potential mechanistic basis for their synergy during treatment.

The application of the Cell Painting assay extends to novel compounds in early preclinical studies. From the morphological profiling readout and similarity-based analysis, cluster #8 was identified as the group containing the largest number of metal-containing compounds from the library. Notably, no organic control compounds were assigned to this cluster label. In terms of morphological effects, this cluster contained the highest degree of altered mitochondrial features, suggesting a mechanistic relationship between these metal-containing compounds and mitochondrial dysfunction. The mitochondrion is the key modulator of metabolic processes and redox homeostasis, impacting malignant tumor progression and drug resistance mechanisms in cancer cells. Consequently, targeting mitochondria by metallotherapeutics has been studied previously. A recent example is the ruthenium-based compound Ru-IM (Figure B), which has been studied previously using this morphological profiling data set. Ru-IM was one of the compounds assigned to cluster #8, and its association with mitochondrial alterations was previously identified. Numerous anticancer agents target signaling pathways upstream of the mitochondria, and directly targeting the mitochondrion itself has also emerged as an alternative strategy for cancer therapy. , Additionally, as the mitochondria serve as the modulator of redox homeostasis, inducing excess ROS continues to be an effective method in anticancer therapy. In particular, there is evidence that elevated mROS has the potential to perturb cancer cells’ ability to regulate redox homeostasis for survival. Therefore, mitochondrial processes represent an intriguing avenue for novel metallotherapeutic development in oncology.

From the compound library presented in this paper, a novel ruthenium 2-(2-pyridyl)­benzimidazole (PBZ-Ru) compound series was identified to be highly efficacious and to target mitochondrial processes, as indicated by the assignment to cluster #8 in morphological profiling. Benzimidazole derivatives are among the most frequently used heterocycle systems for small-molecule drugs. These molecular scaffolds and their derivatives allow for a great range of diversity, pharmacological properties, and synthetic accessibility. Previous studies have demonstrated promising anticancer activity of benzimidazole derivatives via diverse mechanisms targeting a variety of cellular processes. , There also exists a large synthetic space to derivatize and tune both the chemical and biological properties of benzimidazole compounds. In the current study, attaching a pyridyl ring to the benzimidazole scaffold (i.e., formation of the PBZ structure) enabled stable bidentate coordination to the Ru center of the metal complexes. In vitro cell studies of the PBZ-Ru compounds (Br-PBZ-Ru and Cl-PBZ-Ru) altered mitochondrial morphology and increased mROS levels, indicating a mechanism involving redox homeostasis and mitochondrial dysfunction. Furthermore, the elevation of mROS occurred in the absence of global cellular ROS induction, suggesting that mitochondrial alterations may be a primary effect rather than a consequence of systemic cellular redox imbalance. These insights on PBZ-Ru highlight the potential of morphological profiling in metallotherapeutic discovery for guiding hypothesis-driven design through structure–activity relationships.

The Cell Painting assay provided valuable insights into a range of metal-based compounds, but assay-based limitations impeded drawing conclusions for a subset of the compounds tested. Shared morphological similarities do not necessarily translate into a shared mechanism of action, and upstream mechanisms may not generate readily detectable morphological changes via this assay set. This emphasizes the results of cell painting as primarily a starting point for downstream mechanism studies. For example, for a group of compounds, exemplified by auranofin, the concentrations used either conferred no phenotypic change from the vehicle control or resulted in a cluster #12 assignment. This cluster label largely represented cell populations with a significantly lower cell count, signifying a dead or dying cell population. As auranofin has a well-defined MOA, this may reflect a limited dose range for observing biologically relevant morphological changes in U2-OS cells, which may be mitigated by using a broader window of test concentrations. Further, the current work was wide in the scope of the size and diversity of its metal-containing compounds; however, this diversity may limit the ability to infer structure–activity relationships. This work focused on a subset of the compounds utilized in the screen; many of the compounds were not disclosed. This limited the number of compounds used in validating the assays and limited the use of the work as a large-scale profiling resource. Collectively, this necessitates follow-up experiments focusing on specific subsets or structural series of related compounds.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that high-content morphological profiling can serve as an effective target-agnostic approach for the mechanistic interrogation and prioritization of metal-based anticancer compounds. Application of the Cell Painting assay to a chemically diverse metallotherapeutic library enabled differentiation of clinically relevant agents according to their phenotypic signatures. Established distinctions among platinum therapeutics were captured using this approach, and additional insight was provided into the cellular responses associated with the clinical-stage ruthenium compound BOLD-100. Integration of phenotypic clustering with complementary biochemical assays supported these assignments and illustrates how morphological profiling can generate experimentally testable mechanistic hypotheses for metal-containing compounds whose molecular targets are not well-defined.

Beyond benchmarking known therapeutics, this strategy facilitated the identification of a ruthenium 2-(2-pyridyl)­benzimidazole compound series exhibiting selective cytotoxicity and mitochondrial-associated phenotypes, including membrane depolarization and elevated mitochondrial reactive oxygen species. These observations highlight the utility of phenotypic profiling for linking unbiased screening outcomes to subsequent mechanistic studies and for guiding early-stage development of metallotherapeutics.

Overall, these findings establish morphological profiling as a useful component of the metallodrug discovery toolkit capable of providing early mechanistic insight alongside traditional bioactivity assessments. Continued integration of phenotypic screening with molecular and chemical characterization will further clarify the mechanisms of action and support the development of next-generation metal-based anticancer agents.

Methods

Acquisition of Metal-Based Compounds

A library of 158 preclinical metal-containing compounds was assembled with a wide representation of the metal centers. Most of the compounds were preclinical compounds, but clinically relevant metallotherapeutics including BOLD-100, oxaliplatin, cisplatin, carboplatin, and auranofin were included. The following organic compounds were included in the library: 5-fluorouracil, paclitaxel, gemcitabine, thapsigargin, plumbagin, dorsomorphin dihydrochloride, bimiralisib, and PAC-1. This gave a total of 178 compounds in the library. The majority of the metal-containing compounds are not disclosed in this work.

Bioactivity Assays: Cytotoxicity Screening in U-2 OS

A tetrazolium dye-based MTT assay was used to assess the cytotoxicity of the compound library in U-2 OS cell lines (Center ffor High-Throughput Chemical Biology (HTCB) at Simon Fraser University; Burnaby, BC, Canada) (n = 178). All compounds were solubilized in DMSO to 20 mM, with any insoluble compounds excluded from the assay. Stock solutions were diluted to a top concentration of 100 μM. Each compound was tested at 8 doses using 3× serial dilutions. U-2 OS cells were treated 24 h after seeding, and the MTT solution was added 24 h after treatment. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm to assess the metabolic activity as a proxy of cell viability. All samples were tested in a single replicate.

Morphological Profiling: Assay and Data Acquisition

The morphological profiling assay and downstream image and data acquisition were carried out by the HTCB at Simon Fraser University (Burnaby, BC, Canada). The compound library (n = 178) was subjected to the high-content image-based profiling assay known as the Cell Painting assay in U-2 OS (osteosarcoma) cells as per the HTCB’s standard protocol. Four additional control compounds (etoposide, monastrol, brefeldin A, and latrunculin B) were included on each assay plate. Maximum treatment concentrations of 100, 50, 20, 10, or 1 μM were selected for each compound based on the closest standard concentration approximating 27× its IC50 value determined via the MTT assay. Each compound was then tested in 8 dilution doses at 3× serial dilutions. The time point was 24 h post-treatment with compounds. The assay yielded a collection of microscopy images at a single-cell resolution, which consisted of 25 total images per sample over 5 fluorescence channels. Dyes used were MitoTracker (mitochondria), Wheat Germ Agglutinin (Golgi, plasma membrane), Concanavalin A (endoplasmic reticulum), Phalloidin (Actin), Syto 14 (nucleoli, cytoplasmic RNA), and Hoechst (DNA). Image analysis software CellProfiler was used to extract the morphological features of each sample at a single-cell level. Each morphological feature was then aggregated from the single-cell level to the sample level by using their geometric means. This feature aggregation step yielded each sample with its morphological profile of over 2000 features encoded in continuous values.

Morphological Profiling: Data AnalysisPreprocessing, Normalization, and Feature Selection

All programming tasks were executed using Python (version 3.8.5) and base modules NumPy (version 1.24.0) and Pandas (version 2.1.0), unless stated otherwise. To infer the level of cytotoxicity for each sample tested, normalized cell counts were calculated for each well and used as a proxy for cell viability. To remove wells with a sufficiently low number of remaining cells, samples with fewer than 10% normalized cell counts were excluded from analysis. This led to a total sample size of n = 1812 (1497 test samples, 160 vehicle controls, and 155 positive controls).

Feature-level normalization was performed by using the z-score transformation. Z-scores for each feature were calculated by using the parameters obtained from the vehicle control wells. Each plate was normalized separately to minimize plate-to-plate batch effects. After normalization, features with more than 25% missing values were removed. Additionally, features with low overall variance were removed using the nearZeroVar function from the R package caret (version 6.0–94) with the parameter freqCut = 0.05. Highly correlated features were removed using the findCorrelation function from the R package caret (version 6.0–94) and a Pearson correlation coefficient threshold of 0.9 to address multicollinearity.

Morphological Profiling: Data AnalysisHierarchical Clustering

Distance calculation between each morphological profile was performed by using the Python module SciPy (version 1.11.2). After the control samples were removed, cosine distance and complete linkage calculations were used for agglomerative hierarchical clustering. The linkage dendrogram was cut at a vertical height of 70% maximum linkage (i.e., SciPy version 1.11.2 default parameter setting) to define the clusters. T-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) was used for unsupervised visualization of the defined clusters using SciPy, which identified the optimal low-dimensional projection by minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence score. Representations in two dimensions were used for visualization using the Python package Seaborn (ver. 0.12).

Morphological Profiling: Data AnalysisMorphological Feature Analysis

To identify prevalent morphological features in each cluster identified above, two-tailed pairwise Student’s t-tests (α < 0.05) between each cluster’s morphological features and the overall vehicle controls’ features were performed. Additionally, to identify which types of morphological features were over-represented in each cluster, each feature was categorized based on the cell compartmental dyes used to extract them, which consisted of the ER, nuclei, mitochondria, RNA, and actin. Hypergeometric tests using the Python module SciPy (α < 0.05) were then performed for each cluster based on the total set of features and the features that were identified as statistically significant in the t-test versus the vehicle controls.

Morphological Profiling: Data AnalysisCompound Cluster Assignment for the Test Panel

As each compound in the library was tested as a dilution series of 8 total samples, an assignment of a singular cluster labeling was given for each compound. A single cluster was assigned to each compound in the library according to the following criteria. Samples with less than 50% cell counts were not considered. Additionally, the fraction of significantly altered features (absolute z-score of >2) compared to DMSO samples was calculated. Only samples with >10% significantly altered features were considered. The morphological cluster at the highest available concentration that was not assigned to cluster #12 for each sample was then chosen as the cluster assignment. Cluster #12 was omitted from the primary cluster determinations due to this cluster exhibiting the lowest percentage cell counts by far (43.3%) and due to many compounds being assigned to this cluster at their highest doses.

Cellular Marker Testing in BOLD-100 and Other Standard Compounds

HT-29 (colorectal carcinoma) cells were treated with BOLD-100 (25, 50, or 100 μM), oxaliplatin (40 μM), 5-fluorouracil (20 μM), cisplatin (10 μM), sodium arsenite (20 μM), or thapsigargin (100 nM). Cell lysates were collected 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h after drug treatment. Lysates were subjected to protein quantification using the Jess Automated Western Blot System (Bio-Techne) to quantify levels of the following markers: GRP78, CHOP, cleaved CASP3, cleaved PARP, ATF, ATF6, IRE1α, and XBP1. The standard protein normalization method was used, and the expression of each protein marker was represented as the normalized percentage relative to DMSO-treated samples.

Additionally, HT-29 cells were treated with BOLD-100 (100 μM), oxaliplatin (20 μM), cisplatin (10 μM), and 5-fluorouracil (20 μM). RNA samples were collected after 1 and 6 h of treatment. Levels of GRP78 and CHOP transcripts were assessed using quantitative PCR.

Nucleophosmin Imaging Using Immunofluorescence

HCT116 (colorectal carcinoma) cells were treated with BOLD-100 (100 μM), oxaliplatin (20 μM), cisplatin (10 μM), 5-fluorouracil (20 μM), actinomycin D (32 nM), or sodium arsenite (20 μM). After 14 h, cells were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min) and blocked (5% bovine serum albumin). Cells were stained with the NPM1 monoclonal (Alexa Fluor 555) and antimouse IgG (Alexa Fluor 488 conjugate) antibody for immunofluorescence. DAPI was used for nuclear staining. Stained cells were imaged using fluorescence microscopy.

Bioactivity Assays: Further Cytotoxicity Screening

Further cell viability assays were performed using the Guava ViaCount fluorescent dye reagents (Luminex, TX, USA) with the Guava EasyCyte 6HT high-throughput flow cytometer (EMD-Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). HCT116 and Hep-3B (liver adenocarcinoma) cell lines as well as MDA-MB-231 (triple-negative breast adenocarcinoma) spheroid models were tested in the ViaCount assay. HCT116 and Hep-3B cells were seeded 24 h prior to treatment. MDA-MB-231 spheroids were cultured for 24 days. Compound treatment was performed at the following concentrations: 100, 50, 10, 5, 1, 0.5, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001 μM for 72 h. All samples were tested in a single replicate.

The obtained dose–response data for the MTT and the ViaCount assays were subjected to standard model fitting. Using the R package drc (version 3.0–1), a four-parameter log–logistic curve was fitted with the upper bound for the upper limit of the curve set to 1.25 and the lower bound for the lower limit of the curve set to 0. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was obtained from the midpoint of the log–logistic curve.

Bioactivity Assays: Selectivity in Noncancerous vs Cancerous Models

The ViaCount assay measuring the cytotoxicity of the compound library was performed in the MRC-5 (lung fibroblast) cell line using the methods described above (n = 113). The IC50 values were calculated for each compound as described above. The selectivity index (SI) was calculated as the ratio between the IC50 in the MRC-5 and the HCT116 cells for each compound. All samples were tested in a single replicate.

Additional Cytotoxicity Screening of Br-PBZ-Ru

Both Br-PBZ-Ru and its analog Cl-PBZ-Ru were subjected to cytotoxicity screening in HCT116 cells using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega; Madison, WI, USA) and IncuCyte Live Cell Imaging Assay (Sartorius) at 72 h. Top doses of 100 μM were used with 3× serial dilutions of 8 constituent concentrations. IC50 values were calculated as above. Additionally, Br-PBZ-Ru and Cl-PBZ-Ru were subjected to the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Assay and the IncuCyte Live Cell Imaging Assay as above in the Hep G2 (liver adenocarcinoma) and the HEK293T (human embryonic kidney) cell lines. The top dose of 100 μM was used with 2× serial dilutions of 11 constituent concentrations. IC50 values were calculated as before.

Reactive Oxygen Species Quantification of Br-PBZ-Ru

To quantify levels of cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), HCT116, HT-29, and U-2 OS cells were treated with the following compounds: Cl-PBZ-Ru (10 μM), Br-PBZ-Ru (10 μM), and BOLD-100 (100 μM). Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2; 300 μM) and tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP; 250 μM) were used as positive controls. A DCFDA/H2DCFDA Cellular ROS Assay Kit (Abcam) was used to quantify the ROS signal 0.5, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h post-treatment. All global ROS measurements were normalized to DMSO-treated controls.

Additionally, levels of mitochondrial ROS (mROS) were assessed. HCT116, HT-29, and U-2 OS cells were treated with Br-PBZ-Ru or Cl-PBZ-Ru at two concentrations (2 and 10 μM). As before, H2O2 (500 μM) was used as a positive control. Levels of mROS in HCT116 and HT-29 cells were quantified using the normalized intensity of the MitoSOX Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (MitoSox Green, ThermoFisher) 50-, 90-, and 180 min post-treatment. Levels of mROS in U-2 OS cells were quantified via flow cytometry, and data were expressed as the median fluorescence intensity relative to DMSO-treated controls. All samples were run in triple replicates.

Mitochondrial Depolarization Assessment of Br-PBZ-Ru

To evaluate mitochondrial depolarization, HCT116, HT-29, and U-2 OS cells were treated with Br-PBZ-Ru (8 μM), Cl-PBZ-Ru (8 μM), or BOLD-100 (100 μM). Carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP; 50 μM) was used as a positive control. The membrane-permeant cationic dye JC-1 was used as an indicator for the mitochondrial membrane potential. Fluorescence measurements were conducted at an excitation wavelength of 475 ± 20 nm and an emission wavelength of 530 ± 15 nm and 590 ± 17.5 nm using the Tecan Plate Reader. The extent of mitochondrial depolarization was expressed as the ratio of 590 to 535 nm fluorescence intensity, expressed as a percentage change from DMSO-treated samples. Measurements for HCT116 and HT-29 cells were taken at 3-, 6-, and 14 h post-treatment, while measurements for U-2 OS cells were conducted at 1-, 3-, and 6 h post-treatment. All samples were run in triple replicates.
