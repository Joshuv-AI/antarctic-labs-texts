Nucleoid-associated protein HU constrains transcription elongation to influence cellular functions, virulence, and drug tolerance in Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Authors: Meghna Santoshi, Chinmay Anand, Muzammil Hussain, Raju S Rajmani, Soumitra Ghosh, Valakunja Nagaraja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe nucleoid-associated proteins (NAPs) are the primary organizers of chromosome architecture, function, and regulators of gene expression in bacteria. Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) encodes a limited set of NAPs, among which HU (MtHU) is essential for the organism. While MtHU’s DNA-binding properties are well documented, the genome-wide regulatory circuit by which it coordinates transcription remains to be elucidated. Here, we combine CRISPR interference (CRISPRi), chemical inhibition, ChIP-seq, RNA-seq, and RNA polymerase (RNAP) occupancy experiments to dissect MtHU function. Conditional depletion of MtHU resulted in transcriptional deregulation of over 800 genes involved in various cellular processes, indicating its dominant role in controlling gene expression. ChIP-seq analysis revealed widespread MtHU binding across the genome, including intergenic regions and within gene bodies. The large-scale occupation of MtHU within open reading frames (ORFs) hampers RNA polymerase elongation during transcription. MtHU depletion thus resulted in upregulation of genes, indicating its predominant negative regulatory role. Limiting MtHU during Mtb infection resulted in decreased intracellular survival and decreased antibiotic tolerance, linking gene regulation to host-pathogen dynamics. Our findings expand the functional landscape of MtHU, uncovering its multifaceted roles in transcriptional control, immune modulation, and antibiotic tolerance, opening up potential opportunities for the development of new treatment regimens.

Introduction

Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) is one of the most successful pathogens that humans have encountered [1]. Its success as a pathogen is attributed to its remarkable ability to persist within host macrophages, tolerate environmental and antibiotic-induced stresses, and manipulate host immune responses [2, 3]. For Mtb to adapt to hostile microenvironments that include oxidative and nitrosative stress, acidic pH, and nutritional restriction [4–8], the pathogen must reprogram its gene expression. It has evolved a number of Mtb-specific sigma factors, two-component systems, and other regulators to control gene expression and adapt to these conditions [9–11]. The role of nucleoid-associated proteins (NAPs), which control genome architecture and gene expression, although well studied in several bacteria [12–15], needs to be explored in detail in Mtb.

The repertoire of NAPs is relatively small in Mtb compared to other bacteria. Only seven NAPs have been functionally characterized so far [16]. Among them, few are conserved across eubacteria, and others are specific to the genus Mycobacterium. Although MtHU (also known as HupB) is one of the conserved NAPs [17] and similar to other bacterial HUs, the similarity is restricted to only its N-terminal DNA-binding domain [18]. Apart from this conserved N-terminal half, MtHU has a distinctive C-terminal lysine-rich extension that resembles eukaryotic histone H1 [18]. Activity of MtHU has been shown to be modulated by the C-terminal domain (CTD) through its ability to interact with other proteins and post-translational modifications (PTMs) [19–26]. Modifications on MtHU, such as acetylation [22], succinylation [20], and phosphorylation [23], have been shown to alter its DNA-binding activity. MtHU compacts the DNA, stimulates the relaxation activity of topoisomerase I, and binds a range of DNA repair and replication intermediates [16, 18, 27, 28]. Various transposon-based essentiality screens have consistently identified MtHU to be indispensable for Mtb survival [29–32]. Our earlier studies aimed to validate these findings confirmed that MtHU is essential for the survival of Mtb and can be targeted using small molecule inhibitors [18].

Despite its essentiality and druggability, a comprehensive functional dissection of MtHU is yet to be carried out. Although biochemical studies have described the NAP’s DNA-binding properties, its genome-wide binding profile and correlation with transcriptional changes remain to be investigated. Further, its role in Mtb physiology, host-pathogen crosstalk, and immune stimulation also needs to be determined. To address these points and to evaluate its overall contribution towards Mtb biology, a conditional knockdown (cKD) approach using CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) and chemical inhibition of MtHU was employed in this study. We demonstrate that MtHU binds and regulates a diverse set of genes, thereby altering intracellular survival and antibiotic tolerance of Mtb. By stimulating immune effectors, it also influences the pathogen’s interaction with the host.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

Different Mtb strains were grown in Middlebrook 7H9 broth supplemented with 0.2% glycerol, 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 0.2% dextrose, 0.0003% catalase, and 0.085% NaCl (ADC) with 0.05% Tween-80. Anhydrotetracycline [ATc] was added to the secondary culture at OD600 = 0.1. For culturing on a solid medium, Mtb strains were spotted on 7H11 agar supplemented with OADC and 0.2% glycerol. The Mtb H37Rv cells were grown to the exponential phase in 7H9 broth [with 1× ADC, 0.02% glycerol, 0.05% Tween 80, and 0.01% sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS)]. At OD600 = 0.6, the cells were treated with SD1 [18] or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), incubated for 8 h at 37°C, and then proceeded for RNA isolation. Hygromycin (Hyg), kanamycin (Kan), chloramphenicol (Chl), and ATc were used at final concentrations of 50 μg ml−1, 25 μg ml−1, 25 μg ml−1, and 200 ng ml−1, respectively, unless stated otherwise.

Generation of a conditional knockdown of MtHU (HU-cKD)

For the construction of the HU-cKD strain, the CRISPRi method was used. An inactive version of Streptococcus pyogenes cas9, having mutations D10A and H820A (dcas9), was expressed by pRH2502 from a TetR-regulated uvtetO promoter [33]. MtHU-specific small guide RNA (gRNA) targeting the nontemplate strand of the hupB gene 19 bp downstream of the promoter (Supplementary Table S1) was cloned into the pRH2521 vector (HU-cKD strain) under the control of a TetR-regulated smyc promoter (Pmyc1tetO). pRH2521 without a cloned gRNA was transformed into Mtb H37Rv-dCas9 cells to obtain the vector control (control strain). The colonies obtained after electroporation were cultured and screened for a decrease in HU levels upon gRNA induction with 200 ng/ml ATc (anhydrotetracycline) using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and western blotting. For all the experiments, the control and HU-cKD strains were treated with ATc unless stated otherwise. An in-silico analysis was performed using the tool Cas-OFFinder to assess potential off-target activity. The analysis identified only the targeted hupB locus with perfect match criteria, and no off-targets with less than five mismatches in the seed region were identified, indicating negligible risk of functional off-target activity.

RNA extraction and qPCR

The Mtb cells were grown in the presence or absence of ATc to the exponential phase (OD600 = 0.6–0.8). The cells were pelleted down and disrupted by bead beating using zirconia–silica beads in Trizol (Sigma–Aldrich). RNA isolation was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of RNA were determined spectrophotometrically. Purified total RNA (1 μg) was incubated with random hexamer primers at 70°C for 5 min. After cooling on ice, 1 mM dNTPs and 1 U multiscribe reverse transcriptase (Applied Biosystems) were added in a final volume of 20 μl. The reactions were incubated at 37°C for 2 h and then terminated by incubation at 70°C for 5 min. Further, qPCR was carried out in the 10 μl reactions consisting of 1 × Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), 1 mM of each primer (Supplementary Table S1), and 1 μl of complementary DNA (cDNA). Then, 16s ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was used as a reference gene for normalization. Reactions were carried out in duplicates in an Applied Biosystems Quant Studio 6 Real-Time PCR system.

Immunoblot analysis

Protein lysates were resolved on a 12% sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Merck). Membranes were incubated in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) blocking buffer [10 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween-20] with 3% (w/v) BSA for 2 h prior to incubation with primary antibodies diluted (1:15 000) in PBS with 2% BSA overnight at 4°C. Membranes were washed three times in PBST (0.1% Tween-20) and then incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (GE Amersham) were added to detect the protein of interest using a chemiluminescent substrate (Millipore).

Growth analysis

To monitor the growth, control and HU-cKD strains were spotted on 7H11-agar plates containing ATc and incubated at 37°C. In the liquid medium, the strains were grown till exponential-phase (OD600 = 0.4–0.6) and diluted in 7H9 media supplemented with ADC to OD600 = 0.05. The growth curve profiles were obtained by measuring the OD600 of these cultures every 24 h and plotting the data using GraphPad Prism. Growth defect in colony-forming units of HU-cKD after ATc treatment was estimated by plating serial dilutions on 7H11 agar plates. Cultures of control and HU-cKD strains were treated with or without ATc at OD600 = 0.1, and aliquots were taken out for serial dilution and plating at 0-, 24-, 48-, and 72 h post addition of ATc. Colonies were counted after 3 weeks, and colony-forming unit (CFU)/ml was calculated and plotted using GraphPad Prism.

RNA sequencing

The RNA samples were prepared in duplicates for library preparation. The samples were ribo-depleted using a RiboMinusTM transcriptome Isolation Kit, followed by fragmentation and reverse transcription to generate cDNA. The cDNAs were later end-repaired, followed by A-tailing and adapter ligation. Ampure beads were used to purify adapter-ligated fragments, and the purified product was amplified using Illumina Multiplex Adapter primers to generate a sequencing library with barcodes for each sample. The library was quantified using the Qubit DNA high-sensitivity quantification assay, and library quality was analysed through the Bioanalyzer 2100 using an Agilent 1500 DNA kit. The quality control (QC)-passed libraries were run on Illumina HiSeq as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The reads were mapped to the reference genome using the bowtie v2.2.5 aligner [34], and gene-level expression values were obtained using the featureCounts [35] software. Differential expression analysis was carried out using DESeq2 [36], and Genes with absolute fold change ≥1.5 and adjusted P-value ≤.05 were considered significant.

Isolation of peritoneal macrophages and infection

A 4% thioglycolate solution (Hi-Media) was injected into the peritoneal cavity of BALB/c mice. At 96 h post-injection, peritoneal macrophages were isolated and seeded in 96-well plates in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco) containing 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified (5% CO2) incubator. After 16 h, the cells were washed and supplemented with DMEM medium. Peritoneal macrophages, RAW264.7 murine macrophage cell line, and phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)-differentiated THP-1 cells were infected with control and HU-cKD strains. Infection was performed at an multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1:5 for 4 h, followed by washing with warm PBS to remove extracellular bacteria, and then the cells were supplemented with media containing 10% FBS. Macrophages were lysed using 0.05% SDS (made in PBS) at the indicated time points, and further serial dilutions were plated on 7H11-OADC. CFUs were counted after 3 weeks of incubation at 37°C.

Isolation of RNA from intracellular bacteria

THP1 macrophages were infected with HU-cKD and the control strain as described earlier. Infected cells were lysed in 4M GTC buffer (4 M guanidine thiocyanate, 25 mM trisodium citrate, 0.5% N-laurylsarcosine, 0.5% Tween-80, 0.1 M β-mercaptoethanol) and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 45 min. The pellet was washed once with 0.5% Tween-80, resuspended in TRIzol, and added to a 2-ml screw-cap tube containing zirconia beads. RNA was isolated after two rounds of bead beating following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Annexin-V and Propidium Iodide staining

The PMA-differentiated THP-1 cells (0.15 × 106 cells) were infected with control and HU-cKD strains. Infected macrophages were stained with annexin-V (binds to phosphatidylserine on apoptotic cells) and Propidium Iodide (PI) (enters and stains dead cells) at 48 post-infection (hpi) using Dead Cell Apoptosis Kits (Invitrogen). Apoptotic and necrotic cell death in infected macrophages was estimated by flow cytometry, where Annexin V-FITC positive (Q1 + Q2) cells were considered as apoptotic and PI single positive (Q4) as necrotic. The flow cytometry was carried out using BD FACSAria Fusion (BD Biosciences), and data analysis was done using FACSDiva.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data published in our previous publication [26] was used for the analysis of MtHU binding pattern and integrative analysis with RNA-seq. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments with HU-cKD and the control strain were carried out as described earlier [37]. Briefly, exponentially growing cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, resuspended in IP buffer [50 mM HEPES–KOH, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, Antiprotease mini tablet Roche], and subjected to sonication to shear the DNA using Bioruptor (Diagenode). The immunoprecipitation was performed using anti-MtHU and anti-RpoB antibodies. Reactions without any antibody served as Mock.

In vitro transcription assay

In vitro transcriptions were performed as described earlier [38]. As the T7A1 promoter is recognized by mycobacterial RNAP [39], the templates were generated by cloning MtHU-bound regions downstream of the promoter. MtHU-bound loci were PCR-amplified from the Mtb genome using gene-specific primers containing the T7A1 promoter sequence in the forward primer and gene-specific reverse primer. The templates (100 ng) were incubated at 37°C for 1 h with mycobacterial RNAP (200 nM), 1 mM rNTPs, transcription buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0, 25°C), 10 mM magnesium acetate, 100 μM EDTA, 100 μM dithiothreitol (DTT), 50 μg/ml BSA, 50 mM KCl, 5% glycerol], and various concentrations of MtHU (25 or 200 nM). Reactions were treated with DNase I at 37°c for 15 min, followed by proteinase K treatment at 65°C for 15 min, incubation with 2×-formamide dye at 95°C for 10 min, and products were analysed on 2% agarose gel stained with EtBr.

Culture filtrate preparation

Mtb cells were grown in Modified Sauton’s media till 0.8 OD600. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 30 min. The cell pellet was washed twice with PBS, resuspended in STE buffer, and transferred into 2-ml screw cap tubes containing zirconium beads. Four rounds of bead beating were performed to lyse the cells. The supernatant was passed through a 0.22 μm filter and concentrated using a 10 kDa Centricon (Millipore). Cell lysate (CL) and cell filtrate were resolved in 12% SDS–PAGE, and immunoblotting was performed using anti-MtHU antibody. Anti-Rho antibody was used as a marker for cell lysis to evaluate the contamination of cellular proteins in culture filtrate (CF).

Purification of endogenous and recombinant MtHU

The recombinant MtHU (r-MtHU) was overexpressed and purified from Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells, and endogenous MtHU (n-MtHU) was purified from Mtb H37Ra using the protocols described earlier [18, 22]. The proteins were resolved on 12% SDS–PAGE, and the MtHU band was excised. The PTMs were analysed by mass spectrometry carried out by MS-Bioworks. The PMA-differentiated THP-1 cells were stimulated with r-MtHU and n-MtHU at a concentration of 5 µg/ml. Six hours post-stimulation, RNA was isolated using TRIzol, followed by cDNA synthesis and qPCR to estimate transcript levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines.

Mice infection experiments

4-to-6-week-old female BALB/c mice were housed in individually ventilated cages and maintained in accordance with the established animal ethics and guidelines. Mice (n ≥ 5 per group) were infected with 80–100 bacilli per mouse through the aerosol route using the Madison aerosol chamber. Four mice from each set were sacrificed at 24 h postexposure to assess bacterial deposition. Mice were divided into two groups: HU-cKD-Dox [without Doxycycline (Dox)] and HU-cKD + Dox. Dox, a tetracycline derivative used for CRISPRi-based gene knockdown in vivo [40], was supplemented at 1 mg/ml along with 5% sucrose solution as drinking water after 7 days of infection. Water bottles were light-protected and replaced twice a week. After 4 weeks of infection, HU-cKD + Dox (MtHU depletion) and HU-cKD-Dox (Control) groups were further divided into three groups: untreated, rifampicin (Rif)-treated, and isoniazid (INH)-treated.

For Mtb challenge experiments, 4-to-6 week-old female BALB/c mice (n = 5 per group) were vaccinated three times subcutaneously either with 5 µg of n-MtHU or r-MtHU emulsified in adjuvant (mix of dimethyldioctadecylammonium bromide and monophosphoryl lipid A) as previously described [41] or with the adjuvant alone. As a positive reference, one group was immunized with live Mycobacterium bovis BCG, and the group that received only PBS served as a negative reference for estimation of immune protection. Mice were challenged with ∼100 CFU of Mtb through the aerosol route 4 weeks following the final immunization.

At indicated times post-infection, mice were euthanized, and the lungs and spleen were aseptically harvested for bacillary load estimation, histopathology analysis, and pathological scoring. To enumerate bacillary loads in the lungs and spleens, organs were homogenized in PBS, and then serial dilutions were plated on 7H11 agar containing PANTA (polymyxin B, amphotericin B, nalidixic acid, trimethoprim, and azlocillin). CFUs were counted after 4 weeks of incubation at 37°C. Animal studies were conducted as per the guidelines prescribed by the Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Animals (CPCSEA), Government of India. Experiments were approved (CAF/Ethics/850/2021) by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) and conducted at the Biosafety Level 3 containment facility of the Indian Institute of Science (IISc), Bangalore.

Results

MtHU depletion leads to growth retardation of Mtb

MtHU is one of the conserved NAPs found in Mtb, and its essentiality was indicated in various transposon mutagenesis screens [29–32]. Indispensability of MtHU was successfully validated earlier by generating a cKD in Mtb H37Ra using the TetR/Pip OFF method [18]. The strain showed severe growth defects in vitro, with the depletion of about 75% of MtHU [18]. However, in seemingly contradictory subsequent studies, hupB knockout mutants were found to be viable [42, 43]. In contrast, in M. bovis, which is remarkably similar to Mtb, the NAP was found to be essential for the survival of the organism. In these studies, HU from M. bovis BCG could not be deleted, and only cKD of HU could be generated [44, 45], which is consistent with our earlier results. Since MtHU and M. bovis BCG HU (also known as MDP1) are 100% identical and the genomes have 99.9% identity, it is likely that the essentiality of HU is a fundamental characteristic shared by both species. Thus, given the apparent discrepancy, it was necessary to revisit our initial observation of the essentiality of MtHU. Hence, a more regulatable CRISPRi strategy was employed to downregulate the MtHU levels as described in the ‘Materials and methods’ section. Expression of dCas9 and the specific gRNA upon ATc addition decreases transcription of MtHU (Fig. 1A). The downregulation of MtHU was validated by estimating RNA (Fig. 1B) and protein levels (Fig. 1C) through qPCR and western blotting, respectively. With about a 60% reduction in MtHU levels in HU-cKD compared to control strain (Fig. 1C), growth reduction of Mtb in both liquid and solid medium was observed (Fig. 1D–F). The CRISPRi strategy not only revalidated the requirement of MtHU for promoting growth under aerobic conditions but also facilitated further studies to explore its intracellular role, as described next.

MtHU is a transcriptional regulator that impacts various metabolic pathways

To investigate the influence of MtHU on gene expression, RNA was isolated from the ATc-treated control and HU-cKD strains, followed by RNA sequencing analysis (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). Transcriptomic analysis of MtHU-depleted Mtb cells revealed 594 and 273 genes to be significantly upregulated and downregulated, respectively (fold change ≥1.5; Fig. 2A and B). The distribution of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) suggested that the NAP exerts predominantly negative regulation. The deregulated genes were further categorized according to their annotations in Tuberculist [46] (Fig. 2C). The genes were distributed across various functional categories such as virulence, detoxification, adaptation, regulatory proteins, noncoding RNAs, information pathway, PE-PPE, lipid metabolism, intermediary metabolism, respiration, conserved hypothetical, and cell wall processes, indicating the wide-ranging influence of the NAP in controlling the gene repression in Mtb (Fig. 2C).

To validate these findings, representative genes from various pathways such as transcription, translation, polyketide synthesis, immune modulation, dormancy, and drug tolerance were evaluated for their expression. Correlation of these data with that of RNA-seq results (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Table S2) confirmed MtHU’s far-reaching influence on gene expression. Genes regulated by MtHU show temporal regulation, wherein a few genes show expression changes early and others at a later time (Supplementary Fig. S1). The observed downregulation of genes involved in DNA replication, repair, and translation might account for the overall reduction in bacterial fitness seen with the cKD strain. An imbalance in the expression of toxin–antitoxin and dormancy-related genes (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Table S2) suggested a shift toward growth arrest and stress survival adaptation upon depletion of the NAP. Reduced expression of genes related to drug tolerance and efflux suggested diminished capacity of Mtb to respond to drug exposure, verified in the later section. The upregulation of PE/PGRS family genes involved in immune modulation suggested a decreased virulence potential of HU-cKD, which was examined (see later section). The altered expression of transcriptional regulators observed in this expression analysis indicates extended control of MtHU over the Mtb transcriptome. Because the expression of several genes involved in essential cellular functions was affected upon MtHU depletion, we surmise that MtHU is one of the housekeeping NAPs and is indispensable for maintaining optimal growth of Mtb.

Chemical perturbation of MtHU

Given its essentiality for Mtb growth, in our earlier studies, we developed structure-guided inhibitors of MtHU [18]. One of the compounds, SD1, bound to MtHU in its “DNA binding saddle” to competitively inhibit DNA binding. Thus, in contrast to MtHU depletion, which results in its lower cellular levels, MtHU inhibition would restrict the amount of DNA-bound functional NAP available for its action. To understand the effects of MtHU inhibition versus depletion on cellular events, Mtb cells were treated with SD1, and transcriptomic changes were analysed as described in the ‘Materials and methods’ section. The RNA-seq profile revealed that functional inhibition of MtHU resulted in transcriptional alterations. Upregulation among the DEGs was more pronounced, akin to the HU-cKD transcriptome (Supplementary Fig. S2); HU inhibition deregulated the expression of genes involved in virulence, detoxification, adaptation, information pathway, PE-PPE, lipid metabolism, intermediary metabolism, conserved hypothetical, and cell wall processes.

Direct and indirect transcriptional control by MtHU

To address the connection between the binding of the NAP and transcriptional regulation, ChIP-seq data of MtHU were analysed. Comparison of the ChIP-seq and RNA-seq data revealed that MtHU occupancy and DEGs were distributed across the genome (Fig. 3A). The peaks corresponding to MtHU binding were broad, ranging in length from 0.7 to 8.9 kb. Earlier studies with Mycobacterium smegmatis HU also revealed such extended binding by the NAP [19]. This is in contrast to the sharp peaks observed in the case of sequence-specific DNA-binding proteins. MtHU binding was distributed at 371 distinct regions in the genome. These sites encompass 183 intergenic loci and regulatory and/or coding regions of 981 genes. A majority of the binding sites in the latter group were found within the gene bodies belonging to diverse functional categories (Supplementary Table S3, and Supplementary Fig. S3A and B), implicating a direct role for the NAP in their expression. However, MtHU binding was observed in only ∼38% of MtHU-regulated genes (Fig. 3B), implying the rest are regulated indirectly. A majority of those that had MtHU binding showed upregulation in HU-cKD (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S4), reinforcing MtHU’s negative regulatory role. The binding of the NAP would impede the transcribing RNAP in these transcription units (TUs). The results presented in Supplementary Fig. S5 show that full-length runoff transcripts were generated from each of the templates containing MtHU-binding regions in the absence of MtHU. Whereas, in its presence in transcription reactions, concentration-dependent reduction in transcripts was observed. The transcription impediment by MtHU provides a mechanistic insight for the direct regulation by the NAP. Upregulation of pe6, pgrs42, mbtA, eccC4, rv2650, and mazF8, shown in Fig. 2D, is consistent with the observed decreased occupancy of MtHU (Fig. 3D) and increased RNAP (Fig. 3E) in these gene bodies upon MtHU depletion. Notably, a similar pattern, i.e. decreased MtHU occupancy and increased RNAP density, is seen with transcriptional regulators viz, sigI, tcrX, rv1353, rv1358, and rv2282 (Figs 2D, and 3D and E), indicating the similar transcription elongation impediment imposed by MtHU. These transcription regulators control their own subset of target genes (Fig. 3F), thus revealing that regulation by MtHU occurs over a larger set of genes. Thus, the transcriptomic changes observed upon MtHU depletion are a combined effect of both direct binding and indirect regulation.

Depletion of MtHU impacts Mtb survival in macrophages

Considering the impact of MtHU downregulation on the expression of several essential genes and immunomodulatory genes, the bacterium’s survival was evaluated in macrophages to assess Mtb’s intracellular fitness. Towards this, peritoneal macrophages, RAW264.7 murine macrophages, and PMA-differentiated THP-1 cells were infected with the control strain and the HU-cKD strain at an MOI of 5. HU-cKD exhibited compromised survival compared to the control strain in all three cell lines at 24, 48, and 72 hpi (Supplementary Fig. S6A), indicating that MtHU is required for intracellular fitness. Upregulation of immune modulatory genes in HU-cKD transcriptome–pe6, ppe57, and pgrs29 implicated in inducing pro-inflammatory cytokines [47], promoting apoptosis [48], and aiding in intracellular clearance [49], respectively, would account for the reduced fitness of Mtb seen in these experiments. Increased expression of these three genes was verified in Mtb both in vitro and within infected macrophages by qPCR (the ‘Materials and methods’ section, Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. S6B). From these results, it is apparent that MtHU exerts its control on the expression of these genes irrespective of growth conditions.

The increased expression of pe6, ppe57, and pgrs29 upon MtHU depletion would possibly result in more apoptosis in infected macrophages, leading to compromised intracellular survival of the MtHU-depleted strain. To confirm, apoptosis as well as necrosis in infected macrophages was estimated. The expression levels of a representative set of genes from the apoptosis and necrosis pathways were estimated in THP-1 cells infected with control and HU-cKD strains. HU-cKD infected macrophages exhibited elevated levels of pro-apoptotic markers and decreased necrosis markers (Fig. 4A and B). To substantiate, THP-1 cells infected with control and HU-cKD strains were stained with Annexin V and PI at 48 hpi, followed by flow cytometric analysis. A significantly higher apoptosis was observed in THP-1 cells infected with HU-cKD. The population of apoptotic cells was higher with a concomitant decrease in the necrotic cells in HU-cKD infected cells compared to the cells infected with control strain (Fig. 4C and D). The elevated apoptosis and decreased necrosis in HU-cKD infected macrophages account for faster clearance of intracellular bacteria. These observations provide a mechanistic link between transcriptional alterations and reduced Mtb survival within the intracellular niche, wherein MtHU regulates the expression of immunomodulatory genes, thereby modulating host cell fate.

MtHU is required for Mtb growth in mice

Next, to determine the impact of MtHU depletion on the growth and persistence of Mtb in animal models, BALB/c mice were infected with the HU-cKD strain (cultured without ATc) through the aerosol route. The bacillary load in the lungs of infected mice was assessed at days 1 and 7 post-infection prior to MtHU depletion (Supplementary Fig. S7). The infected mice were divided into two groups (Fig. 5A); one group received Dox to initiate MtHU depletion 7 days post-infection, while the other was untreated. The strain multiplied at a rate comparable to wild-type Mtb in the absence of Dox. MtHU-depleted Mtb showed significantly reduced survival in mice at 4 as well as 8 weeks post-infection (wpi) (Fig. 5B). Dissemination of MtHU-depleted Mtb to the spleen was also reduced compared to the control Mtb at 4 and 8 wpi (Fig. 5C). The gross and histopathological evaluation of the lungs indicated that the extent of tissue damage was significantly reduced in mice that were infected with MtHU-depleted Mtb (Fig. 5D and E). The granuloma score was also significantly lower in these mice (Fig. 5F), underlining the importance of MtHU for Mtb pathogenesis.

Decreased drug tolerance of Mtb in macrophages and mice upon HU depletion

Downregulation of genes involved in drug tolerance [50–52], viz. whiB7, iniA, iniB, and efpA, in transcriptomic analysis suggested altered drug tolerance of HU-depleted Mtb within the host. Hence, the expression of these genes was examined in HU-cKD isolated from infected macrophages 48 hpi. The decreased expression of these genes in HU-cKD during infection (Supplementary Fig. S8A) suggested Mtb would have altered intracellular survival in the presence of anti-TB drugs. To verify, THP-1 macrophages were infected with the control and HU-cKD strains in the presence of either rifampicin (Rif) or isoniazid (INH). In the presence of INH and Rif, the bacillary load in macrophages infected with HU-cKD was significantly lower than that of those infected with control strains (Supplementary Fig. S8B and C), indicating that MtHU depletion enhances the sensitivity of Mtb towards these drugs within the host.

To substantiate, the drug tolerance of Mtb was evaluated upon HU-depletion in a mouse model (Fig. 6A) as described in the ‘Materials and methods’ section. Estimation of bacillary loads after 8 wpi revealed that the survival of HU-depleted Mtb was more compromised in the presence of INH and Rif in both the lungs and spleen (Fig. 6B and C). Additionally, the extent of pulmonary tissue damage, as determined by gross and histopathological evaluation of the lungs, was significantly reduced (Fig. 6D–F). Together, both macrophage and animal experiments demonstrate that Mtb with lower levels of MtHU displayed higher sensitivity towards INH and Rif treatment. The decreased drug tolerance of Mtb upon MtHU depletion was similar to what has been observed in M. bovis BCG and M. smegmatis upon HU depletion [44, 53]. Treatment of Mtb-infected macrophages with the SD1 inhibitor also showed a similar drug tolerance pattern [43].

MtHU triggers the host immune response

The detection of anti-MtHU antibodies in the sera of TB patients in earlier studies indicated the NAP’s immunogenicity in the human host during Mtb infection [54, 55]. MtHU was also recognized as an immunogen in various screens [56]. Also, its cell surface localization has been reported in various mycobacteria [54, 57, 58]. Mass spectrometric analysis of Mtb CF showed the extrabacterial accumulation of MtHU [59], confirmed by the analysis of culture supernatant of Mtb using anti-MtHU antibodies (Fig. 7A). Although MtHU is found in the cell surface and extracellular milieu, how it is transported out is not yet known. To investigate whether MtHU triggers an innate immune response, THP1 macrophages were stimulated with recombinant MtHU (r-MtHU), and the expression of various cytokines was analysed. In addition, as PTMs are known to enhance immunogenicity of proteins [60], endogenous MtHU purified from Mtb (n-MtHU), which is post-translationally modified, was also used in these studies. Figure 7B depict the extensive modifications of MtHU. With both r-MtHU and n-MtHU, induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines was observed; however, the induction was nearly two-fold higher with the latter (Fig. 7C). In order to assess whether the immunogenic potential of MtHU translates into protective immunity, BALB/c mice were infected with Mtb H37Rv 4 weeks post-immunization with n-MtHU (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). Mice vaccinated with n-MtHU exhibited less severe tissue pathology and reduced bacterial load in the lungs as well as spleen compared to the lungs of mice immunized with only adjuvant or unimmunized mice (Fig. 7D–F). These results, although are preliminary and would require further validations, indicate that MtHU is also a bona fide immunogen apart from its canonical functions as a NAP and virulence regulator.

Discussion

Among all the NAPs, HU seems to be the most widespread and conserved in eubacteria as well as archaea [61–64]. Apart from maintaining chromosome integrity, the NAP from different species has evolved to carry out species-specific functions. We describe the multidimensional functions of MtHU in Mtb as a transcription regulator, gene silencer, immunomodulator, and virulence regulator. Because of the limited repertoire of NAPs present in Mtb and the apparent lack of backup NAPs, MtHU is not only indispensable but also seems to have gained functional versatility. The effect of MtHU depletion on various pathways, including intermediary metabolism, translation and stress adaptation that support in vitro growth as well as those required for intracellular survival, highlights its role as a key housekeeping regulator in the pathogen. The decreased Mtb survival and reduction in virulence gene expression upon MtHU depletion affirm its role in controlling Mtb pathogenesis.

The binding events of HU to the chromosome in different bacteria may vary depending upon the genome size, number of NAPs present in the respective genomes, and the presence of functional homologues. Therefore, in different bacterial species, including Mtb (this study), Streptomyces coelicolor [65], M. smegmatis [19], and E. coli [66], different numbers of binding peaks on the genome were observed. Nevertheless, MtHU binding sites were more in the ori proximal half of the genome, as observed in other bacteria [19, 65, 66]. HU in E. coli and B. subtilis regulates gene expression by binding to promoter regions [66–69], possibly due to their binding to AT-rich regions. In contrast, a preferential binding of the MtHU in the gene bodies was observed in the present studies with Mtb and in a prior analysis with M. smegmatis [19]. While the significance of HU binding to gene bodies remains to be understood in M. smegmatis, our study reveals that it hinders RNAP elongation in the TUs in Mtb (Supplementary Fig. S5). Such a transcriptional roadblock could result in the generation of incomplete transcripts, as seen in another study [70]. Despite these differences in the mode of regulation, the control of gene expression by HU is both direct and indirect in E. coli as well as in Mtb. For instance, the binding of E. coli HU to the minor groove of DNA indirectly displaces lexA from its binding site, thereby fine-tuning the expression levels of SOS genes in E. coli [71]. Likewise, in the pap operon of uropathogenic E. coli, HU’s effect is likely indirect, where it changes the phase-on/off equilibrium by inducing changes in DNA architecture through its binding. The direct association of E. coli HU, either alone or in complex with other regulators to alter gene expression, has been exemplified in the Gal repressosome, ndh operon, and elsewhere [72–74]. In Mtb, however, MtHU binds to gene bodies of transcriptional regulators to alter their expression and exerts indirect control over their target genes or through polar effects on operonic genes (Supplementary Fig. S9). Upon MtHU depletion, more than 10 transcription regulators are upregulated, which together contribute to the alteration of expression of a larger number of genes (as depicted in Fig. 3F for six representative regulators). These findings indicate that, similar to HU from other organisms [66, 75], MtHU also functions as a transcription regulator in addition to being a chromosome architectural protein.

MtHU occupancy on various insertion sequences and phage-related genes, including several transposons, suggests its role in xenogeneic silencing of the genome, thereby protecting it from random rearrangement events. This function is not attributed to HU from E. coli and other related Gammaproteobacteria, wherein H-NS and its backup NAP StpA are designated silencing proteins [76–79]. Although Mtb lacks H-NS, and Lsr2 seems to be the functional replacement for it in the pathogen [80], MtHU’s contribution towards silencing of the genome seems to be significant. Surprisingly, no increased expression of other NAPs was found in our transcriptomic analysis of the HU-depleted strain, suggesting a lack of backup support from other NAPs, as seen in other organisms [62]. To make a point, among the large pool of PE/PPE genes, insertion and phage sequences in Mtb, some are controlled by Lsr2, whereas MtHU controls a different subset of genes; 38 and 40 Mtb genes belonging to the PE/PPE family, insertion, and phage sequence, respectively, were upregulated upon HU-depletion. Such division of labour between the NAPs is perhaps needed in Mtb because the pathogen has an overrepresentation of these genes in the genome. As such, Mtb has an underrepresentation of NAPs compared to E. coli and other bacteria having similar genome size. How a bacterium copes with a smaller number of NAPs to carry out cellular functions is an intriguing question, especially when other bacteria exhibit an abundance of NAPs. Mtb seems to manage by a few different mechanisms, which we have described [16]: NAP–NAP interaction as seen in the case of MtHU and Lsr2 [26], PTM of NAPs to alter DNA binding repertoire [20, 22, 23], and topoisomerase–NAP interaction [28]. In Mtb, topoisomerase I activity is stimulated by HU through direct physical interaction [28], whereas in S. coelicolor, their crosstalk results in enhanced HU binding to the genome during elevated chromosome supercoiling [65].

The idea that DNA topology and NAPs serve as virulence regulators was put forth more than three decades ago [75, 81, 82]. Several NAPs, including HU from different species such as E. coli, S. typhimurium, Shigella flexneri, Vibrio cholerae, Francisella tularensis, and Porphyromonas gingivalis, have a direct or indirect role in the regulation of virulence gene expression [61, 75, 83–86], indicating that diverse bacterial species utilize NAPs to coordinate expression of virulence genes to gain a survival advantage. However, interestingly, HU controls different virulence effectors in different pathogens. Its control of gene expression includes, but is not restricted to, those of pathogenicity islands, secretion systems, flagella, and immune modulators [87]. Decreased intracellular survival of HU mutants of M. bovis BCG, Helicobacter pylori, and F. tularensis [44, 84, 88], similar to our observations with Mtb, depicts that the NAP is a key virulence determinant in many bacteria.

By and large, NAPs are confined to the nucleoid, engaged in regulating different DNA-transaction processes in addition to their genome compaction role [61, 64]. However, in Mtb and in a few other pathogens, HU seems to be secreted out of the cell [89], indicating its potential as an immunogen, as seen in this study. Its immunogenic potential is likely to be a combined contribution of its surface localization and accumulation outside the cell. Moreover, its cell surface localization, found in Streptococcus pyogenes, Mtb, and other mycobacteria, implies that it could have additional functions, such as cell wall assembly, adhesion, and host interaction [54, 57, 58, 90, 91]. Its association with biofilms in Mtb and several pathogens is also suggestive of its additional roles [89, 92].

The importance of HU and its regulatory capacity is underscored by the similar transcriptomic and survival signatures observed in M. bovis BCG [44] and Mtb. The number of genes showing altered expression was comparable in both organisms. The decreased drug tolerance upon HU-depletion observed in both Mtb and M. bovis BCG suggests that targeting bacterial HU, as demonstrated for Mtb by structure-based inhibitor design [18], could provide a new therapeutic strategy. Such an approach of targeting a bacterial NAP could increase the efficacy of existing drugs. The conserved pattern of alterations seen in two important mycobacterial pathogens with respect to immune response, intracellular survival, and drug tolerance suggests that the HU from various mycobacterial pathogens could be a suitable target for a therapeutic intervention approach.
