Biocultural memory of reciprocity: the Mapuche trafkintu as social-ecological relationships of care and vindication
Authors: Gonzalo Salazar, Magdalena Reyes, Santiago Kaulen-Luks, María Guadalupe Barrera, Alison Burgos, José Tomás Ibarra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Reconsidering the relationship between humans and more-than-human beings amid global crises has brought reciprocity practices between people and biodiversity to the forefront. We examine social-ecological reciprocity practices within Indigenous territories and their direct connection to biocultural memory. Specifically, we explore the Mapuche practice of trafkintu in the Andean zone of Wallmapu, La Araucanía region of southern Chile. Using a mixed-methods framework, from a relational perspective, we integrate spatial analysis of a seed exchange network involving 80 local farmers, with an ethnographic and collaborative phase with 12 Mapuche women—who are part of this network—over three years. We found that social-ecological reciprocity practices—like those in the trafkintu—are constitutive of a biocultural memory. This biocultural memory has been vital for sustaining and transforming social-ecological reciprocity practices amid colonial and neo-colonial pressures. We term this recursion the “memory of reciprocity.” This provides key insights into how reciprocity manifests as a quality of complex social-ecological relationships, marked by mutual care among people, seeds, and other more-than-human beings. It also helps us understand how, amid the colonialism and dispossession endured by Indigenous peoples for centuries, reciprocity has been essential to survival and vindication.

Introduction

In a context of social-ecological crisis and the need to seek alternative paths in the present and for the future, several frameworks have sought to reconceptualize the relationship between humans and the rest of nature [1–3]. Recently, the practices of reciprocity between Indigenous peoples and biodiversity have gained increased recognition [4, 5], as part of acknowledging their key role in caring for social-ecological systems [6]. Different Indigenous groups have vindicated reciprocity as a form of ontological sovereignty [2, 7–9]. On this basis, reciprocity can be understood as a quality of relationships that, for Indigenous peoples, is key in the functioning of inter-personal and inter-species ties [2, 10]. It has also been identified as a concept common to cultures of Buen Vivir, positioning it as a cosmo-political stance [11] found across different Indigenous peoples in Latin America. In this framework, it has been suggested that reciprocity forms part of the concept of Ayni, the basis of the community relationship between the human being and nature in Andean philosophy [12, 13] as well as the exchange practices of the Makuna’s wanore [14], the kelluwün (mutual help), and the values inherent to the Mapuche kvme mogen or good living [15]. According to the latter, the relationship with nature is based on respect, dynamic equilibrium, and reciprocity [16, 17]. Consequently, in Indigenous contexts, reciprocity emerges primarily as a social-ecological phenomenon—i.e., as social-ecological reciprocity [3, 18]. In this reciprocity, social, cultural, cognitive, and spiritual experiences configure true ecologies of interdependence with biodiversity [19, 20]. As Labba [21] and Kimmerer [22] emphasize, reciprocity extends beyond human relations to include biodiversity, where more-than-human beings are engaged through mutual respect and coexistence.

The idea of social-ecological reciprocity has also been explored in ethnobiology and ethnoecology, where it is increasingly understood as a relational quality embedded in culturally and ecologically specific contexts [18, 23–25]. Ojeda et al. [4] propose the notion of “reciprocal contributions” to highlight generative interactions between people and other natural elements—challenging frameworks such as ecosystem services, which tend to prioritize human well-being over ecological interdependence [26]. At the same time, recent work in ethnobiology has underscored that reciprocity practices are deeply grounded in Indigenous knowledge systems and that they also hold critical relevance for global sustainability agendas—provided that such scaling is undertaken through ethically robust and context-sensitive approaches [27]. Taken together, these contributions help delineate an emerging challenge: as social-ecological reciprocity gains prominence in global discourses, there is a risk that its complexity may be diluted through theoretical abstraction, conceptual flattening, or decontextualized generalization.

We start from the premise that research and discussions around the scaling of social-ecological reciprocity must avoid theoretical abstractions that tend to universalize the phenomenon—reducing it to bidirectional transactions of mutual benefit [5, 28]. It is also important to resist decontextualized interpretations that portray reciprocity as a symmetrical and abstract exchange between parties, overlooking how power relations can profoundly shape and complicate these links [28]. We also stress the need to move beyond essentialist framings of reciprocity as a timeless social value or romanticized cultural ideal detached from the political and ecological struggles in which it is practiced [29, 30]. To meaningfully engage with social-ecological reciprocity in Indigenous contexts, it is necessary to attend to the specific histories, situated practices, and intercultural tensions that shape its expression across territories. In this regard, we build on critical scholarship that has examined how Indigenous life-worlds are shaped by asymmetrical power relations, the enduring injustices of colonial and neo-colonial domination, and complex intercultural entanglements [31, 32], situating social-ecological reciprocity within those broader dynamics.

Building on this critical framing, this study seeks to explore social-ecological reciprocity as a situated, lived experience—one that takes shape through specific relationships between people, territories, and more-than-human beings in Indigenous contexts. Rather than treating reciprocity as a static or idealized value, we approach it as a dynamic phenomenon shaped by historical, political, and ecological processes. In particular, we acknowledge that efforts by Indigenous peoples to sustain diverse forms of reciprocity have been deeply affected by the cultural and environmental violence of colonial and neo-colonial regimes—experiences that have left enduring marks in both territorial practices and collective biocultural memory [33]. At the same time, reciprocity today also responds to—and actively engages with—the structural forces that shape contemporary social-ecological relations. These dynamics, often examined through the lens of political ecology, include pressures such as extractivism, export-oriented agribusiness, and neoliberal conservation regimes, all of which are mediated by legal and institutional frameworks (e.g., land tenure laws, protected area governance, intellectual property regimes) that directly influence the conditions under which reciprocity can be sustained [10, 34]. This opens the way for a closer look at how social-ecological reciprocity is enacted and maintained in specific Indigenous contexts—such as Wallmapu1—where it acquires distinct meanings and forms.

The Mapuche people have lived in Wallmapu for centuries and have resisted colonization for over five hundred years. This became particularly intense in the early stages of national independence when, under policies designed to obtain economically profitable land for the Chilean nation-building project, Indigenous peoples were settled, and many times displaced to, Indigenous Reservations. These processes included dynamics of cultural assimilation [35], the loss of socio-political and economic autonomy [36], the erosion of their language [37], the loss of lands [38], territorial displacement [39], the loss of food sovereignty [40], and an increasing health insecurity [41]. At the same time, their territories faced severe environmental degradation from deforestation, pollution, droughts, and biodiversity loss. Despite this, many Mapuche communities have maintained cultural practices that contribute to a relational mode of reciprocal care between people, between these and other more-than-human beings, and with the biodiversity of which they form a part. Reciprocity has been documented in nearby areas of the region. This quality of practices appears intertwined with biocultural diversity, kinship and intercultural networks, especially in southern Chile and Argentina, with emphasis in northern Patagonia and La Araucanía Region [15, 25, 42]. An example of this is the trafkintu. The term comes from Mapudungun2 and has historically referred to practices of exchange that forge ties among the parties involved, traditionally known as trafkin.3 In Wallmapu, such exchanges are ancestral and usually occur between familiar individuals or communities. Today, the trafkintu may be understood as an intimate relationship of social-ecological reciprocity between people, seeds, and biodiversity, in which communities see themselves as caretakers of interdependent dynamics [20, 42–44].

Seed exchange takes place around the world and testifies to the importance of social -ecological networks, reciprocity, and memory in the conservation and dynamization of agrobiodiversity [10, 45]. In Kenya, migration and gender roles shape the distribution of crop varieties, showing how local memory influences the management of agrobiodiversity [46]. In Peru’s Achuar communities, seed exchange networks not only conserve crop diversity but are also closely related to kinship networks and agricultural knowledge, strengthening the intergenerational transfer of knowledge [47]. In Cameroon, where strategies for obtaining seeds vary with climate, the ability of communities to activate social relationships in times of crisis is key [48]. These exchanges are essential for subsistence agriculture and also act as vehicles of reciprocity, sustaining ecological and cultural ties across diverse contexts [49, 50].

We suggest that, to fully grasp the situated practice of social-ecological reciprocity—such as that seen in the Mapuche trafkintu—it is important to view it as part of peoples’ biocultural memory. The latter, as understood through inductive research with Mapuche communities [33, 51], is a collective historical consciousness that safeguards a dynamic of accumulated, inherently relational experiences between human beings, more-than-human beings, and their territories. This view aligns with broader ethnographic research that has examined biocultural memory in Indigenous contexts, highlighting its role in sustaining reciprocal relationships between people, their territories, and more-than-human beings [52, 53]. These studies emphasize that biocultural memory is not merely a repository of knowledge, but an active process shaped by situated practices of care, resistance, and adaptation, making it a crucial framework for understanding social-ecological reciprocity. It, therefore, offers an interpretive lens that gives central place to the relationships of people and communities with their situated social-ecological systems and their histories. Understanding reciprocity as part of biocultural memory thus enables a more comprehensive, historically grounded view of how Indigenous communities sustain social-ecological relations through time.

This raises a number of guiding questions: How have practices of social-ecological reciprocity been constitutive of biocultural memory? How have they persisted despite the challenges posed by colonial and neo-colonial contexts? What are the key elements that shape the practice of social-ecological reciprocity? In this article, we examine reciprocity in the trafkintu within colonial legacies that continue to affect Mapuche communities today. We argue that reciprocity practices are not only embedded in biocultural memory but constitute a specific dimension of it—what we might call a “biocultural memory of reciprocity”—which is essential for its continuity and transformation over time. Each trafkintu expresses this reciprocity, cultivated through relationships of mutual care among people and seeds. Moreover, in the face of historical and ongoing dispossession, reciprocity has been key practice for Indigenous survival and vindication. We extend the notion of biocultural memory by introducing the concept of biocultural memory of reciprocity, which we define as the accumulated and shared knowledge, practices, and values that sustain reciprocal relationships between people and other-than-human beings in specific social-ecological contexts. Our approach emphasizes reciprocity as a central dimension of these memory systems in Indigenous contexts. This focus highlights how acts of mutual exchange and care are maintained, reinterpreted, and enacted in daily life. In doing so, the concept offers a lens for ethnobiology that connects memory with the relational dynamics at the heart of human–environment interactions. We have sought to answer our research questions through a three-year ethnographic process (2022–2024), using a relational methodology [51]. In this process, we have collaborated with Mapuche women who live in the basin of the Liucura River in the Andean zone of Wallmapu.

Study area and methods

The research for this article was conducted in the Andean zone of Wallmapu, the ancestral land of the Mapuche people, which now correspond to southern Chile and Argentina. Specifically, it took place in the Pucón municipal district in Chile’s La Araucanía Region (Fig. 1), home to nearly a million people and the highest proportion of Mapuche population in the country [54]. The Mapuche population is heterogeneous, living in both rural and urban areas throughout Wallmapu. The La Araucanía Region is the Chilean region with the largest number of Indigenous communities, most of which are located on Indigenous Reservations, created under títulos de merced (land titles) issued by the Chilean State, to which the Mapuche population was confined as from the late nineteenth century and throughout the twentieth century [34]. The non-Indigenous population comprises Chilean campesinos (small farmers), migrants from other regions of Chile, mestizo people, and white settlers descended from the European immigrants who benefited from land auctions at the end of the nineteenth and beginning of the twentieth centuries, as well as members of the regional landowning elite. For this study, we used mixed methodologies with a relational approach [57], implemented in two stages of continuous collaboration between early 2022 and August 2024. This means that we sought to approach fieldwork according to an Indigenous principle in which everything is interwoven, and it is not possible for a being to exist outside its network [57]. Following Calvet-Mir et al. [58], the first stage involved interviews with farmers with whom we co-constructed seed exchange lists, based on a questionnaire in which they were asked to list all the people from whom they had received seeds and to whom they had given seeds, the previous year. This resulted in a visual representation of seed exchanges as a network, similar to a snapshot of a particular moment. This information enabled us to map the movement of agrobiodiversity, using a sample of 80 small farms, managed by local campesinos (N = 40) and migrants who had moved to the territory as adults (N = 40), as the constituent nodes of a network. The geographical coordinates of the farms were recorded to spatially represent the relations of exchange that exist in the territory.

We explored the connectivity and structure of the seed exchanges’ network by estimating nodal metrics, such as centrality. Structural analysis of the network and estimation of centrality were carried out using the sna package for R. Isolated nodes, or those that are not related to others in the network, were removed to avoid affecting estimations of the network’s metrics [59]. The data obtained about the network’s characterization were projected in a geographical information system using ArcGIS Pro software. Spatial autocorrelation analysis was employed to determine whether there was a statistically significant correlation between the values of the attribute “type of actor (Mapuche/non-Mapuche)” in two contiguous geographical locations [60]. This process was carried out by calculating Moran’s Index [61]. A value of this index greater than 0 indicates a positive spatial correlation (a clustering pattern) while a value of less than 0 indicates a negative spatial correlation (a disperse pattern) and a value of 0 is associated with an absence of autocorrelation (a random pattern) [62]. Using this calculation, it was determined that the highest tendency for clustering among the Mapuche people in this network occurs in the Liucura River basin.

In the second stage of the study, we took an ethnographic approach to the Liucura River basin, exploring the practice of the trafkintu in detail in the localities of Coilaco, Pichares, Huepil, and Lefincul in collaboration with 12 women from the territory. We used purposive sampling, considering residence within the watershed and roles in seed stewardship. Through long-term collaboration, we allowed participants to define the pace of trust-building and observed a full agricultural cycle.

Our analysis uses an inductive approach, building theory from the ground up through extensive ethnographic research. The concepts of biocultural memory and social-ecological reciprocity were not pre-defined categories but rather emerged as interconnected themes through our engagement with the Mapuche practice of trafkintu. Social-ecological reciprocity was observed through the direct exchange of seeds, where participants emphasized the importance of maintaining balanced relationships, ensuring that giving and receiving are part of long-term commitments to land, biodiversity and social ties. We adopted an ethnographic approach based on the premise that the commitment that arises from participant observation, as a fundamental method of ethnography, serves as a vehicle through which both the researcher and the reader can gain an everyday insight into the phenomenon being studied [63]. The ethnography was based on a mobile methodology framework in Indigenous contexts [64, 65]. It involved close shadowing [66] under which the 12 women were accompanied over a complete agricultural cycle. We collected information by collaborating with them in seed-related agricultural tasks, such as sowing, harvesting, and storage. We also witnessed trafkintu among the participants, including some they had planned beforehand and others that occurred spontaneously as a result of casual encounters. We designed an intercultural interview format that serves as a platform for the exchange of knowledge and emotional encounters and as a space for recounting experiences that are not generally talked about, understood, or appreciated [67]. In addition, we conducted walking interviews [68] with seed keepers. These were complemented by conversations with their families, which were recorded in audio format, field notes, and photographs. Each interview was conducted after disclosing a confidentiality agreement, as required by the Ethics Committee overseeing this research.

From the beginning, we reflected on the differences in position between researchers and participants, focusing on how to address cultural differences when building trust and representing the study accurately. These reflections took place both within the academic team and in daily conversations with participants. To reduce this gap, we held feedback sessions where we shared data and results, and one participant joined this article as a co-author. We also participated in the daily tasks of the seed curators. Our goal was to cultivate transparent and authentic relationships, consistent with a decolonial research approach.

Using the material collected, we conducted an inductive analysis by iteratively reading the interviews and field notes in Nvivo. We then generated emerging codes, which we defined and grouped into broad categories. For instance, we grouped the codes “social-ecological vindication,” “multispecies relations,” and “decommodification” under the broader category of reciprocity. To validate the analysis, we held feedback workshops with some participants to ensure it aligned with their experiences, adjusting it as necessary based on their input. In summary, the network provides the quantitative foundation of the study, Moran’s Index offers insight into the spatial dynamics of seed exchanges, and ethnography reveals the social-ecological dynamics underpinning the phenomenon.

Results and discussion

Trafkintu as an everyday network of reciprocity

“Because if not, what are you going to do with the seeds? Store them and keep them for yourself for the rest of your life? Are you going to be able to cultivate that quantity of seeds if you also have a load of others? I think seeds are born to be shared” (C. S., Pichares). In the Andean foothills of Wallmapu, Indigenous and campesino communities have shared and exchanged seeds on a regular basis for generations, maintaining the ancestral practice of the trafkintu in colonial and neo-colonial times. In this place, exchanges of seeds are not isolated experiences but practices that constitute a network of exchanges connecting people and seeds and linking them to home gardens and the basin as the ecosystem of which they form a part [10, 45]. We initially identified individuals who participated in regular seed exchanges over the course of one year, and from this group, selected 65 participants. Through 110 exchanges (links) conducted in 2022, these participants formed a network that spatially connects various localities and individuals living in the Andean foothills across the Municipal districts of Villarrica, Pucón, and Curarrehue (Fig. 1).

This network of regular exchanges was composed mainly of women (who represent 89% of the nodes in Fig. 1). Mapuche women, in particular, have played a decisive role in shaping a network of exchanges that is understood and configured on the basis of the value of reciprocity, which is dynamically cultivated and safeguarded as a fundamental social and ecological value (discussed below). Central to this role is the practice of seed caring, a responsibility predominantly assumed by women, as the management and administration of seeds and knowledge have historically been entrusted to them [69]. Through this stewardship, women have ensured the continuity of both the material and symbolic aspects of seed care, maintaining the intergenerational transmission of knowledge [70]. The significance of Mapuche women in these exchanges is expressed in both intercultural and spatial terms.

In intercultural terms, out of the total number of people participating in the network [65], only 28% were Mapuche and, of these, 100% were women (Table 1). From the cultural matrix of the Mapuche women, the value of reciprocity expanded, establishing relational ties with non-Mapuche women who also participated in the network: “When you meet all these people, we connect many more with a great territory (…) so beyond this exchange of seeds and being able to make this even bigger, it’s about nourishing ourselves, coming together, and communicating. It’s about expanding (…) Lots of women, lots of knowledge, and all of that multiplies” (C. S., Pichares). In general, non-Mapuche participants observe the practice with admiration and try to incorporate its values into their own activities. Among Mapuche participants, however, the values behind trafkintu prevail because it is a tradition passed down through generations with strong cultural roots. While reciprocity was broadly described as an inclusive value, participation in the everyday networks of trafkintu was nonetheless shaped by informal criteria. Entry into these networks often depended on pre-existing relationships of trust, the contributions that participants were able to offer, and the extent to which the inclusion of new actors was seen as compatible with maintaining the cultural integrity of the practice. As such, exchange networks in the area link two distinct types of actors—Mapuche and non-Mapuche—who establish reciprocal relationships through the exchange of seeds. In this way, the act of exchanging seeds shapes intercultural relationships based on the Mapuche value of reciprocity (Table 1).

In spatial terms, the role of Mapuche women in the network also had a key territorial significance and correlation. Figure 1 shows a concentration of exchanges in the basin of the Liucura River, located in the Andean foothill areas, where there are six títulos de merced (i.e., Indigenous Reservation) and, therefore, a large number of Mapuche communities. In this basin, we found that exchanges intensified, forming a pivotal “larger spatial node” within the network. The actors living in this area represent only 29% of the total number of people in the network but participated in 46% of the exchanges. Moreover, out of the 19 people identified in this basin, 95% were Mapuche women living in two of the area’s six títulos de merced.

Historically, this basin has been subjected to extensive colonial processes, and the Mapuche women’s determination to steward lands, communities, home gardens, and seeds in an interdependent manner is a true spatial epicenter that shapes the network in terms of social-ecological reciprocity. On the first point, a significant group of actors involved in the exchanges lived within the título de merced known as José María Avilés, which had the highest frequency of exchanges in all the basin. According to local biocultural memory, the different Indigenous communities of the Liucura basin once formed a single lof (a traditional Mapuche territorial unit). Its division into títulos de merced and the Chileanization of lands led to the territory’s fragmentation. This was also accompanied by a transformation of production systems and a lack of connection with urban centers. Creating, cultivating, and safeguarding a local supply network, therefore, became a necessity and was possible thanks to the role of women in small-scale farms and the community relations they maintained within the basin.

Given this territorial complexity, the trafkintu has been one of the mechanisms contributing to food sovereignty and the development of local subsistence economies over time. In this sense, there is a correlation between the maintenance of this practice and the immobilization policies historically imposed on Indigenous communities. The president of the José María Avilés community, noted that “[The trafkintu] also happened because there was no easy way to get to Pucón [the basin’s main city] for supplies because life here was very different. It’s not like transport today, which, although you see it as limited, is a lot compared to how it was before. So, we had to save everything to have seeds and food.”. This illustrates that trafkintu plays an important role where limited access to markets and urban establishments reinforces the need for self-sufficiency and the circulation of seeds. Here, reciprocity is not only a quality of relationships, but also a practical necessity of everyday life. The persistence of trafkintu, even in the face of changing accessibility to urban areas and markets, speaks to its value for the intergenerational continuity of the originating culture. This also demonstrates how trafkintu is contextually contingent and constantly reinterpreted according to social-ecological transformations while maintaining biocultural memory at its core.

On the second point about exchanges that enter (A) and exit (B) the Liucura basin (Fig. 3), this basin is seen to act as a node that gives dynamism to the rest of the network. Exchanges do not occur only on limited scales (i.e., within the Liucura basin), but also interconnect different localities in the Andean foothills.

Each meeting and each exchange embed the network in different localities in the territory, producing new iterations of social-ecological reciprocity. In these networks, it is not only seeds that were exchanged, but also knowledge and objects related to the agricultural cycle, such as sowing times, farm management practices, the storage of harvests, the reproduction of species, and the preparation and cooking of different dishes with the local produce. This multidimensional complexity is what enables seed exchanges to contribute to the strengthening of social bonds and trust, promoting social-ecological resilience in multiple contexts [49, 50]. They also included relations between humans and more-than-human beings that shaped the social-ecological meaning of reciprocity, like that cultivated across several generations by Mapuche women in the Liucura basin. “In reality, the interactions most commonly observed in nature are those of collaboration rather than competition. So, I feel like this is a space that brings humans closer to their essence as fundamentally part of nature.” (J.L., Pedregoso). Similarly, another woman told us, “If you intervene in that environment, which is where the community lives, and the community depends on the biodiversity there, then our home gardens are affected by the surrounding environment.” (K.B., Huife).

This network of social-ecological reciprocity, nourished by the regular exchange of seeds and knowledge, is sustained mainly by Mapuche women who, in turn, sustain their communities. Rooted in cultural and territorial traditions yet expanding through broader connections of exchange and knowledge-sharing, it demonstrates its relevance amid ongoing environmental and social changes. The Liucura basin has become an epicenter from which Mapuche women, through their farming activities and communities, forge dynamic social-ecological links via the trafkintu, localizing global histories of colonization and Indigenous efforts to sustain diverse biocultural practices. Participants emphasized that their leadership in sustaining the trafkintu is rooted in confronting the structural injustices embedded in the títulos de merced system and in contemporary neoliberal dynamics shaping agriculture and rural development in Chile [71]. Working within—and simultaneously challenging—the constraints of these historical land allocations, they expand reciprocal networks that connect people and resources across the basin, resisting the commodification of agriculture and defending the visibility of Indigenous practices. For many, seed exchange and stewardship are not only central to community sustenance but also vital acts of resistance against colonial legacies embedded in agrobusiness policies and the forced, indiscriminate introduction of industrial seeds by the global food industry. In this sense, the trafkintu operates as both a safeguard of biocultural diversity and a political tool to defend territorial and agricultural autonomy. This political dimension—anchored in everyday social-ecological care and exchange—shows how reciprocity can function simultaneously as a means of survival and a strategy for reconfiguring power relations in rural territories, further strengthening this network.

Biocultural memory of reciprocity

“They can take everything away, but they can’t take away being Mapuche (…) That’s why it’s worth consuming all these natural products because it makes my piwke4 happy,” says Zuni, a woman with a long life in the territory. In this territorial context, the notion of reciprocity has deep cultural roots, vindicating a substantial ontological difference with Western interpretations of the relationship between people and their territory. While conventional interpretations focus on the transaction, adopting a predominantly economic perspective on relations of exchange, the worldview of the trafkintu centers on interdependence and reciprocity’s cultural roots. Reciprocity in seeds is seen in that the care devoted to them is reflected in their productivity, the health of the crops, and the fertility of seeds for the next season. “If the person loves their seeds, if the person loves what they are doing, they produce” (A. B., Pichares), which will then influence the behavior of the seed keepers in subsequent cycles.

In the Liucura basin, there is a widespread notion that the trafkintu always existed broadly across Mapuche territories but, for various reasons, ceased to be practiced openly, albeit continuing quietly on a small-scale: “My dad, his brother, and other neighbors from over there, when they were going to sow, they would walk a long way, sometimes they didn’t have food for the journey and stopped at certain places. In this way, they practiced exchange” (A. B., Pichares). A.B.’s memory places trafkintu within ancient patterns of mobility and mutual aid. The recounted experience suggests that, historically, trafkintu has been a means of connecting households scattered across the territory through visits and exchanges. Although the logistics have changed today, the principle of using reciprocity to sustain interconnected lives remains.

Conventional historical interpretations suggest that human action has only recently had a significant impact on territories’ ecological and geographical transformation [72]. This is in contrast to Indigenous history, illustrated by the quote above, which demonstrates that people and different agricultural products, along with all their biocultural information, have been a constant among peoples for generations [73].

Today, the trafkintu is linked to values and significances that have their origins in biocultural memory and give meaning to this everyday practice as well as credibility and recognition to those who practice it. However, in challenging historical contexts, the exchange of seeds has been practiced in relative silence and been devalued by negative perceptions linked to economic conditions. Jorge, the Indigenous Community president’s son associates this stigma with the poverty often suffered by campesino and Mapuche people: “I have realized that before people who exchanged seeds were denigrated as in ‘oh, they are poor, that’s why they do it’ so they tended to hide it.” This memory illustrates how the value placed on trafkintu has evolved in response to broader social narratives. The association of seed exchange with poverty reflects a time when market integration and industrial agriculture were seen as signs of progress, which devalued community-based and subsistence-oriented practices. In that context, although reciprocity did not disappear, it was often practiced discreetly, indicating the simultaneous resilience and stigmatization of non-market forms of exchange. The current resurgence of trafkintu reimagined as cultural heritage represents a reversal of that stigma, highlighting that the meaning of reciprocity is historically contingent and shaped by changing economic ideologies. It also demonstrates that the current revival is a cultural and political statement.

Thus, at a historical moment when Mapuche cultural practices are beginning to be revalued, the trafkintu is re-emerging to play a key role in the reconstruction of biocultural memory, both as a collective and identity phenomenon and in relation to seeds, small farming, and biodiversity as a whole: “I believe that the interest in growing our own food is increasing, and I also think that communities are becoming more empowered, making them more willing to reclaim their ancestral knowledge—trafkintu and home gardens, of course, are part of that. So, I think it’s expanding, with seeds being protected by the communities themselves, because the risk of losing them is growing. That’s it—seeds protected above all.” (K.B., Huife).

Similarly, another participant shows us the relevance of the cultural practice as vindication “I think it’s really beautiful because it feels like a form of rescue, a way of reclaiming traditions that, as I was saying, we might not have been fully aware of before—it was just something normal, part of everyday life. So, it’s like going back to our roots, even if just for a day.” (N.R., Coilaco) This reflection frames trafkintu as a cultural vindication that transforms cultural practices, which have become naturalized in everyday life, into a form of conscious resistance. This notion involves the deliberate revitalization of a phenomenon that gains significance through its cultural significance as well as through the exchange of seeds itself. It shows that the “memory of reciprocity” can serve as a temporal bridge, connecting tradition to the mutability of culture through sustainable practices like trafkintu. At the same time, it empowers individuals to shape their cultural narratives.

Engaging in the trafkintu, thus, becomes a reaffirmation of biocultural memory and the history of the territory while strengthening community and social-ecological ties. Magali describes a shift in social perceptions of campesino practices and seed exchange: “Before, it was as if one was embarrassed to talk about small farming just anywhere and to talk about seeds. Now, it’s not” (M. R., Lefincul).

The ongoing practice of the trafkintu is a manifestation of sovereignty rooted in Mapuche biocultural memory. Social-ecological reciprocity is manifested in everyday exchanges between neighbors, where farmers offer seeds with the knowledge that they will be reciprocated in the future. This practice has persisted across generations, although interviews indicate that its scale and meaning is changing due to the increasing commercialization of agriculture and external economic pressures, among other factors earlier discussed. This phenomenon represents a constant effort to vindicate into the present experiences and social practices that have been affected by the fragmentation of memory. The trafkintu are transformed into processes through which biocultural memory is reorganized adaptively. In other words, the notion of cultural tradition is updated in the practice of the phenomenon, which brings relational ontologies present in biocultural memory into everyday life, expressed in the keepers and their seeds. As generations change, the trafkintu also changes, incorporating innovations without losing its foundations. This process of transformation shows the adaptability of the practice, whose essential elements are maintained over time, while new forms of participation and knowledge are incorporated in a conscious effort to maintain cultural practices: “I want to teach everything I learned to children, young people, anyone who wants to learn because they were very beautiful things. I will die soon but I am at peace because I have taught. Our culture will not die” (Z. C., Carhuello).

The biocultural memory of reciprocity is also made tangible in the seed, which is the result of years of reciprocal practices of care involving seeds, women, and communities. The seeds’ resistance to local climatic conditions, their adaptation to the terrain, and cultural suitability are among the reasons why the territory’s families prefer traditional seeds to other types. “People realized that the seeds brought [from outside] lasted that year and, the next year, they didn’t have any and had to buy the same seeds again (…) They lost out, that’s why they search now to find the seeds from before, the same ones from before, to start again with them, to continue with them” (A. B., Pichares). This observation reinforces the value of traditional seeds, encouraging the search for them and their recovery, especially by keepers for whom this is a daily task. In this sense, the trafkintu transcends its impact in the agricultural sphere of Indigenous and campesino life, and influences the territories where these communities are rooted. Thus, a reciprocal tie is established between seed keepers and their territories, consolidating a practice that promotes the nurture of biocultural memory [33, 33, 74].

Care in social-ecological reciprocity

On the banks of the Lefincul River in the Liucura basin, Magali proudly shows us the fields she has cultivated for decades. “Since I was a child, I liked the farms, going out to see how the neighbors had their fields,” she recalls. Her 95-year-old mother is a seed keeper and it was from her that Magali began to learn about farming. “It’s lovely to exchange little plants with the women neighbors; you have this that I don’t have, and I give you this other one, and we would exchange like that. That’s why I say that exchange has always existed.” Magali’s history exemplifies the transfer of knowledge and practices rooted in care for seeds that have a deep emotional significance. Additionally, as she further explains, her mother’s legacy serves as a form of resistance in a context of widespread devaluation of traditional agricultural practices.

The maternal lineage of seed keepers is an essential link between the emotional sphere, seed conservation, and the ecosystem as a whole. “If a person loves their seeds, if a person loves what they are doing, the seed thrives. That is the way I see it” (A. B., Pichares). The responsibilities of this role include the intergenerational transmission of traditions, affections, and agricultural knowledge. For example, Magali’s mother is recognized as a seed keeper but also for having been a teacher to the first generations who grew up in the valley. This intersection of roles has allowed her to foster reciprocal ties between seeds and many people in the territory through education, primarily in oral form. Although her mother is such an important social actor in the territory, Magali acknowledges that each trafkintu is a new learning opportunity and, therefore, an opportunity to deepen biocultural memory. “I can’t tell you that I know everything because there are many things one continues to learn. One always learns something from someone else, from what the other person in these encounters knows; one always comes away with something new one didn’t know” (M. R., Lefincul). In this sense, each trafkintu is an opportunity to deepen understanding and safeguard the practices and knowledge associated with care for seeds and, therefore, a means to expand biocultural memory: “One keeps learning, learning about care, learning to love your seeds, so to speak, the importance of preserving them and that they are not lost” (A. B., Pichares). These learnings, beyond being part of the transmission of biocultural memory, have a vindicative purpose, as they challenge the diminishing of ancestral knowledge.

The trafkintu tends to acquire a dimension of family, roots, and belonging that is transmitted through the act of exchange itself, which is not merely a common transaction, but also often implies a strengthening of biocultural memory and local identity. The identity recovered through the exchange of seeds reinforces cultural values and fosters stronger intergenerational relationships, creating cohesion through the network and helping to sustain communities. Reconnection with these seeds represents a relationship of mutual care between the seeds and the people involved in the exchange: “It’s an energy between the earth, the seed, the person, what is born; the joy you get from seeing that what you planted has been born” (A. B., Pichares).

Given the historical gender pattern of horticulture in the south of Chile, seed keepers tend to be women [42]. Many of them are part of Mapuche communities, although non-Indigenous women can also be recognized as keepers. It is usually women from nearby localities who exchange seeds to diversify their home gardens, ensure the conservation of species, and guarantee the future availability of seeds should their harvest fail. In addition, by expanding the availability of seeds, their exchange helps to strengthen community sovereignty in the face of a threat: “We already heard that seeds were going to come certified and that we would have to buy them every year from a single company, a single brand” (P. A., Menetúe). This concern reflects the crucial role of women seed keepers, who not only defend food sovereignty but also extend seed exchange practices through trafkintu [40]. Although the cultivation of seeds and their care take place in the private sphere, they play a crucial role in sustaining these communities more broadly. This aspect of their role has not received significant social recognition and is underestimated in societies that prioritize capitalist production. In this sense, Patricia illustrates a deeply community and solidarity-based vision of the trafkintu in that her approach seeks to conserve not only seed varieties but also the communities’ agricultural traditions: a fundamental pillar of biocultural memory. “The idea in the end would be to conserve all the communities, all the Mapuche and non-Mapuche territories, that they have seeds to sow, that hopefully there will always be seeds from the old times.” The practice of trafkintu facilitates access to traditional seeds with traits that enhance their resilience to social-ecological crises [67, 68]. It is seeds that, through the need for their reproduction and dissemination, play a key role in enabling the social-ecological relationships that sustain community ties. These practices as shown in other Indigenous contexts—e.g., with Rukai farmers in Taiwan [7]—empower communities by making food sustenance available, maintaining the genetic diversity of agricultural landscapes, and strengthening the social fabric.

Under the Mapuche worldview, relations with the territory are based on fundamental values, such as respect and reciprocity or kelluwün, that guide interaction with social-ecological spaces [19]. The relations between the women of a family or a community also affect the relations they establish with seeds: “My father’s mother always had beans in her hands. I felt that she was caressing those beans and I found them so pretty, and when they were put in the food, it was something you could chew too, like a big, tasty bean (…) I think the reason I most love them is that, for me, they were very much about home and I like that a lot.” (P. R., Pichares). Among the seeds exchanged, one particularly significant example is poroto pallar, which has cultural and nutritional importance in the region. Paty, a seed keeper, has shared poroto pallar with both Mapuche and non-Mapuche farmers, suggesting that its distribution depends on seasonal conditions and reciprocal relationships. The deep connection between people, seeds, and territories alters the dynamics of dispossession and strengthens biocultural memory and care. Consequently, the actions of these women in the private sphere influence the territory of which this network of exchange is comprised, because they sustain the sovereignty of communities through reciprocity, expressed with seeds and other women in the area. The exchange of poroto pallar illustrates how these networks extend beyond cultural boundaries, reinforcing ties between families and ensuring the continuity of traditional crop varieties. This movement between private and community spheres underscores how the actions of local women, as in the trafkintu, can have a far-reaching impact on communities’ sustainment and good living.

The practice of trafkintu and the role of seedkeepers are deeply intertwined with biocultural memories of reciprocity, extending beyond exchange to the emotions, affects and relationships that sustain human and ecological communities. Beyond the act of exchange itself, these practices involve emotions, relationships, and commitments that sustain both human and ecological communities. Through these networks, seeds carry not only genetic material, but also histories of care, adaptation, and resistance.

Conclusions

In this study, we have shown how social-ecological reciprocity practices—such as those seen in the Mapuche trafkintu—are intrinsically related to biocultural memory, which operates as both a collective historical consciousness and a practical foundation for sovereignty and interdependence between people, more-than-human beings, and their territories [75]. This biocultural memory guides and safeguards a relational quality [2] that defines reciprocity, with the trafkintu functioning as an adaptive and dynamically constitutive force within it [2]. The effort, creativity, and constant resistance of women seed keepers in safeguarding, maintaining, and sharing the Mapuche practice of trafkintu over generations illustrate a social-ecological resurgence [76] that seeks, in part, to regenerate a biocultural memory that has been fragmented by centuries of colonization and dispossession. Each everyday trafkintu exchange—between women, seeds, home gardens, and other more-than-human beings—emerges as a “cumulative experience” [51] that shapes a biocultural memory in space and time. This aligns with findings in other contexts, such as Kenya, Peru, and Cameroon, where seed exchange not only conserves agrobiodiversity, but also reinforce social-ecological relationships between people, their communities, and territories [46–48]. It is also in line with frameworks such as the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, which highlight the value of Indigenous knowledge in addressing social-ecological challenges at the planetary level [77] where biocultural memory is key. Although there are parallels with other contexts, the social-ecological specificity of trafkintu offers insights that can be applied to similar cases rather than providing generalizable statistics. Here, we suggest that this interdependent loop between social-ecological reciprocity and biocultural memory can be understood as “biocultural memory of reciprocity, a concept that may inform future research on reciprocity and political processes of decolonization and education in Indigenous and intercultural contexts. Below, we briefly describe some implications of this insight:

Care, affectivity, and interdependence: Our collaborators’ practices and narratives reinforce the Indigenous idea that social-ecological reciprocity is a key value in Indigenous contexts [2, 12, 13, 15]. As evidenced in the trafkintu, not only are human and more-than-human beings understood as part of interdependent ecologies [19, 20], but their relations are also seen as demanding mutual respect, care, and coexistence [21, 22]. Through social-ecological reciprocity practices, the latter emerge as territorialized values and a political stance on how to understand relations with more-than-human beings and biodiversity.

Vindication: The trafkintu has played a crucial role as one element within a broader context of Indigenous community survival in the face of colonization and neo-colonization processes. While it has been constitutive of biocultural memories and knowledge, its endurance and transformation have also been shaped by other factors, including shifting political, economic, and environmental conditions, as well as broader Indigenous resistance strategies. These interconnected dynamics have allowed biocultural memory and reciprocity practices to transcend and adapt to colonial contexts and decolonial intentions in the region. However, these practices are also shaped and constrained by contemporary political-economic structures. Extractivist industries, export-oriented agribusiness, neoliberal conservation regimes, and the legal architectures that sustain them—such as Chile’s land tenure laws, governance of protected areas, and intellectual property regulations on seeds—directly influence the material and cultural conditions for reciprocity. Naming these actors and frameworks clarifies that the trafkintu does not exist in a moral or cultural sphere insulated from the capitalist state, but rather in active contestation with it.

Reciprocity practices like the Mapuche trafkintu are anchored in social-ecological justice and complex intercultural contexts, acting as vindicatory forces that shape identity and justice positions in decolonial processes. Biocultural memory of reciprocity plays a critical role in connecting local practices to global processes, representing resistance and continuity in the face of homogenizing forces. As seen in other Indigenous seed exchange systems worldwide, it is vital that, as these practices expand, precautions are taken to avoid neo-colonial dynamics—such as the trivialization of principles or institutional control over community dynamics. Ensuring that seed exchanges follow local norms and biocultural knowledge is essential for their continuity [46, 48].

Reciprocity as a situated social-ecological practice: Understanding reciprocity from the experience itself allows us to heed Arnold’s [28] warning: in scientific work, it is important to be wary of simplistic and decontextualized interpretations of reciprocity. Rather than viewing it as a mere device to reconfigure the relationship between humans and the rest of biodiversity, it is important to recognize its Indigenous roots in order to avoid its folklorization within scientific discourse. Engaging meaningfully with social-ecological reciprocity means not only adopting it as a conceptual tool, but also ensuring that it is accompanied by respect and a deep commitment to the peoples from whom this practice originates. This includes acknowledging the historical and political contexts in which reciprocity is practiced, particularly within Indigenous communities that have experienced colonialism and dispossession for centuries. In our study, we have integrated relational and transdisciplinary methodologies to engage with these complexities, believing that such approaches contribute to a more contextually grounded scientific practice. Through this perspective, scientific and traditional knowledge can enter into dialog, with social-ecological reciprocity positioned as a key quality of the relationships that emerge in these interactions.

At the same time, the growing recognition of reciprocity within global sustainability discourses introduces a paradox: formalization and institutional support can strengthen visibility and resources, yet they also risk depoliticizing the practice and eroding its justice-oriented dimensions. Our findings suggest that the trafkintu, as practiced in the Liucura basin, embodies this paradox by simultaneously gaining wider recognition and resisting attempts to domesticate it into purely technical or market-oriented schemes. Addressing this tension requires policy and institutional engagement that respects Indigenous agency, protects seed sovereignty, and acknowledges the relational principles at the heart of reciprocity. Rather than subsuming these exchanges under external frameworks, such engagement should start from the lived practices of Indigenous peoples and their communities. This has implications for ethnobiology and Indigenous rights, providing grounded criteria that can help guide institutional engagement with reciprocity so as to avoid replicating extractive logics and to support decolonial pathways. In this way, our study highlights how reciprocity—anchored in everyday social-ecological care and exchange—not only sustains biocultural diversity, but also articulates political claims over territory and agriculture, offering situated lessons for broader debates on Indigenous-led futures.
