Cell membrane-camouflaged nanomedicines for enhanced thrombolysis and blood-brain barrier penetration in ischemic stroke therapy
Authors: Xiachan Chen, Yiming Liu, Jing Zou, Hao Chen, Hengrui Zhang, Yan Guo, Xingjie Zan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Thrombus-induced ischemic stroke (IS) remains a serious threat a serious health threat with limited therapeutic efficacy due to the dual challenges of precise thrombus targeting and restricted blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration. While conventional nanocarriers, such as liposomes, micelles, and polymeric nanoparticles (NPs), demonstrate clinical potential due to their mature preparation protocols, their application is limited by poor targeting accuracy, inadequate biocompatibility, and rapid systemic clearance. In response, microenvironment-responsive biomimetic drug delivery systems based on cell membrane-camouflaged nanomedicines (CM-NMs) have emerged as a promising strategy, leveraging the pathological features of ischemic lesions for enhanced targeting and treatment. CM-NMs stand out by utilizing cell membranes to preserve innate targeting and/or BBB penetration capabilities. This approach also ensures high biocompatibility and minimizes the risk of immune clearance. This review highlights recent advances in CM-NMs for IS treatment, critically discussing three key approaches: (1) platelet membrane-camouflaged nanomedicines (PLM-NMs), which mimic platelet adhesion for thrombus-specific accumulation, (2) immune cell membrane NMs and stem cell membrane NMs, which leverage inflammatory tropism or homing mechanisms for enhanced BBB penetration, and (3) hybrid membrane NMs, which enable multi-targeting capabilities. Furthermore, we discuss ongoing challenges and clinical translation potential of CM-NMs to provide guidance for next-generation CM-NMs.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS), accounting for majority of all stroke cases, is a leading cause of mortality and long-term neurological disability globally (GBD 2019 Stroke Collaborators, 2021; Saini et al., 2021; Campbell et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2024a). IS normally occurs when a vessel that supplies blood to the brain is narrowed or blocked, typically due to the formation of thrombus, leading to an inadequate blood supply (ischemia) and a lack of oxygen (hypoxia) in the brain (Saini et al., 2021). Then, a series of interconnected biochemical events are initiated, known as the ischemic cascade, which includes glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity, reactive oxygen species (ROS) overproduction, blood-brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction, and cell death through apoptosis or necrosis (Fig. 1) (Neves et al., 2023). This cascade also involves the activation of the complement system, initiation of inflammation, and immune response. The interplay of these factors ultimately results in the expansion of ischemic penumbra and irreversible brain damage (Campbell et al., 2019; Stoll et al., 2008; Rehman et al., 2024). After IS, fast restoration of the blood flow (reperfusion) is always employed to restore the blood flow to the ischemic tissue; this process can be initiated through medical interventions such as thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy (Powers et al., 2019; Herpich and Rincon, 2020). Therefore, the treatment of ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury requires strategies that not only restore blood flow but also protect the brain tissue (Zhang et al., 2024b). Conventional therapeutic paradigms confront two main biological barriers: (1) the narrow 4.5–6 h therapeutic window of thrombolytics and a high risk of hemorrhagic transformation due to the high dosage of thrombolytics necessitated by their low targeting efficiency and short half-life (Tsivgoulis et al., 2023; Marko et al., 2020), and (2) the BBB's selective permeability that excludes about 98 % of small molecules and nearly all large molecules (Zha et al., 2024). Nanomaterials have emerged as a promising counterstrategy, with liposomes, micelles, and polymer nanoparticle (NP) systems showing enhanced targeting, extended therapeutic window and therapeutic efficiency (Dong et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021; He et al., 2021a; Li et al., 2021a; Lin et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024). Nevertheless, their clinical translation remains impeded by inadequate stability and biocompatibility, immune-mediated clearance, and insufficient BBB penetration.

Biological membrane-derived nanocarriers (BM-NCs) utilize endogenous substances of natural origin, extracted and purified from organisms or microorganisms, such as cells, viruses, bacteria, and extracellular vesicles. Compared to other nanocarriers, BM-NCs exhibit excellent biocompatibility and degradability. Furthermore, BM-NCs can mimic the native cell surface, which not only facilitates interactions with target tissues and cells but also avoids immune recognition, such as through CD47 “don't eat me” signals on cell membranes, thereby enhancing BBB penetration and targeted delivery, demonstrating the great potential in the treatment of various brain diseases, including IS, gliomas, Parkinson's disease, and Alzheimer's disease (Liao et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2023a). Particularly compelling are cell membrane-camouflaged nanomedicines (CM-NMs), which synergistically combine synthetic NP cores with h cell membrane shells. The physicochemical properties of these cell membranes enable cells to perform complex biological functions, including interactions with the surrounding environment and other cells. Cell membranes from different sources, such as those from thrombus-related cells, immune cells, stem cells, and cancer cells, have been used to treat IS and have shown significant therapeutic effect in laboratory studies. In this article, through analyzing preclinical studies published since 2018, we attempt to provide a comprehensive review of development of CM-NMs for IS treatment and propose an evidence-based framework for the optimization of next-generation CM-NMs.

Drugs for IS treatment

Pharmacological thrombolysis plays a important role in IS treatment (Powers et al., 2019; Herpich and Rincon, 2020), offering distinct advantages over mechanical thrombectomy, such as a wider treatment window, more comprehensive thrombolysis, lower procedural risk, higher accessibility, and similar efficacy and safety (Frank et al., 2022). When combined with antithrombotic and neuroprotective agents, pharmacological approaches support comprehensive IS treatment (Zhang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024c).

Thrombolytics

The longer the obstruction in an IS, the higher the risk of irreversible neurological damage and even death; therefore, rapid thrombolysis and reperfusion are the priority for the effective treatment of IS in clinical practice (Tsivgoulis et al., 2023; Yafasova et al., 2021). Recombinant tissue-type plasminogen activator (rtPA) is currently the only U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drug for the treatment of IS (Tsivgoulis et al., 2023; Marko et al., 2020). RtPA is a kind of fibrinolytic agent that activates the transformation of plasminogen to plasmin, thereby initiating thrombolysis. However, due to its short half-life in circulation (2–6 min), rtPA requires high doses for effective thrombolysis, which always demands a second intravenous intravenous access or an infusion pump. This requirement can be harmful as it may disrupt normal hemostasis and increase the risk of bleeding complications.

New thrombolytic agents offering enhanced safety, efficacy, and ease of use have been developed. It is reported that a third-generation drug, tenecteplase, is preferred over alteplase due to its higher fibrin affinity, quicker action, longer half-life, lower plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 inhibition, simplified administration, and enhanced efficacy in large vessel occlusion cases (Zhong et al., 2021; Kobeissi et al., 2023). Non-immunogenic recombinant staphylokinase and recombinant urokinase are promising thrombolytic agents for large vessel occlusions, owing to their high fibrin affinity and favorable safety profiles (Tsivgoulis et al., 2023). Other fibrin-specific tissue plasminogen activators like lanoteplase, monteplase, pamiteplase, staphylokinase, desmoteplase, and chimeric plasminogen activator, along with non-specific molecules such as urokinase, prourokinase, and anisoylated plasminogen streptokinase activator complex (APSAC), have also shown potential for IS treatment (Kaviarasi et al., 2019). However, all these thrombolytic agents carry a risk of intracerebral hemorrhages and other bleeding complications due to the activation of matrix metalloproteinases and the generation of free radicals.

Additionally, the use of pharmacological agents for the prevention of secondary stroke is also crucial in the treatment of IS, with the primary drugs being antiplatelets and anticoagulants (Tsivgoulis et al., 2023; Kaviarasi et al., 2019; Kapil et al., 2017; Bhatia et al., 2023). Antiplatelets work by inhibiting the activation pathways of platelets, while anticoagulants exert their effects by blocking the coagulation cascade. Clinical studies have shown that antiplatelets are safe and effective in preventing non-cardiogenic strokes; while anticoagulants are more commonly used to prevent cardiogenic strokes. Commonly used antiplatelets include aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor, aggrenox (a combination of aspirin and dipyridamole), cilostazol, prasugrel, and glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors such as tirofiban and eptifibatide (Kapil et al., 2017). Anticoagulants widely used include vitamin K antagonists like warfarin, as well as dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban, and argatroban (Kapil et al., 2017). Each drug possesses its own unique mechanism of action, and they all share some common adverse events, such as gastrointestinal bleeding and, in more severe cases, severe intracranial bleeding.

Neuroprotectant

After IS, as shown in Fig. 1, a cascade of biochemical reactions begins, resulting in neuronal cell death and neuroinflammation (Campbell et al., 2019; Shehjar et al., 2023). At the very beginning, neurons depolarize and release glutamate, causing increased calcium influx and excitotoxic cell death. Additionally, the overproduction of ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) aggravates neuroinflammation by boosting the release of inflammatory factors like tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), leading to further neuronal loss. Moreover, the increased proteolytic activity, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation further enhance the BBB permeability and leukocyte extravasation. Consequently, the second primary goal of IS treatment strategies is to restore brain functions in the ischemic penumbra through neuroprotection and neuroregeneration.

Neuroprotective agents slow or prevent cerebral infarction progression after IS by enhancing the survival of neurons. The most common neuroprotective agents help eliminate highly damaging free radicals, thus protecting ischemic neurons from irreversible damage. They are typically used in combination with other types of drugs, including calcium channel blockers, calcium chelators, GABA agonists, glutamate antagonists, and nitric oxide (NO) antagonists. Currently, there are no clinically approved neuroprotective agents that can reduce brain damage after IS (Kong et al., 2022). On a slightly brighter note, research on neuroprotective agents in the laboratory has never ceased, and some drugs have completed clinical trials and are considered promising neuroprotective candidates, such as human urinary kallikreinogenase (HUK), nerinetide (NA-1), 3K3A-activated protein C (3K3A-APC), and edaravone (Edv); meanwhile, hundreds of neuroprotective drugs have shown promise in preclinical studies (Paul and Candelario-Jalil, 2021; Haupt et al., 2023). In addition, many natural products have demonstrated neuroprotective effects in IS treatment, including flavonoids, polysaccharides, and polyphenols (Kong et al., 2022). Numerous inorganic nanomaterials, such as carbon-based NPs, ceria NPs, platinum NPs, gold nanoclusters, Mn3O4 nanoenzymes, and hollow Prussian blue nanoenzymes, are used as neuroprotective agents (Kong et al., 2022). However, the effectiveness of most neuroprotective agents in treating IS is limited, mainly due to their short half-lives, potential toxicity, poor distribution specificity, and low efficiency in crossing the BBB to achieve effective drug accumulation at the targeted lesions.

Targeting and responsive release of drugs for IS treatment

Currently, the development of novel and highly effective therapeutic agents for IS has been insufficient, while the existing drugs (including both thrombolytic agents and neuroprotective agents) exhibit inherent limitations such as low targeting specificity, non-specific distribution, and short half-life. Therefore, there is an urgent need for innovative drug delivery strategies to enhance the therapeutic performance of existing drugs and overcome clinical application barriers. In the treatment of IS, specific nanocarriers are designed to deliver and release drugs at the desired sites (thrombus and ischemic parenchyma), thereby increasing the local drug concentration. They primarily rely on guidance mechanisms such as the intrinsic tropism of biomimetic nanocarriers, the high affinity between the ligands on the nanocarriers and overexpressed receptors in specific regions, the specific pathological microenvironment (e.g., upregulated ROS levels, lower pH, and specific enzymes), or external stimuli (e.g., light, magnetism, and ultrasound) (Zhan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024a).

Targeting thrombus

After vascular endothelial damage, platelets adhere to the exposed collagen surface and become activated. Once activated, platelets aggregate and intertwine tightly with fibrin to form the initial thrombus that traps red blood cells and other substances in the blood (Joundi and Menon, 2021; Jolugbo and Ariens, 2021). Therefore, a typical thrombus is composed of various proportions of fibrin, platelets, red blood cells (RBCs), and white blood cells (Joundi and Menon, 2021; Jolugbo and Ariens, 2021). There are many thrombus-associated proteins and enzymes, including collagen, von Willebrand Factor (vWF), fibrin, factor XIIIa, phospholipase (PLA2), and thrombin, which can all be used as targets to trigger thrombolysis (Zhang et al., 2022). Peptides that specifically bind to these proteins also can provide precise thrombus targeting capabilities for nanomedicines. Therefore, based on the microenvironment of the thrombus and the inherent characteristics of platelets and RBCs, biomimetic carriers can be designed to achieve targeted drug delivery and controlled release at thrombus site.

Additionally, compared to normal physiological environment, ROS is upregulated at the thrombus site due to the production of ROS by injured endothelial cells and activated platelets. Moreover, the pH value of the thrombus is lower due to the reduced oxygen supply from the peripheral blood surrounding the blood clot; the formed thrombus directly leads to vessel stenosis, which results in abnormally high and complex blood flow shear forces accompanying thrombus formation. The upregulated ROS, the lower pH value, and the significantly increased endogenous shear stress at the thrombus site can all be utilized to trigger the specific release of drugs (Zhan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024a).

External stimuli, such as light (particularly near-infrared light) and ultrasound, have been found to promote thrombolysis, and can also be used to trigger the release of thrombolytic drugs by designing drug delivery systems that respond to these stimuli (Zhan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024a).

Penetrating the BBB and targeting ischemic parenchyma

BBB is a special and inherent structure that regulates the trafficking of fluids, solutes, and cells between brain tissue and blood vessels, playing a crucial role in maintaining the homeostatic microenvironment of the central nervous system (CNS). Studies have shown that the BBB almost completely prevents all macromolecular drugs and nearly 98 % of small molecule drugs from entering brain tissue, making it the main challenge in delivering drugs to brain tissue. Fortunately, there exist a variety of strategies to facilitate the penetration of nanomedicines across the BBB and into the ischemic parenchyma, including passive diffusion, receptor-mediated transcytosis, adsorptive-mediated transcytosis, and carrier-mediated transport (Zha et al., 2024; Furtado et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2019). Certainly, the long-term safety of these methods that enhance the permeability of BBB to allow more nanomedicines to penetrate into the brain still needs further investigation, as excessive opening of BBB could increase the risk of hemorrhagic transformation.

It is important to note that following IS, BBB dysfunction occurs, characterized by increased permeability, leading to an imbalance in the homeostasis of water and ions in the brain, as well as the infiltration and immune cells, including neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages, and migration of stem cells, such as neural stem cells (NSCs) and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs). This change, on one hand, exacerbates brain injury, while on the other hand, the increased paracellular and transcellular permeability offers drugs the potential to cross the BBB and reach the brain parenchyma. Therefore, the infiltration of immune cells has inspired the design of cell-based drug delivery systems. Meanwhile, the overexpression of receptors such as C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4), nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAchR), integrin αvβ3, integrin αvβ1, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), extracellular matrix metalloproteinase inducer (CD147), vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM1), and cannabinoid 1 receptor (CB1) at the ischemic BBB provides specific targets for ligand-functioned drug delivery systems (Zhang et al., 2022).

After penetrating BBB, precisely delivering drugs to neurons or neuroglia, particularly microglia, is also a promising strategy to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of IS. In ischemic neurons or neuroblasts, the overexpression of glutamate receptors (GR), erythropoietin receptors (EPOR), receptors for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE), and growth-associated protein-43 (GAP43) make them potential targets for impaired neurons (Zhang et al., 2022). Microglia play a role in the CNS similar to that of macrophages in the peripheral system. Modulating the polarization of microglia from the pro-inflammatory M1 type to the anti-inflammatory M2 type is an effective therapeutic strategy for IS (Qin et al., 2019). Targeting receptors highly expressed in parenchyma, such as the mannose receptor (MR) and FAS (or CD95) in drug delivery system design may help increase the concentration of drugs around microglia, thereby promoting the transition of microglia from the pro-inflammatory M1 type to the anti-inflammatory M2 type.

Finally, designs targeting the specific pathological microenvironment of ischemic parenchyma, such as low pH, high ROS level, and overexpressed enzymes, along with strategies utilizing external stimuli, has also propelled the advancement of stimulus-responsive drug delivery systems for neuroprotection (Zhan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024a).

Platelet membrane NMs for IS

Platelets, also known as thrombocytes, possess a discoid morphology without nucleus. Platelets primarily participate in hemostasis and coagulation processes, and also play a crucial role in pathological and physiological processes such as cancer metastasis and host defense. The platelet membrane (PLM) is an asymmetric structure, composed of a phospholipid bilayer accounting for 35 % of its structure, 57 % proteins, and the remainder being carbohydrates distributed among glycolipids and glycoproteins. The CD47 protein present in platelets give unique “don't eat me” indication to macrophages, which helps them to survive by reducing macrophage uptake. Platelets express distinct surface receptors, including GPIb, GPIIb/IIIa, and P-selectin, which dynamically mediate adhesion to damaged vasculature, tumor cells, and pathogenic bacteria. Therefore, platelet membrane camouflaged NPs have long circulation time have been prepared widely towards targeted therapy in vascular disease therapy, cancer treatment, and management of infectious diseases (Kunde and Wairkar, 2021; Safdar et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2020).

PLM-based nanocarriers have received increasing attention for IS therapies, as they have natural thrombus-targeting activity, can prolong half-life of the drugs and reduce side effects (Xu et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022a; Quan et al., 2022; Kong et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024). For examples, Fig. 2 illustrates three representative nanoplatelet designs: tP-NP-rtPA/ZL006e (Xu et al., 2019) and PAMNs (Li et al., 2020), as well as the rPZDCu (Kong et al., 2024). The tP-NP-rtPA/ZL006e (Fig. 2a) is composed of a core made of dextran derivative polymeric NPs loaded with ZL006e, and a shell made of PLMs conjugated with a thrombin-cleavable Tat-peptide-coupled rtPA (Xu et al., 2019). This structure is engineered for thrombin-triggered release of rtPA, which then cross the BBB and transported into the brain via Tat-mediated transcytosis to deliver neuroprotectant ZL006e. On the other hand, PAMNs (Fig. 2b) consist of a PLM envelope encapsulating both L-arginine and γ-Fe2O3 magnetic NPs, enabling targeted thrombus delivery of L-arginine and in situ generation of NO via enzymatic decomposition of L-arginine (Li et al., 2020). These NPs leverage the mimetic properties of PLMs to preserve their natural affinity for thrombi. Guided by magnetic field gradients, these NPs can rapidly navigate to stroke lesions, enabling precise and expedited rtPA delivery to the affected area. The rPZDCu NPs (Fig. 2c) is formed by conjugating rtPA onto the PLM that was coated on ZDCu, which is a Cu4.6O NP encapsulated by zein-Se-Se-DHA (a conjugate of zein and DHA linked via a diselenide bond) (Kong et al., 2024). They are specifically designed to target thrombi through their PLM coating, facilitating the dissolution of thrombi with the conjugated rtPA. At stroke sites with elevated ROS levels, these NPs release Cu4.6O and DHA. Cu4.6O serves as an antioxidant, scavenging ROS and thereby reducing inflammation, which promotes the polarization of microglia from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. DHA, meanwhile, exerts neuroprotective effects and inhibits apoptosis in neural cells, safeguarding brain tissue from further damage.Fig. 2Representative PLM-NMs and their mechanism in the treatment of IS. Collectively, platelet membrane NMs enhance therapeutic efficacy by integrating the inherent thrombus-targeting capacity of natural platelets with multimodal synergistic mechanisms including thrombolysis and drug delivery, scavenging of ROS to mitigate oxidative stress, suppression of neuroinflammation, and neuroprotective effects via inhibition of neuronal apoptosis, thereby addressing the multifaceted pathophysiology of IS.

RBC membrane NMs for IS

RBCs, also known as erythrocytes, are biconcave-shaped blood cells that lack a cell nucleus and organelles. They are the most abundant cells in the blood and have a lifespan of approximately 120 days. The biconcave shape of RBCs provides a high surface area-to-volume ratio, which enhances the efficiency of gas exchange and transport. The RBC membrane is primarily composed of 35.1 % lipids, 39.5 % proteins, and 5.8 % carbohydrates (de Oliveira and Saldanha, 2010). The membrane expresses several immunomodulating molecules on its surface, including CD47, C8 binding protein (C8bp), homologous restriction factor (HRF), decay accelerating factor (DAF), membrane cofactor protein (MCP), complement receptor 1 (CR1), and CD59, which play a role in prolonging the lifespan of RBCs in circulation (Kunde and Wairkar, 2021; Safdar et al., 2024). Therefore, RBC-based drug carriers have immense engineering potential for the delivery of various drugs (Kunde and Wairkar, 2021; Safdar et al., 2024). Recently, the RBC membrane has attracted interest as a biomimetic cerebral delivery system due to erythrocytes' availability, prolonged circulation time, uniform size, and shape (Wu et al., 2023a). As RBCs inherently lack CNS-specific targeting abilities, RBC-based carriers for cerebral drug delivery require targeted surface functional modification.

In the field of IS treatment, research on RBC membrane-camouflaged NMs has also achieved progress. Three representative nanocarriers, SHp-RBC-NP/NR2B9C (Lv et al., 2018), MNET (Shi et al., 2020), and T-RBC-DTC (Zhao et al., 2020), exhibit unique advantages and mechanisms in therapy. The SHp-RBC-NP/NR2B9C nanocarrier, which composed of a dextran polymer core modified with ROS-responsive boronic ester (PHB-dextran) and a RBC membrane shell inserted with stroke homing peptide (SHp), aims to mimic RBCs to prolong circulation time and precisely target ischemic brain areas following intravenous injection (Lv et al., 2018). By utilizing SHp-mediated transcytosis, it delivers NR2B9C to neurons where high levels of ROS trigger the release of NR2B9C from PHB-dextran polymer NPs. This selective disruption of NMDAR-PSD-95 interactions prevents excessive NO production, thereby protecting neurons. The MNET nanocarrier, MNET (Mn3O4@nanoerythrocyte-T7) was developed by encapsulating Mn3O4, an antioxidant-mimicking nanoenzyme, within a natural erythrocyte and functionalizing it with T7 peptides to facilitate BBB crossing and accumulation at the infarct site (Shi et al., 2020). It releases oxygen to alleviate hypoxia, scavenges free radicals in neurons before and after thrombolysis, and maintains an oxygen-rich environment following thrombolysis, which is crucial for supporting tissue recovery and minimizing neurological deficits. T-RBC-DTC NPs feature a dextran core linked to tirofiban via an H2O2-sensitive bond, and a RBC membrane shell functionalized with the fibrin-targeting peptide CREKA (Zhao et al., 2020). T-RBC-DTC NPs scavenge H2O2 at the thrombus site and controllably release tirofiban. They protected cells from H2O2 damage in vitro and showed superior accumulation and antithrombotic effects in vivo compared to free tirofiban, with good biocompatibility.

Collectively, these RBC membrane-camouflaged nanocarriers demonstrate the potential for multi-targeted IS therapy, combining antithrombotic action (T-RBC-DTC), neuroprotection (SHp-RBC-NP), and anti-inflammatory (MNET) to enhance recovery and reduce the risk of reperfusion injury.

Immune cell membrane NMs for IS

Immune cells, including neutrophils and monocyte/macrophages are increasingly becoming a hot topic in biomimetic nanocarriers because of their excellent biocompatibility, the innate and specific targeting effect to areas with inflammation, low immunogenicity, improved therapeutic efficacy, and low reverse effect (Oroojalian et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022b). The inflammatory response following IS offers a unique opportunity for drug delivery to the ischemic brain and targeted cells. IS leads to tissue damage and the initiation of an inflammatory response, which involves the recruitment of immune cells to the stroke core and penumbra. Biomimetic nanocarriers that mimic the properties of these immune cells can take advantage of this inflammatory response (Hou et al., 2019).

Neutrophil membrane NMs for IS

Neutrophils make up 40–75 % of leukocytes in humans and are essential for innate immunity. They initiate an inflammatory response using specialized surface proteins like P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1) for endothelial rolling, lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1) and macrophage-1 antigen (MAC-1) for adhesion, and chemokine receptors, such as C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 1/2 (CXCR1/2) for migration to infection sites for eliminating pathogens via phagocytosis, degranulation, and NETosis. Neutrophil membrane NMs, which mimic the properties of neutrophils, have been widely used as targeted drug delivery systems for inflammatory diseases, including arthritis, sepsis, I/R injury, and liver fibrosis (Chu et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2024d).

As neutrophils are the most abundant and responsive leukocytes recruited to inflamed brain tissues after IS, therefore, neutrophils were used to fabricate nanomedicines able to target the ischemic brain, leveraging the innate properties of neutrophils to enhance drug delivery across the BBB and target the ischemic brain (Dong et al., 2019; Feng et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). These nanomedicines, such as MPBzyme@NCM (Feng et al., 2021), Leo@NM-Lipo (Tang et al., 2023), SNM-NP@Edv/RCD (Dong et al., 2023), and NRNs (Zhao et al., 2024), each provide effective means of treating IS (Fig. 3). Leo@NM-Lipo (Fig. 3b) (Tang et al., 2023), a biomimetic nanosystem composed of neutrophil membrane-camouflaged nanoliposomal leonurine, efficiently penetrates the disrupted BBB, robustly remodeling the cerebral microenvironment to reverse I/R injury. It targets the ischemic lesion, neutralizes ROS, inhibits neuronal apoptosis, resolves inflammation, and restores BBB integrity. SNM-NP@Edv/RCD (Fig. 3c) (Dong et al., 2023), a polyprodrug nanomedicine system composed of SHp-modified neutrophil membrane-camouflaged, ROS-responsive PBAP-functionalized β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) loaded with Edv, penetrates the BBB and targets inflamed I/R sites and damaged neurons by combining neutrophil membranes with SHp. Upon BBB penetration, it releases β-CD and Edv to eliminate ROS, thereby inhibiting neuroinflammation and apoptosis. Monocyte/macrophage membrane NMs for IS

Monocytes are the largest type of leukocyte and play a crucial role in the immune system. Upon tissue infiltration, monocytes can differentiate into macrophages in tissues. Monocytes rely on receptors such as cluster of differentiation 14 (CD14) and C—C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2) for migration and pathogen threat sensing, whereas macrophages utilize cluster of differentiation 163 (CD163), toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), and others to achieve precise phagocytosis and immunomodulation. Both monocytes and macrophages are naturally recruited to the sites of inflammation, where they perform functions such as phagocytosis of pathogens and dead cells, and regulation of immune responses. Monocyte/macrophage membrane NMs inherit their surface proteins to evade clearance by the immune system and precisely target inflamed or tumor tissues. Therefore, monocyte/macrophage membrane NMs have fabricated to treat diseases such as cancers, microbial infections, cardiovascular diseases, CNS diseases, and immune diseases (Hou et al., 2019; Krishnamurthy et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2022; Lopes et al., 2022).

Monocyte and macrophage membrane-camouflaged nanomedicines represent a novel therapeutic strategy for IS, inheriting the innate properties of these immune cells to enhance the targeting and efficacy of drug delivery systems (Wang et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021b; Liu et al., 2024a; Liu et al., 2024b; Ma et al., 2024). For examples, The McM/RNPs (Wang et al., 2021), as shown in Fig. 4a, are designed to mimic monocyte surfaces, enabling them to actively target and bind to inflammatory endothelial cells, inhibiting monocyte adhesion as a shield, and facilitate the penetration of endothelium to deliver Rapamycin to inflammatory cells as a sword. This targeted approach enhances the therapeutic effects of Rapamycin, reducing neuronal apoptosis and inflammatory cell infiltration, thereby protecting the ischemic brain. This coating significantly increases the NPs' accumulation in the damaged brain. The MnO2 component of these NPs consumes excess H2O2, converting it into O2, and subsequently decomposes in the acidic environment of lysosomes to release FTY. Microglia membrane NMs for IS

Microglia are resident immune cells in the CNS and are crucial for maintaining brain tissue function under both healthy and pathological conditions. They are the first cells to respond to the pathophysiological changes caused by IS. In IS, microglia play a dual role, acting as both neuroprotective and neurotoxic agents depending on their activation state and the temporal context post-stroke (Qin et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2020). M1-type (classical) microglia are known for generating pro-inflammatory substances such as TNF-α, IL-1β, interferon γ (IFN-γ), IL-6, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and proteolytic enzymes like MMP9 and MMP3. In contrast, M2-type (alternative) microglia are distinguished by their production of IL-10, transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), insulin-like growth factor, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which have pro-angiogenic and anti-inflammatory properties.

Microglia membrane-camouflaged nanomedicines represent an integrated therapeutic approach for IS, combining targeted delivery, immunomodulation, and ROS scavenging. For examples, the TPC@M2 NPs, catalase (CAT)-loaded tannic acid (TA) NPs with a M2-type microglia membrane were constructed (Duan et al., 2022). These NPs can on-demand release TA to coordinate excess Fe2+ and acid-responsively release CAT in response to acid, scavenging multiple ROS. This dual-action mechanism not only reduces oxidative stress but also repolarizes M1 microglia to the M2 phenotype, enhancing neuroprotection and supporting the recovery process in the context of IS. Furthermore, the TPC@M2 NPs exhibit ischemia-homing properties and the ability to cross the BBB. Additionally, microglia membrane are often integrated with other cell membrane to create hybrid membrane for IS treatment (Li et al., 2021c; Wu et al., 2024b), this will be introduced in detail in Section 3.6 on hybrid cell membrane NMs.

Stem cell membrane NMs for IS

Stem cells are a versatile cell type with self-renewal capacity, multipotent differentiation potential, and low immunogenicity. These stem cells, including NSCs and MSCs, are crucial in medical research and have applications in tissue engineering, drug screening, and cell therapy (Zakrzewski et al., 2019). Homing ability in stem cells refers to their inflammation tropism, which depends on the interaction between special ligands on stem cells and ligands in ischemic areas, especially the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis (Kucia et al., 2005; Li et al., 2015). Stem cell membrane-camouflaged nanomedicines represent an innovative therapeutic approach for IS, harnessing the functional properties of stem cell membranes to achieve targeted drug delivery to the injured brain (Ma et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2020; Shi et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2023b; Dong et al., 2024). For instance, Gao’ group modified stem cell membranes (including MSC (Wu et al., 2020; Dong et al., 2024) and NSC (Wu et al., 2023b)) with liposomes to camouflage drug-loaded liposomes NPs (Fig. 5a). The delivery of therapeutic agents such as curcumin (Wu et al., 2020), metformin (Wu et al., 2023b), and NBP (Dong et al., 2024) using these biomimetic vesicles has been shown to significantly enhance drug delivery and suppress neuroinflammation in IS models. Furthermore, by using MSC membranes engineered to overexpress CXCR4 (Fig. 5b), the delivery efficiency of drugs like glyburide (Ma et al., 2019) and A151 (Shi et al., 2021) is significantly improved, thereby reducing inflammation, and enhancing neuroprotection in IS models. Cancer cell membrane NMs for IS

Cancer cell membranes offer unique properties that make them ideal for nanomedicine applications, particularly in targeting cancer cells. These membranes are robust, can be cultured in large volumes in vitro, and possess homotypic targeting abilities, which facilitate the selective drug delivery to cancer cells. Moreover, they reduce immune clearance after administration, enhancing the drug’ circulation time and targeting efficiency. These unique properties make cancer cell membrane-camouflaged nanomedicine a promising approach for cancer diagnosis and therapy (Harris et al., 2019). The interaction between the membrane molecules of cancer cells and the receptors of endothelial cells is crucial for the attachment of cancer cells to brain endothelial cells and subsequent trans-BBB migration. This cell-cell interaction is multivalent and involves multiple substances, which contributes to the superior BBB penetration efficacy of brain metastatic cancer cells compared to most developed brain-targeted systems. He et al. designed a biomimetic nanoplatform, MPP/SCB, which is a succinobucol-loaded pH-sensitive PEG-PDPA NP disguised with a 4 T1 cancer cell membrane (He et al., 2021b). The nanoplatform aims to enhance the preferential targeting of cerebral ischemic lesions to mitigate I/R injury. In tMCAO rat models, MPP/SCB demonstrated a 4.79-fold higher delivery to the ischemic hemisphere compared to the normal hemisphere. It significantly improved microvascular reperfusion, reduced infarct volume by 69.9 %, and exhibited remarkable neuroprotective effects.

Hybrid cell membrane NMs for IS

Hybrid cell membranes, formed by fusing specific functional membranes from more than two different types of cells, integrates the innate properties of these cell membranes. The resulting hybrid cell-membrane-coated NPs show greater multifunctional synergy and multitasking ability than those coated with single-type membranes (Chen et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2024). Hybrid cell membrane NMs represent a cutting-edge approach in the therapeutic management of IS, integrating the unique properties of various cell membranes to enhance drug delivery, targeting, and efficacy.

These nanoplatforms, such as PM-MG-CPIL4 (Li et al., 2021c), YC-1@[RBC-PL] NVs (Liu et al., 2023), PP@PCL (Tang et al., 2024), and HBSsS NCs (Wu et al., 2024b), exploit the hybrid cell membrane NMs with PLMs which possess thrombus targeting property, for delivering various agents. As shown in Fig. 6a, PM-MG-CPIL4 (Li et al., 2021c) was constructed by fusing PLM vesicles with M0 microglia (MG) to form PM-decorated MG (PM-MG), and then conjugating ultrasound-responsive IL-4-loaded liposomes to PM-MG via a click reaction. This formulation allows for the ultrasound-triggered release of IL-4, which binds to IL-4 receptors on microglia, initiating an M2 polarization of microglia, thereby promoting angiogenesis and nerve regeneration in the injured brain after IS. Besides, HBSsS NCs (Wu et al., 2024b) cloaked with platelet-microglia hybrid membranes have been engineered for targeted delivery of siSPHK-1, offering a potential gene therapeutic approach to post-stroke inflammation. Additionally, YC-1@[RBC-PL] NVs (Liu et al., 2023) are composed of PLM modified RBC encapsulating the HIF-1α inhibitor YC-1. The PP@PCL synergistically alleviate I/R injury by preventing neuroinflammation, scavenging ROSs, reprogramming microglia phenotypes, and promoting angiogenesis, leading to significant neuroprotection and functional recovery in IS models.

Conclusions and perspectives

To enhance the therapeutic efficacy of thrombolytic drugs and neuroprotective agents, researchers have developed CM-NMs tailored to the specific microenvironments of thrombi and ischemic brain tissues. This also allows for the selection of tailored CM-NMs to intervene in specific pathological stages, such as the acute thrombus clearance phase, reperfusion injury phase, and neural repair phase. These innovative systems offer significant advantages in IS therapy, including low immunogenicity and prolonged blood circulation time, and integrate multiple synergistic mechanisms, including natural thrombi-specific targeting (e.g. platelet membrane NMs (Xu et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022a; Quan et al., 2022; Kong et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024)), enhanced BBB penetration (e.g. immune cell membrane NMs (Dong et al., 2019; Feng et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024) and stem cell membrane NMs (Ma et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2020; Shi et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2023b; Dong et al., 2024)), and microenvironment-responsive drug release (e.g. response to enzymes (Xu et al., 2019) and pH (Duan et al., 2022)), thereby achieving spatiotemporal precision in IS therapy and minimized off-target toxicity to healthy brain tissue.

Despite these advances, all CM-NMs for IS are still in the preclinical research stage and their therapeutic mechanisms are still not totally understood. For example, the specific way CM-NMs interact with the body's immune system and the long-term effects of their integration with brain tissue remain unclear. Obviously, engineering and technological barriers still exist in the development of CM-NMs. First and foremost is large-scale production of cell membrane materials while ensuring the homogeneity and reproducibility of their components and functions. To effectively tackle these issues, it is crucial to boost the advancement of cell culture and cell-membrane extraction techniques. Enhanced cell culture technologies can cut production costs and supply sufficient and stable-quality raw materials for extraction. Optimized extraction techniques can ensure high yield of cell membrane materials and reproducibility of cell extraction, while maintaining the functional integrity of surface proteins, which are indispensable for mimicking natural cellular interactions. Secondly, existing research indicates that relying only on the inherent biological functions of cell membranes is insufficient to meet the complex demands of IS pathophysiology. Therefore, the design of multifunctional CM-NMs is particularly important and requires further research and development. Additionally, future CM-NMs should integrate real-time imaging capabilities for advanced theranostics, in order to achieve simultaneous, non-invasive visualization and therapeutic action. Finally, to achieve clinical application, a more comprehensive exploration and assessment of the efficacy, safety, and mechanism of action of drugs or treatment is necessary.

In short, from the lab results, CM-NMs offer a highly attractive strategy to tackle the tough problem of IS treatment. But to fully unlock its revolutionary potential, a lot of resources need to be invested in deeply understanding the basic mechanisms, breaking through key engineering and technological barriers, and carrying out the strictest and most comprehensive pre-clinical and clinical evaluations to bridge the “Death Valley” from the lab to the bedside. Looking forward, CM-NMs hold significant potential to advance precision stroke therapy and personalized nanomedicine. By integrating with existing thrombolytic protocols, incorporating real-time imaging tracers, and leveraging AI-driven design for targeting optimization, CM-NMs could enable tailored therapeutic strategies adapted to individual patient profiles and disease stages.
