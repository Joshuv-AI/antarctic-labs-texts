Direct coupling of nod factor signaling to vesicular trafficking initiates legume nodulation
Authors: Jae hyo Song, Mengran Yang, Gabriel Lemes Jorge, Daewon Kim, Shin-ichiro Agake, Jeremiah Traeger, Dehong Hu, Jay J. Thelen, Gary Stacey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Legume–rhizobium symbiosis requires coordinated receptor signaling and membrane trafficking to initiate infection thread formation in root hairs. Here, we identify the soybean Qa-SNARE GmSYNTAXIN111a (GmSYP111a), a close paralog of the cytokinesis-associated protein KNOLLE, as a critical regulator of symbiotic infection. Kinase-client assays and in vivo immunoprecipitation–mass spectrometry showed that GmSYP111a is phosphorylated by the receptor kinase GmSymRKβ at Ser-8 and Ser-128. BiFC, co-immunoprecipitation, and kinase assays validated the interaction and phosphorylation. Nod factor perception promoted clathrin-mediated endocytosis of the GmSymRKβ–GmSYP111a complex and its relocalization to intracellular vesicles. Structural modeling and interaction assays suggest that dual phosphorylation exposes an endocytic motif that recruits the TPLATE adaptor; accordingly, non-phosphorylatable mutations impaired internalization, whereas phosphomimetic substitutions induced endocytosis without rhizobial stimulation. GmSYP111a also interacted with VAMP72, linking endocytic recruitment to vesicle fusion. Genetic analyses in soybean and Lotus japonicus established a conserved requirement for GmSYP111a in nodule initiation. These findings define a phosphorylation-dependent SNARE switch that couples Nod factor signaling to membrane trafficking during legume nodulation.

Introduction

Endosymbiotic interactions, such as the legume–rhizobium symbiosis, demand extensive, and highly coordinated remodeling of the host cell membrane system. During rhizobial infection of roots, bacteria are guided inward through ITs—tubular invaginations of the root hair cell wall and plasma membrane1,2. These structures, and the nascent symbiotic compartments within nodules, require the host to generate new membrane to accommodate the microbes. In fact, infected nodule cells expand their membrane surface area several-fold (up to 20-fold) as they form the symbiosome membrane that envelopes nitrogen-fixing bacteroids3,4. Polar growth processes like IT extension rely on vesicle trafficking: secretory vesicles deliver membrane and cell wall materials to the growing tip, while endocytic pathways likely recycle and reshape the plasma membrane to allow controlled invagination and bacterial containment2,5. Despite the central importance of membrane dynamics in symbiotic infection, how these processes are coordinated with symbiotic signal transduction remains poorly understood.

Plants perceive rhizobial NF signals at the root hair surface through receptor-like kinases (RLKs). In legumes, specialized LysM-domain RLKs (e.g., termed NFR1/NFR5 in Lotus and soybean) bind NFs, and the leucine-rich repeat RLK SymRK acts as a co-receptor to initiate the common symbiosis signaling pathway6. SymRK is essential for both rhizobial and mycorrhizal symbioses7. Mutations in SymRK abolish nodule formation, highlighting its central role in nodulation8,9. However, while decades of research have elucidated many downstream genetic requirements of SymRK-mediated signaling, the direct mechanistic connections between these symbiotic receptors and the membrane trafficking machinery remain poorly understood. Notably, several plant RLKs undergo ligand-induced endocytosis as part of their signaling regulation (e.g., BRI1, which continues to signal from endosomes after internalization10). SymRK is also relocalized into endosomal puncta upon NF stimulation7, suggesting that receptor internalization may modulate signaling. Nevertheless, it is still unclear whether SymRK—or any plant RLK—actively regulates vesicle trafficking events, as opposed to functioning primarily as cargo of endocytosis.

In the context of plant plasma membrane remodeling and internalization, the TPLATE complex (TPC) serves as a critical regulator of CME11. This complex is indispensable for membrane invagination and vesicle formation, particularly in overcoming the high turgor pressure characteristic of plant cells12. As an evolutionarily ancient adapter complex, the TPC represents a plant-unique component absent in yeast and animal systems11–13. The complex consists of six conserved subunits—including TASH3, LOLITA, TWD40-1, and TWD40-2—alongside two plant-specific subunits, AtEH1/Pan1 and AtEH2/Pan114.

Membrane fusion during vesicle trafficking is executed by SNARE proteins, which are key determinants of specificity in exocytosis and endocytosis. Many SNAREs fulfill fundamental roles in plant growth; for instance, the Qa-SNARE KNOLLE (AtSYP111) is a cytokinesis-specific syntaxin required for cell plate formation during Arabidopsis embryogenesis15,16. Emerging evidence suggests that certain SNARE components are also deployed in symbiotic infection. In Medicago truncatula, two homologous v-SNAREs (VAMP721d and VAMP721e) are specifically required for IT progression and for the formation of symbiosomes (the membrane-bound organelle-like compartments housing rhizobia)17. Silencing these exocytotic SNAREs blocks the release of bacteria into host cells without affecting overall nodule organogenesis17. These observations underscore that a dedicated vesicle trafficking pathway underpins bacterial accommodation in legumes. However, it remains unclear how the symbiotic signaling pathway orchestrated by RLKs might interface with this vesicle trafficking machinery.

To address this gap, we employed the KiC assay, an in vitro synthetic peptide phosphorylation platform coupled with LC–MS/MS that enables the identification of direct protein substrates for purified kinases18,19. This assay quantifies kinase activity by monitoring spectral counts of phosphorylated versus unphosphorylated peptides, offering high sensitivity and specificity with a favorable signal-to-noise ratio. To broaden its utility, an 8 k synthetic peptide library was generated, each peptide designed as a 20-mer and enriched for phosphorylation sites conserved across angiosperms, thereby capturing functionally important residues and facilitating kinase substrate discovery across diverse plant species20.

In this study, we discovered a direct molecular coupling between NF signaling and the host membrane-trafficking apparatus. We identified GmSYP111a, a soybean Qa-SNARE, as a legume-specific homolog of KNOLLE that is induced during rhizobial infection. GmSYP111a localizes to the plasma membrane of infected root hairs and appears to have evolved a specialized role in nodulation, as it is specifically induced in root hairs upon rhizobacterial infection, with its upregulation temporally coinciding with IT formation. This raised the hypothesis that GmSYP111a might be directly regulated by symbiotic signaling. Indeed, we found that the cytoplasmic kinase domain of GmSymRKβ physically interacts with GmSYP111a and phosphorylates it at conserved serines (Ser-8 and Ser-128) near the SNARE’s N-terminus. This phosphorylation acts as a switch that enables membrane trafficking. In the absence of NF, GmSYP111a resides at the root hair plasma membrane, but upon GmSymRKβ-mediated phosphorylation at Ser-8 and Ser-128, GmSYP111a is endocytosed along with the GmSymRKβ receptor complex. In essence, activation of the GmSymRKβ receptor triggers the internalization of itself together with GmSYP111a, thereby physically coupling NF perception to an endocytic remodeling of the plasma membrane.

Our data demonstrate that GmSymRKβ directly phosphorylates GmSYP111a, thereby linking rhizobial signal perception to vesicle dynamics required for IT development. This finding provides a basis for the coordination between signal transduction and membrane trafficking, defining an essential, very early mechanistic step in the rhizobial infection process.

Results

Identification of candidate substrates phosphorylated by GmSymRKβ and functional enrichment analysis

To determine the substrate specificity of the GmSymRKβ receptor kinase, we performed a high-throughput KiC assay using the purified intracellular kinase domain of GmSymRKβ. In this in vitro screening, both wild-type (WT) and catalytically inactive mutant forms of GmSymRKβ were incubated with ATP and a synthetic peptide library of approximately 8000 peptides20, followed by mass spectrometry (MS) to detect phosphorylated peptides (Fig. 1a). We assessed overall peptide coverage of the library by MS and confirmed that more than 90% of peptides were reliably detected, demonstrating the robustness of the assay (Fig. 1b). Based on this comprehensive coverage, we then focused on the phosphorylated subset. The resulting peptide spectra were subjected to three sequential filtering steps to ensure high-confidence identifications: Filtration 1, Ascore ≥ 20 and minimum PTM ion intensity ≥ 5%; Filtration 2, Filtration 1 + peptide-spectrum match (PSM) counts ≥ 2; Filtration 3, Filtration 2 + manual spectrum inspection. GO term analysis of the 69 identified proteins revealed significant enrichment of categories related to membrane trafficking and vesicle dynamics, including SNARE complex, exocytosis, endocytosis, and extrinsic component of membrane (Supplementary Data 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). This indicates that the candidate set is closely associated with membrane remodeling and vesicle transport regulation. Additionally, terms such as kinase inhibitor activity and positive regulation of catalytic activity were significantly enriched, supporting the role of phosphorylation-based regulation of enzymatic activity as a key mechanism. Notably, the candidate set also included remorin, a protein previously characterized as an interactor of GmSymRKβ in symbiotic signaling21. Its recovery as a phosphorylated substrate in the KiC assay provides independent confirmation of the assay’s robustness and supports the biological relevance of the identified phosphorylation targets.

Through systematic analysis, one particularly notable target was the soybean Qa-SNARE protein GmSYP111. GmSYP111a consists of 318 amino acids and exhibits a characteristic domain architecture: an N-terminal region containing Ha, Hb, and Hc domains linked to a C-terminal SNARE motif (Qa) and a transmembrane domain (TMD) (Fig. 1c). The phosphorylated peptide identified in a GmSymRKβ-dependent manner was found to align with a homologous N-terminal sequence from the Arabidopsis library, and the phosphorylated Ser-8 residue corresponded to a conserved position in soybean SYP111 (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Data 1). These observations support that GmSymRKβ directly phosphorylates this conserved residue of GmSYP111, as demonstrated by in vitro kinase assays, consistent with the design of the 8 k KiC library enriched for conserved phosphorylation sites across angiosperms20. This finding highlighted the need to further investigate the regulatory role of GmSYP111 in symbiotic signaling and its contribution to IT formation.

GmSYP111a is a key regulator of soybean nodulation

To dissect the function of SYP111 in soybean, which encodes two paralogs (GmSYP111a and GmSYP111b), we employed Agrobacterium rhizogenes–mediated hairy root transformation, using the RUBY selection marker system to identify transgenic roots. We generated composite plants to test individual RNA interference (RNAi) constructs alongside an overexpression (OX) line (GmSYP111a-OX) and an empty vector (EV) control. Following inoculation with Bradyrhizobium diazoefficiens (USDA110), knockdown of GmSYP111a caused a significant reduction in nodule number relative to EV (Fig. 2a, b), whereas SYP111b-RNAi roots did not differ significantly from controls, despite efficient transcript suppression confirmed by RT–qPCR (Fig. 2c). To evaluate whether SYP111 function is conserved within the Leguminosae, we performed a comprehensive phenotypic and molecular characterization of Lotus japonicus syp111 mutants. Phenotypic assessment of 14-day old plants inoculated with Mesorhizobium loti (MAFF303099) revealed that the Ljsyp111 mutant (LORE1 insertion 30098745) exhibited a profound attenuation in nodule organogenesis compared to the WT Miyakojima (MG-20), a defect concordant with the symbiotic signaling deficiencies characteristic of Ljsymrk mutants (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Quantitative analysis substantiated a significant reduction in the number of nodules per plant (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Notably, unlike the embryo lethal phenotypes and cytokinesis defects documented in Arabidopsis knolle mutants16, LjSYP111 deficiency did not impair vegetative development, as root length remained statistically indistinguishable from the WT (Supplementary Fig. 2c).

The Lotus japonicus genome was previously annotated with two fragmented SYP111 gene models (Lj0g3v0109479 and Lj0g3v0109489). However, we successfully amplified a single continuous full-length cDNA, demonstrating that these two models constitute one transcript encoding an intact Qa-SNARE. The LORE1 insertion used in this study resides within the region previously designated as Lj0g3v0109479 (Supplementary Fig. 3a). RT-PCR confirmed the selective loss of this full-length SYP111 transcript in the Ljsyp111 mutant (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The lack of severe developmental defects likely stems from a truncated transcript initiating at an internal ATG, which produces a C-terminal SYP111 variant containing the SNARE domain but lacking the N-terminal regulatory region and Ser-8 phosphorylation site (Supplementary Fig. 3c). These findings suggest that while the C-terminal SNARE domain of LjSYP111 may suffice for basic vegetative growth, the N-terminal regulatory domain is specifically required for symbiotic signaling, confirming that SYP111 is a conserved and essential regulator of root nodule symbiosis in legumes.

To place the functional conservation of SYP111 in an evolutionary context, we performed multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis of SYP111/KNOLLE homologs from representative legume and non-legume plant species. The phylogenetic tree resolved GmSYP111a and GmSYP111b within a legume-associated SYP111 clade together with LjSYP111, MtSYP111, PvSYP111, and VuSYP111, separated from SYP111 homologs of non-legume species (Supplementary Fig. 4). Multiple sequence alignment revealed strong conservation of the core Qa-SNARE motif and TMD across all examined proteins, whereas the N-terminal regulatory region, including the YXXΦ motif and Ha/Hb helices, showed a legume-associated conservation pattern distinct from non-legume homologs (Supplementary Fig. 4). These data support that legume SYP111 proteins retain the conserved SNARE architecture required for membrane fusion while displaying lineage-associated divergence in the N-terminal regulatory region, consistent with acquisition of symbiosis-related regulatory capacity.

To further define the role of GmSYP111a during rhizobial infection, we quantified infection events—defined here as the combined occurrence of ITs and infection pockets—at 5 days post-inoculation (dpi). GmSYP111a-RNAi roots displayed a substantial reduction in infection event numbers compared to EV, a phenotype closely resembling that of GmSymRKβ-RNAi roots, while GmSYP111a-OX roots showed no significant increase (Fig. 2d, e). These results suggest that GmSYP111a contributes to the initiation and/or growth of ITs. The similarity between GmSYP111a-RNAi and GmSymRKβ-RNAi phenotypes recalls observations in Medicago truncatula dmi2 (i.e., SymRK homolog) mutants, where root hair curling occurs, but bacteria are not entrapped and ITs fail to form22. Collectively, the data are consistent with a model in which GmSYP111a functions in concert with GmSymRKβ at an early stage of infection, facilitating bacterial capture within root hair pockets and promoting IT initiation.

GmSYP111a expression and plasma membrane localization in soybean root hairs

To elucidate the contribution of GmSYP111a to the establishment of symbiosis, we first examined its transcriptional behavior across developmental contexts and tissue types. Mining publicly available soybean transcriptome datasets from the BAR database (https://bar.utoronto.ca/) revealed that both GmSYP111a and GmSYP111b are abundantly expressed in roots and root hairs. Importantly, however, only GmSYP111a transcripts exhibited pronounced induction in root hairs following rhizobial inoculation, underscoring its potential specialization in the earliest phases of infection (Supplementary Fig. 5).

To substantiate these in silico observations, we engineered a GmSYP111a promoter:CFP transcriptional reporter and introduced it into soybean hairy roots. Confocal microscopy confirmed strong reporter activity in root hairs (Fig. 3a). RT–qPCR assays revealed that both GmSYP111a and GmSYP111b were upregulated in whole-root samples upon inoculation; nevertheless, only GmSYP111a showed significant and consistent induction within purified root hair fractions (Fig. 3b). We next examined the spatial distribution of GmSYP111a in relation to GmSymRKβ. Transgenic soybean hairy roots expressing YFP–GmSYP111a and GmSymRKβ-GFP under the CvMV promoter revealed strong localization of both proteins at the cell periphery, consistent with plasma membrane association (Fig. 3c). To verify this localization, plasmolysis assays were performed on epidermal cells subjected to 1 M sucrose, which caused the fluorescent signals of both fusion proteins to retract from the cell wall, thereby confirming their identity as plasma membrane residents (Fig. 3d). Upon rhizobial inoculation, the distribution of both proteins shifted markedly: in addition to their peripheral signals, numerous punctate intracellular foci appeared, indicative of endocytic internalization (Fig. 3c). Cross-sectional analyses confirmed that these puncta were positioned inside the plasma membrane, validating their identity as endocytic vesicles engaged in symbiotic membrane remodeling (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that GmSYP111a is specifically induced in root hairs by rhizobial signals and encodes a protein that resides at the plasma membrane. This combination of infection-responsive transcriptional activation and dynamic membrane association identifies GmSYP111a as a key regulatory component responding to early symbiotic signaling.

GmSymRKβ interacts with and phosphorylates GmSYP111a at Ser-8 and Ser-128

To dissect the molecular relationship between GmSYP111a and GmSymRKβ, we combined imaging-based interaction assays, biochemical co-precipitation, and kinase activity measurements. In BiFC assays, EYFPN’–GmSYP111a and GmSymRKβ–EYFPC’ were transiently co-expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana epidermal cells. Robust peripheral YFP fluorescence was consistently observed, confirming a physical association at the plasma membrane (Fig. 4a). Control combinations lacking one partner yielded no signal, excluding artifactual reconstitution. To substantiate these findings, we performed Co-IP assays in soybean hairy roots. FLAG–GmSymRKβ was reliably co-precipitated with HA–GmSYP111aWT, whereas unrelated HA-tagged controls did not yield signal. Notably, the extent of co-precipitation was unaffected by the presence or absence of rhizobial infection (Fig. 4b). Both GmSYP111aS8A and GmSYP111aS8D were also able to co-precipitate GmSymRKβ (Fig. 4c), mirroring the BiFC observations in heterologous leaf tissue and reinforcing that basal physical interaction is preserved across mutant variants.

Extending the analysis to soybean root hairs at 5 dpi, both GmSYP111aWT and GmSYP111aS8D exhibited BiFC signals at IT membrane sites as well as at the root hair plasma membrane, whereas we failed to detect IT membrane signals in GmSYP111aS8A (Fig. 4d).

In addition to GmSymRKβ, we found that both NF receptors GmNFR1a and GmNFR5a also formed BiFC signals with GmSYP111a when co-expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). These interactions were consistently localized to the plasma membrane and were detectable across WT, S8A, and S8D variants, indicating that GmSYP111a can associate not only with SymRKβ but also with the canonical NF receptor pair. This additional receptor–syntaxin connectivity suggests that GmSYP111a may participate more broadly in NF–elicited membrane signaling complexes prior to the engagement of SymRKβ-mediated phosphorylation cascades.

Although the relatively low spectral counts for this specific phosphopeptide preclude formal statistical modeling of fold change, its qualitative enrichment in the presence of symbionts suggests a signaling-responsive modification. Similarly, the frequency of Ser-128 phosphorylation showed a proportional increase from 1.8 percent (1/56) in mock samples to 8.3 percent (4/48) upon inoculation (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Data 3). To verify that GmSymRKβ directly targets these residues identified in vivo, we conducted in vitro kinase assays with purified recombinant proteins. The cytoplasmic domain of GmSymRKβ (residues 261–616; GmSymRKβ-CD) was utilized as the kinase, and His-tagged GmSYP111a variants served as substrates. Incubation with radioactive ATP resulted in robust phosphorylation of GmSYP111aWT, whereas the catalytically inactive D734N kinase-dead variant displayed no activity, demonstrating the catalytic specificity of the reaction (Fig. 5c). The authenticity of this modification was further confirmed via lambda-phosphatase treatment, which completely abolished the radiolabel (Fig. 5d). Consistent with this, phosphorylation was significantly reduced in both the S8A and S128A mutants, indicating that both residues are targeted by GmSymRKβ in vitro (Fig. 5e, f). The stronger reduction in S8A identifies Ser-8 as the major site, whereas the S8AS128A double mutant was not significantly different from S8A (Fig. 5e, f). These biochemical and in vivo results establish GmSYP111a as a direct GmSymRKβ substrate. The observation that phosphorylation does not disrupt complex formation suggests that GmSymRKβ-mediated regulation of GmSYP111a occurs not through the modulation of physical association, but rather via downstream functional changes, such as conformational transitions or altered signaling efficiency.

To further investigate the molecular components involved in GmSYP111a internalization and vesicle trafficking, we performed in vivo IP-MS to identify its interacting proteins. The IP-MS analysis identified several candidates associated with the endocytic and secretory pathways, including members of the Clathrin, Dynamin, SYP71 family, TPC, and VAMP72 family (Supplementary Data 4). Discussion

Evolutionary divergence of SYP111 paralogs in legumes

Soybean harbors two closely related SYP111 paralogs, GmSYP111a and GmSYP111b, which have diverged in function. GmSYP111a is strongly induced during rhizobial colonization and is essential for efficient IT formation and nodulation, whereas GmSYP111b is constitutively expressed and likely retains the ancestral role in cytokinesis. RNAi-mediated depletion of GmSYP111a drastically reduces ITs and nodules without affecting root cell division, while complete loss of both GmSYP111a and GmSYP111b would likely be embryo-lethal due to failed cytokinesis as observed in Arabidopsis knolle mutants16. Thus, we could not recover GmSYP111a/b double -RNAi hairy roots because depleting the cytokinetic paralog likely prevents the development of transformed roots.

No obvious cytokinesis defects were observed in GmSYP111a-deficient soybean roots, presumably due to compensatory functional redundancy. GmSYP111b (and possibly GmSYP132) can substitute at the division plane. In Arabidopsis, SYP132 was shown to partly substitute for KNOLLE, and loss of both KNOLLE and SYP132 abolishes cell plate formation23. However, because our functional analysis relied on RNAi-mediated knockdown rather than complete loss-of-function alleles, we cannot fully exclude a minor, redundant, or context-dependent contribution of GmSYP111b to symbiotic infection. The evolutionary repurposing of GmSYP111a for symbiosis appears to involve infection-responsive transcriptional upregulation and novel post-translational control, notably GmSymRKβ-dependent phosphorylation at Ser-8 and Ser-128. This interpretation is supported by phylogenetic and sequence-conservation analyses, which place GmSYP111a within a legume-associated SYP111 clade and reveal lineage-associated conservation of the N-terminal regulatory region containing the YXXΦ motif and Ha/Hb helices. In contrast, the core Qa-SNARE motif and TMD remain broadly conserved across legume and non-legume SYP111 homologs (Supplementary Fig. 4). This pattern suggests that legume SYP111 proteins preserve the ancestral membrane-fusion module while acquiring N-terminal regulatory features that may have facilitated their recruitment into symbiotic trafficking pathways. Conservation analysis in Lotus japonicus further supports this interpretation: a LORE1 insertion in LjSYP111 leads to a pronounced reduction in nodulation but no cytokinetic defects (Supplementary Fig. 2). This phenotype is consistent with the residual expression of a truncated C-terminal transcript—which encodes the SNARE domain but lacks the N-terminal regulatory region and the Ser-8 phosphorylation site—rather than confirmed full-length protein products (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). These findings illustrate how legume genomes leveraged gene duplication to co-opt a conserved cytokinetic SNARE for symbiotic innovation, allowing a KNOLLE-type syntaxin to orchestrate IT morphogenesis and nodule development through its specialized N-terminal domain.

IT formation and its parallels with plant cytokinesis

The functional role of GmSYP111a in IT morphogenesis highlights a deep mechanistic convergence with plant cytokinesis. GmSYP111a is a close homolog of the Arabidopsis cytokinesis-specific Qa-SNARE KNOLLE, which mediates vesicle fusion at the cell plate through complexes with SNAP33 and VAMP721/722 to drive membrane addition during cytokinesis15,16,24. These findings suggest that the IT tip represents a specialized polarized membrane domain akin to the cell plate. In both contexts, exocytosis supplies new membrane and cell wall polysaccharides, while CME retrieves excess membrane and fine-tunes receptor activity. Inhibition of endocytic components disrupts cell plate maturation13, and in the symbiotic context, perturbation of GmSYP111a phosphorylation compromises IT formation, mirroring these cytokinetic defects.

Our biochemical and imaging analyses established that VAMP72 family members (Glyma.07G223000, Glyma.08G355600) physically associate with GmSYP111a, with high-resolution visualization demonstrating that these complexes predominantly partition into circular membrane structures characteristic of intracellular vesicles or spherical organelles in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves (Fig. 7b). The TyrA23-dependent reduction of cytosolic BiFC puncta suggests that endocytosis is required for the GmSYP111a–VAMP72 complex to access intracellular vesicle compartments where this SNARE module becomes functionally engaged (Supplementary Fig. 13). This interaction likely constitutes a specialized symbiotic SNARE complex, serving a role functionally analogous to the KNOLLE–SNAP33–VAMP721/722 modules that orchestrate membrane expansion during cytokinesis24. Furthermore, GmSymRKβ undergoes infection-induced endocytosis in a manner dependent on GmSYP111a phosphorylation, suggesting that this phosphorylation-coupled CME attenuates receptor signaling while promoting proper IT progression. Direct evidence confirms that NFR1 undergoes infection-induced endocytosis, and stabilization in membrane nanodomains further supports a conserved role for CME in symbiotic signaling25,26. Together, these data position GmSYP111a as a central coordinator, integrating NF signaling, exocytic delivery, and endocytic recycling to sustain polarized IT formation.

Phospho-SYP111a converts symbiotic receptor clusters into trafficking modules

Our findings suggest that GmSYP111a co-localizes with GmSymRKβ in nanometer-scale clusters at the root hair plasma membrane without infection, which are thought to overlap with nanodomain-based receptor assemblies scaffolded by SYMREM1 and FLOT4. This suggestion is consistent with models positioning NF receptors in nanodomains27–29. Consistent with this view, a RLK (RinRK1) promotes NFR1/NFR5 accumulation at root hair tips through Flotillin association, linking receptor signaling to nanodomain organization in legumes30. This arrangement implies that legumes strategically pre-position symbiotic receptors in specialized membrane regions to ensure rapid and spatially confined responses to rhizobial signals.

A central issue raised by these results is how phosphorylation at Ser-8 and Ser-128 of GmSYP111a by GmSymRKβ alters the dynamics of the receptor–SNARE complex. Phosphorylation of plant Qa-SNAREs by kinases can indeed influence their assembly with partners; for example, in Arabidopsis guard cells, the RLCK LKS4/SGN1 phosphorylates SYP121, enhancing its pairing with VAMP72231. Moreover, in legumes, clathrin heavy chain participates in NF signaling and IT formation, supporting a mechanistic link between receptor activation and CME26. Structural modeling via AlphaFold 3 suggests that this dual phosphorylation induces a conformational shift characterized by the localized unfolding of the N-terminal Ha helix (Supplementary Fig. 10). This remodeling increases N-terminal flexibility and likely exposes the YXXΦ endocytic motif, which is otherwise buried or structurally restricted in the WT and single-site mutants. IP-MS provided qualitative evidence that GmSYP111a is phosphorylated at Ser-8 and Ser-128 in soybean roots, whereas BiFC assays support a functional role for these residues in phosphorylation-dependent recruitment of the TPLATE core adaptor. However, because the spectral counts for these phosphopeptides were low, the in vivo phosphorylation data should be interpreted qualitatively and will require independent validation. The detection of Ser-128 phosphorylation in mock-treated samples, together with the basal level of punctate BiFC signals observed for WT GmSYP111a under mock conditions, suggests that a limited degree of basal phosphorylation and/or adaptor recruitment may occur even in the absence of rhizobial inoculation. Consistent with this interpretation, punctate BiFC signals were largely abolished in the S8A mutant. In contrast, the scaffold subunit TWD40-2 appears to associate with the SNARE in a basal manner. Consequently, we propose that GmSymRKβ-dependent phosphorylation does not abolish the GmSymRKβ–GmSYP111a interaction but likely induces a conformational shift that exposes the YXXΦ endocytic motif, enabling the phosphorylation-dependent recruitment of the TPLATE adaptor complex and reprogramming signaling assemblies into trafficking modules.

Receptor kinase–SNARE regulation

Plant receptor kinases classically initiate cytosolic kinase cascades (e.g., MAPKs) and transcriptional programs, and direct control of vesicle trafficking components is considered uncommon. An instructive analogy comes from brassinosteroid signaling, where the BR receptor BRI1 phosphorylates its inhibitor BKI1, driving BKI1’s dissociation from the plasma membrane and enabling downstream signaling32. Nonetheless, emerging evidence indicates that receptor-proximal kinases can directly modulate SNARE machinery. For example, in Arabidopsis guard cells, the receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase LKS4 phosphorylates the Qa-SNARE SYP121 (PEN1) to promote SNARE-complex assembly31.

Against this backdrop, our data support a model in which the symbiotic LRR-RLK GmSymRKβ directly phosphorylates the SNARE GmSYP111a at Ser-8 and Ser-128 (Fig. 5). Crucially, receptor internalization in this context proceeds via CME—as demonstrated in legumes by the requirement for an AP2-type YXXΦ endocytic motif in SYMRK and the sensitivity of SYMRK endocytosis to CME inhibitors (tyrphostin A23 and ikarugamycin)7. As infection advances, we propose that internalized SymRK–SYP111a complexes are recycled through the TGN/EE system and subsequently redirected to the IT membrane. Consistent with this framework, our data show that GmSYP111a associates with the TGN/EE-associated R-SNARE VAMP72 and that this vesicular BiFC signal is strongly reduced by TyrA23 treatment, indicating that formation of the GmSYP111a–VAMP72 vesicular complex depends on CME activity (Supplementary Fig. 13). These findings support a trafficking-associated model in which internalized SymRK–SYP111a complexes may enter TGN/EE-associated vesicular routes involving VAMP72. However, direct evidence that these complexes undergo polarized recycling to the IT membrane is currently lacking, and this aspect of the model remains to be tested. This proposed route is consistent with the behavior of the NF receptor kinase LYK3, which exhibits infection-dependent vesicular accumulation and later redistribution along ITs in Medicago truncatula25. Notably, receptor stabilization within SYMREM1/FLOT4 nanodomains preserves signaling competence by preventing premature CME and degradation27, whereas subsequent endocytic–exocytic trafficking could provide a mechanism to spatially reposition receptor-associated modules during IT development5,17.

Although syntaxins such as SYP111a are classically defined as exocytic Qa-SNAREs, growing evidence indicates that they can also participate in endocytic remodeling. In Arabidopsis, the plasma-membrane SNARE SYP132 regulates the CME of the H⁺-ATPase AHA1, and altering SYP132 abundance changes the efficiency of AHA1 internalization33. This finding highlights the ability of certain syntaxins to function at the boundary between fusion and fission, influencing membrane curvature and retrieval. By analogy, phosphorylated GmSYP111a may tether SymRK-enriched plasma-membrane regions and promote curvature-driven invagination through the recruitment of the TPLATE adaptor complex and the same SNARE machinery that normally mediates vesicle fusion. In this capacity, SYP111a likely acts as a hybrid remodeling SNARE that coordinates local exocytic supply with endocytic uptake, generating a self-sustaining cycle of membrane turnover that supports SymRK internalization and compartmentalized signaling.

In this conceptual framework, the SymRK–SYP111a complex likely functions analogously to NFR1/LYK3, undergoing dynamic transitions between nanodomain confinement, CME-mediated internalization, and polarized recycling. Such coupling of receptor phosphorylation, endocytic routing, and vesicle redeployment would integrate NF perception with localized membrane remodeling, ensuring continuous signaling and sustained IT elongation during symbiotic invasion.

(1) Phosphorylation-driven endocytic switch (Fig. 8a): Upon NF perception, SymRKβ directly phosphorylates SYP111a at Ser-8 and Ser-128. These phosphorylation events trigger a critical structural transition; AlphaFold 3 modeling indicates that the N-terminal Ha helix partially unfolds into an intrinsically disordered state (Supplementary Fig. 10). This conformational shift unmasks a previously buried YXXΦ endocytic motif, which directly recruits the TPLATE core adaptor of the clathrin machinery. Consequently, the active receptor complex is redirected from stationary nanodomains into clathrin-coated pits, initiating CME.

(2) Early endocytic response to rhizobia (Fig. 8b): At the root hair tip, early rhizobial signaling triggers the assembly of the phosphorylated SymRK–SYP111a–TPC module. This stage represents a critical preparatory phase for the formal establishment of the infection pocket, initiating the selective CME of receptor-enriched membrane domains to facilitate localized membrane remodeling.

(3) Infection pocket establishment (Fig. 8c): At the root hair tip, the phosphorylated SymRK–SYP111a module coordinates tightly coupled endocytic and exocytic flows. While the TPLATE-dependent CME facilitates the selective uptake and invagination of the receptor-enriched membrane, SYP111a simultaneously maintains basal, phosphorylation-independent interactions with the R-SNARE VAMP72. Working alongside putative Qbc-SNARE partners, this pre-assembled SNARE complex drives the targeted exocytosis of membrane materials and cell wall precursors. This synchronized integration of cytoskeleton-coupled CME and localized secretion physically constructs the infection pocket, a continuous membrane invagination necessary for initial bacterial entrapment.

(4) IT elongation and maintenance of symbiotic signaling (Fig. 8d): As the IT elongates, the SymRK–SYP111a complex sustains a spatially segregated, self-renewing trafficking circuit. Analogous to the role of the Arabidopsis KNOLLE syntaxin in orchestrating targeted vesicle fusion for cell plate formation during cytokinesis, SYP111a cooperates with VAMP72-type SNAREs at the growing IT tip to drive the polarized exocytosis and membrane expansion essential for symbiotic infection24. Conversely, at the trailing IT membrane, CME components (including CHC1, the TPC, and dynamin-related proteins) mediate continuous rear-end membrane retrieva11,13. The internalized SymRK–SYP111a cargo is then routed through the TGN/EE and recycled back to the growing tip. This robust endocytic-exocytic loop ensures the constant replenishment of active receptor modules, maintaining internal NF perception and sustaining downstream signaling cascades (such as Ca²⁺ spiking) required for uninterrupted IT progression5,34.

In summary, the phosphorylation of GmSYP111a at Ser-8 and Ser-128 by GmSymRKβ establishes a direct molecular link between NF receptor kinase signaling and the trafficking machinery of the plasma membrane. This coupling provides a mechanistic explanation for how legumes synchronize NF perception with dynamic cycles of endocytosis and exocytosis, thereby enabling the spatial precision and temporal continuity required for IT initiation and elongation.

Methods

Plant materials, growth conditions, and bacterial strains

Soybean (Glycine max cv. Williams 82) seeds were surface-sterilized and germinated on sterilized germination paper under controlled growth conditions (16 h light/8 h dark, 26 °C/23 °C light/dark, 80% relative humidity). For nodulation assays, seedlings were inoculated with USDA110 cultured in HM medium35. Bacterial cells were harvested at mid-log phase, washed, and resuspended to an OD600 of 0.1 in sterile water before inoculation. For Lotus japonicus assays, Miyakojima MG-20 WT plants and LORE1 insertion mutant lines were surface-sterilized, germinated on 0.8% agar plates, and transferred to growth pouches under controlled environmental conditions (16 h light/8 h dark, 23 °C/20 °C Day/night). Seedlings were inoculated with Mesorhizobium loti (MAFF303099) grown in YEM medium at 28 °C to an OD600 of 0.05, washed twice with sterile water, and resuspended for root inoculation. Plants were maintained in nitrogen-free B&D medium36, and nodulation phenotypes were assessed at 3 weeks post-inoculation.

Cloning and plasmid construction

The cytoplasmic kinase domain of GmSymRKβ (GmSymRKβ-CD) was cloned into pGEX-5X-1 and expressed as a GST fusion protein, and a kinase-dead mutant (D734N) was generated by site-directed mutagenesis as described previously37. The N-terminal cytosolic region of GmSYP111a (aa 1–280, lacking the TMD) was cloned into pET28a(+) via EcoRI/XhoI to yield His–GmSYP111a (1–280), and a phospho-deficient mutants (S8A, S128A, and S8AS128A) were similarly generated. These recombinant proteins were used for in vitro kinase and KiC assays.

For promoter activity analysis, a 2-kb fragment upstream of the GmSYP111a start codon was inserted into psoyGUS38 (KpnI/PstI) together with ECFP using Gibson Assembly, generating pCAMGFP_ProSYP111a–ECFP.

For OX and RNAi constructs, the full-length GmSYP111a coding sequence and gene-specific fragments for GmSYP111a (180 bp, 5′ UTR) and GmSYP111b (130 bp, 3′ UTR) were cloned into pDONR/Zeo and transferred by LR Clonase II recombination into pCAM-RUBY-OX39 and pCAM-RUBY-CvMV-GWi39, respectively.

For subcellular localization, a modified destination vector, pGTQL–3 × HA–EYFP, was generated from pGTQ1211YN by inserting PCR-amplified 3 × HA and EYFP fragments at the XhoI/PacI sites via Gibson assembly. The coding sequences for GmSYP111a variants (WT, S8A, S8AS128A, S8D, and S8DS128D) were first cloned into pDONR/Zeo and subsequently recombined into pGTQ–3 × HA–EYFP using LR Clonase II to yield the YFP–GmSYP111a variants expression constructs. These constructs were used for confocal imaging and, in the case of HA–EYFP–GmSYP111aWT, for immunoprecipitation followed by LC–MS/MS analysis of in vivo phosphorylation. GmSymRKβ was fused with mGFP5 in pCAMBIA1302 (NcoI/BstEII) by Gibson assembly to generate pCAM1302–SymRKβ–GFP.

For BiFC and Co-IP assays in Nicotiana benthamiana, a modified Gateway-compatible vector (pGTQL1211-2), harboring an N-terminal 3 × HA–YN173 fusion40 by Gibson assembly. Similarly, pGTQL1221-2 was constructed by modifying the N-terminal FLAG-YC155 fusion vector (pGTQL1221YC)40. The coding sequences of GmSYP111a (WT, S8A, S8AS128A, S8D, and S8DS128D), GmSymRKβ, TWD40-2 (Glyma.03G226400), VAMP72 (Glyma.07G22300 and Glyma.08G355600), and TPLATE (Glyma.16G060500) were amplified and cloned into pDONR/Zeo. Depending on the specific experimental requirements, these sequences were subsequently recombined into the pGTQL1211-2, pGTQL1221-2, or pGTQL1221 destination vectors to produce the corresponding BiFC constructs.

For Co-IP and super-resolution imaging, a dual-tagged construct was designed to express FLAG–GmSymRKβ and 3 × HA–GmSYP111a under the CvMV promoter specifically for expression in soybean. GmSymRKβ and GmSYP111a were first cloned into pGWB11 and pGWB15, respectively, via LR Clonase II, and the tagged fragments were amplified and sequentially assembled with the CvMV promoter into pSoyGUS (PacI/PstI) using Gibson Assembly, yielding pCAMGFP–GmSymRKβ–FLAG–3 × HA–GmSYP111a. This construct was used for both Co-IP and high-resolution imaging assays. Primer sequences used for cloning are listed in Supplementary Data 5.

Soybean hairy root transformation and phenotypic assays

Agrobacterium rhizogenes strain K599 carrying the above constructs was used for hairy root transformation41. Transgenic roots were identified by GFP fluorescence or Ruby color. Plants were inoculated with USDA110, and nodules were scored 3 weeks post-inoculation. Quantification of nodule number per root across the different transgenic lines was performed with n = 10–12. Infection events (infection pockets and ITs) were quantified at 5 dpi. At least 10 plants per replicate were analyzed.

Gene expression analysis and GUS staining

Total RNA was extracted from soybean root tissues using TRIzol reagent (Sigma, #T9424). cDNA was synthesized with M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega, #M531A). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR master mix (ABI, #4309155) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system. Expression levels were normalized to the soybean Cons6 reference gene42. Histochemical staining for GUS activity was performed following the established protocol described in ref. 43.

Confocal microscopy

Fluorescence signals in soybean root hairs, roots, and Nicotiana benthamiana leaves were imaged using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope. For plasmolysis analysis, soybean roots were treated with 1 M sucrose for 2 h before imaging. To visualize the cell boundary, roots were stained with 10 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI, Sigma, #P4170) for 1 min immediately prior to confocal microscopy. Infection-dependent localization and internalization were monitored at 24 hpi or 5 dpi. Images were processed using Fiji software. For inhibition of endocytosis, hairy roots expressing fluorescently tagged GmSYP111a and GmSymRKβ were pretreated with 50 µM cycloheximide (MP Biomedicals, #194527) for 1 h, followed by treatment with 100 µM tyrphostin A23 (Thermo Fisher, #J60308) at the time of inoculation. Endocytic internalization was quantified by counting the number of fluorescent puncta across five adjacent cells.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC)

GmSYP111a and GmSymRKβ BiFC constructs were co-infiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. YFP fluorescence was visualized at 48 hpi with confocal microscopy. Negative controls included EVs and split-YFP constructs without one partner. For TyrA23 treatment, 100 μM TyrA23 was infiltrated into leaves after 48 h of BiFC construct expression. For quantification, background-corrected mean BiFC intensity was measured from whole confocal images using Fiji/ImageJ under identical acquisition settings. Background fluorescence was subtracted using the rolling-ball algorithm.

Co-IP and western blotting

Protein extracts were prepared from soybean hairy roots or Nicotiana benthamiana leaves expressing various FLAG- and HA-tagged protein combinations. The harvested tissue was ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen and re-suspended in immunoprecipitation buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton ×-100, and protease inhibitor (Thermo Scientific, PIA32955)) at a 1:2 (w/v) ratio. The lysates were incubated on a rotating wheel for 30 min at 4 °C to ensure efficient solubilization, followed by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris.

The resulting supernatants were subjected to immunoprecipitation using anti-HA magnetic beads (MedChemExpress, #HY-K0201). For protein detection, immunoblotting was performed using HRP-conjugated anti-HA (Roche, #12013819001; 1:3000 dilution) and anti-FLAG (Sigma-Aldrich, #A8592; 1:3000 dilution) antibodies. The resulting signals were visualized by ECL chemiluminescence using an Invitrogen iBright 750 imaging system.

Purification of GST- or His-tagged proteins using affinity chromatography

Rosetta™ (DE3) competent cells (Novagen) transformed with the recombinant plasmid were induced with 0.1 mM isopropyl-β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for the expression of GST- or His-tagged proteins. Following incubation for 4 h at 22 °C, cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min. After removing the supernatant, the cell pellet was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and thawed on ice. GST-tagged SymRKβ proteins (GST-SymRKβ-CD) were purified using a lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM PMSF, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, 0.25 mg/L lysozyme, 1% Triton ×-100, and protease inhibitor. His-tagged Syntaxin proteins were extracted using the same buffer, except that it contained 300 mM NaCl and without EDTA. The resuspended cell lysate was sonicated twice for 4 min each at 4 °C with an amplitude of 5 and a duty cycle of 50%, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 30 min. The resulting supernatant was collected, and GST- or His-tagged proteins were purified using Glutathione resin (GenScript, #L00206) or TALON metal affinity resin (Takara, #635502) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Remove various chemical contaminants (e.g., glutathione) from the eluted protein using a VivaSpin concentrator with a 30 kDa molecular weight cut-off and adjust the final protein concentration to approximately 1 μg/μL.

In vitro kinase assay

An in vitro kinase assay was conducted with slight modifications from previously described protocols (14, 33). For the assay, 2 μg of purified GST or GST-tagged cytosolic domain of SymRKβ containing kinase domain (GST-SymRKβ-CD) were incubated with 2 μg of His-tagged Syntaxin protein as a substrate in total reaction volume of 25 μL. The reaction buffer contained 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 10 mM MgCl₂, 100 mM NaCl, and 2 mM ATP, supplemented with 10 μCi radioactive [γ-³²P] ATP. The mixture was incubated at 30 °C for 1 h. Reactions containing [γ-³²P] ATP were terminated by adding 5 μL of 5 × SDS sample buffer and heating at 100 °C for 5 min using a thermomixer (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). Proteins were resolved by electrophoresis on 12% SDS–PAGE gels. Phosphorylated proteins were visualized by autoradiography using a Typhoon FLA 9000 phosphorimager (GE Healthcare) with an exposure time of 12 h. Gels containing radioactive [γ-³²P] ATP samples were subsequently stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) to confirm equal protein loading. GST was used as a negative control, and MBP was used as a universal substrate.

KiC Assay and IP-MS/MS analysis

Details on library generation and complete screening procedures were described previously20. Briefly, the 8 k synthetic peptide library was divided into eight pools of 1000 peptides, resuspended in DMSO (~ 50 mM), and diluted to ~5 mM. LC–MS/MS pre-scans were performed to confirm peptide detectability. Kinase assays were conducted by incubating purified recombinant GST-tagged GmSymRKβ kinase domain (WT or kinase-dead) with peptide pools at a 1:1 enzyme-to-substrate ratio (14.72 μg each) in kinase buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgCl₂, 5 mM EGTA, 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT] supplemented with 2 mM ATP. Reactions (40 μL) were incubated at 30 °C for 1.5 h with shaking and quenched with 1% formic acid in 99% acetonitrile, followed by storage at −20 °C until analysis. Peptides were separated using an EvoSep UHPLC system equipped with a PepSep C18 column (15 cm × 75 μm, 1.9 μm particle size) using a 31-min gradient at a flow rate of 100 nL/min and analyzed on a Bruker timsTOF Pro 2 in PASEF mode (m/z 100–1700). Data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode with one MS and 10 PASEF MS/MS frames per cycle (1.17 s cycle time). MS/MS spectra were searched using PEAKS Studio 10.0 against a custom FASTA database (8000 entries), with precursor and fragment mass tolerances set to 20 ppm and 0.1 Da, respectively. Phosphorylation (Ser/Thr/Tyr) and methionine oxidation were specified as variable modifications. PSMs were filtered at a 1% false discovery rate. Phosphorylation site localization confidence was assessed using Ascore, retaining sites with Ascore ≥20, PTM ion intensity ≥5%, ≥2 PSMs, and manually validated spectra.

For in vivo phosphorylation analysis, soybean hairy roots transiently expressing HA–EYFP–GmSYP111a under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter were generated by Agrobacterium rhizogenes-mediated transformation as described above. Mock- and rhizobium-inoculated hairy roots were harvested 5 days after inoculation, with three biological replicates collected for each condition. Total proteins were extracted and immunoprecipitated using anti-HA magnetic beads as described in the “Co-IP and Western Blotting” section. The immunoprecipitated HA-bead complexes were then resuspended in 10 μL of urea buffer (6 M urea, 2 M thiourea, 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate). Reduction was performed at 30 °C for 30 min using 10 mM DTT, followed by alkylation with 40 mM iodoacetamide in the same buffer for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Proteins were subjected to on-bead digestion using sequencing grade modified trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI) at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:50 and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. To ensure complete digestion, trypsin was added a second time, followed by an additional 6 h incubation, resulting in a final trypsin-to-protein ratio of 1:25. Following digestion, peptides were stored at −20 °C for 2 h. Beads were then filtered using an Ultrafree® Centrifugal Filter (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA), and one-fifth of the sample was loaded onto EvoTips. Samples were analyzed using the same instrument settings described above, except that peptides were separated using the “30 samples per day” predefined gradient (44 min total runtime) at a constant flow rate of 250 nL/min on a PepSep analytical column (Bruker Daltonics; 15 cm × 150 μm, packed with 1.5 μm C18 particles). Database searches were performed using parameters as described above, with the addition of carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification, and ubiquitination and lysine acetylation as variable modifications. The soybean protein database (Gmax_880_Wm82.a6.v1; 80,375 entries) was used as the reference. PTM site localization confidence was assessed using a minimum Ascore of 20.

Super-resolution imaging

Soybean root hairs expressing FLAG-tagged GmSymRKβ and Ha-tagged GmSYP111a were used for super-resolution imaging. Root hairs were first treated with 0.2% driselase in 2 mM MES (pH 5.7) at 37 °C for 15 min to partially digest the cell wall, followed by plasma membrane permeabilization with 1% Triton ×-100 in PBS at room temperature for 20 min. Samples were then incubated in blocking buffer (1% BSA, 0.1% Tween-20, 2 mM sodium azide) before antibody staining. Anti-FLAG- Alexa Fluor® 488 (1:50, Cell Signaling Technology, #15008) and anti-Ha- Alexa Fluor® 555 (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, #55420) antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C to detect GmSymRKβ and GmSYP111a, respectively, and samples were washed thoroughly with PBS prior to imaging. For microscopy, root hairs were mounted in imaging buffer (50 mM Tris, 10 mM NaCl, 10% glucose, 56 µg/mL glucose oxidase, 34 µg/mL catalase, 1% 2-mercaptoethanol, 1% mercaptoethylamine) on glass-bottom dishes under a coverslip. Super-resolution images were acquired using an Olympus IX-71 equipped with a 100× oil immersion objective, a 542 or 488 nm solid-state laser (5 mW/cm²) with appropriate emission filters, and an Andor iXon Ultra 897 EMCCD camera. Image series (512 × 512 pixels, 160 nm per pixel, 8190 frames at 10 fps) were collected and processed with custom MATLAB scripts to identify and cluster blinking events within 20 nm localization precision.
