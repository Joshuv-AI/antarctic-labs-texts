Altered structural network in temporal lobe epilepsy with focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures
Authors: Yi Ge, Cong Chen, Hong Li, Ruyi Wang, Yuyu Yang, Lingqi Ye, Chenmin He, Ruotong Chen, Zijian Wang, Xiaotong Shao, Yuting Gong, Linglin Yang, Shan Wang, Jiping Zhou, Xunyi Wu, Shuang Wang, Yao Ding
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to investigate whether alterations in white matter topological networks are associated with focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (FBTCS) in temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE). Additionally, we investigated the variables contributing to memory impairment in TLE.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study included 88 unilateral people with TLE (45 left/43 right), and 42 healthy controls. Graph theory analysis was employed to compare the FBTCS (+) group (n = 51) with the FBTCS (−) group (n = 37). The FBTCS (+) group was subcategorized into current‐FBTCS (n = 31) and remote‐FBTCS (n = 20), based on the history of FBTCS within 1 year or longer than 1 year before scanning, respectively. We evaluated the discriminatory power of topological network properties by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Generalized linear models (GLMs) were employed to investigate variables associated with memory impairment in TLE.ResultsGlobal efficiency (Eg) was significantly reduced in the FBTCS (+) group, especially in the current‐FBTCS subgroup. Greater disruption of regional properties in the ipsilateral occipital and temporal association cortices was observed in the FBTCS (+) group. ROC analysis revealed that Eg, normalized characteristic shortest path length, and nodal efficiency of the ipsilateral middle temporal gyrus could distinguish between FBTCS (+) and FBTCS (−) groups. Additionally, GLMs linked the occurrence of current FBTCS with poorer verbal memory outcomes in TLE.InterpretationOur study suggests that abnormal networks could be the structural basis of seizure propagation in FBTCS. Strategies aimed at reducing the occurrence of FBTCS could potentially improve the memory outcomes in people with TLE.

Introduction

Focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (FBTCS) originating from the temporal lobes rapidly propagate to various regions of brain 1 and are typically recognized as the most severe epileptic seizures. 2 Approximately half of individuals with temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) have experienced at least one episode of FBTCS throughout their lifetime. 3 FBTCS poses a substantial risk of unpredictable seizure‐related injuries, sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP), 4 and unfavorable surgical outcomes, 5 particularly in cases of unmanageable FBTCS. Furthermore, substantial cognitive impairment, particularly in the form of memory deficits, can significantly impact the quality of life for people with TLE. It is well established that individuals with FBTCS tend to have poorer memory compared to those without FBTCS.6, 7 However, the imaging correlates of this difference have been less extensively studied.

The mechanism underlying the generalization of FBTCS in TLE is not clearly elucidated. To bridge this gap, quantitative MRI techniques have been increasingly used in recent years to assess various aspects of FBTCS, such as changes in gray matter volume and shape of subcortical structures, 8 modifications in the white matter (WM) pathway, 9 identification of distinct activation patterns in task‐based functional MRI (fMRI). 10 Surveys conducted by He and colleagues 11 indicate dysfunction in a basal ganglia‐thalamus circuit, assessed using resting‐state fMRI, exclusively observed in people with TLE within 1 year of experiencing FBTCS. In recent years, increasing evidence has led to the recognition of TLE as a network disorder, and graph theory has emerged as a valuable tool for quantifying the topological properties of the brain. Whereas previous approaches primarily concentrated on localized anomalies, graph theory can unveil patterns of information segregation and integration throughout the entire brain, providing a macroscopic perspective on network‐level changes associated with FBTCS in TLE. To date, a few studies have reported utilizing graph theory analysis of the functional or the metabolic network associated with FBTCS in TLE.12, 13 In a recent study, it was reported that rich club regions were specifically disrupted in TLE with FBTCS, whereas the non‐rich club regions were primarily affected in TLE without FBTCS within the structural network. 14

In this study, we hypothesized that disruption of the WM network underlying FBTCS in TLE manifests as anomalies at both global and regional levels in the structural network. To test this, we (1) characterized small‐world organization and network efficiency within the WM network; (2) quantified changes in nodal efficiency (Ne) and degree centrality (Dc) within the WM network; and (3) explored clinical variables (including the occurrence of FBTCS) and neuroimage indices of verbal and visual memory impairment in people with TLE.

Methods

Participants

A total of 88 unilateral TLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis (HS) were enrolled from the epilepsy center in our hospital between April 2014 and April 2022. The diagnostic and inclusion criteria have been detailed in our previous publications.8, 15, 16 Diagnosis was conducted by at least two experienced epileptologists based on a thorough medical history, seizure semiology, video‐EEG recordings, and routine epilepsy MRI. A typical FBTCS is characterized by bilateral rigid tonic extremities followed by rhythmic clonic jerks, accompanied by complete loss of consciousness. Patients were categorized into two groups, FBTCS (+) (n = 51), comprising individuals with bilateral tonic–clonic seizures, and FBTCS (−) (n = 37), encompassing those without such seizures. Within the FBTCS (+) group, subjects were further stratified into remote‐FBTCS (n = 20), that is, those who experienced FBTCS more than 1 year before enrollment, and current‐FBTCS (n = 31), including those who experienced FBTCS within 1 year of study enrollment. Additionally, 42 healthy volunteers were recruited as controls for comparative analysis. All participants provided written informed consent and the study was received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, China (certification number: 2022‐0336). Each participant underwent assessment using the Wechsler Memory Scale RC test, including memory quotient (MQ), verbal memory scores (sum of the association and story recall sections), and visual memory scores (sum of pictures recognition, visual reproduction, and visual recognition subsets).

Imaging data acquisition and preprocessing

Participants underwent scanning with a 3.0‐T scanner (Discovery MR750, GE Healthcare) equipped with an eight‐channel head coil. High‐resolution three‐dimensional sagittal T1‐weighted brain volume and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) images were acquired (see Supplementary Materials for detail on Imaging Data Acquisition parameters part).

The PANDA toolbox (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/panda) was employed for image preprocessing and analytical tasks. This included estimating the brain mask (skull removal), cropping raw images, correcting for eddy current effect and head motion, registering DWI images to the b = 0 image by affine transformation, and calculating diffusion tensor metrics.

Network construction

Defining network nodes

The automatic anatomic labeling (AAL) template was utilized to segment the cerebral cortex and subcortical structure into 90 regions, with 45 regions assigned to each hemisphere serving as nodes for WM network analysis (refer to Table S1). Affine transformation was applied to align individual fractional anisotropy (FA) images with corresponding T1‐weighted images. This co‐registration step facilitated subsequent analyses by ensuring spatially corresponded between FA images and T1‐weighted images. Following co‐registration, individual structural images were nonlinearly registered to the ICBM template in standard MNI152 space. The warping transformation obtained from the aforementioned two steps was then inverted and used to warp the AAL template into individual DTI native space (refer to Fig. 1).

Flowchart for the construction of the WM network. (1) Co‐registration of T1‐weighted images to DTI native space. (2) Nonlinear registration of individual T1‐weighted images to an ICBM template in standard MNI152 space through nonlinear spatial normalization. (3) Warping back the AAL90 template onto individual DTI space using an inverse transformation matrix (T−1). (4) Utilization of the FACT algorithm for tracking WM fibers throughout the whole brain. (5) Construction of a symmetric 90 × 90 FA‐weighted WM network. (6) Binarization of the matrix for subsequent analyses.

Diffusion MRI tractography

Diffusion MRI tractography was employed to identify connections in the brain WM, representing the edges of network. Specifically, we conducted deterministic tractography using the Fiber Assignment by Continuous Tracking (FACT) algorithm, seeding from the center of each voxel with FA >0.2 and allowing turning angles within 45°. WM connections were established by identifying any fiber that passed through or terminated in the previously defined brain regions. Based on this fiber linkage, we constructed a 90 × 90 average FA‐weighted WM network matrix for each subject. To facilitate data comparison among patients with HS on different sides, the matrices of patients with right HS (48.9%) were left–right flipped. This flipping process involved exchanging the mirror nodes in the left and right hemispheres. To alleviate the possible potential disparity between the left and right hemispheres, we left–right flipped the WM network matrices in half of the randomly selected healthy controls. 15 Finally, we binarized the network matrices (if FA value >0, the connection is retained) for subsequent analysis.

Network analysis

We employed the GRETNA toolbox v2.0 17 to calculate the global‐ and regional‐level topological properties of the WM network. Global properties comprised measures of small‐world network characteristics and network efficiency. Small‐world network properties included clustering coefficient (Cp), shortest path length (Lp), normalized clustering coefficient (γ), normalized characteristic shortest path length (λ), and network small‐worldness (σ). Network efficiency metrics consisted of global efficiency (Eg) and local efficiency (Eloc). At the regional level, we assessed nodal efficiency (Ne) and degree centrality (Dc). The Ne index quantified the efficiency of information transfer at each node, while Dc represented the number of connections associated with each node. The mathematical calculations related to the matrix indices were adopted from several previous articles,18, 19, 20 as thoroughly reviewed by Rubinov and Sporns 21 (see Supplementary Materials Graph Theoretical Measures part for a complete list of calculations).

Brain structure volume analysis

The volBrain system (http://volbrain.upv.es) was utilized to automatically extract bilateral subcortical structure (thalamus and basal ganglia) as well as hippocampus masks and whole brain masks from T1‐weight images. To account for potential effects of whole brain atrophy, we normalized the volume of each brain structure by the total intracranial volume (TIV), calculating a proportional volume for each structure used in between‐group analysis.

Construction of GLMs of verbal and visual memory outcomes

Generalized linear models (GLMs) were employed to explain variation in the verbal and visual memory outcomes (adjusted for education and age) in people with TLE that can be attributed to variation in the independent variables. Our model incorporated several independent variables, including lateralization of HS, proportional volume of ipsilateral hippocampus (iplHP/TIV), Eg, λ, presence of FBTCS or current FBTCS, focal seizure frequency, epilepsy duration, and Ne of bilateral 90 regions. A bidirectional stepwise process was employed for model construction, with Akaike information criterion (AIC) used for model selection. Additionally, we evaluated the presence of multicollinearity and interaction terms among the independent variables. Lastly, we examined independence of residuals in our model and assessed the systematic and random components of the model (see Supplementary Materials for detailed information on the construction of GLMs of verbal and visual memory and model diagnosis). We further applied this method to construct the GLMs of verbal and visual memory in right TLE (RTLE) and left TLE (LTLE) subgroups.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25. GLMs of verbal memory construction and selection were generated using the R software package (R 4.2.3). The data were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) or medians (percentiles 25%–75%). For clinical data analysis, Student's t‐tests and nonparametric Mann–Whitney U‐tests were used to compare continuous variables, and chi‐squared tests were used to analyze categorical variables. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to identify significantly differences in network properties among the three groups. Subsequently, post hoc tests using two‐sample t‐tests were conducted between two groups. All network comparisons were performed by the GRETNA toolbox while adjusted for age, sex, and matrix density as covariates. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05, with either Bonferroni corrections or controlling false discovery rate (FDR) at q = 0.05. The discriminatory power of the features was evaluated by analysis of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.

Results

Demographic characteristics and proportional volume of hippocampus/subcortical nucleus

The age and gender distributions were similar among the control, FBTCS (−), and FBTCS (+) groups, with no significant differences were found between the FBTCS (−) and FBTCS (+) groups in age at onset, disease duration, lateralization of HS, percentage of refractory epilepsy, focal seizure frequency, education, matrix density, or surgical outcome (Table 1). Further, there were no significant differences in MQ (p = 0.748), verbal memory performance (p = 0.436), or visual memory (p = 0.592) between the FBTCS (−) and FBTCS (+) groups, although both groups exhibited lower average scores than the healthy control group (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in LTLE or RTLE subgroups. The proportional volume of ipsilateral thalamus (iplTHA/TIV) was significantly reduced in FBTCS (+) group (p < 0.001) and FBTCS (−) group (p = 0.041) compared with healthy controls. Additionally, reduced iplTHA/TIV was observed in FBTCS (+) group in comparison of FBTCS (−) group (p = 0.044) (Table 2). The current‐FBTCS subgroup exhibited significantly diminished verbal memory compared to the remote‐FBTCS subgroup (13.39 ± 5.83 vs. 18.50 ± 5.54, p = 0.01), especially in LTLE subgroup (10.75 ± 4.85 vs. 19.11 ± 5.37, p = 0.024) but not in RTLE subgroup (16.20 ± 5.60 vs. 18.00 ± 5.88, p = 0.38) (Table S2). We did not find any significant difference in visual memory analysis between patients with or without FBTCS, neither in FBTCS subgroups (Tables S9 and S10).

Global properties of the WM network

All three groups exhibited a “small‐world” property in their WM networks, with σ values markedly higher than 1. Additionally, both the FBTCS (+) and FBTCS (−) groups had significantly higher γ, λ, and σ values compared to the control group, while the FBTCS (+) group had longer λ (p = 0.001) than the FBTCS (−) group (Fig. 2A–C). Comparisons of efficiency measurements showed no significant differences in Eloc across the three groups (p = 0.84). However, the FBTCS (+) group displayed lower Eg values compared to the FBTCS (−) group (p = 0.005) and the control group (p < 0.001). Similarly, the FBTCS (−) group also had lower Eg values than the control group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2D, Table S3). Within the FBTCS (+) group, the current‐FBTCS subgroup had longer λ (p = 0.034) and lower Eg (p = 0.018) values than the remote‐FBTCS subgroup (Fig. 2F,H, Table S4).

Regional properties of the WM network

Regions with reduced nodal efficiency (Ne) and degree centrality (Dc)

Significant disparities in Ne were observed among the three groups (p < 0.05, FDR corrected). Particularly, Ne values in the ipsilateral lingual gyrus and inferior occipital gyrus were lower in the FBTCS (−) group compared to the control group, both of which are association cortices located in the occipital lobe. Beyond these regions, the FBTCS (+) group exhibited lower Ne than the control group in several nodes mostly in ipsilateral hemisphere. Additionally, the Ne of the ipsilateral middle temporal gyrus was significantly lower in the FBTCS (+) group than in the FBTCS (−) group (p < 0.001) (Table 3).

ROC curve

Given the differences in Eg, λ, and Ne of the ipsilateral middle temporal gyrus between the FBTCS (−) and FBTCS (+) groups, we proceeded to use ROC curves to assess whether these variables could distinguish FBTCS (+) patients from FBTCS (−) patients. Area under the curve (AUC) analysis of these three metrics revealed moderate discriminatory capabilities (EgAUC = 0.601, 95% CI [0.481, 0.722]; λ AUC = 0.680, 95% CI [0.567, 0.793]; NeAUC = 0.729, 95% CI [0.617, 0.841]). However, combining these variables through binary logistic regression increased the AUC to 0.751 (95% CI [0.650, 0.853]), significantly higher than EgAUC (p = 0.021) (Fig. 3A).

Additionally, assessment of whether Eg, λ, or their combination could differentiate between remote‐ and current‐FBTCS subgroups suggested that these indices demonstrated a moderate capacity for discriminating between subgroups. However, the combination of these indices did not yield a higher AUC than either index alone (CombinedAUC = 0.716, 95% CI [0.578, 0.855]; EgAUC = 0.698, 95% CI [0.556, 0.842]; λ AUC = 0.708, 95% CI [0.567, 0.849]) (Fig. 3B).

GLMs of memory outcomes in people with TLE

The model results indicated that a reduced iplHP/TIV (β = 820.94, p = 0.002), left HS (β = −0.37, p = 0.032), and a lower Ne ratio of the contralateral parahippocampal gyrus to the ipsilateral amygdala (β = 4.02, p = 0.0003) were associated with verbal memory impairment in all people with TLE (Fig. 4 and Table S6). Furthermore, the presence of current FBTCS (β = −0.34, p = 0.047) was associated with verbal memory impairment. Importantly, no evidence of multicollinearity (VIF ≈1) or interaction terms among the independent variables was observed in this model (Fig. S1). In the LTLE subgroup analysis, we observed significant associations between impaired verbal memory and current FBTCS (β = −0.51, p = 0.046), as well as Ne of the contralateral parahippocampal gyrus (β = 19.29, p = 0.0004) and Ne of the ipsilateral amygdala (β = −7.90, p = 0.033) (Table S7). Meanwhile, in the RTLE subgroup analysis, impaired verbal memory was linked to diminished ipsilateral hippocampus volume (β = 1154.07, p = 0.0012) and increased Ne of the ipsilateral amygdala (β = −11.06, p = 0.017) (Table S8). No significant findings were observed in any visual memory analysis (Tables S11–S13).

Discussion

In this study, utilizing graph theory‐based analysis, we unveiled significant disruption of both global and regional network properties in people with TLE with FBTCS. Additionally, we identified distinct topological features that could potentially serve as neuroimaging biomarkers to differentiate between people with or without FBTCS. Besides, our findings emphasized the greater extent of topological alterations in the brain within 1 year of FBTCS episode. Finally, our study revealed that occurrence of FBTCS within 1 year is associated with verbal memory impairment.

Disrupted global properties in TLE with and without FBTCS

The “Small‐world” properties (σ >>1) observed in the brain structural networks of both groups (FBTCS (+) and FBTCS (−)), suggest that each retains the ability to segregate and integrate information within their respective networks. People with TLE exhibited elevated γ and λ values, indicating a more lattice‐like (“regularized”) WM network, consistent with findings in a prior investigation. 18 Notably, the FBTCS (+) group demonstrated significantly higher λ values compared to FBTCS (−) patients, suggesting that FBTCS might compromise the capacity for long‐distance information transfer in the structural network of people with TLE.

While some studies have reported a decrease in Eg in structural networks using fiber tractography 18 or morphological measurements 22 in TLE, limited exploration has been done into specific irregularities associated with FBTCS in people with TLE. Our analyses revealed that Eg values are significantly lower in the FBTCS (+) group compared to the FBTCS (−) group. In contrast, a study by Nie et al. investigating functional networks found that people with FBTCS exhibited higher Eg compared to those without FBTCS. 12 Moreover, EEG‐related functional network studies have suggested that increased Eg might represent hyperexcitable pathologic activity correlated with epilepsy.23, 24, 25 Thus, it can be inferred that the altered functional connectivity may promote the spread of epileptic activity across different brain regions, leading to more widespread and generalized seizures. On the other hand, the measure of Eg in the WM network is considered the basis of cognitive processes (i.e., memory performance).19, 26, 27 In our study of TLE people with current FBTCS, compromised Eg may represent the impairment of information transfer in brain, potentially leading to the memory decline.

In another study, 28 people with TLE histologically confirmed as isolated gliosis had slightly longer path lengths compared to controls, while those with HS showed a much greater increase. This may be attributed to variances in the extent of WM damage, as evidenced by a tract‐based spatial statistics (TBSS) study. It revealed more pronounced and widespread WM abnormalities among patients with HS compared to MRI‐negative TLE. 29 These findings suggest that the extent of WM connectome reorganization may reflect interpatient variations in the severity of HS.

Disrupted regional properties in TLE with and without FBTCS

The thalamus has been proposed to play a crucial role in facilitating the propagation of extensive ictal activity across multiple cortical networks in FBTCS.1, 11, 30, 31 In our study, a reduction in the proportional volume of ipsilateral thalamus was observed in the FBTCS (+) group compared to the FBTCS (−) group. It is aligned with our prior studies that demonstrated irregularities in localized gray matter in the ipsilateral thalamus and pallidum, 8 along with atrophy specifically within the WM of the hippocampal‐thalamic pathway. 15 Recent investigations using resting‐state fMRI consistently detected disruption in thalamic circuits in individuals with FBTCS.10, 11 However, alterations in the regional properties of the WM throughout the whole brain in these people have not been thoroughly explored. Therefore, we further conducted regional level analysis to gain a better understanding of how regional properties may differ among TLE people with and without FBTCS.

At the regional level, the FBTCS (+) group exhibited decreased Ne and Dc, with a broader distribution across cortical and subcortical regions compared to control groups. However, in a fMRI‐based network analysis by Nie and colleagues, only a potential decrease in Ne within the contralateral basal ganglia of FBTCS (+) patients was suggested. 12 Findings in network‐based statistical analysis and node abnormality assessment reported by Sinha et al. showed more widespread abnormal nodes in FBTCS (+) group than our study. 32 Several factors may contribute to these discrepancies. In Sinha's study, node abnormality was determined by an abnormality ratio (i.e., the proportion of abnormal connections among total connections) of a node exceeding a threshold obtained by comparison with control subjects. Although Nie et al. 12 employed a graph theory‐based approach using fMRI data, which had a smaller sample size than our cohort, potentially limiting the scope of their findings. Therefore, the characterization of impairment in regional networks may be influenced by both the modality of data collection and the analysis method.

In the present study, we observed that the FBTCS (+) group exhibited the most significant impairment in regions, particularly in the ipsilateral occipital and temporal association cortices, which corroborated the fMRI results reported by He and colleagues. 31 Similar observations were also documented in an intracranial electroencephalography (icEEG) study, which indicated that the propagation phase in TLE involved activation of the posterior‐lateral temporal cortex. 33 Another WM network study observed a wider range of decreased connectivity strength in the FBTCS group, mainly affecting the rich club regions including temporal lobe and stratum. 14 These investigations, in conjunction with our own study, underscore the substantial role of the ipsilateral occipital and temporal association cortices, suggesting a plausible structural foundation for the seizure generalization in TLE.

The topological variances between remote‐ and current‐FBTCS

In our study, we utilized a 1‐year cutoff to categorize FBTCS patients into remote‐ or current‐FBTCS groups. This decision was grounded in substantial evidence from prior studies linking the occurrence of FBTCS with an increased risk of SUDEP, and poorer clinical outcomes, including heightened seizure frequency and severity.10, 11, 34, 35 To our knowledge, this study marks the first application of graph theory to investigate variations in topological network disruption among people with remote and current FBTCS. Our findings support the association of current FBTCS with longer λ and lower Eg values compared to those observed in remote‐FBTCS group. While no nodes remained significantly different between the groups after correcting for FDR, a notably lager portion of cortex and subcortex regions in the current‐FBTCS group tended to exhibit impaired Ne and Dc compared to the remote‐FBTCS group. These observations imply a potential association between current FBTCS and more severe of damage in WM. Consequently, actively managing FBTCS with pharmacological interventions could potentially slow down the progression of structural deterioration in the brain of people with TLE.

Experiencing FBTCS within 1 year is associated with verbal memory impairment in TLE

Previous studies have found several factors associated with verbal memory impairments in unilateral TLE, including lateralization of TLE,36, 37, 38 severity of hippocampal damage,39, 40 and clinical parameters such as seizure frequency and epilepsy duration.41, 42, 43 In this study, while there was no significant difference in verbal memory between FBTCS (+) and FBTCS (−) patients, a significant deterioration in verbal memory was observed in the current‐FBTCS subgroup compared to the remote‐FBTCS subgroup. Furthermore, our GLMs analysis unveiled that FBTCS occurrence within 1 year was associated with verbal memory impairment in people with TLE. Another interesting finding was that the effect of current FBTCS on impaired verbal memory was only observed in LTLE but not in RTLE, which may indicate the different mechanisms underlying the disruption of memory circuits in LTLE and RTLE.

Aligned with prior research, our GLMs confirmed that left TLE and reduced proportional volume of the ipsilateral hippocampus are indicative of poorer verbal memory in TLE. Our study has broadened the scope of investigation by incorporating WM network topological indices compared with other quantitative MRI studies.44, 45, 46 For instance, our model highlighted that lower Ne in the contralateral parahippocampal gyrus, associated with inferior verbal memory performance. The activation of the parahippocampal gyrus showed a positive correlation with recognition memory performance, indicating the potential for network reorganization in TLE. 47 Nevertheless, the impact of the Ne in the ipsilateral amygdala on verbal memory should be approached with caution, given its absence in existing literature. Hence, our results extend current knowledge beyond the effects of lateralization of TLE and the severity of hippocampal damage on verbal memory.

Limitations

Several limitations of our current study should be considered in the interpretation and application of our findings. First, we employed deterministic tractography, which may result in the omission of cross fibers. To address this concern, future research could consider utilizing probabilistic tractography methods. Second, some studies have indicated that patients who showed a poor response to ASMs, as well as those treated with carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine, demonstrated a less integrated functional network.48, 49 For future studies, more detailed information about the medication should be included. Last, our study is of a cross‐sectional nature, and employing a longitudinal approach in future study could potentially offer insights into the progression of WM damage in TLE people with FBTCS.

Conclusions

In this study, we employed diffusion tensor tractography and graph theory analysis to investigate WM damage in TLE people with and without FBTCS. Our findings revealed that FBTCS correlated with prominent global and regional impairment in people with TLE. Furthermore, we clarified the association of current FBTCS and verbal memory impairment in people with TLE. Strategies aimed at reducing the occurrence of FBTCS could potentially improve memory outcomes in people with TLE.
