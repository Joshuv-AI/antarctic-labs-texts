Determinants of severe acute malnutrition relapse among children aged 6–59 months attending outpatient treatment programmes at selected public health facilities in Gode city and district, Somali region, Eastern Ethiopia: a case-control study
Authors: Mustafe Mahamud Abdi, Abdilahi Ibrahim Muse, Girma Tadesse Wedajo, Mohammed Ahmed Tarar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to identify the determinants of severe acute malnutrition (SAM) relapse among children aged 6–59 months.DesignCase-control study.SettingSelected public health facilities in Gode city and district, Somali region, Eastern Ethiopia, from April 1–30, 2024.Participants394 (131 cases and 262 controls) children aged 6–59 who were treated for SAM and discharged.Outcome measures387 (129 cases and 258 controls) participated, resulting in an overall response rate of 98.2%. A logistic regression model with an OR of 95% CI was used to estimate the strength of the association, and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultThe mean age of mothers/caretakers for cases and controls was 30.0 (±6.9) and 29.2 (±6.2) years, respectively, with over half (51.9% cases and 54.3% controls) falling in the 25–34 age range. Significantly associated factors with SAM relapse were food-insecure households (adjusted (AOR)=2.26; 95% CI 1.39 to 3.65), poor hand-washing practices (AOR=3.11; 95% CI 1.90 to 5.08), duration of treatment stay <8 weeks (AOR=2.03; 95% CI 1.25 to 3.30), mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) at discharge <12.5 cm (AOR=2.45; 95% CI 1.37 to 4.39) and not receiving vitamin A supplementation in the 6 months (AOR=3.89; 95% CI 2.35 to 6.44).ConclusionThis study shows the important determinant factors associated with SAM relapse among 6–59-month-old children after discharge from outpatient therapeutic programmes in Godey City and district in Eastern Ethiopia. The significantly associated factors, including household food insecurity, inadequate hand-washing practices, shorter treatment duration, low MUAC at discharge and lack of vitamin A supplementation, indicate the multifaceted nature of this public health problem.Enhancing household food security through community-based programmes, promoting health education on proper hand washing and hygiene practices, extension of outpatient treatment duration to prevent premature discharge, ensuring that MUAC threshold for discharge is properly attained, and integrating postdischarge Vitamin A supplementation into primary healthcare services.Keywords: NUTRITION & DIETETICS, Paediatric gastroenterology, Child

Introduction

Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) is one form of acute malnutrition which refers to weight for height less than −3 SD or weight for height ratio of below 70% or mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) <11.5 cm or presence of nutritional oedema.1 Recovery usually occurs between 4 and 8 weeks from admission after stabilisation of weight and clinical complications. However, relapse is a common problem among children under 5 years who have been treated for SAM.2

SAM relapse can occur due to a variety of factors, often intertwined with the underlying health and environmental conditions. Inadequate nutritional rehabilitation during treatment, incomplete recovery or insufficient follow-up care can lead to a child’s return to a malnourished state. The problem of SAM relapse is a major challenge for the health system in Ethiopia, where more than 400 000 children suffer from SAM every year.3 Globally, about 1 million children die every year from SAM,4 and in Sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia, about 57% of under-five deaths are related to malnutrition, of which three-quarters are related to SAM.5

Prior research on SAM has mostly focused on the causes, immediate effects and treatment strategies; however, little is known about the general health and nutrition of children after discharge. One of the most immediate outcomes that needs to be understood and addressed is a relapse into SAM. The relapse of SAM is one of the challenges in managing children with SAM. A recent systematic review found that up to 37% of children relapse into SAM after being discharged from nutritional programmes. The range was 9% in Bangladesh and 34.6% in the southern Ethiopian region.6 7

To mitigate the risk of relapse among children treated for SAM, it is crucial to identify and address the determinants at every stage of the treatment process. Hence, further research in this area is necessary to develop effective interventions and improve the quality of treatment provided to these children. The findings of this study offer crucial insights for the health sector stakeholders, enabling them to fine-tune their health strategies and interventions by making informed decisions and ensuring the efficient allocation of resources to reduce the rates of malnutrition relapse.

Objective

To identify the determinants of SAM relapse among children aged 6–59 months attending outpatient treatment programmes at selected public health facilities in Gode city and district, Somali region, Eastern Ethiopia, April 2024.

Methodology

Study setting, design and period

An unmatched case-control study was conducted from 1–30 April 2024, in public health facilities located in Gode city and district in Shabelle zone of the Somali region, Eastern Ethiopia. Gode, as the administrative centre of Shabelle Zone, is located 1201 km east of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. Gode City and District have one hospital, four health centres and 22 health posts that give service to 131 928 of the population.8

Study population

The study population was randomly selected children aged 6–59 months with mothers or caregivers who had been discharged as cured from an outpatient treatment programme at selected health facilities in Gode City and district.

Cases

A child was considered a case if he or she had MUAC <11.5 cm and/or the presence of bilateral oedema after being successfully discharged from an outpatient treatment programme within 6 months of discharge.

Controls

A child was considered a control if he or she had MUAC ≥12.5 cm and/or no bilateral oedema after being successfully discharged as cured from the outpatient treatment programme within 6 months of discharge.

Eligibility criteria

Children between the ages of 6 and 59 months, accompanied by their mothers or caregivers, who had been discharged as cured from the outpatient treatment programme at the health facilities in Gode City and District for the last 6 months.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined based on the following assumptions: a two-sided confidence level of 95%, a power of 80%, a ratio of controls to cases of 2:1, and three variables that have shown a significant association with the relapse of SAM in previous studies.

On conducting a comparison of the three determinants associated with the relapse of SAM, it was determined that residence accounted for the largest number. This resulted in a final sample size of n=375 (125 cases; 250 controls). Factoring in a 5% non-response rate, the ultimate sample size for this study was adjusted to 394 (131 cases and 263 controls).

Sampling technique and sampling procedures

We randomly selected four out of five health facilities, all of which offer regular outpatient treatment programmes. Children were selected for the study based on a predetermined sample size, which was allocated proportionately to the four health facilities using data from the previous year regarding discharged children. To ensure fair selection, proportional allocation followed by a simple random sampling method was employed for both cases and controls across each of the four health facilities. Consequently, the study included 137 children (41 cases and 96 controls) from Gode General Hospital, 93 children (33 cases and 60 controls) from Gode Gacalo Health Centre, 88 children (31 cases and 57 controls) from Adawe Health Centre, and 76 children (26 cases and 50 controls) from Elan Health Centre.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, or reporting, due to scarcity of resources and time, but we planned to consult the public in the dissemination of the results by conducting focus group discussions and community forums with parents, caregivers and other stakeholders to gather their insights on the best way to present and disseminate the findings.

Data collection procedures

The data were collected using structured interviewer-administered questionnaires and checklists adopted from the outpatient therapeutic programme (OTP) multi-chart and registration logbook, in addition to anthropometric measurements.9

Four trained diploma nurses for data collection and two BSc graduate supervisors were assigned to complete data extraction forms. Health Extension Workers were deployed to trace discharged children from OTP. We captured variables such as sex, age, date of admission, date of discharge and address from the registration logbook. MUAC and the presence of bilateral oedema were assessed. Sociodemographic, nutritional and clinical information was gathered from mothers or caregivers of the children. Child feeding practices were evaluated using standard indicators for assessing infant and young child feeding practices, including questions related to the initiation of breastfeeding, exclusive breastfeeding and complementary feeding.10

Study variables

Dependent variable

Relapse of SAM status.

Independent variables

Characteristics of mothers/carers and children

Age of mother, maternal educational level, occupation status of mother, family size, under-5 children in the household, age of child and sex of child.

Nutrition and clinical characteristics

Initiation of breastfeeding, colostrum fed, exclusive breastfeeding, treatment stay in weeks, deworming tablet given in the last 6 months, vitamin A supplementation in the past 6 months, vaccination for measles and history of illness (diarrhoea, fever, cough) in the last 2 weeks.

Household food consumption and environmental-related characteristics

Household food security status, distance to water source, main source of drinking water, handwashing practice, toilet with handwashing facility and child faeces disposal.

Operational definitions

Relapse: the criteria for identifying a relapse of SAM involved measuring the MUAC to be <11.5 cm and the presence of bilateral oedema in children who had previously been discharged as cured from the outpatient treatment programme for SAM. Meanwhile, the control group consisted of children who maintained a MUAC of 12.5 cm or greater.4

Handwashing practice: a respondent was considered to have good handwashing practices if they reported washing their hands at three or more of the critical times, such as before eating, before preparing food, after using the toilet and after cleaning a child’s bottom.4

Household food security status: the level of household food insecurity access was assessed using a set of nine standard questions from the household food insecurity access scale (HFIAS). Subsequently, the four food security categories were established in the recommended sequence by food and nutrition technical assistance. Finally, HFIAS category one was designated as food secure, while the remaining categories were classified as food insecure.11

Data quality control

The process of ensuring data quality was comprehensive and meticulous, spanning from data collection and coding to entry, cleaning and analysis. Furthermore, the questionnaire was translated into the local language (Af-Somali), and a pretest involving 5% of mothers and caregivers of children aged 6–59 months was conducted at Ayer-dega Health Centre in Jigjiga City, 2 weeks prior to the actual data collection, to identify and address any potential ambiguities in the questionnaire. Additionally, data completeness was monitored daily by the supervisor, with feedback promptly provided to the data collectors the following morning.

Method of data processing and analysis

After the completion of data collection, the collected data were entered into EpiData V.4.6 and then exported to SPSS V.26 for subsequent analysis. The characteristics of the study subjects were examined using descriptive statistics, which included the use of frequency and percentage for categorical predictors, as well as mean and SD for normally distributed continuous covariates. A binary logistic regression model was initially employed, and independent variables with a p value <0.25 were considered as potential candidates for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression analysis.12 The fitness of the model was then assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, with a p value >0.05 indicating an acceptable fit. Finally, the strength of association was evaluated using a p value <0.05 and adjusted (AOR) with a 95% CI.

Sociodemographic characteristics of study participants

Among the 394 potential participants, 387 (129 cases and 258 controls) were included, resulting in an overall response rate of 98.2% (98.5% for cases and 98.1% for controls). The mean age of mothers for cases and controls was 30.0 (±6.9) and 29.2 (±6.2) years, respectively, with over half (51.9% cases and 54.3% controls) falling in the 25–34 age range (see table 1).

Households and environmental-related characteristics of study participants

The majority of households included in the study exhibited food insecurity, with 74 (57.4%) of cases and 81 (31.4%) of controls. More than half of both cases (83 households, 64.3%) and controls (150 households, 58.1%) used unimproved water sources. Regarding handwashing, 77 (59.7%) of cases and 90 (34.9%) exhibited poor handwashing practices. Moreover, child faeces disposal in the open field was prevalent in both groups, with 75 (58.1%) cases and 141 (54.7%) controls using this method.

Nutrition and clinical-related characteristics of children after discharge from outpatient therapeutic programmes (OTPs)

In the context of child feeding practices, it is noteworthy that almost two-thirds of participants, 79 (61.2%) of cases and 171 (66.3%) of controls, commenced breastfeeding within the first hour after birth. Furthermore, more than two-thirds of participants, 90 (69.8%) of cases and 197 (76.4%) of controls, indicated feeding colostrum to their child. It is also important to highlight that 100 (77.5%) of cases and 190 (73.6%) of controls reported exclusive breastfeeding for a period equal to or exceeding 6 months (see table 2).

Factors associated with SAM relapse

In the bivariable analysis, the sex of the child, the education status of mothers, household’s food security status, household’s water source, hand-washing practice, colostrum feeding, treatment stay in weeks, MUAC at discharge and vitamin A supplement in the past 6 months were identified as candidate variables for multivariable logistic regression analysis at p<0.25.

Household food security (AOR=2.26; 95% CI 1.39 to 3.65), hand-washing practices (AOR=3.11; 95% CI 1.90 to 5.08), duration of treatment stay (AOR=2.03; 95% CI 1.25 to 3.30), MUAC at discharge (AOR=2.45; 95% CI 1.37 to 4.39) and vitamin A supplementation in the past 6 months (AOR=3.89; 95% CI 2.35 to 6.44) were significantly associated at a p value of 0.05 (refer to table 3).

Discussion

In this study, food security status of households, hand-washing practices, duration of treatment stay, MUAC at discharge and vitamin A supplementation in the past 6 months were significantly associated with SAM relapse among children aged 6–59 months.

Children in food-insecure households were found to be twice as likely to experience a relapse of SAM compared with those in food-secure households. This finding is consistent with studies done in India,13 Northwest Ethiopia4 and Kurfa Chelle district, Eastern Ethiopia.14 The situation highlights the necessity for comprehensive strategies that not only address SAM treatment but also its root causes. Children who recuperate from SAM may face a heightened risk of relapse due to the persistent food insecurity in their environment. Therefore, initiatives focused on enhancing food security have the potential to significantly alleviate the burden of malnutrition and enhance the overall health outcomes for children in vulnerable communities.

Hand-washing practice was also found to be significantly associated with SAM relapse, as children whose mothers practised poor hand washing were three times more likely to experience a relapse of SAM compared with children whose mothers practised good hand-washing. This finding is consistent with previous studies in Chad15 and Northwest Ethiopia.4 The WHO emphasises the importance of hand-washing for maintaining optimal health. They recommend hand washing at four critical times: before eating, before preparing food, after using the toilet and after changing a child’s nappy or cleaning up. This practice is crucial for preventing the spread of infection and reducing the risk of malnutrition.16

The duration of a treatment stay at an outpatient treatment programme has a significant impact on the likelihood of relapse of SAM in children. In this study, children with a treatment duration of <8 weeks were twice as likely to relapse into SAM as those with a treatment duration of 8 weeks or more. Therefore, a treatment duration of 8 weeks or longer is associated with lower odds of SAM relapse in children undergoing outpatient treatment. This finding is consistent with studies done in Sudan17 and Northwest Ethiopia.4 This indicates the significance of post-treatment support and monitoring to provide ongoing nutritional support and education to families to maintain the progress achieved in recovering from SAM. By extending support beyond the treatment phase, we can potentially enhance the outcomes for children affected by SAM and mitigate the long-term public health impact of malnutrition.18 According to the information provided, individuals may have been discharged once they achieved a 15% weight gain, but it’s possible that they were not completely recovered. A study conducted in Sudan highlighted the drawbacks of using the 15% target weight gain discharge criteria compared with using MUAC as a measure.17 The study pointed out that using MUAC of at least 125 mm as discharge criteria resulted in children staying in treatment for a longer period and experiencing a higher percentage of weight gain.19

Children discharged with a MUAC <12.5 cm had double the odds of SAM relapse compared with those with MUAC ≥12.5 cm at discharge. This agrees with studies done in Burkina Faso,20 Malawi21 and the Gambella Region, Western Ethiopia.22 The low MUAC level (below 12.5 cm) suggests a delicate nutritional status that may not be fully resolved at the time of discharge, and children may still be susceptible to the factors contributing to their initial malnutrition. To mitigate this risk, it is recommended that OTPs refrain from premature discharge and consider providing supplementary food to these children. Furthermore, incorporating both MUAC and weight-for-height/length z-score into discharge criteria could enhance the comprehensiveness of the recovery assessment.23

Eventually, the likelihood of experiencing a relapse of SAM was 3.89 times greater in children who did not receive vitamin A supplementation in the 6 months prior to the study compared with those who did receive the supplement. This is consistent with studies done in Southern Ethiopia,24 Northeast and Northwest Ethiopia.4 25 Vitamin A plays a crucial role in enhancing the immune system, which is vital for the recovery and prevention of relapse in children with SAM. Studies have shown that children with SAM who received vitamin A supplements had significantly better recovery rates. The absence of these supplements may lead to a weakened immune response, making children more susceptible to infections and illnesses that may increase the risk of relapse. Therefore, vitamin A supplementation is a key component in the treatment and prevention of SAM relapse.26

Conclusion and recommendation

This study shows the important determinant factors associated with SAM relapse among 6–59-month-old children after discharge from OTPs in Godey City and the district of Eastern Ethiopia. The significantly associated factors, including household food insecurity, inadequate hand-washing practices, shorter treatment duration, low MUAC at discharge and lack of vitamin A supplementation, indicate the multifaceted nature of this public health problem.

Enhancing household food security through community-based programmes, promoting health education on proper hand washing and hygiene practices, extending outpatient treatment duration to prevent immature discharge, ensuring the MUAC threshold for discharge is properly attained, and integrating postdischarge vitamin A supplementation into primary healthcare services.
