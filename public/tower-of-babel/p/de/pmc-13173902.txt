3D-printed prothesis combined hinged knee arthroplasty in the treatment of critical-sized osteomyelitis-derived bone defects around the knee joint
Authors: Bingchuan Liu, Zhuo Chen, Zhengguang Wang, Fang Zhou, Caimei Wang, Feng Li, Yun Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTreating peri-knee bone defects caused by osteomyelitis remains a clinical challenge, particularly in accurately repairing critical-sized defects while preserving joint mobility. This study aimed to evaluate the outcomes of an innovative technique combining 3D-printed prostheses with hinged knees to reconstruct bone defects from peri-knee osteomyelitis.MethodsWe built models of critical-sized bone defects in the distal femur and proximal tibia and used finite element analysis (FEA) to describe the stress characteristics of different components. Additionally, we retrospectively enrolled ten patients, with a mean age of 45.6 years. The defects were located in the distal femur (four cases) and proximal tibia (six cases), with a mean defect volume of 123.4 cm³. Prosthesis stability was assessed through continuous X-rays, and new bone regeneration was synthetically evaluated using X-rays and histological staining. Lower Extremity Functional Scale (LEFS) was used to quantitatively assess limb function.ResultsFEA indicated that stress was distributed centrally throughout the models. Components bearing higher stress included intramedullary nails, the lateral flanks of 3D-printed prosthesis, and the grooves of tibial inserts. Among the ten enrolled patients, two experienced osteomyelitis recurrence and underwent transfemoral amputation. The mean follow-up period for the remaining eight patients was 25.9 months. Postoperative X-rays showed that the prostheses remained stable, with new bone gradually growing along the prosthesis surface. Histological staining further confirmed that new bone had grown inside the superficial rough pores of nail. At the last follow-up, these eight patients were able to walk autonomously, with LEFS scores significantly improving compared to pre-surgery (20.1 vs. 8.9, P < 0.001). In terms of surgery-related complications, aside from the mentioned osteomyelitis recurrence, one patient experienced screw breakage and required revision surgery.ConclusionsThe combined application of 3D-printed prostheses and hinged knees is an effective technique to reconstruct peri-knee critical-sized bone defects. Introduction

Knee is one of the most anatomically complex joints, tasked with bearing substantial weight while maintaining significant flexibility. These defects can stem from various causes, including severe fractures [1], tumor resections [2], infections [3], as well as initial and revision arthroplasty procedures [4]. Managing peri-knee bone defects presents a formidable challenge for both healthcare providers and patients. In severe cases, bone defects may coincide with soft tissue and skin deficiencies, further complicating the clinical treatment.

While bone grafting can be effective for small defects accompanying fractures and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [1, 5], its efficacy diminishes for larger, irregular defects, particularly those involving damage to the articular surface due to tumors and infections. On this occasion, arthrodesis or amputation may become the last option [6]. Addressing complicated peri-knee bone defects still demands precise and innovative approaches to repair the defect while preserving joint function.

In recent years, 3D printing technology has emerged as a crucial tool for optimizing the clinical treatment of bone defects [7]. 3D-printed metal prostheses hold significant advantages due to their customizable shapes, porous structures, and sufficient mechanical strength, making them well-suited for efficiently reconstructing various bone defects. Clinical utilization of 3D-printed cones and prostheses in bone tumor resection and revision TKA surgeries has been documented [2, 4]. In addition, our team has successfully employed 3D-printed prostheses to repair defects in the distal femur and proximal tibia [8, 9]. However, to date, there is a dearth of reports on the use of 3D-printed prostheses to repair peri-knee bone defects extending into the articular surface.

Hinged knees are often considered suitable for joint reconstruction in cases with severe bone and ligament damage in TKAs, they have been frequently employed in surgeries involving tumor resections, infections, and revisions [10–12]. Hinged knees can offer improved mobility-stability and are less susceptible to loosening or displacement, even in the presence of bone defects. Combining hinged knees with metal prostheses can further enhance prosthesis stabilization. Studies by Kayani et al. [4] and Tetreault et al. [13] have demonstrated robust reconstructions of femoral and tibial bone defects during revision TKAs using metaphyseal cones. In a case involving Charcot joint, Hua et al. [14] also employed a 3D-printed augment to reconstruct bone defects following tibial plateau osteotomy. However, the combined application of hinged knees and 3D-printed prostheses to reconstruct critical-sized osteomyelitis-derived bone defects around the knee remains underexplored, leaving management strategies and clinical outcomes devoid of meaningful guidance.

This study conducts a retrospective analysis of clinical data from patients with critical-sized peri-knee bone defects treated with hinged knees combined with 3D-printed prostheses. We meticulously examine surgical details, prosthesis design and manufacture, and postoperative outcomes. Additionally, finite element analysis (FEA) is employed to simulate stress distribution. The findings of this study hold significant potential to serve as valuable references for clinical decision-making in related diseases.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This clinical study was approved by the Medical Science Research Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital (No. M2018174), and conducted in accordance with national guidelines. Official informed consents were obtained from all patients and their authorized family members. We have finished the registry for clinical trials in the United States National Library of Medicine (No. NCT03941028).

Patient inclusion criteria included: (1) age over 18 years old; (2) bone defects resulting from osteomyelitis; (3) bone defects repaired through 3D-printed prosthesis implantation; (4) postoperative follow-up lasting at least 1 year. Patients lacking complete clinical and follow-up data were excluded.

Main management process

Prosthesis design

Prosthesis design was conducted based on radiological reviews. Our prosthesis features an outer contour that mimics the anatomical shape of the healthy-side bone, as detailed in our previous report [9], with one key modification: the anterior crest of prosthesis is designed as an arc rather than triangular shape. This adjustment mitigates extrusion forces on the anterior soft tissue and reduces skin tension, thereby facilitating easier direct incision closure. The central part of the prostheses was chiseled to create a cylindrical hole for subsequent nail insertion. A perforated flank structure was designed on one side of the prosthesis for screw fixation to enhance stability. Additionally, the prosthesis body integrates porous structures to foster the ingrowth of new bone and soft tissue. In cases involving tibial tuberosity resection, specialized pores are strategically positioned at the front of the prosthesis to accommodate patellar tendon suturing. The process of prosthesis and hinged knee implantation, and screw fixation was simulated using Unigraphics NX software (Version 12.0, Siemens) (Fig. 1).

Finite element modeling and analysis

Based on FEA, the main purpose is to clarify the stress distribution of the prosthesis, in order to guide the design of the prosthesis and the positioning of the fixation screws.

Modeling of bone, 3D-printed prosthesis, hinged knee and screws

Modeling of bone, 3D-printed prosthesis, hinged knee and screws

The volunteer was a 47-year-old male, and computed tomography (CT) images were obtained with a slice thickness of 0.5 mm. Femoral and tibial CT images in DICOM format were imported into Mimics software (Version 17.0, Materialize, Belgium) for bone segmentation. The CT Hounsfield unit was utilized to differentiate cortical and cancellous bone. AK Hinge Knee (AHK) system (AKEC Medical, China) was selected for TKA. The metal components of the AHK system were made of CoCrMo, while the tibial insert was composed of ultrahigh molecular weight polyethylene. Polymethyl methacrylate cement was used to secure the connection between the AHK system and bone surface. Regarding the 3D-printed prosthesis, its main body was fabricated as porous Ti6Al4V, while the intramedullary nail and lateral flank were constructed as solid Ti6Al4V. Screws were also made of solid Ti6Al4V. The models and material properties of different components were illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1 and in Table 1. Subsequently, surface smoothing and small gap filling were performed, resulting in the acquisition of an STP file.

(2)Meshing

Meshing

The acquired STP file was imported into HyperMesh software (Version 14.0, Altair) for geometric cleaning and meshing (Supplementary Fig. 2). The sensitivity analysis was performed to reduce the influence of meshing differences. All the meshes were tetrahedral with different sizes: bone was 5 mm; prosthesis was 2 mm; screw was 1 mm.

(3)Contacting and stress loading

Contacting and stress loading

The type of surface-to-surface contact between different materials was carefully defined. A sticky connection, preventing any sliding, was established between the contacting surfaces of the prosthesis-hinged knee, prosthesis-bone, and prosthesis-screw. Additionally, a frozen contact, allowing no displacement, was set between the contacting surfaces of the prosthesis-bone cement and bone-bone cement. Stress loading was simulated with the knee extended. Throughout the loading process, the distal tibia was fully constrained, while a loading force of 1764 N was applied along the force line direction. Biomechanical concentration characteristics were assessed based on stress distribution and maximum von Mises stress.

3D-printed prosthesis fabrication

The current prosthesis was constructed using standard Ti6Al4V powders as the primary material, chosen for its favorable biocompatibility and mechanical properties. Fabrication of the prostheses utilized selective laser melting (Arcam EBM, Gothenburg, Sweden), guided by computer-aided design data. Subsequently, all prostheses underwent cleaning in an ultrasonic bath to eliminate loose titanium particles and impurities. Finally, the prepared prostheses were sterilized and packed for surgery.

Main surgical procedures

The prosthesis implantation surgery commenced following the confirmation of complete removal of infected lesions and assurance against recurrence of infection. The average time between infected bone resection and prosthesis implantation was 5.5 months. Before the definitive surgery, we made the detection of blood infection indicators, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, etc., were carried out to ensure that these results were within the normal range. Tissue sampling and cultures were not conducted during the surgery.

The patient was positioned supine, receiving a combination of general anesthesia and nerve block anesthesia. Selection of the surgical incision depended on the knee’s condition, typically favoring a linear incision in front of the knee, or a curved incision along skin flap coverage if present. Upon adequate exposure of the operative area, an intramedullary rod facilitated precise alignment and placement of the prostheses. Excess newly-regenerated bone was meticulously trimmed using a piezosurgery. Subsequently, trial prostheses and hinged knee implants were inserted to evaluate stability, alignment, flexion and extension gaps, joint line restoration, range of movement, and patellar tracking. After assessment, an appropriately sized 3D-printed prosthesis was implanted into the bone defect and fixed with cortical screws. Bone grafting between the cone and host bone was seldom necessary. AHK system were utilized to restore the integrity and mobility of the knee joint (Fig. 2). During and after prosthesis implantation, we applied the corresponding sensitive antibiotics based on the previous microbial culture results. The antibiotic application period after the surgery was 6 weeks. For patients with severe skin defects that prevented the closure of the incision, we used medial gastrocnemius flaps to complete the incision closure.

Postoperative rehabilitation and follow-up

Postoperative weight-bearing and rehabilitation protocols were tailored individually by surgeons and therapists, considering factors such as bone quality and defect scale. Most patients were allowed to bear weight as tolerated, while those with compromised tibial tubercles and ligaments underwent a minimum of twenty days of immobilization. Antero-posterior and lateral X-rays were carefully reviewed. In cases involving revision surgery and removal of the 3D-printed prosthesis, histological staining was employed to evaluate new bone regeneration and osseointegration. Functional assessment before and after surgery was quantified using the Lower Extremity Functional Scale (LEFS). Detailed documentation of all complications, reoperations was recorded.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 22.0 software was used to execute the statistical analysis. Variable data were presented as means ± standard deviations and assessed by ANOVA test. Statistical significance was considered at P-value < 0.05.

Among the ten patients, two experienced osteomyelitis recurrence at 28 and 62 days after prosthesis implantation, respectively. They both opted for transfemoral amputation instead of undergoing debridement and prosthesis replacement surgery, with no recurrence of infection observed during follow-up (22 and 18 months, respectively). For the remaining eight patients, the mean postoperative follow-up period was 25.9 ± 12.2 months. The mean interval between prosthesis implantation and limb weight-bearing was 21.9 ± 2.6 days. The mean range of knee motion was 0 to 95 degrees. According to the LEFS, all eight patients demonstrated significant improvement in functional recovery compared to preoperative scores (20.1 vs. 8.9, P < 0.001), with the ability to walk autonomously without assistance.

Observations from Fig. 5 reveal the gradual development of new bone from the host bone, starting with local bone proliferation and forming a “pedestal-like” structure, which then extends along the 3D-printed prosthesis. Over time, new bone undergoes remodeling, resulting in a smoother shape. Histological staining results (Fig. 6) of a removed 3D-printed prosthesis (Case 8 in Table 1), show some new bone growth into the porous surface of the nail, but an absence of new bone growing at the interface between the prosthesis and host bone.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first investigation focusing on the combined utilization of 3D-printed prostheses and hinged knees in addressing bone defects arising from peri-knee osteomyelitis. Through a comprehensive approach encompassing FEA to clinical implementation, we shared our practical experience about the clinical application of this innovative technique.

FEA has been widely used to study stress distribution and micromotion in various implants [15, 16]. Researchers have utilized FEA to analyze the biomechanical characteristics of metal cones and blocks used in TKAs [17, 18]. In our study, it becomes evident that components such as intramedullary nails, the lateral flanks of 3D-printed prosthesis, and the grooves of tibial inserts are subjected to higher levels of stress. Under vertical compression, stress exhibits a central distribution pattern, with levels remaining below the yield strength of the constituent materials, thereby safeguarding against deformation or breakage. The lateral flanks and screws help to share the load of the 3D-printed prosthesis as well. In previous studies focusing on applying 3D-printed prostheses for repairing femoral and tibial defects, we have conducted FEA reports on the stress distribution [19, 20]. By amalgamating these findings with the outcomes of this study, we have, for the first time, established a comprehensive framework for utilizing 3D-printed prostheses in rectifying bone defects across various femoral and tibial locations. This framework offers valuable insights for prosthesis design and clinical implementation.

Under constant external forces and micromotion, 3D-printed prostheses are susceptible to loosening or displacement, potentially leading to implant failure. Insufficient stability leads to excessive interfacial micromotion, which may result in a higher incidence of implant failure [21]. In our clinical approach, the stabilization process for 3D-printed prostheses is segmented into initial and long-term stages. During the initial stage, stability mainly relies on the friction between the rough surfaces and the mechanical interlocking of different components. Over the long term, stability evolves in tandem with continuous recovery. Newly-regenerated bone and soft tissues can grow inside the prosthesis pores, facilitating mechanical anchoring that reduces prosthesis movement and sustains shear stress, thereby enhancing long-term stability.

Standard management protocols for peri-knee osteomyelitis typically involve antibiotic infusion, irrigation, debridement, and osteotomy. Arthrodesis can be used to reconstruct the knee structure for patients with cured infection [22]. While recent reports suggest success with floating knee arthrodesis in preserving the knee joint [23, 24], simultaneously achieving accurate reconstruction of bone defects and joint function remains challenging. Our innovative technique offers a distinct advantage by simultaneously repairing critical-sized bone defects while preserving knee mobility. This approach significantly enhances patients’ ability to return to normal daily activities compared to knee arthrodesis. Remarkably, in our patient series, the interval between prosthesis implantation and limb weight-bearing was only 21.9 ± 2.6 days. After a mean follow-up of 25.9 months, all eight patients showed significant improvement in limb function, with mean LEFS scores increasing from 8.9 to 20.1 (P < 0.001). They were able to walk autonomously without assistance.

Several clinical studies have proved the gradual growth of new bone along the surface of 3D-printed prostheses [25–27]. Consistent with these findings, Fig. 5 illustrates similar characteristics of new bone regeneration in our study. Overall, the process of new bone regeneration follows the principle of “secondary fracture healing” [28]. Early micromotion between the host bone and the 3D-printed prosthesis triggers local callus hyperplasia, forming a “pedestal-like” structure. Subsequently, new bone gradually extends along the prosthesis surface, undergoing smooth remodeling. This remodeling process is governed by stress conduction, in alignment with Wolff’s law [29]. Histological staining results indicate no apparent new bone formation at the interface region, likely due to the relatively short duration of prosthesis implantation (28 days). However, new bone growth is evident within the medullary cavity, where it integrates with the rough surface structure of the nail.

Regarding to the postoperative complications, one of the most challenging is infection recurrence, particularly concerning periprosthetic joint infection (PJI). As estimated, the overall incidence of PJI following TKA is approximately 1–2% [30]. Notably, for patients undergoing hinged knee arthroplasty to address PJI after primary TKA, the reinfection rate is around 13% [11]. Based on our clinical experience, factors such as high virulence of the pathogenic bacteria, long infection duration, poor soft tissue conditions, presence of diabetes and immune system disorders in patients are all important related factors for the recurrence of postoperative infections. However, due to the relatively small number of included clinical patients, there are currently insufficient specific data to support these factors. Prompt action is essential upon infection confirmation, typically involving prosthesis removal, followed by antibiotic therapy and debridement surgery. Previous reports suggest a transfemoral amputation rate of nearly 8.5% for treating PJI [31]. In our study, two patients experienced osteomyelitis recurrence and chose amputation due to intolerable mental and physical pain associated with repeated debridement. Overall, the innovative surgical method of combining a 3D-printed prosthesis with a hinged knee has a low risk of surgery-related complications.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size of included patients is relatively small, and there exists a lack of follow-up for periods exceeding 5 years, thereby restricting the comprehensive evaluation of the long-term outcomes of our innovative technique. There is no comparison group to non-3D printed prosthesis. Moreover, direct evidence concerning the growth of new bone inside the porous structure remains lacking. Lastly, completely controlling infection before prosthesis implantation and effectively preventing infection recurrence are still difficult problems, which needs to be further optimized in terms of surgical technique and clinical management process.

Conclusions

The combined application of 3D-printed prostheses and hinged knees can be an option to treat peri-knee critical-sized bone defects derived from osteomyelitis. This innovative technique can effectively preserve knee joint function while repairing bone defects, which is more helpful for patients to return to normal life. More patient enrollments and longer continuous follow-up are needed to validate the long-term clinical outcomes of this technique.
