Effect of milrinone-terlipressin infusion on blood loss and portal pressure during hepatic resection: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Vanni Subhash Jhawar, Ashish Singh Aditya, Tanvir Samra, Harjeet Singh, Vikas Saini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow central venous pressure (CVP) and Pringle maneuver are commonly employed strategies to reduce bleeding during hepatic resection. Milrinone and terlipressin (MT) lower hepatic venous pressure and portal venous pressure (PVP), thereby reducing the risk of bleeding. This study compared the intraoperative blood loss between a combined infusion of MT, and terlipressin alone in patients undergoing elective hepatic resection.MethodsA total of 36 patients were randomized to the MT or terlipressin (T) groups, who received infusions starting 30 min before the beginning of liver resection until the end of the surgery. The primary objective was to determine differences in blood loss between the two groups.ResultsThirty-four patients (17 in each group) were assessed by stratification into major and minor hepatic resections. The median (1Q, 3Q) intraoperative blood loss (in ml) was lower in MT group than in T group (570 [329, 880] vs. 824 [750, 1,146]; P = 0.046). In minor resections, MT had lower blood loss than T (373 [273, 556] vs. 798 [725, 1,230]; P = 0.015). During major hepatic resection, the median blood loss in the MT group (785 [570, 920]) was lower than that in the T group (908 [750, 1,120]; P = 0.596). The decreases in PVP and CVP at 30 min post-infusion were significant in the MT group (P = 0.002 and P = 0.027, respectively).ConclusionsThe combination of MT significantly reduces intraoperative blood loss, especially during minor hepatic resections.Keywords: Hepatectomy, Liver resection, Milrinone, Portal pressure, Surgical blood loss, Terlipressin

INTRODUCTION

Hepatic resection is associated with considerable blood loss owing to the rich vascular supply to the liver [1]. Decreasing central venous pressure (CVP) to less than 5 mmHg is the most common intraoperative strategy to reduce blood loss. Surgical methods, including the Pringle maneuver and portal venous occlusion, are routinely used but can cause ischemia of the liver parenchyma with prolonged occlusion [2].

Pharmacological interventions can lower the hepatic venous pressure (HVP) and portal venous pressure (PVP) by reducing venous inflow or facilitating outflow from the liver, which decreases the risk of bleeding. While vasodilators such as nitroglycerin (NTG) are effective in lowering PVP and HVP during liver resection surgery, their use is limited due to systemic vasodilation, which can lead to hypotension and reduced organ perfusion [3-5]. In contrast, milrinone, a vasodilator with positive inotropic and lusitropic effects, maintains cardiac output, prevents acute kidney injury (AKI), and offers hepatoprotection, making it the preferred alternative to NTG [6]. Splanchnic vasoconstrictors, such as vasopressin and terlipressin, reduce portal venous blood flow and pressure. Vasopressin decreases blood loss and PVP in patients with portal hypertension, although its use is limited [4,7]. Terlipressin, being splanchnic-selective, minimizes the risk of intestinal ischemia and AKI, making it preferable to vasopressin [8].

This study compared intraoperative blood loss between combined milrinone and terlipressin (MT) infusion and terlipressin alone in patients undergoing elective hepatic resections. Secondary objectives included comparing the PVP, CVP, surgical bleeding score, portal venous and arterial lactate levels, intraoperative heart rate (HR), and mean arterial pressure (MAP).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective randomized controlled trial was conducted in the surgical gastroenterology operating theatre of a tertiary care teaching and research center. The study was registered at https://ctri.nic.in/ (CTRI/2022/11/047220). A total of 40 participants were enrolled, with recruitment beginning on November 15, 2022, and follow-up on September 15, 2023. This clinical trial was prospectively registered on November 11, 2022, and adherence to the Consolidated Standards for Reporting of Trials (CONSORT) guidelines was ensured. Ethical approval for this study (approval No. INT-IEC/2022/MD-547) was provided by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research, Chandigarh, India on 31 December, 2022.

Patients aged 18–80 years posted for major/minor elective hepatic resection were enrolled in the study. Major resection was defined as the resection of more than three liver segments. Patients with clinical features suggestive of portal hypertension, uncontrolled systemic medical illness, and pre-existing severe hepatic or renal insufficiency (Child-Pugh B/C, glomerular filtration rate < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2) were excluded. After obtaining written informed consent, the participants were randomized equally into the MT and terlipressin (T) groups using computer-generated random numbers with stratification for major/minor hepatic resection. For each patient, a sealed envelope with a random number and method to prepare drug syringes was provided to the anesthetist to administer the infusions. The anesthetist preparing the drugs was not part of the study or involved in patient management. The operating surgeon (HS), attending anesthetist, and patients were blinded to the group allocation.

The patients were monitored in the operating room for HR, non-invasive blood pressure (IBP), continuous electrocardiogram, and arterial oxygen saturation (SpO2) using multichannel monitors (Datex-Ohmeda S/5 Avance, GE Healthcare). An epidural catheter was placed at the lower thoracic level in all patients in both groups, and 0.05 mg/kg of morphine (not exceeding 3 mg) diluted in 6 ml of normal saline was administered. General anesthesia was induced using fentanyl (1–2 mcg/kg), propofol (1–2 mg/kg), and atracurium (0.5 mg/kg). Oxygen, nitrous oxide, and isoflurane (titrated to 1–1.2 minimum alveolar concentration) were used to maintain the anesthesia. Radial arterial cannulation was used to monitor IBP and pulse pressure variation. Central venous cannulation of the right internal jugular vein was performed for CVP monitoring, drug infusion, and intraoperative fluid management.

Once the liver hilum was exposed before the beginning of liver resection, baseline measurements (T0) of the PVP were recorded invasively using a 24-gauge needle inserted into the portal vein or one of its feeders and later connected to a pressure transducer via sterile high-pressure tubing. The transducer was leveled and zeroed at the level of the hepatic hilum. Drug infusions were initiated immediately after the baseline measurement by an attending anesthetist and were not included in the study. To ensure blinding, the drug infusions were prepared by an independent anesthetist. The syringe labeled “Infusion A” contained terlipressin at a concentration of 40 mcg/ml. A loading dose of 1 mg (1,000 mcg) was administered over 30 min, and the maintenance dose was administered at 2 mcg/kg/h. Based on group allocation, the second syringe, labelled as “Infusion B,” contained either milrinone or a placebo (normal saline). Milrinone, diluted to a concentration of 200 mcg/ml, was administered over 10 min with a loading dose of 25 mcg/kg, followed by the maintenance dose at 0.5 mcg/kg/min. Repeated PVP measurements were taken approximately 30 min after starting the drug infusions, before beginning liver resection (T30), and after completing hepatic resection (Te).

Intraoperative maintenance fluid was administered at a rate of 6 ml/kg/h, and any additional fluid was administered at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist. Total blood loss during surgery was calculated by considering the volume collected in the suction drain after subtracting the volume of irrigation saline used, as well as the volume of blood in soaked sponges and gauzes (determined by the weight difference between dry and blood-soaked sponges and gauzes). The surgical team estimated blood loss visually in the surgical field. Significant blood loss, which led to hemodynamic instability and/or a hemoglobin level below 7 g/dl on arterial blood gas analysis, was managed with blood transfusions. Norepinephrine infusion was initiated if the MAP fell below 65 mmHg.

Bleeding during hepatic resection was assessed using the scoring system described by Yu et al. [9], with scores of: 0, no bleeding; 1, minor bleeding, no aspiration required; 2, minor bleeding, aspiration required; 3, minor bleeding, frequent aspiration required; 4, moderate bleeding, visible only with aspiration; and 5, severe bleeding, frequent aspiration required, and very difficult to perform surgery. At the end of the surgery, muscle relaxation was reversed, the patients were extubated, and epidural pain management was continued until 48 h postoperatively. Postoperative AKI (defined according to the Acute Kidney Injury Network criteria) and postoperative surgical complications (calculated by Clavien–Dindo classification) were recorded, and patient outcomes were assessed at 90 days [10,11].

Sample size calculation was based on a previous study that reported a blood loss of 842 ± 145.5 ml in patients who underwent hepatic resection with terlipressin [12]. A sample size of 16 patients per group was determined to detect a 20% reduction in blood loss with combined terlipressin and milrinone infusion, ensuring 90% power and a 95% confidence interval (CI). The sample size was increased to 18 participants per group to account for potential participant dropouts.

Discrete categorical data were compared using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. After testing for normality (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test), continuous data, expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (1Q, 3Q), were compared using t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. For time-related variables with skewed data, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied, while normally distributed data were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA. All statistical tests were two-sided and performed at a significance level of α = 0.05.

To control the family-wise error rate (FWER) for key secondary outcomes that may impact blood loss, Bonferroni correction was applied to the comparisons of PVP and CVP. As these formed a single family of hypotheses (n = 2), the significance threshold was adjusted to α = 0.025 (0.05/2). Other secondary outcomes were considered exploratory, and no correction for multiple comparisons was applied. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (ver. 22.0, IBM Co.).

RESULTS

Forty patients were assessed for eligibility; four patients did not undergo surgical resection because a diagnostic laparoscopy suggested metastasis. Of the 36 patients who were randomized into groups MT and terlipressin, two did not complete the study protocol due to intraoperative refractory shock or portal hypertension. Therefore, 34 patients were included in the final analysis. Fig. 1 shows a flowchart of the study participants.

DISCUSSION

In our study, Group T experienced a median blood loss of 824 ml (750, 1,146), with a reduction in PVP from 9.6 ± 4.6 mmHg to 8.5 ± 4.3 mmHg. Similarly, Mahdy et al. [14] reported a mean blood loss of 842 ± 145 ml with terlipressin, compared with 1,065 ± 202 ml with placebo, associated with a significant decrease in PVP from 17.8 ± 7.3 mmHg to 15.9 ± 6.5 mmHg. Studies indicate that a reduced PVP correlates with lower blood loss during hepatic resection; hence, the use of terlipressin to induce splanchnic vasoconstriction decreases portal blood flow and blood loss [3].

Some authors have used vasopressin infusion to reduce PVP and blood loss in patients with portal hypertension who underwent hepatic resection [4,7]. Adding a vasodilator such as NTG further reduced portal and hepatic venous flow; Westaby et al. demonstrated a 7 mmHg drop in PVP with NTG and vasopressin [15].

Similarly, in our study group MT, the mean PVP decreased by 2.3 ± 1.1 mmHg from baseline value when milrinone was administered along with terlipressin. Milrinone, due to its vasodilatory effect and enhanced diastolic relaxation of the heart, decreases CVP and facilitates venous drainage from the hepatic sinusoids, thus reducing blood loss [6,16]. This could have contributed to the lower blood loss in Group MT (570 [329, 880] ml) than in Group T (824 [750, 1,146] ml). In addition, the effects of milrinone, including peripheral vasodilation and improved venous drainage from the hepatic sinusoids, likely enhanced the effect of terlipressin, similar to the baroreceptor-mediated splanchnic vasoconstriction observed with NTG [14].

The effect of the drugs on hepatosplanchnic circulation could be appreciated more in participants who had undergone minor hepatic resection with a significantly lower median blood loss of 373 (273, 556) ml than in Group T (798 [725, 1,230]). Major hepatic resections are typically preceded by Selective Hepatic Vascular Exclusion (SHVE), which was not applicable for minor resections in our study [17]. SHVE was performed only after drug administration, potentially limiting the full effect of the drug on the liver vascular bed. Consequently, the reduction in blood loss in Group MT was significant for minor resections but not for major resections. Despite uneven resection planes in minor hepatectomies, our study demonstrated that the combination of MT significantly reduced blood loss.

Group MT had a higher HR than Group T, likely because of milrinone-induced vasodilation. Although the MAP was maintained above 65 mmHg in both groups, it was lower in the MT group, probably because of the vasodilatory effects of milrinone. Unlike NTG, milrinone preserves cardiac output through its inotropic effects, thus preventing significant hemodynamic instability [6]. Additionally, the splanchnoselectivity of terlipressin reduces the risk of hypertension associated with vasopressin [15,18].

Increased PVP and blood flow after hepatic resection impart shear stress on the remnant liver. Carrapita et al. [19] found that an absolute PVP increase of less than 2 mmHg during hepatectomy is associated with major complications, such as coagulation impairment, hyperlactatemia, and post-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF). In our study, PVP increased by 0.8 ± 4.4 mmHg in Group MT and 1.2 ± 2.1 mmHg in Group T. Furthermore, Fahrner et al. [20] demonstrated that terlipressin mitigates the abrupt rise in PVP, thereby enhancing liver regeneration and optimizing cellular proliferation after hepatectomy [21].

Lower PVP at Te, due to the effects of MT, mitigated sudden hepatic injury and facilitated a controlled environment for liver regeneration, as evidenced by slower and less pronounced increases in liver enzyme levels. In major hepatic resections, bilirubin levels increased by no more than 2 times in Group MT and 3 times in Group T compared to the 4-fold increase reported in previous studies [22]. The study also showed a less than 25% decrease in serum albumin, with a positive rise by day 3, in contrast to the 50% drop reported by other authors [22,23]. Early recovery of synthetic liver function is a favorable indicator of improved postoperative recovery. None of the patients in either group experienced significant renal function loss or AKI by day 3, consistent with the use of terlipressin. Its splanchnic-selectivity helps preserve renal blood flow and maintain stable hemodynamics [8]. Additionally, prolonged hypotension was prevented by administering norepinephrine to four patients, ensuring an MAP greater than 75 mmHg in both groups.

In our study, all patients diagnosed with PHLF had cholangiocarcinoma, an aggressive liver malignancy often associated with poor outcomes [24]. Two participants, one each in groups T and MT, exhibited features of grade A PHLF and recovered without any complications. In contrast, one patient in the MT group had grade C PHLF, resulting in an overall incidence of 8.8%, which is consistent with previous reports [25]. Of the two patients who died within 90 days, one had grade C PHLF, aligning with a previously reported mortality rate of 85.7% [22]. The other patient’s death could be attributed to poorly differentiated hepatocellular carcinoma on histopathology and nonadherence with chemotherapy. Among all patients, four had significant bile leak, with a median duration of surgery of 450 min, emphasizing surgical duration as a critical risk factor. The incidence of bile leaks was low in minor hepatectomies, likely because major bile ducts were avoided during resection.

This study had several limitations. The direct measurement of HVP and PVP is ideal. However, the unavailability of manometer-tipped catheters may have led to variability in collected data [25]. Additionally, CVP is an unreliable indicator of HVP owing to potential variations in transducer positioning relative to the liver [24]. The cases were heterogeneous; therefore, the primary pathology could have affected intraoperative blood loss and postoperative outcomes. In this study, blood loss estimation was based on suction volume, sponge/gauze weight, and visual assessment of the surgical field; methods that are routinely used in clinical practice are subject to potential interoperator variability and measurement errors. Although efforts have been made to standardize the measurement process, some degree of subjectivity and inaccuracy is unavoidable and may have influenced the reported absolute blood loss values. A key limitation of this study was its relatively small sample size, which may have reduced its statistical power and limited the generalizability of the findings. Further studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate these findings.

The combination of MT reduced the PVP more effectively than terlipressin alone, leading to decreased blood loss. The impact of these drugs on hepatosplanchnic circulation was particularly notable in participants who underwent minor hepatic resections.
