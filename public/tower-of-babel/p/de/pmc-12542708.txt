ABSTRACT
AbstractThe second Design for Implementation (DFI): The Future of Trauma Research and Clinical Guidance Conference gathered experts from diverse sectors to advance trauma clinical guidance design and implementation. Building on the previous year’s minimum viable product, this conference progressed from conceptualization to design phase, focusing on creating scalable, sustainable solutions for trauma clinical guidance. Participants explored innovative approaches addressing critical challenges in trauma care, including interorganizational collaboration, resource-adaptable guidance, and patient-centered design. The conference highlighted the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) to enhance guidance development and maintain clinical expertise and ethical standards. A key advancement was the refinement of a central repository containing 258 guidance documents in the form of an app with improved accessibility features. Discussions emphasized the importance of implementation science principles, advocating for leadership engagement, maintaining academic recognition for guidance contributions, and continuous outcome tracking. The proposed 12-step guidance development process integrates AI capabilities and preserving clinician expertise. Patient voices and lived experiences were emphasized as essential elements in developing trauma-informed systems, with powerful testimonials from trauma survivors illustrating the critical need for comprehensive support services. Participants agreed that practical guidance must be contextually relevant, properly vetted, and integrate seamlessly with electronic health records. The conference concluded with plans to secure sustainable funding, formalize partnerships, and engage broader communities. The DFI series will continue in February 2026, focusing on testing and implementing innovative solutions to advance trauma care and improve patient outcomes.

Introduction

The second Design for Implementation (DFI): The Future of Trauma Research and Clinical Guidance Conference Series was held February 19–20, 2025, at the American College of Surgeons (ACS) in Chicago, Illinois. Organized in collaboration with the Coalition for National Trauma Research and supported in part by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, this conference advanced the foundational goal of relieving human suffering by improving trauma clinical guidance design and implementation.

The conference brought together 135 key partners—including 35 presenters—with expertise spanning healthcare (trauma and burn surgery, pediatric surgery, neurosurgery, critical care, nursing), clinical guidance development, (data science), patient advocacy, dissemination and implementation (D&I) science, health technology, user-centered design, and information science. Perspectives from urban, rural, military, and global systems ensured context-aware solutions.

Day 1 grounded participants in the “why” behind DFI and revisited the Year 1 minimum viable product (MVP), including feedback and testing of a new app (table 1). Breakout sessions and panel discussions explored guidance challenges and innovations. Day 2 highlighted cross-sector strategies for effective guidance delivery and emphasized designing scalable, sustainable solutions (table 2). The meeting concluded with MVP refinements and plans to formalize partnerships. Session recordings and presenter slides are available on the conference website (bit.ly/DesignImplement).

Designing for sustainability

Opening the conference, Dr Eileen Bulger, Medical Director for Trauma Programs at the ACS Committee on Trauma (ACS-COT), highlighted traumatic injury as a critical yet overlooked health crisis. As the leading cause of death for individuals aged 1–44, trauma surpasses HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and malaria combined.1 Although trauma care systems save lives, treatment remains inconsistent for this growing issue.2 3 The conference aimed to redesign and standardize evidence-informed trauma care, reducing the 17-year gap between research and implementation to improve patient outcomes.4

Dr. Lacey LaGrone, conference principal investigator, then introduced the Design for Dissemination and Sustainability Framework, marking the shift from last year’s “Conceptualization” phase to this year’s “Design” phase (figure 1).5 She emphasized co-designing solutions using an MVP approach—favoring progress over perfection. Conference goals included confirming agreement on core problems, addressing society collaboration, and exploring complex challenges like patient integration, trauma-informed care, and cross-resource applicability. Dr. LaGrone noted the timely convergence of artificial intelligence (AI) advancements, global standardization movements, new revenue models for societies, and alignment between military, rural USA, and global majority healthcare needs. She concluded that injury care is an ideal starting point due to existing verification systems and international reach, with collaboration able to drive sustainable, patient-centered solutions.

The Design for Dissemination and Sustainability Framework.

Julie Ford, PhD(c), trauma survivor and advocate, delivered the keynote. After navigating a complex healthcare system following a life-altering assault, she identified critical gaps in survivor support. She helped expand a local resource guide with Crime Survivors NOLA (New Orleans, Louisiana, USA) and advocated for growing “Seeds of NOLA”, a city-funded Trauma Recovery Center offering free therapy, case management, and emergency funds.6 Preliminary data show reduced depression and repeat traumatic injury among survivors using these resources. Ms Ford urged hospitals to adopt similar models or start by offering resource guides, emergency funds, and survivor advocates. Since fewer than one in 10 survivors receive support, trauma-informed care is essential for healing and violence prevention.7

As part of this year’s expanded focus on lived experience, a hallway gallery featured quotes from The Miracle Child: Traumatic Brain Injury and Me, a memoir by Kelly Lang, who served as a patient representative during both conference years.8 Kelly Lang contributed excerpts from her book to a patient-focused display featuring canvas artwork created by Lindsay Sherbondy, owner of Lindsay Letters, whose daughter also experienced a traumatic brain injury (TBI) (online supplemental file 1).9 Kelly Lang shared that observing providers’ reactions to the gallery validated the daily experience of survivors and caregivers. The installation underscored the importance of empathy, personal narratives, and survivor voices in developing trauma-informed systems.

MVP updates and reflections

Then, addressing the shared perception that the complexities of collaboration have historically stymied progress in this arena, Dr Jacob Tebes, Professor of Psychiatry, Child Study, and Public Health at Yale, presented on team science and collaboration, emphasizing that sustained success requires teamwork. Teamwork, defined as coordinated, cooperative, and communicative interactions toward shared goals, is grounded in “team integrative capacity”—developing a shared identity, defining roles, and establishing communication structures.10 11 Team science is driven by social interactions that promote trust and psychological safety alongside knowledge integration that blends competencies.1013 Dr Tebes addressed challenges of rotating team members and conflict, advocating for trust through shared mental models and commitment to the broader mission.

Progress on last year’s MVP output was detailed by Kat Grider, digital marketing expert with a background in development, using the Purpose to Practice (P2P) framework (figures2 3).14 Using principles of collaboration, inclusivity, and accessibility, the core purpose is to relieve human suffering by improving trauma clinical guidance design and implementation. She announced progress on MVP Deliverable 1: A Central Repository for Clinical Guidance, which includes an app developed by Tactuum containing 258 guidance documents. However, participants emphasized that compiling guidance is not enough—they must integrate with electronic health records (EHRs), be contextually relevant, vetted, and regularly updated. Such systems should capture clinical expertise efficiently without over-reliance on volunteerism. Len Beasley, an inclusive design expert, introduced user experience testing of the app, inviting feedback on navigation, search functionality, and the AI-enabled chat feature.

After app testing, discussion shifted to MVP Deliverable 2: Collaborate Instead of Compete. A panel of trauma leaders from the ACS-COT, American Association for the Surgery of Trauma, Western Trauma Association, the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma, and the Society of Trauma Nurses, including Drs Eileen Bulger, Kimberly Davis, Krista Kaups, Richard Miller, Patrick Reilly, Ben Zarzaur, and LeAnne Young, MSN, underscored the need for cross-organizational collaboration. Panelists discussed curating existing guidance and leveraging AI for synthesis to highlight existing recommendations and identify gaps. Challenges include securing stakeholder buy-in and ensuring global and rural provider representation. Panelists stressed maintaining academic recognition and establishing a dedicated group to support sustainability, requiring leadership alignment, resource allocation, and a patient-centered focus.

Dr Nobhojit Roy, an Indian trauma surgeon, currently practicing in Ghana, who specializes in healthcare delivery in resource-limited settings, highlighted MVP Deliverable 3: Adaptive Clinical Guidance. Despite injured patients arriving quickly at community hospitals, many lack advanced diagnostics specified by current guidance. This results in patient transfer to distant facilities, causing harmful delays and emphasizing the need for context-specific protocols for frontline providers. Dr Andrés Rubiano, a neurosurgeon at El Bosque University in Colombia, presented the Beyond One Option for Treatment of Traumatic Brain Injury: A Stratified Protocol project.15 Recognizing that one-size-fits-all approaches fail in diverse settings, his team developed customizable protocols that integrate resource availability (eg, “Do you have a CT scanner? Yes/No”) into clinical guidance. Pilot implementation in Colombia has shown improved resource organization and reduced mortality, with efforts to expand this framework to other conditions and regions. He emphasized cross-society collaboration for adaptable guidance in diverse environments.

Designing for appropriateness and feasibility

Opening the session with a critical assessment of current challenges, Dr. LaGrone described how clinical guidance from organizations like the World Health Organization (WHO) often fails to reach frontline providers due to localization challenges and resource constraints. She proposed leveraging AI to organize information, enabling healthcare professionals to focus on high-level decision-making. She emphasized Agile methodology, which prioritizes individuals, working prototypes, end-user collaboration, and adaptability.16 Although AI poses risks in healthcare, its responsible implementation guided by clinician input and patient-centered values could enhance equity and outcomes.

Shifting focus to ethical considerations, Natalie Meyers from the University of Notre Dame presented recommendations from the AI and Data Visitation Working Group: an AI Bill of Rights, informed consent protocols, ethics committee guidance, and secure processing frameworks.17 Her overview covered responsible AI practices, including acceptable use policies, oversight committees, impact assessments, monitoring strategies, disclosure agreements, vendor due diligence, and bias testing. She stressed the importance of AI alignment—ensuring systems operate ethically through defined goals, documented monitoring, bias detection, privacy protections, and processes for addressing violations.

Five panelists then explored guidance implementation strategies. Dr Simon Oczkowski, an expert in guideline development, outlined the gold standard approach, including diverse expert panels, clearly defined scopes, systematic reviews of available evidence, and structured evidence-to-decision processes. He introduced the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) methodology for evaluating evidence quality before formulating recommendations.18 Despite rigorous methods, guidance often relies on low-certainty evidence, requiring expert judgment and value-based decisions, which should be transparently documented. The labor-intensive process underscores the need for tools that streamline development without sacrificing quality.

Demonstrating practical applications of AI integration, Dr Kristan Staudenmayer, chair of the ACS-COT Trauma Systems Committee, along with Harjot Bambra and Tarun Gujral from Creative Bits AI, showcased an AI-enabled system that synthesizes medical literature into knowledge graphs. Their system accelerates guidance creation, allowing clinicians to focus on decision-making rather than information gathering. Dr Staudenmayer noted that current guidance development is often defined by available effort rather than clinical need. The tool provides a collaborative hub for clinician feedback, with outputs adaptable for EHR integration or patient education.

Building on the theme of accessibility, Chris Champion, CEO of MAGIC Evidence Ecosystem Foundation, advocated for structured, digitized guideline data to improve accessibility and workflow integration. He demonstrated MAGIC’s platform for creating, updating, and distributing guidance, embedded into healthcare systems via application programming interfaces. Structuring guidance enhances adaptability and searchability but must be grounded in strong methodology for quality and reliability.

From theory to practice, Dr Christopher Tignanelli, Associate Dean for Data Science at the University of Minnesota, and Matt Simko, Vice President of Product Management at Stanson Health, described deploying the Berne-Norwood TBI criteria as a computable guideline across multiple health systems.19 Their implementation included real-time EHR alerts and compliance dashboards, with a framework for updates based on new evidence. Evaluation revealed concern about alert fatigue, prompting a complementary dashboard to deliver information without disrupting workflow.

Challenging conventional approaches, Dr Mitchell Cohen, an expert in big data, machine learning, and AI, argued that traditional clinical trials are less relevant in the precision medicine era. He presented computational models that identify patient states and trajectories, enabling real-time, personalized decision support. Dr Cohen shared research demonstrating improved prediction accuracy when incorporating patient state identification into models.20 21 He concluded with a neonatal sepsis prediction tool that displayed risk scores, promoting earlier interventions and reducing mortality by 22%.22

Attention then shifted to the scalability of AI-augmented clinical decision support systems, with panelists debating the utility of an overseeing body to mandate a unifying framework. The discussion explored whether initial implementation should focus on non-emergent settings, given deployment challenges in high-pressure environments like trauma bays. Further examination covered AI applications, from simple physiological monitoring to advanced predictive analytics. Although AI enhances efficiency, human expertise remains critical for appropriate application and interpretation of recommendations.

To conclude the day’s proceedings, Chelsea Church, pro-bono consultant from Boston Consulting Group (BCG), emphasized the complexity and significance of the issues discussed. She acknowledged that many groups are tackling different aspects of the problem and stressed the importance of aligning incentives and fostering collaboration to drive meaningful progress. She concluded with a preview of day 2, which would focus on developing a new approach.

The “How” of Design for Implementation

Day two opened with a powerful keynote from Dr. LJ Punch, founder of The BRIC (Bullet Related Injury Clinic) in St Louis, who reflected on reimagining clinical guidance by truly centering patients. He highlighted the BRIC as a model for removing barriers to patient-centered care through culturally congruent services such as transportation, clinical services, occupational therapy, spiritual care, and the BRIC Box—a care kit provided at discharge. Through rapid feedback loops and a patient advisory board, the clinic prioritizes deep community engagement and continuous improvement. Dr. Punch challenged trauma systems to look beyond hospital walls, emphasizing outpatient care and patient voices as essential components of equitable, high-quality clinical guidance.

Transitioning to implementation science, Dr Molly McNett, Associate Dean of Evidence-Based Practice at The Ohio State University, emphasized that successful guidance uptake depends on D&I science. Although dissemination focuses on spreading information, implementation ensures it becomes embedded in practice. Passive methods, like publication, are ineffective; instead, adoption requires leadership and stakeholder buy-in, phased implementation, and outcome tracking.23 Planning for scalability early ensures guidance can be adapted across diverse settings.

A comprehensive approach to guideline development was presented by Dr Wui-Jin Koh, Chief Medical Officer of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), who outlined the organization’s multidisciplinary approach. Updated annually through a rigorous institutional review, these guidelines involve a full panel of stakeholders, including patient advocates. When critical new data emerges, off-cycle revisions are made. NCCN prioritizes confidentiality, transparency, and member disclosures. The guidelines are freely available and allow for international adaptations by local experts to tailor the recommendations.

Richard Caldwell, from the Defense Health Agency’s Implementation Science Branch, introduced the military-based DOTmLPF-PI model (Doctrine, Organization, Training, Material, Leadership, Personnel, Facility, Policy, Integration) as a framework for analyzing implementation challenges. He also described his guideline “deconstruction” approach that maps current processes, defines objectives, and prioritizes practical guidance.24 His nursing background informed recommendations for tailoring guidance to provider roles, with engagement success achieved through digital platforms and strategically placed posters in busy settings.

The critical role of data infrastructure was highlighted when Dr Jonathan Stallings, Chief Data Scientist at the Joint Trauma System, described challenges with the Department of Defense Trauma Registry (DoDTR), including manual data extraction, antiquated software, and disconnected systems, which delay policy and clinical decision updates. As a solution, he introduced SIMON (System for Injury Monitoring and Outcomes Nexus), a centralized, cloud-based tool that automates and standardizes data collection. He advocated for linking metrics to guidance to benchmark care, drive quality improvement, and inform future trauma care—efforts grounded in a robust data governance framework ensuring accountability, consistency, security, and ethical use.

After exploring various guidance development approaches, participants engaged in breakout sessions focused on redesigning trauma clinical guidance with these concepts in mind. Dr. LaGrone introduced a 12-step, AI-integrated approach that upholds ethics and collaboration (table 3). Emphasizing it as a low-fidelity, evolving model, she invited feedback to refine the process. These sessions aimed to generate improvement ideas, flag concerns, and foster co-design partnerships. Dr. LaGrone proposed a 24-week timeline, from topic selection to a bedside-ready decision tree with narration, highlighting the critical role of subject matter experts, whose leadership and ownership are key.

In a parallel breakout focused on patient inclusion, Dr. Punch and Stephanie Reynolds from Power4STL guided participants through exploring why patients are often excluded from clinical guidance and how to change that. Using the St. Louis Sandbox framework, rooted in the community response to Ferguson, participants examined their power, data agency, and narrative influence.25 Dr. Punch asked, “Patients are not at the center of clinical guidance because…”, prompting deep reflection. Through guided exercises, attendees scored their influence across healthcare levels, revealing gaps between perceived and actual power. Key themes emerged, including structural humility, active listening, and the inseparability of story and data as essential to centering patients. Ultimately, the breakout reframed communication as a relational, systemic, and collective act. “We don’t center patients by putting them into our system”, Dr. Punch said, “we center them by seeing that they ARE the center”.

Structuring for sustainability and scalability

Further reflecting on and maturing the MVP, Kat Grider guided participants through the “structure” component of the P2P framework. The session began with individual reflection on organizing structures and distributing control, followed by paired and group discussions. Several models emerged, including hierarchical structures with bidirectional information flow between central committees and end users, governance models with an inclusive steering committee alongside expert subcommittees, an NCCN-inspired framework enhanced by AI for literature searches, and repository-based approaches standardizing content. Participants emphasized leveraging existing work through common data models, templates, and standardized formats.

Shifting focus to publication models, Ashley Farley from the Gates Foundation discussed reclaiming power in research dissemination. She highlighted the foundation’s shift to preprints and community-owned platforms, valuing researchers’ work over journal prestige. Ms. Farley urged systemic change and resistance to inequitable publishing. She addressed AI’s role in information access, noting its usefulness in managing data overload and emphasizing that librarians remain essential for access and quality assessment.

The limitations of traditional research methods were examined as Dr Anna Maw, an implementation scientist at the University of Colorado, discussed the cost and generalizability constraints of randomized controlled trials. In response, pragmatic research has emerged, using real-world data to enhance validity and accelerate learning. Dr Maw described learning health systems (LHS), which continuously improve care by aligning science, informatics, incentives, and culture.26 She highlighted an AI project capturing patient-generated social determinants data in the EHR, enhancing data capture and patient care. Dr Maw noted that the main barriers to LHS implementation are not technical but related to misaligned incentives between EHR vendors, health systems, and patient outcomes.

As the conference concluded, pro-bono BCG consultants Chelsea Church and Shannon Rosenauer reflected on MVP progress, noting that participants desired interactive, AI-enabled tools over static PDF repositories. Kat Grider shared participant survey results showing 80% alignment on the MVP purpose, with further discussion needed on practice and participants. A central steering committee with delegated subcommittees emerged as the favored structure. Looking forward, Dr. LaGrone outlined next steps, including securing post-2026 funding, formalizing participant roles, and engaging the broader healthcare community. She emphasized sustainability, inclusivity, and an abundance mindset, noting that valuable solutions find ready adopters.

Conclusion

The 2025 Design for Implementation conference highlighted collaboration, innovation, and adaptability in advancing trauma clinical guidance. Discussions centered on scalable, AI-augmented solutions, inclusive governance, and sustainable practices. Participants emphasized integrating diverse expertise, continuous feedback, and real-time data to drive impactful change. As the MVP is iteratively refined, the focus remains on refining structures, enhancing accessibility, and ensuring broad engagement to promote lasting improvements in trauma care, with the patient remaining at the heart of the solution. The series will continue in February 2026 with a focus on further testing and implementation of the solution.
