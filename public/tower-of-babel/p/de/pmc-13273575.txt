Simultaneous saddle pulmonary embolism and right atrial thrombus managed with ICE-guided dual suction Thrombectomy
Authors: Ibraheem Sabah, Sakeena Saife, Ahmad Nouri, Tamim Khaddash, Sarah Bhanushali, Ibrahim Khaddash, Fayez Shamoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSaddle pulmonary embolism (PE) complicated by right atrial thrombus in transit is an infrequent but highly lethal condition. Standard treatments such as systemic thrombolysis or surgical embolectomy carry significant risks, particularly in elderly or comorbid patients. We describe the case of a 76-year-old man with spina bifida, cauda equina syndrome, chronic immobility, and multiple comorbidities who presented in shock. Imaging revealed a large saddle PE with mobile right atrial thrombus and right ventricular strain. Given prohibitive surgical risk and contraindications to thrombolysis due to recent spinal infection and acute kidney injury, and high bleeding risk, he underwent percutaneous suction thrombectomy using the FlowTriever system. Large thrombus burden was successfully removed from both the right pulmonary artery and right atrium, with restoration of flow and hemodynamic stabilization. He was transitioned to long-term anticoagulation and discharged in stable condition. This case highlights the role of dual-site suction thrombectomy as a life-saving option when conventional therapies are contraindicated.

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) remains a leading cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. When it is accompanied by a mobile right atrial thrombus (a clot-in-transit), the risk of acute hemodynamic collapse can reach 40% and climbing even with prompt anticoagulation, especially in the first 24 hours after presentation. Recent registries confirm that echocardiography-detected right atrial thrombi are strong independent predictors of adverse outcomes, underscoring the need for urgent intervention. In patients presenting with high-risk PE and clot-in-transit, traditional strategies like systemic thrombolysis or surgical embolectomy are often limited by bleeding risk, comorbidities, or the need for cardiopulmonary bypass, respectively.

In recent years, catheter-based mechanical thrombectomy has emerged as a promising alternative, offering rapid clot removal without exposing the patient to the risks of systemic lysis or major surgery [1, 2].

Moreover, six-month follow-up data revealed sustained right ventricular recovery, functional improvement, and minimal chronic complications [3].

Although there have been some cases previously described for mechanical thrombectomy in pulmonary embolism or isolated right atrial clot-in-transit, there are few instances in which a two-site procedure was ever completed for both intracardiac and pulmonary thrombi. Unlike some previously described cases, our report focuses on a progressive and intermittently ICE-guided two-suction thrombectomy approach in a deeply comorbid and shock-ingress-situated patient with absolute contraindications for both thrombolytics and surgery [4].

Here, we report a case of active dual-site suction thrombectomy targeting both a mobile right atrial thrombus and a saddle pulmonary embolus in a critically ill, high-risk patient. By combining real-time intracardiac echocardiographic guidance with mechanical aspiration, clear clot removal and rapid hemodynamic recovery were achieved. This case further reinforces the evolving role of percutaneous interventions for PE when standard therapies are contraindicated or too risky.

Case presentation

A 76-year-old male with a history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, stage 3 chronic kidney disease, obstructive sleep apnea, morbid obesity, spina bifida with cauda equina syndrome, and a recent admission for spinal epidural abscess was brought from a nursing facility in shock.

On admission, his blood pressure was 79/52 mmHg, oxygen saturation 93% on room air, and troponin was elevated at 300 ng/l. Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) and NT-proBNP levels were not obtained during the initial evaluation. Laboratory results showed leukocytosis (21 × 109/l), acute kidney injury (creatinine 4.2 mg/dl, baseline 1.0), and elevated C-reactive protein (302 mg/l).

CT pulmonary angiography demonstrated a large saddle PE with extensive right pulmonary artery involvement and right ventricular strain (Fig. 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a large, mobile right atrial thrombus.

Axial CT pulmonary angiography demonstrating a large saddle pulmonary embolism (arrow) at the bifurcation of the pulmonary trunk extending into both pulmonary arteries.

Post-procedural intracardiac echocardiography demonstrating complete resolution of the right atrial thrombus, with no residual mobile mass.

The patient had contraindications for systemic thrombolysis for an epidural abscess that occurred just prior to presentation, severe acute kidney injury with creatinine at 4.2 mg/dl, hemodynamic instability, and the expected risk of significant bleeding. In addition, owing to his age, morbid obesity, baseline stage 3 chronic kidney disease with superimposed severe acute kidney injury, instability, and the expected need for cardiopulmonary bypass, the patient was assessed to be a poor surgical risk for surgical embolectomy. Right femoral venous access was obtained, and a 24F FlowTriever suction catheter was advanced into the right atrium. Initial passes failed to retrieve thrombus, but intracardiac echocardiography confirmed a large clot. At that point, two passes with the thrombectomy catheter had already been performed, and we briefly attempted transthoracic echocardiography (TTE). Due to the patient’s body habitus, TTE was limited and did not clearly demonstrate residual clot. Intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) was then utilized and confirmed a persistent large right atrial thrombus. Repeat suction successfully removed significant thrombus (Fig. 2), and follow-up ICE confirmed complete clearance of the right atrium (Fig. 3). ICE was used intermittently rather than continuously: it was initially introduced to assess clot burden and location, removed during thrombectomy to facilitate catheter manipulation, and subsequently reintroduced to confirm complete thrombus clearance. Rather, ICE was first introduced to identify clot burden and location. The catheter was then removed, and the FlowTriever thrombectomy catheter was advanced to the same level for suctioning. Finally, ICE was reintroduced to confirm successful clot clearance.

Intracardiac echocardiography images showing a large, mobile right atrial thrombus (arrow) prior to thrombectomy.

The catheter was then advanced into the right main pulmonary artery. Pullback thrombectomy extracted large thrombi, and pulmonary arteriography confirmed restored perfusion with only a small residual non-obstructive clot in the right upper lobar artery (Fig. 4). No significant clot was observed in the left pulmonary arteries. The patient tolerated the procedure without complication.

Post-thrombectomy pulmonary angiography demonstrating improved perfusion of the right pulmonary artery (arrow).

He was stabilized in the intensive care unit, transitioned to anticoagulation with warfarin (bridged with enoxaparin), Warfarin was selected over a direct oral anticoagulant due to severe renal dysfunction, extreme body weight, and the need for close therapeutic monitoring. and gradually weaned off vasopressors. Though the patient was maintaining his oxygen saturation of 93% on room air initially, he subsequently required oxygen support post-procedure, likely because of residual ventilation-perfusion abnormalities and deconditioning. His oxygenation needs improved over time, allowing him to be discharged on 3 l/min nasal cannula. He was transferred to a skilled nursing facility on long-term anticoagulation with planned cardiology follow-up and repeat echocardiography.

Discussion

Percutaneous mechanical thrombectomy has rapidly evolved as a vital treatment option for intermediate and high-risk pulmonary embolism (PE), especially in patients who cannot tolerate systemic thrombolysis or surgery. The FLASH registry, a prospective multicenter cohort evaluating the FlowTriever System, demonstrated excellent outcomes: among 800 real-world PE patients (76.7% intermediate–high risk, 7.9% high-risk), major adverse events occurred in only 1.8% within 48 hours. All-cause mortality was exceptionally low 0.3% at 48 hours and 0.8% at 30 days with meaningful reductions in mean pulmonary artery pressure and right ventricular strain on imaging [2].

Reports of simultaneous treatments using mechanical thrombectomy systems, including the FlowTriever platform, for right atrial thrombus and pulmonary embolism have been published previously. Bayona Molano et al. described emergent thrombectomy with continuous echocardiographic guidance, and Bonello et al. [5] reported single-session pulmonary and right heart thrombectomy in a more hemodynamically stable cohort. In contrast, our case describes an intermittently ICE-guided, stepwise dual-site suction thrombectomy strategy performed in a patient presenting in shock with extreme comorbidity and absolute contraindications to thrombolysis and surgery, emphasizing procedural decision-making rather than device feasibility alone. Beyond pulmonary applications, there are growing reports of mechanical thrombectomy deployed in the right atrium. A case series detailed the successful use of FlowTriever for clot-in-transit retrieval when the standard extracorporeal AngioVac system was unsuitable [6].

Similarly, a recent two-case report employed FlowTriever combined with an endovascular snare system to remove adherent right atrial thrombi both cases achieved complete extraction without surgical intervention [12].

Our case builds on these developments by performing dual-site suction thrombectomy intervening on both the right atrium and pulmonary artery in a single procedural session with intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) for procedural guidance. This combined approach is a strategy that remains infrequently reported, particularly in patients presenting with shock and absolute contraindications to thrombolysis and surgery, most reports focus on isolated RA or PA extraction. In contrast, our patient, despite age and multiple comorbidities, achieved rapid hemodynamic stabilization and complete clot clearance, underscoring the feasibility and safety of this strategy.

Crucially, ICE played a pivotal role in confirming thrombus burden and ensuring complete removal. Unlike transthoracic imaging, it provided direct visualization of the right atrium. We used ICE intermittently first to assess clot location, then reintroduced it after thrombectomy to confirm clearance thereby balancing procedural efficiency with imaging accuracy. In practice, our use of ICE was intermittent rather than continuous. We employed it initially to assess clot burden, removed it during suction thrombectomy, and then reintroduced it to verify clot resolution. This stepwise approach reduced procedural complexity while ensuring accurate clot assessment.

Possible complications related to large-bore mechanical thrombectomy include access site hemorrhage, vascular injury, hemolysis, arrhythmias, and cardiac perforation. Theoretical risks are increased with manipulation of the right atrium due to risks of embolization and cardiac injury. There were no complications related to thrombectomy in this patient.

In summary, this case not only reinforces the safety and efficacy of the FlowTriever system in obstructive PE but also expands its utility to select cases of right atrial thrombectomy. It highlights how combining dual-site mechanical aspiration with intracardiac imaging can offer a viable, less-invasive alternative in critically ill patients who are poor candidates for thrombolysis or surgery.
