Fractal hierarchy enables exponential scaling of topological boundary states
Authors: Limin Song, Zhichan Hu, Ziteng Wang, Domenico Bongiovanni, Liqin Tang, Daohong Song, Roberto Morandotti, Jingjun Xu, Hrvoje Buljan, Zhigang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExponential growth describes an extremely rapid process ubiquitous in mathematics and across diverse physical, biological, and technological systems. Here, we introduce a class of fractal-inspired lattices that combine long-range periodic order with self-similar hierarchy, establishing a structural motif that enables exponential scaling of topological boundary states. We demonstrate this phenomenon in (i) a quasi-one-dimensional lattice chain constructed from Koch-curve unit cells and (ii) a two-dimensional periodic tiling lattice composed of Sierpiński-gasket unit cells. We show that, for suitable coupling parameters, both the number of topological boundary states Nℓ and the number of topological minigaps Mℓ grow exponentially with the fractal generation index ℓ. We find that Nℓ is an integer multiple of Mℓ, with the integer determined by the underlying symmetry. This hierarchical scaling law is captured by the multi-topological-phase theory and confirmed experimentally in laser-written photonic lattices. Our results identify fractal hierarchy as a design principle for controlling boundary-state multiplicity, revealing a fundamental interplay between topology, self-similar geometry, and periodic order. More broadly, this work suggests a route toward synthetic materials and integrated photonic platforms in which large numbers of robust boundary modes can be engineered within hierarchically structured architectures.Subject terms: Micro-optics, Transformation optics

Introduction

Topological phases of matter in periodic and aperiodic structures, including fractals and quasicrystals, have emerged as a fertile arena for the discovery of unconventional boundary phenomena. In periodic systems, integer-dimensional lattices endowed with chiral symmetry or discrete rotational (Cn) symmetry are described by a well-defined band theory, enabling the realization of low- and higher-order topological insulators (HOTIs), topological lasers, and topological entangled light sources, with promising implications for advanced photonic devices1–8. In these settings, translational invariance conventionally underpins the momentum-space topology and the bulk–boundary correspondence linking band topology to protected boundary states.

Recently, increasing attention has been directed to topological physics beyond periodic order, including systems with quasiperiodic or fractal geometry, where translational symmetry is partially or entirely absent. Fractal lattices have been predicted and demonstrated to host a range of unconventional topological phases, including (anomalous) Floquet photonic topological insulators9–11, nonlinear fractal corner states12, and HOTI phases in acoustic systems13,14. Beyond boundary localization, fractal architectures have also enabled unconventional transport and localization phenomena such as internal non-Hermitian skin effects15, fractal-induced topological phases16–21, and quantum transport in fractal networks22.

At the same time, these developments highlight an intrinsic challenge of exact fractal systems: the absence of translational invariance and, in many cases, the lack of a well-defined bulk structure, precludes the direct application of conventional momentum-space topology and bulk–boundary correspondence9,17,23. Consequently, key concepts such as Brillouin zones, band gaps, and topological invariants lose their standard meaning, complicating both systematic classification and scalable design. This raises a central and unresolved question: is it possible to construct lattice geometries that retain the hierarchical richness of fractals while recovering a controllable, unit-cell-based topological description? Addressing this challenge would connect two regimes of topological matter that have so far remained largely separated, thus enabling new strategies for engineering topological states with controllable multiplicity and functionality. As a step in this direction, a class of fractal-like lattices has been introduced that interpolates between periodic crystals and exact fractals24. In these systems, different generations of a given fractal are treated as effective unit cells, restoring translational invariance while retaining self-similar internal structure. Such lattices have been shown to support multiple flat bands and real-space topological localization25,26. However, how the interplay between periodicity and self-similarity governs the scaling and organization of topological boundary states remains largely unexplored.

Here, we demonstrate exponential scaling of topological boundary states in photonic lattices with fractal-like geometry. As illustrated schematically in Fig. 1, both quasi-one-dimensional (quasi-1D) and two-dimensional (2D) fractal-like lattices constructed from Koch-curve and Sierpiński-gasket unit cells, respectively, support families of topological edge or corner modes, whose number Nℓ grows exponentially with the fractal generation index ℓ. We develop a unified theoretical description based on our recently discovered multi-topological-phase (MTP) framework27, which not only predicts their existence but also quantitatively determines both the exact number of boundary states Nℓ and the number of topological minigaps Mℓ. Specifically, we find that Nℓ=νMℓ, where ν is an integer determined by the underlying symmetry, and establish scaling laws governing their dependence on the coupling parameters. Experimentally, we directly observe this exponential scaling of boundary modes in laser-written fractal-like photonic lattices. The resulting boundary-state proliferation is robust, reflecting an elegant interplay between topology, fractality, and lattice periodicity. Our work establishes a general strategy for engineering fractal-structured synthetic materials and topology-enabled photonic architectures.

Results

The quasi-1D fractal-like chain lattice

Let us explain the scheme for constructing fractal-like lattices that combines the essence of fractality and periodicity in the first example involving Koch-curve unit cells. These lattices are constructed in generations, in a way that is somewhat similar to, but distinct from, the exact fractals; generations are labeled by the structural control parameter ℓ=0, 1, 2, 3,… (Fig. 2a). In the first generation G(0) the unit cell has only two sites, while in the second generation G(1) it has five sites corresponding to the simplest recognizable Koch-curve element. In generation G(2), the unit cell has 17 sites, which already has the features of a larger Koch-curve segment. In every successive generation, the number of lattice sites (i.e., sublattices) in each unit cell increases exponentially. For the Koch fractal-like lattice of generation G(ℓ), the number of sublattices is Uℓ=4ℓ+1, and the unit cells are arranged along one dimension (x1) periodically, thus forming a quasi-1D periodic lattice with a fractal-like unit cell (Fig. 2b for the first two generations). Such a scenario for constructing fractal-like lattices can be used in higher dimensions as well. The 2D fractal-like lattices constructed from Sierpiński-gasket unit cells (Fig. 2c, e) are discussed below. Importantly, the increase of the generation index ℓ inherently entails simultaneous growth in the number of sublattices, physical footprint, and structural complexity, reflecting a fundamental characteristic of fractal-like lattices.

The total number of topological states Nℓ in each generation is an integer multiple of Mℓ, depending on the lattice symmetry. For (d), each generation supports only a single number of topological band gaps; while for (e), each generation (for ℓ>0) can support multiple topological band gaps, i.e., Mℓ can take values from 1 to max[Mℓ] as the topological complexity of the lattice increases (the example in Fig. 4i below).

The recently developed MTP theory27 is highly suitable to describe the topology of fractal-like lattices. In this theory, one divides the elements of every unit cell into intra- and inter-sites, where the former do not couple to sites in other unit cells along one or more dimensions (Methods). We apply the MTP theory to every lattice generation G(ℓ) separately. In essence, the G(0) lattice chain is equivalent to the well-celebrated topological Su–Schrieffer–Heeger (SSH) model studied previously28–31. It admits two inequivalent choices of intra-sites (site A and site B, see Methods). Each sublattice defines an independent topological invariant: site A corresponds to the localized left-edge state, whereas site B corresponds to the right-edge state. An example involving A as the intra-site is illustrated in Figs. 3a–f. (Methods and Supplementary Information Section 1). For the G(1) lattice, each Koch-curve-like unit cell contains five sublattices (A - E). Under the MTP framework, we may choose the first four sites (A–D) as intra-sites, thereby defining four distinct winding numbers W1~4 (Supplementary Information Section 1 for details) associated with four left-edge states (Fig. 3g–j). The winding numbers W1~4 are either 0 or 1, corresponding to the absence or presence of edge states (Fig. 3h, i). For a representative dimerization parameter Δ=0.8 within the topologically nontrivial regime, the eigenvalue spectrum exhibits four topological bandgaps, each hosting two edge states (Fig. 3i, j).

At this stage, one might be tempted to regard these winding numbers as redundant, since they are nonzero within the same parameter regime. However, such an interpretation misses a crucial point. The MTP invariants used here not only signal the existence of boundary modes, but also quantitatively determine their number Nℓ. More generally, for a Koch-chain lattice of arbitrary generation G(ℓ), each unit cell contains Uℓ=4ℓ+1 sublattices. The MTP theory allows the definition of 4ℓ independent winding numbers, which predict the emergence of Mℓ=4ℓ topological bandgaps (Fig. 2d) and a total of Nℓ=2×4ℓ edge states. Thus, Nℓ=2Mℓ where the integer ν=2 is a consequence of inversion symmetry. This constitutes the first explicit manifestation of the exponential explosion of topological boundary states (Supplementary Information Sections 1 and 4).

To experimentally validate this prediction, we demonstrate the selective excitation and detection of individual topological edge modes in 1D fractal-like photonic lattices of different generations. We fabricate G(0) and G(1) lattices consisting of 10 unit cells and systematically probe only the left-localized edge modes in each predicted topological bandgap, given the inversion symmetry. The experimental results are displayed in Fig. 3e, f, k and l. The lattices are established by the continuous-wave (CW) laser-writing technique, as used in our recent work32–37. Inter-waveguide couplings are controlled by adjusting the waveguide spacing. The spacing is so chosen that only nearest-neighbor couplings are non-negligible, which allows us to experimentally implement the unit cell of G(1) with five equidistant lattice sites in a linear arrangement, while inter-cell coupling is controlled via a zigzag geometry. To selectively excite a given topological edge state, the input probe beams are shaped in both amplitude and phase to match the calculated eigenmodes (insets of Fig. 3e, k), ensuring mode-selective excitation. In the topologically nontrivial lattices, the optical intensity remains localized mostly at the excited edge sites after propagation (Fig. 3e, k). In contrast, under identical excitation conditions, trivial lattices exhibit observable discrete diffraction (Fig. 3f, l). These observations directly confirm both the existence and the generation-dependent multiplicity of topological edge states, providing experimental evidence for the exponential growth predicted by theory. Numerical simulations for long-distance propagation, presented in the Supplementary Information Sections 3 and 5, further corroborate these results. The same approach can be used to verify the exponential sequence of topological bandgaps and edge states for other fractal generations.

The 2D fractal-like lattices and higher-order topology

We now turn to the 2D fractal-like Sierpiński-gasket tiling lattices. For a G(ℓ) lattice composed of L stacked layers along x1,2 directions, the total number of unit cells is LL+1/2. Each unit cell contains Uℓ=3(1+3ℓ)/2,ℓ=0,1,2,… sublattices, yielding a total number of lattice sites S=UℓL(L+1)/2. Unlike genuine fractal lattices, which are self-similar9–11,13,14,23, the fractal-like lattices studied here retain translational invariance while embedding fractal geometry within the unit cell. This key distinction allows the definition of momentum-space topology while preserving fractal-induced hierarchy. For clarity, we focus on the first two generations G(0) and G(1) with an array size L=4 (Fig. 2c), noting that the analysis generalizes straightforwardly to arbitrary values of ℓ and L (Supplementary Information Sections 2 and 4).

Figure 4 summarizes the numerical and experimental results for the two generations under investigation. The G(0) lattice is equivalent to the well-known breathing Kagome lattice, a paradigmatic second-order topological insulator35,38–44. Its three sublattices allow three equivalent choices of intra-sites, each associated with one corner state protected by the generalized chiral symmetry and rotational symmetry39,45. The real-space spectrum of the nontrivial G(0) lattice exhibits a gapped structure with a set of triply degenerate zero-energy corner states (Fig. 4b–d). Rather than reinterpreting the established Kagome lattice physics, we emphasize that G(0) serves as the fundamental seed generation underlying the exponential growth mechanism within the MTP framework. As the index ℓ increases, the number of higher-order topological corner states grows rapidly. For the G(1) lattice, there are three corner states at every band gap (Fig. 4g and j). Unlike the 1D case, we have three distinct winding numbers associated with three topological minigaps in the 2D tiling lattice (Fig. 4h and i). These invariants cannot be unified, as they are non-zero in different parameter regimes. Figure 4j highlights all HOTI corner-state eigenvalues supported by the G(1) lattice, along their corresponding topological minigaps shaded in gray and labeled with gℓn. As the number of available topological bandgaps increases, the individual gap width decreases, reminiscent of spectral minigap formation in topological quasicrystals46. The maximum number of topological minigaps in a G(ℓ) lattice is Mℓ=3×2ℓ-1ℓ\ge1, yielding a total number of HOTI states Nℓ=3Mℓ (in this example ν=3 is a consequence of C3 symmetry), which scales exponentially with ℓ as shown in Fig. 2e. We emphasize, however, that this exponential proliferation of topological minigaps and boundary states is accompanied by a corresponding increase in the number of sublattices, physical footprint, and structural complexity, reflecting an intrinsic property of fractal-like hierarchies.

To experimentally verify the exponential scaling in 2D lattices, we employ the CW-laser-writing technique32–37 to realize photonic G(0) and G(1) fractal-like tiling lattices based on Sierpiński-gasket unit cells (as shown in Fig. 2c), in both trivial and nontrivial geometries. Probe beams are shaped in amplitude and phase to selectively excite HOTI corner modes, as shown in the insets of Fig. 4e and k. In the nontrivial lattices, the output intensity remains strongly confined to the excited corner sites, independent of the specific vertex chosen, reflecting C3 -rotational symmetry protection. In contrast, trivial lattices exhibit strong coupling to neighboring sites and delocalization (Fig. 4f and l). We have thus observed experimentally the characteristic topological corner states predicted to exist in the G(0) and G(1) lattices. These experimental observations, together with the theoretical analysis and numerical simulations (Supplementary Information Sections 2–5) provide direct confirmation of the generation-dependent exponential proliferation of higher-order topological states.

Discussion

The exponential scaling of topological boundary states controlled by the generation index ℓ represents a previously unexplored mechanism for engineering hierarchical topological spectra. The unit cells constructed here possess fractal-inspired geometric structures; however, the underlying principle is more general. One can envision alternative hierarchical constructions, not necessarily based on fractals, in which the number of sites within a unit cell also grows exponentially with ℓ, potentially leading to similar scaling behavior. In the quasi-1D Koch-chain example, the tight-binding description allows the same exponential proliferation of topological boundary states to be reproduced in periodic lattices with conventional unit-cell geometries (Fig. 3). From this perspective, one can argue that fractality is not a necessary condition for exponential scaling of topological boundary states and minigaps. In contrast, for the 2D Sierpiński-tiling lattices studied here, the fractal hierarchy plays a central role (Supplementary Information Section 8). Exponential scaling emerges only when the lattice simultaneously possesses the hierarchical fractal structure and resides in a strongly nontrivial topological regime. Fractality alone, for example, in the weakly nontrivial regime, is therefore insufficient to generate the observed behavior.

Taken together, these results suggest that fractals should be viewed not merely as geometric objects, but as a guiding inspiration and a design paradigm for constructing hierarchical lattices with controllable topological spectra. In this framework, self-similar hierarchy provides a systematic route toward the exponential proliferation of topological boundary states and minigaps, revealing new opportunities for topological mode engineering in synthetic materials and photonic systems.

The fractal-like lattices constructed here are distinct from periodic lattices with simple unit cells containing a fixed number of sites5,45,47,48, where the number of protected boundary states is typically fixed and small; they also differ from exact fractal lattices9,12–14,18–21, where the absence of translational invariance precludes a conventional bulk–boundary correspondence and necessitates purely real-space topological descriptions; yet they also differ from quasicrystalline systems46, in which the number of topological states generally depends on system size and does not obey a simple symmetry-controlled counting law. Instead, the key feature of the present platform is the combination of periodic order and hierarchical unit-cell structure, which enables the MTP framework to predict the number of topological minigaps and boundary states analytically. In this sense, our focus is on the exponential counting of boundary states in periodic fractal-like hierarchies described by MTP theory, rather than on generic topological edge/corner physics or the intrinsic topology of exact fractals and quasicrystals.

In the examples presented here, the topological states are determined by the MTP theory, which relies on constrained inter-cell couplings and symmetry protection27. Accordingly, perturbations that respect these constraints are not expected to destroy the topological states. However, it is important to note that, in our hierarchical construction, the exponential increase in the number of topological minigaps is accompanied by a progressive reduction in their widths. Consequently, perturbations of sufficiently large magnitude may affect the robustness of topological states when the corresponding minigaps become comparable to or smaller than the perturbation scale (Supplementary Information Section 6 for a detailed robustness analysis).

For simplicity, we fix the dimerization parameter Δ and explore the hierarchical lattice geometries across successive generations G(ℓ). Distinct topological minigaps emerge within different parameter intervals (Fig. 4h and i), and these intervals become progressively narrower as the generation increases. As a result, the exponential proliferation of topological boundary states may eventually slow down or saturate at a finite generation in the present Sierpiński-tiling lattice. Choosing Δ closer to unity shifts this saturation to higher generations by enlarging the accessible strongly nontrivial regime. Nevertheless, the rapid growth of Hamiltonian dimension, together with the increasingly intricate parameter dependence at higher generations, prevents us from deriving a universal analytical scaling law for the saturation generation or the ultimate minigap limit. By contrast, if Δ is treated as a generation-independent control parameter, Δ = Δ(ℓ), the exponential scaling can, in principle, be sustained to arbitrarily high generations. More generally, the MTP framework suggests that hierarchy-driven topological spectra need not be restricted to exponential scaling. Depending on the design of the hierarchical couplings, other counting laws may emerge, including Fibonacci-type sequences, general integer sequences, and arithmetic progressions, revealing a broader landscape of hierarchy-enabled topological phenomena that remains largely unexplored (Supplementary Information Section 7).

In conclusion, we have theoretically predicted and experimentally demonstrated, within the framework of MTP theory, a new topological scaling mechanism in periodic lattices with fractal-inspired hierarchy, whereby the number of topological boundary states and minigaps grows exponentially with the generation index. Although realized here in laser-written photonic lattices, the underlying concept is broadly applicable and can be extended to a wide range of physical platforms, including acoustic metamaterials, electrical circuits, fiber-loop systems, and synthetic-frequency lattices. By combining periodic order with hierarchical unit-cell design, these systems provide a route for systematically engineering large families of symmetry-protected boundary modes and topological minigaps. Beyond advancing the study of fractal-inspired topological systems, our results establish a conceptual connection among band topology, self-similar hierarchy, and lattice architecture. More broadly, they identify hierarchical lattice design as a powerful paradigm for engineering topological spectra, revealing new opportunities for topology-enabled wave control in photonic, acoustic, and synthetic-matter platforms.

Methods

Multi-topological-phase theory and bulk-boundary correspondence

In our fractal-like structures, the introduction of complex fractal unit cells makes it challenging to accurately describe these topological boundary states based on the conventional bulk-boundary correspondence1,2,45,47, especially for lattices with high generation index. More specifically, it is neither feasible to precisely predict the regimes where topological states emerge nor their number. However, the topology in fractal-like systems is quite naturally described with the MTP theory27. As the generation index ℓ increases, the number of sites in each unit cell and consequently the number of topological gaps increases exponentially, while the specific intra- and inter-cell connectivity is determined, respectively, by the fractality of the unit cells and the periodicity of the whole lattice in every generation G(ℓ). Every topological band gap along with its associated topological state is related to its own topological invariant, given by the winding number in the MTP theory27.

The MTP theory is designed for a lattice model with N+J sublattices (i.e., sites) in a unit cell, where the first group of N sublattices are the so-called intra-sites and lack inter-cell couplings along dimensions xd (d=1 for 1D case and d=1, 2 for 2D case), and the second group of J sublattices consists of all remaining sites. By directly solving the Hamiltonian of each lattice generation and summarizing the count of topological minigaps determined by the nonzero winding numbers in strongly nontrivial regimes, we find that Mℓ=4ℓ for the quasi-1D Koch-chain lattice and Mℓ=3×2ℓ-1 (ℓ≥1) for the 2D Sierpiński-tiling lattice). Owing to the underlying lattice symmetry, each set of boundary states exhibits ν-fold degeneracy. Accordingly, the total number of topological states is given by Nℓ=νMℓ, where ν is the symmetry coefficient (ν=2 for the quasi-1D Koch-chain lattice and ν=3 for the 2D Sierpiński-tiling lattice). Laser-writing fractal-like lattices and probing topological boundary states

In experiments, the finite-sized fractal-like lattices with Koch-curve and Sierpiński-gasket unit cells were fabricated using a CW-laser-writing technique32–36 (the discussion in the Supplementary Information Section 9). To optically induce a waveguide, a nonlinear bulk crystal (strontium barium niobate) was placed at the waist of the Gaussian-shaped writing beam, and the position through which the beam passes the crystal was controlled by a spatial light modulator (SLM). This procedure was repeated to create the lattices in the desired configuration with fractal Koch-curve and Sierpiński-gasket unit cells. In the experiment, a zigzag-shaped lattice structure (with zigzag angle θ=90∘) is used (Fig. 3) in lieu of the original Koch-curve-shaped geometry (Fig. 2b) to achieve an equivalent lattice coupling, eliminating unwanted nearest-neighbor coupling between the B and D sublattices.

To efficiently excite the topological boundary states, the SLM was used to generate probe beams consisting of multiple Gaussian beams with profiles designed to match the target mode distributions. The location, amplitude, and phase of each constituent Gaussian beam were precisely controlled by the SLM through the corresponding phase mask. In this way, mode-resolved excitation was achieved, thereby suppressing crosstalk arising when different modes occupy the same lattice sites. At output from the lattice, the measured intensity and phase patterns provide the information about the presence (absence) of the edge state in nontrivial (trivial) phase, as seen also from the intensity-plot insets in Figs. 3 and 4. Additional details and simulation results corresponding to experiments are provided in Supplementary Information Sections 3, 5 and 6.
