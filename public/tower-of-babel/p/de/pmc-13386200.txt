Microscopic mechanism study of nitromethane oxidation: based on ReaxFF molecular dynamics simulation
Authors: Wentao Deng, Qian Li, Yun Chao, Bin Chen, Lei Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study conducted a ReaxFF-MD simulation to investigate the oxidation reaction of nitromethane at temperatures ranging from 2400 to 3000 K. Both temperature and oxygen concentration had significant effects on the reaction process. As the temperature increased, the consumption of reactants accelerated, and the conversion of intermediates became more rapid, resulting in an increase in the reaction rate. CH3 and CH2O were identified as key intermediates, among which the accumulation of CH2O was much higher than that of other carbon-containing species. The increase in initial oxygen content promoted the generation of CO2 and H2O, while the yields of H2 and N2 decreased. The concentrations of OH, HO2, and H2O2 were influenced by both temperature and oxygen availability, among which HO2 and H2O2 responded more significantly to changes in oxygen content. According to Arrhenius analysis, the activation energy of nitromethane thermal decomposition was 35.87 kcal mol−1, while the activation energies for fuel enrichment, stoichiometric, and fuel deficient oxidation systems were 32.20, 48.15, and 41.74 kcal mol−1, respectively.

Introduction

Nitromethane (CH3NO2) is one of the most widely studied nitro compounds because of its unique molecular structure and high energy density.1,2 Owing to the coexistence of a methyl group and a nitro group within the same molecule, nitromethane possesses characteristics of both fuel and oxidizer.3 This feature enables it to release considerable energy within a short period and has led to its application in propellant formulations, explosive compositions, racing fuels, and aerospace propulsion systems.4–6 Unlike conventional hydrocarbon fuels that rely entirely on external oxygen for oxidation, nitromethane contains chemically bound oxygen, which allows oxidation reactions to proceed even under oxygen-deficient conditions.7 Its favorable detonation performance and storage stability have also made it a representative model compound in studies of energetic materials.8,9 The oxidation chemistry of nitromethane is highly complex. Under high-temperature conditions, the molecule undergoes initial bond dissociation to form reactive fragments and free radicals, followed by a series of elementary reactions involving hydrogen abstraction, radical recombination, oxidation, and nitrogen-species conversion.10–13 The interactions among these reactions determine not only the overall reaction rate but also the formation and consumption of intermediate species. As the reaction proceeds, the concentrations of radicals continuously change, leading to shifts in dominant reaction pathways and ultimately affecting product distributions and energy release characteristics. Temperature is generally recognized as one of the most important parameters governing combustion chemistry. Variations in temperature can substantially modify the formation rates and lifetimes of radicals and intermediates, thereby altering the evolution of the reaction network and the relative importance of competing pathways.14–16 For nitromethane oxidation, understanding how temperature influences intermediate conversion and radical evolution remains essential for describing the overall reaction behavior. Such information is also important for the development of reliable kinetic mechanisms and for improving the design of energetic fuels and propulsion systems. Therefore, detailed investigation of the oxidation process at the microscopic level is necessary to clarify the reaction pathways and the roles of key intermediate species during nitromethane oxidation.

Nitromethane has been extensively studied in the areas of thermal decomposition, ignition, and detonation chemistry. Using reactive molecular dynamics simulations, Han et al.17 investigated the decomposition process of nitromethane at elevated temperatures and described the evolution of major reaction products during the early stages of reaction. Chang et al.18 reported that cleavage of the C–N bond is more favorable than unimolecular isomerization and suggested that several decomposition intermediates can influence subsequent reaction progress. Experimental studies have also provided important kinetic information. For example, Zhang et al.4 measured ignition delay times in a high-pressure shock tube over a temperature range of 900–1150 K at pressures of 5–10 bar and equivalence ratios of 0.5–2.0, supplying valuable data for the validation of nitromethane kinetic mechanisms. In addition, a number of experimental and theoretical investigations have examined product formation, ignition behavior, and reaction kinetics under different operating conditions.19–23 Although these studies have considerably improved the understanding of nitromethane decomposition and ignition, much less attention has been paid to its oxidation chemistry. Oxidation proceeds through a complicated reaction network involving numerous short-lived radicals and oxygen-containing intermediates. The concentrations of species such as H, O, OH, HO2, and H2O2 may change rapidly within extremely short time scales, making direct experimental observation difficult. Quantum chemical calculations can provide detailed information on individual elementary reactions and energy barriers, but the accessible system size and simulation time are often insufficient for describing the collective evolution of large reaction networks in condensed or reactive environments.24,25 As a result, the pathways governing radical generation, intermediate conversion, and product formation during nitromethane oxidation have not yet been fully clarified. A simulation approach capable of tracking bond-breaking and bond-forming processes in large reactive systems is therefore valuable for investigating the oxidation mechanism of nitromethane at the atomic scale.

Reactive force field molecular dynamics (ReaxFF-MD) provides a practical approach for investigating chemical reactions involving continuous bond breaking and bond formation. By incorporating a bond-order-dependent potential into classical molecular dynamics, ReaxFF enables reactive processes to be followed without predefined reaction pathways. The method has been implemented in the LAMMPS simulation package, allowing large-scale simulations of reactive systems with high computational efficiency.26,27 Over the past decade, ReaxFF-MD has been applied to a variety of problems, including combustion chemistry, energetic material decomposition, catalytic reactions, and detonation processes.28–31 Because the evolution of all atoms can be tracked throughout a simulation, information regarding radical production, intermediate conversion, and product formation can be obtained directly from the trajectories.32–36 This capability is particularly useful for reaction systems in which numerous competing pathways coexist. Several studies have employed ReaxFF-MD to examine oxidation reactions of fuels. Yang et al.37 investigated methanol oxidation in the presence of H2O and CO2 impurities and discussed their influence on reaction behavior. Song et al.38 analyzed the effects of dimethyl ether, hydrogen, and ammonia additives on methane combustion. Chen et al.39 further explored the role of CO2 during the combustion of hydrogen/methane mixtures. These studies demonstrated that ReaxFF-MD can provide detailed information on radical evolution and reaction pathway development during oxidation processes. For nitromethane, however, most ReaxFF investigations have concentrated on thermal decomposition and ignition behavior, whereas oxidation has received considerably less attention. In particular, the influences of temperature and oxygen concentration on radical evolution, intermediate transformation, and product formation during nitromethane oxidation are still not well established. Further investigation is therefore needed to clarify the reaction characteristics of nitromethane under different oxidation conditions.

In this work, ReaxFF-MD simulations were performed to investigate the oxidation behavior of nitromethane under different temperature and oxygen concentration conditions. The consumption of reactants, the evolution of major radicals and intermediate species, and the formation of final products were analyzed throughout the reaction process. Particular attention was paid to the effects of temperature and oxygen availability on reaction pathways and species transformation. Based on the simulation results, the dominant reaction routes and the corresponding kinetic characteristics of nitromethane oxidation were discussed. The obtained results provide additional insight into the oxidation chemistry of nitromethane and may be useful for future studies on energetic-fuel combustion and kinetic model development.

Calculation details

ReaxFF-MD repulsive force field

ReaxFF is a reactive force field method capable of describing chemical reaction processes, with its core idea being the dynamic representation of bond formation and breaking between atoms through bond order.40 Unlike traditional non-reactive force fields that require predefined fixed bonding topologies, ReaxFF calculates bond order changes in real time based on interatomic distances, enabling dynamic evolution of chemical bonds. As atoms approach each other, the bond order gradually increases, leading to the formation of stable chemical bonds; as the interatomic distance increases, the bond order decreases progressively until approaching zero, corresponding to bond dissociation.41 Therefore, ReaxFF can continuously describe bond rearrangements during complex chemical reactions at the atomic scale, making it particularly suitable for studying dynamic processes such as radical reactions, molecular fragmentation, and product formation under high-temperature conditions.

In the ReaxFF method, the total energy of the system is composed of several interaction energy terms, including bond energy, overcoordination energy, undercoordination energy, bond angle energy, torsional energy, conjugation energy, van der Waals interaction energy, and electrostatic interaction energy, as expressed below:41,421Etotal = Ebond + Eover + Eunder + Eval + Etors + Econj + EvdW + ECoulomb

Among them, Ebond represents the bond energy generated during the formation and breaking of chemical bonds; Eover and Eunder respectively represent the overcoordination correction energy and the undercoordination correction energy; Eval represents the bond angle deformation energy; Etors represents the dihedral angle torsion energy; Econj represents the conjugation effect energy; EvdW represents the van der Waals interaction energy; ECoulomb represents the electrostatic interaction energy between atoms. By comprehensively considering the contributions of these energy terms, ReaxFF can more accurately describe the dynamic evolution characteristics of the system structure and energy during the reaction process, thereby enabling atomic-scale simulation of complex chemical reaction processes. In this study, the ReaxFF-lg parameter set developed by Liu et al.43 was used for molecular dynamics simulation of the nitro methane oxidation reaction. This parameter set has been systematically optimized for the C/H/O/N element system and has been verified in various organic compound and energetic material reaction systems, and can well describe the processes of bond breaking and formation, radical evolution, and product formation. Therefore, the ReaxFF-lg parameter set can effectively characterize the complex reaction behavior in the nitro methane oxidation process, providing a reliable theoretical basis for revealing its microscopic reaction mechanism and kinetic characteristics. In this work, the ReaxFF-lg parameter set developed by Liu et al.43 was employed to investigate the oxidation mechanism of nitromethane. This parameterization was specifically optimized for reactive C/H/O/N systems by incorporating long-range interaction corrections and extensive training against quantum mechanical calculations together with available experimental data. The research conducted by Song et al.38 indicates that it can accurately simulate the bond dissociation, bond formation, reaction thermodynamics, radical evolution, and product formation processes in hydrocarbons, nitrogen-containing compounds, and high-energy materials. Compared with earlier ReaxFF parameterizations, the Liu parameter set exhibits good transferability and has been widely adopted in simulations involving complex C/H/O/N reactive chemistry. Although no force field has been developed exclusively for nitromethane oxidation, the chemical environment involved in the present system is well represented within the training domain of the Liu parameterization. Therefore, this force field is considered suitable for describing the bond-breaking, radical reactions, and product evolution during nitromethane oxidation.

The simulation cell dimensions were automatically determined according to the number of molecules and the preset density, resulting in box lengths of approximately 37–45 Å depending on the system composition. Previous studies have suggested that the dominant reaction mechanisms are generally preserved when the system density and molecular composition are maintained.37,38

Simulation settings

To approximate bulk-phase reaction conditions, the simulation cell was defined with periodic boundaries in all three dimensions, thereby minimizing the influence of boundary effects on the calculated reaction behavior. The initial configuration of the CH3NO2/O2 mixture system was randomly generated using the Packmol44 software based on the preset molecular composition and system density. To eliminate possible local atomic overlaps and high-energy configurations in the initial structure and obtain a stable initial state, a 50 ps equilibrium calculation was performed at 1000 K.

After the equilibrium was completed, the system was heated to the target reaction temperature. Considering the limited time scale of classical molecular dynamics simulations, to accelerate the reaction process and fully obtain the kinetic evolution information of the system, reaction simulations were conducted under the NVT ensemble at 2400, 2600, 2800, and 3000 K conditions. The motion equation of the system was integrated using the Verlet algorithm,45 with a time step of 0.1 fs, and the total simulation time was 1 ns. Temperature control was achieved using the Nose–Hoover thermostat,46 with a damping parameter of 0.1 ps. To accurately describe the charge distribution and charge transfer behavior during the reaction process, the QEq (Charge equilibration) method47 was used to dynamically calculate the atomic charges. All simulation work was completed on the LAMMPS platform.48 During the simulation, trajectory information was output every 100 time steps, and the structural evolution, species generation, and product distribution during the reaction process were visualized and analyzed using the OVITO software.49 After the simulation, the species evolution process was statistically analyzed using ReaxTools,50 and the reaction network and main reaction paths were constructed using ReacNetGenerator.51

The ReaxFF-lg parameter set developed by Liu et al.43 was selected for the present study because it has been optimized for C/H/O/N-containing energetic materials and has demonstrated applicability in describing bond dissociation, radical evolution, and product formation processes. The parameterization was developed based on quantum mechanical calculations and has been successfully employed in previous studies of energetic material decomposition and oxidation reactions.38

During the ReaxFF simulations, atomic charges were dynamically determined using the charge equilibration (QEq) method. Unlike fixed-charge approaches, QEq allows atomic charges to continuously adjust according to the changing chemical environment, which is essential for describing charge transfer processes during bond breaking and formation.47 Therefore, the combination of ReaxFF-lg and QEq provides an appropriate framework for investigating the microscopic oxidation mechanism of nitromethane.

Rationale for the selection of simulation conditions

The selection of simulation temperature and duration in reactive molecular dynamics is closely related to the intrinsic timescale limitation of atomistic simulations. In this study, the simulation time was set to 1 ns, which is considerably shorter than the characteristic timescale of practical combustion processes. Therefore, elevated temperatures were employed to accelerate bond dissociation, radical generation, and subsequent chemical reactions, enabling the evolution of the reaction network to be captured within a computationally accessible timescale.

According to the Arrhenius relationship, the reaction rate increases exponentially with increasing temperature. Consequently, high-temperature simulations can effectively accelerate chemical transformations and provide sufficient reaction events for analyzing bond-breaking and bond-forming processes. Based on this consideration, a temperature range of 2400–3000 K was selected in the present work. These temperatures should not be regarded as direct representations of practical combustion conditions, but rather as accelerated conditions for investigating the intrinsic microscopic oxidation mechanism of nitromethane.

The influence of elevated temperature on reaction pathways should also be considered. Increasing temperature may alter the relative abundance and lifetime of transient intermediates by changing the rates of competing elementary reactions. However, the formation and consumption of major reactive species are fundamentally governed by the intrinsic C/H/O/N bond rearrangement processes and reaction network. Similar high-temperature reactive molecular dynamics simulations have been widely applied to investigate decomposition and oxidation mechanisms of energetic materials, where the primary objective is to elucidate reaction pathways rather than directly reproduce experimental reaction times.

Therefore, the present simulations are mainly designed to reveal the dominant reaction pathways and the evolution characteristics of key intermediates and products during nitromethane oxidation. Although direct extrapolation of nanosecond-scale simulation results to experimental combustion times remains challenging, the mechanistic information obtained from these simulations provides valuable insights into the elementary reaction processes. Quantitative prediction of combustion kinetics under practical conditions requires further integration with kinetic modeling and experimental validation.

Results and discussion

Pyrolysis mechanism of nitromethane

Reaction path of nitromethane pyrolysis

The carbon and nitrogen migration pathways during nitromethane pyrolysis were constructed based on the bond evolution information obtained from ReaxFF molecular dynamics trajectories. ReacNetGenerator51 was used to analyze bond dissociation and formation events according to bond-order changes, and the dominant reaction pathways were summarized by combining the evolution of reactive species and intermediates.

Fig. 2 illustrates the main carbon and nitrogen element migration paths during the nitromethane pyrolysis process. The relevant elementary reactions are listed in Table 2. As can be seen from the figure, during the nitromethane pyrolysis process, the carbon and nitrogen elements undergo a series of free radical reactions and intermediate body transformation processes, ultimately forming stable products. For the carbon-containing components, CH3NO2 first breaks the C–N bond to generate CH3 free radicals, or undergoes dehydrogenation reactions to form CH2NO2 intermediates. Subsequently, CH3 further converts to HCHO, while CH2NO2 undergoes subsequent reactions to generate intermediates such as CHNO. As the reaction proceeds, HCHO gradually decomposes to form CO, and further participates in subsequent reactions to form the final carbon-containing products. Therefore, the carbon elements mainly migrate and transform along the path “CH3NO2 → CH3/CH2NO2 → HCHO/CHNO → CO”. Among them, CH3, HCHO, and CO are the key intermediates in the carbon element evolution process and have a significant impact on the development of the subsequent reaction network.

Effect of temperature on the main components

Fig. 3 presents the evolution curves of the main species during the nitromethane pyrolysis process at 2400–3000 K. From the figure, it can be seen that temperature has a significant impact on the pyrolysis behavior of nitromethane and the characteristics of product formation. As the temperature increases from 2400 to 3000 K, the consumption rate of nitromethane significantly accelerates, indicating that increasing the temperature can promote the occurrence of the initial pyrolysis reaction. Within the range of 2400–2800 K, the time required for the complete consumption of nitromethane decreases gradually with the increase in temperature. Although the time corresponding to the complete consumption of nitromethane at 3000 K (161 ps) is slightly higher than that at 2800 K (139 ps), the difference between the two is relatively small. Considering that the reaction rate of free radicals increases significantly under high-temperature conditions and the concentration fluctuations of species in the system increase, this phenomenon may be related to the statistical fluctuations and instantaneous reaction fluctuations in the molecular dynamics simulation. From the evolution pattern of the final products, the production amounts of H2, N2, and CO2 all increase with the increase in temperature, indicating that high temperature is conducive to the in-depth progress of the pyrolysis reaction and the formation of stable small molecule products. In contrast, the production amount of H2O shows relatively weak sensitivity to temperature changes, remaining at a similar level overall and showing only minor fluctuations within the range of 2600–3000 K. At 3000 K, the evolution curves of H2 and H2O intersect, meaning that the production amount of H2 exceeds that of H2O. This result indicates that as the temperature further increases, the reaction pathways involving hydrogen radicals in the system change, and high-temperature conditions are more conducive to the generation of H2, while the generation of H2O tends to stabilize, resulting in a gradually increasing difference in yield between the two.

Fig. 4 shows the evolution patterns of the number of H2, N2, H2O and CO2 molecules over time under different temperature conditions. In general, temperature has a significant impact on the generation behavior of each stable gas product. As the temperature increases, the generation rates of all four products significantly increase, and the time required to reach a stable state is shortened accordingly, indicating that high temperature can accelerate the pyrolysis reaction of nitromethane and the subsequent product transformation process. Among all the products, H2 shows the most obvious temperature dependence, and its final molecular number significantly increases with the increase in temperature. This phenomenon indicates that under high-temperature conditions, the C–H bond breaks more fully, the concentration of H radicals in the system increases, thereby promoting the reaction of radical recombination, and ultimately generating more H2 molecules.

(a–d) Time evolution profiles of the major products formed during nitromethane pyrolysis in System 1 (S1) at different temperatures.

For H2O and CO2, their production amounts increase significantly with the increase in temperature within the range of 2400–2800 K, but the increase slows down when the temperature further rises to 3000 K. This indicates that increasing the temperature is conducive to the conversion of hydrogen and carbon elements to stable oxygen-containing products, but the temperature-promoting effect gradually weakens after the reaction reaches a higher temperature. Additionally, at temperatures ranging from 2600 to 3000 K, the evolution curve of H2O shows an intersection phenomenon, meaning that the cumulative rate and final production amount of H2O under different temperature conditions do not exhibit a strict monotonic change pattern. This result indicates that the production process of H2O is affected by multiple competing reactions. Under high-temperature conditions, although the production rate of H2O increases, the rate at which it participates in subsequent reactions or undergoes further transformation also increases simultaneously. Therefore, the net production rate of H2O depends on the dynamic balance between the generation and consumption processes, and the changes in the relative contributions of both at different temperature conditions may be the main reason for the curve intersection phenomenon.

N2 and H2 are two important stable gas products in the pyrolysis process of nitromethane. To investigate the influence of temperature on their generation behavior, the maximum molecular numbers of N2 and H2 reached throughout the reaction process under different temperatures were statistically analyzed. The results are shown in Fig. 5. From the figure, it can be seen that as the temperature increased from 2400 to 3000 K, the maximum molecular numbers of both N2 and H2 showed an increasing trend, indicating that increasing the temperature is conducive to the progress of the nitromethane pyrolysis reaction and the generation of stable gas products. However, the response degrees of the two products to temperature changes are significantly different. Within the temperature range of 2600–3000 K, the maximum molecular number of N2 only showed a slight increase, while the maximum molecular number of H2 significantly increased, indicating that the generation of H2 is more sensitive to temperature changes.

Maximum molecular numbers of N2 and H2 generated during nitromethane pyrolysis in System 1 (S1) under different temperature conditions.

This phenomenon may be related to the different formation mechanisms of the two products. Under high-temperature conditions, the C–H bond breaks more completely, and the concentration of H radicals in the system significantly increases, thereby promoting the reaction of H radicals to combine and generate H2. In contrast, the formation of N2 usually requires a continuous conversion process of multiple nitrogen-containing intermediates, and its generation process is constrained by the reaction pathway and the concentration of intermediates. Therefore, the promotion effect of temperature increase on the generation of N2 is relatively limited.

Effect of temperature on intermediate products

To further elucidate the initial reaction mechanism during the pyrolysis of nitromethane, the evolution patterns of methyl radicals (CH3) under different temperature conditions were statistically analyzed. The results are shown in Fig. 6(a). As can be seen from the figure, at all temperature conditions, the CH3 radicals exhibited a trend of rapid increase followed by a gradual decrease, indicating that they are important transient intermediates in the nitromethane pyrolysis process. As the temperature increased, the peak time of CH3 significantly advanced, suggesting that high temperatures can accelerate the initial pyrolysis reaction of nitromethane and promote the generation of CH3 radicals. From the peak changes, the maximum number of CH3 molecules generally increased with the increase in temperature and reached the maximum at 2800 K. When the temperature further increased to 3000 K, the peak of CH3 slightly decreased and did not continue to increase. This phenomenon indicates that at extremely high temperatures, both the generation and consumption processes of CH3 are significantly intensified. As an important free radical produced during the initial pyrolysis of nitromethane, CH3 not only originates from the C–N bond breakage reaction but can also further participate in subsequent reactions to generate carbon-containing intermediates such as HCHO. With the increase in temperature, although the generation rate of CH3 significantly increased, its subsequent conversion rate also accelerated simultaneously. Therefore, at 3000 K, CH3 is difficult to accumulate continuously in the system, resulting in a peak value slightly lower than 2800 K.

(a and b) Time evolution profiles of CH3 and CH2O species during nitromethane pyrolysis in System 1 (S1) under different temperature conditions.

Formaldehyde (CH2O) is an important oxygen-containing intermediate with a relatively high content during the cracking of nitromethane, and it is also a key intermediate in the process of carbon elements transforming from fuel molecules to small molecular products. Fig. 6(b) shows the evolution law of the number of CH2O molecules over time under different temperature conditions. From the figure, it can be seen that as the temperature increases, the generation rate of CH2O significantly accelerates, and the peak time gradually advances, indicating that high temperature can promote the initial cracking of nitromethane and the generation process of subsequent carbon-containing intermediates. At the same time, the final number of CH2O molecules decreases with the increase in temperature, indicating that high temperature not only promotes its generation but also accelerates its further pyrolysis and transformation process, thus making it difficult for CH2O to accumulate in the system continuously.

From the peak change pattern, within the temperature range of 2400–2800 K, the peak of CH2O gradually decreases with the increase in temperature, indicating that as the reaction temperature increases, the growth rate of CH2O consumption is greater than that of its generation. However, when the temperature further increases to 3000 K, the peak of CH2O shows a certain degree of recovery and is higher than the peak under the 2800 K condition. This phenomenon indicates that under extremely high temperature conditions, the nitromethane cracking reaction accelerates significantly, and a large number of carbon-free radicals and oxygen-containing free radicals are generated in a short period of time, thereby promoting the rapid formation of CH2O. Although its subsequent pyrolysis reaction is also strengthened, the increase in the instantaneous generation rate of CH2O may exceed its consumption rate, thus forming a relatively high instantaneous concentration in the early stage of the reaction, and ultimately resulting in the recovery of the CH2O peak under the 3000 K condition.

Oxidation mechanism of nitromethane

Pathway of nitromethane oxidation reaction

The carbon and nitrogen migration pathways during nitromethane oxidation were analyzed using the same bond-level analysis method described for the pyrolysis process. ReaxFF molecular dynamics trajectories were analyzed by ReacNetGenerator51 to identify bond dissociation and formation events based on bond-order changes. The dominant oxidation pathways were subsequently summarized by combining the bond evolution information with the transformation of reactive species and intermediates. Fig. 7 presents the main reaction pathways involved in the oxidation of nitromethane. The relevant elementary reactions are listed in Table 3. From the reaction network, it can be seen that under high-temperature oxidation conditions, CH3NO2 first undergoes C–N bond cleavage and dehydrogenation reactions, generating key intermediates such as CH3, CH2NO2, and NO2. The formation of these species determines the subsequent migration and transformation processes of carbon and nitrogen elements. For the carbon-containing components, the CH3 radical further undergoes dehydrogenation to generate CH2, and is gradually converted into CO through continuous oxidation reactions, ultimately being oxidized to CO2. At the same time, CH2NO2 can also undergo denitration and oxidation reactions to generate CH2O, which then converts into CHO and other oxygen-containing intermediates, and further participates in the generation process of CO. Therefore, CH3 and CH2NO2 constitute two important initial reaction branches in the nitromethane oxidation process, while CO is the key intermediate in the process of carbon elements transforming from organic intermediates to the final oxidation product CO2.

Effect of oxygen content on the main components

During the oxidation process of nitromethane, different reaction characteristics will be exhibited due to variations in oxygen supply conditions, thereby influencing the generation and evolution behavior of the main products in the system. Fig. 8 shows the changes in the main components of nitromethane over time under conditions of rich fuel, stoichiometric, and lean fuel oxidation at 3000 K. As can be seen from the figure, in different oxidation conditions, the consumption rate of nitromethane is higher than that of oxygen. Within a 1 ns simulation time, nitromethane is completely consumed, while oxygen still has some residual in the stoichiometric condition. Specifically, under the rich fuel condition, nitromethane is completely consumed at 168 ps; under the stoichiometric condition, its complete consumption time is shortened to 121 ps; and under the lean fuel condition, nitromethane is completely consumed at 147 ps. The results indicate that the change in oxygen content will affect the oxidation rate of nitromethane and also alter the generation behavior of the main products in the system.

Taking the oxidation process under rich fuel conditions as an example (Fig. 8(a)), the nitromethane oxidation reaction can be roughly divided into three stages. In the first stage (0–50 ps), nitromethane begins to be consumed rapidly, and O2 begins to participate in the reaction at approximately 13 ps, while H2 and H2O begin to be generated in the system. The consumption rates of nitromethane and O2 are both high within this stage, indicating that the initial cracking and oxidation reactions in the system are relatively intense. In the second stage, nitromethane continues to be consumed, but its consumption rate significantly decreases; at the same time, the generation rate of H2O increases and is accompanied by certain fluctuations, CO2 begins to be generated, and the number of H2 molecules also shows a relatively obvious fluctuation characteristic. In the third stage, nitromethane has been completely consumed, and the consumption rate of O2 further decreases, and the system mainly undergoes intermediate body transformation and free radical recombination reactions, and the number of H2 and H2O molecules gradually stabilize in fluctuation. Overall, the participation of O2 has a significant impact on the generation of main products such as H2, H2O, and CO2, and its consumption process is closely related to the progress of the oxidation reaction in the system.

Fig. 9 shows the final consumption of O2 and the generation of CO2, H2, H2O and N2 under different oxidation conditions. From the figure, it can be seen that in the rich fuel and stoichiometric conditions, O2 is completely consumed, while in the lean fuel condition, there is still some O2 remaining, indicating that when the oxygen supply exceeds the reaction demand of the system, the additional oxygen cannot be fully utilized, and the utilization rate of oxygen by the system is reduced. As the initial O2 content increases, the generation of CO2 and H2O gradually increases, while the generation of H2 and N2 shows a downward trend. This indicates that increasing the oxygen concentration is beneficial for enhancing the oxidation ability of the system, promoting the further conversion of carbon and hydrogen intermediates into stable oxidation products CO2 and H2O, thereby reducing the generation of incomplete oxidation products such as H2. At the same time, in the oxygen-rich condition, the concentration of active oxygen species in the system increases, making some nitrogen intermediates more inclined to convert to oxidized nitrogen species, thereby reducing the proportion of nitrogen elements migrating to N2. Therefore, oxygen content not only influences the oxidation degree of nitromethane, but also plays an important role in determining the migration pathways of carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen elements, as well as the evolution characteristics of final products.

Reaction rate of reactants and product formation of the five main components in different systems (S2, S3, S4) within the range of 0–1 ns at 3000 K yield.

Fig. 10 shows the variation patterns of the main products within the temperature range of 2400–3000 K under different oxidation conditions. From the figure, it can be seen that nitromethane undergoes a significant oxidation reaction under all temperature conditions, and the generation amounts of CO2, H2, and N2 increase overall with the increase in temperature, indicating that high temperature is conducive to the progress of the reaction and the formation of stable products. For H2O, its temperature response characteristics are closely related to the oxidation conditions of the system. In the rich fuel system, the yield of H2O at 3000 K is only slightly higher than that at 2400 K under the same conditions, indicating that the increase in temperature does not continuously promote the accumulation of H2O. This may be related to the participation of H2O in subsequent reactions under high-temperature conditions, as the concentrations of active free radicals such as H, O, and OH in the system increase significantly with further temperature increase, and the already generated H2O is more likely to re-participate in the radical reaction process, thereby weakening its net generation amount. In the lean fuel system, the generation curves of H2O at 2800 K and 3000 K are relatively close, and the two curves reach the same number of H2O molecules at 761 ps, then continue to increase and stabilize around 1000 ps, showing only slight fluctuations, indicating that in the presence of sufficient oxygen, further increasing the temperature has limited promotion effect on the final generation amount of H2O. In addition, the response of H2 generation to temperature changes shows significant differences under different oxidation conditions. In the rich fuel system, the H2 yield at 3000 K is almost twice that at 2800 K, indicating that high temperature significantly promotes the generation of H2; while in the stoichiometric and lean fuel systems, the differences in H2 yield under different temperature conditions are small. This is because in the oxygen-deficient condition, increasing the temperature can promote the generation of free radicals and their complex reactions, thereby increasing the generation of H2; while in the stoichiometric and lean fuel conditions, the sufficient oxidant in the system makes the hydrogen-containing free radicals more likely to further convert into H2O, so H2 is difficult to accumulate significantly, and its yield changes relatively little under different temperatures.

The curves showing the number of main product molecules as a function of time at 2400 K, 2600 K, 2800 K, and 3000 K correspond to fuel-rich (S2), stoichiometric (S3), and fuel-lean (S4) conditions, respectively.

Fig. 11 shows the changes in the maximum number of N2 molecules during the nitromethane oxidation process under different oxidation conditions. Under fuel-rich conditions, the maximum number of N2 molecules at 2400–3000 K is 9, 16, 21, and 29; under stoichiometric conditions, it is 5, 11, 17, and 25; and under fuel-poor conditions, it is 3, 4, 7, and 10. From the figure, it can be seen that the maximum number of N2 molecules under the three oxidation conditions increases with the increase in temperature, indicating that high temperature is conducive to the formation of N2. This is mainly because increasing the temperature promotes the initial pyrolysis of nitromethane and the generation and transformation of nitrogen-containing intermediates such as NO and NO2, increasing the collision frequency between active nitrogen-containing species in the system, thereby facilitating the formation of N–N bonds and N2. At the same temperature, the overall N2 production shows a pattern of fuel-rich system > stoichiometric system > fuel-poor system. Taking 3000 K as an example, N2 formation reaches maximum values of 29, 25, and 10 molecules under fuel-rich, stoichiometric, and fuel-lean conditions, respectively. With the increase in oxygen content, the N2 production gradually decreases, indicating that the oxidation atmosphere has a significant influence on the migration path of nitrogen elements. This is because excess oxygen promotes the generation and accumulation of nitrogen-containing oxides such as NO and NO2, causing some nitrogen-containing intermediates to be more inclined to transform into oxidized nitrogen species rather than further undergoing N–N coupling reactions to form N2, thereby suppressing the formation of N2 to a certain extent.

Fig. 12 shows the variation of the maximum number of H2 molecules during the nitromethane oxidation process under different oxidation conditions. Under fuel-rich conditions, the maximum number of H2 molecules at 2400–3000 K is 6, 14, 20, and 41; under stoichiometric conditions, it is 6, 9, 11, and 15; and under fuel-poor conditions, it is 5, 9, 6, and 11. A temperature-dependent increase in H2 formation is observed for both the fuel-rich and stoichiometric mixtures, indicating that high temperatures can promote the cracking of nitromethane and the generation and recombination reactions of hydrogen-containing radicals, thereby increasing the H2 yield. For the fuel-poor system, the maximum production of H2 shows an overall upward trend, but it drops briefly at 2800 K. This phenomenon indicates that in the oxygen-rich condition, there is a significant competitive relationship between the generation and consumption of H2. As the temperature increases from 2600 K to 2800 K, the concentration of active oxygen species in the system increases, making H2 and its precursor radicals more prone to oxidation reactions, thereby weakening the accumulation of H2 and resulting in a maximum production of H2 lower than 2600 K. When the temperature further increases to 3000 K, the cracking rate of nitromethane and the generation rate of hydrogen-containing radicals increase significantly, and the generation rate of H2 exceeds its oxidation consumption rate, so the maximum production of H2 increases again. Moreover, under the same temperature conditions, the H2 production amount generally follows the pattern of fuel-rich system > stoichiometric system > fuel-poor system, and this difference gradually increases with the increase in temperature, indicating that the oxygen content has a significant impact on H2 production. In the oxygen-deficient condition, more hydrogen-containing radicals are generated through complex reactions to form H2; while as the oxygen concentration increases, hydrogen-containing radicals are more inclined to further oxidize to form H2O, thereby inhibiting the generation and accumulation of H2.

Effect of oxygen content on intermediate products

Among the transient species observed during nitromethane oxidation, CH3 and HCHO constitute the primary carbon-containing intermediates, whereas OH, HO2, and H2O2 are the predominant reactive oxygen species. Among them, CH3, as the most primary carbon free radical product, plays an important role in the reaction network in terms of its generation and subsequent transformation (such as the evolution to CH2 and oxygen-containing intermediates). Fig. 13 shows the evolution curve of the number of CH3 over time. Under fuel-rich conditions, the maximum number of CH3 molecules at 2400–3000 K is 34, 39, 44 and 51 respectively. From the figure, it can be seen that at 2400 K, the time when CH3 reaches its maximum value is 138 ps, while at 2600–3000 K, the time when it reaches the peak value is 48 ps, 40 ps and 27 ps respectively. The results show that as the temperature increases, the generation process of CH3 radicals significantly accelerates, the peak occurrence time gradually advances, and the time for it to decay to nearly zero also shortens accordingly, indicating that the reaction rate of radicals under high-temperature conditions significantly enhances, and the system enters the subsequent reaction stage more quickly. From the fluctuation characteristics, the number of CH3 changes relatively smoothly at 3000 K, while it is more obvious at 2400 K; similar patterns are also observed under stoichiometric and fuel-poor conditions, indicating that temperature has a dominant role in the dynamic evolution of CH3 radicals, while the influence of oxygen content on its generation characteristics is relatively weak.

Formaldehyde (HCHO/CH2O) is the most abundant intermediate in the nitromethane oxidation reaction. Fig. 14 shows the curve of the change in the quantity of CH2O under different temperatures and oxygen content conditions. In the rich fuel system, CH2O begins to be generated at 39 ps at a temperature of 2400 K, and at 20 ps, 18 ps, and 15 ps respectively at 2600–3000 K, indicating that the increase in temperature promotes the generation of CH2O. The same phenomenon was also observed in the stoichiometric and lean fuel systems. From the figure, it can also be observed that the quantity of CH2O shows a trend of increasing first and then decreasing under the conditions of 2400–3000 K, and the higher the temperature, the earlier the decrease in the quantity of CH2O occurs, indicating that increasing the temperature promotes the further conversion of CH2O.

In the early stage of nitromethane oxidation, molecular oxygen participates in hydrogen abstraction reactions, leading to the formation of reactive oxygen-containing species, including OH, HO2, and H2O2. Fig. 15 shows the relationship between the maximum quantity of intermediate oxidants and temperature, as well as the initial O2 content. From Fig. 15, it can be seen that the formation of OH, HO2, and H2O2 intermediates is all influenced by temperature and the initial O2 content, but the degree of influence varies significantly.

(a–c) Peak abundances of OH, HO2, and H2O2 as functions of temperature at different oxygen levels. (S2, S3, S4).

For the OH radical (Fig. 15(a)), its maximum quantity generally increases with the rise in temperature. Under rich fuel conditions, the maximum quantity of OH only increases from 7 to 8, with a relatively small change; while under stoichiometric and lean fuel conditions, the quantity of OH increases significantly with the rise in temperature, reaching 13 and 15 at 3000 K respectively. This indicates that increasing the temperature is conducive to the formation of reactive oxygen radicals, and adequate oxygen supply further promotes the accumulation of OH. Therefore, the OH formation is most significant in the lean fuel system.

For the HO2 radical (Fig. 15(b)), its maximum quantity also increases with the increase in oxygen content. In lean fuel conditions, the quantity of HO2 is always the highest, reaching a peak of 10 at 2800 K. In contrast, in rich fuel conditions, the quantity of HO2 remains at a relatively low level. This indicates that the generation of HO2 is more sensitive to oxygen concentration, and excessive oxygen can promote the reaction of H radicals with O2 to form HO2, while insufficient oxygen limits this reaction.

For the H2O2 molecule (Fig. 15(c)), its variation pattern is closely related to HO2. Under rich fuel conditions, the quantity of H2O2 is always low, only 3–4; it slightly increases under stoichiometric conditions; and it reaches 10 at 2800 K and 3000 K under lean fuel conditions, significantly higher than other systems. Since H2O2 mainly comes from the reaction of HO2 radicals, its generation trend is basically the same as HO2, reflecting that a rich oxygen environment is more conducive to the accumulation of peroxides.

Overall, as the initial O2 content increases, the maximum quantities of OH, HO2, and H2O2 intermediates all show an upward trend. Among them, HO2 and H2O2 respond most significantly to the oxygen concentration. This indicates that excessive oxygen not only directly participates in the oxidation reaction but also promotes the formation of active oxygen-containing intermediates such as HO2 and H2O2, enhancing the oxidation ability of the system and accelerating the conversion of nitromethane and its pyrolysis products to final oxidation products.

Conclusions

ReaxFF-MD simulations were conducted to examine the oxidation behavior of nitromethane over a range of temperatures and oxygen concentrations, with particular attention given to the underlying reaction pathways and species evolution. It revealed the main reaction pathways, the evolution patterns of intermediates, and the characteristics of product formation. The following conclusions were drawn:

(1) In the nitromethane pyrolysis reaction, the carbon element mainly transforms along the path “CH3NO2 → CH3/CH2NO2 → HCHO/CHNO → CO → CO2”, while the nitrogen element mainly transforms along the paths “CH3NO2 → CH2NO2 → CHNO → CNO → NO → N2” and “CH3NO2 → NO2 → HNO2 → NO → N2”. In the oxidation reaction, the carbon element mainly transforms along the paths “CH3NO2 → CH3 → CH2 → CO → CO2” and “CH3NO2 → CH2NO2 → CH2O → CHO → CO → CO2”, and the nitrogen element mainly transforms along the path “CH3NO2 → NO2 → NO → N2O → N2”.

(2) An increase in temperature significantly promotes the oxidation reaction of nitromethane, accelerating the reaction rate and the conversion process of intermediates, resulting in a varying degree of increase in the yield of the product. The activation energy of the nitromethane pyrolysis reaction is 35.87 kcal mol−1. Under conditions of rich fuel, stoichiometric, and lean fuel, the activation energies for oxidation are 32.20 kcal mol−1, 48.15 kcal mol−1, and 41.74 kcal mol−1, respectively. Under stoichiometric conditions, the nitromethane oxidation reaction is the most difficult to occur.

(3) The oxygen content has a significant influence on the evolution of oxidation products. As the initial O2 content increases, the formation tendency of CO2 and H2O is enhanced, whereas the relative concentrations of H2 and N2 decrease. These results indicate that oxygen-rich conditions promote deeper oxidation of carbon- and hydrogen-containing intermediates and alter the migration pathways of carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen elements during nitromethane oxidation.

(4) The formation of reactive oxygen intermediates such as OH, HO2 and H2O2 is influenced by both temperature and oxygen concentration. Among them, HO2 and H2O2 are the most sensitive to changes in oxygen concentration. Excessive oxygen enhances the oxidation ability of the system by promoting the formation of reactive oxygen intermediates, thereby accelerating the transformation of nitromethane and its pyrolysis products into stable oxidation products.
