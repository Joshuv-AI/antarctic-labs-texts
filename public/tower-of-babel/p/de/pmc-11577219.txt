The Surprise Question in Hemodialysis, Frailty, Nutrition, Patient-reported Quality of Life, and All-Cause Mortality: The Osaka Dialysis Complication Study (ODCS)
Authors: Tetsuo Shoji, Daijiro Kabata, Seiichi Kimura, Yuki Nagata, Katsuhito Mori, Shinya Nakatani, Hisako Fujii, Tomoaki Morioka, Masanori Emoto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveA response “no” (SQ-No) to the surprise question (SQ) of whether a clinician would be surprised if a dialysis patient died in the next 6 months is associated with a higher risk of all-cause death. It is uncertain what domains are intuitively assessed with the SQ. We hypothesized that the SQ would assess the patient’s frailty, malnutrition, or patient-perceived health-related quality of life in a cohort of patients on maintenance hemodialysis.Study DesignCohort study.Setting & ParticipantsA multicenter study including 994 patients on maintenance hemodialysis in Japan.Predictors(1) SQ answered by nurses; (2) frailty by modified Cardiovascular Health Study criteria; (3) malnutrition as evaluated by Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI); and (4) patient-perceived health-related quality of life examined by the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) physical component summary (PCS).OutcomesAll-cause mortality.Analytical ApproachCox proportional hazard models.ResultsMedian age and dialysis vintage were 66 and 5.9 years, respectively, 35.8% were women, and 39.6% had diabetic kidney disease. The prevalence of SQ-No and frailty was 19.7% and 45.9%. Median GNRI and SF-36 PCS scores were 96.3 and 36.9, respectively. During the 5-year follow-up, 247 patients died. SQ-No, being frail, low GNRI, and low SF-36 PCS were each significant predictors of a higher risk for mortality independent of potential confounders. SQ-No remained a significant predictor after further adjustment for frailty or GNRI, but SQ-No was no longer significant when adjusted for SF-36 PCS.LimitationsWe did not assess the agreement of responses to the SQ between different raters.ConclusionsThe predictive ability of the SQ was closely related to SF-36 PCS in hemodialysis patients. Nurses’ answer to the SQ appears to assess the physical domain of patient-perceived health-related quality of life rather than objectively assessed frailty or malnutrition.Index Words: Frailty, Hemodialysis, Nutrition, Quality of life, Surprise question

Introduction

“Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next 6 months?” This question posed to medical staff is called the “surprise question” (SQ), and the answer “no” (SQ-No) has been shown to predict a higher risk of mortality in patients in emergency medicine,1,2 palliative care,3,4 and nephology.5, 6, 7, 8 There are several versions of the SQ that differ in prognostication time frames (3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years).2, 3, 4, 5,9,10 In contrast to the use of predictive models constructed by multiple objective variables, the SQ is intuitive, simple, and quick to perform. It may be useful in choosing treatment strategies such as proactive and/or invasive treatment versus conservative treatment. However, it is unknown what is assessed by the SQ.

Medical staff know various information on a patient that can predict mortality such as a patient’s status of frailty11, 12, 13, 14 and malnutrition or protein-energy wasting.15, 16, 17, 18 In addition, patient-perceived health-related quality of life is a predictor of mortality in dialysis patients.19, 20, 21 Health care professionals, such as nurses who are in charge of care for a patient, have sympathy and empathy for the patient through repeated conversation during dialysis practice, so they may be able to perceive what their patients perceive by intuition. The value of expert intuition has been previously discussed.22 Therefore, we hypothesized that the SQ would assess frailty, nutritional status, and/or patient-perceived quality of life.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This was a prospective cohort study in patients undergoing hemodialysis who participated in the Osaka Dialysis Complication Study (ODCS). ODCS included 1,696 patients on maintenance hemodialysis from 17 dialysis facilities in Osaka Prefecture, Japan.23 This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee, Osaka City University Graduate School of Medicine, Osaka, Japan (Approval No. 2219) and registered at UMIN-CTR (UMIN000007470). All the participants gave written informed consent before participation to this study. The revised protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee, Osaka Metropolitan University Graduate School of Medicine, Osaka, Japan (Approval No. 2021-029).

From all ODCS participants, we selected patients eligible for this study by excluding those who were missing key variables including SQ response, frailty, nutritional status, and patient-perceived quality of life as defined below.

SQ

In this study, we used the 6-month version of the SQ, namely “Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next 6 months?” Before this study, the nurses were instructed to answer the SQ intuitively. For each patient, the SQ was completed by the nurse who was in charge of care of the patient at the time of SQ administration.

Assessment of Frailty

We assessed frailty phenotype basically according to the Cardiovascular Health Study criteria by Fried et al24 with some modifications. Originally, frailty was defined as a clinical syndrome in which ≥3 of the following 5 criteria are present: weight loss (10 pounds in the past year), weakness, slowness, self-reported exhaustion, and low physical activity. In the ODCS, weight loss was diagnosed, and 1 point was given if body weight decreased by ≥2.0 kg in the 6 months before baseline according to the revised Japanese version of the Cardiovascular Health Study criteria.25 Regarding weakness and slowness, 2 points were given if the physical function scale of the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) was <75 according to Johansen et al.26 Regarding exhaustion, 1 point was given if the vitality scale of the SF-36 was <55.26 We used the Japanese-translated version of the SF-36 as described below.27, 28, 29 Regarding low physical activity, 1 point was given if the patient reported that they “never” or “almost never” exercised in response to a questionnaire asking about the frequency of regular exercise. The total points for frailty ranged from 0-5 points. Patients with ≥3 points were defined as frail, and the others were defined as non-frail.

Assessment of Patient-perceived Quality of Life

We used the SF-36, which was originally developed by Ware and Sherbourne35 and translated into Japanese and validated by Fukuhara et al.27,28 In the original SF-36, the self-administered responses of the patient to the 36 health-related questions are used to calculate 8 subscales including physical functioning (PF), limitations on role functioning because of physical health (RP), bodily pain (BP), general health (GH), mental health (MH), limitations on role functioning because of emotional problems (RE), social functioning (SF), and vitality (VT). The original version provides 2 component summary scores, namely the physical component summary (PCS) and the mental component summary (MCS) scores. The model developed later, which includes a third component, the role/social component summary (RCS), performed better than the 2-component model in Japanese (SF-36v2).29 Therefore, we used SF-36v2 in Japanese obtained from iHope International, Kyoto, Japan.

Covariables

We considered the following 4 groups of potential confounders: (1) major demographic factors (age, sex, dialysis vintage, absence or presence of diabetic kidney disease, prior cardiovascular disease, prior hospitalization for infection, and prior diagnosis of malignancy); (2) traditional vascular risk factors (current smoking, hypertension, and dyslipidemia); (3) factors related to renal anemia (blood hemoglobin, serum ferritin, transferrin saturation, use of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, and use of intravenous iron preparations); and (4) factors related to chronic kidney disease (CKD)–mineral and bone disorder (serum-corrected calcium, phosphate, intact parathyroid hormone, use of phosphate binders, use of vitamin D receptor activators, and use of calcimimetics). We did not include factors related to malnutrition and protein-energy wasting in the covariables because GNRI was one of the key exposures in this study.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized and compared by the responses to the SQ (SQ-No vs SQ-Yes). Continuous variables were summarized as medians (interquartile ranges), and comparison between groups was performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as numbers (percentages), and comparison between groups was performed using the χ2 test.

Survival curves were estimated for those patients with SQ-No and SQ-Yes by Kaplan-Meier method, and the difference was assessed using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (95% confidence intervals) were separately calculated for SQ response (SQ-No vs SQ-Yes), frailty (frailty vs non-frailty), GNRI (per interquartile range), and SF-36 PCS (per interquartile range) using multivariable Cox proportional hazard models with and without adjustment for the abovementioned potential confounders. Finally, we calculated hazard ratios (95% confidence intervals) for SQ response, which were further adjusted for GNRI or SF-36 PCS in addition to the abovementioned covariables.

To explore the possibility that the prediction of mortality by the SQ response was related to the mental and/or role/social components of patient-perceived quality of life, we performed additional analyses in which SF-36 PCS was replaced with SF-36 MCS or SF-36 RCS using the same covariables. Similarly, we conducted further analysis in which SF-36 PCS was replaced with each of the 8 subscales of SF-36 using the same model.

All calculations were conducted using the statistical software R version 4.2.1. A 2-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study aimed to address whether the SQ assesses frailty, malnutrition, and patient-perceived health-related quality of life in a cohort of hemodialysis patients. Compared with the patients in the SQ-Yes group, patients in the SQ-No group were more likely to be frail, malnourished, and have a perceived lower quality of life, suggesting a close relationship between these variables. Also, these 4 predictors were significantly associated with mortality. However, the results indicated that SQ-No predicted mortality independent of frailty and malnutrition. In contrast, the association of SQ-No with mortality was no longer significant when adjusted for the association between SF-36 PCS and mortality. Thus, responses to the SQ by nurses appeared to assess patient-perceived health-related quality of life rather than objectively assessed frailty or malnutrition.

There are several studies regarding the SQ and survival in patients with CKD including those undergoing hemodialysis. Moss et al5 showed that hemodialysis patients in the SQ-No group were more likely to be older and have a higher comorbidity score, lower performance status, and higher risk for early mortality. The utility of the SQ was also shown for patients on peritoneal dialysis7 and those with non–dialysis-dependent CKD stages 4-5.8 Da Salva Gane et al6 confirmed that the SQ predicted mortality in hemodialysis patients, although the predictive power was different among rating clinicians. Malhotra et al9 showed that the prediction by the SQ had lower sensitivity than a prediction model in hemodialysis patients. All these studies agree that the SQ can be used as a screening tool for mortality risk in patients with advanced stages of CKD, although these studies did not answer what characteristic of the patient was assessed by the SQ. Thus, this study is the first to address this question.

Frailty is prevalent in patients with advanced stages of CKD including those treated with dialysis treatment, and frailty predicts a higher risk of mortality in these patients.14,36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44 We hypothesized that the SQ answered by nurses would be assessing frailty. This study showed the association of the SQ with mortality was independent of the presence of frailty, although the association of the SQ with mortality was reduced to some extent when adjusted for frailty. Thus, the SQ appears to have some relationship with frailty, but there are other factors that could explain this association in this population.

It is well known that malnutrition predicts mortality.15,45, 46, 47, 48 Several terminologies have been developed to describe that “lean” kidney failure patients have a higher risk of mortality, such as malnutrition-inflammation-atherosclerosis syndrome,15 malnutrition-inflammation complex syndrome,45 uremic malnutrition,46 and uremic cachexia.49 These terms were later harmonized as protein-energy wasting.17 Because GNRI is calculated from serum albumin and body weight, it can be regarded as one of the simple indexes for protein-energy wasting. We then hypothesized that the SQ would assess protein-energy wasting and used GNRI as an index for it. Although the SQ-No group had a significantly lower GNRI than the SQ-Yes group, the association between the SQ and mortality was independent of GNRI. Thus, the results suggest that the SQ is assessing not only patients’ protein-energy wasting, but also some other aspects of health.

The most important finding of this study was that the association between the SQ and mortality was more closely related to patient-perceived health-related quality of life as measured by SF-36 PCS than frailty or GNRI. The SQ-No group had a significantly lower SF-36 PCS, and the association between the SQ and mortality was not independent of SF-36 PCS. These results suggest that the SQ and SF-36 PCS are very similar in their abilities to predict mortality. According to previous studies in hemodialysis patients, SF-36 subscales PF and VT can be used as the alternatives for “weakness and slowness” and “exhaustion,” respectively, in the assessment of frailty.26 Also, SF-36 is known to correlate with malnutritional status.47 These facts suggest that SF-36 PCS is a comprehensive assessment of physical health including frailty and malnutrition. Our results also suggest that the association between SQ-No and mortality was partly explained by frailty and protein-energy wasting. Therefore, it is presumable that SQ and SF-36 PCS are similar in that both of them not only assess frailty and nutritional status but also other broader aspects of physical health.

How is the SQ related to the mental and role/social components of patient-perceived quality of life? To address this question, we made additional analyses in which SF-36 PCS was replaced with SF-36 MCS or SF-36 RCS using the same model. The results indicated that the association between the SQ and mortality was independent of SF-36 MCS or SF-36 RCS, suggesting that the important components were in the physical domain of SF-36. We then conducted further analyses in which SF-36 PCS was replaced with each of the 8 subscales of SF-36 using the same model. We found that the association between the SQ and mortality was not significant when SF-36 PCS was replaced with SF-36 PF or SF-36 RP, although the association between the SQ and mortality remained significant when SF-36 PCS was replaced with each of the other 6 subscales. These data support the interpretation that the SQ could be mainly assessing the physical domain of patient-perceived health-related quality of life in the prediction of mortality.

Because the SQ was answered by a nurse and the SQ response was only in the mind of the nurse, the SQ itself did not affect the patient’s perceived health-related quality of life or mortality. Therefore, it is difficult to assume the causal relationship between the SQ and SF-36 PCS and between the SQ and mortality. Nonetheless, the SQ predicted mortality in a similar manner to SF-36 PCS. We have no clear explanation for this, but we speculate that nurses were able to perceive what their patients perceive through repeated conversation and observations during daily practice on maintenance hemodialysis. If so, the SQ answer of a nurse could serve as a proxy of the patient’s perceived health-related quality of life. As previously reported,6 the predictive power of the SQ varies among raters, which may be explained by the difference in the degree of the raters’ knowledge, experience, sympathy, empathy, and ability to perceive what their patients perceive.

This study showed that SF-36 PCS had the ability to predict mortality risk independently of the SQ and that SF-36 PCS appeared to be superior to the SQ. Presumably, this is explained by the direct response to the questionnaire by the patient in SF-36 versus an indirect assessment by the nurse in response to the SQ and/or by the difference between the SQ as a binary variable versus SF-36 PCS as a continuous scale. Taking a few minutes to administer SF-36 could provide more accurate risk prediction than a quick and simple SQ.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we did not assess the agreement of SQ responses by different raters, and we did not record the characteristics of the nurses who responded to the SQ such as years of experience and whether they had any advanced degree beyond registered nurse. Because the predictive ability of the SQ was shown to be different among rating clinicians,6 the lack of such information might have affected the interrater reliability and introduced concern about potential contamination. In addition, the length of time that the patient had been seeing the nurse at the time of administration of the SQ might have affected the results. Second, frailty phenotype was diagnosed using a modified version of the original Cardiovascular Health Study criteria, not the original version. This might have affected the results. Third, GNRI was used as an index for protein-energy wasting. Other metrics for protein-energy wasting might have provided different results. Fourth, although we used the binary version of the SQ, the use of a 3- or 5-point Likert scale version might have given results with higher granularity. Fifth, we examined the association between the SQ response and all-cause mortality, but we did not have outcome data regarding clinical decisions on the management of end-stage kidney diseases such as goals of care, conservative care, transplantation referral, and permanent access replacement. Such information would be more important if the SQ is administered before kidney replacement therapy is considered. Sixth, although the association between the SQ response and all-cause mortality was adjusted by as many as 21 potential confounders such as prior cardiovascular disease, including coronary, cerebral, and peripheral artery disease, the model was not adjusted for the presence or absence of dementia.50 This might have affected the results. On the other hand, this study is the first that tried to address what is assessed by the SQ, at least in patients undergoing hemodialysis. This is one of the study’s strengths.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we showed that the association between the SQ response and mortality was independent of frailty or nutritional status, but it was not independent of SF-36 PCS in a cohort of patients on maintenance hemodialysis. Since the association between the SQ response and mortality was not fully explained by SF-36 MCS or SF-36 RCS, we interpret the results to suggest that the SQ is assessing or is closely related to the physical domain of patient-perceived health-related quality of life in this population.
