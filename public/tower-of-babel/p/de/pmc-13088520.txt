Multifunctional MoS₂-PMMA bone cement with enhanced strength and antibacterial activity to overcome limitations of conventional materials in orthopedic surgery
Authors: Changsheng Gong, ShengBo Shi, ZiJing Zhang, ZeTian Zhao, Zuo Liu, Zhe Wang, Xiaobing Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsThe increasing incidence of osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) necessitates the development of effective treatment strategies. Polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) bone cement, which is widely used, lacks adequate antimicrobial properties and poses the risk of postoperative infections. Molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂) nanosheets, which are known for their antimicrobial and osteogenic potential, offer a novel approach.MethodsWe synthesized PMMA-MoS₂ nanocomposites by incorporating MoS₂ nanosheets into PMMA bone cement. The mechanical properties of the composites were evaluated using tensile tests. Additionally, finite element analysis was conducted to simulate the stress distribution in the spine after vertebroplasty. Osteoblast viability, differentiation, and maturation were assessed using the CCK-8 assay, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining, and alizarin red S (ARS) staining. As assessed by CCK-8 assays, the presence of MoS₂ led to a marked increase in osteoblast proliferation, with cell viability consistently exceeding 100% at all time points. ALP staining demonstrated a concentration-dependent enhancement in osteoblast differentiation, with the PMMA + 5%MoS₂ composite showing the highest ALP activity. Moreover, the ARS-stained area expanded as the MoS₂ concentration increased, indicating a pronounced increase in the formation of mineralized nodules. Antimicrobial testing confirmed that the PMMA-MoS₂ composites substantially reduced Escherichia coli counts, with the PMMA + 5%MoS₂ composite exhibiting the most potent effect.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that PMMA-MoS₂ nanocomposites offer improved mechanical, osteogenic, and antimicrobial properties, presenting a promising material for orthopedic applications.Keywords: PMMA bone cement, MoS₂ nanosheet, Osteogenic property, Antimicrobial activity, Osteogenic activity, Finite element analysis

Introduction

In recent years, the substantial increase in life expectancy and growth of the aging population have led to an increasing number of patients requiring treatment for OVCFs [1]. Among spinal fractures, osteoporotic thoracolumbar vertebral compression fractures are particularly prevalent, accounting for 90% of all spinal fractures, with compression fractures comprising 50% of these cases [2, 3]. Chronic low back pain and kyphosis, among other complications, not only diminish patients’ quality of life but also elevate their risk of mortality [4, 5]. Percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) has emerged as the most widely adopted surgical approach for OVCFs, reinforcing vertebral strength and stability through the injection of bone cement into the affected vertebrae, thereby achieving fracture fixation [6].

PMMA bone cement is the gold standard in clinical practice owing to its favorable injectability and mechanical properties and is widely recognized for its safety and efficacy in treating OVCFs [7]. Despite its excellent physical and mechanical properties and biocompatibility, the inadequate antimicrobial properties of PMMA bone cement pose a significant challenge in mitigating the risk of postoperative infections, thereby hindering further clinical development. Recent research suggests that local antibiotic delivery via antibiotic-loaded bone cement (ALBC) can achieve significantly higher antibiotic concentrations at the infection site than systemic antibiotic therapy [8]. Currently, bone cements loaded with vancomycin, gentamicin, tobramycin, or clindamycin have been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, making PMMA-based antibiotic-eluting bone cement the gold standard in orthopedic surgery for ALBC [9, 10]. However, increasing bacterial resistance, particularly against commonly used antibiotics such as gentamicin by Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis, has severely compromised the clinical efficacy of ALBC [11]. Furthermore, the incorporation of antibiotics may lead to decreased mechanical strength and prolonged setting times, further limiting their clinical application [11]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop ALBC that contain novel antimicrobials.

The ongoing convergence of materials science and biomedical engineering has propelled the development of novel medical materials as crucial pathways to enhance clinical outcomes and improve patients’ quality of life. Among these, two-dimensional nanomaterials, with their unique physicochemical properties and promising biomedical applications, have garnered considerable attention. As emerging inorganic antimicrobial agents, MoS₂ nanosheets exhibit superior antimicrobial activity and good biocompatibility [12]. By leveraging their distinct layered structure and large specific surface area, MoS₂ nanosheets effectively interact with bacterial cell walls, achieving the efficient eradication of various bacteria through mechanisms such as disrupting bacterial membranes and inhibiting bacterial DNA replication [13, 14]. Notably, MoS₂ nanosheets exhibit low cytotoxicity and minimal impact on normal cells, bolstering their applicability in the biomedical field [15].

Moreover, MoS₂ not only possesses remarkable antimicrobial properties but also demonstrates excellent biocompatibility and osteogenic properties. Studies have revealed that the incorporation of MoS₂ into composite materials significantly enhances the mechanical strength of scaffolds and promotes the differentiation of stem cells into osteoblasts, thereby strengthening the structural support for tissue regeneration [16]. Furthermore, MoS₂ nanosheets can be produced via liquid exfoliation using various biocompatible exfoliants, such as bovine serum albumin and keratin, further enhancing their feasibility and safety in biomedical applications [17].

To date, there have been no reports on the fabrication of MoS₂ nanosheet-integrated bone cement or its application in orthopedic surgeries. Consequently, this study aimed to develop an antimicrobial bone cement, PMMA-MoS₂, incorporating MoS₂ nanosheets as a novel antimicrobial agent, and to evaluate its mechanical properties and antimicrobial activity. By applying this innovative material in orthopedic surgeries, such as vertebroplasty, we aspire to effectively reduce the risk of postoperative infections, enhance surgical success rates, and accelerate patient recovery, thereby offering a novel strategy for ALBC treatment.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of mos₂ nanosheets

This study successfully synthesized MoS₂ nanosheets based on previously published research literature [18]. Specifically, MoS₂ powder (Aldrich, 69860) was meticulously dispersed in N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, Aldrich, 319937) to a concentration of 5 mg/mL. The mixture was ultrasonicated for 12 h, resulting in the formation of a black suspension. Subsequently, the suspension was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 30 min using a high-speed centrifuge. The pale yellow supernatant, which contained thinner MoS₂ flakes, was collected for further processing. To ensure purity, the collected supernatant was centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 30 min. The resulting precipitate was redispersed in an equal volume of fresh DMF. L-cysteine (Aldrich, 168149) was added to the redispersed mixture to achieve a final concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. The mixture was thoroughly agitated to ensure uniform dispersion and then allowed to age at room temperature for 24 h. Following the aging period, the mixture was centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 30 min to collect the precipitates, which consisted of MoS₂ nanosheets. To remove any unbound L-cysteine and residual DMF, the product was subjected to extensive washing with deionized water. The washing process was accompanied by additional centrifugation steps at 12,000 rpm to ensure thorough purification.

Preparation of PMMA

PMMA was prepared using a powder-liquid mixing system [19]. Methyl methacrylate (MMA) liquid, which is a commonly used organic monomer with good chemical activity and fluidity, serves as the liquid component [20]. PMMA powder acts as a solid component, which is the polymer form of MMA, and exhibits excellent mechanical properties and biocompatibility [20]. Both were used as received, without any modification. Following the PALACOS® manufacturer’s instructions, the MMA liquid and PMMA powder were thoroughly mixed using a vortex mixer at room temperature to prepare PMMA bone cement samples. Synthesis of PMMA-MoS₂ nanocomposites

To synthesize the PMMA-MoS₂ nanocomposites, 2 g of PMMA was dissolved in 50 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Aldrich, 472301) and vigorously stirred at room temperature for 24 h to ensure complete dissolution. Subsequently, MoS₂ nanosheets of varying weight percentages (1%, 2%, 3%, and 5%) were introduced into the PMMA solution. The resulting mixture was then agitated at 80 °C for an additional 24 h to facilitate the uniform dispersion of the MoS₂ nanosheets within the PMMA matrix.

After stirring, the blend was poured into a petri dish and allowed to dry under controlled heat conditions to ensure the complete evaporation of DMSO. To date, PMMA-MoS₂ nanocomposites have been prepared for further analysis.

Mechanical property testing of PMMA bone cements

Prior to the start of the experiments, the laboratory temperature was set to 23 °C and the testing equipment and reagents were allowed to equilibrate at (23 ± 1)°C for 2 h. Mechanical analysis was conducted using a universal tester (AG-X100KN, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with 100 N and 1000 N load cells. The PMMA bone cement powder and liquid for each group were mixed uniformly until a dough-like consistency was achieved, after which the mold holes were filled with the mixture. All specimens were cylindrical with a gauge length of 12 mm and a diameter of 6 mm, in strict accordance with ISO 5833 [21]. The thickness of the specimens was assessed using calipers with an average thickness of 1 mm.

For the elastic modulus testing, the uniaxial tensile speed was set to 20 mm/min. Ten specimens per composition were tested, and their elastic moduli (in MPa) were determined and averaged using the QMAT software. Simultaneously, the tensile strength was evaluated by recording the maximum load sustained by the specimens during tensile testing. The tensile strength (MPa) was calculated as the peak load divided by the original cross-sectional area of the specimens, with ten replicates tested per group to ensure statistical validity.

Establishment and analysis of a finite element model of the T11-L1 vertebral bodies

Spinal computed tomography (CT) data were obtained from a healthy male volunteer (70 kg, 175 cm) with no history of spinal trauma or osteoporosis. This study was approved by the ethics committee of our hospital (Approval Number: 20210809131), and informed consent was obtained from all the volunteers.

Utilizing DICOM-formatted data files, we accurately imported the CT-scanned spinal data into MIMICS software for three-dimensional (3D) model reconstruction, ensuring the accuracy and completeness of the model. Subsequently, the reconstructed 3D model was imported into Geomagic Studio 2024 in the STL format for reverse engineering. Through surface smoothing, separation of cortical and cancellous bones, noise reduction, and surface fitting, we significantly enhanced the precision and realism of the model.

The optimized 3D geometric model was then exported in the STP format and imported into SolidWorks 2024 for detailed assembly of the vertebral bodies. Using the loft and split functions, we meticulously trimmed and combined the cortical and cancellous bones, while also constructing critical structures such as the lumbar intervertebral discs, annulus fibrosus, nucleus pulposus, endplates, articular cartilage, and adding linear ligaments including the anterior longitudinal ligament, posterior longitudinal ligament, interspinous ligament, supraspinous ligament, ligamentum flavum, intertransverse ligament, and capsular ligament. The cortical bone thickness of the vertebral bodies was set at 1.5 mm, and the endplate thickness at 0.5 mm, based on reports from authoritative literature [22].

After completing the 3D model construction, we imported it into ANSYS Workbench 24.2 for in-depth finite element analysis. We conducted a fine tetrahedral mesh division of the vertebral bones, bodies, endplates, and intervertebral discs, with a mesh size of 0.5 mm for articular cartilage to accurately capture its stress distribution and a mesh size of 2 mm for the remaining parts, balancing computational accuracy and efficiency [22]. The spinal column and intervertebral discs were analyzed as independent entities, with a tetrahedral 10-node element type selected to accurately simulate complex loading conditions. For the ligaments, we employed the LINK180 element type, considering only their tensile properties to simplify the calculations and improve efficiency.

To simulate the loading conditions of the human spine under various states comprehensively, realistic loads were applied to the finite element model. An axial compressive load of 500 N was applied to the upper surface of the T11 vertebral body to simulate the pressure exerted on the spine while standing. Furthermore, we simulated four spinal motion states, namely flexion, extension, left lateral bending, and right lateral bending, and applied these states to models infused with four different types of 4 mL bone cement: pure PMMA, PMMA with 1% MoS₂, PMMA with 3% MoS₂, and PMMA with 5% MoS₂.

Cultivation of MC3T3-E1 preosteoblastic cell line

The MC3T3-E1 cell line, which serves as a model for murine calvarial osteoblasts, was obtained from ATCC (CRL-2593, Beijing, China). To maintain a normal physiological state, cells were cultured at a constant temperature of 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, mimicking in vivo conditions. Alpha-minimum essential medium (α-MEM, Aldrich, SLM-244) devoid of ascorbic acid, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) as a nutrient source, 1% antibiotic-antifungal solution to prevent microbial contamination, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate to optimize cellular metabolism. The medium was replaced every 3–4 d to ensure a stable and fresh growth environment. To prevent contact inhibition caused by excessive growth during cell proliferation, the cells were subcultured using trypsin/EDTA solution (9002-07-7 and 60-00-4, Beijing Suo Laibao Technology Co., Ltd.) when their confluency did not reach 80%, thereby maintaining their continuous proliferative capacity. In all experimental procedures, MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded at an initial density of 2000 cells/cm² on culture dishes and allowed sufficient time to grow to full confluence. Upon reaching this growth stage, the cells were ready for subsequent experimental manipulation.

Assessment of osteoblast viability

Pure PMMA, PMMA + 1%MoS₂, PMMA + 3%MoS₂, and PMMA + 5%MoS₂ were sterilized using CO60 irradiation. According to the ISO 10,993 standard and methods reported in the literature, the sterilized samples were soaked in complete culture medium at a ratio of 0.2 g:1mL and incubated for 48 h [23]. The extracts were then transferred to sterile centrifuge tubes supplemented with FBS and antibiotics. Osteoblasts under good growth conditions were then selected and prepared into cell suspensions. An automated cell counter (Countess 3, Thermo Fisher Scientific Co, US) was used to count the cells, and the suspension density was adjusted to 1 × 10⁴/mL. Diluted cell suspensions were inoculated into 96-well plates at 100µL per well, for a total of 20 wells per plate. Three plates were seeded to ensure reproducibility. After inoculation, 96-well plates were placed in a cell incubator for culturing. After 24 h, cell adherence was observed, and the culture medium was replaced. All groups received the corresponding bone cement extract.

The viability of MC3T3-E1 cells was evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Aldrich, 96992). At predetermined time points (1, 3, and 5 days), the plates were removed and the supernatant was aspirated. One hundred microliters of culture medium containing the CCK-8 working solution was added to each well and incubated for 1 h. The absorbance (OD value) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Varioskan ALF, Thermo Fisher Scientific Co, US), and cell viability was calculated based on the OD values.

Alkaline phosphatase and Alizarin red S staining

To assess the effects of the bone cement extracts on osteoblast differentiation and mineralization, the extracts prepared as outlined in Sect. 2.7 were utilized. Osteoblasts were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/mL and cultured for 24 h to promote cell attachment. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with the corresponding bone–cement extract. After 7 and 14 days of cultivation, the medium was aspirated and the osteoblasts were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Aldrich, P2272) prior to fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Aldrich, 158127) for 10 min. After fixation, the cells were washed again with PBS and stained with ALP (Aldrich, APMB-RO) staining solution for 20 min to evaluate the degree of osteoblast differentiation. Similarly, after 14 and 21 days of culture, the medium was removed, and the osteoblasts were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. After fixation, the cells were washed with PBS and stained with a 1% ARS (Aldrich, TMS-008) solution for 20 min to visualize the formation of mineralized nodules. The stained cells were examined under a microscope to quantify the ALP activity and the number of mineralized nodules.

Quantitative analysis of ALP and ARS staining was performed using ImageJ v1.53. ALP-stained images were acquired under standardized illumination using a phase-contrast microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti2, Japan, 10× objective), whereas ARS-stained samples were imaged using both transmitted light and fluorescence modes to enhance contrast, especially at low concentrations. Images were converted to 8-bit grayscale and the stained regions were segmented using the IsoData thresholding method. For ALP, color thresholding parameters were set as follows: hue, 20–40; saturation, 30–255; and brightness, 0–200. For ARS, thresholds were Hue: 0–15, Saturation: 50–255, and Brightness: 0–150. Fluorescence intensity thresholds were calibrated against unstained controls to eliminate background noise. Three non-overlapping regions of interest (ROIs) per sample were analyzed, and the percentage of positive areas was calculated as the ratio of stained pixels to the total ROI pixels. To ensure reproducibility, two blinded researchers independently analyzed 20% of the images, confirming inter-observer variability below 5%.

Microbial contamination with Escherichia coli

The mixed contamination consisted of Escherichia coli (E. coli) (ATCC, 8739) previously grown in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI, BioMaxima, Lublin, Poland) agar broth at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, one colony forming unit (CFU) of each microorganism was transferred to 10 mL of BHI medium and incubated overnight under the same culture conditions while shaking. Prior to testing, the films were soaked in 70% ethanol for 1 h and washed with sterile PBS.

Detection of antibacterial activity

Following the revival of E. coli, a bacterial solution with a density of 110 × 107 CFU/mL was prepared. Four groups of bone cement samples were individually submerged in glass vials, each containing 2 ml of bacterial solution. The vials were then incubated in a shaking incubator at 37 °C for 5 min. Next, 100 µL of the bacterial suspension was collected and plated onto BHI agar media. The medium was subsequently placed in a CO2-controlled incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the number of bacterial colonies was enumerated using a quantitative plating method and photographic records were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was conducted using SPSS 21.0. The results were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for statistical analysis. For comparisons among multiple means, a significance level of P-Value < 0.05 was adopted to determine statistical significance.

Result

Mechanical properties analysis of various PMMA-MoS₂ composites

The elastic modulus and tensile strength of pure PMMA and PMMA-MoS₂ nanocomposites were systematically evaluated to assess their mechanical suitability for orthopedic applications (Fig. 1). As shown in Table 2, the elastic moduli of pure PMMA, PMMA + 1%MoS₂, PMMA + 3%MoS₂, and PMMA + 5%MoS₂ were measured as 2100.1 ± 29.7 MPa, 2312.2 ± 25.5 MPa, 2598.6 ± 32.4 MPa, and 2706.1 ± 14.7 MPa, respectively. A statistically significant increase (P < 0.05) in the elastic modulus was observed with increasing MoS₂ content, demonstrating enhanced stiffness owing to the reinforcing effect of MoS₂ nanosheets within the PMMA matrix.

Concurrently, tensile strength exhibited a concentration-dependent improvement, rising from 45.2 ± 2.1 MPa for pure PMMA to 56.9 ± 1.5 MPa for PMMA + 5%MoS₂ (Table 2). This increase in tensile strength is attributed to the synergistic interaction between the layered MoS₂ nanosheets and the polymer matrix. The nanosheets acted as stress distributors, mitigating crack propagation and enhancing interfacial adhesion, thereby improving the resistance of the material to fracture under tensile loads. These mechanical enhancements align with the clinical requirements for bone reconstruction and replacement materials, where both high stiffness to match bone biomechanics and superior tensile strength to resist fragmentation under dynamic physiological stresses are critical.

Finite element analysis stress values

Specifically, the maximum von Mises stress values generated by pure PMMA on the T11 vertebral body during flexion, extension, left lateral bending, and right lateral bending were 2.788, 3.684, 3.101, and 2.185 MPa, respectively (Fig. 2). This stress distribution pattern is consistent with those observed in the PMMA + 1%MoS₂, PMMA + 3%MoS₂, and PMMA + 5%MoS₂ groups under the same conditions.

Similarly, for the T12 vertebral body, the maximum von Mises stress values generated by pure PMMA under the four conditions were 2.303, 1.463, 2.912, and 1.711 MPa, respectively (Fig. 3). The corresponding stress values for the PMMA + 1%MoS₂, PMMA + 3%MoS₂, and PMMA + 5%MoS₂ groups were 2.299 MPa/1.463 MPa/2.911 MPa/1.709 MPa, 2.294 MPa/1.463 MPa/2.91 MPa/1.706 MPa, and 2.293 MPa/1.463 MPa/2.909 MPa/1.705 MPa, respectively, which were virtually identical to those of the pure PMMA group. This suggests that the incorporation of MoS₂ nanosheets, even up to 5%, does not affect the overall stress distribution across the T12 vertebra. The load-bearing capacity and stress conduction characteristics of the composite remain consistent with those of the unmodified cement, indicating good biomechanical compatibility.

For the L1 vertebral body, the maximum von Mises stress values generated by pure PMMA under the four conditions were 2.738, 1.961, 2.581, and 2.381 MPa, respectively (Fig. 4). This result was also consistent with those observed for the PMMA + 1%MoS₂, PMMA + 3%MoS₂, and PMMA + 5%MoS₂ groups under the same conditions, reinforcing the conclusion that MoS₂ incorporation does not alter the stress profiles within the vertebral body. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences in the stress distributions on the surfaces of adjacent vertebrae among the different types of PMMA-MoS₂ bone cements (P-Value > 0.05). Together, these results confirm that the mechanical reinforcement achieved by MoS₂ addition occurs at the material level without compromising the macroscale biomechanical behavior critical for safe orthopedic use.

Cell proliferation evaluation

The proliferation of MC3T3-E1 osteoblasts exposed to extracts from various bone cements was assessed on days 1, 3, and 5 using the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 5). The results showed that the viability of MC3T3-E1 cells exceeded 100% for PMMA + 1%MoS₂, PMMA + 3%MoS₂, and PMMA + 5%MoS₂ compared to pure PMMA at all time points. An increasing trend in cell viability was noted with increasing MoS₂ content. However, the MC3T3-E1 cell viability of the PMMA + 1%MoS₂ group exhibited an upward trend compared with that of the pure PMMA group on days 1 and 3, although the difference was not statistically significant. This indicates that PMMA-MoS₂ nanocomposites enhance osteoblast proliferation, with a more notable effect at higher MoS₂ concentrations.

Influence of PMMA-MoS₂ on osteoblast differentiation

ALP staining was conducted on days 7 and 14 to investigate the influence of different PMMA-MoS₂ composites on osteoblast differentiation. ALP-positive regions, appearing as gray-brown granular deposits in the cytoplasm, were ranked in descending order on day 7 as: PMMA + 5%MoS₂, PMMA + 3%MoS₂, PMMA + 1%MoS₂, and pure PMMA (Fig. 6). Similar results were observed on day 14, with a marked increase in the ALP-positive regions for all composites compared with day 7. These results suggest that PMMA-MoS₂ nanocomposites boost ALP expression in osteoblasts, facilitating their differentiation, with a more pronounced effect at higher MoS₂ concentrations.

Influence of PMMA-MoS₂ on osteoblast maturation

Mineralized nodule formation is a crucial marker of osteoblast maturation [24]. ARS staining of osteoblasts cultured for 14 days revealed the presence of dark red mineralized nodules in all PMMA-MoS₂ co-cultured groups, with a significant increase in nodule numbers corresponding to higher MoS₂ concentrations. Specifically, the PMMA + 5%MoS₂ group exhibited the highest number of nodules, followed by PMMA + 3%MoS₂, PMMA + 1%MoS₂, and pure PMMA (Fig. 7). Similar results were observed on day 21, with PMMA + 1%MoS₂ showing a marked increase in nodule number compared to pure PMMA. These findings suggest that PMMA-MoS₂ nanocomposites promote osteoblast maturation, as evidenced by increased mineralized nodule formation, with a more pronounced effect at higher MoS₂ concentrations.

Antimicrobial properties of PMMA-MoS₂

The antimicrobial test results for the various PMMA-MoS₂ composites are presented in Figs. 8. One-way ANOVA revealed that the colony counts of E. coli for PMMA + 1%MoS₂, PMMA + 3%MoS₂, and PMMA + 5%MoS₂ were significantly lower than those for pure PMMA (P-Value < 0.001). Antimicrobial activity was observed at an MoS₂ concentration of 1% in PMMA bone cement, with a gradual decrease in colony count on BHI agar medium with increasing MoS₂ concentration. The PMMA + 5%MoS₂ group exhibited the lowest colony count. These results indicate that the antimicrobial properties of PMMA bone cement increased with increasing MoS₂ concentration, demonstrating a positive correlation between MoS₂ concentration and antimicrobial efficacy.

Discussion

Despite playing a pivotal role in preventing postoperative infections, PMMA-based ALBC faces limitations in clinical application owing to antibiotic resistance and deterioration of mechanical properties [25, 26]. Although numerous studies have aimed to optimize ALBC performance, the complex synthesis processes, uncertain biosafety, and high production costs have resulted in few PMMA products being suitable for large-scale clinical use [27–29]. To advance clinical translation, this study incorporated MoS₂ nanosheets into PMMA bone cement, innovatively preparing a PMMA-MoS₂ nanocomposite material. As an emerging inorganic antimicrobial agent, MoS₂ exhibits notable antimicrobial activity, good biocompatibility, and osteogenic properties [30, 31]. The results indicate that the incorporation of MoS₂ nanosheets significantly enhances the mechanical strength, osteogenic capacity, and antimicrobial efficacy of PMMA bone cement, effectively overcoming the limitations of traditional ALBC.

As a bone substitute material, PMMA bone cement must provide stable support in the human body over the long term. However, previous studies have shown that the internal pores in PMMA can lead to decreased mechanical properties, affecting its load-bearing capacity [32, 33]. This study found that the elastic modulus of PMMA-MoS₂ composites increased with the concentration of MoS₂ nanosheets, highlighting the potential of MoS₂ to enhance the mechanical properties of PMMA. This enhancement is attributed to the unique layered structure and large specific surface area of MoS₂, which might effectively interact with the PMMA matrix to improve overall mechanical stability [34]. Furthermore, we constructed a T11-L1 three-dimensional finite element model to accurately simulate the complex mechanical behavior of the spine, particularly the stress distribution in the vertebrae after PVP. The results showed that the stress distribution of PMMA bone cement with different MoS₂ concentrations in the vertebrae did not significantly alter the stress patterns of the adjacent vertebrae, verifying the mechanical stability of MoS₂-enhanced bone cement and avoiding the decrease in mechanical strength caused by antibiotic addition in traditional ALBC.

Biocompatibility is a primary consideration before an implant is used clinically [35]. In this study, a CCK-8 assay was conducted to co-culture bone cement extracts with mouse embryonic osteoblast cells MC3T3-E1, observing the OD values and cell viability to assess the in vitro biocompatibility of the material. The results showed that at different time points, the viability of cells exposed to PMMA-MoS₂ extracts was higher than that of the pure PMMA group, and increased with the MoS₂ content. This indicates that the PMMA-MoS₂ composites promoted osteoblast proliferation in vitro. Additionally, ALP and ARS staining revealed increased ALP expression and mineralized nodule formation in osteoblasts exposed to PMMA-MoS₂, indicating enhanced osteoblast differentiation and maturation, which was positively correlated with MoS₂ concentration [36].

In orthopedic infections, Staphylococcus, especially Staphylococcus aureus, is the most common pathogen among gram-positive bacteria [9]. Given that previous studies have extensively reported the bactericidal mechanism of MoS₂ nanosheets against Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli was selected as a representative gram-negative bacteria to verify whether the antimicrobial mechanism of MoS₂ has broad-spectrum applicability [37, 38]. The results showed that the PMMA-MoS₂ composites exhibited superior antimicrobial activity to pure PMMA, with the antimicrobial effect positively correlated with MoS₂ content. Among them, PMMA + 5%MoS₂ demonstrated the strongest antimicrobial capability, and even at a 1% MoS₂ concentration, it significantly reduced the number of E. coli colonies. This highlights the potential of MoS₂ as a novel antimicrobial agent for PMMA bone cement. We speculated that the antibacterial activity of PMMA-MoS₂ arises from dual mechanisms involving physical and chemical interactions. First, the sharp edges and high surface area of MoS₂ nanosheets physically disrupt bacterial membranes, leading to cytoplasmic leakage and cell death. Second, MoS₂ generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) under physiological conditions, inducing oxidative stress that damages bacterial DNA and metabolic enzymes [39]. Prior reports on MoS₂’s activity against S. aureus suggest similar mechanisms, underscoring the potential for broad-spectrum utility [40]. These combined effects account for the observed reduction of over 90% in E. coli viability at 5% MoS₂ loading.

Although this study achieved positive results, it has several limitations that require further attention. First, the mechanical property analysis focused solely on the elastic modulus, and further research is needed to comprehensively assess other mechanical properties, such as compressive strength and fracture toughness. Secondly, this study lacked animal models to validate the mechanical stability of the material under dynamic loads and long-term implantation. Furthermore, although in vitro models showed a concentration-dependent promotion of osteogenic activity, the long-term effects of in vivo bone regeneration and integration with the host bone need to be confirmed through animal experiments. Moreover, the antimicrobial performance study targeted only a single strain of E. coli, without fully reflecting the complex infection environment in clinical settings. Future research should extend to common orthopedic pathogens, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and drug-resistant strains. Finally, the potential cytotoxic threshold of high-concentration MoS₂ (> 5%) and its long-term biocompatibility in human tissues, including chronic inflammatory responses or metabolic effects, have not been clarified. Multiscale toxicological studies, such as in vitro cell apoptosis detection and in vivo organ pathological analysis, are needed to improve the safety assessment system.

Conclusion

This study confirms that integrating MoS₂ nanosheets into PMMA bone cement significantly improves its multifunctional performance. The PMMA-MoS₂ nanocomposite exhibited a 28.8% increase in elastic modulus from 2100.1 ± 29.7 to 2706.1 ± 14.7 MPa and a 25.9% increase in tensile strength from 45.2 ± 2.1 to 56.9 ± 1.5 MPa, enhancing mechanical stability. Finite element modeling supports its biomechanical reliability under physiological stress.

Biological assays showed that PMMA + 5%MoS₂ increased MC3T3-E1 cell viability to over 120% of that of the pure PMMA group, along with a 3.5-fold increase in ALP activity and a 4.2-fold increase in mineralized nodule formation. Antibacterial tests revealed over 90% reduction in Escherichia coli adhesion at 5% MoS₂ concentration and approximately 40% reduction at 1%.

These findings suggest that PMMA-MoS₂ nanocomposites hold strong potential as a multifunctional alternative to conventional antibiotic-loaded bone cements by combining mechanical reinforcement, osteogenic enhancement, and intrinsic antibacterial activity. To support clinical translation, future studies will focus on bridging in vitro results with preclinical models, with particular attention to evaluating long-term biocompatibility and biomechanical performance in vivo under dynamic physiological conditions. At the same time, efforts will be directed toward optimizing key material properties such as setting time, injectability, and radiopacity to meet the procedural requirements of vertebroplasty. In addition, thorough validation of the antimicrobial efficacy against multidrug-resistant pathogens, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae, will be essential in light of the growing challenge of antibiotic resistance. These steps are critical not only for initiating clinical trials but also for addressing the current limitations of bone cements that struggle to provide both infection control and durable mechanical support. Ultimately, this research aims to establish PMMA-MoS₂ as a clinically viable solution that offers safer and more effective treatment options for patients with osteoporotic fractures and compromised bone health.
