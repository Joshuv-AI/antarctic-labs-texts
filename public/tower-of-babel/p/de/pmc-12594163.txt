Oral Pathobionts Aggravate Myocardial Infarction Through Mobilization of B2 Cells
Authors: Bo-Yan Chen, Hong Zhu, Yu-Lin Li, Jun Zhang, Shuo Xu, Lu-Jun Zhou, Lin-Juan Du, Ting Liu, Xue-Nan Sun, Guo-Cai Tian, Jie Li, Xiao-Qian Meng, Hui-Lin Ye, Xue-Bing Bai, Wen-Zhen Lin, Hu-Xiao Li, Jian-Xuan Li, Jian-Yong Sun, Yan Liu, Wu-Chang Zhang, Lu Zhang, Jing-Yuan Fang, Daqian Xu, Mengfei Yu, Yuxiang Dai, Qianming Chen, Sheng-Zhong Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Myocardial infarction (MI) is a high-prevalence disease that threatens human survival and quality of life worldwide. Considerable evidence has suggested that periodontitis (PD) is detrimental to MI. However, the direct impact of PD on MI is unclear; which oral pathobionts contribute to and how microbial signals regulate the pathogenesis of MI remain obscure.METHODS:The effect of PD on MI was assessed in a mouse model that combined ligature-induced PD with MI. Ectopic accumulation of oral pathobionts in infarcted hearts was identified by bacterial sequencing and fluorescence in situ hybridization. Oral pathobionts–reactive B2 cells detrimental for MI were determined by flow cytometry and verified in gnotobiotic mice. Several mouse strains, including CD45.1, Kaede, and knockout strains for Ighm, Rag1, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 13, S1pr, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α, were used for mechanistical exploration. Mutant strains of Fusobacterium nucleatum and Porphyromonas gingivalis deficient in adhesion proteins were generated to investigate bacterial mechanism involved in MI exacerbation. Patients with MI with or without PD were recruited to test the deteriorating impact of PD on MI and the association between oral pathobionts and cardiac function.RESULTS:Ligature-induced PD and subgingival plaques of patients with PD exacerbated MI in mice by oral pathobionts. Prevotella intermedia, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Selenomonas sputigena, Porphyromonas gingivalis, and Bacteroides plebeius expanded in the oral cavity and ectopically accumulated in the infarcted hearts through impairment of epithelial and endothelial cell barriers. These oral pathobionts mobilized B cells, particularly B2 cells that produced interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α, to aggravate MI. Mechanistically, oral pathobiont–promoted B cells egress from cervical lymph nodes by a sphingosine-1-phosphate–sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor axis and infiltrate infarcted hearts by a C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 13–C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 5 axis.CONCLUSIONS:Oral pathobionts and proinflammatory B2 cells significantly exacerbate MI, supporting the oral-heart axis as a novel pathogenic pathway. Interventions targeting PD, oral pathobionts, or related immunological mechanisms may improve the therapy of MI.

Myocardial infarction (MI) is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide and therefore a significant socioeconomic burden.1 Although advances in our understanding of the pathophysiology and therapies of MI have improved survival, residual risk factors make the global prevalence remain unacceptably high.2 Emerging evidence has considered periodontitis (PD), a bacterially induced periodontal inflammatory disease posing a notable hazard for public health, as a prevalent risk factor for MI.3–6 Comprehensive elucidation of the connections between PD and MI could substantially benefit the prevention and alleviation of MI.

Studies have illustrated the associations between PD and MI. Patients with MI have not only deeper periodontal pockets but also a higher number of missing teeth.7–9 PD increases the risk of a first MI and is positively associated with acute myocardial infarct size.3,5 Patients with MI and PD have elevated inflammatory parameters, including C-reactive protein, interleukin (IL)-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α), erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and fibrinogen compared with those without PD.10–12 Therefore, the evidence for the association between the 2 diseases is sufficient and solid.13 However, it is uncertain whether PD plays a direct role in the progression of MI.

Oral microbiota are a potentially important contributor for the adverse effects of PD on MI. Investigations have indicated that typical periodontal pathobionts, including Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Porphyromonas gingivalis, and Tannerella forsythia, significantly enrich in the periodontal environment of patients with MI; their abundances were correlated with the levels of troponin-I and cytokines, as well as cardiac function.14–19 Oral bacteria, including Streptococcus, Porphyromonas, Prevotella, Fusobacterium, and Actinomyces, have been detected in thrombus aspirates of patients with MI.20,21 Results of 16S rRNA gene sequencing have demonstrated a high detection rate of common oral bacteria (Actinomyces, Fusobacterium, Klebsiella, and Streptococcus) in cerebrocardiovascular samples of human subjects with suspected infection.22 PD-elicited bacteremia may be responsible for the systematic translocation of oral bacteria.23,24 Given the strong clinical correlation between P gingivalis and MI,25,26 the majority of the studies aiming to reveal the connections between PD and MI have focused on this specific periodontal pathogen. The impacts of oral pathobionts on MI have not been systemically explored. Furthermore, the mechanistic links have not been clearly elucidated.

Oral pathobionts may elicit immune response to affect MI. Immune dysregulation has been proposed as a critical mechanism connecting PD to systemic diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease, arthritis, diabetes, and atherosclerosis.27–30 It has been reported that P gingivalis, one of the most widely studied oral pathobionts, or its lipopolysaccharide (LPS) not only promotes post-infarct cardiac rupture by inducing apoptosis and the matrix metalloproteinase 9 activity of the myocardium31,32 but also exacerbates detrimental ventricular remodeling after MI likely through provoking the infiltration of inflammatory cells such as macrophages and CD8+ T cells.33,34 However, it is not known whether all oral pathobionts cause similar immune response during MI. Moreover, the roles of different populations of immune cells have not been completely investigated; the underlying molecular mechanism remains largely unknown.

In the present study, we set out to systemically investigate the causal role of PD and oral pathobionts on MI and to explore the underlying immunological and molecular mechanisms. We first studied the influence of PD on MI using a mouse model that combined PD and MI. We then unbiasedly screened for enriched oral pathobionts in infarcted mouse hearts. Subsequently, we studied the impacts of oral pathobionts on MI and immune dysregulation, identified 5 MI-aggravating oral pathobionts, uncovered mobilization of oral pathobiont–reactive B2 cells as the major immune response, and dissected the mediating molecular mechanisms. Last, we verified our observations in germ-free (GF) mice and patients with MI.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with Prism (GraphPad Software, Inc). Normality tests were conducted for all data before statistical analysis. When the sample size was 6 to ≈50, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used; when the sample size was >50, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used. Parametric tests were used for statistical analyses of data with normal distribution. Specifically, 2-tailed unpaired Student t test (parametric) was used for all comparisons between 2 groups. One-way ANOVA followed by the Fisher tests (parametric) was used for all comparisons between ≥3 groups. For experiments involving time courses, 2-way ANOVA followed by the Tukey multiple-comparison test was used. The significance of structural variation of microbial communities among groups was detected by permutational multivariate analysis of variance. Differences with values of P≤5×10−2 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Ligature-Induced PD and Human Subgingival Plaques Aggravate MI in Mice

A mouse ligature-induced PD (LIP) model and coronary artery ligation–induced MI model were combined to explore the direct impacts of PD and subgingival plaques (PL) from patients with PD on MI (Figure 1A). LIP and PL did not significantly affect basal physiological parameters in mice before MI and significantly differed from the sepsis model (Figure S1A through S1D). In sham and MI groups, LIP caused significant alveolar bone loss that was further aggravated by PL, and the effects of LIP and PL were more evident in the MI groups (Figure S1E and S1F). Moreover, the expression of proinflammatory genes in gingiva was upregulated by LIP and PL (Figure S1G). Echocardiography illustrated that ejection fraction and fractional shortening of mice in the MI+LIP+Vehicle and MI+LIP+PL groups were significantly decreased compared with those in the MI+Vehicle and MI+PL group and that left ventricular internal dimension at end diastole and left ventricular internal dimension at end systole were significantly increased (Figure 1B and 1C). Moreover, significant differences were observed between the MI+LIP+Vehicle group and MI+LIP+PL group (Figure 1B and 1C). PL inoculation without LIP did not aggravate cardiac dysfunction compared with vehicle after MI (Figure 1B and 1C). Neither LIP nor PL affected the physiological parameters and cardiac function of mice in the sham groups or the survival rate of mice after sham/MI induction (Figure S1H through S1J). Masson trichrome staining showed that LIP markedly increased cardiac infarct size after MI and that LIP+PL further exacerbated the phenotype (Figure 1D and 1E). Results of quantitative reverse transcriptase–polymerase chain reaction demonstrated significantly higher expression of proinflammatory gene and lower expression of anti-inflammatory gene in heart samples of mice in the MI+LIP+PL group compared with the rest groups (Figure 1F).

To further investigate the components of PL that aggravate MI, we pretreated it with antibiotics, DNase I, and proteinase K to deplete bacteria, DNA, and proteins in PL, respectively (Figure S2A). Bacteria-depleted PL, rather than DNA- or protein-eliminated PL, rescued the deteriorating effect on cardiac function after MI (Figure S2B and S2C). These results suggested that oral bacteria played a critical role in PL-mediated exacerbation of MI. We therefore hypothesized that cardiac accumulation of microbes plays an important role in the exacerbation of MI by LIP and PL. We first determined the potential accumulation of bacteria in the post-MI heart. LIP+PL significantly expanded bacterial load in oral ligatures of both sham and MI groups (Figure 1G; Figure S2D). In particular, bacterial load was multiplied in the heart of mice in the LIP+PL group compared with the PL and LIP+Vehicle group after MI (Figure 1G), whereas there was no difference among groups after sham operations (Figure S2E). Polymerase chain reaction analysis of the full-length 16S rRNA gene revealed apparent bacterial DNA accumulation in infarcted heart tissues but undetectable levels in sham-operated controls (Figure S2F).

We further performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing to profile the microbial composition of human PL and mouse samples comprehensively. The unsupervised microbiome clustering by principal component analysis indicated that the oral ligature and heart tissue in MI+Vehicle, MI+LIP+Vehicle, and MI+LIP+PL groups had distinct microbial communities (Figure S2G). Different microbial communities also appeared in oral ligatures of the sham groups (Figure S2H). LIP caused oral microbiota dysbiosis and PL inoculation successfully promoted colonization of human-derived oral bacteria in the periodontal environment of mice (Figure S2I through S2J). Notably, the infarcted hearts had different bacterial compositions among the MI+Vehicle, MI+LIP+Vehicle, and MI+LIP+PL groups at the species and genus levels (Figure 1H; Figure S2I). The overlapping species and genus among the oral ligature, heart, and subgingival plaques were primarily enriched in the MI+LIP+PL group (Figure 1H and 1I; Figure S2I through S2K). Compared with the MI+Vehicle and MI+LIP+Vehicle groups, the MI+LIP+PL group displayed a unique bacterial clustering at the species level, suggesting that LIP+PL elicited a selection and accumulation of particular oral pathobionts in the oral cavity and infarcted hearts (Figure 1H). We further performed linear discriminant analysis effect size to identify bacterial species that significantly differed in infarcted hearts in the presence of LIP+Vehicle and LIP+PL (Figure 1J and 1K). Most species enriched in heart tissues of the MI+LIP+PL group also existed in oral ligatures and PL used for oral inoculation (Figure S2K). Based on the linear discriminant scores annotated by the Human Oral Microbiome Database for the MI+LIP+PL group (Given that PL were inoculated to the mice, Human Oral Microbiome Database annotation could accurately identify species from the human oral cavity in this group) and by the Greengenes Database for the MI+LIP+Vehicle group (Greengenes Database could effectively annotate mouse bacteria in this group because no human microbes were introduced), the top 11 cultivated species (Figure 1L) were identified in the heart of the MI+LIP+PL group and 1 species (Figure 1M) of the MI+LIP+Vehicle group, all of which were selected for further experiments. Neither LIP nor PL caused gut dysbiosis (Figure S2L through S2N). These results indicated that oral bacterial species from human subgingival plaques enriched in the mouse oral cavity and heart after MI.

Oral Pathobionts Exacerbate MI by Inducing the Transmigration of B Cells From Cervical Lymph Nodes to the Heart

The 12 strains of bacteria (identified in Figure 1L and 1M) were cultured and inoculated into specific pathogen-free mice as a mixture (12Mix) to investigate the impacts of these oral pathobionts on MI. We observed successful colonization of the 12 bacterial strains, except for Dialister invisus, in oral ligature of the MI+LIP+12Mix group (Figure S3A). Fluorescence in situ hybridization also indicated that 9 of the 12 strains accumulated in the infarcted hearts of MI+LIP+12Mix group (Figure S3B). Mice in the MI+LIP+12Mix group showed significantly aggravated cardiac dysfunction compared with the MI+LIP+Vehicle and MI+12Mix groups (Figure 2A–2D). The 12Mix alone (without LIP) did not exacerbate cardiac dysfunction in either sham or MI mice (Figure S3C through S3E). Masson trichrome staining also indicated a significantly larger infarct size of hearts after LIP+12Mix treatment (Figure 2E). The cervical lymph nodes (cLNs) are the draining lymph nodes of the oral cavity and participated in the development of systemic diseases.28,35 Our morphology analysis demonstrated that the cLN weight was markedly increased by LIP+12Mix treatment after MI (Figure 2F), suggesting a robust local immune response. Continuously elevated plasma LPS after inoculation of oral pathobionts and LIP implied systematic diffusion of bacteria (Figure 2G).

To further characterize the immune responses induced by oral pathobionts after MI, we analyzed the alteration dynamics of immune cells in multiple tissues by flow cytometry. In general, the MI+LIP+12Mix groups displayed a variety of alterations in percentages/numbers of major types of leukocytes compared with the MI+LIP+Vehicle and MI+12Mix groups (Figure 2H through 2L; Figure S3F and S3G). It is important to note that mice in the MI+LIP+12Mix group had increased percentages/numbers of B220+ B cells in all the tissues surveyed compared with those in the MI+LIP+Vehicle and MI+12Mix groups (Figure 2H through 2L). Among different tissues, mouse hearts and cLNs had the most significant increase of B220+ B cells induced by 12Mix+LIP at the different time points after MI (Figure 2H through 2L). B220+ B cells peaked in the infarcted heart 7 days after MI (Figure 2I). However, the 12Mix alone (without LIP) lacked the ability to induce the increase of B cells (Figure S3H through S3K). Both LIP and LIP+12Mix significantly increased the percentages and numbers of B220+ B cells, as well as Ki67+B220+ B cells in cLNs, suggesting the proliferation of B cells (Figure S3L through S3N). Next, a cLN excision experiment was performed to test whether the effects of oral pathobionts on MI depended on cLN (Figure 2M). The 12Mix induced a significant decrease in cardiac function and an increase in infarct size 7 days after MI, both of which were rescued by cLN excision (Figure 2N through 2Q). Consistently, the 12Mix substantially increased the percentages/numbers of B220+ B cells in infarcted hearts and blood, which were reversed by cLN excision (Figure 2R and 2S). These results suggested that oral pathobiont–induced immune response in cLNs mediated the deteriorating effect of LIP on MI.

Furthermore, given the dramatic rise of B cells induced by oral pathobionts, a B-cell deficiency experiment was performed in Ighm-knockout (KO; Bnull) mice that lacked mature B cells to determine the role of oral pathobiont–induced B220+ B cells in aggravating MI (Figure 3A). B-cell deficiency reversed the 12Mix-induced deteriorations of cardiac function and infarct size after MI and LIP (Figure 3B through 3G). LIP and 12Mix did not affect cardiac function or infarct size after MI in the absence of B cells (Figure 3B through 3G). Previous studies have demonstrated transmigration of immune cells from cLN to the extraoral organs.28,36 Our results showed coordinated increases of oral pathobiont–induced B220+ B cells in the cLN and hearts, suggesting that B cells transmigrated from cLNs to the heart and exacerbated MI. B-cell adoptive transfer and cell tracking experiments were carried out to test this hypothesis from the following 3 aspects.

First, cLN-derived B220+ B cells were adoptively transferred from wild-type (WT) mice to Rag1−/− mice to test the role of B cells (Figure 3H). B cells originated from Ctrl mice were defined as control B cells and those from mice treated with LIP+12Mix as pathobiont-reactive B cells. Similar to the effects of oral pathobionts, pathobiont-reactive B cells caused worse cardiac function and larger infarct size than control B cells and PBS vehicle (Figure 3I through 3L). The transferred B cells from cLN markedly accumulated in the heart and blood (Figure 3M; Figure S4A), and significantly more pathobiont-reactive B cells were recruited to infarcted hearts than control B cells (Figure 3M). These results suggested that oral pathobiont–reactive B cells of cLN migrated into infarcted hearts through blood and exacerbated MI.

Second, we further assessed the transmigration of cLN-derived B cells to the heart using Kaede transgenic mice.37 We irradiated cLN with violet light (405 nm) to induce photoconversion in cLN lymphocytes before MI (Figure 3N). LIP and LIP+12Mix both aggravated MI in Kaede mice, as illustrated by echocardiography (Figure 3O and 3P) and Masson trichrome staining (Figure 3Q and 3R). We examined the migration of B cells from cLNs to the hearts under MI conditions by evaluating the numbers/percentages of photoconverted B220+Red+ B cells (Figure 3S). LIP significantly increased B220+Red+ B cells in hearts, cLNs, and blood after MI, which were further elevated by the 12Mix (Figure 3S; Figure S4B and S4C).

Third, we adoptively transferred cLN-derived B cells from Kaede mice to Rag1−/− recipients to further test the role of B cells (Figure S4D). Compared with control B cells transfer, cLN-derived B cells from LIP donor (LIP-reactive B cells) and pathobiont-reactive B cells both decreased cardiac function and increased infarct size after MI (Figure S4E through S4H). Pathobiont-reactive B cells provoked more severe MI phenotypes than LIP-reactive B cells (Figure S4E through S4H). Notable accumulation of B220+Green+ B cells was detected in the infarcted hearts, and LIP and oral pathobionts induced significantly more B220+Green+ B cells (Figure S4I). From these observations, we considered that cLN-derived B cells induced by LIP and oral pathobionts could exacerbate MI by transmigrating to infarcted hearts through blood.

Oral Pathobionts Exacerbate MI by Eliciting Proinflammatory B2 Cells in cLNs

To identify the critical oral bacterial strains responsible for the deteriorating effects on MI and the stimulatory activity on B cells, the impacts of the 12 strains (selected from Figure 1L and 1M) were analyzed individually. The dose-response experiment for P gingivalis indicated that it significantly exacerbated MI at 109 colony-forming units without causing significant weight loss (Figure S5A and S5B). Echocardiography and Masson trichrome staining indicated that 5 of the 12 strains, including Selenomonas sputigena, Prevotella intermedia, P gingivalis, Fusobacterium nucleatum ss vincentii, and Bacteroides plebeius, significantly decreased cardiac function and increased infarct size compared with vehicle after MI+LIP (Figure 4A through 4D). Six of the 12 strains, including the 5 that aggravated MI, markedly increased cLN weight (Figure 4E and 4F). Flow cytometry was performed in multiple tissues to examine the impacts of the single strains on B cells (Figure 4G through 4K). All 5 MI-aggravating strains significantly elevated B cell percentages and numbers in mouse hearts (Figure 4H), cLNs (Figure 4I), and blood (Figure 4J). P intermedia and C gingivalis markedly increased B220+ cells in spleens, whereas F nucleatum ss polymorphum, C granulosa, A naeslundii, and S noxia caused a decrease (Figure 4K), suggesting varied effects of oral pathobionts on splenic B cells. Leptotrichia buccalis lacked B cell–inducing capabilities in all sample types (Figure 4G through 4K). Oral pathobiont inoculation did not affect the survival rate after MI compared with vehicle (Figure S5C). To address the potential effects of antibody production by B cells on cardiac function, we detected immunoglobulins in the blood and infarcted hearts of mice by ELISA. The 12Mix significantly increased the blood level of immunoglobulin (Ig) G but not IgA or IgM and did not significantly affect any of these immunoglobulins in infarcted hearts (Figure S5D). Higher levels of IgG were also detected in the blood and B-cell culture media of mice inoculated with single oral pathobionts, particularly the 5 MI-aggravating strains (Figure S5E and S5F). However, no significant correlations were observed between oral bacteria–specific IgG levels and cardiac function (Figure S5G).

B cells affect MI by regulating inflammation through different subpopulations.38–40 To gain more mechanistic insights, we further characterized the B-cell subsets of mouse cLNs and hearts after MI+LIP by flow cytometry (Figure S6A). A total of 5 strains, including B plebeius identified from the mouse oral cavity and the 4 MI-aggravating strains from PL, were selected for further exploration. L buccalis did not induce any significant changes in B cells (Figure 4G through 4K) and therefore was used as a negative control. Regardless of groups, B2 cells were the major subpopulation in both cLNs and hearts, and only small percentages/numbers of B1a, B1b, and regulatory B cells were detected (Figure 5A through 5F; Figure S6B through S6E). Compared with vehicle after MI+LIP, all 5 strains increased the percentage or number of B2 cells in cLNs and hearts, whereas L buccalis failed to do so (Figure 5A and 5B). The 5 strains stimulated B2 in a relatively specific manner, having very little influence on B1a, B1b, or regulatory B-cell subsets (Figure S6B through S6E). B lymphocytes promote inflammatory disease by producing proinflammatory cytokines.41,42 All 5 strains increased the percentage or number of TNF-α– or IL-6–producing B2 cells in cLNs and hearts (Figure 5C through 5F). Next, a mixture of the 5 strains (5Mix) was inoculated to Kaede mice to track the possible transmigration of oral pathobiont–elicited B2 cells from the cLN to the heart (Figure S6F). The 5Mix markedly impaired cardiac function and enlarged infarct size after MI+LIP compared with the vehicle (Figure S6G through S6J). Flow cytometry analysis indicated that LIP induced more photoconverted Red+ B2 cells in cLNs, hearts, and blood than the vehicle after MI, which were prominently elevated by the 5Mix (Figure 5G through 5I). These results suggested that 5Mix promoted the transmigration of proinflammatory B2 cells from cLNs to hearts.

To evaluate the functional role of proinflammatory B2 cells induced by 5Mix during MI, gain-of-function and loss-of-function experiments were performed. For gain-of-function experiments, we adoptively transferred photoconverted B2 cells from cLNs of Kaede mice to Rag1−/− recipients (Figure S6K). B2 cells derived from the Vehicle group were defined as control B2 cells, those from the LIP+Vehicle group as LIP-reactive B2 cells, and those from the LIP+5Mix group as pathobiont-reactive B2 cells. Pathobiont-reactive B2 cells significantly decreased cardiac function and increased infarct size compared with control and LIP-reactive B2 cells in Rag1−/− mice after MI (Figure S6L through S6O). Consistent with the phenotype, markedly more pathobiont-reactive B2 cells accumulated in the heart and blood of Rag1−/− mice compared with control and LIP-reactive B2 cells (Figure S6P and S6Q). For loss-of-function experiments, anti–B cell–activating factor antibodies were used to deplete B2 cells (Figure 5J) as previously reported.43 Anti–B cell–activating factor antibodies significantly reduced B2 cell percentages/numbers in the cLN, heart, and blood (Figure 5K and 5L; Figure S6R) and ameliorated the exacerbating effects of the 5Mix on MI (Figure 5M through 5P). We did not observe the aggravating effect of LIP+5Mix on MI after depletion of B2 cells (Figure 5M through 5P). TNF-α and IL-6 play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of MI in humans and mice.44,45 To further determine the functions of pathobiont-reactive B2 cell–derived IL-6 and TNF-α in aggravating MI, IL-6 KO, TNF-α KO, and WT mice underwent LIP+5Mix treatment, and B2 cells were isolated from cLNs for adoptive transfer experiment (Figure 5Q). Transfer of either IL-6 KO or TNF-α KO B2 cells significantly reversed the detrimental effects of WT B2 cells on MI (Figure 5R through 5T). The levels of cardiac IL-6 and TNF-α were markedly decreased in mice that received IL-6 KO or TNF-α KO B2 cells (Figure 5U). These results suggested that the pathobiont-reactive B2 cells of cLN produced TNF-α and IL-6 to enhance cardiac inflammation, thereby exacerbating MI.

To explore the molecular basis of active transmigration of B cells from cLNs to the heart in response to stimulation of oral pathobionts, we determined the expression of chemokines and chemokine receptors in mouse hearts and cLNs respectively. Cxcl13 was the most prominently upregulated chemokine gene in infarcted regions of the MI+LIP+5Mix group (Figure 6A). The transmigration of B cells depends on the C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 13 (CXCL13)–C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 5 (CXCR5) axis.46,47 LIP and 5Mix also induced a significantly increased expression of CXCL13 receptor Cxcr5 in cLNs (Figure S7A), suggesting the potential CXCL13-CXCR5 signaling axis between the heart and cLNs. An evident increase of CXCL13 production in the MI+LIP+5Mix group was detected in infarcted regions by immunostaining and in the blood by ELISA (Figure 6B through 6D). CXCL13 production in the MI+LIP+Vehicle group also had an increasing trend compared with the MI+Vehicle group (Figure 6B through 6D). Flow cytometry showed that LIP and 5Mix significantly promoted the CXCL13 production in CD45+ but not CD45− cells compared with vehicle after MI (Figure 6E and 6F). Further analysis of the CD45+CXCL13+ cells by flow cytometry revealed that CD4−CD8+ T cells were the largest subpopulation in the MI+Vehicle and MI+LIP+Vehicle groups and that Ly6C+F4/80+ macrophages were the largest one in the MI+LIP+5Mix group (Figure 6G; Figure S7B). LIP induced a notable increase of CXCL13-producing macrophages, which were further expanded by 5Mix in the setting of MI (Figure 6G).

We next asked whether oral pathobionts directly induced CXCL13 production in macrophages. We assessed the CXCL13-producing ability of macrophages after oral pathobionts induction by coculturing. Flow cytometry analysis showed that 5Mix robustly elevated the secretion of CXCL13 in cardiac macrophages compared with L buccalis (a control bacterium) and PBS (Figure 6H). Results of quantitative reverse transcriptase–polymerase chain reaction demonstrated that nuclear factor κB subunit (Nfκb) 1, Nfκb2, and retinoic acid receptor α (Rara), key transcription factors that control the expression of Cxcl13,48–50 were significantly upregulated in macrophages after coculturing with 5Mix, further supporting the capability of oral pathobionts to stimulate CXCL13 production (Figure S7C). The expression of Tlr4 and Myd88 was increased by 5Mix in macrophages (Figure S7D), which may lead to upregulation of nuclear factor-κB transcriptional activity. To further investigate the chemotactic activity of CXCL13 secreted by bacteria-stimulated macrophages on cLN-derived B cells, cardiac macrophages of WT and CXCL13 KO mice were isolated and cocultured with 5Mix, and then the supernatant was collected for the Transwell experiments. Markedly fewer B cells migrated into the supernatant of CXCL13 KO macrophages than WT macrophages (Figure 6I). The cardiac function was substantially improved in CXCL13 KO mice compared with WT mice under the condition of MI+LIP+5Mix (Figure 6J), accompanied by decreased B cells in the infarcted hearts of CXCL13 KO mice (Figure 6K).

CXCR5, the receptor of CXCL13, was highly expressed in B cells among cLNs, hearts, and blood (Figure S7E). LIP and 5Mix prominently increased CXCR5+ B2 cells in cLNs, hearts, and blood after MI (Figure S7F). Thus, oral pathobionts induced both CXCL13 secretion from cardiac macrophages and CXCR5 expression in B2 cells, allowing reinforced recruitment of CXCR5+ B2 cells from cLNs into the myocardium after MI.

Then how did the B2 cells migrate from the cLNs to the heart? Studies using the parabiosis model revealed that LIP and 5Mix triggered more CD45.2+ B2 cells transmigrating from donor mice into the blood and heart of CD45.1+ recipients, increased the accumulation of CD45.2+TNF-α+ and CD45.2+IL-6+ B2 cells in the infarcted heart of recipients, and exacerbated cardiac dysfunction after MI (Figure S7G through S7L). These results supported that the cardiac accumulation of cLN-derived pathobiont-reactive B2 cells was through circulation after MI.

So far, our results demonstrated that oral pathobiont–reactive B2 cells proliferated in cLNs during PD and aggravated cardiac dysfunction after MI by trafficking into the heart. However, the pathway and underlying mechanism involved in the transmigration of cLN-derived B2 cells remain unclear. The sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P)–S1P receptor (S1PR) axis regulates the egress of lymphocytes from secondary lymphoid organs into circulatory fluids, and S1PRs guide lymphocytes migrating toward the high S1P concentration.51,52 Under the setting of LIP, S1P concentration was significantly decreased in cLNs and increased in the blood after 5Mix inoculation (Figure 6L), suggesting that oral pathobionts were able to promote the egress of lymphocytes from cLNs and entering the circulation. S1PRs internalize and degrade once activated by S1P.52 Correspondingly, S1pr1 and S1pr5 expression in B2 cells of cLNs was upregulated in MI+LIP+Vehicle mice, whereas all S1prs except for S1pr2 were upregulated in MI+LIP+5Mix mice (Figure 6M). The immunosuppressive drug FTY720 blocks the egress of lymphocytes in lymph nodes.53 Treatment with FTY720 successfully alleviated the cardiac dysfunction of MI+LIP+5Mix mice (Figure 6N; Figure S7M and S7O). FTY720 inhibited B2-cell trafficking from cLN to the heart through the blood, evidenced by substantially increased B2 and CXCR5+ B2 cells in cLNs and decreased B2 cells in hearts and blood (Figure 6O; Figure S7P through S7Q). FTY720 induced increased S1P accumulation in cLNs and reduced S1PRs expression of B2 cells (Figure S7R and S7S). Consistent with the effects of FTY720, S1PR1+/− mice manifested significantly improved cardiac function compared with S1PR1+/+ mice after MI+LIP+5Mix induction (Figure 6P). B2 cells were significantly increased in cLNs and decreased in hearts and blood of S1PR1+/− mice (Figure 6Q; Figure S7T). TNF-α+ B2 cells and IL-6+ B2 cells were also decreased in the infarcted hearts of S1PR1+/− mice (Figure 6R; Figure S7U). These results indicated that oral pathobionts promoted lymphocyte egress through S1P-S1PR signaling, leading to the cardiac accumulation of cLN-derived pathobiont-reactive B2 cells after MI.

Oral Pathobionts Aggravate MI by Impairment of Epithelial and Endothelial Cell Barriers

Because we identified that oral pathobionts accumulated in the oral ligature and infarcted hearts in MI+LIP+12Mix mice, we investigated the invasion properties of these strains. Transwell experiments showed that the 5 MI-aggravating bacteria could penetrate the barriers of both human oral keratinocytes and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (Figure 7A through 7C). The 5 MI-aggravating bacteria also showed more attachment to human oral keratinocytes and human umbilical vein endothelial cells than L buccalis control (Figure 7D and 7E). The disruption of barrier function elicited by oral pathobionts is an important pathological process linking to systemic diseases.54–56 E-cadherin and Ve-cadherin are crucial intercellular adhesion molecules for barrier integrity.57,58 We thus examined the expression of E/Ve-cadherin by flow cytometry and Western blotting. The 5 MI-aggravating bacteria together or individually decreased the expression of E/Ve-cadherin both in vivo (oral mucosa and infarcted hearts) and in vitro (human oral keratinocytes and human umbilical vein endothelial cells) compared with vehicle, whereas L buccalis did not (Figure 7F through 7I). These results indicate that 5Mix exerted the deteriorating effects on MI through stronger bacterial adhesion and invasion than the others.

To decipher the underlying mechanism that oral pathobionts attach and invade cell barrier, we used homologous recombination to generate a F nucleatum (Fn) △radD mutant strain and a P gingivalis (Pg) △rgpB mutant strain, both of which were deficient in adhesion protein expression. The decreased expression of E/Ve-cadherin triggered by the WT strains was rescued by Fn △radD and Pg △rgpB in both human oral keratinocytes and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (Figure 7J and 7K). Pg △rgpB and Fn ∆radD markedly reversed cardiac dysfunction after MI (Figure 7L through 7N) and diminished B-cell accumulation in cLNs and hearts compared with their WT counterparts (Figure 7O and 7P). Moreover, adhesion protein deficiency in Pg and Fn also impaired the CXCL13 production from macrophages in infarcted hearts (Figure 7Q and 7R). Pg ∆rgpB and Fn ∆radD also significantly suppressed the extravasation of fibrinogen out of cardiac vasculature compared with the WT strains (Figure 7S and 7T), suggesting that bacterial adhesion and invasion mediated the damage of vascular endothelial barrier and increased vascular permeability in mice under the condition of MI+LIP+pathobionts. Consistently, we also observed decreased accumulations of Pg △rgpB and Fn △radD compared with the WT strains in infarcted hearts (Figure 7U and 7V). These results implied that bacterial attachment and invasion mediated the ectopic accumulation of oral pathobionts in infarcted hearts and the MI-aggravating effects.

Oral Pathobionts Aggravate MI in GF Mice and Contribute to Decreased Cardiac Function in Patients With MI and PD

To validate the exacerbating effects of LIP and oral pathobionts on MI, 5 MI-aggravating strains were individually inoculated into GF mice after LIP, and bacteria significantly accumulated in the oral cavity of mice (Figure S8A). Different from the results under specific pathogen-free condition, echocardiography and Masson trichrome staining showed that LIP+Vehicle did not deteriorate cardiac dysfunction of GF mice compared with Vehicle after MI (Figure 8A through 8D). However, all oral strain-constituted gnotobiotic mice experienced worse cardiac dysfunction and larger infarct size than the LIP+Vehicle GF mice after MI (Figure 8A through 8D). Consistent with the results in specific pathogen-free mice treated with 5Mix, the individual oral pathobionts elevated CXCL13 and S1P levels in plasma but reduced the S1P levels in cLNs of gnotobiotic mice after MI (Figure 8E and 8F). Immunofluorescence demonstrated that oral pathobionts significantly increased CXCL13 expression in the cardiac infarcted region (Figure S8B and S8C). Efficient induction of B2 cells, IL-6+ B2, and TNF-α+ B2 cells in cLNs (Figure 8G) and hearts (Figure 8H) of gnotobiotic mice was observed after MI, although some strains showed only increasing trends. It is worth pointing out that LIP+Vehicle significantly decreased S1P in cLNs of GF mice after MI, which may suggest more egress of lymphocytes from cLNs (Figure 8F) but did not lead to accumulation of B2 cells in the heart (Figure 8G and 8H), implying that oral pathobionts were essential for B-cell expansion in cLNs and recruitment to infarcted hearts. These results confirmed the pathogenicity of oral pathobionts on MI during PD and suggested microbiota dependence of this phenotype.

Last, to gain more clinical translational perspective, we extended our analysis to patients (Table S1) with MI (n=36) and those with MI and PD (MI+PD; n=36). Patients with MI+PD manifested more severe cardiac dysfunction as indicated by decreased ejection fraction and increased blood BNP (brain natriuretic peptide) (Figure 8I). Following the gating strategy previously reported,59 our flow cytometry analysis demonstrated increased CD19+CD20+ B cells and IL-6+ and TNF-α+ memory B2 cells in the blood of patients with MI+PD (Figure 8J; Figure S8D and S8E). ELISA showed elevated plasma levels of CXCL13, S1P, and LPS in patients with MI+PD (Figure 8K). 16S rRNA gene sequencing of PL identified higher α diversity in patients with MI+PD and significantly different β diversity between the 2 groups (Figure S8F and S8G). Linear discriminant effect size analysis revealed that the 4 MI-aggravating bacterial strains (S sputigena, P intermedia, P gingivalis, and F nucleatum ss vincentii) were significantly enriched in the MI+PD group (Figure 8L), and the last 3 were also detected by random forest regression analysis (Figure S8H). The total relative abundance of the 4 MI-aggravating strains significantly correlated with ejection fraction, blood CD19+CD20+ B cells, and plasma CXCL13 in patients with MI (Figure 8M). These results suggested that the adverse effects of oral pathobiont–elicited proinflammatory B2 cells on MI exist not only in mice but also in humans. Last, we summarized the mechanism by which oral pathobionts exacerbated MI (Figure 8N).

Discussion

Patients with PD have an increased risk of MI, but the precise role of oral pathobionts connecting these 2 diseases remains unclear. Here, we demonstrated that LIP and PL exacerbated MI in mice and identified 5 MI-aggravating oral pathobionts (S sputigena, P intermedia, P gingivalis, F nucleatum ss vincentii, and B plebeius). These oral pathobionts invaded infarcted hearts by impairment of epithelial and endothelial cell barriers and promoted the mobilization of proinflammatory B2 cells from cLNs and the infiltration of these cells to infarcted hearts, which were regulated by the S1P-S1PR and CXCL13-CXCR5 axes, respectively. Furthermore, the deleterious effects and mechanisms of these oral pathobionts on MI were verified in GF mice and human patients.

Our results substantiated the detrimental impacts of PD on MI. Not only does PD increase the risk of MI, but its extent and severity are also positively correlated with the size of acute MI in patients.3,5 We observed that LIP, particularly in combination with PL, substantially decreased cardiac functions and increased cardiac infarct size after MI. Strong evidence supports the contributions of systemic inflammation to MI.60 Patients with MI and PD have higher levels of inflammation than those without PD,61 suggesting that inflammation may be a pathogenic association between PD and MI. Consistently, our investigations demonstrated that LIP and PL rapidly elicited increased inflammation in periodontal tissues and infarcted hearts, further supporting the aggravating effects of PD on MI.

PD and MI together provided an essential route for the seeding and cardiac invasion of oral pathobionts. On one hand, LIP and oral pathobionts increased inflammation levels of periodontal tissues, endorsing oral bacteria to enter the bloodstream because inflamed periodontal epithelium and vasculature would create favorable conditions for systemic seeding of oral pathobionts from adjacent gingival microcirculation.23,62 Accordingly, we detected higher levels of LPS in the plasma of LIP mice inoculated with oral pathobionts and that of patients with MI and PD, suggesting a higher level of bacteremia related to oral bacteria. On the other hand, our results suggested that ectopic accumulation of oral bacteria in the heart depended on cardiac injury because no bacteria accumulation was detected in the hearts of sham groups. Recent studies have indicated that bacteria directly invade hearts, often through endothelial lesions and immune-pathogen interactions.63 MI induces intense immune inflammatory responses in ischemic and hypoxic cardiac tissue, causing increased permeability of blood vessels by endothelial dysfunction.64 We observed that 9 oral strains, including several non–MI-aggravating bacteria, accumulated in the heart after induction by LIP and the mixture of 12 pathobionts, which suggested the synergistic effect between oral strains. MI-aggravating strains induced more serious damage to epithelial and endothelial cell barriers than non–MI-aggravating ones and increased vascular permeability and bacterial ectopic accumulation. The severe cardiac dysfunction and vascular permeability caused by WT strains after MI were improved when their adhesins were deficient. Our findings provided evidence that oral pathobionts invaded infarcted hearts through systemic circulation under the condition of periodontal inflammation.

Oral pathobionts played major roles in the aggravating effects of PD on MI. Previous studies have indicated oral microbiota as a key link between PD and MI.15,65 We systematically screened oral pathobionts that affected MI. Our data showed that several oral bacteria, including the periodontal pathobionts P intermedia, F nucleatum, S sputigena, and P gingivalis originating from PL and B plebeius from mice, accumulated in the oral cavity and infarcted hearts under the condition of PD and exacerbated MI. The presence of periodontal pathobionts, including P intermedia, P gingivalis, and F nucleatum, is associated with increased odds of having MI.66,67 Animal studies have demonstrated that gingipain/LPS of P gingivalis promotes cardiac rupture by stimulating matrix metalloproteinases.32 However, almost all related studies have focused on P gingivalis.31,33,34,68 P intermedia and F nucleatum contribute to the progression of atherosclerosis,69,70 an essential pathological basis for MI. The present study is the first to report the direct impacts of these 2 oral pathobionts on MI. Clinical observations have indicated that B plebeius is enriched in feces of patients with hypertension or cardiac valve calcification.71,72 The present study is also the first to report the impacts of B plebeius on MI. Furthermore, the role of S sputigena in cardiovascular diseases was unknown before this study. Taking these findings together, we comprehensively revealed the exacerbating effects of oral pathobionts on MI.

Oral pathobionts exacerbated MI by eliciting proinflammatory B cells. Recent studies have demonstrated the fundamental function of B cells in mediating immune responses after infection.73 Our results showed that cLNs, the local drainage lymph nodes near the oral cavity, were swollen after LIP and inoculation of oral pathobionts and that the number of B cells was drastically increased. B cells rapidly undergo mitosis in the germinal center through antigen-specific clonal proliferation.74 Oral pathobionts increased Ki67+ B cells in cLNs, implying enhanced B-cell generation when oral pathobionts provided rich antigens for immune responses. cLN excision significantly relieved the cardiac dysfunction induced by oral pathobionts under LIP condition after MI, highlighting their importance for the pathogenesis of aggravated MI. Using Kaede mice, our study indicated that transmigration of oral pathobiont–reactive B cells from cLN to hearts after MI was more frequent than normal B cells. We further identified the cardiac accumulation of oral pathobiont–reactive B cells as a crucial mechanism contributing to the aggravation of MI. Previous studies have illustrated the pathological roles of B cells in MI.39,75 The present study is the first to report a significant increase of B2-cell subsets secreting the inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α in infarcted cardiac tissue after MI, suggesting a substantial contribution of oral pathobionts to cardiac inflammation in MI. In GF mice, LIP did not activate B2 cells or influence MI without bacteria inoculation, further substantiating the importance of oral pathobionts. Consistent with animal experiments, clinical observations revealed that patients with MI and PD had worse cardiac function and higher CD19+CD20+ B cells, IL-6+ memory B2 cells, and TNF-α+ memory B2 cells than patients with MI without PD. B lymphocytes play crucial roles in mediating both innate and adaptive immune responses through antibody-dependent and antibody-independent mechanisms.73 Although we observed elevated levels of blood IgG in mice after induction with LIP and oral pathobionts, IgG specific to these strains was not significantly correlated with cardiac functions. Our present work demonstrated the antibody-independent mechanisms of oral pathobiont–reactive B on MI, although the direct role of antigen-specific IgG in exacerbating MI needs to be further verified after purification in future investigations. These results supported the functional relevance of the proinflammatory B2 cells in MI induced by LIP and human periodontal pathogens.

The coordination of a 2-part mechanism regulated the transmigration of oral pathobiont–reactive B cells from cLNs to infarcted hearts. One part involved the S1P-S1PRs axis that primed B cells to egress from cLNs into circulation. cLNs were crucial elements linking PD and systemic diseases.28 Our results showed that cLN excision markedly decreased blood B cells, implying that the oral pathobiont–reactive B cells migrated from cLNs into the circulation. It has been reported that the egress of B cells from cLNs is regulated by S1P/S1PRs.76 LIP and oral pathobionts reduced the concentration of S1P and elevated S1PR expression of B cells in the swelling cLNs, resulting in increased S1P levels in the blood. Treatment with the FTY720 suppressed B-cell emigration from cLNs, decreased the S1PRs expression, and increased S1P levels in cLNs. Accordingly, improved cardiac function caused by FTY720 can be explained by B-cell retention in cLNs and B-cell reduction in blood and infarcted hearts after MI. The effects of FTY720 were further elaborated by the phenotypes in S1pr1+/− mice. These findings indicated the strong inducibility of oral pathobionts on B-cell egress from lymph nodes. However, the relationship between the S1P-S1PRs pathway and microbiota has yet to be fully investigated. It has been shown that infection of Pseudomonas aeruginosa elevates S1P level in the lungs by sphingolipid kinases.77 Our previous clinical studies have identified the correlations between S1P and oral/gut microbiota in a hypertension cohort.78 In the present study, our clinical observations showed higher S1P and B cells in patients with MI and PD, also suggesting S1P-mediated migration of B cells. Future research is needed to clarify the mechanisms responsible for S1P production and degradation induced by oral pathobionts.

In the other part of the mechanism, oral pathobionts promoted cardiac macrophages secreting more CXCL13 to recruit cLN-derived CXCR5+ B2-cell subsets. The importance of the CXCL13-CXCR5 axis for the migration and retention of B cells has been well documented.46 Under physiological conditions, CXCL13 is produced mainly by fibroblastic reticular cells and functions in secondary lymphatic organs.79 B cells from bone marrow and spleen infiltrate the infarcted heart and affect cardiac function directly or indirectly under MI conditions,75,80,81 although the underlying mechanism still needs further explanation. Macrophages are an important source of CXCL13 on LPS stimulation or viral infection.82,83 In line with these data, we showed that CXCL13 secretion from oral pathobiont–induced macrophages was increased in infarcted hearts. Furthermore, the plasma CXCL13 concentration positively correlated with the relative abundance of periodontal pathobionts in patients with MI. These results suggested that the ability to induce CXCL13 from macrophages was a notable characteristic of oral pathobionts. More mechanistically, upregulation of Nfκb1, Nfκb2, Rara, and Oct4 on stimulation by oral pathobionts enhanced CXCL13 expression in macrophages. LPS is a potent agonist for TLR4.84 LPS from oral pathobionts might have activated nuclear factor-κB through TLR4–MyD88, leading to upregulated CXCL13 secretion in macrophages. We observed that oral pathobionts promoted CXCR5+ B2 cells and CXCL13 to accumulate in infarcted regions after MI, suggesting that this B-cell subset was attracted to CXCL13-rich sites. B2-cell depletion and adoptive transfer experiments revealed that pathobionts-reactive B-cell accumulation was a causative factor leading to exacerbation of MI, not a bystander. Oral pathobionts aggravated MI by inducing the cLN-derived B2 subset to secret more proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α, the canonical and noncanonical nuclear factor-κB activators,85,86 which may further enhance the transcription of CXCL13. CXCL13 deficiency impaired B-cell chemotaxis in infarcted hearts of oral pathobiont–inoculated mice and toward the conditioned media of oral pathobiont–stimulated macrophages. Therefore, a positive feedback loop may form between the recruitment of proinflammatory B2 cells and CXCL13 secretion from macrophages in the process of MI aggravation by oral pathobionts.

Emerging research has explored the various mechanisms underlying the relationship between oral health and cardiovascular diseases. A latest report indicated that SiglecF+ neutrophils increased in infarcted hearts with PD and deteriorated MI by inducing excessive fibrosis.87 Our results suggest that the ectopic accumulation of oral bacteria and pathobiont-reactive B2 cells derived from cLNs in infarcted hearts was necessary for the deterioration of MI. We further unveiled the mechanistic pathway by which PD aggravated MI through microbiological and immunological regulations. In the context of oral pathobionts and MI, our findings provide a conceptual framework for the oral-heart axis, which relies on bacterial invasion, expansion of pathogenic B cells, and their transmigration into the heart. Combining our study with other reports in this field suggests that targeting PD and oral pathobionts may provide therapeutic interventions to ameliorate MI and many other comorbidities of PD.
