Development of thoracic spine kyphosis and lumbar spine lordosis in the growing child from birth to adulthood: protocol for a systematic review
Authors: Omar Al-Allaf, Omar Ezzat Saber Mostafa, Nicola Henegan, Holly Austin-Davies, A Gardner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThoracic kyphosis, a posterior curvature of the thoracic spine and lumbar lordosis, an anterior curvature of the lumbar spine, are essential components of spinal alignment that facilitate a biomechanically efficient upright stance. Understanding the normal growth of the spine and its constituents, especially during periods of rapid growth, is important as deviations from normal growth are thought to be initiating factors of spinal conditions such as scoliosis. While in the coronal plane the spine is straight, there is variability in the reported normative values of sagittal spinal shape, reported in the paediatric population. This variability may well represent the range of normality allowing for population differences of ethnicity and sex, but could also be attributed to differing imaging techniques and measurement methods. The aim of this systematic review is to investigate the normative values for thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis between birth and adulthood. The review’s findings should enhance the understanding of the development of the spine and aid in establishing the range that results from differences in techniques of measurement and imaging modalities used.Methods and analysisThis review protocol is reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols. Inclusion criteria are participants aged 18 years or less without pain or spinal pathology, such as scoliosis, any spinal or thoracic surgery and any other medical conditions that could affect spinal growth and development. The review will consider studies reporting serial quantitative measurements of thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis. Inclusion will be limited to studies published in English. The PubMed, Embase, Ovid MEDLINE and Cochrane Library databases will be searched. Following Cochrane guidance, a dual reviewer system will be used where both reviewers will screen 20% of titles and abstracts to confirm concordance, and then one reviewer will screen the remaining articles. During full-text screening, one reviewer will review all the full-text articles while the second reviewer will review all the articles excluded by the first reviewer, with a third reviewer being involved to resolve any discrepancies. A risk of bias assessment will be performed on the identified studies. The data extracted will be analysed through meta-analysis if it proves feasible, synthesising continuous data such as the size of the kyphosis and lordosis, with further subcategorisation by sex and ethnicity as appropriate. Correlation coefficients will be used to analyse relationships between variables, and I2 values will be used to assess heterogeneity. Alternatively, if a meta-analysis is not possible, the data will be presented narratively using the synthesis without meta-analysis guidelines. An overall statement of confidence in the cumulative results and findings will be provided using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation system.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required for this study as no original data will be collected. The findings will be disseminated to academic audiences through peer-reviewed journals and conference presentations.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024502782.Keywords: Spine, Musculoskeletal disorders, Child, Scoliosis, Anatomy, Posture

Introduction

Thoracic kyphosis is described as the curvature of the thoracic spine in the sagittal plane in which the convexity of the curve is directed posteriorly.1 Lumbar lordosis is described as the curvature of the lumbar spine in the sagittal plane in which the convexity of the curve is directed anteriorly.2 The combination of thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis is part of the mechanism, along with the shape of the pelvis, that allows humans to adopt a biomechanically efficient upright stance.3 It is of importance to understand the development of both the sagittal shape of the whole spine, and of the constituent parts of that shape during normal growth for a number of reasons. Of particular interest is the understanding of the development of spinal pathology in children and adolescents related to an abnormal development of spinal shape, given that alterations in spinal growth away from normality have been described as the initiating factor for the development of scoliosis.3 4 This literature describes an abnormal relationship between the amount of thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis during childhood and adolescence leading to the development of intervertebral rotational deformities and scoliosis4 with a negative effect on quality of life and overall health of affected individuals.5

More recently, the development of ‘vertebral body tethering’, a technique described that manipulates the growth of the child to correct their own scoliosis through the inhibition of the growth of the convexity of a scoliosis, has been described.6 Vertebral body tethering requires an understanding of spinal growth and development such that predictions of the timing and type of intervention can be made to correct a scoliotic deformity. This is analogous to the use of the ‘eight plate’ used to correct the ‘bow legs’ and ‘knock knees’ in paediatric knee deformities where a nomogram exists that describes when the intervention should be applied and removed to correct the deformity based on age, growth remaining and size of deformity.7

There has been previously published literature that discusses the shape of the spine in terms of the magnitude of the kyphosis and lordosis in adults where the growth of the spine has ceased and is not a factor that requires consideration.8 However, it is recognised that the shape of the spine changes during growth. Through the use of surface topography methods, both Willner and Johnson9 and Gardner et al10 have separately shown that not only does the amount of kyphosis and lordosis change with age, but that a difference between males and females is also seen with females generally having a larger lumbar lordosis than males, which increases with age for both sexes. However, while thoracic kyphosis in males increases with age, females have a period of relative flattening of the thoracic spine in their early to mid-teenage years.10

The literature also describes a number of different methods for the measurement of the shape of the spine. Classically, thoracic kyphosis has been defined as the angle formed between the superior endplate of T1 and the inferior endplate of T12 in the sagittal plane.11 Lumbar lordosis is correspondingly defined as between the superior endplate of L1 and superior endplate of S1.11 Historically, radiographic studies have struggled to accurately image the superior thoracic spine due to the radiographic density of the shoulder girdle and as such the reported superior limit of measurement has varied between the T1 and T5 vertebral bodies.12,14 In studies that use a surface topography measure, the point of change between the thoracic kyphosis to the lumbar lordosis is not predefined to a particular vertebral level, rather is based around the point of inflection where kyphosis becomes lordosis, accepting that there will be some variability to this between subjects.9 10 This difference in the upper and lower limits of measurement may contribute to variability in reported values between studies. Adding to this, there are a number of different clinical tools described for the measurement of the size of the thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis, including radiographs, inclinometers, De Brunner’s kyphometers and flexicurves,15,18 along with a number of different angles that have been described with the Cobb angle widely regarded as the gold standard.19,23 The end result of a number of measurement techniques and angles described in the literature reports a range of results evidenced by papers quoting normal thoracic kyphosis in children that varies between 21–33° 24 and 20–50°.25 It may be reasonable to suggest that the differences in measurement methods and the demographics of the samples studied contribute to the variation in reported normative values for kyphosis and lordosis in the paediatric population.

With no established agreement on the normal levels of thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis in males and females during growth, it is crucial to understand the various imaging modalities, measurement methods and resulting normative values cited in the current literature. There remains a knowledge gap in our understanding of how the spine develops in children without pathology, preventing the definition of the range of normality and subsequently the ability to describe when a spinal shape ceases being within the expected range and becomes abnormal.

Study objectives

The primary objective is to evaluate and summarise normative values for thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis throughout childhood and adolescence.

The secondary objective is to explore the potential influence of sex and ethnicity on the development of thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis.

Methods and analysis

This protocol has been devised following collaboration between experts in spinal surgery, research and musculoskeletal rehabilitation (AG and NH). In accordance with the National Health Service research ethics tool, ethical approval is not required for this systematic review.

To ensure quality of reporting and prevent duplication or publication bias, this review protocol is reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Protocols26 (see online supplemental appendix 1) and is registered on the PROSPERO database (CRD42024502782).

Eligibility criteria

Participants

Inclusion criteria: participants aged 18 years and below without spinal pathology or pain.

Exclusion criteria: restrictions will be applied to any participants with confounding factors or associated medical conditions that may affect growth of the spine or spinal alignment including: scoliosis of any cause, spinal surgery for any reason, surgery for any reason that involved a sternotomy or thoracotomy of any type, Scheuermann’s disease or other causes of hyperkyphosis, spinal pain, vertebral fractures, rheumatological conditions, history of spinal trauma, history of cardiac or lung disease, history of cancer and history of genetic conditions affecting the neurological or musculoskeletal systems.

Outcomes

Studies reporting quantitative measurements of the size of kyphosis of the thoracic spine and lordosis of the lumbar spine will be included. Thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis will be treated as two distinct and separate parameters. Studies reporting either or both measurements will be included and data of each curvature will be extracted and analysed separately. No restriction will be applied to the imaging modality or method of measurement used.

Study designs

This review will include observational studies, either cross-sectional or longitudinal. Conference abstracts will be excluded due to concerns around the presentation of the data with inconsistencies between the data found in the conference abstract and subsequent published full text.27 Opinion papers will also be excluded. Any previous reviews will be reviewed for any information that adds to this systematic review that would not be considered otherwise.

Search strategy

There will be systematic searches of electronic databases provided by PubMed, Embase, Ovid MEDLINE and the Cochrane Library. Search blocks will include terms relevant to the population of interest and the outcomes identified (table 1). No restrictions to the year of publication will be applied. To ensure the review is up-to-date, the search will be conducted initially and then repeated prior to the final data synthesis to capture any new or missed studies. The research will be limited to studies published in English. Search records will be uploaded into RefWorks28 and duplicates will be excluded.

Study selection process

A dual reviewer system will be implemented29 which following Cochrane guidance instructs the two reviewers to initially collaborate with a standardised form that lists titles and abstracts from the same 100 articles, to calibrate and test that both reviewers are implementing the inclusion and exclusion criteria in the same way. Following this, the two reviewers will each independently screen the same 20% of abstracts, with conflict resolution by a third reviewer to confirm concordance of review. Then one main reviewer will screen the remaining abstracts for appropriate papers to include in the review.

During the full-text screening of the previously identified abstracts, the two reviewers will conduct a pilot exercise together, examining the same 10 articles to calibrate and test the review form, again to confirm that each reviewer performing the review appropriately. The main reviewer will then review the remaining full-text articles independently. Once this is completed, a second reviewer will review all the full-text articles excluded by the first reviewer to confirm that they should be excluded. A third reviewer will be involved in resolving any discrepancies. A PRISMA flow diagram with the proposed layout and subheadings is included below in online supplemental appendix 3.

Risk of bias assessment

The main reviewer will independently assess the quality and reliability of each study using the Anatomical Quality Assessment (AQUA) tool.30 The second reviewer will then verify the judgements of the first reviewer. The AQUA tool was developed in collaboration with experts in anatomical research to assess the quality and reliability of anatomical studies based on five key domains of: (1) objectives and subject characteristics, (2) study design, (3) methodology characterisation, (4) descriptive anatomy and (5) reporting of results. Each domain consists of specific questions with binary responses (yes, no or unclear) aimed at facilitating the assessment of study quality. To be deemed low risk of bias within a single domain, all questions within that domain must receive affirmative answers.

However, determining the overall quality of a study poses a challenge, as guidance on this aspect is lacking. Therefore, our research team established criteria: for a study to achieve high-quality status, it must exhibit low risk of bias across all five domains. Studies demonstrating low risk of bias in three or four domains are deemed moderate quality, while those falling short in multiple domains are considered low quality.

Data items to be extracted

In cases where data are missing or unclear, we will attempt to contact the authors of the original studies to request the necessary information. This will be done through email correspondence, providing a clear explanation of the missing data and its relevance to our review. If the authors do not respond after a reasonable period or if the data remain unavailable, we will acknowledge the missing data in our analysis and discuss its potential impact on the results in the limitations section.

This review will treat thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis as independent outcomes and conduct two parallel analyses. In studies where both thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis are reported, we will extract the data of each curvature separately. The extracted study data from each paper will be imported into Cochrane Review Manager (RevMan Web).31 Correlations between variables will be analysed using correlation coefficients, with a p value of <0.05 set as the threshold for statistical significance. This p value is chosen to determine if there are statistically significant differences between groups (eg, differences in age group, sex or ethnicity) regarding the normative values of each spinal curvature, with analyses conducted separately for kyphosis and lordosis. It is likely that different imaging modalities will have been used in both a supine and upright positioning, using different anatomical points at the limits of measurement. Consequently, and where feasible, subgroup analyses will be performed based on imaging modality and anatomical landmarks used in each study to assess whether different measurement techniques contribute to variability in normative values. Subgroup analyses will also be conducted, where feasible, to assess the influence of imaging position (supine/weight bearing) on normative values of kyphosis and lordosis.

If a meta-analysis is viable, continuous data on the normative values of kyphosis and lordosis will be synthesised to generate the mean value, SD and range, subcategorised by age, sex and ethnicity where appropriate. The I2 statistic will be used to measure heterogeneity; values below 25% will be regarded as low heterogeneity, values between 25% and 75% as moderate heterogeneity and values above 75% as high heterogeneity.32 To assess the effect of using random versus fixed effects models on the overall results, we will perform a sensitivity analysis, taking into account the potential variability in study populations and measurement techniques. The fixed effects model makes the assumption that all studies have the same normative values, which may be appropriate if the populations and methods of measurement described in the study are highly similar.33 However, because of the expected heterogeneity resulting from variations in methodology and demographics, the random effects model is likely to offer a more precise and broadly applicable summary of the normative values, reflecting the real-world differences among the studies.

If the criteria for a meta-analysis are met, results will be synthesised and presented with detailed tables of participant demographics, study characteristics and summary statistics. Plots will be used to visualise the normative values and CIs for each study included in the meta-analysis, highlighting key findings and correlations between variables. If a meta-analysis is not feasible due to data heterogeneity, findings will be presented narratively in accordance with PRISMA34 and the synthesis without meta-analysis guidelines.35

To provide a statement of confidence in the cumulative results and findings, we will use the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system.36 The GRADE system evaluates the quality of evidence across studies by considering factors such as study design, consistency of results, directness of evidence, precision and risk of bias.36 This approach allows us to categorise the quality of evidence as high, moderate or low and enhances the reliability of our conclusions by offering a structured, transparent and systematic evaluation of the evidence.

Discussion

The planned systematic review will investigate the development of thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis during childhood and adolescence. This period is crucial as the spine, along with the rest of the body, undergoes significant growth during this time. By systematically reviewing the development of both thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis throughout the growth of a child, the review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of how these spinal curvatures evolve, highlighting normal variations and developmental patterns at different ages in different populations and between males and females, which are issues that remain unclear in the current literature. The review will also examine the effects of methods of measurement and different imaging modalities to establish a baseline for consistency and comparability in future research.

The implications of this systematic review are significant for the understanding of normal spinal development during the growth of the child from birth to adulthood. By describing the normal development of the shape of the spine in the sagittal plane, the review will help delineate the boundary between normality and abnormality. This distinction is crucial for diagnosing and understanding abnormalities in the sagittal plane which are thought to contribute to scoliosis37 and abnormal amounts of kyphosis,38 conditions that can have lifelong implications for affected individuals.5 Public health initiatives could also benefit from these insights, potentially allowing early preventive measures to mitigate the impact of spinal pathologies, such as screening programmes for early detection of abnormal spinal curvatures, educational campaigns and physical activity guidelines aimed at maintaining spinal health. These measures could help mitigate the long-term impact of spinal pathologies by identifying and addressing issues before they progress.

The findings from this review will serve as a foundation for future epidemiological studies by providing baseline data on the prevalence and development of spinal curvatures in healthy populations. This will help to track trends, identify risk factors for spinal abnormalities and support the development of preventive strategies. Additionally, the findings will inform biomechanical studies by offering normative values that can be used to understand the mechanical forces at play in spinal development. By providing a clearer understanding of normal spinal growth, the review will add to the knowledge of the aetiology of paediatric spinal deformities, guiding future research and clinical practices. Furthermore, insights into how spinal curvature development differs between males and females can lead to more tailored approaches in both clinical and research settings, addressing specific needs based on sex-specific growth patterns.

The strengths of this review include a robust protocol that follows appropriate guidelines, searches across multiple databases, use of the appropriate risk of bias tool for anatomical studies, predefined criteria for performance of a meta-analysis and the use of GRADE for an overall assessment of the quality of the evidence (performed by two reviewers independently). There are some limitations to acknowledge, however. First, the decision to restrict studies to only include those published in English may introduce language bias, potentially limiting the global applicability. Second, while the aim of the review would be to perform a meta-analysis, the potential variability caused via the different imaging modalities, anatomical landmarks used for the limits of measurement and different measurement techniques may limit comparability and precision in pooled estimates.

In summary, the systematic review outlined in this protocol aims to fill significant gaps in the current understanding of spinal curvature development in children and adolescents. By providing a clearer picture of normal development, the review will offer valuable guidance for future research, clinical practice and public health initiatives, ultimately improving outcomes for individuals with spinal deformities.
