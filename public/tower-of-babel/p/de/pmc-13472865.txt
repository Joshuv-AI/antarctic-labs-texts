Metabolic determinants of cancer immunotherapy outcomes identified by plasma profiling
Authors: Déborah Suissa, Marine Fidelle, Ella Reich, Thao-Nguyen Pham, Simon Thomas, Johannes R Björk, Peng Liu, Liwei Zhao, Koji Kitaoka, Eléonore Piard, Isabelle Lebhar, Ai-Ling Tian, Cassandra Thelemaque, Carolina Alves Costa Silva, Eric Deutsch, Yohann Loriot, Nicola Segata, Gianmarco Piccinno, Geke A P Hospers, Saman Maleki Vareki, Michael S Silverman, John G Lenehan, Véronique Bataille, David Boulate, Tatiana Kuznetsova, Rinse K Weersma, Meriem Messaoudene, Sylvère Durand, Carlijn M van der Aalst, Harry J de Koning, Beatrice Schuler-Thurner, I Jolanda M de Vries, Edmond Rafie, Renée Maria Saliby, Marc Machaalani, Sebastian Haferkamp, Bastian Schilling, Serena Porcari, Chiara Ciccarese, Roberto Iacovelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune-checkpoint inhibitors benefit a subset of patients with advanced cancer, and the metabolic determinants of response remain unclear. Here, using targeted metabolomics and metagenomics, we profiled 4,336 plasma samples from 1,714 patients across five tumor types and 16 cohorts spanning Europe and North America, longitudinally sampled during five immune-checkpoint inhibitor-based treatment modalities, including fecal microbiota transplantation. A multimodal machine-learning framework integrating 154 metabolites with clinical variables identified five metabolites, age, body mass index and renal function as predictors of 12-month progression-free survival. The model achieved areas under the curve of 0.88 in training and 0.73 in validation cohorts of 105 and 30 patients, respectively and generalized across seven external cohorts. Histidine was a favorable prognostic feature of survival, whereas long-chain fatty acids and succinate were negatively associated with outcome. Histidine supplementation enhanced antitumor immunity in mice. Histidine-rich diets improved progression-free survival in patients lacking dysbiotic microbiome signatures associated with histidine catabolism.Subject terms: Cancer metabolism, Cancer therapy

Main

Metabolic checkpoints refer to a series of metabolic switches that ensure an accurate and dynamic conversion of distinct metabolic pathways particularly critical for the transition from tolerance to immunogenicity1–7, potentially relevant to account for immune mechanisms of resistance. They regulate development, differentiation and function of dendritic cells (DCs) and T cells, in a competing landscape with fibroblasts, myeloid suppressors and tumor cells for nutrients in the tumor microenvironment (TME)8–19. Furthermore, metabolic checkpoints can modulate the expression profiles of immune-checkpoint receptors and ligands8,17,20. Finally, our metabolism results from complex host–commensal interactions. The host–microbe immunological axis influences key physiological processes, including metabolism, epithelial integrity and brain functions. Microbial therapies leveraging microbial metabolites have emerged as new therapeutic strategies21–23. Therefore, interventions targeting the microenvironmental or systemic metabolism might restrain immunoresistance, urging for immune-checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based clinical trials targeting biologically relevant metabolic pathways4,24–29. Some studies focused on the analysis of metabolomics (MB) of plasma samples at diagnosis and/or during immunotherapy in selected cohorts of patients with cancer30–35. While inhibitors targeting the adenosine and tryptophan (Trp) pathways have reached the clinical stage of development, metabolic modulators and dietary nutrients that can improve the anticancer immune response remain unmet medical needs4.

We conducted an unbiased, MB analysis on 4,336 early on-treatment plasma samples from 1,714 patients enrolled in 16 independent cohorts, from eight different geographical sites, bearing five distinct tumor types, which included longitudinal follow-up. The study encompassed five different ICI-based treatment modalities, including fecal microbial transplantation (FMT). Using state-of-the-art methods of machine learning (ML), our goal was twofold: (1) to uncover a low-dimensional, uncorrelated clinical–metabolic blood signature strongly associated with treatment response, and (2) validate its robustness and relevance in clinical outcome. These analyses were conducted using training and validation cohorts (Fig. 1) and the DynForest36 (a random forest-based joint longitudinal survival model) followed by a rigorous statistical ablation framework to uncover the most relevant biomarkers for progression-free survival (PFS). The retained model was validated in seven external cohorts comprising 1,523 patients in Cox regression. We next used fecal MB, metagenomics, immunomics and animal models to explore the biological relevance of our best hit (Fig. 1). While succinate and long-chain fatty acids were associated with treatment failure, histidine emerged as the most consistent dynamic biomarker for successful immunotherapy, resulting from protein-rich diets and microbiota fitness.

Results

Biomarker discovery of progression-free survival in immunotherapy-treated patients with cancer via robust machine learning on clinical and metabolomic data

Leveraging a comprehensive, multicohort compendium of plasma and fecal MB data (Extended Data Fig. 1a), we analyzed 4,956 specimens (4,336 plasma and 620 fecal samples) collected across 14 distinct cohorts within the ONCOBIOME network37 together with two independent external cohorts (PRIMM38,39, CheckMate-025 (ref. 40); Fig. 2a). The cohorts spanned lung, colon, kidney, bladder and skin cancer, covering diverse treatment strategies, such as ICI with or without FMT, chemotherapy (CT), radiotherapy (RX) and/or tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) (Supplementary Table 1a–k). They also covered a spectrum of clinical stages, from patients with high-risk early-stage to advanced (stages III and IV) disease (Fig. 2a, Extended Data Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1a–k). Metabolite categories and comprehensive clinical covariates, including age, sex, treatment modalities, number of longitudinal samples per patient, cancer stage and body mass index (BMI) are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1a–f.

We developed a predictive, low-dimensional, longitudinal ML model using a training set composed of two heterogeneous cohorts (IML1 (NCT04567446; Supplementary Table 1a) and stereotactic ablative body radiation (SABR) (NCT02992912; Supplementary Table 1b)). The IML1 cohort enrolled patients with stage III–IV metastatic lung cancers and advanced kidney and bladder cancers treated with ICI, primarily anti-PD-1/PD-L1 agents, either as monotherapy or in combination with TKIs or CT41. Notably, approximately 80% of patients received ICI therapy as first-line therapy. This cohort was merged with the SABR trial, a phase II trial enrolling patients with stage IV colorectal, lung, and kidney tumors treated with SABR plus anti-PD-L1 antibodies42,43. The PANDORE cohort (NCT03212651; Supplementary Table 1c), used for validation, included stage III patients with muscle-invasive nonmetastatic bladder cancer receiving neoadjuvant anti-PD-1 therapy44, offering an independent performance test.

The model was subsequently tested in seven independent cohorts by confirming the statistical relevance of the selected predictors. This set of cohorts refers to AtezoTRIBE45 (NCT03721653), a cohort of patients with proficient mismatch repair/microsatellite-stable metastatic colon cancer treated with CT and antiangiogenic agents with or without atezolizumab (n = 130 patients; Supplementary Table 1d), MIND-DC31 (NCT02993315), patients with stage III melanoma undergoing adjuvant DC (or placebo)-based immunization (n = 143 patients; Supplementary Table 1e), PRIMM study (NCT03643289) enrolling patients with stage III–IV melanoma from The Netherlands and UK38,39 (n = 133 patients with 291 serum samples), the publicly available CheckMate-025 study40 (NCT01668784) that enrolled patients with metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) (n = 743 patients, 2,229 samples) and finally in three cohorts of patients with atheromatous cardiovascular disease at high cancer risk before cancer diagnosis (Flemish Study on Environment, Genes and Health Outcomes (FLEMENGHO), PREVALUNG (NCT03976804) and ROBINSCA; Supplementary Table 1f–h).

Mass spectrometry-based MB generated a high-dimensional longitudinal dataset comprising 154 plasma metabolites measured at irregular time points (2–5 samples per patient over up to 27.8 months), together with right-censored PFS outcomes. Our integrative analysis sought to identify holistic, low-dimensional, robust and noncorrelated clinical and metabolic predictors of disease progression under immunotherapy. Sparse sampling and limited cohort size precluded reliable training of (1) recurrent neural networks (for example, long short-term memory variants), which showed unstable convergence and overfitting or (2) adoption of large language models (LLMs; such as LLM variants) due to the discrete/continuous heterogeneous feature space. We therefore applied dynamic random forest-based joint survival framework that models longitudinal and time-to-event data by summarizing individual trajectories into low-dimensional, interpretable features, while accommodating mixed covariates and irregular sampling.

Metabolomic profiles were preprocessed using imputation, normalization and batch-effect correction to reduce technical variability. Model performance was estimated using bootstrap resampling with the 0.632+ correction to mitigate small-sample optimism and calibration was assessed using out-of-bag (OOB) Integrated Brier Scores (IBSs). To improve interpretability and reduce redundancy, we implemented a variable importance (VIMP)-driven ablation framework informed by both statistical evidence and clinical constraints. Models were trained on the pooled IML1–SABR cohorts and externally validated in the independent PANDORE cohort (30 patients and 81 samples), with discrimination assessed using time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC–AUC) from a 6-month landmark (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, VIMP analysis across all iterations confirmed that only iteration 152 and 153 contained exclusively positive %VIMP scores, indicating that each feature contributed constructively to the model’s performance and reinforcing the validity of our minimum description length (MDL)-based selection criteria (Extended Data Fig. 2a). The final MDL-selected model retained eight predictors (Fig. 2c, d) and achieved a 12-month AUC of 73.1% (95% CI 51.2–94.9) in PANDORE external validation (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 2b). The final signature at iteration 153 included two clinical features (age and BMI) and six metabolites: histidine, succinic acid, carnitine, docosatrienoic acid, hexadecanedioic acid and creatinine, all contributing positively to the prediction of PFS (Fig. 2d). Notably, histidine emerged as the dominant predictor, exhibiting consistently high and positive %VIMP across all models (Extended Data Fig. 2a). In the pooled IML1–SABR cohort, plasma histidine levels positively correlated with distinct clinical parameters, such combination treatments as gender (female) (Fig. 2f). To establish a clinical parameter-based model, we trained a random forest survival model using only clinical variables (age, sex, BMI, tumor type, treatment group and stage). VIMP percentage analysis identified treatment group and stage as the most predictive features of PFS (Extended Data Fig. 2c). The clinical model was trained on the IML1 and SABR cohorts and externally validated on the PANDORE cohort, achieving a 12-month AUC of 80.8% (95% CI 60.8–100.8) in the validation cohort (Extended Data Fig. 2c). Given the therapeutic potential of the metabolic signature, we pursued the validation of our ML model in multiple external cohorts and experimental models (Fig. 1).

Prognostic value of plasma histidine for survival in seven external cohorts

Next, we stratified patients according to the median of the mass spectrometry-based values of plasma histidine at baseline to perform univariable Cox regression analyses of PFS or overall survival (OS) by Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves across multiple cohorts (Figs. 1 and 3), including IML1 and SABR (Supplementary Table 1a,b), AtezoTRIBE45 (Supplementary Table 1d) and MIND-DC31 (Supplementary Table 1e). Higher plasma levels of histidine were associated with prolonged PFS (P = 0.029; Fig. 3a), and OS (P = 0.031; Fig. 3a) in the IML1–SABR, with longer OS in AtezoTRIBE (P = 0.013; Fig. 3b), and with improved recurrence-free survival (RFS) in the DC (but not placebo) group in MIND-DC (Fig. 3c; P = 0.049 (left) and P = 0.56 (right)). In the first external PRIMM cohort of patients with stage III–IV melanoma38, high histidine levels measured before and shortly after the initiation of ICI also correlated with longer PFS at 12 months or beyond (Fig. 3d). Another external dataset was obtained from the phase III CheckMate-025 trial, which included 743 patients with mRCC randomized to receive either nivolumab or everolimus40. Metabolomic profiling was performed on plasma samples collected at baseline, week 4, and week 8 using targeted mass spectrometry. Histidine levels were markedly elevated in patients from the favorable risk group compared to both intermediate and poor risk groups (defined with the Memorial Sloan-Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) score46), with strong statistical significance (P < 0.001). Similarly, a highly significant difference was also observed between intermediate and poor risk groups (P < 0.001), further reinforcing the robustness of this association (Fig. 3e). Moreover, baseline histidine had a prognostic value as higher baseline histidine levels were strongly associated with better PFS (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 2a) and better OS (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 2b) in the whole cohort in multivariable analyses, considering histidine as a continuous variable (Supplementary Table 2) or dichotomized at the median (Fig. 3e). Of note, a significant interaction was observed between baseline histidine levels and treatment arm for both PFS (P = 0.007) and OS (P = 0.002).

We next analyzed the prognostic value of baseline histidine in populations at high cancer risk before cancer diagnosis (Figs. 1 and 3f–h). In the retrospective FLEMENGHO cohort47 comprising 97 individuals diagnosed with atheromatous cardiovascular disease who were monitored for tobacco-associated cancer incidence48, higher histidine levels tended to be associated with a reduction of the risk to develop cancer (Supplementary Table 1f and Fig. 3f; P = 0.078). Confirming this trend, high histidine levels at baseline were associated with a reduced risk of developing or dying of tobacco-associated cancers in two other cohorts of smokers that either had already experienced an acute cardiovascular event (PREVALUNG49) or were at high risk of dying from cardiovascular disease (ROBINSCA50) (Supplementary Table 1g, h and Fig. 3g, h).

Moreover, we showed that the predictive value of plasma histidine did not depend on the treatment modality, considering multivariable Cox regression analyses of PFS (Supplementary Fig. 2a) and OS (Supplementary Fig. 2b) in the IML1–SABR training cohorts. Of note, when considering the most explanatory metabolic and clinical predictors together (histidine and BMI with a cut off >25 kg m−2), we observed that the high histidine/high BMI subgroup tended to be associated with a more favorable prognosis gathering IML1 and SABR cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 2c,d).

Last, we analyzed the trajectories of plasma histidine during treatment. Kinetics of circulating histidine levels were evaluated across six cohorts (IML1–SABR, CheckMate-025 (ref. 40), FMT-LUMINate51, MIMic23, TACITO52 (Supplementary Table 1i–k) and PROMIT53), in various therapeutic settings. Across all cohorts, responders (defined according to PFS) exhibited a significant increase in plasma histidine levels over time. In the PROMIT trial, where dacarbazine (CT) preceded ICI, patients exhibiting a higher CT-induced rise in plasma histidine (Δhistidine) were more likely to achieve partial response to anti-PD-1 antibodies (Extended Data Fig. 3a–f).

Altogether, high levels of circulating histidine indicate a reduced risk of developing cancer in smokers and higher odds to respond to immunotherapy in patients with a variety of different cancer types.

Histidine is an immunostimulating essential amino acid with anticancer properties

Histidine is an essential amino acid mediating a broad range of effects on inflammation54, energy expenditure55 and brain neuroprotection56. To decipher the causality links between histidine recirculation and ICI immunostimulatory potential, we immunophenotyped patients with cancer and performed L-histidine-based diet interventions in tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 1). First, we undertook a 40 and 37 marker-based spectral flow cytometric analysis of 290 longitudinal blood samples to phenotype peripheral blood mononuclear cells over time in two distinct cohorts of patients with cancer, nontreated (IML1) or treated with FMT (MIMic–FMT-LUMINate23,51; Supplementary Table 1i,j), bearing in mind that oral histidine can be metabolized by the gut microbiota57. Focusing on commonalities between the two cohorts (Extended Data Fig. 4a), we found that circulating histidine in patients with cancer correlated positively with activated CD4+ T cells expressing IL-2Ra/CD25 (Extended Data Fig. 4b), type 1 helper T (TH1) cells (CXCR3) (Extended Data Fig. 4c) and follicular helper (TFH) cell markers (CXCR5) (Extended Data Fig. 4d), as well as with CCR6+ B cells (Extended Data Fig. 4e) and nonclassical monocytes (Extended Data Fig. 4f). The percentage of blood double-positive T cells, a hallmark of T cell receptor (TCR) engagement, was also increased in patients with higher histidine serum concentrations (Extended Data Fig. 4g). To demonstrate the direct immunostimulatory properties of histidine with or without ICI-based therapies, we turned to orthotopic preclinical models of fibrosarcoma (MCA205) and melanoma (RET) (Fig. 1).

In therapeutic settings, 8-week-old female C57BL/6J mice were administered L-histidine by oral gavage starting after tumor implantation and before immunotherapy, and continued every other day. Histidine was delivered at doses of 0.1 or 1 g kg−1, in combination with anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (MCA205) or anti-CTLA-4 plus anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibodies (RET)55,56,58. Oral histidine supplementation tended to accelerate immunotherapy-mediated rejection of fibrosarcomas at low or high doses (Fig. 4a–c), as well as that of RET melanoma (Fig. 4d–f), especially at the highest dose. In prophylactic settings, histidine was administered via the drinking water before tumor implantation, resulting in delayed MCA205 tumor establishment and reduced aggressiveness at both dosing regimens, with a stronger effect at the highest dose (Fig. 4g–i). Pharmacokinetics of oral histidine were assessed by targeted mass spectrometry-based metabolomics longitudinally in the plasma and feces and after 15 and 18 days of histidine uptake in tumor beds (Fig. 4j–m and Supplementary Fig. 3) and ilei (Supplementary Fig. 3). We observed an ascending trajectory and increase of circulating histidine levels after 15 and 18 days of uptake at high dosing (Fig. 4k, P = 0.017 (left) and P = 0.021 (right)). At both doses, the metabolic landscape changed in tumor beds by day +8 (Fig. 4j) with a relative increase, in not only histidine (Fig. 4l; P = 0.05) but also, albeit not significantly, cis- and trans-urocanic acids, carnosine, histamine and 3-methylhistidine (Fig. 4j and Supplementary Fig. 3a–g). Of note, fecal levels of histidine anticorrelated with intratumoral histidine concentrations (Fig. 4m; r = −0.36, P = 0.053). In addition, histidine supplementation significantly increased its metabolites, histamine and imidazole propionate (ImP) in the ileal content (Supplementary Fig. 3h). Moreover, there was an inverse correlation between fecal and ileal histidine concentrations, in accordance with its direct absorption (Supplementary Fig. 3i; r = −0.47, P = 0.009). As an off-target effect, the metabolomics revealed that histidine supplementation reduced and augmented tumoral concentrations of adenosine monophosphate and tryptophan respectively, metabolites associated with immunosuppressive or immunostimulating potential respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3j,k). Hence, after oral histidine intake, there was a dose-independent increase of plasma and tumor histidine concentrations that could modulate lymphocyte function.

Pre-effector CD8+ T cells are known to depend on one-carbon metabolism for their metabolic fitness and CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity60,61. These ‘so called P4’ CD8+ T cells downregulated the transcription of 1C-metabolism -related genes and exhibited altered mitochondrial respiration60–62. Therefore, we analyzed the effects of histidine on murine naive CD8+ T cell basal and spare respiratory capacities. Using Seahorse extracellular flux analysis, we measured oxygen consumption rates (OCRs) in activated naive (CD44low) CD8+ T cells cultured under low-nutrient, fatty acid oxidation (FAO)-permissive conditions. Histidine significantly increased mitochondrial respiration, indicative of enhanced oxidative metabolism. This increase in OCR was abrogated by etomoxir, a CPT1a inhibitor, demonstrating that the histidine-induced metabolic effect is FAO-dependent (Fig. 4p). Together, these data identify FAO as a key pathway through which histidine enhances mitochondrial energy production in CD8+ T cells. Consistent with improved functional fitness, histidine enhanced CD8+ T cell performance in a repetitive stimulation model of in vitro exhaustion. Under conditions of repeated TCR engagement without co-stimulation starting at day 2, histidine-treated naive CD8+ T cells showed significantly increased cumulative fold expansion over time, indicating preserved proliferative capacity and resistance to exhaustion-associated dysfunction (Extended Data Fig. 5f).

We next demonstrated the effects of histidine-exposed T cells on DC antigen presentation in a previously described DC/ B3Z T cell cross-presentation in vitro assay62 (Extended Data Fig. 5g,h) as well as the direct effects of histidine on anti-CD3 monoclonal antibody-stimulated human T cells in vitro (Extended Data Fig. 5i).

Altogether, histidine supplementation accelerated the ICI-induced antitumor effects in mouse models, acting directly on T cells in vitro by promoting mitochondrial FAO and limiting exhaustion-associated metabolic failure.

Histidine-rich diet influenced clinical benefit to ICI with a major impact of gut microbiota in this outcome

Dietary histidine is metabolized by the liver and the gut microbiota into several bioactive metabolites (Fig. 5a). We next investigated the links between patient dietary habits and plasma histidine levels at baseline (Fig. 1), having access to food questionnaires in two independent cohorts, a Canadian prospective nutritional BIOBANK IO cohort63 conducted in 147 patients with lung cancer eligible to receive ICI-based therapy (Supplementary Table 3a) and the French PREVALUNG cohort of patients at risk of tobacco-associated cancers49 (Supplementary Table 1g). Canadian patients’ dietary consumptions were assessed once at baseline using a modified version of a previously published short-form food frequency questionnaire (FFQ)64. The instrument included 47 food items, each with standardized portion sizes and frequency scale ranging from zero to more than five times on a weekly basis. The questionnaire was designed and administered by a trained nutritionist, who ensured consistency in how patients interpreted portion sizes and frequency categories. Nutrient intake was then computed using a validated nutrient calculation tool, enabling derivation of daily nutrient intakes in grams65 (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 3b). Ingested histidine corresponded to protein-rich foods (fish, meat, eggs mostly ingested at least 3–4 times a week; Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). We first analyzed the impact of histidine consumption with PFS (Fig. 5c). To this aim, we segregated the cohort of 147 patients with lung cancer according to the median histidine intake normalized to total calorie intake (1.25 grams per 1,000 kcal per day), revealing in univariable and multivariable Cox logistic regression analysis that histidine consumption ≥1.25 g per 1,000 kcal per day was associated with prolonged PFS (hazard ratio (HR) 0.64 (0.43–0.96); P = 0.03; Supplementary Tables 3b and 4); however, plasma histidine did not correlate with PFS or OS in this study (Supplementary Fig. 2e,f). To explore why histidine intake but not plasma histidine was associated with PFS, we analyzed the fecal profile of histidine metabolites (Fig. 5a) determined by mass spectrometry and their correlation with stool taxonomic composition determined by shotgun metagenomics sequencing (MGS). Fecal but not plasma histidine levels positively correlated with the MGS index of favorable microbiota, the S-score previously reported to be associated with clinical benefit to ICI66,67 (Fig. 5d,e); however, gut dysbiosis (characterized by low S-score < 0.5) was associated with higher levels of the immunosuppressive histidine end-product, ImP in the plasma (Fig. 5d,e). In fact, plasma and fecal ImP levels correlated together and the plasma ImP/ fecal histidine ratio anti-correlated with the S-score (Extended Data Fig. 7a–c), suggesting that gut dysbiosis affects histidine recirculation. In fact, the median of the ratios between fecal trans-urocanic acid/ImP and fecal ImP/histidine were positively and negatively associated with clinical outcome, respectively (HR 0.34 (0.10–1.13), P = 0.07; HR 2.2 (0.99–4.86), P = 0.05, respectively) (Fig. 5f). Furthermore, the plasmatic trans-urocanic acid/histidine ratio was negatively associated with PFS (HR 1.70 (0.99–2.92), P = 0.05) (Fig. 5g).

Fecal histidine anticorrelated with immune-related adverse events

We next investigated the impact of histidine plasma and fecal metabolites on side effects (Supplementary Tables 1 and 5a,b). There were no association between plasma histidine levels and grade ≥3 immunotoxicity in the IML1 nor in the combination of IML1 and AtezoTRIBE cohorts (P = 0.191 and P = 0.782, respectively). Fecal metabolomics was available in four metastatic melanoma cohorts: MIMic (NCT03772899)23, FMT-LUMINate (NCT04951583)51,68, PROMIT (NCT04225390)53,69 and MelAutim (01ZX1905A) (Supplementary Table 5a,b), gathering 142 patients evaluated for toxicity. The PROMIT was a multicenter single-arm phase 2 trial evaluating the clinical activity of ICI (anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody ± anti-CTLA-4 monoclonal antibody) after administration of dacarbazine (DTIC) in stage III–IV melanoma53,69. MelAutim evaluated adverse events and quality of life in patients with melanoma treated mainly with anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 monoclonal antibodies70,71. In these two German trials, the incidence of colitis was 5.6% and 24.5% for the other immune-related adverse events (irAEs). FMT-LUMINate included 40 patients with first-line advanced melanoma or non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) treated with anti-PD1 monoclonal antibody alone or combined with anti-CTLA4 monoclonal antibody in conjunction with FMT51,68. In the melanoma cohorts, 13 patients (65%) experienced grade 3–4 adverse events (AEs) (including 20% diarrhea/colitis), whereas in MIMic (FMT + anti-PD-1 alone), grade 3 irAEs occurred in 25% (refs. 23,51,68). In the NSCLC cohort, no patients experienced ≥ grade 3 AEs51. Using linear mixed-effects models, after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, tumor, cohort, date of stool sampling, patient ID (random effect) and pooling all time points of stool collection during immunotherapy (n = 84 patients, 165 feces), we found that stool histidine (alongside other essential amino acids) inversely correlated with the occurrence of a severe irAEs (grade ≥3) (Extended Data Fig. 10a; P = 0.04). Similar findings were previously reported in mRCC treated with ICIs72. Hence, high fecal histidine levels correlated with reduced odds to develop irAEs.

Metabolic immune checkpoints

Besides histidine, other predictors of PFS were identified by the ML algorithm in iteration 153 (Fig. 2c,d). Indeed, succinate was the second metabolic feature retained by the DynForest model. In accordance with many reports showing that succinate is a proinflammatory oncometabolite73–76, we found that high baseline succinate plasma levels (using the median as the cutoff value) tended to be associated with dismal prognosis, shorter PFS in Cox regression analysis in the IML1–SABR training cohort (Extended Data Fig. 10b; P = 0.065), as well as in the validation cohorts, AtezoTRIBE arm CT/ICI (Extended Data Fig. 10c; P = 0.009) and PRIMM at baseline before CT/ICI (Extended Data Fig. 10d). In MIMic, plasma succinate levels increased in patients with progressive disease (Extended Data Fig. 10e; P = 0.057). Moreover, three metabolites involved in lipid metabolism (carnitine, hexadecanedioic acid and docosatrienoic acid) were retained in our DynForest model. Docosatrienoic acid is a naturally occurring polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) formed through a two-carbon chain elongation of arachidonic acid. Patients with elevated levels of this circulating PUFA tended to exhibit reduced PFS in Cox regression univariate analyses in IML1–SABR (Extended Data Fig. 10f). AtezoTRIBE external cohort confirmed the clinical relevance of other fatty acids, such as hexadecanedioic acid, high levels of hexadecanedioic acid being associated with reduced PFS, for the whole cohort and the immunotherapy arm (Extended Data Fig. 10g; P = 0.029, left, P = 0.061, right). Altogether, long-chain fatty acids and succinate may act as metabolic checkpoints of immunotherapy.

Discussion

We conducted a meta-analysis integrating 154 metabolites from baseline and on-therapy timepoints across independent epidemiological and interventional cohorts encompassing diverse geographic sites, tumor histologies, and treatment modalities, applying DynForest36 to address the challenge of modeling high-dimensional, time-dependent metabolomics data in small-sample settings. Here, we identified histidine as a favorable gut and plasma metabolite with immunostimulatory potential in immune-oncology and microbiota-centered interventions. The ML algorithm also highlighted two metabolic immune checkpoints (host-derived succinate and microbial long-chain fatty acids) whose dynamic trajectories were linked to immunoresistance. This work represents one of the most extensive longitudinal, multicohort metabolomics efforts in immunotherapy response and toxicity profiling to date, offering a scalable and interpretable framework for biomarker discovery; however, several limitations warrant consideration. Training/validation cohorts were of moderate size and clinically heterogeneous, potentially limiting generalizability. Moreover, the high dimensionality of the metabolomic profiles, relative to the available training population sample sizes, introduces a residual risk of false discovery, despite stringent feature selection and validation across independent external datasets. Of note, our findings are in line with two small studies showing the prognostic impact of serum histidine in patients with cancer30,77. Finally, although experimental models support a causal role for histidine in antitumor immunity, prospective clinical studies will be necessary to establish its prognostic relevance and assess its therapeutic potential in patients.

Histidine degradation contributes to the cytosolic folate-bound one-carbon (1C) pool via 5-formimino-tetrahydrofolate78. Age-related defects in T cell mitochondrial biogenesis have been linked to impaired induction of 1C metabolism enzymes60,61, and among the various 1C donors, only histidine, serine and formate feed directly into the cytosolic folate-bound 1C pool. This raises the possibility that histidine’s immunostimulatory effects are mediated, at least in part, through restoration of 1C metabolism in aged or metabolically compromised T cells. Our results indicate that histidine acts directly on T cells in vitro by promoting mitochondrial FAO and limiting exhaustion-associated metabolic failure. Indeed, histidine could provide a costimulatory signal 2 in TCR-engaged (signal 1) murine and human T cells in vitro, leading to sustained proliferation or cytokine release. Alternatively, histidine was shown to act as an adjuvant to antifolate CT. In hematologic malignancies and rheumatoid arthritis, high histidine enhanced methotrexate (MTX) efficacy by diverting tetrahydrofolate toward histidine catabolism, thereby lowering the MTX dose required for cytotoxicity79,80; however, supraphysiological histidine (at 100 mM), 8- to 80-fold higher than in our tumor models, induced outgrowth of murine hepatocarcinoma via histamine-mediated T cell exhaustion and stromal reprogramming81. Histamine, produced by liver L-histidine decarboxylase, can suppress immune function via H1–H4 receptors, a mechanism supported by the observed synergy between H1 receptor antagonists and PD-1 blockade82–84; however, in our cohorts, plasma histamine was undetectable.

Of note, our dietary surveys conducted in two independent cohorts of patients with chronic inflammatory disease (stage IV NSCLC and tobacco-associated cardiovascular disease) unveiled no correlation between fish or meat-rich diets and plasma histidine concentrations. These findings suggest that circulating histidine levels may not be solely determined by the dietary intake but could also reflect microbial histidine production and consumption57,85. A notable microbial histidine derivative, ImP, implicated in atherosclerosis57 and in type 2 diabetes85, was elevated at baseline in nonresponders and decreased in responders during melanoma immunotherapy. Indeed, we confirm that the degradation of histidine into ImP in stool is associated with poor survival. In accordance with previous work85, we found that gut dysbiosis correlates with fecal ImP as well as high fecal ImP/histidine ratios and anticorrelates with plasma histidine levels. Indeed, in our study, the ratio between plasma ImP and fecal histidine is markedly influenced by the taxonomic composition of the gut microbiota and increased with the relative overrepresentation of inflammatory and immunosuppressive pathobionts that characterize gut dysbiosis and resistance to ICI.

From a translational standpoint, oral L-histidine supplementation seems safe. Thus, most inherited deficiencies in key histidine-catabolic enzymes are largely phenotypically silent86–88 and individuals with histidine ammonia lyase deficiency89, resulting in fourfold to tenfold higher circulating histidine, exhibit no major illness. Histidine loading (with a dose of 0.054 g kg−1) has been safely applied in pediatric leukemia to assess folate status90. Moreover, small clinical trials in prediabetes and obesity (4–8 g day−1) improved postprandial glucose, increased mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells, as well as shifted the gut microbiota and the epigenetic regulation of the amino acid transporter SLC7A5 (refs. 91–94). Given the prevalence of folate depletion in patients with cancer95–97, controlled dietary histidine supplementation merits further investigation, stratifying on gut dysbiosis, as an adjunct to immunotherapy in patients with first-line stage III or IV cancer, particularly in older patients or as an interceptive measure to prevent or delay cancer incidence in individuals at high cancer risk. In all cases, fecal and plasma histidine and ImP monitoring, and metagenomics or TOPOSCORE (to follow bacterial histidine consumers or producers) should be conducted to identify subsets of patients who could benefit from this food supplement. As a proof of concept to show the adjuvant effects of histidine during ICI in 60% of patients with advanced cancer presenting a gut dysbiosis at baseline, histidine injection by an intravenous route (to bypass gut dysbiosis) may be a reasonable option.

Methods

Cohort description

The ONCOBIOME international consortium (https://www.oncobiome.eu/) has received funding from the European Union’s Horizon 2020 research and innovation program under grant agreement number 825410 for the project ‘Gut OncoMicrobiome Signatures (GOMS) associated with cancer incidence, prognosis and prediction of treatment response’ launched in 2019 (ref. 37). This consortium includes the following studies: ONCOBIOTICS (IMMUNOLIFE1), SABR, MIMic, FMT-LUMINate, PROMIT, MIND-DC and AtezoTRIBE.

ONCOBIOTICS (NCT04567446) is a multicenter observational study involving 14 participating centers in France and Canada, encompassing the IMMUNOLIFE (IML1) cohort, aiming to discover microbiome-based biomarkers in patients with cancer. Metabolomics data acquisition and preprocessing

For each patient, 50 μl of human plasma was mixed with 500 μl of ice-cold extraction mixture (methanol:water, 9:1 spiked with a cocktail of internal standards) and centrifuged (15 min at 10,000g, 4 °C). Regarding murine plasma, 25 µl of biological sample was mixed with 250 µl of the same ice-cold extraction mixture and treated as human samples. Supernatants were collected and several fractions were split to be analyzed by liquid chromatography (LC) and gas chromatography (GC) coupled with mass spectrometry (MS)98. Regarding human samples, 150 μl of supernatant (50 µl for murine) were transferred to glass vials and evaporated to be used for GC–MS. Then, 50 μl of methoxyamine (20 mg ml−1 in pyridine) was added on dried extracts, which were stored at room temperature in the dark, overnight. The day after, 80 μl of N-methyl-N- trimethylsilyl-trifluoroacetamide were added, and the final derivatization was made at 40 °C during 30 min. Samples were then transferred into vials and directly injected into the GC–MS system (7890A gas chromatograph coupled with a triple quadrupole 7000C, Agilent Technologies) equipped with a high-sensitivity electronic impact source operating in positive mode. This method was inspired by developments reported previously. Other aliquots (polyamines, bile acids and short-chain fatty acids) were analyzed by our LC–MS 1290 UHPLC (ultra-high performance liquid chromatography) coupled to a QQQ 6470 (Agilent Technologies). Biliary acids and polyamines were analyzed by LC-MS/MS with a 1290 UHPLC (Agilent Technologies) coupled to a QQQ 6470 (Agilent Technologies)31. Small organic acid and ketone were analyzed by multiple reaction monitoring analysis in both positive and negative polarity, alternatively, gas temperature was set to 300 °C with a gas flow of 12 l min−1. Capillary voltage was set to 4 kV in positive and 5 kV in negative polarity mode. A 10-μl sample was injected on a column Zorbax Eclipse XDB (P/N 981758-902, 100 × 2.1-mm particle size 1.8 µm 1,200 bar) from Agilent technologies, protected by a guard column XDB-C18 (5 × 2.1-mm particle size 1.8 μm) and heated at 50 °C by a Pelletier oven. Gradient mobile phase consisted of water with 0.1% of formic acid (A) and 0.1% of formic acid in acetonitrile (B) freshly made. Flow rate was set to 0.7 ml min−1 and gradient was set as follows: initial condition was 80% phase A and 20% phase B for 3 min, changing to 45% phase B over 4 min before rinsing phase and equilibrium before next injection. Peak detection and integration of the analytes were performed using the Agilent Mass Hunter quantitative software (B.07.01).

Pseudo-targeted analysis by UHPLC–high resolution and accurate mass was performed on a U3000 (Dionex)/Orbitrap q-Exactive (Thermo) coupling, previously described by Durand et al.99 and Abdellatif et al.100. All targeted treated data were merged and cleaned with a dedicated R package (v.4.0) (https://github.com/kroemerlab/GRMeta).

Plasma and fecal metabolomic profiling were performed using untargeted LC–MS/MS across multiple cohorts. Each sample was analyzed to extract area and height peak intensities for each metabolite. Quality control samples were injected regularly within each batch and used to compute intra batch-corrected values, denoted ‘AreaQCCorr’, used for all downstream analyses.

Before any inter-batch-effect correction, each batch of raw metabolomics data underwent standardized preprocessing:

Filtering of missing data: metabolites with more than 30% missing values across samples within a batch were removed.

Filtering of missing data: metabolites with more than 30% missing values across samples within a batch were removed.

Missing data imputation: the remaining missing values were imputed using k-nearest neighbor imputation, implemented via the bnstruct knn.impute function (bnstruct v.1.0.15). Imputation was performed separately for each batch.

Missing data imputation: the remaining missing values were imputed using k-nearest neighbor imputation, implemented via the bnstruct knn.impute function (bnstruct v.1.0.15). Imputation was performed separately for each batch.

Transformation: we applied a log10 transformation of AreaQCCorr values for all downstream metabolomics analyses.

Transformation: we applied a log10 transformation of AreaQCCorr values for all downstream metabolomics analyses.

When combining data from multiple cohorts involved in the ML analysis (IMMUNOLIFE1, SABR, PANDORE and MIMic), we retained only those metabolites consistently present across all batches and cohorts.

Subcutaneous cancer mouse models

The MCA205 fibrosarcoma cell line was obtained from the Merck Millipore (ref SCC173), and the syngeneic RET melanoma line (generated by transgenic expression of the RET proto-oncogene under control of the metallothionein-1 promoter) was kindly provided by V. Umansky (German Cancer Research Center). Both lines, compatible with C57BL/6 mice, were maintained at 37 °C in 5 % CO2 in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10 % fetal calf serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 IU ml−1 penicillin–streptomycin, 1 mM sodium pyruvate and MEM non-essential amino acids. Cultures were routinely screened for Mycoplasmaand discarded after ten passages.

Female C57BL/6 mice were injected subcutaneously with 0.8 × 106 MCA205 or 0.5 × 106 RET cells. Anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (clone RMP1-14, 250 µg per mouse) or its isotype control (clone 2A3, 250 µg) was administered intraperitoneally on days 6, 9 and 12 after MCA205 implantation. For RET tumors, combined anti-PD-1 (RMP1-14, 250 µg) and anti-CTLA-4 (clone 9D9, 100 µg) antibodies or matched isotypes (2A3, 250 µg; and MPC-11, 100 µg, respectively) were given on days 7, 10 and 13. All antibodies were purchased from Bio X Cell. Tumor length and width were measured three times per week with a digital caliper. In accordance with our institutional ethics committee guidelines, the maximal permitted tumor burden was 1,500 mm3 and this limit was not exceeded in any experiment.

L-histidine oral gavage in mouse models

L-histidine monohydrochloride monohydrate (Thermo Scientific Alfa Aesar, cat. A10413.22) was prepared in sterile 0.9% NaCl at concentrations of 20 mg ml−1 and 200 mg ml−1. Oral dosing started on day 4 in the MCA205 protocol and on day 3 in the RET protocol, before the initiation of immunotherapy, and was continued every other day until the end of the experiment. Each mouse received 100 µl every other day, supplying either 0.1 g kg−1 (≈2 mg) or 1 g kg−1 (≈20 mg) histidine, while control animals were given vehicle alone. Given the every-other-day administration schedule, these regimens correspond to daily equivalent doses of approximately 50 mg kg−1 day−1 and 500 mg kg−1 day−1, respectively.

L-histidine oral supplementation in drinking water in mouse models

For prophylactic experiments, L-histidine monohydrochloride monohydrate (Thermo Scientific Alfa Aesar, cat. A10413.22) was administered via the drinking water before tumor implantation. L-histidine was dissolved in sterile drinking water at concentrations of 0.24 g l−1 or 2.4 g l−1, corresponding to approximate daily doses of 50 mg kg−1 or 500 mg kg−1, respectively, based on average body weight and water consumption. Supplementation was initiated 7 days before subcutaneous injection of MCA205 cells and maintained throughout the experiment. Control mice received standard drinking water without supplementation. Water bottles were replaced regularly to ensure stability and consistent dosing. Histidine levels after supplementation were assessed by metabolomics in plasma, intestinal tissues, feces and TME.

Flow cytometry in mouse models

MCA205-bearing mice receiving oral L-histidine were sacrificed on day 11, 48 h after the second anti-PD-1 injection. Tumors, tdLNs and spleens were collected for analysis. Tumors were minced, digested for 30 min at 37 °C in RPMI-1640 containing liberase (25 µg ml−1; Roche) and DNase I (150 IU ml−1; Roche), crushed through 70-µm strainers and further clarified with filter-bottom plates. Spleens were pressed through 70- and 30-µm strainers, centrifuged, subjected to ACK red blood cell lysis, washed and resuspended. tdLNs were mechanically disrupted in RPMI-1640 and filtered through a 70-µm strainer. Cell suspensions were counted and plated in V-bottom 96-well plates at 4 × 106 splenocytes per well or 1 × 106 cells per well for tumor lymphocytes and tdLN samples. Fc receptors were blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 (clone 93; eBioscience) for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by surface staining for 30 min at 4 °C.

Intracellular markers were stained using the FoxP3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (eBioscience), and dead cells were excluded with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua (BioLegend). Antibody panels are listed in the Supplementary Methods Table 1. Samples were acquired on a 13-color CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using Kaluza 2.1 software. Regulatory T cells were defined as CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ events.

Spectral flow cytometry analysis of patient PBMCs

Aliquots of 5–10 × 106 peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) cryopreserved in fetal calf serum/ dimethylsulfoxide solution were defrosted, washed and resuspended in FACS Buffer (Brilliant Stain Buffer Plus BD + 5% fetal calf serum + 2 mM EDTA). Dead cells were excluded using LIVE/DEAD Fixable Blue Dead Cell Stain kit (Invitrogen). Antibody panels for the FMT-LUMINate/MIMic and ONCOBIOTICS/IMMUNOLIFE1 cohorts are listed in Supplementary Methods Table 2. Stained samples were acquired on a Cytek Aurora five-laser spectral analyzer and analyses were performed on FlowJo v.10.8.1.

Manual gating strategies

Flow cytometric analysis on markers of PBMCs was conducted by filtering out debris, doublets and dead cells, and selecting alive CD45+ cells. New .fcs files were generated with FlowJo v.10.8.1 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Single-cell cytometry annotation network (Scyan) automatic annotation

Cell-type annotations were automatically performed using Scyan101, a biology-driven model that leverages prior knowledge of cell types. This approach allowed us to identify nine main populations: basophils, NK cells, B cells, monocytes, DCs, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, double-positive T cells and double-negative T cells. Subsequently, for each patient, separate .fcs files were generated for each of these nine populations. The percentages of all subpopulations were then determined through manual gating using FlowJo v.10.8.1 software (Supplementary Fig. 4).

In vitro experiments assessing murine T cell metabolism

Mice

C57BL/6N (6-week-old) female mice were purchased from Japan SLC. Mice were handled in compliance with protocols approved by the Animal Research Committee, Graduate School of Medicine, Kyoto University (protocol approval no. 25086). They were maintained under SPF conditions.

In vitro assays

For in vitro analysis in the Seahorse assay and a repetitive stimulation model, axillary, brachial and inguinal lymph nodes from both sides of tumor-free female mice (7 weeks old) were collected, homogenized and pooled. Spleens from tumor-free female mice (7 weeks old) were collected, homogenized and then treated with ACK (ammonium–chloride–potassium) lysing buffer for 2 min. After pooling the cells of lymph nodes and spleens, naive (CD44low) CD8+ T cells were isolated using MojoSort system (BioLegend, cat. 480044) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

In vitro metabolic analysis of histidine using a Seahorse assay

Isolated naive CD8+ T cells were stimulated with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 coated Dynabeads (Gibco, cat. 11453D) at a cell-to-bead ratio of 2.5:1 in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, 100 U ml−1 penicillin, 100 μg ml−1 streptomycin and 50 µM 2-mercaptoethanol, in the absence or presence of 1 mM L-histidine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. A10413.22) and incubated at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator for 42 h. Cultured cells were seeded in an XFe96 plate (2.5 × 105 cells per well) and OCR were measured by the XFe96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. OCR measurement was performed using Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test. XF RPMI Base Medium (Agilent Technologies) supplemented with low-glucose (2.5 mM) and containing 0.75 mM L-carnitine hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. C0283) was used during the assay. To assess endogenous FAO, 5 μM etomoxir sodium salt (Selleck, cat. S8244) or its vehicle (water) was added 20 min before the assay. Then, 1.5 μM oligomycin (Abcam, cat. ab141829), 2 μM carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP; Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., cat. C3463) and a mixture of 0.5 μM each of rotenone (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. R8875) and Antimycin A Complex (Cayman Chemical Co., cat. 34799) were injected sequentially. ATP-linked respiration was defined as (last rate measurement before oligomycin injection) – (minimum rate measurement after oligomycin injection).

In vitro analysis of histidine effects using a repetitive stimulation model

To assess the effect of histidine on T cell exhaustion-associated phenotypes in vitro, a repetitive stimulation model was applied based on a previous study102. Isolated naive CD8+ T cells were resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells per ml in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, 100 U ml−1 penicillin, 100 μg ml−1 streptomycin, 50 µM 2-mercaptoethanol, 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco), 1× non-essential amino acids (Gibco), and 100 U ml−1 recombinant human IL-2 (Imunace; Shionogi, YJ code 6399411D1022). Cells were seeded onto a plate coated with 1 µg ml−1 anti-CD3 antibody (clone 145-2C11, eBioscience, cat. 16-0031-85) and 1 µg ml−1 anti-CD28 antibody (clone 37.51, eBioscience, cat. 16-0281-82) and cultured at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator. Starting on day 2, cells were passaged at 1 × 106 cells per ml in the same medium and seeded onto a freshly coated plate with 1 µg ml−1 anti-CD3 antibody alone. This procedure was repeated every 2 days. Cells were cultured from day 0 in the presence or absence of 1 mM L-histidine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. A10413.14), which was maintained throughout the culture period. Fold expansion of viable cells was calculated cumulatively from day 0, based on the ratio of the number of recovered cells at each time point (days 2, 4, 6 and 8) to the number of cells at the previous seeding (days 0, 2, 4 and 6, respectively). Viable cells were determined by trypan blue exclusion at each time point. The cells were also analyzed by flow cytometry on day 8.

Flow cytometry analysis of the in vitro repetitive stimulation model

Cell suspensions from the day 8 repetitive stimulation model were plated in V-bottom 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well. Fc receptors were blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 (clone 2.4G2, BD Biosciences, cat. 553142) for 5 min at 4 °C, followed by surface staining for 12 min at 4 °C. Intracellular markers were stained using the FoxP3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (eBioscience), and dead cells were excluded with Zombie NIR Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend). Antibody panels are listed in Supplementary Methods Table 3. Samples were acquired on a LSRFortessa X-20 (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo software (v.10.10.0). Cells from the repetitive stimulation model were analyzed by gating on live cells. Statistical analyses and data visualization for the in vitro histidine study using the Seahorse assay and a repetitive stimulation model were performed using Prism (v.10.3.0). To compare two groups, a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used, and to perform multiple-group analysis, a one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s multiple comparisons was used. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± s.e.m.

In vitro antigen presentation assays

Cell lines and culture conditions

The inducible immortalized DC precursor (iniDC) line was originally generated by Richter et al.103 and subsequently subcloned in our laboratory. The B3Z T cell hybridoma reporter line was provided by S. Amigorena (Institut Curie). Both lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 10 mM HEPES and 1× penicillin–streptomycin. For iniDC maintenance, this medium was further supplemented with 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol, 10 ng ml−1 recombinant GM–CSF, 100 nM dexamethasone and 2 µM doxycycline (‘Dex & Dox’) to sustain immortalization. Removal of Dex & Dox for 3 days induced differentiation into nonproliferative, immature DCs (de-iniDCs), which were employed in functional assays. B3Z cells were cultured in the same base medium containing only 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol.

In vitro antigen presentation assay

De-iniDCs were seeded in 12-well plates at 4 × 105 cells per well and pulsed overnight with either soluble ovalbumin (1 mg ml−1) or the SIINFEKL peptide at low (0.8 ng ml−1) or high (4 ng ml−1) concentration; medium alone served as a negative control. Concurrently, B3Z cells were plated in six-well plates at 2 × 106 cells per well and treated overnight with varying concentrations of histidine. Following antigen pulse, de-iniDCs were washed twice with base medium. B3Z cells were then collected and co-cultured with de-iniDCs at 8 × 105 B3Z cells per well. After 4 h of incubation at 37 °C, supernatants were collected, cleared by centrifugation (500g, 5 min) and stored for cytokine analysis.

IL-2 ELISA

IL-2 was quantified by ELISA following an established protocol62. In brief, 96-well high-binding plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with capture antibody (cat. no. 503702, BioLegend) diluted in coating buffer, washed and blocked for 2 h at room temperature. Samples and standards were applied for 2 h, followed by incubation with biotinylated detection antibody (cat. no. 503804, BioLegend) and then HRP-conjugated avidin (BioLegend, cat. no. 405103). Color development was achieved using TMB substrate, the reaction was stopped with sulfuric acid and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Cytokine concentrations were interpolated from a standard curve after adjusting for dilution.

Human PBMC isolation and T cell activation assay

PBMCs were obtained from healthy adult donors (n = 2). Blood products consisted in cytapheresis rings provided by the Etablissement Français du Sang Saint-Louis, Île-de-France. PBMCs were isolated by density-gradient centrifugation using lymphocyte separation medium (Eurobio Scientific, CMSMSL01-01). All donors provided informed consent for research use of blood products, and procedures complied with institutional ethical guidelines.

Freshly thawed PBMCs were used to isolate T lymphocytes via negative-selection (Pan T Cell Isolation kit, Miltenyi Biotec, 130-096-535) using MS columns (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-042-201).

Flat-bottom 96-well plates were coated with anti-CD3 antibody (OKT3, Invitrogen, 14-0037-82) at two suboptimal concentrations (0.05 µg ml−1 and 0.2 µg ml−1).

Cells were cultured in complete RPMI 1640/GlutaMAX medium (GIBCO, 61870-010) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated human AB serum (Institut Jacques Boy, 201021334), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (GIBCO, 15140-122), 1% HEPES (GIBCO, 15630-056), 1% sodium pyruvate (GIBCO, 11360-039) and 1% polymyxin B (final concentration 10 µg ml−1; Merck, 81271-10ML). T cells were seeded at 100,000 cells per well onto the anti-CD3-coated plates and cultured in the presence of increasing concentrations of L-histidine (Thermo Scientific, A10413.22) (0, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10 and 30 mM) for 48 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Supernatants were collected at 48 h and stored at −20 °C until analysis. IFN-γ and IL-17A secretion were quantified by ELISA (BioLegend, ELISA Max Deluxe Set Human IFN-g, 430116; ELISA Max Deluxe Set Human IL-17A, 433914) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Machine learning and statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (v.4.4.1). Model development, validation and reporting followed established principles for clinical prediction and survival modeling to minimize overfitting, control optimism and support reproducibility across independent datasets. Procedures were prespecified before external validation and implemented using scripted, version-controlled pipelines with fixed random seeds.

Data preprocessing and batch harmonization

Metabolite intensities were log10-transformed to stabilize variance and reduce right-skewness. Inter-batch effects were corrected using the ComBat empirical Bayes procedure (sva package, v.3.52.0). Batch assignments reflected FMT status to account for systematic acquisition differences. Adjustments were performed in a supervised framework preserving variance attributable to prespecified clinical covariates, including age, sex, BMI, tumor type, time from sampling to immunotherapy initiation and treatment group when noncollinear with tumor type.

Cohort definition and longitudinal structure

Eligibility criteria were defined to ensure valid longitudinal inference and consistent survival endpoints. Patients were included if they had at least two post-treatment samples, available PFS data (event or right censoring) and samples collected after immunotherapy initiation and before progression or censoring. Cohorts were retained only when acquisition protocols, preprocessing steps and clinical annotations were harmonized, thereby reducing immortal-time bias and ensuring consistency in outcome definitions. Model training was performed using pooled IMMUNOLIFE1 and SABR cohorts, while independent PANDORE and MIMic cohorts were entirely withheld for external validation without shared preprocessing, feature selection or tuning.

Longitudinal survival modeling

Progression risk was modeled using DynForest (v.1.2.0), a random survival forest framework for time-dependent covariates and right-censored outcomes. Tree-based ensemble survival methods were selected a priori for favorable bias–variance characteristics under moderate sample sizes, robustness to multicollinearity and the ability to model nonlinear effects without parametric assumptions or temporal interpolation. Alternative sequence models, including long short-term memory and time-aware recurrent neural networks, were evaluated in preliminary analyses but exhibited unstable convergence and limited generalization under sparse longitudinal sampling; lower-complexity ensemble methods therefore provided more stable estimation and interpretability.

Dimensionality control and feature selection

Given that the number of metabolites exceeded the number of patients, model complexity was constrained to limit variance inflation. An iterative permutation-importance ablation procedure ranked predictors by importance and sequentially removed the least informative variables until a parsimonious subset was obtained. This approach reduced redundancy and improved interpretability while reflecting pragmatic events-per-variable considerations for survival prediction. Baseline clinical covariates (age, sex, BMI, tumor type, treatment group and disease stage) were retained in all iterations to mitigate confounding.

Internal validation and performance estimation

All preprocessing, feature selection and model fitting were confined to training cohorts to prevent information leakage. Internal predictive performance was estimated via bootstrap resampling with .632+ correction. Discrimination was quantified using time-dependent ROC–AUC and Harrell’s concordance index, while prediction error was assessed using the IBS with inverse probability of censoring weights. Individual risk predictions were generated within a 6-month landmark framework to standardize prediction horizons and avoid look-ahead bias.

External validation and assessment of transportability

External validation was performed on fully independent cohorts obtained at distinct institutions and time periods. Model discrimination and prediction error were evaluated without recalibration or retuning. Cohort heterogeneity with respect to tumor type, disease stage, treatment regimen and sampling schedule was intentionally preserved to evaluate robustness under distributional shift and to support generalizability across clinically diverse settings. Predictors were considered reliable only when associations were reproducible across independent populations.

Clinical-only baseline modeling

To quantify the incremental predictive value of metabolomics beyond baseline clinical features, a comparator survival model was trained using only clinical variables with randomForestSRC (v.3.3.1). One sample per patient (earliest post-treatment time point) was included, and external validation followed the same procedure as for the longitudinal model.

Linear mixed-effects modeling of metabolite trajectories

Associations between clinical variables and metabolite dynamics were assessed using separate linear mixed-effects models (lmer, lme4 v.1.1.35.5). Fixed effects included age, sex, BMI, disease stage, tumor type, treatment group and time to immunotherapy initiation, with interaction terms where appropriate. Patient-specific random intercepts accounted for within-subject correlation. Statistical inference for fixed effects used Satterthwaite’s approximation of degrees of freedom (lmerTest v.3.1.3) and FDR correction (Benjamini–Hochberg) was applied across metabolites separately for each fixed-effects term. Equivalent modeling was performed for fecal metabolite datasets with additional adjustment for study membership and irAEs.

Survival and nonparametric analyses

KM survival curves and Cox proportional hazards models were estimated using the survival package (v.3.8.3). Continuous biomarkers were dichotomized at cohort-specific medians for visualization. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were derived from Wald statistics. Continuous associations were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation, and longitudinal group comparisons used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

Microbe–metabolite joint modeling

Joint modeling of microbial and metabolite compositional profiles was conducted using the Orthus framework (fido v.1.1.1), a Bayesian multivariate latent-variable model estimating posterior cross-covariance structures between log-ratio-transformed features. Posterior samples were converted to proportionality metrics (ρ) accounting for compositional constraints. Associations were retained only when 95% credible intervals excluded zero and effect sizes were within the upper quantile of the empirical distribution to provide conservative control of false positives for downstream network analyses.

Statistical consideration

Model complexity was deliberately constrained to ensure stable estimation under high dimensionality. Confounding was mitigated through prespecified covariate adjustment and supervised batch harmonization. Development and validation datasets remained completely separated to limit optimistic bias. Analytical procedures and evaluation criteria were defined before external testing, and no post hoc feature tuning was performed. Data distributions were assessed before analysis. When normality assumptions were not met, nonparametric tests were used instead. Where relevant, individual data points are shown in the figures. No animals or data points were excluded from the analyses.
