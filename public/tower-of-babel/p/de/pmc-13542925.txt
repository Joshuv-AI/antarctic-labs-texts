Migratory periocular silicone granulomas following breast implantation mimicking sarcoidosis
Authors: Maureen Whittelsey, Tatiana Abrantes, Benjamin Kahn, Leslie Robinson-Bostom
Published: 2026

Case description

A 46-year-old immunocompetent woman with a history of bilateral silicone breast implants (SBIs) (2011) placed in the United States following standard surgical protocol and cosmetic blepharoplasty (2016) presented to dermatology in 2021 with a 3-month history of a progressively enlarging, pruritic, skin-colored nodule near the right medial canthus and discrete papules along the upper eyelid (Fig 1). She reported systemic symptoms since SBI implantation, including fatigue, nonspecific rashes, and alopecia.

Clinical image showing a skin-colored nodule near the right medial canthus (arrow) and discrete skin-colored papules on the upper eyelid.

Periocular symptoms began in 2013 with bilateral periocular pain, right upper eyelid swelling, and ptosis. At that time, CT imaging revealed subtle right lacrimal gland enlargement, and subsequent biopsy showed chronic inflammation and fibrosis of the gland, leading to a presumed diagnosis of dacryoadenitis.

In 2015, a skin biopsy was taken from 1 of the persistent right upper eyelid nodules and revealed noncaseating granulomas; sarcoidosis was suspected, but subsequent systemic workup was unremarkable.

The patient underwent cosmetic blepharoplasty in 2016 due to eyelid asymmetry concerns. Following this procedure, she continued to present with right upper eyelid nodules. Sequential biopsies (2018-2021) demonstrated dense connective tissue, lipid drop-out spaces, and asteroid bodies—initially attributed to sarcoidosis, lipogranulomas, or a foreign body reaction.

A final biopsy in March 2021 (Fig 2) identified a sarcoidal granulomatous reaction to nonpolarizable foreign material with numerous vacuoles and cystic spaces, leading to the diagnosis.

Histopathologic findings. Right medial canthus biopsy demonstrating a sarcoidal granulomatous reaction to nonpolarizable foreign material with characteristic vacuoles and cystic spaces consistent with silicone. The arrows in image (C) highlight the nonpolarizable silicone material. (A-C, Hematoxylin and eosin stain. A, × 4; B, ×10, and C, ×40 original magnification.)

The patient elected for SBI and capsule removal in July 2021. Following removal, the periocular lesions and her systemic symptoms resolved completely, with no recurrence and no evidence of sarcoid disease to date.

Discussion

Silicone granulomas are a rare but significant complication of silicone exposure, most commonly reported after SBI rupture or leakage, dermal filler injections, unregulated cosmetic silicone injections, or ocular silicone oil use.1 They represent a chronic immune-mediated response to foreign silicone material, resulting in granuloma formation.1 This reaction may occur months to years after exposure.2,3 Even without overt implant rupture, silicone can permeate intact implant shells in microscopic quantities—a phenomenon known as gel bleed.1,3 Once extracapsular, silicone particles can migrate through lymphatic or hematogenous routes to distant sites such as the periocular region.1, 2, 3, 4

This case exemplifies the diagnostic challenge of silicone granulomas, which, in this case, masqueraded clinically as dacryoadenitis, then sarcoidosis, over nearly a decade before the correct diagnosis was established. This highlights how closely silicone granulomas can mimic other granulomatous conditions. Key histopathological mimics include sarcoidosis, atypical mycobacterial infection, silica granuloma, and chalazia. Sarcoidosis shares the noncaseating granulomas and asteroid bodies seen in silicone granulomas but lacks vacuolated cystic spaces with nonpolarizable foreign material.5 Atypical mycobacterial infection was ruled out with negative acid-fast bacilli and Fite staining.5 Silica granulomas contain birefringent, polarizable particles, unlike silicone which is refractile but nonpolarizable under polarized light microscopy.6 Chalazia are lipogranulomatous inflammations of the meibomian (sebaceous) glands that may also contain central vacuoles but lack nonpolarizable refractile material.7,8 The diagnosis of silicone granuloma was confirmed by the presence of vacuolated cystic spaces and granulomatous inflammation surrounding nonpolarizable foreign material, with negative adipophilin staining.3

Treatment options include intralesional and systemic corticosteroids, minocycline, TNF-α inhibitors, or surgical intervention.9 In this case, complete resolution following SBI removal is consistent with prior reports and underscores the efficacy of surgical intervention.9

This case illustrates how silicone granulomas can present at sites distant from the silicone source, years after exposure, with intact silicone implants, and mimic other granulomatous conditions. Clinicians and pathologists should maintain a high index of suspicion in patients with granulomatous lesions and any history of silicone exposure to prevent diagnostic delays.
