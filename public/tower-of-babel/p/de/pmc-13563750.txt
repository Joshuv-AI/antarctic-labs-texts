Relationship between nursing notes sentiment scores and length of hospital stay in elderly knee osteoarthritis patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Qingmei Wu, Qin Feng, Yiyang Liu, Wen Wang, Minyan Song, Fei Pan, Yufei Shao, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith ongoing population aging, the prevalence of knee osteoarthritis (KOA) keeps increasing in the elderly population. For end-stage KOA patients, total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains the definitive and effective treatment. Although numerous studies have examined factors associated with length of hospital stay (LOS) in TKA patients, these investigations predominantly focus on patient characteristics and surgical variables, often overlooking the “human factor” within the nursing process. This study aimed to examine the association between nursing note sentiment scores and prolonged length of hospital stay (PLOS) for KOA patients undergoing TKA.MethodsIn this retrospective observational study, sentiment analysis of nursing notes from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV database was performed using natural language processing methods. Both random forest and AdaBoost algorithms were utilized to assess the significance of variables. Multivariable logistic regression was used to further clarify the relationship between PLOS and continuous/tertiles sentiment scores, and to construct a nomogram model. Quantile regression was utilized to determine dynamic associations between two continuous variables. The receiver operating characteristic curve was utilized to determine the nomogram model’s performance.Results648 KOA patients were included, with a median hospital LOS of 3.570 days. Multivariable logistic regression revealed that continuous scaled polarity was negatively associated with PLOS (OR = 0.209, 95%CI: 0.061–0.715). This association persisted when sentiment polarity was converted to a categorical variable (ORQ2=0.589, 95%CI: 0.372–0.931; ORQ3=0.616, 95%CI:0.386–0.982). Additionally, factors such as myocardial infarction, rheumatic disease, mild liver disease, smoking status, anticoagulant, and red blood cell distribution width were identified as influencing factors of PLOS (all OR > 1). The nomogram model constructed based on these factors demonstrated moderate discriminatory performance for PLOS (AUC: 0.702). Quantile regression indicated that when LOS < 70th percentile (3.750 days), LOS showed minimal correlation with scaled polarity. However, at LOS ≥ 70th percentile, LOS was significantly correlated with scaled polarity, with the negative correlation becoming stronger as LOS lengthened.ConclusionSentiment polarity was negatively associated with hospital LOS, but this effect was primarily evident following PLOS. Monitoring nurses’ sentiment states at critical junctures during inpatient treatment may enhance their motivation and facilitate patient recovery.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04919-y.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Knee osteoarthritis, Sentiment, Healthcare

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic degenerative condition affecting joints and surrounding tissues, mainly characterized by the progressive deterioration and destruction of articular cartilage. This process eventually involves peripheral structures like subchondral bone and synovium, leading to joint dysfunction and persistent pain [1]. As one of the most prevalent forms of OA, knee osteoarthritis (KOA) presents an increasingly significant disease burden. Statistics reveal that around 32.5 million adults in the United States are affected [2]. Among older adults, OA is widely recognized as the leading cause of knee pain. With ongoing population aging, KOA prevalence keeps increasing in the elderly population. A cutoff of ≥ 55 years to define older adults has been commonly used in research on both KOA and joint replacement surgery [3–6]. Therefore, this study adopted ≥ 55 years as the age criterion for including older KOA patients.

Due to its frequent association with severe musculoskeletal pain and physical functional limitations, KOA has become a major global contributor to long-term disability and reduced quality of life [7]. Furthermore, owing to its chronic progressive nature, patients typically require long-term or lifelong medical support. Individuals with OA exhibit significantly higher healthcare resource utilisation (HCRU) and associated medical costs [8, 9]. Current primary treatments remain symptom-relieving measures, with total knee arthroplasty (TKA) serving as the definitive and effective treatment option for end-stage patients [10]. TKA is widely acknowledged as a cost-effective procedure that greatly enhances joint function, reduces pain, and improves quality of life. However, it also imposes a significant economic burden, affecting patients’ recovery and quality of life post-surgery [11]. Length of hospital stay (LOS) is a crucial metric for evaluating patient severity and hospital resource efficiency. Prolonged hospital stays directly escalate medical expenses, increasing the financial strain on patients [12]. In the context of ongoing Diagnosis-Related Groups (DRG)/Big Data Diagnosis-Intervention Packet (DIP) medical insurance payment reforms and the widespread implementation of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery principles [13, 14], effectively reducing LOS for TKA patients while maintaining healthcare quality and patient satisfaction presents a significant challenge for healthcare institutions.

Notably, under the DRG/DIP payment system, the link between nursing staffing levels and economic pressures has become more evident. Studies show that nurse-to-patient ratios are gradually decreasing, while nursing workloads are increasing. This trend may further affect the quality of nursing care and patient outcomes [15]. Nursing, as a profession requiring high emotional investment, demands that practitioners manage their emotions while providing professional care daily [16]. Indeed, more than half of nurses often experience complex emotions such as empathy and grief [17]. “Sentiment” generally refers to the sentiment polarity of a text string. It is often measured on a numeric scale from − 1 (very negative) to 1 (very positive). Alternatively, classifiers can estimate the probability of a text being “positive” or “negative” [18]. Using sentiment analysis, we can capture subjective expressions from clinical notes. This allows us to gain insight into clinicians’ emotions and attitudes toward their patients, which may help predict patient outcomes [19]. According to emotional labor theory, positive nurse sentiment supports better nurse-patient relationships, promotes patient recovery, and shortens hospital stays [20]. In contrast, emotional exhaustion or negative sentiment among nurses may reduce care quality and is linked to poorer prognosis [18, 21].

Although numerous studies have examined factors influencing hospital LOS in TKA patients, these investigations predominantly focus on patient characteristics (e.g., age, comorbidities) and surgical variables (e.g., anaesthetic technique, surgical approach) [22–25]. They often overlook the “human factor” within the nursing process, particularly nurses’ sentiment investment and its potential influence on patient recovery. In this context, this study aims to utilize the large-scale public Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) clinical database. By employing natural language processing (NLP) techniques to quantitatively analyze sentiment content in nursing notes, it systematically examines the relationship between nursing sentiment scores and hospital LOS in KOA patients undergoing TKA. This study seeks to provide real-world data supporting the clinical value of “sentiment nursing” and offer evidence-based guidance for hospital management. By prioritizing nurses’ sentiment well-being and optimizing human resource allocation, it ultimately aims to enhance nursing quality and improve patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Data resource and population

This study was retrospective, with data sourced from the MIMIC-IV database. Established by Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) and Massachusetts Institute of Technology, this repository compiles information on patients admitted to intensive care units for the first time within the Commonwealth of Massachusetts between 2008 and 2019. The MIMIC-IV database is a public repository where patient information has been anonymised. Ethical approval was waived by the Institutional Review Board of BIDMC. Therefore, no informed consent from patients was required [26].

We retrieved 2,005 patients with KOA from the MIMIC-IV database, applying the following inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 55 years (3, 4); (2) undergoing TKA. Patients with LOS ≤ 1 day (inadequate duration for obtaining certain laboratory data) were excluded, resulting in 648 elderly KOA patients undergoing TKA being included in the study.

Outcome

The outcome of this study was prolonged length of hospital stay (PLOS). Consensus remains elusive regarding the optimal cutoff point for PLOS classification across studies, with common thresholds being 14 days, 28 days, or the 75th percentile [27]. Given that TKA studies report a mean LOS of 2.470 days [28], cutoffs of 14 or 28 days prove unsuitable. Husted H et al. [29] employed a 4-day threshold when investigating LOS for TKA, whereas Chen TL et al. [30] selected the 75th percentile as the basis for grouping. The mean LOS for TKA in this study was 3.960 days, with a median of 3.570 days. To better align with the LOS patterns of our study population, we pre-specified LOS > 75th percentile as PLOS. The 75th percentile in this study was 4.440 days. We rounded this value to 4 days for two reasons: (1) clinical interpretability-a 4-day threshold is more intuitive for clinicians to apply in practice; and (2) consistency with previous TKA literature (e.g., Husted H et al. used a 4-day cutoff) [29]. To check whether rounding affected patient classification, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using the original 75th percentile value of 4.440 days as the cutoff. This analysis confirmed that the rounding did not alter the direction or significance of the core findings (see Results 3.4 section). Thus, patients with LOS > 4 days were assigned to the PLOS group (n = 176), while those with LOS ≤ 4 days were assigned to the non-PLOS group (n = 472).

Sentiment scores

The Python programming language and TextBlob natural language processing library were employed to calculate sentiment scores from nursing notes, reflecting nurses’ feelings toward patients. This syntactic approach is methodologically consistent with prior work in nursing informatics. Specifically, Waudby-Smith et al. successfully applied the same method to analyze sentiment in MIMIC nursing notes, ensuring comparability with existing literature [19]. TextBlob is a well-established NLP library. Its PatternAnalyzer employs a transparent lexicon-based approach (polarity lexicon, modifier word recognition, and negation handling), providing interpretable results that are particularly suitable for this exploratory hypothesis-generating study, as it does not require a domain-specific annotated corpus. Detailed technical documentation is available at: https://app.readthedocs.org/projects/textblob/downloads/pdf/dev/. After selecting eligible participants based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, the original, unprocessed nursing notes were passed directly to TextBlob. TextBlob’s pattern module assessed the emotional tone of text strings within nursing notes. Sentiment polarity scores ranged from − 1 to 1, while subjectivity scores ranged from 0 to 1. Higher scores indicated more positive and subjective sentiment.

Variables collected

This study gathered baseline information potentially associated with PLOS, including demographic characteristics, medication use, comorbidities, and laboratory indicators. Variable selection was guided by prior literature on LOS following TKA [22–25]. Demographic characteristics comprised: age (years), gender (male and female), race (white and other), body mass index (BMI, kg/m²), insurance (medicare, medicaid, and other), marital status (married and other), drinking status, and smoking status. Race was classified as white or other, with other representing all non-White categories (e.g., Black, Asian, Hispanic). Marital status was classified as married or other, where other includes single, widowed, or divorced. Medication use during hospitalization included: antibiotics (yes/no), anticoagulants (yes/no), non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (yes/no), acetaminophen (yes/no), opioids (yes/no), and local anaesthetics (yes/no). Comorbidities encompassed: myocardial infarction (MI) (yes/no), congestive heart failure (CHF) (yes/no), peripheral vascular disease (PVD) (yes/no), cerebrovascular disease (CVD) (yes/no), dementia (yes/no), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (yes/no), rheumatic disease (yes/no), mild liver disease (MLD) (yes/no), diabetes with or without complications or comorbidities (cc) (yes/no), and renal disease (yes/no). The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was also considered. Laboratory parameters were measured within the first 24 h of hospital admission and included: blood urea nitrogen (BUN, mg/dL), phosphate (mg/dL), glucose (mg/dL), calcium (mg/dL), creatinine (mg/dL), hemoglobin (g/dL), platelets (K/µL), potassium (mEq/L), red blood cell (RBC, m/µL), red blood cell distribution width (RDW, %), and white blood cell (WBC, K/µL).

Statistical analysis

Data were processed and analyzed using SPSS (version 25.0) and R software (version 4.4.2). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables were described using frequency and percentage, with group comparisons performed using chi-square tests. For continuous variables, normality was first assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were described using mean and standard deviation, with intergroup comparisons performed using t-tests. For non-normally distributed data, statistical descriptions employed median and quartiles, with intergroup comparisons conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Additionally, the “mice” package was used to perform multiple imputation as the primary analysis for variables with missing values that were significant in baseline comparisons (Table S1). Complete-case analysis was also conducted as a sensitivity analysis to validate the robustness of the results. Multicollinearity diagnostics were performed on the imputed data, with a variance inflation factor (VIF) > 10 indicating collinearity.

To assess the importance of nursing note sentiment scores for PLOS, both random forest and AdaBoost algorithms were employed to rank the importance of all variables. Random Forest represents a Bagging strategy, while AdaBoost represents a Boosting strategy. Using both provides complementary perspectives on variable importance. An increase of one unit in mean sentiment polarity corresponds to half the variable’s possible range. Consequently, adjusted odds ratios (OR) would be extremely small, or OR values would be extremely large, making interpretation difficult. To address this, the authors scaled both variables (sentiment polarity or subjectivity) by a factor of 10, making changes of 0.1 units more interpretable [18]. Multivariable logistic regression was used to further clarify the relationship between PLOS and continuous/ tertiles sentiment scores after adjusting for potential confounders. Subgroup analyses and interaction analyses were conducted to validate the robustness of this association.

Beyond regression, we explored potential nonlinear and dynamic patterns. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were employed to explore relationships between PLOS or LOS and sentiment scores. Quantile regression was utilised to determine how dynamic associations between sentiment scores and LOS vary across different percentiles of LOS. Finally, we built a clinically applicable discriminative model to distinguish PLOS from non-PLOS patients. A nomogram was constructed based on variables significant in the multivariate analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) analysis were used to determine the nomogram model’s performance in discriminating PLOS compared to a single index, and to assess the degree of performance improvement.

Results

Patient demographics

This study included 648 patients with KOA undergoing TKA. The median age was 65.000 years. Female patients constituted 64.506% of the cohort, while males accounted for 35.494%. The mean hospital LOS was 3.960 days, with a median of 3.570 days. Comparing baseline characteristics between the non-PLOS and PLOS groups revealed differences in demographic features: the PLOS group had higher age (67.000 vs. 65.000, smoking (3.977% vs. 1.217%), medicare coverage (53.977% vs. 45.975%), and medicaid coverage (4.545% vs. 2.331%) than the non-PLOS group (all P < 0.05). The anticoagulant use rate in the PLOS group (73.864% vs. 51.695%, P < 0.001) was also higher than in the non-PLOS group. Regarding comorbidities, the PLOS group exhibited higher prevalence rates for MI (11.364% vs. 4.237%), CHF (15.341% vs. 6.144%), rheumatic disease (6.250% vs. 2.331%), MLD (6.818% vs. 2.331%), diabetes with cc (11.364% vs. 5.297%), and renal disease (16.477% vs. 6.780%) were higher than in the non-PLOS group (all P < 0.05). Sentiment polarity in the PLOS group (0.055 vs. 0.059, P = 0.002) was lower than in the non-PLOS group. Both RDW and BUN levels in the PLOS group were higher than in the non-PLOS group before and after imputation (all P < 0.05) (Tables 1, 2, and 3). Imputation diagnostics showed that density plots revealed high overlap between imputed and observed values (Figure S1), indicating that the imputed values were plausible and did not introduce systematic bias.

Results indicated no multicollinearity among the different variables (all VIF < 3) (Table S2). Subsequently, both random forest and AdaBoost algorithms were employed to rank the differential variables by importance. Nursing note sentiment polarity score ranked second in the random forest model and first in the AdaBoost model (Table 4 and Fig. 1).

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed three sensitivity analyses: First, a complete-case analysis was conducted. The results were consistent with the primary analysis: continuous scaled sentiment polarity remained negatively associated with PLOS (OR = 0.238, 95% CI: 0.066–0.861) (Table S4). Second, an alternative PLOS cutoff was used (> 4.440 days, the 75th percentile). Using the primary cutoff (> 4 days), 176 patients were classified as PLOS; using the alternative cutoff (> 4.440 days), 161 patients were classified as PLOS, with 15 patients having a LOS between 4 and 4.440 days. The results remained consistent: continuous scaled sentiment polarity was negatively associated with PLOS (OR = 0.140, 95% CI: 0.039–0.503) (Table S4). Therefore, we propose to retain the clinically intuitive “>4 days” as the primary definition for PLOS.

Third, we conducted subgroup analyses and interaction analyses based on the variables that were identified as significant in the multivariate analysis. Regarding the influence on PLOS, continuous scaled sentiment polarity showed no interaction with other variables, yielding relatively stable results (all P for interaction > 0.05) (Fig. 3). Additionally, the relationship between sentiment polarity and LOS was also examined. Unadjusted RCS revealed a nonlinear relationship between sentiment polarity and LOS (P nonlinear = 0.002) (Fig. 4A). Further quantile regression analysis revealed that when LOS was below the 70th percentile (3.750 days), LOS showed little correlation with scaled polarity. However, when LOS reached or exceeded the 70th percentile, LOS correlated with scaled polarity, with the negative correlation intensifying as hospital LOS lengthened (Fig. 4B). This aligned with the RCS trend and further substantiated the scientific rationale for using a 4-day threshold in analysing the relationship between hospital LOS and polarity in this study.

Based on multifactorial study results, significant variables were incorporated into the nomogram model (Hosmer-Lemeshow test χ2 = 8.934, P = 0.348) (Fig. 5A). Using the Bootstrap resampling method (B = 1000), the calibration curve demonstrated satisfactory model performance, with a calibration intercept of 0.040 and a calibration slope of 0.860 (Fig. 5B). The model’s Brier score of 0.176 similarly attests to its moderate performance. ROC analysis yielded an AUC value of 0.702 for the nomogram model (Fig. 5C and Table 6), while IDI analysis demonstrated improved performance relative to single-marker approaches (Table 7).

Discussion

With the widespread adoption and advancement of Electronic Health Record (EHR) systems, substantial volumes of free-text data have accumulated within clinical practice. This has established clinical text processing as a significant research domain [31, 32]. As a vital component of EHRs, nursing notes document changes in patient condition and nursing interventions during hospitalisation in an unstructured, free-narrative format, capturing rich details absent from other structured data or clinical documentation [33]. In recent years, the application of NLP techniques has progressively extended to nursing note analysis, such as identifying patients at high risk of falls or predicting home-care patients requiring emergency care or readmission [34, 35], demonstrating its potent capability in extracting latent clinical information.

Emotional labor is expressed and transmitted through verbal and non-verbal communication in nurse-patient interactions [36]. The sentiment polarity captured in nursing notes may serve as a textual trace of nurses’ emotional labor. According to Hochschild’s theory of emotional labor, nurses continuously regulate their emotions during patient interactions through two main strategies: “surface acting” (concealing genuine feelings) and “deep acting” (actively adjusting inner feelings) [37]. These emotion management processes are inadvertently externalized through linguistic features in nursing notes, such as word choice and emotional tone. Furthermore, Watson’s Theory of Human Caring suggests that positive sentiment polarity reflects a state of “emotional availability” in nurses, enabling them to deliver authentic caring; conversely, negative polarity may indicate emotional resource depletion, potentially hindering caring behaviors [38, 39].

We employed NLP techniques to extract sentiment scores from nursing notes within the MIMIC-IV database and analysed the relationship between these scores and hospital LOS in KOA patients undergoing TKA. Results revealed that nursing note sentiment polarity was associated with PLOS, with subgroup analyses confirming the robustness of this association. Quantile regression analysis revealed that when LOS was below the 70th percentile (3.750 days), the correlation between LOS and polarity was negligible. However, when LOS reached or exceeded the 70th percentile, a significant correlation emerged, with the negative association between LOS and polarity intensifying as hospital LOS increased. In healthcare settings, emotions and related labour constitute an indispensable component. Particularly in high-stress contexts (such as surgical procedures or critical care), they facilitate the establishment of positive nurse-patient relationships, enhancing patients’ sense of security and comfort [40, 41]. Frequent exposure to emotionally demanding tasks requires individuals to invest greater emotional, cognitive, or physical resources to manage tension, potentially leading to diminished self-efficacy and work engagement, thereby contributing to emotional exhaustion and burnout [42, 43]. Nurses’ burnout risk is related to longer hospital LOS [15, 44]. From the perspective of emotional labor theory, the negative correlation between sentiment polarity and LOS was only significant when LOS exceeded the 70th percentile (approximately 4 days), reflecting a combination of active emotional engagement and passive stress accumulation. During short LOS, nurse-patient interactions were primarily task-oriented, and nurses predominantly adopted “surface acting” (concealing genuine feelings), resulting in limited impact of emotional investment on outcomes. In contrast, patients with PLOS often have complex conditions requiring nurses to perform “deep acting” (e.g., empathy, patient communication). In this context, positive sentiment polarity can actively promote recovery and shorten LOS. Concurrently, persistent PLOS leads to cumulative emotional stress in nurses, decreasing sentiment polarity and forming a vicious cycle of “stress-exhaustion-adverse outcomes.” When hospital LOS was under 4 days, sentiment polarity showed no link with the LOS. Therefore, it is possible that focusing too much on patient emotions during this time might increase emotional labor burdens. This could potentially contribute to burnout and decreased work efficiency. When hospital LOS exceeded 4 days, the nurse’s sentiment polarity was related to the LOS. At this point, it is plausible that clinical nurses’ attention to patients could be associated with reduced hospital LOS, potentially saving healthcare resources. In conclusion, the timing of focused attention on patients during hospitalization may be a relevant factor in understanding nurse burnout, though this relationship warrants further investigation.

Beyond sentiment polarity, we identified MI, rheumatic disease, MLD, smoking status, anticoagulant use, and RDW as additional factors associated with PLOS in KOA patients undergoing TKA. MI patients have increased cardiac risk requiring close monitoring; impaired cardiac function results in extended recovery, while polypharmacy complicates treatment management [45]. Patients with rheumatic disease show systemic inflammation and multiple comorbidities. Immunosuppression raises infection risk and contributes to severe joint deformity and poor bone quality [46, 47], while MLD often occurs alongside metabolic issues like obesity and diabetes [48]. Research in orthopaedics indicates that smokers suffer from greater loss of articular cartilage and more peripheral joint pain than non-smokers [49]. Smoking also hinders bone formation, affecting natural healing and fusion processes [50]. The use of anticoagulants can heighten the risk of bleeding and haematomas in patients, potentially delaying both surgery and discharge [51, 52]. RDW is an indicator of chronic inflammation and malnutrition, which can impair tissue healing and reduce overall physiological resilience. High RDW levels (> 14.5%) are associated with longer hospital LOS [53]. These factors rarely existed in isolation. They were interconnected and acted synergistically, forming a complex risk network. This network ultimately necessitated extended hospital LOS to ensure patient safety, manage complications, and meet discharge criteria.

Additionally, we constructed a nomogram model using variables significant in multivariable analysis. This model helps identify patients at risk of PLOS and enables targeted resource allocation. It integrated multi-dimensional indicators, including laboratory parameters, smoking status, history of illness, and nursing sentiment polarity, to comprehensively quantify the risk of PLOS. Compared to single indicators, our model demonstrates enhanced overall discriminative performance (AUC: 0.702), highlighting the significant value of multi-factor comprehensive assessment in clinical practice. Although no dedicated predictive model for PLOS in KOA patients after TKA has yet been reported, the research by Chen TL et al. [30] employed multiple machine learning models to forecast PLOS after TKA, with artificial neural networks yielding the best performance (AUC: 0.71). Another study indicated that machine learning models did not outperform traditional multiple regression models (AUC: 0.70) in predicting the risk of hospital LOS exceeding two days after total hip arthroplasty/TKA [54]. In distinguishing PLOS, our model demonstrated comparable discriminatory performance to these studies that did not incorporate nursing emotional factors. However, our study innovatively integrated nursing emotion, focusing not only on patient-specific factors but also encompassing the emotional state of the nursing staff. This approach offers a more comprehensive and humanistic perspective on risk assessment.

This study examined the association of sentiment scores in nursing notes on PLOS of KOA patients undergoing TKA. Short LOS showed no correlation with sentiment polarity, whereas prolonged LOS exhibited a negative correlation with sentiment polarity. These findings suggest that initiating consideration of nursing emotions at an appropriate point during inpatient treatment might help in understanding factors potentially related to nursing burnout and could be associated with patient outcomes. Furthermore, the nomogram model demonstrated favourable discriminatory performance. Utilizing this model for risk stratification may help identify high-risk patients, potentially enabling rational allocation of healthcare resources and enhancing clinical efficiency. Based on the above theoretical framework and our findings (although causal inference is limited by the retrospective design), we propose the following hypothetical recommendations for nursing management: (1) optimise staffing in high-emotional-load units by assigning nurses with strong emotion regulation skills during critical postoperative periods; (2) monitor sentiment trends for early warning by integrating sentiment polarity into quality monitoring systems. A persistent decline in patient-related polarity may indicate clinical deterioration, whereas a systematic decline at the nurse or ward level may signal burnout risk; (3) help nurses develop deep acting skills and reduce emotional exhaustion through training based on Watson’s Caring Theory and the emotional labor framework, alongside establishing regular support groups.

However, this study has the following limitations: (1) As a retrospective observational study using MIMIC-IV data, it precludes causal inference. Specifically, we cannot exclude reverse causality (i.e., PLOS or greater patient severity may lead to more negative sentiment in nursing notes) or confounding by indication (i.e., unmeasured disease severity may influence both LOS and sentiment polarity). Additionally, the MIMIC-IV database primarily includes high-risk or medically complex TKA patients (e.g., those with critical illness or complex medical conditions), whose LOS and recovery trajectories differ from those of routine elective TKA patients managed in general wards. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings is limited and may not extend to the average elderly elective TKA population. Future prospective cohort studies are needed to establish causal relationships (2). Small sample sizes in certain subgroups may have impacted statistical power; findings require validation through large-scale cohort studies (3). No manual validation of the sentiment analysis results was performed. Although TextBlob has been widely used for sentiment analysis, the accuracy of its predictions in the specific context of nursing notes has not been independently verified. Future studies should incorporate manual annotation or external validation to confirm the reliability of NLP-based sentiment extraction (4). Although the MIMIC-IV database used in this study has been de-identified, the application of sentiment analysis in real-world clinical settings would require strict privacy protection mechanisms, including data encryption, access control, and audit trails, to prevent misuse or leakage of sentiment information. Additionally, nurses may alter their documentation behavior if they are aware of such analysis (Hawthorne effect), potentially reducing the authenticity of the records. Future research should obtain informed consent from nurses and ensure that analysis results are used for quality improvement and staff support rather than performance evaluation (5). Although burnout was not directly measured in this study, our discussion of burnout is not presented as a direct finding of this study, but rather as a reasonable hypothesis grounded in prior literature on emotional labor [42, 43]. Future research should directly assess nurse burnout using validated instruments (e.g., Maslach Burnout Inventory) to examine its relationship with nursing note sentiment polarity and patient outcomes.

Conclusion

Sentiment polarity was associated with PLOS. When LOS exceeded four days, sentiment polarity scores showed a significant negative correlation with LOS, which suggested that targeted adjustment of emotional support timing could be considered for optimizing resource allocation and improving outcomes. The study further identified MI, rheumatic diseases, MLD, smoking, anticoagulant use, and elevated RDW (> 14.5%) as independent risk factors for PLOS. The nomogram model constructed based on multivariable factors may enable individualised risk stratification, potentially facilitating early identification of high-risk patients with PLOS. From a nursing practice perspective, monitoring sentiment polarity in nursing notes may enable real-time assessment of nurse-patient interaction quality, support workload management by identifying emotional exhaustion, and highlight the value of patient-centred documentation that captures authentic emotional content. This study provides a practical risk assessment tool for clinical practice, though its conclusions require further validation through large-scale prospective studies.
