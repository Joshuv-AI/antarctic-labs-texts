Value of cardiovascular magnetic resonance–derived quantitative myocardial blood flow assessment in the setting of chronic coronary occlusion
Authors: Georgios Moutzoukis, Thomas Schroeder, Marie K Lorenz, Elif Roedel, Kelvin Chow, Peter Kellman, Raffi Bekeredjian, Nico Schmid, Heiko Mahrholdt, Andreas Seitz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsFor many years, visual assessment has been the mainstay of detecting obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) by stress perfusion cardiovascular magnetic resonance (S-CMR). Recently, fully automated quantitative assessment of myocardial blood flow (MBF) has been introduced. The value of MBF quantification in patients with coronary chronic total occlusion (CTO) is unknown. In this study, we sought to investigate the diagnostic performance of CMR-derived fully automated quantitative MBF assessment in patients with CTO.Methods and resultsConsecutive patients who underwent S-CMR, as well as coronary angiography at our institution between February 2023 and November 2024 were included. S-CMR perfusion imaging was evaluated visually and quantitatively. Stenosis severity was assessed angiographically and >70% was considered severe stenosis. One hundred thirty patients [age 66.7 (59.7–75.2) years, 79.2% male] were included in this analysis. Patients were grouped according to CAD severity (non-obstructed coronary arteries <70%, n = 57; severe stenosis >70%, n = 37; CTO, n = 36). Stress MBF (SMBF) and myocardial perfusion reserve (MPR) were significantly reduced in patients with CTO compared with those with severe stenosis and non-obstructed coronary arteries, respectively. Segmental SMBF <1.02 mL/g/min differentiated CTO from severe stenosis with a good predictive power [area under the curve: 0.792 (95% confidence interval: 0.678–0.887)]. Moreover, integrated visual and quantitative perfusion assessment provided high sensitivity in detecting additional severe stenosis in CTO patients (91.3%).ConclusionAddition of fully automated MBF quantification to clinical routine visual S-CMR reading facilitates non-invasive differentiation between CTO and severe stenosis and provides higher sensitivity regarding true ischaemic burden in patients with CTO and multi-vessel disease, thereby aiding clinical decision-making.Keywords: myocardial perfusion, MRI, CMR, CTO, MBF, quantitative perfusion

Introduction

Stress perfusion cardiovascular magnetic resonance (S-CMR) plays a key role in the work-up of (suspected) coronary artery disease (CAD) and is recommended by current guidelines.1,2 Recently, CMR-derived fully automated inline myocardial blood flow (MBF) assessment has been introduced and first studies suggested improved diagnostic accuracy compared with visual assessment regarding the detection of significant coronary stenosis.3,4

Among patients with CAD, those with chronic total occlusions (CTOs) represent a special challenge both for cardiac imagers and interventionalists. Awareness and early suspicion of CTO facilitates treatment of these patients in expert centres with the goal to increase the likelihood of successful percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and minimization of complications.5,6

S-CMR, known as the ‘one-stop-shop’ cardiac imaging technique, is routinely used to assess ischaemia and viability in patients with known CTO from prior invasive coronary angiography (ICA). In patients who undergo S-CMR prior to ICA, it is currently unknown whether S-CMR allows to distinguish CTO from severe stenosis. Moreover, true ischaemic burden in patients with multi-vessel disease may be underestimated by visual S-CMR assessment in the presence of a CTO.7

In the present study, we sought to investigate the value of quantitative perfusion in the setting of CTO. We hypothesized that fully automated inline quantitative MBF assessment may help to (i) facilitate early identification of patients with chronic occlusive CAD and (ii) improve the diagnostics accuracy regarding true ischaemic burden in patients with CTO and multi-vessel disease.

Methods

Study design and population

Among the patients undergoing CMR who consented to participate in the prospective ‘Stuttgart Registry for Myocardial Perfusion’ between February 2023 and November 2024, a total of 138 patients underwent S-CMR with quantitative MBF assessment, as well as coronary angiography at our centre within 6 months. Patients with poor CMR image quality (e.g. due to artefacts) and insufficient vasodilator stress [stress MBF (SMBF) <1.43 mL/g/min in all myocardial segments and heart rate response <15 bpm]8 were excluded. Thus, the final population for further analysis was 130 patients (n = 128 with ICA and 2 patients with coronary computed tomography angiography). This was an exploratory, hypothesis-generating analysis. The study protocol was approved by the local institutional review board (University of Tübingen), and all patients gave written informed consent.

Cardiovascular MR imaging

Each patient underwent standardized S-CMR using a 1.5 T scanner (Magnetom Aera, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) according to our institutional protocol9 and in line with current recommendations.10 In brief, the S-CMR protocol included CINE steady-state free precession (SSFP) images followed by stress and rest myocardial first-pass perfusion and finally late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging. CINE images were acquired in short and long axes covering the entire left ventricular myocardium. Subsequently, vasodilator stress first-pass perfusion imaging was performed in three short-axis slices using a previously described SSFP sequence based on the dual-sequence technique and with fully automated inline MBF quantification. The sequence and image reconstruction were previously described in detail elsewhere.11,12 Vasodilator stress was established using either continuous intravenous adenosine infusion (140 µg/kg/min) or regadenoson (400 µg) bolus injection under continuous heart rate and blood pressure monitoring. All patients were asked to pause any antianginal medication and abstain from caffeine consumption 24 h before CMR exam to ensure adequate vasodilation and prevent previously reported potential underestimation of hyperaemic myocardial perfusion.13

Rest perfusion images were acquired 10 min after stress perfusion imaging. LGE images were obtained using a segmented inversion recovery fast low angle shot sequence with manual adjustment of the inversion time. The dosage of the contrast agent per perfusion acquisition (stress and rest, respectively) was gadoteridol 0.075 mmol/kg at a flow rate of 3.5 mL/s followed by a 40 mL saline flush at the same flow rate.

Visual and quantitative MBF assessment

Visual assessment of first-pass perfusion was performed independently by three experienced observers (European Society of Cardiologie/ European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging CMR Level 3 certified) who were blinded to the coronary angiography results. Fully automated MBF quantification for the global left ventricular myocardium as well as for all 16 American Heart Association (AHA) segments individually was provided inline and depicted using the Gadgetron software package as described elsewhere.11,14 Myocardial perfusion reserve (MPR) was calculated for each segment and vessel territory as the ratio of stress and rest perfusion. MBF and MPR data were processed and investigated according to different approaches: a ‘lowest segment’ approach (single segment with lowest SMBF/MPR), a ‘lowest two segments’ approach (mean of the two segments with lowest SMBF/MPR), and a ‘vessel territory’ approach (vessel territory with lowest SMBF/MPR according to AHA classification), respectively. Moreover, besides transmural MBF data, further MBF data was available for 50% sub-endocardial and 50% sub-epicardial left ventricular (LV) myocardium.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are demonstrated as medians (Q1–Q3), while categorical variables are presented as frequencies [numbers (%)]. Continuous variables with skewed distribution were analysed using non-parametric Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis test as appropriate. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared using student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance. Categorical data were analysed using χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed to evaluate diagnostic accuracy of SMBF and MPR, and optimal cut-off values were determined based on optimal sensitivity and specificity according to Youden’s index. The diagnostic performance of quantitative S-CMR perfusion variables was reported as area under the curve (AUC), positive and negative likelihood ratios (LR+ and LR−) as well as positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV). Data analysis was performed using Jamovi and SPSS software. Internal validation of cut-off values by bootstrapping with 1000 replicates was performed with MATLAB software (R2024b). A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

Between February 2023 and November 2024, a total of 130 patients who underwent S-CMR as well as coronary angiography [128 patients with invasive angiography and 2 patients with rule-out of obstructive CAD (OCAD) by coronary computed tomography angiography (CTCA)] at our institution within 6 months were included in this study (see Supplementary data online, Figure S1). In most patients (87/130, 67%), S-CMR was performed first followed by ICA and in 33% vice-versa. The average time interval between S-CMR and ICA in the overall study population was <1 month and only 10 patients had a time interval between 3 and 6 months. In the overall study population, patients were on average 66.7 (Q1–Q3: 59.7–75.2) years old, and 79.2% were male. Patients were grouped according to the severity of CAD by coronary angiography [non-obstructive CAD (NOCAD, <70% stenosis), n = 57; severe stenosis (>70%), n = 37; and CTO, n = 36]. Patients with OCAD (severe stenosis or CTO) were older than patients with NOCAD. Most patients were symptomatic (chest pain 56.9% and dyspnoea 40%), and symptoms were comparable across the spectrum of CAD severity.

On a per-vessel basis, lesion location in the severe stenosis group was proximal 74%, mid 15% and distal 11%. In the CTO patients, most lesions were located in the proximal (41%) or mid-vessel (41%) segments, and only 18% were distal occlusions. All patients with CTO had collateral flow (Rentrop 1: n = 5 patients, Rentrop 2: n = 14 patients, and Rentrop 3: n = 17 patients).

On CMR, there was a higher prevalence of previous myocardial infarction (i.e. ischaemic LGE) and—although overall preserved in all subgroups—a slightly lower LV ejection fraction (LVEF) in patients with CTO compared with patients with severe stenosis or NOCAD. Among the 36 patients with CTO, 26 patients (72.2%) had some evidence of ischaemic LGE within the CTO territory, whereas 10 patients (27.8%) did not have any LGE. Diagnostic performance of visual S-CMR perfusion assessment

Sixty-three out of 130 included patients (48.4%) had evidence of myocardial ischaemia on visual S-CMR assessment. Among them, coronary angiography confirmed OCAD in 53 patients (84.1%; 22 patients with severe stenosis and 31 patients with collateralized CTO), whereas 10 patients (15.8%) had NOCAD. Of the 67 patients with negative visual perfusion S-CMR, coronary angiography revealed NOCAD in 47 patients (70.1%), but in 20 patients (29.8%; 15 patients with severe stenosis and 5 patients with collateralized CTO), OCAD was found despite the negative visual S-CMR reading. Thus, diagnostic accuracy of visual perfusion S-CMR assessment for the detection of OCAD by experienced observers was as follows: AUC 0.775 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.692–0.858], sensitivity 72.6%, specificity 82.4%, PPV 84.1%, and NPV 70.1%.

Diagnostic benefit of quantitative S-CMR perfusion assessment

As previously described, SMBF and MPR data were analysed following three different approaches (lowest segment, lowest two segments, and vessel territory). For all three approaches, transmural SMBF as well as MPR differed significantly between patients with severe stenosis, collateralized CTO, and NOCAD (Figure 1; Table 1). Similar differences between the three groups were observed for the sub-endocardial and the sub-epicardial layers (data not shown). To determine the possible impact of previous myocardial infarction, we performed a subgroup analysis in patients without evidence of ischaemic LGE (see Supplementary data online, Table S3), which confirmed significant differences regarding SMBF values between the ‘severe stenosis’ and CTO group also in the absence of LGE. The findings regarding MPR differed numerically between the groups as well; however, this was statistically not significant anymore, which was most likely due to the smaller subpopulation.

SMBF and MPR were significantly lower in patients with CTO compared with patients with NOCAD or severe stenosis, respectively.

Based on these observations we sought to assess whether quantitative S-CMR can accurately detect patients with OCAD. Diagnostic accuracy (AUC, LR+, LR−, PPV, and NPV) and optimal cut-off values for all three approaches are summarized in Table 2. Segment-based strategies provided slightly higher discriminatory ability compared with a vessel territory–based approach. Moreover, diagnostic performance was higher when using SMBF data compared with MPR. The optimal SMBF cut-off value for the prediction of OCAD using the ‘lowest segment’ approach in this study was <1.26 mL/g/min. Notably, optimal cut-off values varied between the different approaches (Table 2).

Can S-CMR discriminate between severe stenosis and CTO?

There were significant differences in quantitative S-CMR parameters between the ‘severe stenosis group’ and the ‘CTO group’, which were most pronounced for transmural SMBF. In contrast, no significant difference was observed regarding rest MBF values between these groups. Figure 3 depicts quantitative perfusion mapping, corresponding LGE images and coronary angiography of an exemplary patient with collateralized CTO of the right coronary artery (RCA) and full viability in the RCA territory. We sought to assess whether quantitative S-CMR has the potential to distinguish non-invasively between severe stenosis and CTO. Figure 4 depicts ROC curve analysis for the detection of CTO based on quantitative S-CMR among patients with OCAD. Of all quantitative parameters (rest MBF, SMBF, MPR; all endocardial, epicardial, and transmural sections), the best discriminatory ability was again observed for transmural SMBF and segment-based models provided higher AUC compared with the territorial approach (Table 3). The optimal SMBF cut-off value for the prediction of CTO in the ‘lowest segment’ analysis was <1.02 mL/g/min (Table 3).

Diagnostic performance and cut-off values of SMBF/MPR for the detection of CTO among patients with OCAD

Subgroup analyses revealed a significant impact of lesion location on diagnostic performance in the ‘severe stenosis group’: Sensitivity was higher for proximal stenoses (68.8%) compared with mid-vessel (55.6%) or distal stenoses (57.1%, P < 0.001). In contrast, lesion location in patients with CTO was not associated with its S-CMR-derived detection accuracy (sensitivities: proximal: 81.3%, mid: 68.8%, distal: 85.7%; P = 0.273). In CTO patients, instead, an association of the angiographic degree of collateralization (according to Rentrop scale) with SMBF and MPR values in the CTO territory was observed, which, however, was not significant (SMBF: P = 0.058 and MPR: P = 0.120, respectively). No significant difference in the diagnostic performance of quantitative S-CMR was observed between left anterior descending artery (LAD) and non-LAD lesions.

Next, we assessed the diagnostic performance of CTO prediction among patients with ‘same-territory LGE’, which was more prevalent in patients with CTO compared with those with severe stenosis. Thereby, we observed further improvement in diagnostic performance, e.g. AUC 0.828 vs. 0.703 in the ‘lowest segment’ model (see Supplementary data online, Table S1).

Improved detection of multi-vessel disease with quantitative S-CMR assessment

In the CTO group, 17 of 36 (47.2%) patients had an additional severe stenosis in another vessel. Of these, only seven (41.1%) patients had a matching perfusion defect on visual S-CMR reading. Of the 19 (52.7%) patients who did not demonstrate an additional stenosis in another vessel, 1 (5%) patient had a false positive visual assessment. In order to determine the diagnostic performance of quantitative S-CMR perfusion assessment in the setting of CTO and additional stenosis, we calculated the optimal SMBF threshold for the detection of severe stenosis, which was <1.51 mL/g/min using the ‘lowest segment’ model (AUC 0.703, LR+ 2.175, LR− 0.258, PPV 56.1%, NPV 86.3%; see Supplementary data online, Table S2). Applying this cut-off to the non-occluded vessels of patients with CTO, the ‘lowest segment’ assessment correctly identified 87.0% of additional severe stenoses with a specificity of 36.4%. The ‘vessel territory’ approach yielded lower sensitivity (69.6%) but slightly higher specificity (56.8%).

Considering the high specificity of the visual assessment and the high sensitivity of the quantitative assessment, we finally tested a combined approach where SMBF thresholds were applied to those patients without visual evidence of additional stenosis in a non-CTO vessel. This combined approach yielded the highest sensitivity of 91.3% (specificity 36.4%) for the detection of additional severe stenosis in non-CTO vessels (Figure 5).

Discussion

The present study characterizes S-CMR-derived quantitative myocardial perfusion in patients with different severities of CAD including CTO. The major findings of our study are as follows:

SMBF and MPR differed significantly between patients with NOCAD, severe stenosis, and CTO, respectively. We provide diagnostic accuracy metrics and optimal cut-off values for SMBF and MPR to predict OCAD.

Detection of severe stenosis

First of all, in line with previous studies,3 we observed significant differences in SMBF and MPR according to the severity of CAD (NOCAD vs. severe stenosis vs. CTO). In more detailed analysis, the inverse correlation of SMBF and MPR with CAD severity, respectively, was found both for sub-endocardial and sub-epicardial myocardial sections as well as in the global, territorial and segmental analysis. Based on ROC curve analysis, we identified SMBF and MPR cut-off values for the detection of severe stenosis. While our observations and diagnostic performance indices are overall in line with recent studies by Crawley et al.,3 Kotecha et al.,15 and Hoek et al.,16 our SMBF cut-off values determined by ROC curve analysis differ from theirs (‘lowest 2 segments’ model: SMBF 1.53 vs. 1.94 mL/g/min3 and MPR 2.01 vs. 1.903; ‘vessel territory’ model: SMBF 1.78 vs. 1.943,15 vs. 1.43 mL/g/min16 and MPR 2.53 vs. 1.9615 and 1.973). A somewhat older study by Biglands et al.17 reported a similar SMBF but a significantly lower MPR cut-off value (SMBF 1.27 mL/g/min and MPR 1.11, respectively). These inter-study differences may be explained by several methodological differences with known impact on SMBF and MPR, such as differences in the study population’s baseline characteristics (i.e. patient age, comorbidities and sex,18) procedural differences (i.e. magnetic field strength, contrast agent and dose, proportion of adenosine and non-adenosine scans),19 as well as differences regarding the severity of epicardial CAD (high prevalence of CTO in our study population) and presumably microvascular disease. We excluded patients with evidence of suboptimal hyperaemia according to current recommendations (i.e. heart rate response <15 bpm or SMBF <1.43 mL/g/min in all segments8) from our analysis in order minimize the impact of insufficient hyperaemia on the determination of cut-off values. Nevertheless, future larger studies are needed to assess the feasibility and usefulness of different SMBF and MPR cut-off values adjusted to the individual patient`s characteristics (age, sex, etc.).

Distinguishing CTO from severe stenosis by S-CMR

Besides patients with severe stenosis, the present study population comprised a considerably large number of patients with collateralized CTO, which allowed us to investigate the diagnostic performance of MBF quantification in this special subgroup. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that demonstrates the ability of S-CMR to non-invasively predict CTO among patients with positive S-CMR with high accuracy and provides SMBF and MPR cut-off values for this purpose.

Improved sensitivity in patients with CTO and multi-vessel disease

Multi-vessel disease with additional severe stenosis in a non-CTO vessel is a frequent scenario in patients with CTO.20 As previously reported, visual S-CMR reading in these patients can be particularly challenging since multiple perfusion defects may be perceived as a single one or may be missed completely in triple vessel disease with balanced ischaemia.7 Thus, we evaluated whether perfusion imaging quantification could help to overcome this limitation. Indeed, in the present CTO population additional severe stenoses were overlooked by visual S-CMR assessment in more than half of the patients. Applying a SMBF cut-off <1.51 mL/g/min (‘lowest segment’ approach) for severe stenosis as determined above, quantitative perfusion alone demonstrated a sensitivity of 87.0% in the detection of additional severe stenosis in patients with CTO. However, specificity was only modest (36.4%). This may be explained by presumably higher prevalence of coronary microvascular disease in patients with more severe CAD, as suggested by differences in the highest segmental SMBF and MPR values between the three groups in the present study (Table 1). Eventually, combining visual and quantitative perfusion assessment yielded the best diagnostic performance with a very good sensitivity (82.6%) underscoring the importance of integrating rather than competing traditional qualitative and automated/quantitative approaches.

Potential clinical implications

Optimal management of patients with complex CAD, including CTO and multi-vessel disease, is challenging. Accurate non-invasive characterization of the individual severity and complexity of CAD, including prediction of CTO, by quantitative S-CMR is not only of academic interest. It may have potential clinical implications for patient care as it could inform clinical decision-making. For example, in patients with high suspicion of CTO after S-CMR, additional non-invasive stratification (e.g. using CTCA) may be performed to reduce the need for diagnostic invasive angiography, as conservative management of CTO—particularly in asymptomatic patients—is a reasonable approach.21 On the other hand, in the current age of precision medicine and the recent advent of CT guided CTO PCI, a non-invasive diagnostic sequence of S-CMR followed by CTCA and finally planned CTO PCI is also conceivable. Our results further suggest an improved detection of true ischaemic burden in CTO patients with multi-vessel disease by quantitative S-CMR (Figure 6). In the setting of (guideline recommended) ischaemia-driven PCI, this may improve complete revascularization in these patients, which is known to be associated with improved outcome.22

Improved detection of ischaemic burden in a patient with CTO and three-vessel disease. Visual assessment of S-CMR perfusion images suggested two-vessel disease of the LAD and RCA, whereas quantitative SMBF assessment demonstrated impaired perfusion in the left circumflex artery (LCX) territory as well. Invasive angiography revealed severe three-vessel disease with collateralized CTO of the RCA and severe proximal stenoses of the LAD and the LCX, thereby confirming the quantitative S-CMR results.

Proposed algorithm for contemporary S-CMR perfusion reading

Based on the results of this analysis as well as our institutional CMR setup and protocol, we propose a novel diagnostic algorithm for contemporary S-CMR perfusion reading integrating quantitative and qualitative assessments (Figure 5, right panel). In summary, patients with visual induced perfusion defect in a coronary artery territory and a transmural SMBF ≤1.02 mL/g/min in at least one AHA segment are very likely to have a CTO of the supplying artery. A second induced perfusion defect with SMBF ≤1.51 mL/g/min in ≥1 segment indicates multi-vessel disease with CTO and concomitant severe stenosis. On the contrary, the presence of CTO is unlikely in patients with transmural SMBF values 1.02–1.51 mL/g/min throughout, and these patients most likely have severe stenosis in the presence of a regional perfusion defect matching a coronary territory. Finally, obstructive disease is unlikely in patients with SMBF >1.51 mL/g/min in all segments.

Limitations

There are some limitations that should be acknowledged: First, this was a hypothesis-generating single-centre study, and optimal cut-off values may be different in other centres and settings. Larger multi-centre studies are needed to confirm our observations as well as evaluate and further refine optimal cut-off values for quantitative S-CMR perfusion assessment. Second, stenosis severity was estimated by visual angiographic assessment, and only a minority of patients underwent intracoronary physiology measurements, precluding further analysis based on intracoronary physiology data. Ideally, these future studies should include different centres with different vendors and scan protocols in order to investigate the impact of different CMR set-ups and protocols on cut-off values. Third, although the average delay between S-CMR and ICA was <1 month in this study, potential progression of severe stenosis to CTO between S-CMR and ICA cannot be excluded in individual cases. Finally, some patients in this study underwent regadenoson stress, and as recently demonstrated,19 this could have had led to overestimation of rest MBF and thus underestimation of MPR. This could potentially explain the slightly higher diagnostic accuracy of SMBF, which is unaffected from residual hyperaemia in regadenoson S-CMR, compared with MPR for the detection of OCAD and CTO (Tables 2 and 3). Whether MPR will have similar accuracy as SMBF when the regadenoson effect is reversed (e.g. by aminophylline) remains to be determined by future studies.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate the impact of adding fully automated inline MBF quantification to routine visual S-CMR reading and provide SMBF, as well as MPR cut-off values for the detection of severe stenosis and CTO. Integrating quantitative assessment facilitates the non-invasive prediction of CTO with high accuracy and provides higher sensitivity regarding the detection of true ischaemic burden in patients with CTO and multi-vessel disease, thereby aiding clinical decision-making.
