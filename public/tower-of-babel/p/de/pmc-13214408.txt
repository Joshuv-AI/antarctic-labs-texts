Study protocol for the: HIV enhanced access testing in emergency department program using a systems analysis and improvement approach (HEATED-SAIA) cluster randomized trial

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Kenya, incident and undiagnosed HIV infections are concentrated among populations who are inadequately reached by existing HIV services. These difficult-to-reach populations include men, adolescents and young adults, people who engage in sex work, and people who use substances, populations which also frequently access unscheduled emergency care. Though Kenya’s guidelines recommend evaluation of HIV testing and prevention services (HTS) needs during outpatient emergency encounters, emergency departments have not been well leveraged for HIV testing and prevention programming.MethodsThis cluster randomized trial will evaluate the Systems Analysis and Improvement Approach (SAIA) as an implementation strategy to improve HIV screening, testing, and linkage to treatment and prevention from emergency care settings in the three counties in the Coast region of Kenya. SAIA is a multicomponent strategy for continuous care enhancement that empowers frontline healthcare personnel to improve services in their clinical settings. Ten public facilities will be cluster-randomized in a 1:1 ratio, with half exposed to the SAIA implementation strategy and the other half following usual procedures. The trial will be evaluated across Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance (RE-AIM) domains through quantitative systems-level data, qualitative insights from key stakeholders, and micro-costing data. Analyses will utilize Poisson regression and mixed-effect models to compare inter-arm HIV testing and prevention services (HTS) cascade outcomes. Framework matrix and coincidence analyses will be used to understand determinants of program success. Cost-effectiveness of the SAIA strategy will be assessed using incremental cost-effectiveness ratios and scale-up modeling.DiscussionEmergency care interactions represent an underutilized opportunity to deliver evidence-based HIV testing and prevention services to difficult-to-reach populations. This trial will robustly assess the potential of the SAIA implementation strategy to improve and expand HTS from emergency care settings, and generate key data relevant to program effectiveness, feasibility and scalability in the Kenya context to inform public health programming.Trial registrationThis study is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06747221). Registration date: December 18, 2024.Keywords: Emergency care, Africa, Kenya, HIV testing services, Systems analysis and improvement approach, Implementation science

• This trial will provide comprehensive implementation science data from Kenya to inform use of emergency care settings as strategic venues for HIV testing and prevention services to support programming for higher-risk persons with barriers to traditional HIV care engagement.

• This study will provide the first cost-effectiveness data from Africa for ED-based HIV testing services and for the SAIA implementation strategy as an approach to enhance them.

• The trial will advance understanding of the SAIA implementation strategy by providing its first evaluation in emergency care settings, where its adaptable design could be an impactful approach to systems improvement across dynamic emergency care contexts.

Background

Globally, approximately 40 million people are living with HIV (PLH), with most residing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1, 2]. New HIV infections are concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and among hard-to-reach populations [2–5] who often have higher-risk profiles for HIV, making them priority populations to receive HIV Testing Services (HTS) [2, 4, 6, 7]. Among hard-to-reach populations, certain men [4, 8], adolescents and young adults (AYAs) aged 15–24 years [7, 9], and persons from key populations (KPs; including men who have sex with men, people who inject drugs, and people who engage in sex work) [10, 11] are crucial groups that must be regularly engaged for HTS to achieve global control targets. In LMICs, emergency departments (EDs) provide unscheduled, as-needed care to large numbers of persons who often do not otherwise access health services [12, 13]. Studies from sub-Saharan Africa suggest that persons seeking emergency care have high HIV burdens, and desire ED-based HTS (ED-HTS) [14–17]. However, data from multiple African settings reveal that HIV testing during emergency care is infrequent, with fewer than 25% of patients engaged for ED-HTS [18–20]. EDs in LMICs, therefore, represent a strategic setting to deliver evidence-based HTS to difficult-to-reach and higher-risk persons for HIV who are already in contact with health facilities.

In Kenya, the HIV epidemic has persistent gaps in diagnosis, treatment and prevention: fewer than half of men reached for testing at appropriate frequencies, AYAs account for one third of new infections, and persons from KPs driving hyper-endemic transmission [4, 21–23]. Kenya’s national guidelines call for utilizing facility-based care to deliver HTS for difficult-to-reach populations with high HIV prevalence [22, 24]. While the guidelines include EDs as outpatient service delivery points (SDP), ED-HTS are not systematically provided in Kenya and evidence-based best practices to enhance delivery are still in development.

The HIV Enhanced Access Testing in Emergency Departments (HEATED) program in Nairobi, Kenya was developed to enhance delivery of HTS in the Kenyatta National Hospital ED, through locally derived systems initiatives [25]. The HEATED program was a pragmatic, multi-component intervention that employed setting-appropriate strategies focusing on training, infrastructure realignment, and stakeholder engagement and integration. The pilot program significantly improved the likelihood of ED-HTS for the overall ED patient population by 31%, and significantly increased testing for higher-risk populations by 67%. In addition, stakeholders perceived the program as acceptable, and adoption was supported [17, 26]. Although evaluation of the HEATED program improved ED-HIV testing, more robust understanding of optimal implementation strategies, impacts on linkage to HIV care, costing, and maintenance data are needed to inform ED-HTS models in Kenya.

The current study builds on the HEATED pilot program by utilizing and evaluating the Systems Analysis and Improvement Approach (SAIA) implementation strategy to enhance HTS delivery via a cluster randomized trial in public facilities in Kilifi, Mombasa, and Kwale Counties in Kenya (termed HEATED-SAIA) [27, 28]. SAIA is a multi-component implementation strategy that utilizes cascade analysis, process flow mapping, and micro-strategy development and testing in ‘plan-do-study-act’ cycles to improve service delivery [29]. SAIA implementation has previously been shown to improve HTS in maternal-child health clinics and family planning (FP) clinics in Africa [27, 28]. This study tests the HEATED-SAIA program’s impact in improving HIV screening, testing, and linkage to treatment and prevention, and seeks to inform public health programming in Kenya and other similar settings.

Methods

Study design

The cluster randomized trial will be conducted in EDs in the Coast region of Kenya to evaluate the impacts of the HEATED-SAIA program, compared to routinely delivered services, in improving HIV screening, testing, and linkage to treatment and prevention among persons presenting for emergency care. The trial sites will include ten public facilities (clusters) with EDs and on-site HTS capacities in Kilifi, Mombasa and Kwale Counties. The facilities will be randomized (1:1) to HEATED-SAIA or control (standard care). Outcomes will be evaluated across Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework domains utilizing systems-level quantitative data, participant-level qualitative data, and costing assessments [30, 31]. The primary effectiveness outcome will be HIV test completion among ED patients.

Study setting

The ten trial sites represent all public facilities in Kilifi, Mombasa, and Kwale counties with the clinical resources needed to deliver emergency care and HIV services required for trial involvement at time of protocol development. Emergency care is defined as unscheduled healthcare provided in outpatient clinical settings and encompasses care provided in accident and emergency units, casualty units, and outpatient departments. In Kenya, national policy requires that EDs must provide 24-hour care with appropriate medical and surgical resources to address emergency patients’ needs [32]. Geographic distribution of trial facilities

The facilities serve both urban and rural catchment areas with average monthly individual facility patient volumes ranging from 1175 to 17,100 per month. All study facilities have one or more HTS service delivery points accessible to patients seeking emergency care. All facilities also have HIV comprehensive care clinics (CCC) to provide antiretroviral therapy (ART) or pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) to eligible persons.

Community engagement

Personnel from each of the three county Departments of Health Services (DOHS) were engaged in protocol development and will provide a priori intention for collaboration of the DOHS and the facilities they oversee. To support clinical care development within each county, the Emergency Medicine Kenya Foundation (EMKF), a Kenyan non-governmental organization that is the national leader in emergency care systems and policy development, will serve as a collaborating partner for the research development and execution [33]. Before the trial is launched, engagement meetings will be held by research staff and DOHS collaborators together with administrative and clinical personnel from each facility, to provide an overview of procedures, ensure potential risks and benefits are understood, and address questions. Throughout the trial phases, interval feedback meetings will be held with the DOHS collaborating bodies to provide rapid dissemination of trial results and obtain feedback on best utilization in the research setting to align with best practices in community-centered research [34].

Pre-trial emergency care and HIV testing services register alignment

All study facilities maintain registers independently for ED and HTS encounters that capture individual-level information for each healthcare interaction. The ED registers, which are overseen by the County DOHS, will be revised, with input from the county DOHS, trial facility stakeholders, and EMKF collaborators, with the addition of three new fields: (1) Patient screened for HTS eligibility (2) Patient eligible for HTS, and (3) Patient referred for HTS. The addition of these fields will allow for aggregated assessment of facility-level data between the EDs and HTS SDPs across the care cascade, without requiring use of protected patient-level health information. DOHS collaborating personnel and EMKF collaborators will train clinical and administrative staff at each site on use of the aligned registers. All HTS registers will allow for identification of whether or not patients were referred for HIV care from the ED.

HIV testing services cascade metrics

The HEATED-SAIA program aims to improve HTS for ED patients at five points in the HIV care cascade (Fig. 2): (1) Screening and counseling for testing, (2) Delivering testing per national guidelines, (3) Identification of people with HIV (4) Linkage to HIV treatment, and (5) PrEP screening and referral for PrEP initiation. As Kenyan guidelines recommend all persons be screened for HTS needs during every facility-based care interaction [22], the National AIDS and STIs Control Programme (NASCOP) has developed standardized screening tools for HIV testing and PrEP eligibility used by healthcare facilities nationally [22, 35]. The recommended frequency of HIV testing is every 6–12 months for select higher-risk men, every 3–6 months for AYA, every 3 months for KPs, and every 2 years in the general population [22]. All persons identified as having HIV should be linked to care, and those testing negative should be screened and, where indicated, referred for PrEP services. Although the study will evaluate the HEATED-SAIA implementation strategy across all RE-AIM framework domains, HTS cascade metrics will comprise the effectiveness evaluation with HIV test completion as the primary outcome.

Randomization

Facilities will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio, by an independent statistician, to either the HEATED-SAIA program intervention or to standard care, with all facilities randomized at the same timepoint. To reduce imbalance and between-cluster variability, clusters will be stratified by county and monthly ED encounter volumes [36].

Intervention program

Following randomization, intervention-arm facilities will begin the HEATED-SAIA program implementation strategy via facility-level SAIA cycles. Figure 3 illustrates the SAIA process, which is a continuous five-step ‘plan-do-study-act’ cyclical implementation strategy used by healthcare teams to systematically identify and address system bottlenecks and develop and implement solutions in the form of micro-strategies to improve a healthcare delivery service [28, 29]. Micro-strategies are systems adaptations designed, by the clinical personnel that will use them, to address modifiable barriers to steps in care delivery. As they are setting-specific and stakeholder-derived, the micro-strategies are not pre-defined by the trial methodology or the SAIA implementation strategy.

Systems Analysis Improvement Approach Cycle Schematic

The HEATED-SAIA program cycle steps are summarized in Table 1. Following program initiation, the five-step cycles will be repeated every 4–6 weeks for continuous quality improvement at intervention-arm facilities. DOHS collaborators will be trained by research staff in the HEATED-SAIA program through interactive didactic trainings to become SAIA Trainers and Facilitators [27]. DOHS personnel will thereafter train a cadre of primary SAIA facilitators within each county from among personnel with relevant vocational roles and working relationships with the trial facilities. Subsequently, the primary SAIA facilitators will train staff from each of the intervention facilities, who will operate as the primary SAIA implementers. The SAIA facilitators will guide the SAIA implementers through HEATED-SAIA program cycles while also supporting implementers in mapping, developing, implementing, assessing and adopting micro-strategies.

Trial phases

Figure 4 illustrates the HEATED-SAIA cluster randomized trial phases. The projected time course of the trial is 45 months consisting of a baseline phase and randomization, intervention phase, and maintenance phase.

HEATED-SAIA cluster randomized trial phases. HEATED: HIV Enhanced Access Testing in Emergency Departments; SAIA: Systems Analysis Improvement Approach

Baseline & randomization

A three-month baseline phase will be completed during which the revised ED registers will be rolled out in all ten trial facilities, with onsite training at all ten trial facilities by research staff and county DOHS personnel. During the baseline phase, quality assurance assessments will be conducted at all randomized facilities to ensure correct and sufficient use of the revised registers, supported by the county DOHS. Additionally, during the baseline phase and prior to HEATED-SAIA randomization, data will be collected to characterize clinical infrastructure and processes, cost(s) of standard HTS provision, and local policies relevant to ED-HTS. At the end of the baseline phase, facilities will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio to the HEATED-SAIA program or the control arm of the trial.

Intervention phase

During the intervention phase, facilities randomized to the HEATED-SAIA program will initiate and undertake the cyclical implementation strategy with all cycles supported by county DOHS and research personnel. Facilities randomized to the control arm will receive regular clinical support and standard oversight by county DOHS personnel but no further trial-supported interventions. The intervention phase will last 18 months, during which systems-level data on HTS cascade metrics will be collected for all facilities. For facilities randomized to the intervention, HEATED-SAIA cycle characteristics, organizational metrics on climate, culture and behaviors, and costing data will be iteratively collected. Qualitative data on HEATED-SAIA program determinants will also be collected.

Maintenance phase

A 24-month maintenance phase will be completed to assess the HEATED-SAIA program implementation strategy’s long-term sustainability. During the maintenance phase, systems-level data on HTS cascade metrics will be collected for all facilities. For facilities randomized to the intervention arm, quantitative data on HEATED-SAIA cycles and costing data will be collected, together with qualitative data, to understand determinants and mechanisms of implementation strategy penetration in the absence of research staff support at facility and county levels.

Data collection & management

Quantitative data

Using the aligned ED and HTS registers, systems-level cascade metrics for HIV care will be assessed every three months by study staff throughout the baseline, intervention, and maintenance trial phases at all randomized facilities. These data will be collected using structured abstraction tools that will capture de-identified counts of emergency care encounters for HTS screening, HTS eligibility for testing, acceptance for referral for HTS, linkage to testing, testing completion, identification of HIV positive encounters, linkage to HIV treatment, and screening and referral for PrEP. These count data will be aggregated for all ED encounters per facility over each three-month sampling period. Aggregated data will be assessed overall and stratified based on sex and age for each sampling period. De-identified abstracted data will be entered into secure REDCap databases [37, 38] with a random sampling for double entry of 10% of records assessed to ensure accuracy with a minimal agreement concordance of 95%. Research staff will collect HEATED-SAIA program cycle data from clinical leads of emergency services and HIV services from each intervention site. These data will be oriented to each intervention facility based on the HEATED-SAIA program cycles completed and will longitudinally assess the numbers and roles of staff participating in cycles, as well as the micro-strategies identified, implemented, and adopted. Assessments will be completed with healthcare personnel using psychometric Likert-scale assessment tools which have been previously validated in the Kenyan context [17, 39]. The psychometric data will assess perceptions of organizational climate change commitment and professional behaviors over time [40, 41]. These data will be collected during the baseline and intervention phases of the trial. Purposive sampling will be performed to represent all vocational cadres of stakeholders relevant to the ED and HTS service delivery focus.

Qualitative data

To inform understanding of key facilitators and barriers as determinants of the HEATED-SAIA program implementation strategy, mechanisms of uptake (or non-uptake) and fidelity of micro-strategies, the Implementation Research Logic Model (IRLM) [42], will be used together with the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [43]. The IRLM is an approach used to organize and execute implementation projects to elucidate constructs and causal pathways within frameworks to improve transparency, rigor, and reproducibility in implementation research studies and has been used by the study team in prior SAIA evaluations [42]. Semi-structured interview guides will be developed, mapped to the CFIR, and center on determinants of (1) Outer Setting (2) Inner Setting (3) Individuals (4) Implementation Process (5) Intervention characteristics [43]. The interview guides will be piloted, with local stakeholders, for validity and to identify additional constructs not captured in the a priori specified domains as is recommended in use of CFIR in LMICs [44].

Qualitative data will be collected through in-depth interviews (IDIs) conducted with purposively selected adult participants (≥ 18 years) who provide informed consent. Participants will include (1) Primary SAIA facilitators (county DOHS personnel), (2) Primary SAIA implementers (ED and HTS facilities staff) (3) County DOHS administrative personnel and (4) patients who engaged in ED-HTS. Qualitative sampling will occur during the baseline, intervention, and maintenance phases. This sampling approach will support understanding of constructs in relation to early adoption and longitudinal determinants of the HEATED-SAIA program during implementation and maintenance phases. For all enrolled consenting participants, qualitative data collection will occur in private settings by trained study personnel fluent in both English and Kiswahili (Kenya’s primary languages). To reduce response bias, study personnel conducting the IDIs will be independent of the research staff involved in the HEATED-SAIA program intervention. Neutral, open-ended questions that avoid leading language will be used. Triangulating findings with other data sources will promote candid responses and strengthen the validity and internal consistency of qualitative inferences.

Data sessions will be audio recorded with qualitative data transcribed, translated into English, and back-translated into Kiswahili by certified translators. After each IDI session, moderators and investigators will review and address targeted and emergent data through debriefing sessions and identify agenda revision to improve identification of relevant constructs and enhance trial evaluation. A coding structure will be developed with parallel deductive and inductive approaches to capture a priori and emergent themes. Deductive codes will be derived from the IRLM-CFIR domains [43], and inductive codes will be derived from debriefing and conceptualizations that emerge as unique domains [45]. Using qualitative analysis software, transcripts will be independently coded by two trained coders; applied codes will be compared, and discrepancies adjudicated to establish agreed-upon master codes. Subsequently, coding will generally be completed by a single coder. Throughout, inter-coder agreement will be monitored by regular double coding by independent coders and if concordance falls < 85%, master codes will be reassessed and revised with as-needed booster training.

Costing data

Costing is imperative to implementation evaluations to inform program uptake and sustainability [46]. As Kenya has a decentralized healthcare system, in which counties allocate fixed resources for health programs based on their priorities and population needs, cost evaluations are of key importance [47]. In the trial, micro-costing will be used to derive cost data, using time and motion assessments, interviews with staff and budget managers, information on labor costs, and review of budget and accounting records. Personnel costs will be assessed using Time-Driven Activity-Based Costing (TDABC), estimating the time required to conduct each step of ED-HTS. TDABC is a process-based micro-costing methodology that identifies the resources allocated over the course of service provision and employs a time estimate for utilization of each resource to determine costs of services [48]. At each HTS cascade step, trained study personnel will complete direct time-and-motion facility observations using a digital tool to collect data on activities related to ED-HTS and time requirements [49]. Study personnel will indicate the duration of each activity to yield the total and median amount of time engaged per activity. Median time estimates will be multiplied by associated hourly salaries to estimate personnel costs per observed activity. To complete TDABC, an inventory of resources used to provide services at each step of ED-HTS will be created and expressed as a function of currency per time using the Capacity Cost Rate (CCR). The CCR provides accurate economic information of the cost of resources divided by the practical resource capacity [50]. All other non-personnel costs will be captured using a study-specific tracking tool, interviews with budget managers, and review of facility accounting records. Implementation costs will be collected from all facilities randomized to the HEATED-SAIA program, and will include the personnel time (e.g., time to complete the cascade analysis tools) and capital costs (e.g., printing checklists, internet time to coordinate meetings) associated with each of step of the SAIA cycle. Program Implementation costs will be tracked using a study-specific tracking tool similar to those used in prior research [27]. Overall program and unit costs per patient will be calculated as the sum of implementation and intervention costs.

Data on implementation and intervention costs will be collected from all sites (both intervention and control) once prior to randomization (baseline period) and during the intervention phase. HEATED-SAIA program implementation strategy costs will be evaluated during the intervention and maintenance phases. These data will include costs associated with pre-trial start-up, and program continuation during the trial intervention and maintenance phases. Micro-costing will capture personnel, transport, communication, capital, and overhead costs, as well as all costs for the HEATED-SAIA program implementation strategy.

Reach

For healthcare staff engagement, the proportion of personnel taking part in HEATED-SAIA program cycles over time will be assessed. Engagement will be defined as participating in ≥ 1 HEATED-SAIA cycles per quarter across intervention sites. Engagement of patients will be evaluated using the proportional rate of screened and eligible patients accepting referral to HIV testing for the overall ED encounters population and among men and AYA.

Effectiveness

Effectiveness will be evaluated longitudinally through HTS cascade metrics comparing proportions of individuals screened, referred, tested, linkage to treatment or PrEP between intervention and control facilities. HTS cascade metrics will be evaluated for the overall ED population and for subgroups of men, AYA and KPs.

Adoption

Qualitative data on willingness to initiate, and determinants of uptake of the HEATED-SAIA program will be evaluated across the study participant populations (Primary SAIA Facilitators, Primary SAIA Implementers & DOHS personnel). Qualitative data will further assess process implementation of specific micro-strategies along with determinants of success or lack of success among data from frontline stakeholder participants. Serial IDIs will be used to map determinants and mechanisms of adoption (or non-adoption) and factors pertinent to early or later adoption for the HEATED-SAIA program. Anonymized longitudinal operational change readiness and professional development data from Likert scale assessments will be used to assess intentions and motivations for ED-HTS over time.

Implementation

Fidelity of the implementation strategy will be defined by the delivery of HEATED-SAIA cycle micro-strategies following identification and continuation of those deemed appropriate for ≥ 6 months. Fidelity outcomes will be assessed using quantitative cycle evaluations from intervention facilities applying Carroll’s fidelity framework [51]. Feasibility of, facilitators, barriers to, and mechanisms to achieve micro-strategies from HEATED-SAIA program cycles will be assessed through qualitative data. Cost data collected during the intervention phase from facilities randomized to undergo HEATED-SAIA will inform the resources required to implement each stage of the SAIA cycle within the context of delivering ED-HTS.

Maintenance

The 24-month maintenance phase will assess the HEATED-SAIA program’s long-term penetration and sustainability. Successful sustainability will be defined as > 3 cycles per each 6-month period. Throughout the maintenance phase, the proportions of individuals screened, referred, tested, linked to treatment or prevention services between intervention and control facilities will be evaluated for sustainability of HTS as the evidence-based intervention. Quantitative HEATED-SAIA program cycle data and qualitative IDI data will be used to understand determinants and mechanisms of penetration of the implementation strategy in the absence of research staff support. Costing data during the maintenance phase from facilities randomized to undergo HEATED-SAIA will inform the resources required to implement the adopted micro-strategies in emergency departments facilitating HTS services.

Statistical analyses

Sample size

The trial is powered based on HIV testing completion as the primary study outcome. Data from prior research in Kenya found that ~ 20% of ED patients undergo HIV testing and that a 31% relative increase in testing was achieved with implementation of HTS supporting initiatives [17]. Using a 20% baseline, an expected 31% relative increase (Cohen’s H effect size of 0.26), an intra-class correlation (ICC) of 0.1, and an alpha of < 0.05, randomization of 5 clusters per arm with a cluster size of 350 observation encounters per three month cluster-period will have > 90% power to detect a ≥ 6% change in HIV testing [52, 53].

Quantitative analysis

For quantitative data, summary statistics will be calculated by arm and cluster. Prevalence rate ratios using Poisson regression will be used to compare inter-arm outcomes evaluating the impacts of the HEATED-SAIA program on the ED-HTS cascade (screening, referral, testing, identification of HIV disease and linkage to care) [36]. Given the range of average ED encounters per month and number of available clusters, mixed effects cluster-level analysis weighted using the inverse of the variance will be employed in modeling the random effect. This statistical approach reduces type-I error and is a validated method in analyses with limited clusters of variable size [54]. Analyses for temporal trends in HTS per cluster period will be performed using interaction terms with assessments of changes in slope for HTS cascade outcomes. If significant interactions are found, models will be adjusted appropriately. Analyses will be completed for the overall ED populations and pre-specified subgroups of men, AYA and KPs. Threshold levels for significance will be adjusted for multiples comparisons. Psychometric Likert-scale assessments will be compared using rank-sum tests for overall scores and by individual constructs with tests of trend of the serial assessments.

Qualitative & mixed methods analyses

Qualitative data will be evaluated using framework matrix analysis to identify and interpret thematic constructs from the IRLM-CFIR data cache on determinants of, and mechanisms by which, the overall HEATED-SAIA program and specific micro-strategies impact outcomes across RE-AIM domains [42, 43]. A thematic analysis with matrix approach will be oriented to data sources based on participant characteristics as well as trial phase. Using the longitudinal HEATED-SAIA cycle data, coincidence analysis (CNA) will be performed to identify combinations of micro-strategies that support successful delivery of ED-HTS. CNA uses cross-case modeling of Boolean algebra and logic to identify crucial set(s) of difference-making factors; both complex difference-making conditions (multiple conditions must appear together for the outcome) and equifinality (multiple pathways yield the outcome) [55, 56]. CNA in the trial will support understanding of specific combinations of micro-strategies (factors) that improve or hinder delivery of ED-HTS services. Subsequently, the CNA factors will be cross-analyzed with IRLM-CFIR qualitative data to inform determinants of feasibility, and the configuration of those determinants, among the cache of micro-strategies. These integrated analyses will be performed at completion of the intervention and maintenance phases to provide data-driven understanding of effective micro-strategy combinations that are contextually grounded in in the clinical settings to inform locally acceptable and feasible systems changes for ED-HTS.

Costing analysis

Total program costs for care will be calculated overall and by individual ED-HTS cascade steps of: (1) Screening; (2) Testing; (3) Identification of HIV disease; (4) Linkage to HIV treatment; and (5) Screening and referral for PrEP initiation. Analysis of TDABC data will aggregate CCR and time and motion data to yield respective total cost per step of ED-HTS overall and stratified by county and study arm. Overall program and unit costs per HTS encounter will be calculated. Costs for development, rollout and sustainment of the HEATED-SAIA program will be derived at facility and county levels. The cost-effectiveness of the HEATED-SAIA program will be estimated using differences in costs between intervention and control facilities divided by the differences in effects between the intervention and control facilities, with the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio expressed as the cost per additional HIV testing completed with the HEATED-SAIA program implementation strategy. Markov models will be parameterized to inform the medium-term cost-effectiveness and potential cost-savings of scaling up the program under difference scenarios in Kenya [57]. Cost and relevant RE-AIM (e.g., Reach, Adoption, Effectiveness, Maintenance) parameters will be varied systematically across plausible ranges in one-way sensitivity analyses. Those exhibiting the largest magnitudes of influence will be co-varied in multi-way sensitivity analyses [58]. A 5-year time horizon and 5% discount rate for costs and effects will be used to project the longitudinal cost and health impacts incurred and/or averted by the program [59]. Costs will be converted into constant US dollars (USD) using the Kenyan consumer price index and the international average exchange rate.

Discussion

Globally, EDs provide care to large numbers of persons that often are not engaged in traditional health services, many of whom are also persons at higher risk for HIV who must be engaged for recurrent testing and care to achieve global control targets [12, 18, 60]. Given the foundational data from Kenya and other countries in sub-Saharan Africa on ED-HTS showing high patient acceptability but low service delivery, emergency care interactions represent strategic pragmatic opportunities for evidence-based HTS to persons already in contact with health facilities [19, 20, 61]. This cluster-randomized controlled trial will evaluate the HEATED-SAIA program as an implementation strategy to improve services and reach populations in need of HIV care in three counties in Kenya. The study will provide robust data to inform programming and scalability in Kenya and other similar settings.

Although pilot implementation science research from Kenya demonstrated that ED-HTS can be improved and leveraged to increase testing for higher-risk persons and key populations for HIV care [17, 20, 26], data to inform the most effective and sustainable implementation strategies are needed to guide programming and best harness ED opportunities. Studies on HTS and SAIA from outside of the emergency care setting from Kenya and sub-Saharan Africa more broadly support the use of the SAIA implementation strategy in promoting stakeholder engagement and adapting care to enhance HIV services [27, 28, 62]. However, the dynamic and high-paced nature of emergency care makes it unique from prior clinical settings where SAIA has been applied and evaluated, and formal evaluation is necessary. A parallel cluster trial design is the most rigorous approach to assess the implementation strategy. The HEATED-SAIA program is a systems level intervention, and this trial design will allow evaluation of large numbers of encounters that could not be realistically evaluated in participant-level randomized trial which would be subject to cluster effects. Additionally, given the potential for changes in HIV programming over the assessment periods and risks for contamination, a step-wedge design would be less appropriate in achieving the study’s aims. With the study design and the integrated application of robust evaluation frameworks, as well as the innovative analytical approaches using embedded and explanatory sequential mixed methods and coincidence analysis, the trial will rigorously assess and provide comprehensive data to evaluate and inform future scaling of the HEATED-SAIA program.

This trial is designed and will be conducted with continuous community engagement procedures during each study phase to give feedback and ensure it is in line with broader healthcare agendas in the study setting. The study also importantly leverages local experts with common goals from both governmental bodies (DOHS) and nongovernmental agencies (EMKF), in line with WHO global health equity standards encouraging participatory approaches for ensuring the success of sustainable health interventions [63, 64]. The methodological approaches in the trial for costing evaluations and cost effectiveness, which are key considerations in the context of public health program development and scale-up, will support the applicability of the results [65]. In Kenya, where a decentralized healthcare system exists, this are vital data to inform resources allocation and support health priorities at the county level [47].

This HEATED-SAIA trial has the potential to advance evidence for the SAIA improvement approach, through its first time application in emergency care settings and through the long-term maintenance evaluation that will be completed. Although SAIA has a strong evidence-base in clinical care development, it has not been employed in emergency care venues. Establishing its impacts in the ED settings would support utilization for wide-ranging ED-based applications both with regard to HIV care and other emergency care services. Finally, the trial will provide one of the longest-term evaluations of SAIA’s impact, with the maintenance phase evaluation spanning 24 months. This will better develop understanding of SAIA’s sustainability among implementation strategies.

Findings from the HEATED-SAIA trial have the potential to improve delivery of evidence-based HTS by leveraging the continuous care access available through EDs, which have been underutilized in providing HIV services. The trial results will yield robust data across key implementation science domains and outcomes that can be used to further develop and scale programming to support progression towards global control targets by improving services for higher-risk persons that must be reached to go the ‘last mile’ in the HIV epidemic [66].
