Small-molecule chemical probes for the potential therapeutic targets in alcoholic liver diseases☆
Authors: Ashish Dogra, Feng Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Alcoholic liver disease (ALD) encompasses a range of conditions resulting from prolonged and excessive alcohol consumption, causing liver damage such as alcoholic fatty liver, inflammation, fibrosis, and cirrhosis. Alcohol consumption contributes to millions of deaths each year. So far, the effective treatments for ALD are limited. To date, the most effective treatment for ALD is still prevention by avoiding excessive alcohol consumption, and only few specialized medicines are in the market for the treatment of patients suffering from ALD. Small molecules targeting various pathways implicated in ALD pathogenesis can potentially be used for effective therapeutics development. In this review, we provide a concise overview of the latest research findings on potential therapeutic targets, specifically emphasizing small-molecule interventions for the treatment and prevention of ALD.

Introduction

Currently, there exists a significant global burden associated with alcohol use and abuse. In 2020, it was estimated that approximately 1.34 billion people worldwide consumed harmful amounts of alcohol, including 1.03 billion men and 312 million women.1 Alcohol use disorders were responsible for causing 1.78 million fatalities in 2020.1 Alcoholic liver disease (ALD) is a condition caused by excessive and prolonged alcohol consumption that leads to liver damage, including alcoholic fatty liver, alcoholic hepatitis, liver fibrosis, and cirrhosis.2, 3, 4, 5 In individuals with chronic, excessive alcohol consumption, fatty liver is observed in approximately 90%–95% of cases.4 Fatty liver leads to liver inflammation, known as steatohepatitis, and around 20%–40% of patients progress to liver fibrosis.4 Liver fibrosis can result in cirrhosis in 8%–20% of patients.4 Every year, alcohol consumption contributes to millions of deaths.4,5 ALD diagnosis at early stages is crucial to curtail the progression of liver fibrosis as well as control the complications related to cirrhosis. Regrettably, ALD is frequently diagnosed at a later stage of the disease with decompensated cirrhosis.

ALD progression can be influenced by various factors that pose a risk. These factors include genetic variations, obesity, female sex, dietary choices, smoking, microbiota as well as viral hepatitis and human immunodeficiency virus.6,7 So far, the effective treatments for ALD are limited and the treatment strategy also depends on the severity of the disease. Alcohol abstinence combined with lifestyle changes, the use of supplements (e.g., vitamin and mineral supplements), medication (e.g., corticosteroids for liver inflammation), and even liver transplantation in severe cases are recommended options for ALD treatment. To date, the most effective treatment for ALD is still prevention by avoiding excessive alcohol consumption and only few specialized medicines are in the market for the treatment of patients suffering from ALD.8, 9, 10, 11 As such, there is a clinical demand for the development of effective and improved treatments for ALD. Advancements in comprehending the molecular biology of ALD have revealed targetable disease mechanisms for drug therapies.12,13 Small molecules targeting various pathways implicated in ALD pathogenesis can potentially be used for therapeutic development.12,13 In this review, we provide a concise summary of the latest research findings on potential therapeutic targets, emphasizing small molecule interventions in treating and preventing ALD.1

Pathogenesis of ALD

Understanding the mechanisms of ALD is challenging due to its complex nature, which involves intricate interactions among genetics, epigenetics, environment, microbiota, and molecular pathways.6,14,15 ALD presents in various forms such as alcoholic fatty liver, hepatitis, liver fibrosis, and alcoholic cirrhosis, each characterized by distinct molecular pathways, further adding to the complexity of ALD mechanisms.5,12, 13, 14,16 ALD is marked by oxidative stress, disruption of hepatocyte metabolism, liver inflammation, alterations in the regeneration process, and the transfer of bacterial products (e.g., lipopolysaccharide (LPS)) from the gut microbiota into the bloodstream via the portal vein.3,15

Alcohol is metabolized to acetaldehyde by alcohol dehydrogenase and cytochrome P450 2E1 (CYP2E1) in hepatocytes (Fig. 1).16, 17, 18 Acetaldehyde is extremely toxic and carcinogenic, which binds to proteins, leading to structural alterations and functional impairment (for example, of mitochondria).19 Alcohol consumption can induce oxidative stress by triggering the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), mainly hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and superoxide anion (O2−).4,20,21 The primary origins of ROS are the mitochondria (via the respiratory chain), the endoplasmic reticulum (ER, via CYP2E1), and Kupffer cells (via NADPH oxidase).22 ROS can induce lipid peroxidation with the production of 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA), which could modify DNA bases to form carcinogenic exocyclic etheno-DNA adducts.23,24 ROS also inhibit the anti-oxidant genes like glutathione (GSH), catalase (CAT), alpha-glutathione S-transferase (α-GST), and microsomal glutathione transferase 1 (MGST1). Chronic alcohol consumption initially develops the fatty liver, characterized by the accumulation of lipids (such as triglycerides, phospholipids, and cholesterol esters) within hepatocytes. Alcohol induces the fatty liver by affecting lipid metabolism through various mechanisms (Fig. 1): (1) Increasing fatty acid (FA) synthesis by upregulating sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c) and related lipogenic genes. Alcohol activates SREBP-1c through factors such as elevated LPS, ER stress, NADH/NAD+ imbalance, or inhibition of adiponectin, NAD-dependent protein deacetylase sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), and H3K9 methylation (trimethylation of lysine 9 of histone 3).5,20,21,26,27 Upregulating SREBP-1c enhances the FA synthesis by activating lipogenic enzymes FA synthase (FAS) and stearyl CoA desaturase (SCD). (2) Alcohol promotes FA accumulation by inhibiting 5ʹ-adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK), subsequently increasing the activity of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC).3,16,21 ACC catalyses the synthesis of malonyl-CoA, which is the substrate for FA synthesis. However, it inhibits the activity of liver isoform (CPT1L) and carnitine O-palmitoyltransferase 1, which mediates the rate-limiting step of FA oxidation. Inhibiting AMPK could also upregulate SREBP-1c. (3) Alcohol inhibits FA beta-oxidation via the inactivation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-α (PPAR-α) and downstream β-oxidation genes via the modulation of several factors.16,20,28, 29, 30 (4) Furthermore, alcohol impacts the mobilization of FAs and lipids from adipose tissue and the intestine to the liver, in addition to its effect on lipid metabolism.30, 31, 32 Additionally, autophagy plays a crucial role in eliminating lipid droplets in hepatocytes, and sustained alcohol consumption hinders autophagy, leading to a decrease in lipid clearance.32, 33, 34

If alcohol intake is not ceased, alcoholic fatty liver carries a high risk of progressing to inflammation, which is a pivotal factor in the advancement towards fibrosis, and cirrhosis.14 Alcohol consumption can increase a release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs, e.g., mitochondrial DNA) and also induces gut microbiota overgrowth and dysbiosis, leading to the elevation of pathogen-associated molecular patterns ((PAMPs), e.g., LPS and bacterial DNA).35 Alcohol consumption upregulates a variety of factors that activate Kupffer cells, stellate cells, and hepatocytes. Activation of Kupffer cells leads to the production of oxidative stress and proinflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-1, and IL-17 (e.g., LPS interacts with Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) to activate the MyD88-independent (TRIF/IFN regulatory factors (IRF3)) signaling pathway).36 IL-17 stimulates hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) to produce chemokines, IL-8, and CXCL1, which might promote the recruitment of neutrophils,37 followed by induction of neutrophil infiltration.38 Alcohol exposure also decreases proteasome activity and elevates IL-8 expression in hepatocytes. Chronic alcohol consumption increases the expression of miRNA-155 in Kupffer cells, which contributes to increased LPS-triggered TNF production, hereby augmenting the activation of the alcohol-induced inflammatory cascade in the liver.39 Studies suggested miR-122 inhibition accelerates alcohol-induced liver injury, steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis.40 Alcohol uptake can activate adaptive immunity by ROS, which causes the formation of many protein-adducts that might serve as anti-gens in the adaptive immune response, resulting in the accumulation of T and B cells in the liver.41 Taken together, infiltration of neutrophil and T/B cells results in alcoholic liver inflammation. ALD is associated with infiltration of CD4 and CD8T cells in the liver (Fig. 1).

If patients suffering from alcoholic liver inflammation persistently engage in heavy alcohol consumption, they will develop liver fibrosis. Alcohol liver inflammation activates Kupffer cells, which triggers the production of numerous cytokines and growth factors that stimulate the activation of HSCs and contribute to the development of liver fibrosis.42,43 Furthermore, the acetaldehyde resulting from alcohol can directly activate HSCs.44 Additionally, natural killer (NK) cells can directly eliminate the activated HSCs and generate interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), which promotes HSC death and cell cycle arrest.45 However, excessive alcohol consumption diminishes the anti-fibrotic capabilities of NK cells.46 As fibrogenesis intensifies, it severely impacts the structure of the liver, eventually leading to cirrhosis when the fibrotic progression reaches an advanced stage.47 The concise mechanisms associated with ALD at various stages are depicted in Fig. 1.

Therapeutic target and management strategies for ALD

Although alcohol abstinence is the most effective approach for ALD management, it may not be practical clinically, such as alcohol addiction and high relapse potential.48 Hence, it is crucial to develop an effective therapeutic approach and individualized treatment plan that caters to the specific needs of each patient, ensuring the successful management of ALD.12,13 As discussed above, researchers have made significant advancements in understanding the pathogenesis of ALD and are actively investigating therapeutic targets. These efforts provide a promising pathway for the development of potent drugs to effectively address ALD. However, the inadequate impact of marketed drugs is a foremost challenge to conquer. Small molecules targeting different pathways in the liver can potentially be developed as therapeutics for the treatment of ALD. CYP2E1

CYP2E1 metabolizes alcohol and triggers ROS production, directly linked with the development of ALD.28,49,50 CYP2E1 is known to be upregulated by chronic alcohol consumption, leading to increased production of ROS and oxidative stress in the liver. Upregulation of CYP2E1 activity is closely linked with lipid peroxidation, collagen deposition, and ALD.49,51 Inhibition of CYP2E1 efficiently prevents alcohol-induced lipid peroxidation and decreases liver injury.51,52 Additionally, transgenic mice that overexpress CYP2E1 augmented the oxidative stress and fibrogenesis in tissues.50 Targeting CYP2E1 aims to reduce the production of toxic acetaldehyde and the subsequent oxidative stress associated with ALD. Small molecules that specifically inhibit CYP2E1 activity have been investigated. Chlormethiazole is a medication used primarily as a sedative and anti-convulsant, which shown to inhibit CYP2E1 activity, potentially reducing the generation of acetaldehyde and oxidative stress in ALD models.53 Silibinin, a flavonoid, may inhibit CYP2E1 activity, leading to reduced oxidative stress and liver injury in ALD models, which may be responsible for its hepatoprotective properties.54 The use of CYP2E1 inhibitors as therapeutic agents for ALD is still an active area of research, and further studies are needed to evaluate their safety and efficacy in human subjects.

TLR4

TLR4 plays a crucial role in initiating inflammatory responses by recognizing PAMPs and DAMPs.55 Current studies indicated LPS recognition by TLR4 on the sinusoidal epithelial cells and HSCs, promoted the progression of ALD.28,56 Modification of TLR4 inhibitors or inhibition of LPS-induced TNF-α production suggests a therapeutic target for ALD treatment. Activation of TLR4 in the liver by alcohol and its byproducts leads to the production of proinflammatory cytokines and exacerbates liver inflammation and injury in ALD. Small-molecule inhibitors targeting TLR4 have blocked TLR4 signaling and subsequently reduced the production of proinflammatory cytokines.57 TAK-242 and Eritoran are selective antagonists of the receptor TLR4, which have shown beneficial effects in preclinical models of ALD.58 Natural compound, curcumin, possesses TLR4 inhibitory properties and has been shown to inhibit TLR4 signaling and reduce liver inflammation in ALD models.59 More research is needed to determine the efficacy and safety of these approaches in human subjects.

TNF-α

TNF-α is a proinflammatory cytokine that plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of ALD, contributing to liver inflammation and injury. It has been recognized as a potential drug target for the treatment of ALD. Several approaches have been explored to target TNF-α in ALD treatment. Except for its monoclonal antibodies, small-molecule inhibitors and therapeutic agents targeting TNF-α biosynthesis or downstream signaling molecules have been investigated in preclinical models of ALD. Pentoxifylline is a phosphodiesterase inhibitor that can reduce TNF-α production, which has been studied in preclinical models and clinical trials for ALD.28 Pentoxifylline is used mainly to treat ALD patients that are unable to tolerate glucocorticoids.4,21 Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) is a bile acid derivative that has been shown to modulate TNF-α production and exert anti-inflammatory effects. It has been studied in preclinical models and clinical trials for ALD, showing potential in reducing liver injury and improving liver function.60 Resveratrol, a natural compound, has been shown to inhibit TNF-α production and attenuate liver injury in preclinical models of ALD.61 S-adenosyl-l-methionine (SAMe) possesses anti-oxidant activities by preserving the mitochondrial functions and thus downregulating TNF-α expression and inhibits ALD.52 In addition, clinical studies with SAMe should be required to further establish for ALD treatment with the help of well-intended trials.62,63 These agents have shown promise in preclinical models or clinical trials of ALD, further research is needed to evaluate their efficacy and safety in human subjects.

PPAR-α

It is a nuclear receptor that plays a crucial role in regulating lipid metabolism and inflammation, expressed in tissues with high rates of FA oxidation. Activation of PPAR-α can have beneficial effects on ALD. In alcoholic ailments, RXR downregulates the PPAR-α as well as the AMPK pathway in fatty liver conditions.28,52 Fibrates activate PPAR-α, leading to increased FA oxidation and reduced triglyceride synthesis. Fenofibrate and bezafibrate have shown promise in preclinical models of ALD by reducing hepatic inflammation, steatosis (fatty liver), and fibrosis.64 In addition, clofibrate and Wy14643 are the PPAR-α agonists that inhibit liver steatosis induced by alcohol via PPAR-α signaling.65 Currently, no specific PPAR-α-targeting drugs have been approved for ALD.

ASK1

Apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1) is a broadly articulated mediator of fibrosis, inflammation, and cell death. ASK1 is activated in response to oxidative stress, anti-oxidant therapies have been explored to target ASK1 indirectly. Several inhibitors have shown potential in preclinical studies to prevent ASK1-mediated signaling pathways, reduce inflammation, and attenuate liver injury in models of ALD. Selonsertib (also known as GS-4997) is an investigational small-molecule inhibitor of ASK1. It has demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models of ALD by reducing liver inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis.66 NQDI-1 is an ASK1 inhibitor that has shown potential in various disease models, including ALD.67 ASK1 expressed in different tissues and acts as an upstream regulator of c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and p38/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)-mediated diseases.68,69 Inhibition of p38/MAPK has shown promising results in reducing liver inflammation and fibrosis associated with ALD. SB203580 and BIRB 796 are p38/MAPK inhibitors that have been studied in various inflammatory diseases, showing a reduction in liver inflammation and fibrosis.70 It has shown beneficial effects in reducing liver injury and inflammation associated with ALD. Further research is necessary to evaluate their safety and efficacy in human patients.

CXC chemokines

CXC chemokine family members (IL-8, chemokine ligand (CXCL) 5, CXCL6, and CXCL10) were substantially upregulated in ALD.28,71,72 Chemokine receptor (CXCR) 3 is the common receptor of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 promote steatohepatitis and hence, the blockage of CXCR3 prevent the development of liver diseases.72 In ALD, CXC chemokines play a vital function in facilitating the attraction and stimulation of immune cells, specifically neutrophils, within the liver. In liver tissue, the levels of CXCL1, CXCL2, and CCL like CCL20 expression are elevated.2,28 Other CCL including CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CCL17, and CCL19 were involved in liver diseases.73 Out of these, CCL20 upregulated mostly in alcohol-related diseases.20 Other mediators like TNF-α, IL-1β, and LPS also induce CCL20 which plays an important role in adaptive and autoimmunity.10,20 Suppressing CCL20 may reduce the genes linked with LPS-induced inflammation and fibrosis. Furthermore, the elevated expression of CXCL5, CXCL6, and IL-8 are linked together with the ALD prognosis.3,5,10,71 Therefore, targeting CXC chemokines presents a potential therapeutic approach to mitigate liver inflammation and halt the progression of ALD. Several small-molecule inhibitors investigated as potential therapeutic agents targeting CXC chemokines in the context of ALD. Reparixin is a small-molecule inhibitor that specifically targets the CXCR2.74 SB225002 is another small-molecule inhibitor that selectively targets CXCR2. It has been shown to inhibit the recruitment of neutrophils and reduce liver inflammation in experimental models of ALD.75 AMD070 is a specific inhibitor of CXCR4, another CXCR that is implicated in ALD.76 By blocking CXCR4, AMD070 can potentially modulate the migration and activation of immune cells, leading to reduced liver inflammation in ALD. Besides, CXCL12 is the only ligand of CXCR4, implied in multiple pathological mechanisms in inflammation. Notably, inhibitors of CXCR4 or CXCL12 also play an important role in fibrosis-related diseases.72

Cannabinoid receptor

Endogenous cannabinoids (CBs) facilitate lipid metabolism as well as inflammatory reactions in the liver and contribute to ALD via CB-1 and CB-2 signaling pathways.28,77 Drugs antagonists to CB-1 as well as agonists to CB-2 may safeguard the liver injury induced by alcohol via different pathways.77 CB1 and CB2 have been explored as potential drug targets for ALD. AM251, a potent and selective CB1 receptor antagonist, has been widely used in preclinical research. AM251 has been utilized in studies investigating the role of CB1 receptors in liver inflammation and fibrosis.78 SR141716A acts as a CB1 receptor inverse agonist, which blocks CB1 receptor activity and has been explored in various research studies focusing on the effects of CB1 receptor modulation in liver disease.79 JD5037 is a selective CB1 receptor antagonist/inverse agonist and has been demonstrated to reduce hepatic inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis in experimental ALD.80 Currently, there are no well-established small-molecule drugs specifically developed as CB2 receptor antagonists or inverse agonists for the treatment of ALD. Dual CB1/CB2 receptor modulators have been also investigated as potential therapeutic options for ALD. Otenabant (CP-945, 598) is a dual CB1/CB2 receptor modulator that has been explored for its potential in ALD treatment. Otenabant has shown promise in preclinical studies for reducing liver inflammation and fibrosis.81 The development and utilization of dual CB1/CB2 receptor modulators for ALD are still at an early stage.

SCD1

SCD1 is an enzyme involved in the synthesis of monounsaturated FAs, including oleic acid. It has been implicated in various metabolic disorders, including ALD. The reduction of SCD1 levels alleviates liver fibrosis in experimental animals, demonstrating the potential of targeting SCD1 as a viable treatment approach for advanced liver damage.82,83 Several pharmacological agents targeting SCD1 have been investigated. A939572 is a selective small-molecule inhibitor of SCD1, which has shown effectiveness in reducing liver steatosis (fat accumulation) and inflammation in preclinical models of ALD.82 CAY10566 is another SCD1 inhibitor that has been evaluated in preclinical studies, which has shown beneficial effects in reducing liver injury and inflammation associated with ALD in preclinical studies.84

Liver X receptor

Liver X receptor (LXR) is a nuclear transcription factor that plays a critical role in regulating cholesterol and lipid metabolism, which has been identified as a potential drug target for ALD.85 LXR activation can help restore lipid homeostasis by promoting the efflux of cholesterol and triglycerides from hepatocytes, reducing hepatic steatosis (fatty liver). LXR also regulates inflammation and immune responses in the liver, which are key processes in ALD pathogenesis. Various synthetic LXR agonists have been developed and tested in preclinical studies for their potential therapeutic benefits in ALD. T0901317 is a synthetic LXR agonist and has shown promising results in reducing hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis in animal models.86 WAY-252623 is a selective LXR agonist that has been evaluated in preclinical models of liver disease.87 LXR-623 has been studied for its potential use in non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), a liver disease closely related to ALD.88 These LXR agonists have shown promising results in preclinical models, the translation of these LXR agonists to human clinical trials is still in the early stages of investigation.

cGAS-stimulator of IFN genes pathway

The cGAS-stimulator of IFN genes (STING) pathway is an important cellular signaling pathway involved in innate immune responses, particularly in the recognition of cytosolic DNA and activation of anti-viral and inflammatory responses.89,90 Chronic alcohol consumption can activate the cGAS-STING pathway, which may contribute to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IFNs, and exacerbate liver inflammation and injury.91 Small molecules targeting key components of the cGAS-STING pathway, such as cGAS or STING, are being investigated for their potential to inhibit pathway activation and subsequent inflammation, for example, RU.521 (a cGAS inhibitor) and C-176 (a STING inhibitor).89,92,93 However, specific small-molecule inhibitors for ALD are currently not available and require further study.

IFR3

Alcohol induces apoptosis by stimulating the ER stress and caspase-independent/dependent apoptotic pathways.5,94 Some findings also showed that the IRF3 pathway is a significant factor of ALD, linked ER stress together with apoptosis.94 Alcohol stimulates ER stress, initiating IRF3 binding to STING and resulting in IRF3 activation.5,95,96 Further, IRF3 activates Bax resulting in apoptosis. Deletion of STING inhibits apoptosis by preventing the alcohol-induced IRF3 phosphorylation and the absence of IRF3 prevents hepatocyte apoptosis.29,30,94 Hence, targeting the STING-IRF3 pathway is an important approach for treating patients with ALD. Petrasek et al.94 suggested that the STING-IRF3 pathway is an important factor of ALD, that links up ER stress and hepatocyte apoptosistargeting the IRF3 pathway has been proposed as a potential therapeutic approach for ALD.96, 97, 98 It has been reported that a small-molecule auranofin could induce autophagic degradation of IRF3 and inhibit its transcriptional and proapoptotic activities.99 In vitro, auranofin inhibits FA-induced apoptotic cell death of hepatocytes. The research on targeting the IRF3 pathway in ALD is still evolving.

MicroRNAs

MicroRNAs are biologically trimmers, articulated in liver tissues and different cells of gut. MicroRNAs contribute towards oxidative stress, inflammation, and lipid metabolism, and activate hematopoietic stem cells by controlling the transcription of target proteins.28,100 In the liver, the alcoholic ailments result in significant elevation of miR-132, miR-155, and miR-182 expressions, while miR122, miR-148a, and miR-203 were downregulated.28,50 Inhibitors of miRNA function or stabilization of specific miRNAs may be considered as the therapeutic targets for ALD treatment. Small molecules such as aurintricarboxylic acid (ATA) and NSC207895 have shown inhibitory effects on miRNA activity by directly binding to miRNAs and interfering with their function.101,102 On the other hand, a small-molecule arctigenin has been shown to enhance the stability of miRNAs.103 It's important to note that the development of small-molecule modulators specifically targeting miRNAs is still an active area of research, and there are no widely established small molecule-based therapies for miRNA modulation in ALD at present.

Except above targets, gut microbiota plays a crucial role in the development and progression of various diseases, including ALD. Studies have shown that alterations in the composition and function of the gut microbiota can contribute to liver inflammation and damage, which are key features of ALD. The modification of gut flora is deemed as an efficient strategy for ALD treatment.104 Antibiotics like rifaximin, ciprofloxacin, and amoxicillin, have been explored to manipulate the gut microbiota and improve liver health in ALD.105, 106, 107 Identifying specific microbiota strains that contribute to ALD and developing targeted strategies against them represents a promising avenue for the development of novel therapies for ALD. Farnesoid X receptor (FXR) agonists provide hepatoprotective effects by exerting anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidant effects and by regulating lipid and bile acid metabolism.108,109 A phase II randomized clinical trial using obeticholic acid, an FXR agonist, in patients with alcoholic hepatitis has been conducted.110 Nevertheless, the clinical trial was terminated because of hepatotoxicity associated with obeticholic acid. The development of effective and safe FXR agonists has a great potential to offer a new therapeutic approach for ALD. Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) is a bioactive sphingolipid, necessary for the migration of Th17 cells to the inflamed gut. S1P synthesis occurs in response to inflammatory stimuli. In vivo intervention blocking of S1P signaling markedly attenuated alcohol-induced liver inflammation, steatosis, and damage. Hence, S1P signaling was crucial in the pathogenesis of ALD in a Th17 cell-dependent manner. Thus, S1P may represent a unique targeted approach in the treatment of ALD.111

Conclusions

To summarize, we conducted a concise review on small molecules that target signaling pathways involved in the pathogenesis of ALD, which have been investigated in preclinical or clinical settings. These molecules have demonstrated beneficial effects in ALD treatment, making them potential medications for ALD. However, there are currently no potent small molecules available that target promising miRNA and nuclear transcription factors such as IRF3. It would be highly appealing to identify specific microbiota strains that contribute to ALD and develop targeted strategies against them. With the advancements in our understanding of the mechanisms underlying ALD and the emergence of new technologies for drug screening platforms (e.g., DNA-encoded chemistry library technology, artificial intelligence, and structure/ligand-based virtual screening) and new approaches for drug development (e.g., proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC), degradation of disease-causing proteins facilitated by small molecules), therapy focusing on small molecules holds great promise for the treatment of ALD. The limitation of this review doesn't delve into the specific challenges and complexities of developing effective treatments for ALD, such as molecular interactions and potential side effects which can impact the successful translation of these interventions from research to clinical practice.
