Ethnopharmacological study of medicinal plants used for breast cancer treatment among traditional herbalists and patients in Central and Western Cameroon
Authors: Constantin Chokofi Fongho, Alfloditte Flore Feudjio, Natacha Lena Yembeau, Paul Jean Assam Assam, Etienne Okobalemba Atenguena, Constant Anatole Pieme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe high diversity of ecology, climate, and floral of Cameroon constitutes a veritable reservoir of medicinal plants. The search for new, safe, effective, and inexpensive alternative therapies for the treatment of breast cancer is an urgent necessity in Africa. The aim of the study was to identify the medicinal plants recipes used by traditional herbalists to manage breast cancer diseases in four districts of the Central and Western region of Cameroon.MethodsWe conducted cross sectional, qualitative and descriptive research through an ethnopharmacological survey from August 2024 to February 2025 amongst 92 participants, including 21 traditional herbalists from four districts in the Central and Western regions, and 71 patients with breast cancer disease. Traditional herbalists were selected using the snowball. A semi-structured questionnaire was used to collect data on plant-based treatment supplemented with a comprehensive literature review on the phytochemicals and therapeutic properties of these plants.ResultsWe have identified 29 plants species belonging to 19 families with total of 23 recipes used by the herbalists. 74% of these recipes (17) were prepared with more than one plant. The whole plant (n = 6 ;26.1%) and the bark (n = 5 ;21.7%) were the most commonly plant parts used for the recipes while the maceration was the main preparation method (n = 10;43.5%). Water was the solvent frequently used (n = 18;78.3%) and all these remedies were administrated only per os (n = 23;100%) with a dosage ranging from one and half to four glasses per day for a duration of treatment from one to twelve months. Only 10% of breast cancer patients reported being completely satisfied, unlike traditional herbalists who claim that 50% of breast cancer patients who received their treatment were cured.In most of the remedies, there was anticancer plants used as main ingredient and an adjuvant. Part of Syzygium guineense (Willd) DC, Var Guineen constitute the most frequent main ingredient (n=3/RFC=0.13) of the recipes while the most represented adjuvant (n=8/RFC=0.35 ) was Allium sativum L. The highest mean informant consensus factor for breast cancer treatment was obtained for Allium Sativum L. (0.36).ConclusionThe study reveals a wide diversity of recipes for the treatment of breast cancer in the Cameroonian pharmacopoeia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-026-05446-8.

Introduction

One of the major challenges in sub-Saharan Africa remains the effective and sustainable treatment of chronic diseases. The treatment and monitoring of these diseases constitute a real additional socio-economic burden for Africans and population other developing countries [1]. Cancer is a malignant tumor resulting from an anarchic and uncontrolled proliferation of cells that invade and destroy the surrounding tissues [2]. Breast cancer is the most diagnosed and fatal cancer disease in women, accounting for 28% of all cancer cases and 20% of all cancer deaths in women worldwide [3]. In Cameroon, breast cancer remains a recurring public health challenge in the female population that affects more than 4,107 women, or 20.1% of new cases of cancer diagnosed [1, 4]. Chemotherapy drugs used for breast cancer treatment demonstrated severe sides effects in the liver, kidneys and are ineffective against metastatic breast cancer [5]. Alternatively, many natural substances are used to fight cancers, especially in underdeveloped countries where 80% of patients turn to herbal remedies [6]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), about 65–80% of the world’s population in developing countries depends essentially on traditional medicine for their primary health [7]. In Cameroon, enormous population of different areas are continuing to use different therapeutic recipes prescribed by traditional herbalists to treat different illness including breast cancer. Due to the ecological diversity, climate, floristic and population, knowledge on Cameroon flora varied and enormous plants with different actives molecules capable to treat breast cancer disease discover in different regions.

Therefore, ethnopharmacological surveys are needed in these different regions of Cameroon to identify medicinal plants used by healers to treat several human ailments such as tumors and cancer. Few studies have been conducted in other regions of Cameroon [8, 9]. Our study aims to investigate plants species used by traditional healers of some areas of to treat breast cancer.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in four districts in the Central (Yaoundé 1, Yaoundé 2, and Yaoundé 5) and Western (Baham) regions of Cameroon, as illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2. These areas, which differ geographically, culturally, and agroecologically, were chosen primarily because of previous reports indicating a high proportion of the population using traditional medicine, their ethnic and ecological diversity, and the presence of traditional healers claiming to be able to treat the disease. Patients with breast cancer being treated at the Yaoundé General Hospital were recruited.

Yaounde

Traditional medicine is deeply rooted in Yaoundé. More than 80% of households rely on it for their primary healthcare. In the Yaoundé 1, 2, and 5 districts, this practice is heavily based on herbal remedies (phytotherapy), used to treat numerous ailments. The political capital of Cameroon, Yaoundé is a true cultural mosaic. With more than 240 ethnic groups from across the country, the city is home to a highly cosmopolitan population, a mix of indigenous peoples, communities from all regions, and foreign populations. Yaoundé is located in the agro-ecological transition zone of the Guinean highlands (southern forested area). From a climatic and environmental perspective, the region is characterized by a Guinean-type equatorial climate with four seasons, marshy valleys, and ecologically sensitive areas (such as Mount Messa). This ecological and climatic diversity is the source of the rich medicinal flora in the Yaoundé 1,2, and 5 districts, which boasts a highly varied selection, estimated at over 800 species used in Cameroon. These urban and peri-urban districts concentrate significant resources from forest regrowth and marshy lowlands, although this diversity is threatened by rapid urbanization.

Baham

Traditional medicine in the Baham district of the Western Region draws upon a rich and diverse ancestral pharmacopoeia. Local traditional healers use barks, roots, and leaves to prepare natural remedies, targeting specific ailments such as malaria, typhoid fever, cancer, diabetes, and female infertility. The district boasts exceptional flora. Its environment, characterized by alternating wooded savannas, gallery forests, and agroforestry areas, supports a vast traditional pharmacopoeia, a high-altitude climate (with alternating dry and rainy seasons), and vegetation cover including gallery forests and species such as eucalyptus and cypress. The Baham district presents a cosmopolitan socio-cultural landscape where indigenous peoples and non-indigenous communities coexist.

Data collection

The field survey was conducted from August 2024 to February 2025. The respondents consisted of traditional herbalists recruited from the localities of Yaounde 1, Yaounde 2, Yaounde 5 and Baham, the breast cancer patients followed up at the Yaounde General Hospital. The selection of these localities was based on the diversity of the population, climate and floristic as well as the facilities that we have like facilitators and translators. This research used a qualitative descriptive approach with snowball sampling, as no directory of traditional herbalists treating breast cancer exists due to their scarcity and reluctance to share their knowledge for fear of damaging their reputation. In order to break down linguistic and cultural barriers, we contacted the leader/ president of traditional herbalist’s association in the central region as well as his homologue in the Baham chiefdom who served as a translator and facilitator for the recruitment of traditional herbalists. After recruiting the first traditional herbalist for our study, they recommended other herbalists with the desired characteristics. These herbalists, in turn, made further recommendations, and so on, until the list reached saturation. In total, we had seven rounds of recommendations. The final sample size was determined when data saturation was reached, meaning when new interviews no longer generated new participants for recruitment. Data collection was facilitated by semi-structured interviews, which were translated into Ghomola for traditional herbalists in Baham and Ewondo for traditional herbalists in Yaoundé who did not speak French by our facilitators. This questionnaire was submitted to the validation stage (face validity, content validity) by two experts in the field. The questionnaire included four groups items: sociodemographic data of the respondents, ethnobotany of the plants, ethnopharmacology of the different recipes, and other pathological treated by the plants concerned (Supplementary material). It included information on methods of preparing herbal recipes, the parts of the plants used, extraction techniques and solvents used, quantities of plants used, preservation methods, prescribed dosages and duration of treatments. This questionnaire was developed only for this study and this was followed by a pre-test were 15 participants was involved.

Traditional herbalists were included in the study based on their knowledge on breast cancer, their confidence and ability claimed to treat the mentioned disease and had given their consent. Were not included in the study, those who were unable to treats breast cancer. For breast cancer, patients confirmed diagnosed, under any conventional treatments or herbal medicine or not, who had given their informed consent were included in the study. The number of both herbalist and breast cancer patient were non probabilistic, non-exhaustive and they were recruited consecutively. The plant species identified were recorded in the National Herbarium of Cameroon, and their frequency of occurrence in the traditional herbal remedies was calculated.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

Our study combined 92 participants, including 21 (18%) traditional herbalists and 71 (77.1%) breast cancer patients with an average age of 47 ± 17 years. The average age of the traditional herbalists was 46 ± 8 years, while that of the breast cancer patients was 47 ± 14 years. The range age of the participants was between 25 and 75 years. Amongst traditional herbalists,14% (n = 3) of them were women. Nearly half of the breast cancer patients and traditional herbalists enrolled in the study had completed secondary school, while 17% of the breast cancer patients enrolled in the study had never been to school (Table 1). Medicinal plants identified and collected

During our ethnopharmacological investigation, we identified 29 plant species belonging to 19 families and 23 of them were used for recipes. These remedies were prepared with one, two or three different plants species. Some of the anticancer plants identified in our study served as the main ingredient while other was used as an adjuvant in the composition of the herbal recipes. Syzygium guineense (Willd) D.C Var guineen represented the most frequent main ingredient used in the recipes (n = 3/RFC = 0.13) and the most frequent adjuvant the recipes was Allium sativum L.(n = 8/RFC = 0.35 ). These two predominant plants species belong to the Myrtaceae and Alliaceae families, respectively (Table 2 and Fig. 3).

Evaluation of the medication by breast cancer patients and traditional herbalists

According to the results of the study, only 10% of breast cancer patients reported being completely satisfied after following a rigorous treatment prescribed by traditional herbalists for a period of more than 3 months. This satisfaction being reflected in the considerable regression of the tumor during their follow-up examination such as mammography, breast ultrasound, MRI and especially biopsy, while 90% expressed a slight improvement in their health (Fig. 8). We should note these patients combined both conventional treatment and natural medicine. In the contrary, traditional herbalists claim that 50% of breast cancer patients who received their treatment recovered and were cured of the disease, a claim verified by conventional examinations at hospitals (Fig. 9). However, it should be noted that of the 71 breast cancer patients identified, only 3 received only herbal therapy only; the other 68 received both herbal therapy and conventional treatment, including chemotherapy.

Discussion

Africans populations and Cameroonians hold enormous traditional knowledge and practices, on the use of medicinal plants and natural products for the treatment and management of various diseases including cancer. Most of this knowledge is transmitted, from generation to generation as heritage. We conducted an ethnopharmacological survey through an interviews on 92 participants, including 21 traditional herbalists and 71 breast cancer patients. The results revealed that age of all the participant ranged from 25 to 75 years. The average age of the patients with breast cancer was 47 ± 14 years, while that of the traditional herbalists was 46 ± 8 years. This finding is almost similar to results obtained by Ndamba who explored the histo-epidemiological profile of breast cancer in Cameroon and found the average age of 46 years [12]. Also, it corroborates other results which demonstrated that traditional medicine is generally practiced by adults, who tend to provide more reliable information [13]. Similar results were found in other studies in the northwest of Cameroon and Togo [14, 15].The fact that a large number of breast cancer patients (n = 12 ;17%) participating in the study had never been to school does not invalidate their answers, because a patient’s recollection of her treatment history or the evolution of her symptoms, is not influenced by a low level of education; however, a low level of education may increase dependence on traditional. These results were in contrast with 5% of illiterate healers obtain in Ghana [16]. However, nearly half of the breast cancer patients and traditional herbalists enrolled in the study had completed secondary school. In our study, we have identified 29 medicinal plant species belonging to 19 families that used to prepare 23 different remedies that are currently employed in the treatment and management of breast cancer in some districts in yaounde and Baham.

These preparations were mostly composed of three(n = 7;30%), single medicinal plant(n = 6;26%), combinations of two(n = 5;22%), four(n = 4;18%), or even five(n = 1;4%) different plants. Similar results were obtained in Algeria with remedies with three or four plants [17, 18]. These anticancer plants played a role, either as the main ingredient or as an adjuvant, in the composition of the identified herbal preparations. Syzygium guineense (Willd) D.C var. guineense was the mostly represented main ingredient, appearing 3(RFC = 0.13) in the preparations. In these remedies the most frequently represented adjuvant was Allium sativum L. present in 39% in the preparations. The consensus factor among informants regarding the treatment of breast cancer in these localities is less than 0.5 for most species, indicating a low homogeneity of ethnopharmacological knowledge of traditional herbalists on the treatment of breast cancer in these localities, they use a range of plant species in herbal recipes to overcome this pathology. In our study, both main ingredient and adjuvant are plants while in Algeria adjuvants include honey, olive oil, fat, vinegar, plant oil, sulfur and tar [19]. The main ingredient and adjuvant plants belong respectively Myrtaceae and Alliaceae families. This can be explained by the fact that the choice of plants used in the preparations by traditional herbalists in these areas are used either for the management of the cancerous tumor or other symptoms and diseases presented by the patient. By utilizing mixtures of plants, these traditional herbalists expected to obtain good and efficient results. This approach expected to have the synergistic effect of different bioactive components of the extracts of plants in the treatment of breast cancer. Polyherbal mixtures without precise dosage present a high risk of drug interactions with conventional chemotherapy, which may induce hepatotoxicity or neutralize oncological treatments. Several studies demonstrated in vitro and in vivo anticancer of these plants by used as single preparation [20, 21]. The whole plant(n = 6;26.1%) and bark bark (n = 5 ;21.7%) were the parts of the plants most used in therapeutic recipes followed by root(n = 2 ;7%) and stem(n = 1 ;3%). The preference for the used parts could be explained by availability, ease of harvesting [22, 23]. However, excessive harvesting of the whole plants over the long term can lead to the disappearance of some plant species and the local extinction of medicinal plants. The use of variable plant parts demonstrates the possibility to obtain a wide variety of biological molecules which justify the therapeutic action of plants [24]. The plants used and the methods of preparation are often closely guarded secretly by healers. Most of the recipes were prepared from plants a dried state (59%) with water as the extraction solvent either by infusion; maceration; pressing and decoction. The maceration in water, was the most predominant preparation methods probably because it allows the most active ingredients to be collected in addition to water being the most common extraction solvent [25, 26]. However, this results were different from other study in Uganda where herbal remedies were mainly made by decoction [27]. The oral administration was the only route used during the treatment of the disease this can be explained by nature of the remedies in which active ingredient soluble in the liquid is generally easily assimilated by this route [28, 29]. Also the internal localization of the tumor plays in favor of oral administration. The estimated dosage and duration of the treatment varied depending on the degree of the cancer diseases as well as the sex and age of the patient. The finding regarding administration routes corresponds to the finding made by others authors [30]. A literature review on the plants identified during our study demonstrated several biological activities on a broad spectrum of diseases including breast cancer. Moreover, these plants are riches in wide range of secondary metabolites with predominance of alkaloids, polyphenols, flavonoids [31]. The main phytochemicals of the mentioned plants included four of the five main phytochemical secondary metabolites such as alkaloids, polyphenols, steroids, terpenoids. Three of the phytochemical compounds—polyphenols, flavonoids, and terpenoids—possess anticancer properties described in the literature (Table 4), both in vitro and in vivo, against breast cancer and other types of cancer. Our results revealed a considerable discrepancy between the statements of traditional herbalists and breast cancer patients regarding the effectiveness of prescribed traditional plants. Indeed, 10% of patients reported total satisfaction after following the herbal treatment, while the herbalists claimed that 50% of patients achieved a complete cure after taking their herbal remedies. This difference can be explained either by the fact that traditional healers overestimated the effectiveness of the breast cancer remedies prescribed to patients to preserve their reputation, or simply by a poor medical interpretation or knowledge on their part, confusing total cure with short-term symptomatic relief (pain reduction) without real tumor regression. In some cases, the measurement of remedies is rough, lack precision and dosage given to the patient has no strict specification. This approximate approach to establish the strict dosage of remedies to patients may lead to dosage errors which can interact with the curative effects. The lack of regulated and scientifically precise dosage and preservation methods are the disadvantages of treatment with medicinal plants in the traditional pharmacopoeia [32, 33]. The anticancer and chemopreventive properties of plant metabolites are increasing interest researchers however, their efficiency, effectivity and low toxicity need to be proven by clinical trials. Although our work has certain limitations related to parameters such as small sample sizes, limited coverage of the survey area (one of two regions), potential selection or recall biases related to the memory of traditional herbalists, and the absence of clinical verification, phytochemical analysis, and in vitro and in vivo anticancer testing, this work was conducted to provide an useful database on plants used by healers in the central and western regions of Cameroon, with a view to subsequent in vitro, in vivo, and clinical studies.

Conclusion

We identified 29 medicinal plant species across 19 families, yielding 23 medicinal plant recipes for breast cancer. Most these remedies were made of mixture of several dried plant materials prepared with water through maceration. These plants exhibited a range of pharmacological effect, rich in secondary metabolites, particularly flavonoids, which several bioactivities have been demonstrated according to literature review. These findings provide a useful data for future pharmacological studies to assess the therapeutic effectiveness and safety of these promising medicinal plants.
