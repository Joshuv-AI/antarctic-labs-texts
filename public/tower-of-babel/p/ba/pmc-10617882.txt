Hepatitis is still a major health concern in Pakistan: short communication
Authors: Sidhant Ochani, Khushi Ochani, Amrita Ochani, Laiba Imran Vohra, Simran Ochani, Md. Al Hasibuzzaman, Kaleem Ullah
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Hepatitis is often called a ‘silent killer’ as most patients remain asymptomatic and hence remain unaware of their illness. They are either diagnosed incidentally or get symptomatic when their illness advances years after getting the infection. In Pakistan, transmission has increased due to ignorance of sterilization techniques, sharing personal items, and unsafe healthcare practices and the burden of liver cancers and transplants has risen three-fold during the past 20 years. Chronic hepatitis often goes undetected, leading to a rise in liver cancers and transplants. Pakistan bears the second-largest burden of hepatitis C globally, with a nationwide prevalence of 4.8%. From 2015 to 2019, there was a 5% increase in hepatitis C-related deaths and an 8% increase in hepatitis B-related deaths. During the last 4 years, out of the 110 million population, only 281 578 individuals were screened for hepatitis B and C, and 1 634 614 individuals were registered at various hepatitis clinics. However, only 278 308 patients were treated. Despite a national vaccination strategy, coverage falls short, and regular screening is neglected. The burden of hepatitis-related morbidity is a considerable challenge for the Pakistani government and healthcare system, being a low-income country with limited health resources and limited access to treatment. Increased awareness, education, and emphasizing preventive measures, such as hepatitis B vaccination, is crucial. Careful supervision of healthcare workers and the promotion of safe practices are essential. Pakistan can learn and implement the Egypt model to combat hepatitis effectively. This article aims to discuss barriers and challenges and provides possible recommendations.

Viral hepatitis, including hepatitis B and C, is the leading cause of cirrhosis and liver cancer. The mode of transmission for both hepatitis B and C is similar. The disease is transmitted to newborns via vertical transmission. In developing countries like Pakistan, the culprit for the increased transmission of hepatitis B and C is ignorance of sterilization techniques secondary to unskilled and illegal healthcare practices, along with sharing of personal items such as toothbrushes and razors, unprotected sex, and unscreened transfusions of blood products. Also, non-healthy community exposure, including barbering, tattooing, and ear and nose piercing techniques, are responsible for increased transmission1.

Chronic hepatitis is often called a ‘silent killer’ as most patients remain asymptomatic and hence remain unaware of their illness. They are either diagnosed incidentally or get symptomatic when their illness advances years after getting the infection. In Pakistan, the burden of liver cancers and transplants has risen three-fold during the past 20 years2. According to the WHO report, hepatitis is the eighth highest cause of mortality. Pakistan bears the second-largest burden of hepatitis C globally, with a nationwide prevalence of 4.8%. From 2015 to 2019, there was a 5% increase in hepatitis C-related deaths and an 8% increase in hepatitis B-related deaths3. As of 2020, 4.55 million are suffering from hepatitis B, and 8.74 million are affected by hepatitis C4,5. A recent project report concerning the prevention of hepatitis displayed the hepatitis B and C disease burden. They reported that during the last 4 years in Punjab province, out of the 110 million population, only 281 578 individuals were screened for hepatitis B and C, and 1 634 614 individuals were registered at various hepatitis clinics. However, only 278 308 patients were treated6.

Despite a national strategy for hepatitis B immunization in Pakistan, in 2020, only 77% of newborns received the full three-dose hepatitis B vaccines, falling short of the WHO objective of at least 90% hepatitis B vaccination3. Despite adopting some preventive measures, the prevalence of hepatitis B and C and its related mortality has not decreased in Pakistan. One of the big reasons for this is the negligence of regular screening. Most hepatitis C screening and treatment services are offered at the tertiary care level by commercial and public health care providers. However, they are expensive and hence out of budget for the at-risk and marginalized populations that face the brunt of this illness7. As of the June 2022 report, the provincial hepatitis program catered to only 72 000 cases per year, where it needs to treat at least 200 000 annually8. The treatment and vaccination of hepatitis could be as high as $15 000 in developed countries like the U.S. However, amidst Pakistan’s economic crisis, the antivirals for hepatitis C treatment should be available at a cheaper rate, that is, up to US$20 per 3 months course. Similarly, hepatitis B vaccination can cost up to US$306. Hepatitis B vaccination is a significant step that shall have a big impact and drastically lower the disease burden, so it should be strongly encouraged9.

The burden of hepatitis-related morbidity is a considerable challenge for the Pakistani government and healthcare system. Pakistan is a low-income country with limited health resources. The healthcare system only receives 0.75% of Pakistan’s gross domestic product10. In this economic crisis, it is arduous to screen the public frequently. The prevalence of hepatitis can be reduced strategically by increasing awareness and educating people to opt for preventive measures and to participate in screening. Hepatitis B can be prevented with vaccines, while hepatitis C can be treated with new antivirals in 95% of cases11. For the eradication of viral hepatitis till 2030, the WHO recently planned the Global Health Sector Strategy. The main objectives of the Global Health Sector Strategy are to decrease hepatitis mortality by 65% and its incidence by 90% by 2030. This project demands global political involvement, financial commitment, and assistance from the pharmaceutical, medical, and civil communities12.

Pakistan should learn and adopt the Egypt model of eliminating and combating hepatitis by enhancing local production of antiviral drugs, negotiating low prices on testing kits, making screening accessible to everyone, and collaborating between public, private, and non-profits to secure internal funding as well as external funding from the World Bank making sure access to free hepatitis treatment for all13.

Careful supervision of healthcare workers and hiring trained employees to ensure the use of sterilized disposable instruments and syringes will reduce the blood-borne spread. Furthermore, the government should initiate awareness campaigns and give full support. Also, collaboration with private and non-profit organizations can be helpful for funds provision. Moreover, highlighting the sexual transmission of hepatitis as conventional primary prevention can decrease the prevalence of hepatitis in Pakistan. In this endemic era where hepatitis is rising expeditiously, Pakistan’s healthcare system and government should take multiple initiatives to control the disease.
