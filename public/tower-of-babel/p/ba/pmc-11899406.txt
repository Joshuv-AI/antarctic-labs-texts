Clinical usability of deep learning-based saliency maps for occlusion myocardial infarction identification from the prehospital 12-Lead electrocardiogram
Authors: Nathan T. Riek, Tanmay A. Gokhale, Christian Martin-Gill, Karina Kraevsky-Philips, Jessica K. Zègre-Hemsey, Samir Saba, Clifton W. Callaway, Murat Akcakaya, Salah S. Al-Zaiti
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Deep learning (DL) models offer improved performance in electrocardiogram (ECG)-based classification over rule-based methods. However, for widespread adoption by clinicians, explainability methods, like saliency maps, are essential.Methods:On a subset of 100 ECGs from patients with chest pain, we generated saliency maps using a previously validated convolutional neural network for occlusion myocardial infarction (OMI) classification. Three clinicians reviewed ECG-saliency map dyads, first assessing the likelihood of OMI from standard ECGs and then evaluating clinical relevance and helpfulness of the saliency maps, as well as their confidence in the model's predictions. Questions were answered on a Likert scale ranging from +3 (most useful/relevant) to −3 (least useful/relevant).Results:The adjudicated accuracy of the three clinicians matched the DL model when considering area under the receiver operating characteristics curve (AUC) and F1 score (AUC 0.855 vs. 0.872, F1 score = 0.789 vs. 0.747). On average, clinicians found saliency maps slightly clinically relevant (0.96 ± 0.92) and slightly helpful (0.66 ± 0.98) in identifying or ruling out OMI but had higher confidence in the model's predictions (1.71 ± 0.56). Clinicians noted that leads I and aVL were often emphasized, even when obvious ST changes were present in other leads.Conclusion:In this clinical usability study, clinicians deemed saliency maps somewhat helpful in enhancing explainability of DL-based ECG models. The spatial convolutional layers across the 12 leads in these models appear to contribute to the discrepancy between ECG segments considered most relevant by clinicians and segments that drove DL model predictions.

Introduction

Deep learning (DL) models are popular artificial intelligence (AI) models due to their ability to capture more complex nonlinear relationships and improve classification performance [1,2]. However, the underlying architectures of DL models can be complex, requiring additional methods of explainability, especially in healthcare settings that typically rely on easily interpretable rule-based methods [1,2]. Accordingly, development of AI systems for electrocardiogram (ECG) classification tasks must also consider the clinical end users. For these systems to be adopted into clinical practice, clinicians must trust and understand their decisions [1,3-5]. Thus, explainability must be improved both globally (i.e., how the overall model works, confirming learning from relevant information from training data) and locally (i.e., how model reaches prediction for a specific patient, serving as a potential decision support tool) [1].

To solve this problem, the field of explainable AI (XAI) has focused on enhancing the explainability of DL while leveraging the higher performance of these large models [1,2,5]. One XAI method, saliency maps, has been widely used to enhance the explainability of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [1,6,7]. These saliency maps identify the most important, or salient, datapoints in the input to the model that help the model reach its prediction [8]. For binary classification, the most salient datapoints are the points that push the model prediction most towards either the negative or positive classification.

In our previous work, we developed ECG-SMART-NET, a CNN for the identification of occlusion myocardial infarction (OMI) from a prehospital 12-lead ECG median beat [9,10]. We used saliency maps and found the ST-T segment was the most informative region for the model to predict OMI, which is consistent with clinical knowledge. This step was important to confirm that the model learned clinically important information during training, improving its global explainability. However, the model's local explainability and clinical usefulness were unknown. To judge the clinical usefulness of saliency maps, previous works have leveraged clinician feedback on saliency maps from their test sets [6,7,11]. Inspired by these works, we leveraged survey data from three clinicians to assess the clinical utility and local explainability of ECG-SMART-NET's saliency maps for individual ECGs. The purpose of this study was to identify if ECG-based saliency maps could help increase clinicians' trust in a DL model for OMI identification.

Materials and methods

The dataset in this work contained prehospital ECGs from patients with a chief complaint of non-traumatic chest pain. These data were collected from hospital systems in Pittsburgh, PA and Mecklenburg County, NC. A total of 10,893 ECGs were recorded from 7297 unique patients with binary OMI labels [10]. The dataset and preprocessing are described in more detail in our previous work [9]. Briefly, ECG-SMART-NET was trained on 8691 ECGs and validated on 1117 median beat ECGs [9]. In the test set, there were 1085 median beat ECGs from 730 unique patients. To make OMI adjudication of the model clinically fair, we kept only the first ECG from each of the 730 patients. A decision threshold of 0.57 was selected to maximize the F1 score on the validation set [9]. On the test set (730 ECGs), the model achieved an accuracy (Acc) of 0.964, sensitivity (Sens) of 0.750, specificity (Spec) of 0.982, positive predictive value (PPV) of 0.778, negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.979, F1 score of 0.764, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.930, and average precision (AP) score of 0.832. This corresponded to 662 true negatives, 12 false positives, 14 false negatives, and 42 true positives.

This project first created saliency maps, which were visual representations of the most influential parts of the 12-lead ECG waveform in ECG-SMART-NET. To generate saliency maps for all ECGs in the test, we used the gradient-based saliency class from the Captum Python library. This gradient-based method calculates the absolute gradient of the target output with respect to each of the input features. These gradients are calculated by backpropagating partial derivatives through the different model layers. The saliency map values were averaged into 40- millisecond windows as a post-processing step. For a visual impact, the saliency maps ranged from bright yellow to dark red – least to most contributory to driving the model decision. Percent contributions were provided below each lead name on the ECG and showed which leads were contributing the most to the model decision, with all lead contributions summing up to 100 % [9].

Next, we determined how informative the saliency maps were for the target end users of ECG-SMART-NET. Clinicians, the users, judged whether an ECG indicated that a patient should be sent for cardiac catheterization or should receive additional workup to confirm the presence of OMI. To gauge the local explainability of ECG-SMART-NET and the associated saliency maps, we created a Qualtrics survey for 100 ECGs. Fig. 1 shows an example set of survey questions for one patient. All questions were answered on a Likert scale ranging from +3 (most useful/relevant) to −3 (least useful/relevant). The first page of the survey displayed a median beat ECG in the standard three-by-four layout and requested an ECG over-read for the likelihood of OMI (Question 1). On the second page, the user was shown the colored saliency map ECG median beat in the same layout and asked to assess the clinical relevance of colored regions (Question 2) and whether the clinician felt these saliency colors were helpful in reading the ECG (Question 3). On the last page of the survey, the clinician was provided with the output of the model and asked to what extent they had confidence in this decision (Question 4). To ensure validity of the work, clinicians could not return to previous pages of the survey to change their annotations after they were unblinded to the model's predictions. Responses were collected from three clinicians with different backgrounds: a board-eligible cardiologist (T.A.G.), a board-certified emergency and EMS physician (C.M.G), and a clinical research scientist (S.S.A). The three clinicians had a combined 38 years of ECG interpretation experience.

Unlike the previous works that selected the true positives and true negatives with highest model confidence [6,7], we selected all 12 false positives (FP), 13 false negatives (FN), and randomly selected 37 true positives (TP) and 38 true negatives (TN) for a total of 100 cases. To mimic what occurs in clinical practice, we included false positives, false negatives, and any other low confidence correct predictions, exposing the clinicians to the shortcomings of the model.

OMI prediction performance of clinicians was compared to ECG-SMART-NET using accuracy (Acc), positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), sensitivity (Sens), specificity (Spec), area under the receiver operating characteristics curve (AUC), average precision (AP), and F1 score ([2*TP]/[2*TP + FP + FN]). The latter score is a harmonic mean of positive predictive value and sensitivity. Average precision is the area under the precision-recall curve. Pairwise Pearson's correlations were calculated between questions 2 [relevance], 3 [clinical usefulness], and 4 [confidence]. The objective of this statistical test was to identify if higher saliency map relevance correlated with greater confidence in ECG-SMART-NET's predictions of OMI or increased clinical usefulness. Due to inter-rater variability, clinician responses were aggregated, giving a sample size of 300 responses to each question.

Results

Table 1 summarizes accuracy (Acc), positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), sensitivity (Sens), specificity (Spec), F1 score, area under the receiver operating characteristics curve (AUC), and average precision (AP) on the 100 selected ECGs for ECG-SMART-NET and for each of the three clinicians. The threshold for clinicians was set so that all OMI predictions greater than zero on the Likert scale were labeled as OMI and all predictions less than zero were labeled as no OMI. All three clinicians and ECG-SMART-NET had roughly similar performance metrics, correctly labeling at least 75 out of the 100 ECGs with slight differences in false positive and false negative tradeoffs.

Fig. 2 shows four selected ECG examples from ECG-SMART-NET: a true positive ECG from a 76-year-old female with left anterior descending (LAD) and left circumflex (LCX) occlusions; a true negative ECG from a 68-year-old female; a false negative ECG from a 50-year-old male with LAD occlusion; and a false positive ECG from a 73-year-old male.

For each of the four questions within an ECG example, we calculated the mean clinician response and standard deviation across clinicians. These responses and standard deviations were then averaged across all 100 ECG examples. The responses and standard deviations were also averaged within ECG-SMART-NET's true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives separately. All results are summarized in Table 2. Clinicians were confident in the model decisions, even though on average, the saliency maps were found to be only slightly clinically relevant and slightly helpful in identifying/ruling out OMI.

We ran pairwise Pearson's correlations between questions 2 [relevance], 3 [clinical usefulness], and 4 [confidence]. The correlation between question 2 and 3 was significant (r = 0.851, p < 0.001). There were no significant correlations between question 2 and 4 (r = 0.071, p = 0.220) and between question 3 and 4 (r = 0.055, p = 0.338).

Lastly, at the completion of the survey, clinicians were each separately asked to summarize their main critique of saliency maps. There was a consensus among the group that the main distractor was seeing leads I and aVL often emphasized by the model, even when obvious ST changes were present in other leads.

Discussion

In this study, we sought to assess the clinical utility of saliency maps in improving the explainability of DL-based models for ECG decision support tools. On a representative ECG test subset, we demonstrated that ECG-SMART-NET performs on par with expert clinicians for OMI identification from prehospital ECGs and that clinicians felt confident in the model's predictions. Clinicians, however, found saliency maps only slightly relevant and slightly helpful in making clinical decisions. This perception was mainly driven by the discordance between which waveform regions the clinicians thought were most important in individual ECGs compared to what the model used, despite the comparable performance achieved by the latter.

To judge the clinical usefulness of saliency maps, previous works leveraged clinician feedback on saliency maps from their test sets. One work evaluated saliency maps on 8-lead, 10-s ECG data from a model that predicted sex, selecting fifteen saliency maps from male and female ECGs with the highest model certainty and to be assessed by two clinicians. [7]. This work found saliency maps to be unhelpful to clinicians in differentiating between a male and female ECG as no consistent patterns could be identified. Alternatively, another study gauged the clinical usefulness of a saliency map on single-lead ECG median beats from a model that predicted hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) [6,11]. Saliency maps were generated for 100 patients with an HCM diagnosis and high model certainty of HCM. One clinician identified the ST-T segment of the median beat was the most informative to HCM prediction on average for 100 patients, aligning with clinical expectations.

Similarly, in our previous work, we used the average saliency map from all patients with OMI to identify the ST-T segment as the most informative region for OMI prediction [9], which aligned with clinical findings and provided global explainability for our DL model. In this study, saliency maps were used to quantify local explainability on individual ECG cases. Although clinicians thought that saliency maps were only slightly clinically relevant and slightly helpful, it is important to note that what clinicians identify as informative regions of the ECG is driven by what is clinically taught. The value of explainability studies like ours is to uncover new areas of focus that may change clinical practice over time in addition to providing new mechanistic insights into the physiology of OMI. Thus, our results are promising and encourage future use of saliency maps to help clinicians understand what these high-performing models are seeing, which may inform how clinicians are taught to interpret ECGs, including which leads and which areas of the waveforms are most important and most relevant.

It is intriguing that clinicians in our study were most confident in the model's predictions in the true positive and true negative cases, and least confident in false positive cases. That is, clinicians could disregard the model's predictions if their over-read of the ECG suggested no OMI (i.e., clinicians were less prone to automation bias). However, the false negative class was even more intriguing, where clinicians were somewhat confident in model's predictions. This suggests that the DL model and the clinicians were missing the same cases. Further analysis of this class reveals that these ECGs had more interpretation confounders (e.g., LBBB) or were from initial recordings where significant STT changes only appeared in subsequent recordings not included in this subset of first available ECGs. Both factors are known challenges in clinical cardiology.

Our results suggest that there is room for improvement in optimizing saliency maps for clinical decision support. Clinicians in this study noted that lead I and aVL were highlighted as the most salient leads even when other leads had more obvious ST changes. Fig. 2, for instance, shows this pattern where Lead I and aVL are weighted the most in all four examples. This can be explained by the spatial convolutional layer in our DL model. While our model achieved better performance with the addition of this spatial convolutional layer, it resulted in weighting some leads more than others. That is, systematic patterns in our training dataset made our model learn that certain leads are more informative than others in identifying OMI. For example, our DL model seemed to pick up on the reciprocal ST-T changes in lead aVL as an important feature to boost the precision of the prediction. This can be dataset-specific given that ours, like many other datasets, had more LAD and RCA occlusions. Thus, the model was screening for presence or absence of these reciprocal changes in lead aVL in every ECG, consistently highlighting the ST-T segment in this lead in most cases. This artificial reasoning seemed to distract clinicians especially when primary ST-T changes were present in other leads or when the ECG seemed largely normal. Better understanding of these contributions to the model may help clinicians understand the importance of both primary (ST elevation) and confirmatory findings (reciprocal changes) in optimizing the sensitivity and PPV for the classification outcome.

Interestingly, the saliency maps that the clinicians perceived to highlight relevant segments of the ECG were also perceived to be the most helpful in identifying/ruling out OMI. While intuitive, these perceptions were unexpectedly independent from clinicians' confidence about the model's predictions. In other words, clinicians seemed to be more confident in the decision of the model if they agreed with its predictions, regardless of whether the saliency maps were highlighting the same regions they felt were most relevant or useful. This very intriguing finding suggests that clinicians are willing to exchange some degree of the explainability of DL models if the predictions are reasonably accurate, which has important implications for the DL community.

This study had several limitations. First, only three clinicians participated in the survey. While using three reviewers to reach consensus is typical for the ascertainment of outcomes in clinical research, it may not be sufficient for evaluating clinical utility. Ideally, future studies should include a larger and more diverse group of clinicians with varying backgrounds and years of experience to improve the reliability of the clinical usability assessment. Additionally, the survey was conducted on 100 patient cases. While including more examples could have provided additional insights, it would have required significantly more time from the participating clinicians.

In summary, our clinical usability study demonstrated that DL models can achieve high classification accuracy, comparable to that of expert clinicians, and saliency maps can be an effective technique for improving the explainability of DL-based ECG models. On average, clinicians found these maps to be somewhat clinically relevant and helpful in guiding decision-making. However, the use of spatial convolutional layers across the 12 leads appears to contribute to discrepancies between the ECG segments the clinicians find most relevant and those that result in the highest DL model performance. Future work should explore additional explainability methods to reconcile these differences and enhance ECG models' interpretability.
