A prospective longitudinal interventional cohort study on maternal nutrition and pregnancy outcomes in urban Cameroon (CAMELIA): study protocol
Authors: Sylvain Zemsi, Diane Ekwoge, Brice Saha, Valdes Kalla, Azalée Minkam, Doris Ayeni, Michel Tsanga, Jean Arnaud Ndi, Arnold Powo, Thierry Baban, Magellan Guewo, Jean Claude Katte, Xavier Ondobo, Joelle Sobngwi-Tambekou, Leopold Fezeu, Eugene Sobngwi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal nutritional exposures during pregnancy are critical determinants of maternal and neonatal outcomes, yet remain inadequately characterized in many African settings, including Cameroon. The CAMELIA (Cameroon Maternal and Early Life Assessment) cohort aims to evaluate maternal nutritional status early in pregnancy, implement a structured nutritional intervention, and assess its impact on maternal, neonatal, and long-term metabolic outcomes.MethodsCAMELIA is a prospective, longitudinal interventional cohort enrolling 1,200 pregnant women at less than 22 weeks’ gestation in an urban primary healthcare facility in Cameroon (Hôpital Monseigneur Jean Zoa, Yaoundé). Participants are recruited at the first antenatal visit and followed through delivery and up to 42 days postpartum. Data and biological samples are collected across four visits: at enrolment (< 22 weeks), between 24 and 32 weeks of gestation, at delivery, and at 42 days postpartum. A structured multi-modal nutritional intervention (SUN-APP program) is initiated at enrolment, delivered through individual counselling, group education, monthly interactive workshops, and digital health support via weekly WhatsApp and SMS messages. Gestational hyperglycaemia is screened using fasting capillary blood glucose at V1 and V2, applying a threshold of ≥ 92 mg/dL. Primary outcomes are feasibility and acceptability of the intervention. Secondary outcomes include prevalence of gestational hyperglycaemia, hypertensive disorders, anaemia, low birth weight, and preterm birth. The study holds ethical approval from the Cameroon National Ethics Committee (CNERSH; approval no. 2025/04/1790/CE/CNERSH/SP) and is registered in the ISRCTN registry (ISRCTN13961105).ConclusionsThe CAMELIA study provides a scalable and replicable framework for embedding structured nutritional risk screening and targeted interventions into routine antenatal care in a low-resource African setting. Findings will inform strategies for the prevention of gestational metabolic disorders and adverse perinatal outcomes across generations.Trial registrationISRCTN13961105. Introduction

Malnutrition in pregnancy remains a global public health concern, with growing recognition of the double burden of malnutrition (DBM)—the coexistence of undernutrition and overnutrition—particularly in low- and middle-income countries [1, 2]. Both forms of malnutrition are associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, including gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders, low birth weight, and increased long-term risk of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) [3–5]. In line with the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) framework, early-life exposures during pregnancy play a critical role in shaping lifelong health trajectories, extending into childhood and adulthood [6].

In sub-Saharan Africa, and particularly in Cameroon, the ongoing epidemiological transition has resulted in a rising burden of NCDs alongside persistent undernutrition [7, 8]. Despite relatively high antenatal care attendance, maternal morbidity remains substantial, and health systems face challenges including limited data systems, weak referral pathways, and insufficient integration of NCD prevention into routine care [9, 10]. Furthermore, existing data on pregnancy outcomes and maternal exposures in the region are often fragmented, with a lack of robust longitudinal cohort studies capable of capturing the dynamic interplay between nutritional, metabolic, and environmental risk factors across pregnancy and into early childhood [11].

Although the World Health Organization recommends nutrition counselling as a core component of antenatal care, its implementation remains inconsistent and insufficiently tailored to individual needs in many low-resource settings [12]. Counselling on gestational weight gain, dietary diversity, and metabolic risk is rarely systematically integrated into routine care, limiting opportunities for early prevention of both undernutrition and overnutrition-related complications [13, 14]. In addition, environmental and lifestyle exposures, including alcohol use, tobacco consumption, and pesticide exposure, may further influence maternal metabolic health and fetal development, yet remain underexplored in this context [15].

In this context, establishing a pregnancy cohort in Cameroon provides a critical opportunity to generate high-quality evidence on maternal nutrition, metabolic health, and environmental exposures, while evaluating scalable interventions. The CAMELIA study is designed as a prospective cohort embedded within routine antenatal care services, incorporating a culturally adapted, multi-component nutritional education program. The study aims to: (i) characterize maternal nutritional and metabolic profiles during pregnancy; (ii) assess their associations with maternal, neonatal, and early childhood outcomes; and (iii) evaluate the implementation, uptake, and potential impact of a structured nutrition and lifestyle intervention. Findings from this study are expected to inform context-specific strategies for integrating NCD prevention into antenatal care and improving health outcomes across the life course.

Objectives

General objective

The CAMELIA study aims to assess the feasibility and acceptability of integrating a structured, multi-modal nutritional intervention into routine antenatal care, and to characterise its effects on maternal metabolic health, environmental exposures, and perinatal outcomes in an urban Cameroonian population.

Methods

Study design and setting

CAMELIA is an open prospective hospital-based longitudinal interventional cohort study, with an embedded single-arm quasi-experimental component. The study is conducted at the maternity unit of the Hôpital Monseigneur Jean Zoa of Nkolndongo, located in the Yaoundé IV health district in the Centre Region of Cameroon. This primary health facility was selected as it serves a densely populated, lower-to-middle-income urban community, offering affordable and socially accessible care, with approximately 4,000 deliveries managed annually. Since no concurrent control group is included, the evaluation of intervention effectiveness will use adherence tertiles as the internal comparison strategy (see Statistical Analysis).

The antenatal consultation is conducted by a gynaecologist or a midwife. It includes a full clinical evaluation, prescription of relevant laboratory tests, and scheduling of the next visit according to gestational age.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes

Feasibility is defined as: (1) a recruitment rate of ≥ 80% of eligible women approached; (2) a retention rate of ≥ 75% of enrolled participants at six weeks postpartum (V4); and (3) complete data capture for ≥ 85% of key variables at each study visit.

Acceptability is defined as: (1) ≥ 70% of enrolled participants attending at least three of the four scheduled study visits; and (2) ≥ 70% self-reported satisfaction with the nutritional intervention components, assessed via a structured exit questionnaire at V4.

Sample size calculations

The sample size was estimated based on the study’s primary comparative objective: detecting a clinically meaningful difference in the prevalence of gestational hyperglycaemia (fasting capillary blood glucose ≥ 92 mg/dL) between participants with low versus high adherence to the nutritional intervention. Based on a prevalence of gestational hyperglycaemia of 18% in the low-adherence group — consistent with the most recent estimates for Central Africa using IADPSG-equivalent fasting glucose criteria [16, 17] — and assuming a minimum detectable difference of 7% points (P2 = 11% in the high-adherence group), corresponding to a relative reduction of approximately 38%, a two-sided significance level of 5% (α = 0.05), a statistical power of 80%, and a 2:1 allocation ratio between low- and high-adherence participants reflecting the natural distribution of adherence levels expected in the cohort, a minimum of 348 participants were required for the primary comparative analysis. Accounting for an anticipated attrition rate of 15% across the four study visits, this yields a minimum analytic sample of 410 participants. The total target enrolment of 1,200 participants was therefore determined primarily to ensure adequate statistical precision for secondary prevalence estimates of gestational hyperglycaemia (± 2.1% points at 95% confidence), anaemia, hypertensive disorders, and adverse perinatal outcomes across the full cohort.

Recruitment and retention

Trained research assistants identify potential participants during their initial antenatal care (ANC) booking visit. Eligibility is systematically assessed using a standardized screening form. Eligible candidates receive a comprehensive briefing regarding study objectives, procedures, and potential risks. Enrolment is finalised only after obtaining written informed consent, provided in the participant’s preferred language (English or French). A unique study number is assigned to each enrolled participant. Enrolment commenced in May 2025 and is planned to close after 52 weeks of consecutive recruitment, anticipated in May 2026, by which time the target enrolment of 1,200 participants is expected to be met or exceeded.

All data are captured electronically using REDCap v14.5 (Research Electronic Data Capture), with built-in validation rules to improve accuracy and reduce transcription errors.

Visit 1 (V1) – enrolment and baseline assessment (< 22 weeks)

At V1, written informed consent is obtained prior to any study procedures. Data collection encompasses: (i) a comprehensive socio-demographic questionnaire covering household conditions, obstetric and gynaecological history, medical history, food security, and gender-based violence; (ii) a dietary diversity questionnaire (7-day recall of food group consumption); (iii) assessment of environmental exposures including alcohol consumption (AUDIT-C), tobacco use, medication history, and pesticide exposure; and (iv) a physical activity questionnaire. In a selected subsample, physical activity is monitored continuously for 7 days using a smartwatch. Clinical assessments include blood pressure, fasting capillary blood glucose, haemoglobin measurement, and an extensive anthropometric profile (weight, height, BMI, body composition via skinfold thickness, MUAC, hand-grip strength, and bioimpedance analysis). Blood (2 EDTA + 2 serum tubes) and urine samples are collected. The visit concludes with the participant’s initial session of individualized nutritional education, marking the start of the embedded intervention.

Visit 2 (V2) – mid-gestation follow-up (24–32 weeks)

V2 focuses on monitoring intervention adherence and screening for mid-gestation complications. Participants complete follow-up questionnaires on clinical events, environmental exposures, dietary diversity, and changes in physical activity since baseline. An intervention adherence questionnaire is administered (REDCap items im1g_1 to im1l). Clinical assessments include blood pressure, weight, fasting capillary blood glucose, and haemoglobin measurement. Anthropometric measures (bioimpedance, MUAC, hand-grip strength) are repeated. Blood and urine samples are collected. A refresher session of individualized nutritional education reinforces the intervention.

Visit 3 (V3) – delivery / childbirth

Data collection at V3 captures labour and birth outcomes. Research staff document details of labour, obstetric complications, and immediate neonatal parameters. Questionnaires assess longitudinal adherence to nutritional recommendations, late-pregnancy environmental exposures, and adverse events. Clinical measurements include maternal blood pressure and obstetric parameters. Haemoglobin is measured. Biological samples are collected from the mother (2 EDTA + 2 serum tubes, urine) and, where applicable, umbilical cord blood. Neonatal anthropometry (weight, length, head circumference, MUAC) is measured within 24 h of delivery. Before discharge, nutritional counselling is reinforced and a delivery kit is provided.

Visit 4 (V4) – six weeks postpartum

The final study visit is conducted at approximately six weeks postpartum, coinciding with the infant’s routine vaccination schedule. V4 evaluates both.

Biological sample processing and storage

Blood samples are processed within 2 h of collection. Maternal blood is centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 10–15 min. Following centrifugation, serum and plasma are aliquoted into cryotubes (4 serum, 4 plasma, 2 white blood cell pellet, 2 erythrocyte pellet, 2 ml each) and labelled with a unique QR code-based label encoding the participant identifier, visit number, and sample type. Cord blood is processed identically.

Samples are initially stored on-site at the CASS Nkolndongo at − 20 °C in a dedicated freezer. They are transported twice weekly to the long-term biobank, either at the Centre National d’Obésité (CNO), Hôpital Central de Yaoundé, or at the Biotechnology Laboratory, University of Yaoundé I, where samples are stored at − 80 °C. Freezer temperatures are monitored twice daily (morning and evening) using a calibrated thermometer with a temperature log.

Unused samples will be retained in the biobank for future ethically approved research, as consented by participants (see Ethical Considerations, section 26b). Samples may be shared with other research teams only under a formal data- and material-sharing agreement and with CNERSH authorisation.

Nutritional intervention (SUN-APP program)

The intervention is grounded in the DOHaD hypothesis and aims to improve maternal nutrition and physical activity during pregnancy to optimise fetal programming and reduce the long-term risk of non-communicable diseases. It is delivered through a multi-component, culturally adapted package combining educational, behavioural, and digital strategies.

Individual counselling (approximately 10 min per ANC visit) is provided face-to-face during routine ANC visits by trained healthcare providers using a structured approach based on assessment, problem-solving, and personalised goal setting. Group education sessions are conducted daily within the health facility using illustrated image boxes. Interactive workshops are held twice monthly and include practical activities such as cooking demonstrations, budget-friendly meal planning, home gardening, and guided physical activity. Digital support includes weekly audio podcasts and health tips via WhatsApp, and a 24/7 WhatsApp hotline. A community home visit is conducted once between V2 and V3. Educational videos are displayed continuously in the ANC waiting hall.

Intervention adherence monitoring

Adherence to the intervention is monitored systematically at V2 using a structured self-report questionnaire (REDCap items im1g_1 to im1l), covering: attendance at individual counselling sessions; compliance with nutritional, supplementation, and physical activity recommendations; regular reading of WhatsApp messages; receipt of weekly nutritional SMS messages; viewing of educational videos in the ANC waiting hall; and participation in practical nutrition workshops.

In addition to participant self-report, adherence is reinforced through the following strategies implemented by healthcare providers and the study team: active encouragement by midwives during ANC consultations; free haemoglobin and glucose screening as an incentive; systematic follow-up telephone calls (see Recruitment and Retention section); and distribution of a delivery kit at discharge.

Adverse event monitoring and reporting

There are no anticipated major risks from study participation. Minor and temporary discomfort may occur during blood sampling (bruising, pain, or infection at the puncture site). All blood samples are collected by trained research staff using new sterile lancets, minimising these risks.

Adverse events (AEs) are identified through two systematic mechanisms:

Follow-up telephone calls: During scheduled calls, research assistants discuss participants’ clinical status and specifically enquire about any adverse events that may not have been reported since the last visit. If an AE is identified, the research team ensures the participant has been seen by a qualified healthcare provider.

All AEs are documented on a standardised adverse event form and entered into REDCap. Adverse event forms are extracted weekly and reviewed at the cohort monitoring meetings held every Monday and Wednesday. Serious adverse events (SAEs) are reported to the CNERSH within 24 h of identification, in accordance with national research ethics regulations.

Participants identified with gestational hyperglycaemia, anaemia, or hypertensive disorders are referred for clinical management within the existing ANC system at CASS Nkolndongo at no additional cost to the participant.

Study management and oversight

The study is supported by a structured management and coordination framework. Since the initiation of participant recruitment in May 2025, regular steering meetings have been held weekly, bringing together the core study staff and investigators to review enrolment rates, assess recruitment performance, and identify operational challenges.

Weekly coordination meetings are held every Monday and Wednesday with the study team to review data completeness and accuracy, address protocol deviations, and reinforce standard operating procedures. Adverse event forms are reviewed at these meetings. Particular attention is given to safeguarding participants’ rights and ensuring the well-being and safety of all participants.

Data quality is monitored through regular review of REDCap completion rates by the Principal Investigator and the study coordinator. No formal independent Data Monitoring Committee is established for this study; however, protocol continuation is assessed annually by the study leadership team.

Statistical analysis

General approach

All statistical analyses will be performed using R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided significance threshold of 5% (α = 0.05) will be applied to all inferential analyses. All analyses will follow a pre-specified Statistical Analysis Plan documented prior to database lock and reported in accordance with STROBE guidelines for observational studies.

Feasibility and acceptability analyses

Feasibility outcomes (recruitment rate, retention rate, data completeness, intervention uptake) will be expressed as proportions with 95% confidence intervals computed using the Wilson score method. A pre-specified feasibility threshold will be considered met if the lower bound of the 95% CI for each metric exceeds the pre-specified threshold. Acceptability will be summarised descriptively. Retention across visits will be assessed using Kaplan-Meier methods to account for loss to follow-up.

Prevalence estimation

Prevalences of gestational hyperglycaemia, anaemia, hypertensive disorders, low birth weight, and preterm birth will be estimated with 95% confidence intervals using the Wilson score method.

Nutritional intervention exposure: adherence score

Since no concurrent control group is available, effectiveness of the nutritional intervention will be examined by comparing outcomes across tertiles of a composite adherence score constructed from REDCap items im1g_1 to im1l at V2. The score will be computed as the sum of all items, standardised to a 0–100 scale. Internal consistency will be assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Participants in the lowest adherence tertile (T1) will serve as the reference group in all comparative analyses.

Primary analytical framework

Baseline characteristics will be compared across adherence tertiles using the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and the Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. For continuous metabolic outcomes, multivariable linear regression will be used. For binary outcomes, multivariable logistic regression will be applied. All models will adjust for: maternal age, parity, gestational age at enrolment, pre-pregnancy BMI, educational attainment, and household wealth index. Results will be expressed as adjusted regression coefficients (β, 95% CI) or adjusted odds ratios (OR, 95% CI).

Secondary analyses

Longitudinal trajectories across pregnancy visits will be modelled using linear mixed-effects models (lme4 package), including a random intercept per participant. A before/after sensitivity analysis will compare within-individual changes in metabolic parameters between V1 and V2 (ΔV2–V1) across adherence tertiles.

Missing data

The extent and pattern of missing data will be characterised by variable and by visit. For primary feasibility outcomes, complete-case analysis will be used. For secondary analytical objectives, Little’s MCAR test will be applied. If data are not missing completely at random, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE, mice package) will be performed with 20 imputed datasets combined using Rubin’s rules.

Discussion

The CAMELIA cohort is designed to respond to a critical and under-addressed gap in maternal health programming in sub-Saharan Africa: the systematic integration of targeted nutrition and metabolic risk screening into routine antenatal care. In Cameroon, where the double burden of malnutrition coexists with rising rates of obesity, anaemia, and gestational metabolic disorders, routine ANC provides contact with pregnant women in urban areas [1, 2, 18]. However, this contact has historically not been leveraged to deliver structured, risk-stratified nutritional interventions [19, 20]. CAMELIA moves beyond descriptive epidemiology by embedding implementation science within a high-volume first-level facility, positioning ANC as a platform for early identification and prevention of NCDs across generations [21–23].

One of the central innovations of CAMELIA is the early (< 22 weeks) metabolic assessment combined with repeat screening later in pregnancy and postpartum. By incorporating early fasting capillary glucose assessment and longitudinal follow-up, CAMELIA will help clarify whether a proportion of hyperglycaemia detected in pregnancy reflects previously undiagnosed pre-existing dysglycaemia rather than classical gestational diabetes. A key limitation of the current protocol is the absence of an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), which was not performed due to resource constraints. Gestational hyperglycaemia screening therefore relies solely on fasting capillary blood glucose (≥ 92 mg/dL). This means that cases of isolated post-load hyperglycaemia will not be detected, and prevalence estimates from this study are not directly comparable to those derived from full IADPSG-based OGTT protocols. This limitation is explicitly acknowledged and will be discussed in all resulting publications.

CAMELIA differentiates itself by using a multi-modal, layered intervention approach reflecting principles from behavioural science, including repetition, peer engagement, goal setting, and environmental cueing [12, 14]. The intervention is culturally adapted and operationalized within existing clinic workflows [20, 24]. The integration of low-cost digital communication is particularly relevant in urban Cameroon, where mobile phone penetration is high.

A further strength is the inclusion of environmental exposures—specifically pesticides and alcohol consumption—within a maternal metabolic framework. By linking environmental data to maternal and neonatal outcomes, CAMELIA extends into the domain of DOHaD and intergenerational cardiometabolic risk [25, 26].

Several limitations merit consideration. As a single-site study in an urban first-level facility in Yaoundé, generalizability to rural or tertiary settings may be limited. The absence of randomization limits causal inference regarding intervention effectiveness; residual confounding may influence observed associations. Self-reported dietary intake and environmental exposure data are subject to recall and social desirability bias [19]. Attrition, particularly postpartum, may introduce bias if loss to follow-up is differential.

If CAMELIA demonstrates high feasibility and signals improvements in metabolic or perinatal outcomes, it could provide an operational blueprint for scaling structured maternal nutrition programs across Cameroon and comparable LMICs [24]. Future research could build on these findings through cluster-randomized designs, cost-effectiveness analyses, and longer-term offspring follow-up.

Conclusion

The CAMELIA study provides a scalable and replicable framework for embedding structured nutritional risk screening and targeted interventions into routine antenatal care in a low-resource African setting. By combining prospective longitudinal clinical tracking with culturally adapted education and digital reinforcement, this design addresses the critical missed opportunity of routine ANC visits in Cameroon. Findings will inform strategies for the prevention of gestational metabolic disorders and adverse perinatal outcomes across generations and may serve as a blueprint for similar programs across sub-Saharan Africa.

There are no overt major risks associated with study participation. Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The CAMELIA study received ethical clearance from the National Ethics Committee for Research in Human Health (CNERSH) of Cameroon (approval no. 2025/04/1790/CE/CNERSH/SP). Written informed consent is obtained from all participants. For participants under 21 years of age, written parental consent and participant assent are required. All study procedures are conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) and the Declaration of Helsinki.

All information collected is kept strictly confidential. Participants are identified by a unique coded number; no names, addresses, or telephone numbers are disclosed to any researcher outside the core study team. Records and samples are securely archived for future research. Participants have explicitly consented to the storage of biological samples (blood and urine, including umbilical cord blood) in the RSD Institute biobank and to their use in future ethically approved research studies.

The study protocol, information sheets, and consent forms have been submitted to the CNERSH for approval and remain subject to review for any substantial amendments. Any amendments will be communicated to the ISRCTN registry within 15 days.
