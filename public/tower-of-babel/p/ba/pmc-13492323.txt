Introduction

The majority of cardiovascular implantable electronic device (CIED) infections are caused by staphylococci. CIED infection caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis is rare. The diagnosis may be easily missed because patients often present with a pocket swelling with little or no systemic symptoms. A high index of suspicion is needed in endemic areas, especially in patients presenting with cold abscesses.

Case report

An adolescent patient underwent patch closure for a ventricular septal defect at the age of 6 years followed by implantation of an endocardial permanent single-chamber pacemaker for postoperative complete atrioventricular block. There was eventual recovery of atrioventricular conduction with no requirement for ventricular pacing, although there was residual complete right bundle branch block. The pacemaker was programmed in VVI mode for backup pacing at a rate of 40 beats per minute. An elective pacemaker generator replacement in the same right subpectoral pocket was performed 10 years later. The chest radiograph done after the generator replacement showed no lung parenchymal lesions (Figure 1A). The patient presented 15 months after the generator replacement with painless swelling of the pacemaker pocket. The swelling was insidious in onset, with the patient noticing it just 1 day before presentation, although on probing she gave a history of feeling of fullness in that area for a few days. There was no history of trauma, pain, or discharge from the site. There were no constitutional symptoms including fever, anorexia, or weight loss.

On examination, the patient was malnourished with a body mass index of 16.54 kg/m2. Pulse rate was 60 beats per minute, and blood pressure was 100/60 mm Hg. Pallor was present. On local examination, a nontender, cold, and fluctuant swelling was noted at the pacemaker site. A midline scar of sternotomy was noted. There were no other scars or draining sinuses. Cardiovascular and chest examination were unremarkable.

Ultrasound examination showed an anechoic ill-defined collection around the device in the subcutaneous and muscular plane measuring 7.2 cm × 5.2 cm × 3.5 cm in the right infraclavicular area. A provisional diagnosis of late pocket infection was made, and antimicrobial agents including vancomycin and ceftriaxone were initiated empirically. Laboratory investigations showed normal leucocyte count and procalcitonin levels with elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein. Blood cultures were sterile. Severe anemia was noted, which on evaluation was found to be caused by iron deficiency.

Chest radiograph showed an arc-shaped consolidation in the right upper zone and loculated pleural effusion in the right lower zone (Figure 1B). A fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography–computed tomography done to evaluate for lead involvement revealed a small pleura-based consolidation in the upper lobe of right lung showing 18F-FDG uptake. There were also multiple pockets of loculated pleural effusion with 18F-FDG uptake in the right lung. The pleura-based lesion in the right upper lobe was observed to be communicating with the pacemaker pocket suggestive of empyema necessitans extending into the pacemaker pocket site (Figure 2). 18F-FDG–avid soft tissue thickening and collection were noted around the pacemaker pocket in the right upper anterior chest wall. The uptake around the device was most intense along the inferior aspect and the header, which favored infection over inflammation secondary to the generator change.1 In any case, the replacement had been done 15 months before the current presentation. There was no involvement of the lead. The 12-lead electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with complete right bundle branch block and prolonged PR interval. Device interrogation revealed no requirement for ventricular pacing since the generator replacement.

Approximately 63%–77% of pacemaker pocket infections occur within 1 year, and 23%–37% occur after 1 year.2 Early infections are more likely to present with pocket erythema, swelling, and pain, whereas late infections are more often associated with pocket erosion and valvular vegetations.3 Staphylococci remain the major causative organisms in most patients with both early and late CIED infections.4 In our case, the presence of underlying lung disease and lack of device erosion or signs of local inflammation led to suspicion of an indolent infection such as tuberculosis, which is endemic in India.

Because of the size of the collection and lack of requirement for pacing, evacuation of the collection and explantation of the device were done. Approximately 60 mL of pus was aspirated and sent for microbiological analysis. Localized pus collection in the subpectoral pocket around the device and lead coils was noted. The generator was explanted. The lead was not extracted owing to logistic reasons. The pocket was debrided and capsulectomy was done. After an antibiotic wash, the wound was closed in layers. There was no recurrent collection or discharge after the procedure.

GeneXpert of pus was positive with detection of M tuberculosis sensitive to rifampicin. Bacterial culture was sterile.

Based on clinical examination, imaging, and microbiological confirmation, a diagnosis of tubercular empyema necessitans with pacemaker pocket cold abscess was made, and weight-based 4-drug antitubercular therapy (ATT) was initiated and continued for 6 months. Empirical antibiotic agents were stopped. Iron folic acid supplementation was done for anemia.

There was resolution of pulmonary lesion with no recurrent collection at follow-up of 18 months.

Discussion

CIED infection caused by M tuberculosis is rare with only a handful of case reports published to date. The described mechanisms of tubercular pacemaker pocket infection include reactivation of latent bacilli at sites of surgery or trauma or direct seeding from an active infection at a distant site or in miliary tuberculosis.

The first reported tuberculous CIED infection dates back to 1996, when miliary tuberculosis with involvement of a pacemaker was described postmortem in a 70-year-old lady implanted with a device 10 years back.5 Hellwig et al6 described tuberculous infection of an epicardial pacemaker in an 8-year-old child who presented 11 months after device implantation with a subcutaneous abscess at the site of device implantation. Kumar et al7 published a case series of 3 patients who presented with tubercular pacemaker pocket abscesses. However, none of them had active tuberculous infection elsewhere. All 3 patients improved after ATT. Al-Ghamdi et al8 described a case of tubercular infective endocarditis owing to involvement of a cardiac resynchronization therapy pacemaker lead with tubercular vegetations. The patient presented with miliary tuberculosis 8 months after device implantation. Despite surgical removal of leads and large vegetations and ATT, the patient succumbed to the illness. Subpectoral location is preferred in young patients to minimize the risk of device erosion through relatively thin skin and subcutaneous tissue and for aesthetic consideration. The base of a subpectoral pocket is formed by a relatively thin pectoralis minor muscle, subpectoral fascia, ribs, and intercostal muscles; thus, it is in close proximity to the pleural space. To the best of our knowledge, extension of tubercular empyema to the pacemaker pocket (tubercular empyema necessitans) has not been described to date.

This case underscores the importance of a high index of suspicion for tuberculosis in endemic areas, especially in cold abscesses or with atypical presentation. The report also highlights the potential for extension of pleura-based infections into the subpectoral pacemaker pocket owing to anatomic proximity. Early recognition may prevent misdiagnosis and avoid unnecessary use of broad-spectrum antibiotic agents.
