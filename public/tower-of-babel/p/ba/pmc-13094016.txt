Astragaloside IV Suppresses Gastric Cancer by m6A-dependent FSP1 Modulation and Ferroptosis Induction
Authors: Wei Yin, Xi Chen, Bi Chen, Xiaodi Xu, Wenxian Guan, Haixiao Wang, Yang Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAstragaloside IV (AS-IV), a key active component derived from the traditional Chinese medicinal plant Astragali, has been reported to exhibit various biological activities, including antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, immunoregulatory, and antineoplastic properties. This study aimed to elucidate the role of AS-IV in inhibiting gastric cancer (GC) growth, focusing on its impact on cell ferroptosis and the underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsProliferation and migration of GC cells upon AS-IV treatment were examined using CCK-8, colony formation, and Transwell assays. Ferroptosis induction was analyzed via ELISA, flow cytometry, and transmission electron microscopy. Ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) mRNA stability was assessed by the ActD assay, while RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) was employed to confirm the interaction between FSP1 mRNA, Fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) demethylase, and YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA-binding protein F2 (YTHDF2). The dual-luciferase reporter assay was used to explore FTO binding to N6-methyladenosine (m6A)-modified sites on FSP1 mRNA. Furthermore, AS-IV’s anti-tumor effects (20 mg/kg) were validated in vivo using gastric cancer xenograft and lung metastasis mouse models.ResultsAS-IV significantly suppressed the proliferation and migration of GC cells by inducing ferroptosis. Mechanistically, AS-IV down-regulated FTO, thus impairing its interaction with FSP1 mRNA and leading to increased m6A modification on FSP1 mRNA. This modification facilitated m6A recognition protein YTHDF2-mediated recognition and subsequent degradation of FSP1 mRNA. The reduction of FSP1 triggered ferroptosis, while the overexpression of FSP1 or inhibition of ferroptosis by ferrostatin-1 partially reversed AS-IV’s effects on cell viability and migration. In vivo, AS-IV effectively inhibited tumor growth and metastasis.ConclusionsThis study highlights potent anti-GC effects of AS-IV, mediated by the suppression of FSP1 via the FTO/YTHDF2/m6A axis.Graphical AbstractThe treatment of AS-IV inhibits the expression of demethylase FTO in GC cells, subsequently disrupting the binding between FTO protein and FSP1 mRNA. This disruption leads to a increase in the level of m6A modification on FSP1 mRNA, thereby enhancing the recognition and binding of m6A-recognizing protein YTHDF2, promoting the decay of FSP1 mRNA. Ultimately, the downregulation of FSP1 enhances ferroptosis in GC cells. Collectively. AS-IV holds great promise as a novel therapeutic strategy for gastric cancer treatment. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a common malignant tumor [1, 2]. The main risk factors for GC include Helicobacter pylori infection, age, low vegetable intake, and high salt intake [3]. The current primary treatment strategies for patients with GC include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and biological therapy [3]. Recent advancements in immunotherapy, especially in anti-HER2 treatments and biomarker-based therapies, have shown promising results in GC. For instance, anti-PD-1 antibodies have demonstrated significant efficacy, leading to prolonged survival in metastatic GC patients who have not received prior treatment [4]. Furthermore, novel anti-HER2 agents, including T-DXd and disitamab vedotin (RC48), have brought about major breakthroughs in the management of HER2-positive GC [5]. However, these methods have unsatisfactory therapeutic efficacy, and approximately 50% of the patients with GC experience relapse and ultimately die within two years after surgery [6]. Notably, tumor invasion and distant metastasis, both critical factors contributing to mortality in patients with GC, remain significant challenges in clinical practice [7, 8]. Consequently, developing innovative treatment approaches for patients with GC is imperative.

Bioactive compounds derived from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) have gained significant attention in recent decades as promising anti-cancer agents due to their cost-effectiveness, favorable safety profiles, and potent anti-tumor efficacy [9, 10]. TCM treatment can remarkably suppress tumor growth, alleviate drug resistance, reduce adverse reactions, and improve patients’ quality of life [11, 12]. For instance, Baicalin attenuates the immune escape of oral squamous cell carcinoma by reducing lactate accumulation in tumor microenvironment [13]. Berberine can represses GC cell growth by inducing cytostatic autophagy via inhibition of MAPK/mTOR/p70S6K and Akt signaling pathways [14]. Quercetin is able to inhibit the proliferation of GC cells by activating the pyroptosis pathway [15]. Curcumin has been reported to attenuate GC cell growth by blocking the PI3K pathway in a does-dependent manner [16]. These findings indicate that TCM may exert critical role in suppressing GC progression. Astragaloside IV (AS-IV; C41H68O14, molecular weight: 784.97 Da), a critical bioactive compound extracted from the root of the Chinese medicinal plant Astragal [17], exhibits diverse biological properties, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-tumor property [18, 19]. Notably, AS-IV inhibits lung cancer progression by modulating macrophage polarization [20]. AS-IV can mitigate the aggressiveness of hepatocellular carcinoma by blocking the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [21]. Despite preliminary evidence documenting the anti-tumor effects of AS-IV in GC via stromal remodeling [22] or metabolic reprogramming [23], its molecular targets and potential mechanism remain poorly defined.

Astragaloside IV directly binds to FTO and regulates its mediated m6A methylation [24]. The m6A modification process involves multiple key enzymes, primarily including Writers (e.g., METTL3) [25, 26], Erasers (e.g., FTO) [27], and Readers (e.g., YTHDF1/2/3). m6A modification regulates the RNA stability and translation efficiency of ferroptosis-related genes (e.g., SLC7A11, GPX4), thereby influencing cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis [28].

In contrast to autophagy, apoptosis, and pyroptosis, ferroptosis is an iron-dependent programmed cell death with diminished or absent mitochondrial crests and lipid peroxidation resulting from excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [29, 30]. Ferroptosis is closely associated with numerous physiological processes and pathological conditions, particularly in cancer [31–33]. In GC, ferroptosis has been reported to be closely involved in tumor progression. Xu [34] et al. found that increased activation transcription factor 2 (ATF2) expression could inhibit sorafenib-induced ferroptosis in gastric cancer, thus causing a poor clinical prognosis. Cysteine protease inhibitor SN (Cystatin SN, CST1) has been reported to suppress GC cell ferroptosis and enhance tumor metastasis by regulating glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) stability [35]. Additionally, lncRNA-BDNF played a critical role in mediating ferroptosis in the peritoneal metastasis of GC by regulating voltage-dependent anion channel 3 (VDAC3) ubiquitination [36], thus indicating a close association between ferroptosis and GC development and progression. Notably, natural products derived from TCM are also able to induce ferroptosis; for example, curcumenol, a bioactive compound in Wenyujin, induces ferroptosis in lung cancer cells, thereby impeding tumor progression [37]. Berberine, a plant alkaloid, mediates ferroptosis by inhibiting GPX4, thus preventing nasopharyngeal carcinoma metastasis [38].

In the present study, we found that AS-IV could significantly suppress GC tumor growth by inducing ferroptosis in GC cells. We aimed to explore the role of AS-IV in suppressing GC and its metastasis, along with the underlying potential mechanism to provide a novel therapeutic strategy for patients with GC. This article is compliant with the TITAN Guidelines 2025, and Artificial Intelligence (AI) was not used in the research and manuscript development [39].

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Transfection

The Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Shanghai (China) provided the GC cell lines (HGC-27 and MKN-45). MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) medium, respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. For the construction of the FSP1 overexpression plasmid, human FSP1 cDNA was synthesized and cloned into the pCDH vector. To obtain the FSP1 overexpression lentivirus, we transfected the pCDH, psPAX2, and pMD2.G plasmids into 293 T cells. Stable transfected HGC27 cells were obtained using FSP1 overexpression lentivirus infection.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) was used to examine cell viability. Approximately 2 × 103/well GC cells were seeded into a 96-well plate. Next, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added 24 h later, and the cells were incubated again for 1 h. Finally, absorbance was recorded at 450 nm.

Colony Formation Assay

HGC-27 and MKN-45 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate and maintained for approximately 2 weeks. The medium was removed, and the colonies were fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. Subsequently, images of the colonies in each well were captured.

Trans-well Assay

Cell migration was examined using a transwell assay. Approximately 600 µL of complete medium was supplemented into the lower chamber, and approximately 5 × 104 GC cells were resuspended in a serum-free medium and added to the upper transwell chamber. After 24 h, the cells were collected and fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde. Unmigrated cells were carefully removed, and migrated cells were treated with 0.1% crystal violet. Finally, the migrated cells were observed and photographed using an optical microscope (Nikon, Japan, 10×).

RNA Extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells and tissues using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, USA), followed by cDNA synthesis. RT-qPCR was performed using a Lightcycler480 (Roche, Switzerland) with SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China). GAPDH was used as the internal control. By normalizing to GAPDH, relative gene expression was determined through the ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences were listed in Table S1.

Western Blotting Analysis

Cell and tissue proteins were isolated using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China). A BCA kit (Solarbio, China) was used to determine the protein concentration. Proteins were separated on a 10% SDS–PAGE gel and transferred onto the PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The membranes were then blocked with fat-free milk for 2 h at room temperature and incubated with the indicated primary antibodies (Table S2) overnight at 4 ℃. The membranes were then incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies (1:2000; Beyotime) 1 h. Protein bands were exposed to ECL reagent (Vazyme, China), and images were observed using a chemiluminescence imager (Bio-Rad, USA). Protein intensity was quantified using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP)–qPCR

The RIP assay was performed using a Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Merck Millipore). First, GC cells were lysed using a RIP lysis buffer and then supplemented with primary anti-FTO, anti-YTHDF2 (Table S2), or control IgG linked to magnetic beads at 4 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, proteinase K was added to digest the bead-bound immunoprecipitates and separate the RNAs. Next, a phenol–chloroform–isoamyl alcohol solution was used to purify the RNAs, which were then subjected to reverse transcription. The enrichment of FSP1 mRNA was examined using RT-qPCR. Total RNAs were immunoprecipitated using IgG and N6-methyladenosine (m6A) primary antibodies to analyze the m6A modification of FSP1 (Table S2). After washing and elution, the immunoprecipitated and input RNAs were transcribed into cDNA, which was then subjected to RT-qPCR.

Global m6A Modification Quantification

Global m6A modifications in both GC cell lines were determined using the EpiQuik m6A RNA Methylation Quantification Kit (Epigentek, USA) and a dot blot assay. For dot blot, mRNAs were denatured at 95 ℃ for 5 min and then immediately cooled. After spotting onto a Hybond-N + membrane (Millipore, Germany), the mRNAs were cross-linked to the membrane using a UV cross-linker. The membrane was then incubated with an anti-m6A antibody at 4 ℃ for 12 h, followed by incubation with a corresponding secondary antibody. Subsequently, the membrane was visualized using a chemiluminescence imager (Bio-Rad). Methylene blue (Solarbio) was used as a loading control to stain the membrane.

Dual-luciferase Reporter Assay

Luciferase reporters harboring the wild-type (WT) and mutated-type (MUT) m6A motifs within the FSP1 mRNA were generated by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). The Dual-luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) was used to detect luciferase activity following the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, the luciferase activity was quantified by normalizing the luciferase activity of the firefly to that of Renilla.

Determination of Intracellular Iron, Malondialdehyde (MDA), and ROS Levels

Intracellular iron and MDA levels in GC cells were detected using an iron assay kit (Abcam, ab83366) and an MDA detection kit (Abcam, ab118970), respectively, following the manufacturer’s protocols. The intracellular ROS levels were examined with DCFH–DA (MCE, USA). Briefly, cultured GC cells were treated with DCFH-DA (5 µM) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Cells were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the suspension was used to analyze ROS levels using flow cytometry (BA Bioscience, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Measurements of Intracellular Lipid Peroxidation

GC cells were incubated with the BODIPY 581/591 C11 probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 37 °C. Lipid peroxidation fluorescence was quantitatively assessed using flow cytometry (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

The HGC-27 and MKN-45 cell lines were initially fixed with 3% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and then with 1% OsO4. Following dehydration, approximately 60-nm-thick sections were stained with uranyl acetate and plumbous nitrate. Images were captured using a JEM-1230 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).

mRNA Stability

The stability of mRNA in GC cells was detected by incubating cells with actinomycin D (5 µg/mL, ActD, MCE, USA). Cells were harvested for total RNA isolation at 0, 2, 4, and 6 h. RT-qPCR was used to detect FSP1 expression. The half-life (t1/2) of FSP1 mRNA was determined using linear regression analysis and normalized relative to β-actin.

Animal Study

A total number of 58 female BALB/c nude mice (weight: 18–22 g, age: 4–5 weeks) were obtained from the Animal Center of GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. NCG mice (n = 18) were purchased from gempharmatech (Nanjing, China), and 615 mice (n = 6) were provided by the Institute of Hematology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Tianjin, China). All mice were maintained in specific pathogen-free conditions with a circadian rhythm, and are free to drink water and food. All animal assays were approved by The Affiliated Huaian No.1 People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. DW-P-2024-036-01) and were performed according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. HGC-27 cells (5 × 106) were subcutaneously injected into BALB/c nude mice. When a palpable mass developed, the mice were randomly divided into the following groups using a computer based random order generator: DMSO (control), AS-IV (20 mg/kg), AS-IV (20 mg/kg) + Fer-1 (30 mg/kg), and AS-IV (20 mg/kg) + FSP1. The tumor volume (V) was determined as V = (L × W2)/2. Three different investigators were involved as follows: a first investigator, who was the only person aware of the treatment group allocation, developed the treatment plan. A second investigator conducted the intervention procedure, whereas a third investigator performed the analysis. 12 days later, tumor tissues were harvested after anesthesia with pentobarbital sodium, and tumor weights were measured. All treatments and measurements were conducted following a fixed procedure to minimize potential confounders. If the tumor weight exceeds 10% of mouse body weight, average tumor diameter exceeds 20 mm, or if ulceration occurs resulting in infection or necrosis, the experiment will be terminated, and euthanasia will be performed on the animals. A mouse model of lung metastasis was established by intravenously injecting Luc-HGC-27 cells through the tail vein into NCG mice. Mice were divided into DMSO and AS-IV treatment groups, and lung metastasis was observed using a living imaging assay. Lung tissue was collected from each mouse for further hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining. NCG mice were used to establish an orthotopic gastric cancer model with Luc-MKN-45 cells. Briefly, a 50-µL cell suspension containing 5 × 10⁵ Luc-MKN-45 cells was injected into the submucosal layer along the greater curvature of the stomach. After injection, the needle was retained for 30 s to prevent backflow, then slowly withdrawn. The formation of orthotopic gastric cancer was detected using an in vivo imaging assay. A tumor-bearing model using the mouse forestomach carcinoma (MFC) cell line in immunocompetent 615 mice was established for the detection of tumor microenvironment (TME) components. Moreover, to elucidate the biosafety of AS-IV, additional 6 normal healthy BALB/c mice without the injection of tumor cells were selected and randomly divided into DMSO (n = 3) and AS-IV-treated (n = 3) groups. On day 30, blood samples were collected from DMSO and AS-IV-treated mice to examine the ALP, ALT, AST, CREA, TP, and UREA levels. This study was approved by the Animal Care Committee of The Affiliated Huaian No.1 People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines [40].

H&E and Immunohistochemistry (IHC) Staining

The major organs and tumors of the mice were harvested and preserved in 4% polyformaldehyde. Primary organ tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were embedded in paraffin. The tissue sections were stained with the H&E method. Xenograft tumor samples were subjected to IHC staining to evaluate FSP1 expression. The DAB solution was utilized to visualize tissues, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin and sealing. Finally, the slides were observed under a microscope (Nikon).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad, USA). Each experiment was repeated three times and the data were expressed as the mean ± SD. The significance of between-group differences was assessed by the Mann-Whitney U test (non-normally distributed data), two-tailed Student’s t-test (normally distributed data with homogeneous variance) or two-tailed Student’s t-test with Welch’s correction (normally distributed data with heterogeneous variance). For normally distributed data, ANOVA with Tamhane’s T2 test (heterogeneous variance) or the S-N-K test (homogeneous variance) were conducted to evaluate differences among groups; for non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test was used. Analysis of data with two variables was performed using two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

AS-IV Attenuated GC Progression

GC cells were treated with different dose of AS-IV (0, 5, 10, 20, 40 µM), whereas the 0 µM was control group. CCK-8 assay revealed that AS-IV decreased GC cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1A). We conducted further experiments using 20 µM AS-IV, as it exhibited a significant inhibitory effect on GC cell viability, and the DMSO treatment group was set as control group. Subsequent colony formation assays demonstrated a marked growth suppression in both GC cell lines following treatment with AS-IV (Fig. 1B). GC cell migration was clearly impaired, as evidenced by the transwell assay (Fig. 1C). The inhibitory effects of AS-IV on the migration and proliferation of GC cells were further validated in AGS and N87 cell lines (Figure S1). The results consistently demonstrated that AS-IV significantly suppresses GC cell migratory and proliferative capacities. Furthermore, we elucidated the tumor-suppressive role of AS-IV in vivo. The xenograft mice were randomly divided into DMSO (n = 5) and AS-IV (n = 5) treatment groups. AS-IV administration attenuated tumor growth compared to the DMSO group, as evidenced by the reduced tumor weight (Fig. 1D) and decreased tumor volume (Fig. 1E) at the experimental endpoint. GC often metastasizes to distant organs. Therefore, we constructed mouse models of GC with lung metastasis via intravenous injection of GC cells. A live imaging assay revealed decreased tumor metastasis in the lungs of AS-IV-treated mice (n = 5) compared to the DMSO-treated mice (n = 5) (Fig. 1F). Subsequently, we harvested lung tissues and, by HE staining, observed notable alleviation of lung metastasis in the AS-IV group compared to the DMSO group. (Fig. 1G). The anti-tumor efficacy of AS-IV was further validated in the orthotopic gastric tumor model (DMSO: n = 4, AS-IV: n = 4). In vivo imaging assay showed that AS-IV significantly hindered the growth of orthotopic gastric cancer (Fig. 1H, I). Tumor tissues collected at the experimental endpoint indicated that AS-IV inhibited in situ tumor formation. (Fig. 1J).

To evaluate the potential immunomodulatory effects of AS-IV, we analyzed its influence on key tumor microenvironment (TME) components in 615 mice (DMSO: n = 3, AS-IV: n = 3), including CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells, dendritic cells (DCs), and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), using IHC. Our data revealed that AS-IV treatment did not significantly alter the proportions of these immune cell populations following AS-IV treatment (Figure S2). In addition, we measured AS-IV plasma concentrations over time in mice following intravenous injection to characterize its pharmacokinetic profile (n = 15) (Figure S3A). The tissue-specific accumulation of AS-IV was also assessed in primary organs and tumors (n = 3) (Figure S3B). Moreover, to evaluate the biosafety of AS-IV, we selected additionally 6 normal healthy mice treated with DMSO (n = 3) and AS-IV (n = 3), respectively. The blood samples were collected from DMSO and AS-IV-treated normal mice on day 30. No significant differences between the two groups were observed in ALP, ALT, AST, CREA, TP, and UREA levels (Figure S4A–F). Primary organs were harvested from the xenograft mice. No apparent pathological changes were detected in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney tissues following AS-IV treatment compared to the DMSO-treated group (Figure S4G), indicating no notable toxic effects of AS-IV. Collectively, AS-IV could repress tumor growth and distant metastasis of GC with no notable toxic effects.

AS-IV Inhibited GC Cell Malignancy by Inducing Ferroptosis in an FSP1-dependent Manner

We treated GC cells (with or without AS-IV treatment) with ferroptosis inhibitor DFO, necroptosis inhibitor Nec-1, autophagy inhibitor 3-MA, and apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK to elucidate the pathway underlying GC cell growth inhibition. The DMSO treatment group was control group. CCK-8 assay revealed that only DFO could reverse the AS-IV-induced inhibition of GC cell proliferation (Fig. 2A), indicating that AS-IV might inhibit GC cell growth by inducing ferroptosis. AS-IV-treated GC cells exhibited higher intracellular MDA and Fe2+ levels than DMSO-treated cells (Fig. 2B, C). Furthermore, ROS levels in HGC-27 and MKN-45 cells also increased remarkably following AS-IV treatment (Fig. 2D). Lipid peroxidation was determined by adding BODIPY 581/591 C11 to the GC cells. Flow cytometry revealed that AS-IV treatment significantly enhanced lipid peroxidation (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, mitochondrial matrix condensation, formation of enlarged cristae, and ultrastructural changes associated with ferroptosis were observed in GC cells following AS-IV treatment (Fig. 2F, G). Therefore, AS-IV possibly induces ferroptosis in GC cells.

GC cells were divided into four groups: DMSO, Fer-1, AS-IV + Fer-1, and AS-IV, with different treatments. CCK-8 assay revealed that the inhibitor of ferroptosis, Fer-1, partially improved cell viability inhibited by AS-IV (Fig. 3A). Colony formation experiments showed that the impaired growth of both GC cell lines following AS-IV treatment was reversed by Fer-1 (Fig. 3B). Although AS-IV markedly inhibited GC cell migration, supplementation with Fer-1 partially alleviated this effect (Fig. 3C). Therefore, AS-IV may inhibit the malignancy of GC cells by inducing ferroptosis. By detecting the expression of critical ferroptosis-related proteins, we found that AS-IV remarkably decreased FSP1 mRNA and protein levels but not those of GPX4, ACSL4, and SLC7A11 (Fig. 3D, E). In addition, FSP1 mRNA stability was affected by AS-IV treatment (Fig. 3F). As FSP1 is a crucial ferroptosis suppressor, we further verified whether AS-IV induces ferroptosis in GC cells by inhibiting FSP1 expression. Increased Fe2+ and MDA accumulation due to AS-IV treatment was reduced following FSP1 upregulation (Fig. 3G, H), indicating that FSP1 may induce ferroptosis in GC cells via AS-IV. Additionally, the impaired cell viability, growth, and migration of GC cells following AS-IV treatment were reversed by FSP1 overexpression (Fig. 3I–K). Therefore, AS-IV inhibits GC cell malignancy by inducing ferroptosis in an FSP1-dependent manner.

AS-IV Reduced FSP1 Expression in an m6A-dependent Manner through the FTO/YTHDF2 Pathway

In mammalian cells, m6A modification is the primary post-transcriptional modification of RNAs and is closely related to mRNA stability. We identified several m6A modification sites in the FSP1 mRNA using the SRAMP tool (http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp) (Figure S5A). Dot blot and m6A quantification assays revealed that AS-IV increased m6A modifications in GC cells compared to the DMSO-treated group (Fig. 4A, B). MeRIP–qPCR assay showed that AS-IV augmented the m6A modification of FSP1 mRNA (Fig. 4C). m6A was dynamically modulated by some methylases and demethylases. Following AS-IV treatment, we detected the expression of primary methylases METTL3 and METTL14 and critical demethylases ALKBH5 and FTO. Notably, AS-IV reduced the protein levels of FTO, whereas no significant changes were observed in those of METTL3/14 and ALKBH5 (Fig. 4D). The Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA) results demonstrated that AS-IV treatment significantly enhanced the thermal stability of FTO, suggesting a direct interaction between AS-IV and FTO protein (Figure S5B). Subsequently, the RIP assay demonstrated that FTO could bind to FSP1 mRNA (Fig. 4E). Based on the SRAMP prediction results, we selected the m6A motif “GGACU” with a high combined score and high confidence to construct luciferase reporters loading the wild-type (WT) and mutant-type (MUT) sequences (Figure S5C). A dual-luciferase reporter assay revealed that FTO recognized and bound to the m6A-motif of FSP1 mRNA (Fig. 4F). Overexpression of FTO, however, reversed the AS-IV treatment-induced increase in the m6A modification on FSP1 mRNA (Figure S5D). Rescue assays illustrated that FTO overexpression reversed the inhibitory effect of AS-IV on FSP1 (Fig. 4G, H). The metabolism of m6A-modified mRNAs was primarily determined by their binding proteins. Among these, YTHDF2 is responsible for recognizing m6A in mRNAs and subsequently degrading them. The interaction between YTHDF2 protein and FSP1 mRNA was confirmed using RIP assay (Fig. 4I). Additionally, YTHDF2 upregulation significantly impaired FTO mRNA stability (Fig. 4J). Overall, AS-IV suppressed FTO demethylase expression, leading to increased levels of m6A modification in FSP1 mRNA. Subsequently, the “reader” YTHDF2 recognized m6A modifications on FSP1 mRNA and promoted their degradation, ultimately reducing FSP1 expression in GC cells.

AS-IV Suppressed Tumor Growth by Reducing FSP1 Expression and Inducing Ferroptosis

To elucidate whether AS-IV suppresses GC tumor growth in vivo, we first detected FSP1 expression in xenograft mice. FSP1 was significantly downregulated in tumor tissues harvested from mice following AS-IV treatment compared to that of DMSO treatment group (Fig. 5A, B). In addition, IHC revealed low FSP1 expression in the tumor tissues of AS-IV-treated mice (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, xenograft mice (n = 15) were divided into three groups (DMSO, AS-IV, and AS-IV + FSP1). Tumor growth was inhibited by AS-IV, as evidenced by reduced tumor weight and volume, whereas simultaneous FSP1 overexpression partly reversed this effect (Fig. 5D, E). In tumor tissues, AS-IV promoted Fe2+ and MDA accumulation; however, FSP1 overexpression reversed this effect (Fig. 5F, G), indicating that AS-IV repressed tumor growth by inhibiting FSP1 expression, thus resulting in enhanced ferroptosis in GC cells. Additional xenograft mice (n = 15) were divided into three groups (DMSO, AS-IV, and AS-IV + Fer-1). Results showed that Fer-1 promoted tumor growth suppressed by AS-IV (Fig. 5H, I). Fe2+ and MDA levels were also reduced in the AS-IV + Fer-1 group compared with those in the AS-IV group (Fig. 5J, K).

Discussion

In recent years, various natural products from TCM, such as berberine [41], ginsenosides [42], atractylodes [43], and Angelica sinensis [44], have gained considerable attention because of their excellent biological effects and few adverse effects. AS-IV has long been used as a nutraceutical owing to its abundant pharmacological effects on primary organs and systems. Additionally, many studies have demonstrated the anti-tumor effects of AS-IV, indicating its potential application for patients with cancer. In this study, we performed a series of in vitro and in vivo assays to verify the efficacy of AS-IV against GC, so as to provide alternative choice for the treatment of patients with GC.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent cell death process that primarily regulated by the GPX4 pathway [45]. By employing molecular inhibitors targeting distinct cell death pathways (ferroptosis, necroptosis, autophagy, and apoptosis), we demonstrated that AS-IV repressed GC cell viability specifically through ferroptosis induction. To further explore the potential mechanism by which AS-IV enhanced ferroptosis, we detected the influence of it on conventional GPX4 pathway at first. However, we found AS-IV had no significant regulation on GPX4, ACSL4, and SLC7A11 genes, indicating that AS-IV might modulate ferroptosis through other manners. Recently, Doll [46] and Bersuker [47] simultaneously reported that FSP1 (previously known as AIFM2) can regulate ferroptosis in an approach different from the GPX4 signaling pathway. In general, FSP1 maintains phospholipid hydroperoxides by serving as an oxidoreductase that transforms ubiquinone (CoQ) into ubiquinol (CoQH2) to protect cells from ferroptosis [48, 49]. Moreover, FSP1 contributes to the chemoresistance or radioresistance of tumors by inhibiting ferroptosis in tumor cells [50, 51]. In addition, N-acetyltransferase 10 can accelerate colon cancer progression by impairing ferroptosis by stabilizing FSP1 mRNA [52]. Inhibition of FSP1, however, promotes ferroptosis and enhances anti-tumor immune responses, thus blocking hepatoma progression [53]. Interestingly, we found with the treatment of AS-IV, FSP1 expression was remarkably decreased in GC cells. Moreover, the simultaneous FSP1 overexpression was able to alleviate the cell ferroptosis caused by AS-IV, and further reverse the inhibitory effect of AS-IV on tumor growth. The above results indicated that AS-IV could reduce FSP1 expression, subsequently leading to the ferroptosis in GC cells, which contributed to the inhibition of GC tumor progression. Accordingly, although GPX4 is a well-established regulator of ferroptosis, our data suggested that AS-IV selectively targets the FSP1 axis without engaging the GPX4-glutathione system. This mechanistic specificity may confer therapeutic advantages, as GPX4 inhibition is often associated with systemic toxicity, whereas FSP1 suppression via AS-IV exhibited acceptable biosafety in preclinical models. However, other ferroptosis-related pathways (e.g., DHODH, GCH1) remain unexplored in this study and warrant future investigation to fully clarify the AS-IV’s effects on ferroptosis.

m6A modification is the most abundant and prevalent modification on mRNAs in mammalian cells [54]. m6A modifications have been reported to participate in distinct human diseases such as cardiovascular diseases [55], neurological diseases [56] and cancers [57]. m6A modifications play a critical role in diverse physiological and pathological processes by modulating gene expression in an epigenetic transcriptional manner [58]. In this study, we found AS-IV was able to reduce and remove the global m6A modification in GC cells and FSP1 mRNA, respectively. Considering that m6A modification is a dynamic regulation process co-regulated by m6A “writers” and “erasers” [59], we attempted to explore how AS-IV regulated the m6A modification in GC cells. As our results shown, AS-IV did not change the expression of the major methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) and methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14) and the eraser AlkB homologue 5 (ALKBH5), whereas the expression of the other essential eraser, FTO, was notably reduced, indicating that AS-IV might increase the m6A modification in GC cells by downregulating FTO. AS-IV may downregulate FTO expression by reducing its synthesis at the transcriptional or translational level, or by influencing its protein degradation in vivo. More importantly, beyond affecting protein expression levels, AS-IV could potentially impair the enzyme’s catalytic activity through direct binding [60]. Furthermore, RIP and dual-luciferase reporter experiments revealed that the FTO protein could interact with FSP1 mRNA’s m6A sites and enforced FTO expression could reverse the inhibitory effects of AS-IV on FSP1 expression, suggesting that FTO might mediate FSP1 regulation via AS-IV. Although our data demonstrated that AS-IV does not significantly alter the expression levels of other m6A-related proteins (e.g., METTL3, METTL14, ALKBH5), indirect functional or regulatory effects could still occur. For instance, AS-IV might influence the enzymatic activity, subcellular localization, or co-factor interactions (e.g., with WTAP or RBM15) of METTL3/METTL14, thereby modulating m6A methyltransferase complex assembly or function. Additionally, post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation or ubiquitination could also alter the stability or activity of these proteins without affecting their total expression. Furthermore, targeted inhibition of FTO protein by AS-IV might shift the dynamic equilibrium of m6A deposition and removal, potentially triggering compensatory feedback loops or competitive interactions among m6A regulators. More efforts are required to elucidate these indirect mechanisms in future studies. In addition, the fate of m6A-modified mRNAs was controlled by m6A recognition proteins. In regarding of the downregulation of FSP1 mRNA with increased m6A modification and impaired mRNA stability caused by AS-IV, we speculated that the m6A binding protein YTHDF2, which is responsible for recognizing and degrading m6A modified mRNAs [61, 62], might be involved in the repression of FSP1 expression by AS-IV. RIP assay further elucidated an interaction between the YTHDF2 protein and FSP1 mRNA. Furthermore, an mRNA stability assay verified YTHDF2-mediated FSP1 mRNA degradation. Therefore, we hypothesized that AS-IV decreased the demethylase FTO, leading to an increase in the m6A modification level on FSP1 mRNA, which enhances the recognition by the YTHDF2 protein, ultimately causing the degradation and downregulation of FSP1 expression.

Although we have focused on the mechanism of AS-IV action through m6A-mediated degradation of FSP1 in the current work, there may be other potential mechanisms involved in the anti-cancer effects of AS-IV. For example, in hepatocellular carcinoma, AS-IV can inhibit tumor progression by regulating KAT2A-mediated succinylation of PGAM1 [63], or continually suppressing the development of fibrosis and regulating pSmad3C/3L and Nrf2/HO-1 pathways [64]. To further explore the anti-tumor effect of AS-IV in GC, the transcriptomic or proteomic analyses are warranted to identify any unintended molecular targets or signaling pathways affected by AS-IV treatment, which will offer a more comprehensive view of the potential therapeutic effects of AS-IV.

As a natural terpenoid compound extracted from TCM Astragalus membranaceus, AS-IV exhibits a favorable safety profile with no significant systemic or organ-specific toxicity observed at therapeutic doses. It may be integrated into current GC treatment protocols with the following strategies for future studies. At first, AS-IV can be combined with conventional chemotherapy (e.g., 5-fluorouracil, cisplatin) or targeted therapies to enhance their therapeutic efficacy. For instance, combining AS-IV with HER2-targeted therapies in HER2-positive GC patients may offer a synergistic approach to improve treatment outcomes and overcome resistance. Synergizing AS-IV with platinum-based chemotherapy can allow dose reduction of cytotoxic drugs and improvement of cisplatin resistance while maintaining efficacy and improving tolerability. Notably, AS-IV in combination with the FSP1 selective inhibitor iFSP1 may exert synergistic anti-gastric cancer effects, offering a novel strategy for the treatment of gastric cancer. In addition, AS-IV treatment may also serve as an adjuvant therapy, either in conjunction with conventional therapies or as a maintenance therapy following initial treatment to prevent tumor recurrence, thus improving the outcome of patients with gastric cancer. While our results indicate that AS-IV has no significant influence on immune cell infiltration within TME, its ferroptosis-inducing activity may prime gastric cancer for immune checkpoint blockade therapy, such as the application of anti-PD-1/PD-L1. Therefore, AS-IV displays significant translational potential in GC therapy due to its favorable safety profile, anti-tumor activity, and capacity to synergize with other conventional treatments.

However, translating AS-IV into clinical application to treat GC requires careful consideration of dosing regimens and delivery methods. Multiple studies have shown its use in a variety of therapeutic contexts, primarily for immune modulation and anti-inflammatory effects [65, 66], whereas the specific dosing required for its anti-GC effects has yet to be established. Future studies should focus on determining the optimal dose and frequency for AS-IV, based on preclinical pharmacokinetic studies. Another challenge is the effective target delivery of AS-IV. Encapsulating AS-IV into nanoparticles or liposomes might be alternative delivery methods that can improve its solubility, stability, and targeted delivery to tumor regions. Notably, toxicity is a critical consideration for AS-IV being translated from preclinical models to human use. Despite that AS-IV has been shown to have relatively low toxicity, clinical trials are required in the future to carefully definite the appropriate dosage and frequency, and the long-term safety profile of AS-IV in patients with GC.

Concluding Remarks

Our study demonstrated that AS-IV acts as a tumor suppressor in GC through ferroptosis induction via the m6A–FTO/YTHDF2–FSP1 pathway. Our findings highlight an alternative therapeutic approach for the management of GC.
