Unravelling the role of natural herbs for the management of diabetes mellitus
Authors: Sayed Mohammad Firdous, Zainab Irfan, Sumon Giri, Ngutanu Sandrine Asaba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Insulin resistance or insufficiency causes hyperglycemia, which is a hallmark of diabetes mellitus, a chronic metabolic disease. Due to their poor long-term efficacy and frequent side effects, conventional antidiabetic medications have sparked interest in alternative therapy. Many bioactive substances, including flavonoids, alkaloids, terpenoids, and polyphenols, have antidiabetic properties by protecting pancreatic β-cells, reducing oxidative stress, blocking enzymes responsible for the breakdown of carbohydrates, and improving insulin secretion and sensitivity. In both type 1 and type 2 diabetes models, herbs such as Bauhinia variegate, Caesalpinia sappan, Prunus persica, and Syzygium cumini have demonstrated encouraging outcomes. Additionally, by neutralizing reactive oxygen species, natural antioxidants can reduce the problems associated with diabetes. Even though clinical data is increasing, more research is necessary to guarantee therapeutic efficacy, safety, and uniformity. A comprehensive approach to diabetes treatment may be provided by combining herbal medicine with tailored lifestyle changes. This review investigates how natural herbs and phytochemicals produced from plants can help mitigate diabetes and its complications.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Diabetes is a chronic illness that emerges due to the fluctuations of glucose levels, due from insulin dysfunction, which can be caused by inadequate insulin production or utilisation due to ineffective pancreatic function [1]. Increasing prevalence rates and significant repercussions on public health make it a global health concern [2]. Diabetes mellitus (DM) is well known to boost the likelihood of developing several severe and potentially fatal health complications arising from the disruption and damage of organs such as the kidneys, heart, skin, blood vessels, and nerves. These can result in both macrovascular and microvascular difficulties leading to neuropathy, diabetic vision loss, renal disease, atherosclerosis, hypertension, as well as stroke, which are reported to be the major reasons for mortality in diabetic patients [3].

Numerous anti-diabetic drugs are currently available to treat hyperglycaemia. These medications primarily function by enhancing insulin responsiveness, augmenting insulin, boosting insulin secretion, and promoting glucose utilization. However, the anti-diabetic medications metformin and sulfonylureas have some adverse reactions, including excess lactic acid in the blood, diarrhoea (which metformin exhibits) and liver failure, increased body weight, tachyarrhythmia, and underactive thyroid (which sulfonylureas exhibit) [4]. Natural products are currently being explored as a potential substitute in the investigation and creation of new drugs. Plants are thought to be one of the most dependable sources of disease-curing substances since ancient times, and many synthetic medications are either directly or indirectly produced from them [5]. Among the biggest benefits of medicinal plants lies in their vast chemical diversity. Plant-derived compounds, such as polyphenols, alkaloids, flavonoids, and terpenoids, have many different biological functions, including anti-diabetic properties [6]. These bioactive components are believed to ameliorate diabetic syndromes through six main mechanisms of action, including enhanced insulin secretion and sensitivity, muscle cell and adipose tissue glucose uptake, inhibition of intestinal glucose absorption and hepatocyte glucose production, and anti-inflammatory qualities [7]. There have been numerous reports of the potential anti-diabetic effects of herbal plants, including Momordica charantia, Momordica cymbalaria, Azadirachta indica, Zingiber officinale, Syzygium cumini, Cinnamomum burmannii, and Ocimum basilicum. The antidiabetic effect has been reported to be attributed to bioactive substances like alkaloids, triterpenoids, and flavonoids. Momordica charantia contains biologically active substances called vicine, polypeptide-p, and charantin, which are proven to enhance intestinal glucose absorption, enhance glucose uptake, and raise insulin secretion [8]. It has also been speculated that Momordica cymbalaria and Zingiber officinale's bioactive components cause the hypoglycemia effect [9, 10]. In addition, the bioactive chemicals found in Azadirachta indica are said to exhibit potential effects on insulin-sensitization and insulin-mimetic, as well as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and β-cell regeneration properties [11]. Several bioactive substances found in Syzygium cumini, also referred to as jamun, including jamboline, ellagitannins, and anthocyanins, have been demonstrated to improve insulin production and boost cells' absorption of glucose. They can also reduce oxidative stress and shield pancreatic cells from injury, which is crucial for the generation of insulin [12]. The anti-diabetic benefits of Cinnamomum burmannii have garnered interest mostly because of its active ingredient, cinnamaldehyde. By improving glucose metabolism, lowering gluconeogenic enzymes, and increasing insulin sensitivity, it is claimed to lower blood glucose levels [13]. Ocimum basilicum exhibits benefits against diabetes via multiple mechanisms, including enhanced antioxidant qualities, elevated insulin synthesis, and enhanced glucose absorption [14].

To fully understand the safety profiles, efficacy, recommended dosage schedules, underlying mechanisms of action, and long-term effects of these medicinal plants and other herbal plants on the treatment of diabetes, more research is necessary. A promising approach in the search for effective diabetes management strategies is the investigation of medicinal plants possessing anti-diabetic properties. According to the 2022 American Diabetes Association (ADA) Standards of Medical Care in Diabetes, there is absolutely no clear evidence of either herbs or supplements benefiting diabetics without additional conditions. ADA recommends that persons with diabetes mellitus must essentially consult healthcare professionals before using any herbs or supplements, especially if they are already on other medications [15]. By improving the quality of life for persons who have diabetes, these programs might pave the path to the progress of innovative anti-diabetic drugs. This review emphasizes how oxidative stress is linked to the pathogenesis of diabetes mellitus. In addition, it explores the various plants and phytoconstituents that could be used medicinally as sources for new anti-diabetic medications and how they should be produced.

Methodology

The study acquired articles on antidiabetic phytochemicals found in medicinal plants by conducting a thorough literature search using electronic databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Terms including ‘antidiabetic phytochemicals,’ ‘medicinal plants,’ ‘diabetes,’ ‘mechanism of action,’ and ‘therapeutic potential’ were among those used in the search. The research required to look into the mechanism of action, assess the therapeutic potential of medicinal plants in the treatment of diabetes, and concentrate on phytochemicals with antidiabetic properties. Non-peer-reviewed papers without an antidiabetic focus, and studies without a clear mechanism of action or therapeutic evaluation were the exclusion criteria. The chemical structures used in the table were drawn using ChemDraw. In addition, other structures used were taken from PubChem. This approach reduces the possibility of errors that could occur from hand drawing while guaranteeing accuracy and uniformity across all structures.

Pathogenesis of diabetes mellitus

The hormone insulin plays a crucial role in the metabolism of glucose by encouraging the body to use glucose and lowering blood sugar levels [16]. Diabetes mellitus (DM) develops when the body has too much blood sugar and its ability to use insulin is hindered. Depending on the level of insulin dependence, it can be categorised as either insulin- or non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus, sometimes referred to as type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), respectively [17]. Insulin insufficiency is caused by the autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells by CD4 + and CD8 + T cells and macrophages in type 1 diabetes [18]. Approximately 85 percent of patients have islet cell antibodies, and the majority of these antibodies work in contrast to the glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) present in the pancreatic β-cells [19]. Insulin secretion is affected due to the immune system attacking the pancreatic islets of Langerhans, which results in metabolic disorders associated with T1DM. Additionally, in individuals with T1DM, pancreatic α-cells begin to malfunction and produce a disproportionate amount of glucagon, aggravating the metabolic abnormalities already brought on by insulin insufficiency [20]. Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a dangerous disease, can strike patients with diabetes. Blood becomes acidic due to the body's accelerated breakdown of fat, which the liver converts into ketones (Fig. 1). Accelerated fasting plasma glucose levels are accompanied by a very slow progression of the disease. The specific tissues inability to react appropriately to insulin is also brought on by a decrease in the glucokinase enzyme in the liver and the GLUT-4 transporter protein in adipose tissue [21].Fig. 1Pathophysiology of Diabetes Mellitus

Pathophysiology of Diabetes Mellitus

About 90–95% of cases of diabetes are type 2 diabetes (T2DM), which is predominantly driven by insulin resistance and involves several intricate processes relating to oxidative stress (OS), lipid and glucose metabolism, inflammation and immunology [22]. According to Yaribeygi et al., OS is the outcome of an imbalance between the antioxidant system’s capacity to neutralise free radical production [23]. This imbalance reduces insulin sensitivity and contributes various biological mechanisms that cause to the development of type 2 diabetes. OS induces lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, and mitochondrial failure in T2DM patients, which is crucial for the pathophysiology of several diabetes problems [24, 25]. The endoplasmic reticulum's and mitochondria's interactions, sometimes called mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs), have important implications in regulating lipid exchange, calcium signalling, cell survival and cellular metabolic homeostasis. Many insulin-signalling proteins, including AKT kinase, mTORC2, PP2A, and PTEN, are known to be present in these MAM contacts and to play a role in insulin signalling. An increasing amount of research has demonstrated that these MAMs contribute to T2DM by impairing the function of the β cells that produce insulin leading to insulin resistance in peripheral organs [26, 27]. Due to changes in T cell and macrophage proliferation as well as compromised NK and β cell activity in T2DM patients, the pathophysiology of the disease is believed to be associated with both innate and adaptive immunity [28]. Reduced physical activity and exercise training, along with an increase in sedentary behaviour, are associated with enhanced markers of chronic systemic inflammation in T2DM [29]. In this case, pro-inflammatory substances such as interleukin 1 (IL-1), tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF), C-reactive protein (CRP), and interleukin 6 (IL-6) are produced into the bloodstream and into certain organs, causing metabolic inflammation [30]. By decreasing β-cell activity and triggering the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated β cells (NF-β) transcription factor, which damages and kills β-cells, IL-1 contributes to the pancreatic autoimmune response. The pathophysiology of diabetes is thus largely dependent on oxidative stress, inflammation, immunology, hyperglycaemia, and insulin resistance [3].

Potential anti-diabetic medicinal plants

The literature review states that while biguanides, sulfonylureas, α-glucosidase inhibitors, and glinides have all been used to treat diabetes and its complications, significant barriers remain, including side effects associated with their use. Diabetes is currently treated and managed with insulin and some oral hypoglycemic medications, including metformin, acarbose, repaglinide, sitagliptin, and dapagliflozin. However, these medications are typically linked to several adverse effects. They are designed to stimulate insulin secretion and improve insulin sensitivity while also lowering blood glucose levels by enhancing glucose uptake or excretion in adipose tissue. These include, but are not limited to, weight gain, hypoglycemia, gastrointestinal disorders, liver damage, renal failure, hypersensitivity reactions, diarrhea, flatulence, and bloating in the abdomen [31]. In consideration of the aforementioned, a number of these herbal plants are reviewed in this communication, exhibiting potential in the management and prevention of diabetes (Fig. 2). However, research is required to ensure whether long-term consumption of this medicinal plant in diabetic patients is safe or harmful, as the level of toxicity varies depending on many factors, such as method of extraction, species, dosage, route of administration and victim susceptibility. For example, research has shown that excessive quantities of Erythrina abyssinica seeds are toxic and result in paralysis, unconsciousness, and even death from respiratory failure [32].Fig. 2Potential Herbal Plants with Antidiabetic Properties

Potential Herbal Plants with Antidiabetic Properties

Bauhinia variegate

Bauhinia variegate, an ancient medicinal plant, has been used to treat various illnesses due to its phytoconstituents, including glycosides, flavonoids, and steroids. The leaves contain these compounds, which have been found to have anti-inflammatory effects, antioxidants, antibacterial, antifungal and anti-hyperlipidaemic properties. The ethanolic extract of the leaves has been found to stimulate insulin release in an insulin-secreting cell line. Bauhinia variegata leaves also contain insulin-like proteins that can reduce blood glucose levels. The flower extract of Bauhinia variegate shows potential as an antioxidant and α-amylase inhibitory agent. Blood glucose and HbA1c levels were significantly reduced in diabetic rats treated with an ethanolic extract of Bauhinia variegate at a dose of 200 and 400 mg/kg for 28 days in a streptozotocin-associated diabetic neuropathy and neurodegeneration [33–40].

Caesalpinia sappan

One member of the Caesalpiniaceae family of medicinal plants is Caesalpinia sappan. It is also referred to as Indian redwood, Brazil wood, or sappan wood [41]. Brazilin is a highly significant bioactive natural component from the heartwood of Caesalpinia sappan, with a wide range of industrial uses. According to Vij et al. (2023), this plant extract is not only safe for the environment but also non-toxic to humans [42]. According to Taif et al. (2019), brazilin is used in traditional Chinese medicine to treat enhanced blood circulation, encourage menstruation, and have analgesic effects [43]. A glucose tolerance test using an ethanol extract from wood exhibited hypoglycemic action [44]. Brazilin's antidiabetic action was demonstrated in a recent investigation by Ahmad et al. (2020) by the inhibition of Dipeptidyl peptidase IV [45]. Using the in vitro α-glucosidase enzyme inhibition method and in vivo albino Wistar rat model, the antidiabetic efficacy of the ethanolic extract of Caesalpinia sappan was assessed. At 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg body weight, there was a considerable decrease in blood glucose and HbA1c levels in alloxan-induced diabetic rats after 21 days, according to the histopathological and blood parameter analysis [46].

Christia vespertilionis

Island pea, mariposa, and butterfly wing are among the popular names for Christia vespertilionis, an attractive plant in the Fabaceae family. This herbaceous shrub is native to Southeast Asia and Brazil. It is widely distributed in Malaysia, where the locals refer to it as “Rerama” [47]. The C. vespertilionis leaf extract has demonstrated potential action, according to preliminary anti-inflammatory and anti-cancer investigations carried out by the Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences of UPM's Scientific Research group. Apart from that, C. vespertilionis leaves are also well-known for their traditional use because of their anticancer qualities [48]. The highest levels of α-glucosidase inhibitory activity and ferric-reducing antioxidant power have been identified in the hexane: ethyl acetate extract of C. vespertilionis, according to Murugesu et al. (2020). Given that these are the two key characteristics needed to treat diabetes, chemicals found in C. vespertilionis leaf extracts may be in charge of their α-glucosidase inhibitory and antioxidant properties, which might indicate an antidiabetic effect [49].

Hybanthus enneaspermus

The perennial small herb Hybanthus enneaspermus is belongs to Violaceae family [50]. It treats conditions including aphrodisiac, anti-infertile, anti-arthritic, CNS, anti-ulcer and antisecretory, in-vitro aldose reductase, hypolipidemic, antidiabetic, and antibacterial. It is also utilized in ayurveda, Siddha, and other traditional remedies. The antidiabetic potential of Hybanthus enneaspermus was reported by the significant reduction in blood glucose levels observed in diabetic rats given alcoholic extract at doses of 125, 250, and 500 mg/kg; for 21 days [51–56]. The anti-urolithiatic and antidiabetic effects of the generated silver nanoparticles were investigated in vitro using stem bark extracts from Hybanthus enneaspermus. The study found that silver nanoparticles prevent the action of free radical scavenging, alpha-glucosidase reduces blood glucose levels, and alpha-amylase slows the breakdown of carbohydrates. Overall, it was noted that under in-vitro conditions, the extract exhibited the highest inhibitory activity of antidiabetic, anti-urolithiatic, and antioxidant properties [57].

Irvingia gabonensis

Commonly referred to as “bush mango,” Irvingia gabonensis is an African plant species. It has been utilized for both nutritional and therapeutic purposes [58]. In addition to the seeds being used to produce “African soup” and supposedly having anti-diabetic qualities, the plant's leaves are traditionally used to cure diabetes. It has been established through existing experimental evidence that Irvingia leaf and stem extracts regulate lipid profiles and blood sugar levels [59]. Irvingia gabonensis seeds were demonstrated to lower plasma lipids and raise HDL cholesterol in a clinical investigation [60]. A randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical research revealed similar outcomes, showing that those who took Irvingia gabonensis for 90 days experienced decreased blood glucose, blood cholesterol, and waist circumference [61].

Merremia tridentate

Merremia tridentata (M. tridentata), belongs to the Convolvulaceae family, is a naturally occurring perennial herb with a thick rootstock that spreads over hedges and open wastelands in Vietnam, particularly in the country’s northern mountainous regions [62]. Urinary tract conditions, rheumatism, pain, weakness, hemiplegia, haemorrhoids, swelling, laxatives, and toothaches have all been treated with the plant in the past. Its flavour is tonic, astringent, and bitter. Furthermore, M. tridentata is used to treat diabetes, purpura, and malaria [63–65]. In alloxan-induced diabetic mice, Van et al., (2022) reported that it tastes bitter, astringent, and tonic in contrast to the reference drugs, metformin and glibenclamide. In addition, M. tridentata is also used to treat malaria, purpura, and diabetes. Furthermore, in contrast to the reference drug acarbose, stem-ethanol extract and flavonoid-rich fraction shown strong α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, respectively. Nevertheless, to develop the extract as a powerful antidiabetic medication, further extensive research on chronic models is required to clarify the precise mode of action of the extract and its constituents [66].

Prunus persica

China is most likely the place of origin of Prunus persica (Family: Rosaceae), which subsequently travelled westward via Asia to the Mediterranean region and eventually reached Europe. Numerous plant parts have a broad range of pharmacological effects, such as those related to the gastrointestinal tract, the immune system, vascular health, antitussive, acetylcholine esterase inhibition, antimicrobial, antidiabetic, analgesic, cytotoxic, antioxidant, antiparasitic, and many more [67, 68]. The antidiabetic activity of a quercetin-rich ethyl acetate extract of Prunus persica leaves (100 and 200 mg/kg, orally) was evaluated in a streptozotocin-induced diabetic rat model. Sharma et al. (2018) found that giving diabetic rats a 200 mg/kg dosage of an ethyl acetate extract high in quercetin significantly decreased blood sugar levels and raised body weight [69].

The potential of Prunus persica flower extract components to increase insulin secretion was examined in a glucose-stimulated insulin secretion study using INS-1 rat pancreatic β-cells. Methyl caffeine affected glucose-stimulated insulin production that was similar to gliclazide. The phosphorylation of the total insulin receptor substrate-2 (IRS-2), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), and Akt, which affect β-cell function and insulin secretion, was also elevated, as were the related signalling proteins of the activated pancreatic and duodenal homeobox-1 (PDX-1) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPAR-γ) [70].

Quercus coccifera

One species in the Fagaceae family that is commonly cultivated in Jordan is Quercus coccifera, additionally referred to as Kermes Oak [71]. A variety of ailments, including haemorrhage, persistent diarrhoea, injuries, and burns, have been treated using the decoction of various parts made from Kermes oak [72, 73]. As per the findings of Jaber (2023), the most effective antidiabetic activity was observed in both in vitro and in Vivo of the methanolic extract, which contains the principal chemical compounds with potential antidiabetic activity. Furthermore, boiling water extract showed encouraging biological activity and a moderate potential to lower blood sugar levels [74]. In-vitro analysis of the phenolic content of African Q. coccifera acorns revealed that it might have antidiabetic properties [75]. Furthermore, in 2021, Mingxia Wu and associates discovered that Q. coccifera extracts significantly affected dipeptidyl peptidase IV, an enzyme necessary for blood glucose control [76].

Stevia rebaudiana Bertoni

Stevia rebaudiana Bertoni, belonging to the chrysanthemum or aster family. It is mostly found growing in Paraguay’s Amambay Mountain range [77]. It has immunomodulatory, gastro- and renal-protective, anti-oxidant, anti-tumour, anti-hyperglycemic, anti-diarrheal, and diuretic properties [78]. Recent research indicates that stevia has antioxidant properties and increases the release of insulin via acting on the beta cells in the pancreas [79, 80]. Additionally, it enhances the expression of the protein, glucose uptake, and glucose transporter type (Glut) 4 gene [81]. Stevioside has a positive effect on hyperglycemia by decreasing the duodenum's absorption of glucose and blocking glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, according to results from studies on the anti-hyperglycemic effects of S. rebaudiana conducted on rats and humans [82].

Syzygium cumini,

Growing over Southeast Asia, the Indian subcontinent, Queensland, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and China, Syzygium cumini is a tropical evergreen plant. It is a member of the Myrtaceae family. Jamun, Java Plum, and Black Plum are some of its common names [83]. Based on the latest research, the ability of S. cumini seed to heal or promote health is attributed to the bioactivity of several phytochemicals, such as oleic acid, hexadecenoic acid, corilagin, and eicosane [84]. S. cumini is a herb that has been used for many years in traditional and folk medicine. It deals with various problems such as regulating blood sugar, liver protection, and digestive disorders. Rizvi et al. (2022) report that the seeds, both dried and powdered, are widely utilized in treating diabetes. Studies have demonstrated that the seeds of S. cumini are abundant in flavonoids and polyphenolic compounds, including ellagic acid, gallic acid, ascorbic acid, caffeic acid, and synergic acid. Additionally, these compounds have anti-diabetic attributes because they inhibit intestinal α-glucosidases [85]. In addition, GLUT4-mediated enhancement of glucose translocation and enhanced glucose uptake is characteristic of these phytochemicals, particularly gallic acid and kaempferol [86].

Senna auriculata

The tall, evergreen shrub Senna auriculata (Fabaceae), sometimes referred to as “Tanner's Cassia” or “Avaram Senna,” grows quickly and has numerous branches [87]. Senna auriculata (L.) Roxb., or Avaram, offers therapeutic uses [88]. It is utilized to control cholesterol metabolism [89] and cure diabetes [90]. Rather than using regular tea, diabetic patients are served tea made from flowers and buds. According to Nambirajan et al. (2018), the seeds of this species also have anti-diabetic properties [91]. It has been observed that the flower part of this plant contains proanthocyanidins, β-sitosterol, and flavonoids. Bioactive anti-diabetic principles include terpenoids [92], phenolic acids [93], sterols [94], and flavonoids [92]. It is known that flavonoids can repair damaged beta cells. Antihyperglycemic medicines that work well are discovered to be phenolic compounds. The inhibitory potential of alpha-amylase and glycosidase presents a potential therapeutic strategy for treating diabetes [95].

Sphagneticola trilobata

Sphagneticola trilobata is a creeping ornamental plant in the Asteraceae family. Several classification changes have occurred for this species, including those to Compalaya, Acmella, and Wedelia before it was formally assigned the name Sphagneticola [96]. In S. trilobata, several metabolites have been found, including fatty acids, phenolic compounds, terpenes, and sterols. The various pharmacological benefits attributed to these bioactive chemicals include wound healing, antidiabetic activity, hepatoprotective, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties [97]. Oral administration of 250 mg/kg body weight of S. trilobata flower ethanolic extract to diabetic rats for eight weeks significantly reduced the rats' fasting blood glucose and cholesterol levels compared to the diabetic control group, indicating that the extract may have antidiabetic potential [98].

Suillellus luridus

Suillellus luridus, formerly known as Boletus luridus, is a member of the Boletaceae family and genus Suillellus. It is mostly found in southwest China, particularly in the provinces of Yunnan and Sichuan. S. luridus has an array of biologically active compounds in addition to having low levels of crude fat and high levels of protein and carbohydrates [99]. The anti-diabetic properties of three polysaccharides that were isolated from Suillellus luridus using hot water extraction and ethanol precipitation were examined in diabetic rats that were administered streptozotocin (STZ). Using diabetic lab rats treated with STZ as test subjects, the study sought to determine the herb's antidiabetic properties. The mice treated with STZ showed notable protective, antihyperglycemic, and antihyperlipidemic effects [100].

Tephrosia bracteolate

Tephrosia bracteolata, a member of the Fabaceae family, is a common plant in tropical Africa that grows in an open area. According to Emeka et al. (2023), it is typical in both the southern and northern regions of Nigeria [101]. Previous research has confirmed the scientific validity of using Tephrosia bracteolata leaf preparation for the treatment of diabetes in Nigeria [102]. Another study looked at the antioxidant, antidiabetic, and antibacterial qualities of Tephrosia bracteolate leaf extracts. This study examined the plant's n-hexane extract's capacity to inhibit the alpha-glucosidase enzyme. The results showed the plant extract's potent alpha-glucosidase inhibitory activities, which further illustrated the extract's potent antibacterial, antidiabetic, and antioxidant qualities[103].

Urtica dioica

Due to its capacity to reduce blood sugar, Urtica Dioica (UD) is a plant that has great promise for treating diabetes, regulate blood lipid levels, reduce inflammation, and combat oxidation [104]. It has been demonstrated that UD leaves are useful for stimulating insulin secretion and serving as an insulin imitator. Additional research revealed that UD extract might inhibit the activities of alpha-glucosidase enzymes and can promote cell growth [105]. Rats with diabetes caused by alloxan received an intraperitoneal injection of the ZnO extract in a study reported by Bayrami et al., (2020), and the impact on blood levels of insulin, total triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and total cholesterol was evaluated. The outcomes were then contrasted with ZnO, UD leaf extract, and insulin impacts on the same parameters. Because of the strong synergistic interaction between the ZnO—U. dioica extract components, the rats receiving this therapy showed the best antidiabetic performance out of all the investigated treatments[106].

Role of oxidative stress and antioxidants in diabetes mellitus

Oxidative stress and diabetes mellitus

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are the results of increased mitochondrial activity that causes oxidative stress in cells. Intracellular signalling is facilitated by the cellular response when ROS are low. But if the ROS levels are high enough, cell death might happen [117]. It has also been suggested that elevated ROS and oxidative stress are the main causes of insulin resistance and diabetes mellitus. According to recent research, hyperglycemia activates several molecular pathways, includes increased hexosamine pathway hyperactivation, polyol pathway flux, autoxidation of glucose, increased protein kinase C (PKC) activation, and AGE formation, which all contribute to oxidative stress through ROS overproduction [118, 119]. Therefore, individuals with diabetes exhibit a state of heightened oxidative stress, marked by raised levels of glycosylation and peroxidation, in addition to a diminished antioxidant system [120]. It is hypothesized that the oxidative stress caused by hyperglycemia may damage insulin signalling, leading to insulin resistance and the development of problems associated with diabetes [121]. Furthermore, research suggests that ROS hyperproduction might lead to insulin resistance and impaired pancreatic β-cell function, which can ultimately result in hyperglycemia. Both insulin resistance and β-cell activity reduction are known to contribute to persistent hyperglycemia. Insulin production is impacted by pancreatic β-cell malfunction, but insulin resistance occurs when target tissues lose their sensitivity to insulin despite normal insulin secretion [122].

Antioxidant and diabetes mellitus

The excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the oxidation of biomolecules can cause oxidative stress, which antioxidants can lessen or prevent. Typically, the body uses a variety of enzymes, such as glutathione peroxidase (GPx), catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutases (SOD), along with some non-enzymatic antioxidants, such as ubiquinones, vitamins C and E, uric acid (UA), glutathione (GSH), and bilirubin (BIL), to protect itself against oxidative stress. Excess ROS production is suppressed or eliminated by this defense mechanism. The main regulator of H2O2 (generated by SOD and other biological activities), the CAT is mostly present in peroxisomes and is known to shield β-cells [123]. Unfortunately, diabetes impairs or causes these regular antioxidant defense systems to malfunction [124, 125]. For example, patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) and diabetic comorbidities have lower plasma SOD activity and lower GSH concentrations [126]. Therefore, to overcome the stress circumstances, external supplies of antioxidants are needed. Additionally, antioxidants from diets rich in folic acid, vitamins A, E, and C, flavonoids, and nutritional polyphenols have been shown in recent research to have ameliorative effects on diabetes and its consequences [127]. Since the natural antioxidants in food—mostly vitamins have a reputation for stimulating the body's antioxidant system, which can scavenge free radicals, this could be an additional intriguing strategy to fight oxidative stress. Research suggests that Chrysobalanus icaco antioxidant-rich aqueous extract helps reduce blood sugar levels [128].

Often found in berries, red wine, peanuts, and grapes, resveratrol is a kind of polyphenolic molecule with strong anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. According to Huang et al. (2020), it has protective properties through scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), inhibiting nuclear factor-κB, and boosting antioxidant enzymes such as SOD [129]. In a different study, resveratrol administration (10–20 mg/kg) in rats given STZ-induced diabetic neuropathy had an ameliorative effect on diabetic neuropathy by lowering oxidative stress and associated biomarker (MDA), peroxynitrite, DNA fragmentation, and amplifying CAT levels [130].

Quercetin is a member of the flavonoid class and is mostly present in green tea, apples, broccoli, onions, grapes, and other plants. Research has also indicated that quercetin may be beneficial for several illnesses, such as cancer, neurological conditions, and cardiovascular ailments [131]. Because quercetin has anti-hyperglycemic and anti-oxidative qualities, it has been shown to have nephroprotective benefits by considerably reducing renal abnormalities such as glomerulosclerosis and normalizing the levels of urea, creatinine, and uric acid in a diabetic model [132].

Phenols are very low molecular weight compounds with a wide range of advantageous properties, including the preservation of organs, hypoglycemia and hypolipidemia, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, and more. An investigation conducted by Li et al. suggests that luteolin may aid with diabetic cardiomyopathy by lowering inflammation. It has also been shown that kaempferol reduces diabetic neuropathy utilizing an oxidative effect [133]. Puerarin was also found to have the potential to regulate blood sugar by blocking gluconeogenesis[134].

Thus, by neutralizing lipid free radicals, natural antioxidants—also referred to as chain-breaking antioxidants—form stable compounds. These include vitamins, flavonoids, carotenoids, bioactive proteins, polysaccharides, and catechins that can target metabolic processes linked to diabetes. Also, vitamins such as Vitamin B, alpha-tocopherol (Vitamin E), and ascorbic acid (Vitamin C) are essential for the treatment of diabetes. Hydroxycinnamic derivatives, which are present in foods like wheatgerm, corn and green leaves, reduce glycohemoglobin by increasing adiponectin secretion and controlling TNFα expression. Diabetes management via herbs involves different strategies to modulate glucose metabolism and insulin activity. These approaches provide a variety of therapeutic modalities for controlling blood sugar levels, improving insulin activity, and safeguarding pancreatic cells in the treatment of diabetes. Inhibition of enzymes in carbohydrate digestion

α-glucosidase inhibition

An enzyme called α-glucosidase, found in the small intestine's mucosal brush boundary, helps break down carbohydrates. By delaying the digestion of carbohydrates, α-glucosidase inhibitors decrease the absorption of glucose and lessen the blood sugar rises that occur after meals. This process might be helpful in treating diabetes mellitus that is not insulin-dependent (NIDDM) [143, 144]. Nojirimycin [145] and 1-deoxynojirimycin [146] are two examples of α-glucosidase inhibitors that are present in plants that affect the blood glucose levels following food intake. In order to evaluate the molecular interactions between α-glucosidase and the inhibitors, Sgariglia et al. (2022) reported ellagic acid and its derivatives that were isolated from Caesalpinia paraguariensis and carried out in silico structure–activity relationship research. According to his research, at IC50 values of 87.3, 65.1, 73.03, and 263.05 µM, respectively, ellagic acid, 3-O-methylellagic, 3,3′-O-dimethylellagic acid, and 3,3′-O-dimethylellagic-4-O-β-D-xylopyranoside demonstrate potent α-glucosidase inhibitory effect. The values of acarbose are substantially higher than this [147].

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibition

Insulin is released when DPP-4 breaks down the incretin hormones glucose-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP). DPP-4 inhibitors increase insulin release and decrease glucagon production, extending the effects of these hormones and regulating blood sugar levels [148, 149]. According to current research, the garlic bulb has a strong DPP-IV inhibitory effectiveness (IC50 = 70.88 μg/ml) due to the presence of caffeic acid 3-glucoside, malonylgenistin, and calenduloside E, all of which have extremely high binding energies [150]. In contrast to vildagliptin (a common plant medication), the phytochemicals guggulsterone E, guggulsterone Z, and glucogallin that were isolated from Commiphora mukul exhibit strong DPP-IV inhibitory action. Furthermore, the DPP-IV target molecule is significantly inhibited by phytochemicals such as pzrogallol, beta-glucogallin, and gallic acid that have been isolated from the plant Phyllanthus emblica [151].

α-amylase inhibition

The presence of α-amylase in saliva and pancreatic juice helps in the breakdown of carbohydrates. Inhibiting α-amylase can help with glycemic management by preventing elevated postprandial blood glucose levels [152]. Luyen et al. (2019) investigated the Vietnamese medicinal plant Wedelia trilobata, which was well-known for its effectiveness in treating type 2 diabetes, as part of their search for novel anti-diabetic substances from plants. They found two novel ent-kaurane diterpenoids with α-amylase inhibitory properties, wedtrilosides A and B [153]. Verma et al. (2021) used computational research, in vivo trials, and in vitro experiments to assess the anti-diabetic properties of glochidon, a new triterpenoid isolated from Phyllanthus debilis. Glochidon demonstrated superior α-amylase inhibition in the in-vitro tests (IC50 = 38.15 μM), nearly matching that of acarbose. Furthermore, in rats with STZ-induced diabetes, it demonstrated dose-dependent hypoglycemic effects [154].

Enhancing insulin secretion and sensitivity

Increased insulin secretion

In pancreatic β-cells, elevated intracellular calcium levels are associated with insulin secretion. When voltage-dependent calcium channels are opened by the closure of ATP-sensitive potassium channels, the intracellular calcium ion concentration rises, and insulin production is stimulated. Through their effects on calcium channels or phosphodiesterase inhibition, phytochemicals such as p-methoxycinnamic acid can increase cAMP levels and promote insulin release [155, 156]. In the Cichorium intybus plant, two prominent antidiabetic phytoconstituents that can enhance glucose absorption in L6 muscle cells are chlorogenic acid and chicoric acid. Additionally, both phytochemicals can increase insulin production from the rat islets of Langerhans and the INS-1E insulin-secreting cell line [157].

Increased insulin sensitivity

Certain substances enhance glucose management by increasing cells' sensitivity to insulin. Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) signalling is triggered by insulin binding in muscle and fat cells, which attracts GLUT4 glucose transporters to the cell membrane. Glycemic regulation is enhanced due to increased glucose absorption via GLUT4 [158]. Garlic, high in organosulphur phytoconstituents like quercetin and allicin, has antihyperglycemic, renoprotective, and antioxidant properties. Both quercetin and allicin have critical roles in increasing insulin sensitivity and boosting glucose absorption [159]. The primary phytochemicals in broccoli, including glucosinolates, isothiocyanates, sulforaphane, sinapic acid, gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, apigenin, kaempferol, luteolin, quercetin, and myricetin, improve insulin sensitivity, lower inflammation, and fight oxidative stress, all of which aid in the management of diabetes. In addition, they shield the pancreatic β-cells and control glucose metabolism [160].

Nourishment and preservation of pancreatic β-cells

Diabetes development can be avoided by maintaining the mass and function of β-cells. Insulin, which is vital for the metabolism of glucose, is produced by β-cells; however, autoimmune reactions (type 1 diabetes) or oxidative stress and persistent hyperglycemia (type 2 diabetes) can harm these cells. To strengthen β-cells, anti-apoptotic gene expression is increased, inflammatory pathways like NF-kB are inhibited, and antioxidant defenses—both enzymatic and non-enzymatic—are strengthened. Reducing cell damage, promoting β-cell function, and maintaining insulin production help prevent hyperglycemia [161, 162]. It has been reported that Catharanthus roseus increases β-cell-mediated insulin production through actions on Ca2+ channels, hence reducing the risk of diabetes. Additionally, it has been demonstrated to improve insulin sensitivity, increase glucose metabolism, and safeguard pancreatic β-cells from oxidative damage. According to Kumar et al., (2022), the plant’s antihyperglycemic properties are believed to be attributed to gallic acid, rutin, coumaric acid, caffeic acid, quercetin, kaempferol, chlorogenic acid, ellagic acid, and coumarins [163]. Furthermore, onion greatly reduces oxidative stress and α-glucosidase activity, increases insulin production, and shields pancreatic β-cells [164]. Also, its ability to decrease cholesterol and glucose may be attributed to the presence of anthocyanins, apigenin, rutin, myricetin, kaempferol, catechin, resveratrol, and quercetin [165, 166].

Reduction of HbA1c and glycated plasma proteins

High blood sugar levels associated with diabetes cause protein glycation, which results in the formation of dangerous by-products such as advanced glycation end products (AGEs) and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c). By lowering these substances, glycation inhibitors can help avoid challenges from diabetes [167–169]. Despite significant undesirable impacts on the liver, berberine also demonstrated potential efficacy in comparison to rosiglitazone and metformin by lowering HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, and postprandial blood glucose [170]. In addition, streptozotocin-induced female reproductive diabetic rats were shown to have lower HbA1C and blood glucose levels following the administration of an ethanolic extract produced from the aerial section of the Matricaria chamomilla plant [171].

Enhancement of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1)

Insulin secretion is stimulated and glucagon release is inhibited by the hormone GLP-1, which is involved in glucose management. Additionally, it decreases hunger and slows stomach emptying. However, DPP-4 rapidly breaks down GLP-1, decreasing its potency. By raising intracellular calcium and ATP levels, GLP-1 receptor agonists improve insulin production and secretion by preventing DPP-4 breakdown. By encouraging the release of insulin and preventing the generation of glucagon, these agonists enhance glucose regulation [172, 173]. According to studies, geniposide from Gardenia jasminoides fruit enhances glucose-stimulated insulin production by activating the glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor (GLP-1R) in INS-1 cells and inhibits neuron death brought on by oxidative stress [174, 175]. In addition, berberine may help with the manifestations of diabetes by altering the gut flora, which causes the intestine to secrete GLP-1. Berberine also reduces cytochrome c migration out of the mitochondria and mitochondrial stress [176].

Regulation of GLUT-4

The glucose transporter 4 (GLUT-4) is essential for muscle and fat cells' insulin-stimulated glucose absorption. GLUT-4, which normally resides inside the cell, is deployed to the cell membrane when insulin binds to it or when you exercise. The approach entails turning on the insulin receptor and the signalling pathways that follow, including PI3K and Akt, which cause the translocation of GLUT-4. Improved glucose uptake and glucose homeostasis are supported by increased GLUT-4 activity [177, 178]. Utilized in traditional Uygur medicine, aloperine (ALO), a quinolizidine alkaloid derived from Sophora alopecuroides L., demonstrated notable efficacy in enhancing glucose absorption by specifically targeting GLUT4. ALO increases GLUT4 expression and translocation to the PM through the PI3K/Akt and G-protein-PLC-PKC pathways, which in turn increases glucose absorption. Type 2 diabetes mellitus was effectively prevented by administering ALO to diabetic rats in vivo [179]. From Trichosanthes dioica, a type of tetracyclic triterpenoid called cucurbitacin B was extracted. Due to the activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, Cucurbitacin B enhanced insulin tolerance and glucose consumption by translocating GLUT4 [180].

A single phytochemical or a group of phytochemicals has been linked to the antidiabetic effects of plant materials. Medicinal plants generate a variety of phytochemicals, including alkaloids, phenolic acids, flavonoids, glycosides, saponins, polysaccharides, stilbenes, and tannins, all of which are being extensively researched for their potential to cure diabetes. Many mechanisms, such as the regulation of lipid and glucose metabolism and insulin production, account for the beneficial effects of phytochemicals, NF-kB signalling pathway stimulation, inhibition of gluconeogenic enzymes and ROS, and activating β cells [181]. Effect of herbal plants in T1DM, T2DM, and gestational diabetes

The inability of the body to process and control blood glucose, either as a result of the pancreas secreting too much insulin or the insulin’s incapacity to control the blood glucose levels, is the cause of diabetes. Diabetes exists in three primary forms: type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and gestational diabetes (diabetes brought on by pregnancy) [194].

Hyperglycemia and insulin insufficiency are hallmarks of type 1 diabetes, a chronic autoimmune disease. A complete lack of insulin is the result of autoimmune beta-cell death in the pancreas. According to reports, over 90 plant compounds and extracts have been shown to help T1D through multiple mechanisms involving β cells and/or immune control. According to a summary of mechanistic research, flavonoids, terpenoids, and anthranoids can inhibit enzymes involved in energy metabolism, β-cells, immunity, and aldose reductase, as well as MAP kinases, NFκB, and/or IκB kinases [195, 196].

The inability of tissues to react appropriately to insulin and insufficient insulin secretion by the pancreatic β-cells are the two primary characteristics of type 2 diabetes. High blood sugar, obesity, hypertriglyceridemia, poor eating habits, inactivity, aging, family history, stress, anxiety, and depression are risk factors associated with this illness. Sesame oil, American ginseng, and cocoa flavanols are examples of plant secondary metabolites that are effective in reducing blood glucose levels and blood pressure, two important aspects of managing T2DM. Melissa officinalis efficiently lowers inflammation in diabetic individuals and prevents the disease. Indicating potential advantages for both illnesses, flaxseed oil and fish oil both decreased inflammatory markers and insulin levels. Additionally, bilberry extract demonstrated potential for enhancing T2DM patients' glucose control [197, 198].

Pregnant women who have high or elevated blood sugar are said to have gestational diabetes. Some women only experience it during pregnancy, and it can have an impact on both the mother and the unborn child. Acupuncture from traditional Chinese medicine and herbal remedies are examples of complementary and alternative medicine that many pregnant women use for symptom treatment or health maintenance, according to studies. For example, Orthosiphon stamineus and red raspberry tea showed promise in reducing blood sugar and mitigating the pathophysiology of gestational diabetes while posing the least amount of harm to the mother and fetus [199]. There has been little information on the long-term risk assessment of different antiglycemic formulations and strengths in human pregnancy, which is concerning because pregnant women are most concerned about the safety of therapies [200]. Thus, the effects of herbal plants on various forms of diabetes vary. The reports state that its effectiveness is higher in T2DM than in T1DM and gestational diabetes.

Effect of natural herbs on the macrovascular and microvascular complications of diabetes mellitus

Diabetes, if left untreated, can lead to several serious health issues that affect both small and large vessels, known as microvascular and macrovascular complications, respectively. The most expensive complication of diabetes is nephropathy, which also increases the risk of diabetic foot ulcers and/or amputations. Additionally, retinopathy and macrovascular conditions like stroke, peripheral artery disease, and coronary heart failure can cause blindness [2].

A typical microvascular consequence of diabetes that affects 85% of older people is retinopathy. Both renal insufficiency and the development of diabetes-related renal impairment are influenced by aging. White blood cell function is compromised by diabetes, which inhibits wound healing and reduces wound healing because of immunological impairment. The length of time and intensity of hyperglycemia are directly correlated with the likelihood of developing retinopathy. Elevated blood glucose causes sorbitol accumulation in cells by increasing the circulation of sugar molecules. The damage that hyperglycemia causes to cells may also be attributed to oxidative stress. Wild asparagus, fennel, and parsley are examples of herbal remedies that can help lower blood sugar and vascular dysfunction. Crushed fennel seeds can be combined with sugar and almonds, and wild asparagus, which is rich in glutathione and antioxidants, lowers the risk of cataracts and night blindness [201, 202]. One major cause of kidney failure is diabetic nephropathy, which, if left untreated, can lead to proteinuria and visible diabetic nephropathy. This transition occurs in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, and the kidneys are affected by metabolic abnormalities such as mesangial nodules, microaneurysm formation, and increased glomerular basement membrane width. The management of blood sugar is essential for lowering the risk of diabetic nephropathy. Antihypertensive drugs and rigorous management of elevated blood glucose levels are beneficial for patients with diabetic nephropathy. The main goals of herbal treatment for diabetic nephropathy are blood pressure reduction, blood sugar regulation, and dyslipidemia control. Animal models of diabetic nephropathy have been used to evaluate a variety of natural antioxidants, herbs, and plant extracts, including curcumin, catechins, and allicin [203]. Peripheral nerve anomalies in diabetics are known as diabetic neuropathy, and they can manifest in many ways, including as tactile, focal/multifocal, and autonomic neuropathy. Sexual dysfunction, muscular rigidity, resting tachycardia, silent infarction, diarrhea, anhidrosis, bladder disturbance, digestive issues, bowel issues, and even cardiogenic shock are all symptoms of autonomic neuropathy, a leading cause of morbidity and even death in diabetic patients. Diabetic neuropathy has no known cure, although there are a number of drugs that can be used to treat its symptoms. Diabetes management involves eating a balanced diet, avoiding processed foods, consuming as little starch and sugar as possible, drinking lots of water each day, and including lean proteins, foods high in fiber, and essential fats. To reduce pain from neuropathy, some organic oils and essential minerals, such as primrose oil, cinnamon, lavender, peppermint, or frankincense essential oils, can be taken every day [204, 205].

The main characteristics of diabetes-related macrovascular problems are disorders of the cerebrovascular structure, peripheral arteries, and coronary arteries. These side effects raise the risk of necrosis, myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular illness, and nerve entrapment. An estimated 34% of men and women with diabetes have heart disease or stroke, making cardiovascular disease the primary cause of illness and death in these individuals [206]. The macrovascular complications that result from high blood sugar, high levels of free fatty acids, and glucose intolerance include protein kinases, oxidative stress, and binding sites for glycation end products. Vascular constriction, elevated blood pressure, inflammation, thrombosis, hypercoagulation, platelet activation, and decreased fibrinolysis are all consequences of these problems, which ultimately result in atherosclerosis, the disease's underlying cause [207]. Patients who use aspirin may have a decreased risk of cardiovascular events. Conventional medical systems evaluate diabetes based on its evident symptoms, which include exhaustion, headaches, chest pain, and irregular heartbeats [208]. 25% of prescription drugs and 75% of new drugs used to treat dangerous illnesses are derived from natural plant resources. Studies have looked into how Nigella sativa may help with many medical conditions, including diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, high blood pressure, and obesity. It has also been utilized in traditional medicine [209]. Broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, cauliflower, radish, rutabaga, turnip, and baby spinach are examples of cruciferous vegetables that contain isothiocyanates, which can manufacture the redox-controlled cardioprotective protein thioredoxin. Arginyl-fructose and phenolic compounds found in temperature-ready onion concentrate have anticarcinogenic and postprandial blood glucose-lowering properties [210]. A very uncommon number of flavonoids, which are used to treat cardiovascular conditions like heart disease and the chest pain it causes, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol, may be found in Terminalia arjuna bark [211].

Interventions in diabetes mellitus

Diabetes, a chronic metabolic disease marked by increased blood glucose levels, requires behavior modification and lifestyle changes. In order to help patients, control their diabetes and improve their general health, non-pharmacological methods aim to provide them with the information and abilities they need to make healthy lifestyle choices [212]. Personalized lifestyle interventions that are tailored to each patient's particular requirements and preferences have demonstrated promising outcomes in the treatment of diabetes. Personalized lifestyle interventions have proven to be quite effective in a number of areas related to managing diabetes. By customizing meal plans, recommending physical activity, and creating all-encompassing treatment plans for each patient, these interventions successfully address unique obstacles and difficulties that are unique to each individual. This method successfully increases treatment compliance, promotes sustained behavioral change, and boosts patient involvement in self-care. Individualized dietary regimens, like glycaemic index-based meal plans and carb counting, have been demonstrated to help patients achieve better glycaemic control [213]. By implementing tailored physical activity recommendations, patients are guaranteed to engage in pleasurable and manageable workouts, which improves glucose management. Type 2 diabetes can be significantly increased by obesity, which also increases the likelihood of developing complications from the disease [214, 215]. By applying specialized techniques including portion control, calorie restriction, and physical activity regimens, tailored lifestyle therapies seek to address weight management. According to research, compared to generic therapies, the use of individualized techniques results in better long-term weight management outcomes and enhanced effectiveness in reaching weight loss goals. People with diabetes may experience psychological effects such as stress, anxiety, and sadness. To successfully address emotional barriers and improve psychological well-being, personalized lifestyle therapies usually include behavior modification strategies like motivational interviewing and cognitive-behavioral therapy. People are better equipped to manage the challenges of managing their diabetes when they receive individualized support and counseling [216, 217]. In order to improve treatment adherence and support long-term behavior change in the management of diabetes, it is imperative to employ behavior modification approaches. These methods place a high priority on empowering patients to embrace healthy behaviors, set reasonable objectives, and build self-efficacy. A crucial habit is self-monitoring, which includes recording physical activity, eating habits, and blood glucose levels on a regular basis. According to research, people who regularly track their progress are more likely to see reoccurring patterns and make wise decisions to improve their diabetes care [218]. Research has indicated that individualized food planning and exercise regimens can greatly enhance the treatment of diabetes. Improved glycaemic control and weight loss in people with type 2 diabetes have been associated with low-carb diets that emphasize nutrient-dense foods like proteins, healthy fats, and non-starchy vegetables [219]. Additionally, these diets lower the risk of heart disease. Fruits, vegetables, whole grains, nuts, and olive oil are all part of the Mediterranean diet, which has been linked to better glycaemic management and a lower risk of cardiovascular disease. For diabetics, these diets offer significant health benefits and can be tailored to personal preferences. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that tailored physical activity recommendations that take into account variables like age, fitness level, and comorbidities connected to diabetes improve physical fitness, optimize glucose management, and reduce cardiovascular risk factors in diabetic patients. All things considered, these individualized methods can greatly enhance diabetic care and general wellness [220].

Formulations for polyherbal antidiabetics

Nanoformulations, often referred to as nanoparticulate frameworks, are dispersions of colloidal substances with a very tiny size ranging between from 1—1000 nm, a substantial surface area relative to their volume, and the appearance and set of attributes that are dependent on the constituent parts and methods of processing [229]. In the last several years, medical research has focused a great deal of interest on the use of nanotechnology for both diagnosis and therapy [230]. Materials at the nanoscale have been demonstrated to exhibit special physical, chemical, and biological characteristics that make them valuable for a variety of biomedical applications [231]. In comparison to current drug delivery systems, the engineering of nanocarriers such as polymeric nanoparticles, metallic nanoparticles, liposomes, niosomes, micelles, dendrimers, nanostructured lipid carriers, and nanofabricated structures has been found to be significantly more effective in terms of potency, durability, bioavailability, bio-distribution, and drug release [232]. The necessity of creative fixes

Globally, diabetes has emerged as the primary cause of mortality and disability, making it one of the greatest health emergencies of the twenty-first century. Its estimated prevalence is 8.8%, with a predicted 10% increase by 2040. Researchers need to develop time-saving, financially acceptable, easily navigable, and feasible options and alternatives for delivering the best possible diabetes treatment, given the rising number of individuals with diabetes globally and the significant fraction of those individuals without access to specialized care. Therefore, we should work to provide appropriate diabetes care for those with limited access to the health care system, especially those who find it difficult to make regular onsite visits. Examples of these individuals include (1) those who live in rural areas where commuting time to a clinic is considerable; (2) those who are socially disadvantaged and find it difficult to integrate into the standard healthcare system; and (3) those who are incapable of regularly visiting medical facilities due to physical or mental impairments [234]. Currently, no suitable curative class of anti-diabetic drugs is known to exist; those that do are mostly employed to treat symptoms and have many side effects and drug interactions. These classes are not cost-effective, so it is difficult for the lower classes to afford them. Consequently, more investigation is needed to find novel anti-diabetic medication types.

Upcoming changes

A major global health issue, diabetes mellitus necessitates innovative approaches to improve patient outcomes. It has been difficult for conventional one-size-fits-all therapy techniques to address the condition's variable character. Personalised medicine has emerged as a revolutionary strategy. By adjusting treatment regimens to the particular genetic, lifestyle, and clinical traits of each patient, personalized medicine has the potential to improve glycemic control, encourage patient participation, and maximize treatment choices. Given the complexity of diabetes and the possibility that a standardized strategy won't produce the best results for every patient, this method must be put into practice. Cutting-edge medical innovations including genetic testing, telemedicine, and continuous glucose monitoring enable informed decision-making and encourage patient participation [235]. Additionally, efforts to develop a cure for diabetes are being advanced by the use of immunotherapy and β-cell regeneration, two of the most promising treatments to date, in the long-term management of the disease. In order to prevent and treat diabetes in the future, lifestyle modification and obesity therapy should be given priority in the treatment of prediabetes and diabetes mellitus [236].

Conclusion

A chronic metabolic disease with serious long-term implications, diabetes mellitus is becoming more and more common worldwide. Although traditional antidiabetic drugs are effective, they frequently have side effects and fail to address the intricate oxidative and inflammatory mechanisms that underlie the condition. Due to their superior safety profiles and diverse mechanisms, herbal medications offer promising alternatives in this regard. Several medicinal plants and phytochemicals have been discussed in this review for their potential antidiabetic properties. Compounds like flavonoids, alkaloids, and terpenoids work in a variety of ways, such as boosting insulin sensitivity and secretion, blocking enzymes that break down carbohydrates, increasing glucose absorption, lowering oxidative stress, and maintaining pancreatic β-cell function. Quercetin, curcumin, and resveratrol are examples of natural antioxidants that are important for reducing reactive oxygen species and enhancing insulin function. The microvascular and macrovascular consequences of diabetes, such as neuropathy, nephropathy, and cardiovascular disorders, may also be lessened by herbal treatments. Herbs have different effects on different types of diabetes. Although safety data is still scarce, certain herbs may help treat T1DM and gestational diabetes, even though the evidence for T2DM is higher. To legitimize herbal medicines, more clinical trials, dose standardization, and safety assessments are necessary. For long-term diabetes management, a multidisciplinary strategy that combines conventional knowledge with contemporary research can provide a safer, more comprehensive, and more successful approach.
