Poor adherence to second-line antiretroviral therapy and its contributing factors among people living with HIV in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia
Authors: Bekelech Bayou Feyissa, Abay Sisay, Anteneh Yalew, Geoffrey Setswe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPoor adherence to second-line Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) remains a major risk factor for virological failure among people living with HIV (PLHIV), posing a significant public health challenge. In Ethiopia, limited data exists on this issue, prompting a study to assess adherence levels and contributing factors among adults in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.MethodsA concurrent design, both quantitative and qualitative data were collected and analyzed using SPSS v28 and Atlas.ti v24, respectively.ResultsAmong 369 participants, 16.5% exhibited poor adherence, with varied progress over time. Following up, 89 (24%) of clients had high viral loads, and 34 (38%) of them achieved suppression after undergoing Enhanced Adherence Counseling (EAC). Key factors influencing poor adherence included the presence of opportunistic infections; clients with such conditions were over five times more likely to have poor adherence (5.186 [1.963–13.698], p = 0.001). Additionally, nondisclosure of HIV status was significantly associated with poor adherence (0.507 [95% CI: 0.265–0.971], p = 0.041). In contrast, literacy emerged as a protective factor; individuals with secondary education were 2.5 times more likely to maintain optimal adherence [95% CI: 1.061–5.925], p = 0.036). Supportive evidence was also generated from the qualitative data.ConclusionWhile many participants demonstrated good adherence, variability persisted. EAC proved effective in improving viral suppression. The study underscores the importance of targeted interventions that address opportunistic infections, promote HIV status disclosure, and enhance health literacy to improve treatment outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12981-026-00878-1.

Background

Second-line Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) is prescribed for people living with HIV (PLHIV) who have experienced first-line treatment failure [1]. UNAIDS’ 95-95-95 target aims to ensure that 95% of PLHIV know their status, 95% receive treatment, and 95% achieve viral suppression by 2030. In Ethiopia, 90% of PLHIV knew their HIV status, 94% received treatment, and 96% achieved viral suppression [2].

In Ethiopia, primarily HIV transmitted through heterosexual and vertically from mother to child, with a generalized, low-intensity epidemic. Despite progress in ART in Ethiopia, challenges such as treatment failure, drug resistance, and long-term toxicities persist [3]. Timely detection of virological failure and its determinant factors is essential, with poor adherence to second line therapy being a primary cause, significantly impacting treatment effectiveness [3–5]. Inadequate management of ART adherence can result in unnecessary regimen changes and reduced program effectiveness [6].

Adherence, defined as strict compliance with healthcare providers’ instructions on medication timing, dosage, and frequency, is crucial for successful treatment outcomes [7]. A 95% adherence rate is recommended to achieve viral suppression; lower rates increase the risk of ART failure [1, 8]. Furthermore, poor adherence is a key factor in the failure of second-line ART. Emphasizing adherence can enhance efficacy, with virological suppression rates of 83% at six months and 93% at twelve months, highlighting ART’s potential for durable viral control and improved long-term outcomes in treatment-experienced individuals [9, 10].

Previous studies in Ethiopia reported as ART adherence rates ranging from 51.8% to 73.1% [10, 11], with an average of 66.8%, below WHO recommendations [8]. Suboptimal adherence was linked to factors such as younger age, being widowed, stigma, opportunistic infections, lack of social support, adverse drug reactions, and nondisclosure of HIV status, increasing the risk of treatment failure. Aiming for a 95% adherence rate is crucial for maximal viral suppression [10, 11]. Positive adherence to second-line ART is often associated with a history of adherence to first-line ART, participation in support programs, and absence of substance use [8]. Other factors influencing adherence include literacy levels, comorbidities, forgetfulness, stress, homelessness, poverty, and medication side effects [12–14], along with treatment complexity and pill burden [15, 16].

Healthcare providers often lack the time for thorough adherence assessments, which can lead to confidentiality breaches and increased stigma. This time constraint is especially challenging for rural patients and those who haven’t disclosed their HIV status [17, 18].

Various proven strategies have been implemented to improve adherence among PLHIV, including pill counting, self-reporting, regular viral load monitoring, electronic real-time tracing systems, and peer support and supervision programs. Moreover, Enhanced Adherence Counseling (EAC); it is a patient centered counseling intervention for PLHIV, is the most preferred strategy especially for those with high viral loads. These approaches have shown promise in enhancing adherence and improving treatment outcomes [18].

Maintaining adherence to second-line ART is crucial for quality care, preventing drug resistance, and minimizing public health risks. However, challenges in adherence continue to hinder the long-term success of the global HIV response [12].

This study evaluated the level of adherence to second-line ART among adult PLHIV in Addis Ababa who switched from first line to second line in 2018–2022, along with the factors contributing to adherence. Understanding these factors is vital for creating effective public health strategies, optimizing resource use, and identifying patients at risk of ART failure. Moreover, this research provides a baseline for future studies and supports investigations into ART drug resistance, ultimately improving the effectiveness of HIV care programs.

Methods

Study design and period

This study employed a concurrent mixed-method research design, utilizing both quantitative and qualitative data, from August 20, 2024, to November 25, 2024.

Study setting

The study was conducted in selected health facilities in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. It is the country’s commercial capital and the headquarters of the African Union (AU). According to the 2019 population projection, the city has an estimated total population of 7,823,600. The city has 14 public hospitals; six are operated by the Addis Ababa Health Bureau (AHB), six by the Federal Ministry of Health (FMoH), and two by the Ministry of Defense and the Federal Police Commission, offering teaching, specialized, and referral services [19]. Out of these, Zewditu Hospital and Yekatit 12 Hospital Medical College were selected for this study purposively. More illustrated at S file 1.

Zewditu Memorial hospital is Ethiopia’s leading health facility for treating patients with antiretroviral therapy (ART) and also serves the highest number of HIV patients each month since 2003 with the support of the US CDC-Ethiopia. Likewise, Yekatit 12 Hospital Medical College, is the second oldest hospital in the country. The whole ART services and related expenses are free and covered by the government and generous support of partners.

Study population

The study population was taken from Zewditu Memorial General Hospital and Yekatit 12 Medical College Hospital, encompassing 7,632 and 3,322 PLHIV on ART, respectively, and all hospital-level ART service providers (clinicians, pharmacy professionals, adherence supporters, case managers, and data clerks), and HIV programme managers, at various levels.

Sampling

In this study, adult PLHIV aged 18 years and older who were receiving second-line ART between 2018 and 2022 for more than six months, had undergone at least two follow-up viral load (VL) tests, and had complete medical records and data in the electronic database were included. In addition to this, PLHIV willing to participate in key informant interviews (KII), along with ART service providers, adherence supporters, and HIV program managers from the AACAHB sub-city who volunteered for focus group discussions, were included. Exclusions made for individuals on first-line ART, those under 18, participants with incomplete medical records, and those unwilling to engage in KII or FGDs.

Quantitative sample size determination and sampling techniques

For the quantitative data the sample size was determined using the formula, n =Z2α/2 p(1-p)/d2. The anticipated population proportion of non-adherent to ART was assumed 20.68% as reported in meta-analysis study from Ethiopia [20] and margin of error, D = 4.3%. Accordingly, a 10% non-response contingency considered and resulting in a final minimum required sample size of 375 for the study.

The total sample size proportionally allocated to each hospital based on its PLHIV population. A systematic random sampling (SRS) technique then applied to recruit the participants from each site. Accordingly, 251 participants were recruited from Zewditu Memorial Hospital and 124 participants from Yekatit 12 Hospital Medical College. This sampling strategy was employed to enhance the representativeness of the sample, which in turn strengthens the reliability and generalizability of the research findings.

Qualitative sampling

Participants who met the inclusion criteria were selected for the key informant interviews (KII) and assigned to the respective hospitals where the research was conducted. Originally, the plan was to conduct interviews with twenty (20) PLHIV who were on second-line ART. However, data saturation was reached after conducting 15 interviews, representing 75% of the intended sample, using the KII guides.

In addition to the KII, the researchers conducted three Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) to explore factors affecting adherence to second-line ART among PLHIV aged 18 years and older in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. This component included two FGDs with hospital-level ART service providers (one for each hospital) and the third FGD involved HIV program managers from the Addis Ababa City Administration Health Bureau (AACAHB) and sub-city offices. Each focus group consisted of five to seven participants who were invited based on predefined inclusion criteria.

Data collection

For quantitative part, structured data collection checklist was developed to abstract information from both the medical records of PLHIV and an electronic database. The checklist was then converted into the Kobo Toolbox application and the quantitative data were collected using the Kobo Toolbox application, which facilitated real-time monitoring and efficient data management. To ensure the quality of the data collection tool, the checklist was pretested with 5% of the total sample, which was excluded from the final analysis.

Qualitative information was gathered using KII and FGD guide, prepared in English and translated into Amharic, then transcribed back to English [21]. Each hospital had one supervisor and two trained data collectors selected based on experience and educational background. All signed confidentiality agreements. Data quality and reliability were ensured through a sufficient sample size, pre-tested checklists, and well-defined criteria to prevent selection bias. A one-day training for data collectors provided, and regular quality checks were conducted throughout the data collection period. Informed consent also obtained from key informants and focused group discussants.

Data analysis

The quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS version 28, calculating descriptive statistics such as counts, percentages, means, and confidence intervals to estimate the proportion of second-line ART virological failure. Logistic regression was applied to identify independent predictors while controlling for confounding factors.

The transcribed qualitative data were analyzed with Atlas.ti version 24 [22], . The analysis involved systematic coding to identify recurring patterns and key themes affecting adherence among adults with PLHIV on second-line ART. Following the separate analysis of both the quantitative and qualitative data, the findings were integrated and triangulated. This approach provided a more comprehensive understanding and allowed for robust conclusions and recommendations.

Level of Adherence was defined as: Good if > 95% of doses are taken or ≤ 3 missed; Fair if 85–94% taken or 3–9 missed; Poor if < 85% taken or > 9 missed [23].

First line ART: Is the initial regimen for starting treatment for PLHIV. Second line ART: is a regimen prescribed for PLHIV who have experienced first-line treatment failure [5].

Virological Failure: Viral load > 1000 copies per mL in a two consecutive VL laboratory results within 3 months gaps with EAC following the first VL test [5].

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 369 PLHIV participants who were receiving second-line ART, representing a 98.4% response rate of the calculated sample size, were enrolled. There was a 14.9% (55/369) virological failure of the total study subjects. The overall median duration of treatment is 60 months with a range of 20–74 months. Among the participants, 191 (52) were male, 232 (63) had 40 year and above. Also, 185 (50%) had never married, while 60 (16%) did not disclose their HIV status to others.

Patients linked from other health facilities demonstrated a statistically significant association with virological failure (Chi-square, P-value: 0.040). Similarly, HIV disclosure status (Fisher’s exact test, P-value: 0.017) and prior experience of ART regimen change (Chi-square, P-value: 0.021) were significantly associated (Table 1).

Factors affecting the level of adherence to second-line ART

In this study, on average, 83.5%( 95%CI: 79.7–87.3%) of individuals who were taking second-line ART achieved the WHO ≥ 95% adherence level. Clients experiencing opportunistic infections during second-line ART treatment were 5.186 times more likely to have poor adherence levels compared to those who did not acquire opportunistic infections (AOR:5.186 [1.963–13.698], p-value = 0.001). Additionally, HIV-positive clients on second-line ART who disclosed their status were less likely to have poor adherence (AOR: 0.507 [95% CI: 0.265–0.971], p-value = 0.041).

Furthermore, clients with functional working patients status were 0.541 times (95% CI: 0.294–0.994, p-value = 0.048) less likely to have poor level adherence than bedridden patients. Whereas literacy contributed to good adherence, PLHIV with a secondary level of education were 2.5 times (95% CI: 1.061–5.925, p-value = 0.036) more likely to have optimal adherence (Table 3).

Effect of EAC on HIV viral load suppression among PLHIV on 2nd Line ART

Among the study participants, 89 (24%) had a high viral load. Of these, 34 (38%) achieved HIV viral suppression through Enhanced Adherence Counseling (EAC), while 55 (62%) did not. Figure 1 and S file 2.

After completing 3 to 6 months of EAC, 34(38%) out of 89 clients became HIV viral suppressed. The EAC was designed for PLHIV who have unsuppressed viral loads (≥ 1000 copies/ml) after six months of ART. This counseling session aims to improve adherence through timely follow-up, identifying and addressing potential barriers to treatment, and any difficulties the patients may be experiencing (Fig. 2).

Results from the qualitative data sources

The results from the key informant interviews (KII) with adult PLHIV taking second-line ART who fulfilled the inclusion criteria and FGD from ART providers and program personnel are presented in the following sections. The data analysis has followed the steps indicated in S file 3.

Bio-data of participants

In the KIIs, 15 (10 females, 5 males), adults PLHIV on second-line ART, were recruited from Zewditu Memorial General Hospital and Yekatit 12 Medical College Hospital. The average age was 35.4 years, ranging from 24 to 47 years. In this study, three FGDs sessions, two with ART service providers (hospital level) and one with program managers, involving 16 participants. This included three ART focal persons (2 female), 3 adherence counselors (1 female), and four case managers (1 male). The program manager FGD included six individuals (5 females, 1 male).

Factors affecting adherence to second line ART from KIIs and FGDs

Understanding the factors influencing adherence to second-line ART is crucial for improving treatment outcomes, patient lifestyle, and ensuring the long-term success of HIV programs. To gain insights from a programmatic perspective as a way of “hearing from the horse’s mouth,” three FGD sessions were conducted with 6 to 7 participants each, including ART service providers and HIV program managers working at AACAHB regional health bureau, sub-city health department, and two hospital levels.

The findings presented in the following sections offer a comprehensive overview of ART adherence from both a service delivery and programmatic standpoint, highlighting practical, scalable solutions to strengthen adherence support and improve treatment outcomes for PLHIV on second-line ART. Accordingly, 19 codes, 5 code groups or themes, and 146 quotations were generated from the 15 KII and 3 FGDs. The code and group codes were presented based on factors that affect adherence to second-line ART, including ART drug side effects, stigma & discrimination, rejection or isolation, low community awareness, and behavioral factors, as indicated in Table 4.

Barriers to second line ART adherence to second-line ART from FGDs

The FGD participants identified key barriers mainly associated with psychological, emotional, psychosocial, cultural, and religious factors, explored successful strategies, and proposed innovative solutions to enhance patient adherence to ART. For simplicity and clarity, the researchers presented the findings accordingly.

Participants from the three FGDs thoroughly discussed the factors that negatively affected adherence to ART. Among the adherence barriers, stigma and discrimination have been discussed more. Accordingly, one FGD discussant said,

“Many PLHIV are afraid of stigma and discrimination, so they choose to keep their status a secret. Because of this, they miss out on the help they could get from their family, partner, and colleagues, which could have made it easier for them to follow their treatment properly.” 12:36 ¶ 10 in F 1.

ART providers in the FGD described that “many PLHIV struggle to accept their HIV-positive diagnosis, experiencing denial, disbelief, or panic. This emotional distress can lead to delayed treatment adherence, potentially affecting their long-term health outcomes.”

In line with this, the study examined various mechanisms aimed at addressing stigma and discrimination, recognizing the significant impact of these issues on the adherence of PLHIV to ART. It explored strategies such as community awareness to reduce misinformation and improve socio-cultural barriers. By tackling these barriers, the study aimed to identify effective approaches to improve ART adherence and overall well-being among PLHIV.

One FGD participant said, “To address stigma and discrimination and improve patient adherence to ART, community awareness efforts must be strengthened. ---we need to revitalize public education campaigns, stakeholder engagement, engage the media, and increase community-level education about HIV, its transmission, and the importance of treatment adherence.” 12:48 ¶ 25 in F 3.

Substance addiction was identified as a barrier to PLHIV taking ART. From the focus group discussions, an adherence supporter at the hospital level raised, “We’ve observed that substance addiction significantly affects patients’ ability to adhere to their ART.” 12:23 ¶ 6 in F 1.

Misconceptions about ART also highlighted one of the barriers to good adherence. A case manager at the hospital noted during the FGD that, “Misconceptions surrounding ART, such as fears of side effects and doubts about its effectiveness, often discourage patients from remaining on their treatment.”

Hospital-level FGD participants shared religious insight as a barrier to adherence, “--- certain religious believer patients considered themselves as they have been ‘cured’ of HIV through prayer, resulting in them discontinuing their ART without consulting us. Additionally, some patients personally believe they are cured after engaging in some religious practices. Consequently, they stop their medication, assuming it is no longer needed.”

The FGD discussion also explored economic problems and distance as barriers to adherence. An FGD discussant from the hospital indicated, “---Some clients travel long distances, even from regional states, to access their ART due to fear of social stigma and discrimination.” 12:45 ¶ 9 in F 1.

ART focal persons shared a particular case of a client who traveled 397 KM from a regional state to Addis Ababa for his medication, “---recently, a client missed his appointment simply because he couldn’t find transportation due to road inaccessibility.” 12:36 ¶ 10 in F 2. The shortage of second-line and third-line ART is another issue. A case manager remarked, “--- when the hospital faces stock outs, patients are often referred to other health facilities to continue their treatment. However, this process can be discouraging for them and may lead some to miss doses.”

Another factor for poor adherence mentioned was food insecurity. A case manager expressed that, “---there are patients who experience food insecurity, with some even requesting food support from us. When patients lack proper nutrition, the risk of treatment interruption increases.” 12:31 ¶ 23 in F 1.

The researchers assessed the feelings of participants in relation to the disclosure of their HIV status to others. A participant expressed the feeling, stating, “Disclosing my HIV status to others has never been an easy decision. Fearing stigma and discrimination, I prefer to keep my HIV status private, sharing it only with my family.” 47:9 ¶ 7 in P4.

The researchers assessed the participation of PLHIV in peer support groups. The other KII participant said, “…I haven’t joined any social support groups. It is not that I do not see their value; However, I have some reservations. I am concerned about my privacy and the possibility of unintended disclosure of my HIV status, which could lead to stigma or discrimination.

Low community awareness of HIV leads to non-disclosure of HIV status

Participants raised their concern that low community awareness leads to non-disclosure of their HIV status. A key informant interview expressed,

This decision stems from a fear of judgment and the negative reactions I might face if someone discovers my HIV status. While this approach helps me adhere to my treatment schedule, it limits my flexibility and affects my daily routine, as I have to plan my activities around being at home for my doses. Discussion

This study evaluated adherence levels and associated factors among people living with HIV (PLHIV) aged 18 years and older who were on second-line ART between 2018 and 2022 in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The findings revealed a virological failure (VF) rate of 14.9%, which aligns with previous studies reporting similar rates, 15.4% in Ethiopia [24] and 15% in Pune, India (59/400) [25]. The study identified potential hindering factors for adherence. The recent study indicates that clients with poor adherence to ART are a significant risk factor for virological failure, with an AOR of 6.641 (95% CI: 1.077, 40.95, p-value: 0.041). It is a concordance finding with studies in Ethiopia, showing that individuals with poor adherence are significantly more likely to experience virological failure compared to those with good adherence. Patients with poor adherence were six times more likely to develop treatment failure compared to those with good adherence [26]. Likewise, it was supported by research in South Africa states that non-adherence to ART is the most identified cause for second-line ART VF [27].

The FGDs and KII findings in this study revealed that stigma and discrimination, particularly perceived stigma, remain significant risk factors for poor adherence among HIV-positive clients on second-line ART. This can contribute to suboptimal adherence to treatment and loss of engagement in care due to fears of disclosure and uncertainty about the consequences. As a result, this can lead to substandard health outcomes and a decreased quality of life. Previous studies conducted by Perger and Madiba also identified stigma as a significantly associated factor for poor adherence that could lead to a VF [28].

Besides, individuals experiencing drug toxicity in their treatment follow-up course that can lead to suboptimal adherence to the prescribed regimen of ART have non-virological suppression, which directly and potentially leads to virological failure, compared with those who do not, which is supported by other studies [26, 29].

Non-disclosure of HIV status was associated with a higher risk of non- adherence which is directly contributed with virological failure. Which was strongly supported by our recent qualitative data from hospital-level ART providers and program managers participating in focus group discussions (FGDs). They highlighted that non-disclosure hampers medication adherence because of the lack of social support. Conversely, individuals who disclose their status to partners, relatives, or parents often receive emotional encouragement, practical assistance, and medication reminders, all of which enhance adherence.

Likewise higher level of literacy appears to play a significant role in improving adherence among PLHIV on second-line ART. Higher literacy levels enhance patients’ ability to understand treatment instructions, recognize the importance of consistent medication use, and engage more effectively with adherence support interventions. In our study, individuals with a secondary level of education were 2.5 times more likely to achieve optimal adherence (95% CI: 1.061–5.925, p = 0.036), underscoring the contribution of education to better treatment outcomes.

Although the quantitative data and FGDs underscore the benefits of disclosure, key informant interview (KII) respondents raised important concerns. In communities with low HIV awareness and widespread stigma, disclosure can have negative effects, leading to non-adherence. Fear of rejection, isolation, and discrimination may cause individuals to skip doses, especially during social activities or when away from home, where they tend to be more protective of their privacy. KII participants emphasized that in areas with limited understanding of HIV, disclosure, while potentially supportive, can also act as a barrier due to anticipated or actual social repercussions [30] supports this, finding that stigma and discrimination significantly contribute to social isolation among people living with HIV (PLHIV). Similarly, a study in South Africa showed that disclosure often resulted in mistreatment [31].

Interventions that promote disclosure as a strategy to improve adherence and prevent virological failure must be carefully tailored to the specific context. These interventions should be accompanied by strong efforts to raise community awareness about HIV, reduce stigma and discrimination, and create supportive environments for individuals living with HIV. Additionally, healthcare providers should be proficient in assessing the unique circumstances of each patient and support them in making informed decisions about disclosure. This should involve considering both the potential benefits of social support and the risks associated with stigma in their specific social contexts. Further research should investigate how social support and perceived stigma mediate the relationship between disclosure and virological outcomes to refine the understanding of these dynamics [28].

Quantitative results from this study also identified being a transferred-in ART client as a significant risk factor for second-line virological failure because of poor adherence. Participants explained that transfers often occurred due to recurrent comorbidities that local facilities cannot manage, indicating complex clinical histories and compromised health. Additionally, long distances travelers were linked to missed doses and poor engagement in care, increasing the risk of failure. Transfers may interrupt established routines and support networks, especially for those already disadvantaged by distance. These findings are consistent with a Sierra Leone study, which found that distance negatively impacted ART adherence [6].

To mitigate these issues, receiving facilities must provide tailored support, taking into account clinical histories and logistical barriers. Ensuring seamless transitions and reinforcing support systems for transferred-in patients are crucial for maintaining adherence and achieving viral suppression.

Another significant factor identified for poor adherence was the experience of intolerable side effects, which often causes patients to skip doses in an attempt to relieve discomfort. The finding aligns with a study conducted at Nekemte Hospital in Ethiopia, which also identified ART side effects as a significant factor associated with adherence [10] as non-adherence weakens treatment effectiveness. To address this, clinicians must also offer personalized support, considering individual clinical histories [32].

Furthermore, this research finding underscores the importance and role of EAC in HIV ART viral suppression among second-line ART clients, as 38% of the study participants achieved HIV viral suppression through EAC. This research highlighted and underscored the significance of promptly applying EAC for a reasonable number of clients in reducing high HIV viral loads among PLHIV who experienced high viral load in the course of second-line ART follow-up. It is concordant with published research [35–37], with a notable percentage of participants achieving viral suppression after completing EAC sessions, 40.9% (35.7%-46.5%). It is a structured intervention aimed at helping patients achieve and sustain viral suppression. It focuses on identifying and addressing the suboptimal adherence barriers by developing personalized plans to support medication adherence, which aligns with findings from previous similar studies conducted in Ethiopia [33], in Nigeria [34] and in Cameron [35].

Moreover, although high amount of viral load may predisposes PLHIV to OIs through immune suppression, the occurrence of OIs can also transiently increased viral replication through systemic inflammation and immune activation. From this, we understand this bidirectional relationship underscore the complexity of interpreting association b/n OIs and viral load as potential for reverse causality. We have noted that this point should more extinct and more researched using a longitudinal studies to disentangle the cause and effect and better characterize the temporal dynamic contexts [26].

Non-disclosure, being transferred in-patient, fear of stigma, distance from health facility, pill burden, and ART side effect, are factors contributing to poor adherence and notably led to second line Virological failure that influenced zero infection of HIV by 2030.

Limitations of the study

While interpreting the findings of this research, readers should note a potential recall bias in the qualitative aspects, as participants might inaccurately remember past events, affecting reliability. To mitigate this, a pre-tested, validated questionnaire was used, and participants were encouraged to base their responses on recent and relevant experiences. We highlighted the importance of a comprehensive longitudinal study to distinguish the cause and effect by considering the temporal dynamic contexts.

Conclusion

Second-line ART virological failure is rising, driven by poor adherence, drug resistance, and limited treatment options, especially in resource-constrained settings like Ethiopia. Urgent action is needed to improve monitoring and align with WHO and national HIV guidelines to prevent further resistance and poor outcomes.

This study examined the factors contributing to poor adherence among second-line ART in adult HIV-positive patients. Key risk factors included opportunistic infections, loss to follow-up, and history of frequent regimen changes, perceived stigma, disclosure concerns, and drug toxicity.

Enhanced Adherence Counseling (EAC) proved to be an essential intervention for achieving and maintaining viral suppression among second-line ART patients, by identifying individual barriers and customizing support strategies. Early detection of high-risk patients, those with poor adherence, and irregular follow-up is vital to preventing treatment failure.

Second-line ART failure is rising due to poor adherence, drug resistance, and limited options. Risk factors include opportunistic infections, stigma, and drug toxicity. EAC is vital for viral suppression, along with early detection and regular monitoring of high-risk patients.

The study recommends that policymakers, healthcare providers, and stakeholders prioritize regular adherence level monitoring and implement responsive strategies to reduce virological failure. Strengthening HIV care and treatment programs is a key to improving the quality of life for PLHIV.
