Comment on the “Analysis of the implementation effect and evaluation of the vaccine protection effect of the live attenuated varicella vaccine program for school-age children in Bao’an district of Shenzhen, China”
Authors: Dalmacito A Cordero
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest the article published in this journal regarding the varicella vaccine program for school-age children in Bao’an district of Shenzhen, China. The authors analyzed the implementation effect of the Live Attenuated Varicella Vaccine (VarV) Vaccination Program for eligible children and evaluate the vaccine effectiveness. They concluded that overall, the vaccination program achieved positive results as it resulted in a decline of 54.6% across age groups, with the largest reductions among 7- and 6-year-olds, of the overall varicella incidence.1 With this, it dawned on me the possibility of applying the same vaccination program in the Philippines, especially during this time when pediatric chickenpox is again causing a serious health problem for many Filipino families. However, a serious challenge is hindering the administration of this essential vaccination program, that is, the unavailability of this free vaccine in public health facilities in the country. This unavailability is unfortunate, especially for underprivileged locals who rely only on free vaccines.

Just recently, Dr. Benito Atienza, a pediatrician and the vice-president of the Philippine Federation of Professionals Associations stressed the need for parents and guardians to bring their children to private doctors to have their children get vaccinated against chickenpox because of the rising cases.2 Pediatric chickenpox is a serious health concern that must not be neglected. In the Philippines, a 3-year-old child had chickenpox symptoms, which did not cause worries to her parents, and treated it as a part of childhood. After a week, the condition worsened, and she was rushed to the hospital and diagnosed with possible pneumonia. Unfortunately, she had a cardiac arrest a few hours later and died. A pathology report revealed that the virus had spread to her lungs and wreaked widespread damage, causing lesions that proved ultimately fatal.3 With this, it dawned on me how impactful the effects of this disease if not given full attention, especially in developing countries.

Chickenpox is a highly contagious disease caused by the varicella-zoster virus (VZV). It causes an itchy, blister-like rash on the chest, back, and face, then spreads over the entire body. The disease can be prevented through vaccines, administering two doses for children: the first at 12 through 15 months of age and the second at 4 through 6. The vaccine has prevented an estimated 91 million cases, 238,000 hospitalizations, and 2,000 deaths in the United States.4 As mentioned, the vaccine is unavailable in public health facilities and I see two reasons for this. First, there is the dilemma of increasing the risk of shingles in later life if chickenpox vaccination is given. The same varicella-zoster virus infection causes shingles which is a painful and debilitating condition. On the contrary, many large-scale epidemiological studies have attested that the United States and other countries have not reported the feared increase in shingles cases over the last three decades. Even the UK’s Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunization (JCVI) had recently recommended the chickenpox vaccine in its program.2 The second reason is due to budget constraint or lack of additional funding by the government to procure the expensive chickenpox vaccines. The average cost of the vaccine is more than PhP3,000 for every dose in private hospitals, and two doses are needed to complete the regimen (PhP6,000 or almost $100). However, since most vaccine-preventable diseases for children are already freely available in public health centers, then why not include chickenpox vaccine? This issue is critical and needs to be addressed, especially with the rising cases annually. There is a sufficient budget allocated to the Department of Health for the current year, 2024, which is approximately PhP241.1 billion5 for its programs, especially prioritizing vaccine procurement. In relation to this, the country’s Department of Health (DOH) should also organize the complete four phases of clinical trials for the vaccine to ensure its safety and efficacy. Clinical trials are a type of research that studies new tests and treatments and evaluates their effects on human health outcomes. They are carefully designed, reviewed and completed, and need to be approved before they can start. People of all ages, including children, can take part in this procedure.6 In this way, the perennial problem of vaccine hesitancy will be addressed and families will be fully convinced to avail of the government’s vaccination program.

It is a must, therefore that the government must critically examine its policies and management in considering diseases that should be prioritized for its focused programs. Every life is of great value, regardless of how young or old it is. The country can always learn from the relevant and successful studies and practices of other nations. If the lack of funding is the problem, a well-planned financial management must be prepared and ensured for application. This preparation can be supported by seeking financial aid with private companies and from foreign donations, which can never be outdone in generosity as experienced by the country in the previous health crisis.

Biography

Dalmacito Cordero Jr. is a Full Professor at the Department of Theology and Religious Education (DTRE) of De La Salle University. He also taught at the University of Santo Tomas, De La Salle-College of St. Benilde, Philippine Women’s University, Don Bosco-Makati, and Don Bosco-Tarlac. He earned his Bachelor of Arts in Philosophy at Our Lady of Peace College Seminary (2001), Master of Arts in Theological Studies (2009) at San Sebastian College-Recoletos, and Doctor of Philosophy in Applied Theology at De La Salle University (2017). He authored several published journal articles and a textbook on Christian morality. He has also presented papers at various national and international academic conferences. His research interests are moral theology, youth studies, public health, and liturgical music.
