Genetic inactivation of TRPM4 does not alter the temperature-dependent movement of mouse microglia
Authors: Rei Nishimoto, Yoshikazu Matsuoka, Makoto Tominaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTemperature sensing shapes behavior and cellular functions, yet the molecular basis of thermosensitivity in non-neuronal cells remains poorly defined. Microglia are resident immune cells of the central nervous system that help maintain brain homeostasis via immune surveillance and injury responses. We previously showed that microglial motility is temperature dependent and is largely mediated by the thermosensitive ion channel transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4), a thermosensitive ion channel. However, the contribution of transient receptor potential melastatin 4 (TRPM4) is unclear because suitable Trpm4 mutant mice were not available in our earlier work. Here, we generated functional Trpm4-knockout mice (TRPM4KO) using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing based on a published strategy. Time-lapse imaging of primary microglia across a range of temperatures revealed that Trpm4 deficiency did not alter temperature-dependent motility in vitro. These results indicate that TRPM4 is dispensable for temperature-dependent microglial motility.Keywords: Microglia, Thermosensitivity, TRP channels

Introduction

Thermal sensation, such as vision and hearing, is a vital physiological function by which organisms perceive their surroundings [1]. By sensing temperature, organisms can judge whether a thermal environment is pleasant or aversive and use this information to guide behavioral adaptations essential for survival [2], [3], [4]. Temperature sensing is not limited to the organismal level; many cell types can also detect temperature, and a wide range of cellular functions are modulated by temperature [1], [5], [6]. Thus, understanding cellular thermosensitivity is important for determining how temperature regulates cellular functions.

The identification and cloning of transient receptor potential (TRP) channels prompted research into cellular temperature perception [7]. TRP channels are six-transmembrane, non-selective cation channels that act as polymodal receptors responding to not only endogenous ligands but also mechanical and thermal stimuli [1], [8], [9]. The discovery that temperature sensation is mediated by the activation of thermosensitive TRP channels in sensory neurons led to subsequent studies showing that TRP channels contribute to temperature sensing in many non-neuronal cell types, including keratinocytes, immune cells, and glial cells in the brain [10], [11], [12]. Collectively, these findings suggest that thermosensitive TRP channels serve as a broadly conserved molecular basis underlying temperature-dependent regulation of cellular functions.

Microglia are the resident immune sentinels of the central nervous system, and their dysfunction has been implicated in diverse neurological and psychiatric disorders, including neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration [13], [14]. Equipped with a rich repertoire of receptors, microglia continuously survey the neural milieu, rapidly migrate toward sites of injury or inflammation, and phagocytose cellular debris and pathogenic aggregates [15], [16], [17]. Microglia maintain central nervous system homeostasis in healthy conditions and thus orchestrate immune responses in disease states.

We previously reported that microglial motility is temperature dependent and that a thermosensitive subtype TRP vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) plays a major role among thermosensitive TRP channels [18]. In that study, we investigated TRP melastatin 2 (TRPM2), TRP melastatin 4 (TRPM4), and TRPV4 as candidate mediators of the temperature sensitivity of microglia based on their mRNA and protein expression and examined their contributions using genetic and pharmacological approaches. Whereas the functions of TRPM2 and TRPV4 were assessed in knockout mice, that of TRPM4 was initially investigated using 9-phenanthrol, a TRPM4 inhibitor, because Trpm4-knockout mice were not readily available. However, because we found that 9-phenanthrol also inhibits TRPM2 and TRPV4, the specific involvement of TRPM4 could not be conclusively determined [19]. Therefore, in this present study, we generated functional Trpm4-knockout mice and then examined whether TRPM4 can be involved in the temperature dependence of microglial motility.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6 N mice (SLC Japan, Hamamatsu, Japan) were used as the wild-type (WT) strain. Trpm4-knockout mice (TRPM4KO) were generated for this study as described below. Primary microglia were obtained from postnatal pups (P0 through P3) derived from WT or TRPM4KO mice. Mice were housed in a controlled environment (12 h light/12 h dark cycle; room temperature, 22 °C to 24 °C; 50–60% relative humidity) with free access to food and water. All of the animal care and experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for Animal Experiments of Okayama University and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Okayama University for Animal Research (Approval number: 18094) and the Animal Care and Use Committee, Okayama University (Approval numbers: 2018615, 20186161, 2018617).

Generation of Trpm4-knockout (TRPM4KO) mice

TRPM4KO mice lacking exons 15 and 16 were generated using a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing approach, in which Cas9 protein and single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) complexes were electroporated into early pronuclear-stage mouse zygotes as described previously [20], [21], [22]. Following a previous strategy, two sgRNAs were designed to target the intronic regions flanking exons 15 and 16 of the mouse Trpm4 gene, thereby inducing a genomic deletion encompassing both exons [20]. The sgRNA target sequences were as follows: sgRNA1 5′-GCCATGGCGTTAATCCCACG-3′ within intron 14, and sgRNA2 5′-GATCCTGAAACTGTCCATGA-3′ within intron 16. The sgRNAs were synthesized via PCR by using the forward primers 5′-TTAATACGACTCACTATAGGGCCATGGCGTTAATCCCACGGTTTTAGAGCTAGAAATAGCAAGTTAAAAT-3′ and 5′-TTAATACGACTCACTATAGGGATCCTGAAACTGTCCATGAGTTTTAGAGCTAGAAATAGCAAGTTAAAAT-3′ for sgRNA1 and sgRNA2, respectively, as previously described [23]. C57BL/6 N zygotes were collected on the day of plug. Electroporation of the zygotes was performed in the 1-mm gap of an LF501PT1–10 electrode (BEX, Tokyo, Japan) filled with 10 μL of freshly prepared 200 ng/μL Alt-R S.p. Cas9 Nuclease V3 (Integrated DNA Technologies, Tokyo, Japan) and 100 ng/μL sgRNA in Opti-MEM. The electric conditions consisted of 2 sets of 5 pulses of 3 ms at 15 V, with inverting polarity between sets by using the CUY21 EDIT II electroporator (BEX, Tokyo, Japan). Following electroporation, zygotes were cultured in KSOM medium (ARK-Resource, Kumamoto, Japan) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ until the two-cell stage and then transferred to the oviducts of pseudopregnant females on the day of the vaginal plug. Genomic DNA was extracted from tail biopsies or embryonic tissues, and deletion of Trpm4 exon 15–16 was confirmed by PCR using primers flanking the targeted region, followed by sequence analysis. The specific primers used for PCR were as follows: forward (Fw)1 5′-CCTGTGTCGATCTGTTTTTAGTCAATCTTGTATG-3′, Fw2 5′-GGTGAGTGACAGTCCAGCATTGTG-3′, and reverse (Rv) 5′-GCCTGGTCTTTAAAAGATCTCCATGGG-3′. The mice confirmed to harbor deletion of Trpm4 exons 15–16 (TRPM4KO) were maintained to establish a colony and used for subsequent experiments.

Mouse TRPM4 expression vector and transfection

Mouse TRPM4 cDNA in the pcDNA3 expression vector was generously provided by Dr. Veit Flockerzi (Universität des Saarlandes, Germany). The entire sequence of this vector was confirmed by DNA sequencing. HEK293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (D-MEM, Wako, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% (volume/volume) fetal bovine serum (BioWest or Gibco), penicillin–streptomycin (50 units/mL and 50 μg/mL, respectively, Gibco) and GlutaMAX (2 mM, Gibco) and seeded at densities of 5 × 105 cells per 35 mm dish 24 h before transfection. For protein collection, HEK293T cells cultured in OPTI-MEM I medium (Invitrogen) on 35 mm dishes were transfected with 1 μg expression vector using Lipofectamine LTX (Invitrogen) and Plus reagents (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After incubation for 3–5 h, cells were reseeded on glass coverslips and further incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. The transfected cells were used for protein expression assays at 20–36 h after transfection.

Preparation of primary mouse microglia

Primary mouse microglia were obtained according to a previously described method [18]. Mixed glial cultures were prepared from cerebral hemispheres of postnatal (P0 through P3) pups. The tissues were minced and trypsinized with DNase (Roche) at 37 °C for 10 min before adding horse serum (Biowest). The dissociated cells were seeded as mixed glia cultures in 75 cm2 tissue flasks containing DMEM (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% (volume/volume) heat-inactivated bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich), penicillin–streptomycin (10 units/mL and 10 mg/mL, respectively, Gibco), bovine insulin (5 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich), and glucose solution (2 mg/mL, Otsuka). The cells were then cultured for 10–20 days at 37 °C in a humidified CO2 incubator until reaching 100% confluence. Primary microglia were obtained by shaking the flasks containing the mixed glia cultures and the supernatant containing floating cells was collected. Cells were seeded on 35 mm glass bottom dishes or 12 mm glass coverslips (Matsunami Glass) at a density of 1 × 104 cells/dish and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified CO2 incubator. Cells were used for experiments within 6 days of isolation.

RT-PCR

Total RNA was purified from mouse brain samples and primary mouse microglia obtained from WT or TRPM4KO mouse using the RNeasy Plus Mini kit (Qiagen) with DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich) treatment to eliminate genomic DNA according to the manufacturer’s protocols. cDNA was synthesized from total RNA (up to 1 μg) by reverse transcription using the SuperScript III first-strand synthesis system for RT-PCR (Invitrogen). PCR was performed using KOD DNA polymerase (Toyobo) according to the manufacture’s protocol together with specific primer sets. The forward and reverse primers used were as follows: 5′-CCACAACAGTGAAGAGCGAG-3′ and 5′-CAGATGGGAGTTGTGCTGTC-3′ for Trpm4 of mouse brain samples, 5′- TGGATGCTCTGCTGAATGAC-3′ and 5′-GACTCTAGGCGAGCCATCAC-3′ for Trpm4 in mouse primary microglia, 5′-CATCCTGCACCACCAACTG-3′ and 5′-TGGATGCAGGGATGATGTTC-3′ for Gapdh in mouse brain samples, 5′-TGAAGGGTGGAGCCAAAAGG-3′ and 5′-GGAAGAGTGGGAGTTGCTGTTG-3′ for Gapdh in mouse primary microglia. The amplified fragments were confirmed by sequencing.

Protein expression assay

Samples were prepared from HEK293T cells expressing mouse TRPM4 or from colon tissues isolated from WT or TRPM4KO mice. HEK293T cells were homogenized in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer containing a protease inhibitor mixture (Cell Signaling Technology) and lysed. Colons were washed with chilled PBS three times and sliced into the ∼5 mm pieces. The colon slices were further cut into 1 mm pieces and then homogenized as described above for the HEK293T cells. For Western blot experiments, 10 μg protein from HEK293T samples and 50 μg from the colon samples were used. The proteins were denatured using sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl, 2% SDS, 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 10% glycerol, and 0.01% bromophenol blue at 95 °C for 5 min before resolution by SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). After transferring the proteins to a nitrocellulose membrane, the membrane was blocked for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with anti-TRPM4 antibody (1:300, ACC044, Alomone) overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with phosphate buffered saline with Tween 20 (PBS-T), the membrane was incubated for 1 h at room temperature with an anti-rabbit IgG horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked antibody (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology). After incubating the membrane for 5 min with Clarity Western ECL substrate (BioRad), proteins were detected with a molecular imager (ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system, BioRad).

In vitro time-lapse imaging of microglia movement

Glass bottom dishes containing primary mouse microglia were placed on the stage of an inverted Keyence BZ-X700 (Keyence) fluorescence microscope fitted with a temperature-controlled stage incubator (Tokai Hit) under a circulating mixture of gases (20% O2, 5% CO2, and 75% N2). The stage/cover temperatures were set at 33/33 °C (33 °C), 37/40 °C (37 °C), or 40/41 °C (40 °C) for each experiment 1 h before cell image acquisition. Dishes were first equilibrated on the stage for 1 hr under the 37 °C condition, and then the stage/cover temperatures were switched to the target recording condition. The temperature setting for each condition was determined in advance by measuring the medium temperature with a wire probe and a digital thermometer (Unique Medical). Cells were imaged at 3 min intervals with a 20 × phase contrast objective lens (Nikon Instech). Image acquisition was performed using BZ-X700 software with a Z-stack function. Focused images taken with the Z-stack function were automatically selected using a BZ-II analyzer (Keyence) in each frame correction and then reconstructed into a series of stacking images along the time axis. The migration distances of microglia were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH) and the Manual Tracking and Chemotaxis and Migration plugin tool. Excel software (Microsoft) was used to calculate the migration distances of individual cells. The migration distances were evaluated in all observable microglia from at least two individual glass bottom dishes and two distinct cell preparations.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± SEM. All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 software. Two-way ANOVA with the post hoc analysis Bonferroni test was applied where indicated. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Generation of Trpm4-knockout (TRPM4KO) mice

To inactivate the TRPM4 function, we deleted exons 15 and 16, which encode the first transmembrane segment of TRPM4, via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing in mice (Fig. 1A) [20], [21], [22]. Genotypes were determined by PCR using a mixture of specific primer pairs, which produced a 695 bp amplicon for the wild-type (WT) allele (Fw2/Rv) and a 928 bp amplicon for the deletion (KO) allele (Fw1/Rv). PCR produced both bands in a sample from a heterozygous mouse (Fig. 1B). In WT genomic DNA samples, a faint ∼2000 bp amplicon was detected using the Fw1/Rv primer pair under our PCR conditions, whereas no corresponding band was observed in heterozygous genomic DNA samples. Subsequently, the Trpm4 mRNA level was assessed and compared between the WT and KO mutants. RT-PCR generated products of the expected size for Trpm4 in primary mouse microglia as well as mouse brain tissues (Fig. 1C and D). We therefore decided to validate TRPM4 expression at the protein level. Unfortunately, TRPM4 protein could not be detected in WT brain lysates or primary microglia cultures in our experimental setting, possibly due to low endogenous expression levels [24]. The antibody in this study reliably detected TRPM4 in an overexpression system, so we next examined colon tissue, in which TRPM4 mRNA expression is most abundant according to a publicly available dataset [24]. In WT colon lysates, a band at the expected molecular weight for TRPM4 (134 kDa) was detected, whereas this band was absent in colon lysates from TRPM4KO mice (Fig. 1E). These results strongly suggest that this mutant represents a systemic Trpm4 null line, and it was therefore used for subsequent experiments examining temperature-dependent microglia movement.

The bottom fragment (695 bp) was amplified using the WT primer pair. The top fragment (928 bp) was amplified using the KO primer pair, indicating successful deletion of exons 15 and 16, which was confirmed by direct sequencing. (C and D). TRPM4 inactivation did not alter the temperature-dependent microglia movement

Next, we isolated microglia from the TRPM4KO mice and examined whether their motility exhibited temperature dependence using time-lapse imaging [18]. We utilized a temperature-controlled time-lapse imaging system to observe microglia movement at a single-cell level as previously established [18]. After a 1 hr incubation at 37 °C, time-lapse imaging was performed for 2hrs under three different temperature conditions according to a fixed schedule. At 37 °C, WT microglia moved randomly, consistent with our previous findings, and TRPM4KO microglia appeared to show similar behavior. We then analyzed the trajectories of primary microglia from a representative preparation of each genotype at 33 °C, 37 °C, or 40 °C, showing that microglia of both genotypes moved more as temperature increased (Fig. 2A). To compare the microglia movement between genotypes more precisely, we quantified the moving distance of microglia from WT or TRPM4KO mice under the three different temperature conditions. As we reported previously, WT microglia exhibited temperature-dependent motility, with migration distance increasing as the environmental temperature increased (Fig. 2B). Consistently, TRPM4KO microglia showed a similar temperature-dependent increase in migration distance. These results indicate that Trpm4 deficiency does not alter the temperature dependence of microglial motility.

Trajectories of primary microglia from a representative preparation of WT and TRPM4KO mice recorded for 2 h at 33 °C, 37 °C, and 40 °C. Paths are arranged to show origins at x (horizontal axis) = y (vertical axis) = 0. Each line indicates the trajectory of a single cell. n indicates the number of cells analyzed per preparation. B. The average distances of migrating microglia isolated from WT or TRPM4KO mice exposed to 33 °C (WT, n = 117; TRPM4KO, n = 151), 37 °C (WT, n = 235; TRPM4KO, n = 240), or 40 °C (WT, n = 150; TRPM4KO, n = 175) were measured. Open circles indicate the migration distance of each microglia over 2 h. Horizontal lines indicate means ± SEM. At 33 °C, 37 °C, and 40 °C, WT microglia moved 112.94 ± 6.1 μm, 175.28 ± 5.24 μm, and 204.31 ± 7.27 μm, respectively, whereas TRPM4KO microglia moved 113.46 ± 5.1 μm, 175.28 ± 4.13 μm, and 201.35 ± 5.61 μm, respectively. **P < 0.01 (two-way ANOVA followed by post hoc Bonferroni test for multiple comparisons).

Discussion

In this study, we generated a TRPM4KO mouse line using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing and used primary microglia derived from these mice to test whether TRPM4 contributes to temperature-dependent microglial motility. Despite prior difficulties with obtaining TRPM4KO animals, this approach enabled a direct genetic assessment. Our results indicate that TRPM4 is dispensable for the temperature dependence of microglial motility under the conditions examined.

Whether temperature-induced changes in microglial behavior are beneficial or detrimental for central nervous system homeostasis remains unclear. Brain temperature is not uniform and is thought to fluctuate locally with neural activity or inflammation [25], [26], [27]. Brain temperature could also change reflecting deep body temperature fluctuation [25], [27], [28]. However, progress has been constrained by the technical challenges of measuring local brain temperature in a minimally invasive manner. Considering this background, our previous in vivo time-lapse imaging demonstrated that microglial process dynamics are temperature dependent, and that this temperature dependence is lost in Trpv4-deficient mice, implicating TRPV4 as a key mediator of temperature-dependent microglial motility [18]. The present findings add to that model by showing that TRPM4 is not required for the temperature-dependent component of microglial motility, suggesting that TRPV4-dependent thermosensitivity is mediated through pathways that do not rely on TRPM4.

TRPM4 is widely regarded as an amplifier of Ca2+-dependent signaling downstream of Ca2+-permeable channels and receptors [29]. In lymphocytes, TRPM4 activation shapes membrane potential and Ca2+ oscillations, and TRPM4 loss can lead to dysregulated Ca2+ entry and altered cytokine responses [30]. In addition, physical coupling between extrasynaptic NMDA receptors and TRPM4 has been implicated in excitotoxicity in ischemia models, in which TRPM4-mediated sustained depolarization promoted mitochondrial dysfunction and exacerbated injury, and disruption of this coupling was protective [31]. TRPM4 has also been linked to cytoskeletal regulation, including contexts in which TRPM4 and TRPV4 may interact functionally [32], [33], [34]. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that Ca2+ influx via TRPV4 might activate TRPM4 to promote temperature-dependent microglial process motility. Our genetic data argue against a major contribution of TRPM4 to this phenomenon and instead point to alternative downstream effectors of TRPV4 in microglia. However, this study does not rule out the possibility that TRPM4 contributes to other microglial functions. In a mouse model of cardiac arrest, the inhibition of overactivated TRPM4 by flufenamic acid, a TRPM4 inhibitor, or gene elimination of Trpm4 were reported to be associated with a shift toward anti-inflammatory microglia/macrophage polarization and improved neurologic outcomes [35]. Therefore, while TRPM4 appears dispensable for the temperature-dependent motility phenotype examined in this study, it may still modulate other microglial functions.

A greater challenge in elucidating the molecular basis of microglial motility is the limited tractability of microglia for genetic manipulation. The commonly used microglial cell lines show poor motility, and primary microglia readily shift toward an activated phenotype, complicating functional analyses [36]. While RNA interference and overexpression strategies are straightforward in immortalized lines, robust manipulation of primary microglia typically requires viral transduction and careful control of the activation state; even then, quantitative single-cell motility assays are not easy. In this context, primary cultures derived from genetically modified mice provide a practical and physiologically relevant platform to assess candidate molecules.

This study also highlights the technical limitations in validating TRPM4 protein expression in the brain and microglia, likely due to low endogenous abundance and antibody performance. Nevertheless, the absence of the TRPM4 band in high-expressing tissues from Trpm4-deficient animals supports that the generated mouse line harbors a systemic Trpm4 null allele. We anticipate that this mouse line will be useful beyond the present study, facilitating future work on TRPM4 function across tissues and disease models.

In summary, we established a Trpm4-knockout mouse line and used primary microglia to determine the role of TRPM4 in temperature-dependent microglial motility. Our results show that TRPM4 is not required for microglial temperature sensitivity in vitro, refining the mechanistic framework for the TRPV4-dependent thermosensitivity of microglial dynamics.
