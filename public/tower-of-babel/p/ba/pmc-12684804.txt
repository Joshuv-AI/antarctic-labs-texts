Predictive Value of Serum N‐Terminal pro‐B‐Type Natriuretic Peptide and Troponin T for Incident Heart Failure: A Meta‐Analysis of 9 International Cohorts
Authors: Nisha Bansal, Morgan E Grams, Josef Coresh, Kunihiro Matsushita, Shoshana H Ballew, Yingying Sang, Aditya Surapaneni, Johan Ärnlöv, Samira Bell, Jarett D Berry, Kevin Damman, James A de Lemos, Mirela Dobre, Shih‐Jen Hwang, Ron T Gansevoort, Michael G Shlipak, Markus P Schneider, for the CKD Prognosis Consortium
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrior prediction equations for heart failure (HF) omitted cardiac biomarkers and used select populations. We assessed the added value of NT‐proBNP (NT‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide) and hsTnT (high‐sensitivity troponin T) as predictors of HF, across a broad population, including participants with chronic kidney disease or atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.MethodsAmong 41 427 individuals free of HF from 9 prospective cohort studies, we performed an individual‐participant data meta‐analysis, quantifying the associations of NT‐proBNP and hsTnT with incident HF when added to a clinical model. Changes in Harrel’s C‐statistic with and without NT‐proBNP or hsTnT were estimated within each cohort and then pooled using random effects meta‐analysis.ResultsOver a mean of 11 years, 4599 incident HFs occurred. A 2‐fold higher NT‐proBNP was associated with incident HF (meta‐analyzed hazard ratio [HR], 1.47 [95% CI, 1.38–1.56]), P<0.001, with no interaction between NT‐proBNP and estimated glomerular filtration rate (HR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.94–1.02], P=0.33) or atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (HR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.93–1.02], P=0.48). Adding NT‐proBNP significantly increased the C‐statistic by 0.030 (95% CI, 0.021–0.040, P<0.001) over the clinical model. Similar results were seen for hsTnT [meta‐analyzed HR per 2‐fold higher hsTnT 1.41 (95% CI, 1.30–1.54), P<0.001], but C‐statistic increased by only 0.008 (95% CI, 0.005–0.011, P<0.001). In models that included NT‐proBNP, addition of hsTnT had a smaller effect (change in C‐statistic, 0.002 [95% CI, 0.001–0.003], P<0.001).ConclusionsNT‐proBNP improved risk discrimination of incident HF when added to traditional HF risk factors, even in individuals with chronic kidney disease and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. The contribution of hsTnT was modest. Measurement of NT‐proBNP may help identify individuals at risk of HF.Keywords: atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, NT‐proBNP, troponin TSubject Categories: Epidemiology, Risk Factors

Research Perspective

What Is New?

This large study of >40 000 individuals found that the addition of the widely available cardiac biomarker, NT‐proBNP (NT‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide), improved prediction of heart failure, including among patients with kidney disease or preexisting heart disease.

What Question Should Be Addressed Next?

These data suggest that efforts to implement the use of natriuretic peptides for screening and prevention of heart failure should be evaluated.

These data suggest that efforts to implement the use of natriuretic peptides for screening and prevention of heart failure should be evaluated.

The incidence and prevalence of heart failure (HF) continue to rise worldwide, 1 contributing to higher risks of death, repeat hospitalizations, and poor quality of life. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 There are now effective therapies for prevention of HF, 7 and thus early identification of individuals at risk for HF could inform clinical management.

HF prediction equations have been previously developed and validated in different populations. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Most recently, the American Heart Association Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease Events (PREVENT) equation was developed to predict 10‐year risk of incident HF in individuals without prevalent cardiovascular disease. 12 The PREVENT equation focused on a preselected list of traditional risk factors including age, sex, diabetes, smoking, body mass index (BMI), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) but did not include widely available cardiac biomarkers, which may provide important information on early HF pathophysiology.

NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide) is secreted from cardiac myocytes in response to myocardial stretch from pressure or volume overload and can often precede the development of HF. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 The American College of Cardiology, American Heart Association, and Heart Failure Society of America guidelines highlight the role of NT‐proBNP in the diagnosis and management of HF, noting its utility in both acute and chronic settings. Concentrations of hsTnT (high‐sensitivity troponin T) rise in response to myocardial injury or myocardial remodeling, which may be due to ischemia, increased myocardial strain, or other myocardial stress, which may lead to HF over time. Elevated hs‐TnT levels are associated with the severity of HF symptoms, left ventricular dysfunction, and adverse prognosis, independent of NT‐proBNP levels. 17 , 18 There have been numerous reports demonstrating strong and consistent associations of elevated NT‐proBNP and TnT with incident HF in diverse populations. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 These associations have even been observed in those with chronic kidney disease (CKD), despite the influence of decreased kidney excretion on circulating levels of these biomarkers, leading to higher levels of both NT‐proBNP and TnT.

It is therefore possible that NT‐proBNP and troponin may provide additional risk information for incident HF beyond traditional risk factors and across a broader patient population. Previous studies of HF risk prediction have included a limited number of participants, focused on US cohorts only, or have included select populations, for example, those without atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) or only those with normal kidney function. Therefore, we examined the consistency of multivariable adjusted associations between absolute NT‐proBNP and hsTnT levels and incident HF across 9 cohorts (including 2 cohorts with CKD) and patient characteristics including level of kidney function.

METHODS

Data Availability and Sharing Statement

Under agreement with the participating cohorts, CKD Prognosis Consortium cannot share individual data with third parties. Inquiries regarding specific analyses should be made to ckdpc@nyulangone.org. Investigators may approach the original cohorts regarding their own policies for data sharing (eg, https://aric.cscc.unc.edu/aric9/researchers/Obtain_Submit_Data for the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study).

Study Population

The study population was derived from a subset of cohorts contributing data to the CKD Prognosis Consortium, a global consortium of cohorts with information on kidney function and subsequent outcomes. 25 Investigators collaborating with the CKD Prognosis Consortium were invited to participate in the current meta‐analysis if their represented cohorts included participants with eGFR, albuminuria, laboratory values for NT‐proBNP, and ≥50 HF events during subsequent follow‐up. Nine cohorts (including 2 cohorts with CKD) had adequate data to be included, and all agreed to participate. Laboratory values for cardiac troponin were also requested but were not a requirement for inclusion in this study. Additional information on the selection of included cohorts is detailed in Data S1. At the cohort level, participants were included if they had nonmissing demographic and clinical covariates, NT‐proBNP measured, and no prior history of HF. This study was approved by the institutional review board at the New York University Grossman School of Medicine, New York, NY, USA (#i23‐00919). Given the use of secondary limited data in this analysis, a waiver for informed consent was approved for this analysis.

NT‐proBNP and High‐Sensitivity Troponin T

NT‐proBNP was measured using Roche assays across all cohorts, with biospecimen collection following the research protocols of the individual cohorts. Data S1 details the assay information for each cohort. Values under the lower limit of detection were imputed as 2.5 pg/mL. For analyses of NT‐proBNP as a continuous exposure, values were log‐base2‐transformed. For categorical analyses, NT‐proBNP was assessed as a binary variable, <125 or ≥125 pg/mL, based on the recommended cutoff for ambulatory patients. 26 hsTnT was assessed as a secondary exposure. Most cohorts used Roche assays on plasma to quantify hsTnT. Because of the large number of values below the lower limit of detection, hsTnT was categorized for primary analyses based on guideline recommendations, with bins for undetectable, <10 or ≥10 ng/L in women, and <15 or ≥15 ng/L in men. 26 , 27 In secondary analyses, hsTnT was modeled as a continuous exposure and undetectable values were imputed as 3 ng/L.

Incident Heart Failure

Incident HF was defined as any hospitalization or death with heart failure. Details on how each cohort or data set defined incident HF (including International Classification of Diseases [ICD] codes) and causes of death are summarized in Data S1.

Covariates

Demographic data on age and sex were included based on self‐report. Measurements of systolic blood pressure (SBP), use of antihypertensive medications, diabetes, current smoking, height and weight (to calculate BMI), eGFR based on serum creatinine, urine albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (ACR), and history of ASCVD were collected according to research protocols (detailed descriptions of how these were derived in Data S1).

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics and mean follow‐up time were reported within each cohort overall and stratified by the presence or absence of ASCVD. The distribution of NT‐proBNP and hsTnT were reported using violin plots as well as through median and interquartile range within participants with values in the detectable range within each cohort.

To assess whether there might be differences in NT‐proBNP calibration across cohorts, linear regression was used to model log2‐NT‐proBNP as a function of age, sex, SBP, diabetes, current smoking status, BMI, eGFR, antihypertensive medication use, log‐ACR, ASCVD, and the interaction between ASCVD and age, as well as interaction between ASCVD and the 2 eGFR spline terms within each cohort. Then, estimated NT‐proBNP was plotted as a function of age and as a function of eGFR for a standardized set of covariates in each cohort.

To assess whether NT‐proBNP was associated with incident HF beyond traditional risk factors, a base Cox proportional hazards model was fitted separately in each cohort with the following covariates to be consistent with the PREVENT‐HF equations: age, sex, SBP splines, diabetes, current smoking status, BMI splines, eGFR splines, antihypertensive medication use, log‐ACR, and the interaction terms of antihypertensive medication with SBP >110 mm Hg, age with SBP >110 mm Hg, age with diabetes status, age with current smoking status, and age with eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2; additional covariates beyond the PREVENT score were ASCVD, age with ASCVD, and ASCVD with SBP >110 mm Hg. Adjusted hazard ratios were estimated in each cohort and pooled using random effects meta‐analysis (metan command in Stata). The I2 statistic was estimated to determine heterogeneity. The regressions were performed using NT‐proBNP as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable. In sensitivity analyses, we used fixed‐effects meta‐analyses.

Age was scaled per 10 years older, and linear splines were used to model SBP per 20 mm Hg higher with a knot at 110 mm Hg, BMI per 5 kg/m2 with a knot at 30 kg/m2, and eGFR per 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 lower with a knot at 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. ACR was natural log‐transformed.

To determine whether the strength of association between NT‐proBNP and incident HF was modified in different scenarios, we performed several sensitivity analyses. First, to assess for confounding by albuminuria, we repeated models without adjustment for log‐ACR. Second, we included interaction terms between NT‐proBNP and the following characteristics in separate models: BMI ≥30 kg/m2, eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, ASCVD, diabetes, and log‐ACR. Third, because BMI and diabetes are highly correlated, we tested the interactions between NT‐proBNP and diabetes and NT‐proBNP and BMI ≥30 kg/m2 in a single model. Finally, to assess whether risks were similar over time, we tested the interaction of NT‐proBNP and follow‐up time, defined as <5 years, 5 to <10 years, and 10+ years.

To assess whether including NT‐proBNP improved discrimination of incident HF, Harrel’s C‐statistic was estimated for the base model alone. Then, log2‐NT‐proBNP was added to the model, and the C‐statistic was recalculated. Change in C‐statistic between the 2 models was estimated within each cohort and then pooled using random effects meta‐analysis. The same exercise was performed using NT‐proBNP as a binary variable.

To quantify the relative importance of NT‐proBNP compared with SBP and hypertensive medications, diabetes, smoking, BMI, eGFR, log‐ACR, and ASCVD, we evaluated the change in C‐statistic from a model including all variables compared with a model removing each of the variables of interest. This exercise was performed within each cohort, followed by pooling via random‐effects meta‐analysis of the change in C‐statistic variable.

To assess the individual importance of hsTnT as a predictor for incident HF, a base Cox proportional hazards model was fitted separately in each cohort with the same covariates as with NT‐proBNP. Adjusted hazard ratios associated with hsTnT were estimated in each cohort and pooled using random effects meta‐analysis. To assess whether including hsTnT improved discrimination of incident HF, Harrel’s C‐statistic was estimated for the base model alone, then reestimated with the addition of hsTnT. Change in C‐statistic between the 2 models was estimated within each cohort and then pooled using random effects meta‐analysis and, in sensitivity analysis, using fixed effects meta‐analysis.

To demonstrate the differences in absolute risk of incident HF across cohorts according to different risk characteristics, we estimated 10‐year predicted risk of HF in each cohort using a cause‐specific model of HF accounting for the competing events of mortality. 28 This was done by applying the meta‐analyzed hazard ratios in each cohort to estimate the cohort‐specific adjusted base hazard, and then applying these estimates to calculate a linear prediction for the uniform characteristics of age 55 years, male sex, SBP 130 mm Hg, BMI 25 kg/m2, no smoking, and no antihypertensive medications while varying the following 4 characteristics: presence or absence of diabetes, presence or absence of ASCVD; eGFR of 30, 60, and 90 mL/min/1.73 m2; and NT‐proBNP levels of 25, 125, and 625 ng/L. A similar analysis was conducted for the hsTNT categories not detectable, low (<10 ng/L in women or <15 ng/L in men), and high (≥10 ng/L in women or ≥15 ng/L in men).

All analyses were conducted using Stata MP 18.1. All statistical testing was 2 sided. Statistical significance was determined as P<0.05.

RESULTS

Characteristics of the Study Population

Among 41 427 individuals across the 9 studies, mean age ranged from 45 to 76 years and percentage female ranged from 30% to 61% (Table 1). Mean SBP was between 120 and 130 mm Hg for most studies, with a large proportion of participants taking antihypertensive medications. Percentage of participants with diabetes ranged from 4% to 46% across cohorts, with the highest proportion in the cohorts with CKD. In the 2 cohorts with CKD, mean eGFR was 45 and 53 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Table 1) Participants with ASCVD within each cohort had higher BP, were taking more BP medications, and were more likely to have diabetes and lower eGFR (Table 1).

Association of NT‐proBNP With Incident Heart Failure

There was a strong and statistically significant association between higher NT‐proBNP (per 2‐fold higher as well as >125 pg/mL) with incident HF across all studies (Table S1). In adjusted meta‐analyses, every 2‐fold higher NT‐proBNP was associated with greater risk of incident HF (hazard ratio [HR], 1.47 [95% CI, 1.38–1.56], P<0.001). A NT‐proBNP >125 pg/mL was associated with a 2‐fold higher risk of incident HF (HR, 2.51 [95% CI 2.14–2.95], P<0.001) (Figure 2A and 2B). Findings were similar when ACR was not included in the model (Figure S2 and S2). Findings were qualitatively consistent and slightly attenuated when using fixed‐effects meta‐analyses (Figure S3). In the multivariable meta‐analyzed model, diabetes, age, and ASCVD were strong risk factors for incident HF (Table S2).

In sensitivity analyses of meta‐analyzed models of NT‐proBNP with incident HF, there were no significant interactions of NT‐proBNP with age, sex, eGFR, ACR, and ASCVD. However, there were significant interactions of NT‐proBNP with diabetes and incident HF, as well as NT‐proBNP, BMI, and incident HF, whereby associations between NT‐proBNP and incident HF were weaker in those with diabetes and with higher BMI (Table S3). There was also a statistically significant association by time with the strongest association observed between higher NT‐proBNP and risk of incident HF within 5 years, which weakened over time.

NT‐proBNP and Prediction of Incident Heart Failure

The base model that included the PREVENT variables of age, sex, SBP, diabetes, ASCVD, smoking history, BMI, eGFR, antihypertensive medication, and ACR had C‐statistic values across studies that ranged from 0.68 to 0.86. For all studies, addition of either continuous log2NT‐proBNP or NT‐proBNP >125 pg/mL to the model significantly increased the C‐statistic (mean increase ranging from 0.016 to 0.066). In the meta‐analyzed model, the continuous log2NT‐proBNP significantly increased the C‐statistic by 0.030 (95% CI, 0.021–0.040, P<0.001) and NT‐proBNP >125 pg/mL increased the C‐statistic by 0.015 (95% CI, 0.011–0.020, P<0.001) (Table 3). In comparison with other variables in the model, NT‐proBNP had by far the greatest effect on the C‐statistic (overall mean change in C‐statistic 0.030), even when compared with eGFR (overall mean change in C‐statistic 0.001) and ASCVD (overall mean change in C‐statistic 0.004) (Table S4).

HsTnT and Risk of Incident Heart Failure

The median hsTnT was higher in the CKD studies and had a wide range of concentrations (Table 2 and Figure S4). The proportion of participants with undetectable hsTnT ranged from 15% to 74% across the 9 cohorts (Table 2). The category of high hsTnT (>10 ng/L for women and >15 ng/L for men) was associated with higher risk of incident HF (meta‐analyzed HR, 1.82 95% CI, 1.512.20, P<0.001) (Figure 2D). Results were similar but slightly attenuated when using fixed‐effects meta‐analyses (Figure S3). Addition of categorical hsTnT to the meta‐analyzed based model significantly increased the C‐statistic by 0.006 (95% CI, 0.0040.008, P<0.001) (Table 3). Similar results were seen using continuous hsTnT (per 2‐fold higher hsTnT: HR, 1.41 [95% CI, 1.301.54], P<0.001) (Figure 2C and Table S1). However, in the models that included NT‐proBNP, the addition of hsTnT had a more modest effect on the C‐statistic (meta‐analyzed change in C‐statistic, 0.002 [95% CI, 0.0010.003], P<0.001) (Table S5). In meta‐analyzed models of hsTnT with incident HF, there were no significant interactions of hsTnT with age, sex, BMI, diabetes, ACR, and ASCVD (Table S6). There was a significant interaction of hsTnT with eGFR on the association with incident HF, whereby associations between hsTnT and incident HF were weaker in those with eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Similar to the interaction results in NT‐proBNP, the association between higher hsTnT and risk of incident HF weakened over time.

In models examining 10‐year absolute risk of incident HF, there was a graded and consistent association between higher NT‐proBNP and higher 10‐year absolute risk of incident HF across levels of eGFR, diabetes status, and presence of ASCVD (Figure 3A). There was also a graded and consistent association between higher hsTnT and 10‐year absolute risk of incident HF. DISCUSSION

In this meta‐analysis of >41 000 individuals enrolled in 9 international cohorts (including 2 cohorts with CKD), we found that NT‐proBNP significantly and consistently improved discrimination of risk of incident HF when added to a base model of traditional HF risk factors. NT‐proBNP was a stronger risk factor than other known risk factors for HF including eGFR and ASCVD. In contrast, although hsTnT was strongly associated with incident HF, the change in C‐statistic with the addition of hsTnT was modest, particularly when NT‐proBNP was included in the model. In view of the rising prevalence of HF globally, 1 and considering the associated morbidity, mortality, and health care costs, 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 measurement of NT‐proBNP (in addition to other common risk factors) may aid in identifying individuals at risk of HF earlier, enabling targeted interventions.

We demonstrated that the addition of NT‐proBNP to traditional risk factors strongly improved prediction of HF risk in the ambulatory setting, as demonstrated by a significant increase in the C‐statistic when NT‐proBNP was added to a model that included 10 traditional risk factors for HF. Biologically, NT‐proBNP is released from cardiac myocytes in response to myocardial stretch from pressure, volume overload 13 or increasing left ventricular mass. 14 , 15 , 16 Prior studies in select populations have also demonstrated that NT‐proBNP improves discrimination of future risk of HF. 29 , 30 For example, in a cohort of patients with type 2 diabetes, addition of NT‐proBNP to a baseline prediction model significantly increased the C‐statistic for a composite cardiovascular outcome from 0.723 to 0.731. 31 In a study of 28 general population cohorts free of cardiovascular disease, NT‐proBNP was strongly associated with HF and, similar to our findings, significantly increased the C‐statistic by 0.0399. 32 Given the relatively strong baseline discriminatory performance of currently used prediction models, even increases in C‐statistic in this range are considered clinically meaningful. Our study builds upon these prior reports by studying a diverse population including those with a range of kidney function, including advanced CKD, as well as those with and without ASCVD, which may increase generalizability of measurement of NT‐proBNP to broader populations.

We found that NT‐proBNP significantly improved discrimination for incident HF in patients with CKD with a broad range of eGFR (including 2 dedicated cohorts with CKD), which have been excluded from other previous studies. People with CKD have 3‐fold higher risk of incident HF compared with those without CKD, 33 , 34 but there is controversy as to the validity of NT‐proBNP in CKD given higher concentrations with reduced kidney clearance. Although we did observe strong associations between lower eGFR and higher NT‐proBNP, the improvement in HF discrimination with addition of NT‐proBNP was similar in the cohorts with and without CKD. Moreover, when NT‐proBNP was treated as a binary variable with a relatively low threshold of 125 ng/L, NT‐proBNP still improved discrimination at lower eGFR values.

We found that NT‐proBNP was a strong predictor of incident HF in individuals with and without ASCVD, as well as those with and without diabetes (although this was stronger in those without diabetes). These data highlight the expanded use of this cardiac biomarker in broad populations, even in those with known HF risk factors. For example, HF and ASCVD, although overlapping, are still 2 distinct CVD subtypes. Prior investigations have focused on those without ASCVD. For example, a recent large analysis from 28 general population‐based cohorts without ASCVD found that from several biomarkers, NT‐proBNP best predicted HF. 32 The recently published PREVENT HF risk equation also excluded individuals with ASCVD. However, given the overlap between HF and ASCVD, those with ASCVD also represent a high‐risk population for HF and further risk stratification can be challenging. We also noted significant associations of NT‐proBNP with incident HF in those with and without diabetes, although the association was stronger for those without diabetes. Although the range of NT‐proBNP may be higher in this population, it appears it may also add prognostic value independent of other known HF risk factors, such as diabetes.

In this regard, our analyses support the concept of HF stages as recently proposed in the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Failure Society of America Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure. 26 This guideline proposes to classify all subjects according to severity into 1 of 4 HF stages: “stage A: at risk for HF,” “stage B: subjects with pre‐HF,” “stage C: symptomatic HF,” or “stage D: advanced HF.” Stage A: at risk for HF includes all subjects without any symptoms or signs of HF, and with no evidence of structural/functional heart disease or abnormal biomarkers, but with at least 1 clinical risk factor for the development of HF. The presence of diabetes or ASCVD are mentioned here as key clinical risk factors (together with arterial hypertension and obesity), each one sufficient to allocate such individuals into this early HF stage. Stage B: subjects with pre‐HF, as a higher risk category, includes subjects without any symptoms or signs of HF, but with evidence of structural or functional heart disease (eg, by echocardiography) or abnormal biomarkers, with elevated natriuretic peptides or persistently elevated troponins both being mentioned here. Our analyses strongly support this concept, in that the presence of elevated biomarkers indicate an even greater risk of incident HF than in the presence of clinical risk factors alone, even when strong clinical risk factors such as diabetes or ASCVD are present.

In comparison to NT‐proBNP, hsTnT held less prognostic information in our analyses, with an even more modest effect on the C‐statistic when clinical models also included NT‐proBNP. It should be noted that some cohorts had a large proportion of participants with undetectable hsTnT, which may further limit the clinical utility of this biomarker. TnT levels rise in response to myocardial injury, remodeling, or left ventricular hypertrophy. 17 , 18 Although TnT is strongly associated with incident HF, in our study, NT‐proBNP had stronger associations and greater effect on discrimination and predictive ability for incident HF. The data from other studies on the discriminatory value of hsTnT have also varied. For example, systematic review of 16 studies found only half reported improvements in the C‐statistic for prediction of HF. 23 Data from our study and others may suggest less benefit in hsTnT testing for HF prediction, particularly when weighing costs and other resources. The preference for NT‐proBNP is also reflected in the aforementioned 2022 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Failure Society of America Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure, which gives screening of subjects at risk of HF (stage A) with natriuretic peptides a Class 2A (moderate strength) recommendation. Although persistently elevated troponin levels are being mentioned as an alternative biomarker in support of classifying subjects into the stage B: subjects with pre‐HF category, there is no such specific recommendation on its use.

Prediction models for HF have several important applications. Clinical risk scores could be used for risk stratification, guiding primary prevention strategies such as better BP management or lifestyle modifications, or could prompt clinicians to assess symptoms of subclinical HF more frequently for patients at high risk of HF. Clinical models to identify patients at high risk of HF might also be used for enrichment of clinical trials of HF therapies, especially as future therapies may need better risk stratification. 35

For implementation of a wider testing strategy, the issue of which cutoff value to use to indicate an abnormal test result is clearly relevant. Among the most important factors influencing NT‐proBNP levels, aside from cardiac stretch, are age, sex, and the level of kidney function. We examined associations of NT‐pro‐BNP levels with eGFR and age after adjusting for other risk factors and residual variation across cohorts to estimate the extent to which further standardization of measurement methods may be needed and useful. However, even after adjustment of NT‐proBNP levels for these and other clinical factors, we observed substantial residual variation in NT‐proBNP levels across the cohorts included in our analysis. Some of this variation may not be due to actual differences in NT‐proBNP levels between populations but due to differences between NT‐proBNP assays used. A wider testing strategy, and thus availability of more data, would help to better adjust measurements for common clinical factors and would also provide more population‐specific data. This would aid to define risk thresholds in conjunction with age, sex, and specific comorbidities, above which further diagnostic testing, such as echocardiography, would be recommended. So far, the Generation Scotland Scottish Family Health Study in >18 000 subjects has been the largest population‐based cohort to publish data on the association of demographic factors with NT‐proBNP levels. 36 This study has shown that an NT‐proBNP level ≥125 pg/mL is common not only in older subjects but surprisingly also in apparently healthy young female subjects. 36

Even considering the increased use of resources, testing of NT‐proBNP may have beneficial effects. The STOP‐HF (St Vincent’s Screening ti Prevent Heart Failure) trial in 1374 subjects at risk of HF showed that screening with BNP and performing echocardiography and referring those with levels ≥50 pg/mL to a cardiologist can reduce the development of left ventricular dysfunction, clinical HF, and reduce emergency hospitalizations. 37 Further, the PONTIAC (NT‐proBNP Guided Primary Prevention of CV Events in Diabetic Patients) study in 300 patients with type 2 diabetes showed that screening with NT‐proBNP and referring those with levels ≥125 pg/mL to a cardiologist increases uptitration of renin–angiotensin system inhibitor therapy and decreases hospitalization and death. 38 These studies, although limited in size, provide some first evidence of the effectiveness of natriuretic peptide‐based screening efforts in patients at risk of HF. However, a wider testing strategy for NT‐proBNP would need to be carefully balanced with cost and burden of testing, which may not be comparable in all health care settings. Prior studies have suggested that testing for NT‐proBNP may be cost effective in certain settings such as the emergency department or when the clinical presentation is ambigious. 39 , 40 Point‐of‐care testing may also be a promising strategy to reduce costs of NT‐proBNP testing. 41

Our study had several notable strengths. We studied a diverse, international population of >41 000 individuals across a range of ages and kidney function, which are important predictors of NT‐proBNP and hsTnT. We acknowledge some limitations as well. Measurement of NT‐proBNP and hsTnT were performed centrally by each cohort and there were differences in the assays that were used, which may have affected our findings. Our study used single baseline measures of NT‐proBNP and hsTnT, which may not capture changes over time, or any misclassifications. Furthermore, current assays may underestimate total NT‐proBNP because glycosylation often changes the bound epitope. Certain variables such as ACR were not available in all participants. Ascertainment of the incident HF events may have differed across cohorts with some using administrative codes and other central adjudication. We were not able to examine subtypes of HF in the analysis. The observational nature of this study prevents causal inferences. This was a meta‐analysis of research cohorts, which may not be generalizable to all patient populations.

Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrate that NT‐proBNP substantially improved the prediction of HF in a broad population sample, including individuals with ASCVD and those with CKD. Considering the robust data linking natriuretic peptides in the ambulatory setting to the future development of HF, as well as the increasing availability of interventions that can prevent the development of HF, efforts to implement the use of natriuretic peptides for screening and prevention of HF should be evaluated.
