Family support, stigma, and psychological detachment in hemodialysis patients: a cross-sectional mediation analysis
Authors: Liu Cong, Wu Jianyi, Lyu Xiaoran, Wang Qin, Liu Nian, Li Xianglin, You Lang, Zou Li, Wei Yongting, Xi Zuyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients face profound psychological challenges that impede their quality of life and treatment outcomes. Understanding the interplay between family support, illness-related stigma, fear of progression, and psychological detachment is crucial for developing effective psychosocial interventions.AimsThis study aimed to investigate the relationships among family support, stigma, fear of disease progression (FoP), and psychological detachment in MHD patients, and to examine the mediating roles of stigma and FoP.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from November 2024 to January 2025. Using convenience sampling, 232 MHD patients were recruited from a hemodialysis center in China. Data were collected using the Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form (FoP-Q-SF), Social Impact Scale (SIS), Family APGAR Index, and the Psychological Detachment Scale for MHD. Mediation analysis was performed using the PROCESS macro (Model 6) with 5000 bootstrap samples.ResultsThe mean score for psychological detachment was 193.10 ± 24.37. Family support was positively correlated with FoP (r = 0.16, p < 0.05) and negatively correlated with stigma (r = -0.21, p < 0.01) and psychological detachment (r = -0.22, p < 0.01). Stigma was positively correlated with psychological detachment (r = 0.59, p < 0.01). Mediation analysis revealed that stigma significantly mediated the relationship between family support and psychological detachment (indirect effect = -1.33, 95% CI [-2.30, -0.25]), indicating that higher family support was associated with lower stigma, which in turn was associated with lower psychological detachment. However, fear of progression did not mediate this relationship (indirect effect = 0.07, 95% CI [-0.05, 0.30]), nor did it function as a chain mediator with stigma (indirect effect = 0.05, 95% CI [-0.02, 0.13]). These findings suggest that stigma, but not fear of progression, is a key mechanism linking family support to psychological detachment in MHD patients.ConclusionPsychological detachment in MHD patients is influenced by family support through the mediating mechanism of stigma, while fear of progression does not play a mediating role.Interventions aimed at enhancing family-centered care, reducing illness-related stigma, and addressing excessive fear of progression may improve patients’ psychological detachment and overall psychosocial well-being.Clinical trial registrationThis study is a cross-sectional observational study, and clinical trial registration is not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04953-7.Keywords: Maintenance hemodialysis, Psychological disengagement, Stigma, Fear of progression, Family support, Mediating effect

Introduction

Maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) is the most prevalent renal replacement therapy for patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD). By the end of 2022, the population of MHD patients in mainland China exceeded 840,000, ranking first worldwide [1]. Although MHD sustains life, it imposes a relentless burden. Patients endure not only physical complications like cardiovascular disease and mineral bone disorders but also a significant decline in health-related quality of life [2, 3]. The relentless treatment schedule, dietary restrictions, and functional limitations act as chronic stressors, predisposing patients to varied psychological distress, including depression, anxiety, and restlessness [4, 5].

The stress process theory [6] posits that an individual’s health outcome under stress is shaped by the interplay of external resources and internal appraisals. Within this framework, psychological detachment emerges as a positive coping mechanism. Derived from the effort-recovery theory [7], it refers to the individual’s ability to mentally disengage from work (or illness-related) stressors during off-time, allowing for the recovery of depleted cognitive and emotional resources. In the context of MHD, psychological detachment involves both physical and cognitive disengagement from the dialysis environment and illness-related worries during inter-dialytic periods, facilitating stress reduction and emotional replenishment.

Conversely, fear of disease progression (FoP) represents a maladaptive cognitive-emotional response, defined as the apprehension about disease worsening, recurrence, and its potential physical and social consequences [8]. Prolonged and severe FoP is closely linked to depression and anxiety [9]. Stigma, often termed the social impact of illness, refers to the internalized sense of shame, social rejection, and devaluation experienced by patients due to their disease status [10]. In dialysis populations, stigma can trigger emotional distress, reduce treatment adherence, and lead to social withdrawal, acting as a significant risk factor for depression [11]. Fear of progression (FoP) refers to patients’ apprehension about disease worsening, recurrence, and its impacts on physical and social functioning [8]. Stigma denotes the internalized shame, social rejection, and devaluation experienced due to illness, often manifested as social isolation and self-devaluation [10]. Psychological detachment, within the framework of positive psychology, refers to the ability to mentally disengage from illness-related stressors, facilitating emotional recovery and resource replenishment [7]. However, it is important to note that psychological detachment is not inherently adaptive. While moderate detachment may facilitate recovery, excessive or context-inappropriate detachment can reflect avoidance coping, wherein patients disengage not to recover, but to escape from persistent illness-related stressors [12]. In chronically ill populations, such avoidance has been linked to poorer psychosocial adjustment and increased emotional distress [3].

Theoretically, one might anticipate that stigma, as a negative social experience, could drive a desire for psychological withdrawal or detachment. However, the nature of this relationship—whether it is direct or mediated by other emotional states like fear—remains empirically unexamined in MHD patients. Furthermore, external resources like family support are crucial buffers. Research indicates that robust family function and support are intricately associated with better quality of life and psychological adaptation in hemodialysis patients [13, 14].

It is important to note that stigma and coping processes like hope and detachment are highly context-sensitive, shaped by cultural and social norms [15, 16]. In collectivist societies like China and Iran, the family unit holds paramount importance. Qualitative research with Iranian ESRD patients highlights that hope—a positive future-oriented mindset—is deeply intertwined with family support and spiritual beliefs, serving as a key strategy to counter negative emotions like uncertainty and despair [13]. Another study found that hope fully mediated the effects of social support and uncertainty on self-management behaviors [16], underscoring the importance of positive psychological resources in chronic illness management.

However, existing studies on MHD patients’ psychology often focus on isolated variables. A comprehensive model exploring how family support influences psychological detachment through the serial mechanisms of stigma and FoP is lacking. Clarifying this pathway is crucial for developing targeted, multi-level interventions.

Methods

Participant selection

We used convenience sampling to recruit MHD patients from the Blood Purification Center of Yichang Central People’s Hospital, Hubei Province, between November 2024 and January 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age > 18 years; (2) receiving MHD for ≥ 3 months; (3) clear consciousness, normal intelligence and hearing, able to complete the questionnaire. Exclusion criteria included: (1) severe comorbid organ dysfunction (e.g., NYHA Class IV heart failure); (2) major negative life events (e.g., bereavement) within the past 3 months; (3) diagnosed mental illness (e.g., schizophrenia); (4) non-local temporary dialysis patients; (5) scheduled for kidney transplantation or peritoneal dialysis within 3 months.

The sample size was calculated using the formula for cross-sectional studies: n = 4[(µα + µβ)/ln((1 + r)/(1 - r))]² + 3, with α = 0.05 and β = 0.1 [17]. Based on a pilot study indicating a minimum correlation coefficient of r = 0.269 among key variables, the minimum required sample size was 142. Accounting for a 20% invalid response rate, the target sample size was 178. We ultimately distributed 250 questionnaires and obtained 232 valid responses, exceeding the minimum target to enhance statistical power.

Survey instruments

Demographic questionnaire

The researchers designed the demographic questionnaire in accordance with the research’s objectives and relevance, including age, gender, marital status, educational level, employment status, family monthly per capita income, dialysis duration, and medical payment method. The Demographic Questionnaire was self-designed for this study, and its English version is available in Supplementary File S2.

Fear of progression questionnaire-short form

The Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form (FoP-Q-SF)was originally developed by Mehnert et al. [8] in 2006 through the simplification of the Fear of Progression Scale, was translated into Chinese and adapted by Wu Qiyun et al. [18] in 2015, was used in this study, with written permission obtained from the original developers (Mehnert team) via email [see 2.5 Ethics considerations].It assesses fears related to physical health (e.g., “I am afraid of pain”) and social-familial consequences (e.g., “I am worried about being a burden to my relatives”). It consists of 12 items across two dimensions: Physical Health and Social-Family. The total scores, derived on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “never,” 5 = “always”), range from 12 to 60, with higher scores indicating an increased fear of progression. The Cronbach’s α coefficient is 0.883. The total Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale in the study was 0.888.

Stigma scale

We used the Social Impact Scale (SIS) developed by Fife et al. [10] to measure the social and psychological impact of the disease, specifically stigma. Revised and translated by Pan et al. [19] in 2007.Authorization for using the Chinese version was confirmed by Prof. Fife’s team via email [see 2.5 Ethics considerations].The scale includes four dimensions: Social Rejection(e.g., “I feel others are uncomfortable with me”), (9 items), Economic Discrimination (3 items), Internal Shame (5 items), and Social Isolation (7 items), amounting to a total of 24 items. The 4-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree,” 2 = “disagree,” 3 = “agree,” 4 = “strongly agree”) yields total scores between 24 and 96, where higher scores signify greater stigma. The Cronbach’s α coefficient is 0.85–0.90 [10]. The total Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale in the study was 0.951.

Family APGAR index

Designed by American physician Smilkstein [20] and introduced to China by Chinese scholars LYU Fan et al. [21] in 1995, is a public-domain instrument requiring no specific authorizationthis. The scale assesses patients’ satisfaction with family function by evaluating their perception of family care level. The scale, exhibiting a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.83, comprises five items: Adaptation, Partnership, Growth, Affection, and Resolve. Utilizing a 3-point Likert scale (2 = “often,” 1 = “sometimes,” 0 = “almost never”), total scores range from 0 to 10. Tota scores of 0–3 indicate severe family dysfunction, 4–6 denote moderate dysfunction, and 7–10 reflect healthy family function. The total Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale in the study was 0.895.

Psychological detachment scale for MHD patients

Tian Chunying [22] meticulously developed this scale and constructed an item pool to assess psychological detachment levels in MHD patients, drawing from qualitative research findings on psychological detachment in MHD patients and referencing research on hemodialysis complications [13, 23, 24], employment status, [25], marital quality, and positive disease perceptions. Its English version is provided in Supplementary File S3. The scale includes 55 items across five dimensions: Physical, Psychological, Social, Environmental, and Positive Feelings–Gains. Total scores, utilizing a 5-point Likert scale (1–5), can reach a maximum of 275. Higher scores indicate higher levels of positive factors influencing psychological detachment. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient is 0.949. The total Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale in the study was 0.921.The scale was developed through a rigorous process including item generation based on qualitative interviews with MHD patients, expert review, and pilot testing [22]. Exploratory factor analysis supported the five-dimensional structure, explaining 68.4% of the total variance. Confirmatory factor analysis in the present study indicated acceptable construct validity (CFI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.06).

Data collection

Data were collected from November 5, 2024, to January 30, 2025, marking the duration of data collection.The investigators contacted the medical personnel at the blood purification center in advance. Subsequently, with the aid of the medical personnel at the blood purifying center, the investigators enlisted patients to partake in this survey. The researchers presented the objective and details of the study to the participants. After obtaining the patient’s electronic informed consent form the investigators disseminated the questionnaires and apprised them of the pertinent considerations for completion. Demographic data was self-reported by the patients, whereas disease-related information was obtained by the investigator from the patients’ medical records. For patients unable to independently complete the questionnaire, the investigator read the items aloud and recorded the responses accordingly. Upon completion of the questionnaire, a token of appreciation was bestowed upon the patient. After collection, a researcher verified completeness, excluding obviously erroneous responses, such as work experience listed for age. Invalid questionnaires were identified based on specific criteria: ① Response time below the theoretical minimum (30 min); ② Regular responses (e.g., 10 consecutive items with the same option); ③ Logical contradictions (e.g., age < 18 years but dialysis duration ≥ 5 years). Two researchers independently determined invalidity, while a third researcher mediated the disputes. We distributed a total of 250 questionnaires, yielding 232 valid responses (valid return rate: 92.8%).

Statistical analysis

Data were exported from Wenjuanxing as .sav files and analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and AMOS 26.0. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were calculated for demographic and clinical characteristics. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine bivariate relationships among family support, stigma, fear of progression, and psychological detachment.

Prior to testing the mediation hypotheses, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using AMOS 26.0 to assess the construct validity of the measurement model. Model fit was evaluated using the following indices: chi‑square/degrees of freedom (χ²/df), goodness‑of‑fit index (GFI), comparative fit index (CFI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). Acceptable model fit was defined as χ²/df < 3, GFI > 0.90, CFI > 0.90, and RMSEA < 0.08.

To test the hypothesized chain mediation model, we used the PROCESS macro (Version 4.2, Model 6) for SPSS with 5000 bootstrap samples. This macro employs ordinary least squares regression to estimate direct and indirect effects and generates bias‑corrected 95% confidence intervals for indirect effects. An indirect effect is considered statistically significant if its confidence interval does not include zero. All statistical tests were two‑tailed, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

Levels and influencing factors of psychological detachment

Before discussing the findings, it is important to clarify the conceptualization of psychological detachment in this study. While detachment can serve as an adaptive recovery mechanism [7], excessive or context-inappropriate detachment may reflect avoidance coping, wherein patients disengage not to recover, but to escape from persistent illness-related stressors [12]. In this study, the positive association between stigma and detachment (r = 0.59) suggests that higher detachment scores may indicate maladaptive withdrawal rather than healthy psychological recovery. Therefore, we interpret psychological detachment in MHD patients as a potentially double-edged construct, with higher levels in the context of stigma representing avoidance behavior.

The moderate level of psychological detachment (mean score 193.10) suggests there is considerable room for improvement in helping MHD patients achieve mental respite. The particularly low score in the Environmental dimension indicates patients struggle to find restorative settings outside the clinical environment, likely constrained by physical symptoms, fatigue, and social limitations [12, 27]. Consistent with prior research, symptoms like post-dialysis fatigue and pruritus create substantial barriers to psychological recovery [3].

Notably, older patients (≥ 61 years) and urban residents reported higher detachment levels. This may reflect an age-related narrowing of social roles or better access to supportive resources, recreational facilities, and social networks in urban areas [28]. Conversely, lower detachment scores among patients with low household income and those from rural areas underscore the exacerbating effect of socioeconomic disadvantage, where pressing survival and logistical concerns can overshadow the need for psychological recovery [25]. These disparities highlight the necessity for resource-sensitive and tailored interventions.

The mediating role of stigma

Our findings confirm stigma as a critical mechanism through which family support influences psychological detachment. Stronger perceived family support was associated with lower levels of internalized stigma (β = -1.03, p = 0.0017), which in turn was associated with lower psychological detachment (indirect effect = -1.33, 95% CI [-2.30, -0.25]). This negative indirect effect indicates that family support protects patients from the detrimental effects of stigma, thereby reducing their tendency toward maladaptive withdrawal. These results align with stress-buffering models and are consistent with research from similar cultural contexts. For instance, studies with Iranian hemodialysis patients emphasize the family as a foundational source of hope and a buffer against societal devaluation [15]. A supportive family environment provides emotional security, reduces feelings of shame and being a burden, and encourages continued social engagement, thereby mitigating the urge for maladaptive withdrawal [29, 30].

This underscores a key clinical implication: interventions must actively engage the family system. Assessing family function, providing psychoeducation to families, and facilitating open communication about the illness experience can strengthen this natural support network, thereby reducing stigma and fostering healthier forms of detachment.

The mediating role of fear of progression

Contrary to our hypothesis and some previous research, fear of progression (FoP) did not mediate the relationship between family support and psychological detachment. The indirect effects involving FoP—whether as a single mediator or in a chain with stigma—were not significant, as their confidence intervals included zero.

Several explanations may account for this finding. First, the bivariate correlation between stigma and FoP was negative (r = -0.41), indicating that patients with higher stigma reported lower fear of disease progression. This counterintuitive relationship may reflect an avoidance coping mechanism: individuals who experience high levels of shame and social rejection may suppress or deny their fears about the future as a way of managing emotional distress [31]. Such denial could lead to underreporting of fear on self-report measures.

Second, after controlling for stigma and family support, FoP was not significantly associated with psychological detachment (β = 0.63, p = 0.129). This suggests that fear of progression may operate independently of the family support–stigma pathway, and its relationship with detachment may be more complex than a simple linear mediation. Future research should explore potential moderators, such as coping styles or illness duration, that might influence the impact of FoP on psychological outcomes.

Importantly, the lack of mediation by FoP does not diminish its clinical relevance. Fear of progression remains a significant concern for many MHD patients, as evidenced by its mean score (46.98 ± 3.24). However, its role in the context of family support and stigma appears to be less direct than initially theorized.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

Strengths

This study has several strengths, including the novel integration of key psychosocial variables into a comprehensive SEM model specific to MHD, the use of robust statistical methods to test mediation, a focus on modifiable intervention targets, and the use of validated instruments with good reliability in our sample.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inferences. Second, the use of convenience sampling from a single center limits the generalizability of findings to other regions and healthcare settings. Third, the reliance on self-report measures may introduce response bias. Fourth, although informative, our model does not include other potentially relevant variables such as individual coping styles, resilience, personality traits, or spiritual well-being, which may also influence the adaptation process [15, 16]. Fifth, the relatively low proportion of rural patients (8.19%) in our sample restricts the generalizability of the findings to rural MHD populations. Sixth, the measure of psychological detachment used in this study, while reliable, requires further validation. Although it demonstrated good internal consistency and factorial validity in the development sample [22], its construct validity in diverse MHD populations warrants additional investigation.

Future Research

Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to establish causality and explore the dynamic nature of these relationships over time. Multi-center studies with larger, more geographically and socioeconomically diverse samples are needed. Researchers should also consider incorporating qualitative methods to gain deeper insights into the lived experience of stigma and detachment. Extending the model to include other resilience factors like hope, coping flexibility, and social support from non-family sources would provide a more comprehensive understanding of psychosocial adaptation in MHD.

Conclusion

This study reveals that psychological detachment in MHD patients is influenced by family support through the mediating mechanism of stigma. Higher family support was associated with lower stigma, which in turn was associated with lower psychological detachment—suggesting that stigma-driven withdrawal may be mitigated by strengthening family resources. Fear of progression, however, did not mediate this relationship, indicating that it operates through a different pathway. These findings highlight the importance of family-centered interventions aimed at reducing stigma, while also acknowledging that fear of progression requires separate clinical attention. A multifaceted approach addressing both social and emotional aspects of the illness experience is essential for promoting psychological well-being in patients undergoing long-term hemodialysis.
