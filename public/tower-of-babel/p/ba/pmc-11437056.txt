Hydrocarbon generation potential in Jurassic source rocks from hydrous pyrolysis experiments under ultradeep conditions
Authors: Zhanyuan Cao, Mingliang Liang, Xilong Zhang, Long Su
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the hydrocarbon generation potential of Jurassic source rocks under ultradeep conditions by utilizing semiclosed system hydrous pyrolysis experiments to simulate the geological processes at play. This study examines four distinct lithologies (shale, mudstone, carbonaceous mudstone, and coal) and three kerogen types (I, II, and III) across a temperature range of 250–650 °C and fluid pressures ranging from 34 to 100 MPa. The results indicate a bimodal distribution in total oil yields, with peak generation observed at approximately 350 °C and 600 °C. This pattern suggests a complex relationship between temperature, pressure, kerogen type, and hydrocarbon output. Notably, while I-Shale and II-Mudstone display higher hydrocarbon efficiency, III-type rocks exhibit superior oil and gas production rates because of their higher total organic carbon content. Research revealed that the oil expulsion rate exceeds 99% at temperatures above 500 °C, with the expelled oil predominantly comprising nonhydrocarbons and asphaltenes. Our findings suggest that Jurassic hydrocarbon source rocks maintain oil and gas potential under ultradeep conditions (high-over maturity stages at depths exceeding 6000 m). Under sustained fluid overpressure exceeding 70 MPa, the hydrocarbon generation peaks of various kerogen types in source rocks are markedly delayed. This phenomenon enables the source rocks to retain the potential for liquid hydrocarbon generation even during the stages of overmaturity. The results significantly enhance the theoretical framework of hydrocarbon generation under ultradeep conditions, thereby providing a crucial extension to the existing thermal boundary theory of hydrocarbon formation. These insights are crucial for the oil and gas industry, directing future exploration efforts towards ultradeep reservoirs and informing the development of new technologies to exploit these resources. The exploration of ultradeep (depths below 6000 m) formations represents a significant area of advancement in the oil and gas industry1,2. In recent years, significant advancements have been made in the exploration of ultradeep oil and gas reservoirs, particularly in regions such as the Sichuan Basin, Tarim Basin, and Qaidam Basin. These developments indicate substantial potential for further exploration of ultradeep reservoirs within China3–5. The discovery of ultradeep reservoirs has challenged the oil and gas temperature boundaries of classical theories, such as the maturity theory of hydrocarbon generation from kerogen6. Previous research has indicated that liquid hydrocarbons remain in formations with burial depths between 7544 and 9600 m when the vitrinite reflectance (Ro) reaches 3.0%7. The generation and expulsion of hydrocarbons from source rocks under extremely high-temperature and high-pressure geological conditions in ultradeep areas is a matter of significant concern8.

Hydrous pyrolysis is a laboratory technique that effectively replicates the natural processes involved in petroleum generation within geological settings9. The simulation of hydrocarbon generation involves manipulation of experimental parameters, including temperature, pressure, and the rate of temperature increase, to accurately replicate geological conditions10,11. Numerous previous studies have focused on the differences in the evolution of hydrocarbon generation among various kerogen types and lithologies within source rocks. Hui conducted a quantitative analysis to examine the impact of kerogen type on the hydrocarbon generation potential of lacustrine source rocks from the Liaohe Basin12. This study demonstrated that Type I and Type II1 source rocks exhibit earlier hydrocarbon generation, faster hydrocarbon expulsion, and narrower windows of hydrocarbon generation than Type II2 and Type III source rocks. Zhang conducted a simulation experiment on hydrocarbon generation from various kerogen types present in low-mature shales under closed conditions13. The findings suggest that Type II and Type III kerogen exhibit similar stages of hydrocarbon generation, yet they diverge in terms of the duration and progression of hydrocarbon generation between the liquid and gaseous phases. The temperature and rate of heating are critical factors that influence the advancement of hydrocarbon generation and expulsion in hydrocarbon simulation experiments conducted with source rocks. Conventional theory suggests that the temperature of the raw-oil liquid window is between reflectances (Ro) of 0.60% and 1.35%14–16, but exploration practices show that liquid hydrocarbons are still present when Ro is 3.0%17. For some high-temperature and high-pressure simulation experiments, the temperature can reach above 500 oC18 or even 1200 oC19, and the effects of pressure can be confusing. Tissot14 and Braun20 posited that pressure plays a significant role in hydrocarbon generation, following temperature, and that pressure promotes hydrocarbon generation. Conversely, Landais21 and Price22 suggested that pressure inhibits the thermal evolution of organic matter. Gu23 posited that a significant factor contributing to the persistence of liquid oil in ultradeep reservoirs of the Tarim Basin, at depths exceeding 7,500 m, is the inhibitory effect exerted by fluid pressures surpassing 60 MPa. The process of hydrocarbon generation in ultradeep oil and gas systems, which are characterized by elevated levels of thermal maturity and pressure, is more intricate and less well understood2,7.

In this study, thermal simulation experiments were conducted using four distinct source rocks with varying kerogen types (I, II, and III) and lithologies (shale, mudstone, carbonaceous mudstone, and coal). To simulate the processes of hydrocarbon generation and expulsion in source rocks under ultradeep conditions, high-temperature (230–650 oC) and high-pressure (lithostatic pressure of 88.3-259.7 MPa and liquid pressure of 34–100 MPa) hydrous pyrolysis experiments were designed for semiclosed systems (Table 2). The aim of this study was to examine the influence of temperature and pressure on hydrocarbon generation and expulsion under ultradeep conditions, and the effect of kerogen type was also considered.

Materials and methods

Geological setting

The Qaidam Basin represents the sole oil and gas production region within the Qinghai‒Tibet Plateau (Fig. 1), and is a Cenozoic continental basin formed by subduction and collision between the Indian and Eurasian Plates24,25. The Qaidam Basin is rich in natural gas resources, and the current geological reserves of natural gas are distributed mainly in shallow layers above 3500 m burial depth, with a proven rate of only 12.2%. Jurassic strata are the most important source rock in the centre of the northern margin of the Qaidam Basin. These Jurassic source rocks are characterized by significant thickness, extensive distribution, and diverse kerogen types. In recent years26, natural gas reservoirs with burial depths exceeding 7000 m have been identified along the northern margin of the Qaidam Basin, indicating that Jurassic source rocks also have ultradeep hydrocarbon generation potential.

Sample collection

The organic-rich source rocks used here were selected from the CY1 core at a depth of 1940 m (shale), the S86 core at a depth of 3501 m (mudstone), and the DMG1 core at depths of 428 m (carbonaceous mudstone) and 422 m (coal). These samples represent the Jurassic hydrocarbon source rocks from the northern margin of the Qaidam Basin (Fig. 1). The fundamental geochemical characteristics of the simulated samples are presented in Table 1. The kerogen Type I shale is immature, with a vitrinite reflectance (VR) of 0.39% Ro and a Tmax of 436. A hydrogen index (HI) of 545 mg/g TOC indicates that this shale contains very oil-prone kerogen. The data from the total organic carbon (TOC), Rock-Eval, VRo, and carbon isotope analyses suggest that the mudstone mainly contains low-mature Type II kerogen, whereas the carbonaceous mudstone and coal contain Type III kerogen (Table 1; Fig. 2). In this study, the samples were comminuted into rock particles with a size range of 0.3 to 0.5 cm.

Experimental methods

Hydrous pyrolysis was carried out via a WYMN-3 HTHP (Fig. 3), and the experimental temperature and pressure conditions of the instrument have been improved27. Schematic diagrams of the WYMN − 3 HTHP instrument can be found in the literature28. The experimental configuration is a semiopen system capable of controlling the temperature and lithostatic and fluid pressures. In the present study, the apparatus reached a maximum temperature of 650 °C, with corresponding maximum lithostatic and water pressures of 259.7 MPa and 100 MPa, respectively. The heating process was conducted in phases. Initially, the furnace was heated at a rate of 10 °C/min from room temperature to approximately 20 °C below the target temperature. This intermediate temperature was maintained for 0.5 h. The heating rate was subsequently reduced to 0.5 °C/min until the target temperature was reached, at which point the temperature was held constant for 72 h (Table 2). The fluid and gas expelled were collected when the fluid pressure exceeded the predetermined range29. When the hydrocarbon generation pressure, as indicated by the pressure gauge, exceeded 20% of the predetermined fluid pressure, magnetic valve 3B was actuated to release hydrocarbons into the cold trap 11. Upon the pressure falling below 20% of the initial fluid pressure, magnetic valve 3B was closed, and magnetic valve 3 A was subsequently opened to facilitate the injection of distilled water via a high-pressure liquid pump. This process persisted until the water pressure was restored to its original value, at which point magnetic valve 3 A was closed.

Two liquid organic phases were collected from the reactor after hydrous pyrolysis, namely, expelled oil and bitumen, which are termed generated oil. The expelled liquid products, along with those washed from the sample cell and pipeline via dichloromethane (DCM), were collected as expelled oil. The extract from the solid residues was collected as bitumen. Both the bitumen and expelled oil were separated into four fractions: saturated hydrocarbons, aromatic hydrocarbons, nonhydrocarbons and asphaltenes. Samples at each step and product were weighed via an electronic balance. The generated gases were collected in saturated brine glass flasks via the drain gas extraction method and analysed for gas composition via a MAT271 high-resolution gas composition mass spectrometer (HRGCMS). Carbon isotopes for gas components were analysed via a Finnigan Delta Plus XP stable isotope mass spectrometer. The dried extracted solid residues were retained for Rock-Eval analysis, which was conducted via a Leco CS-344 analyser27,30.

Total oil and gas yield

The total oil yield of the four samples exhibited bimodal distributions (Fig. 4a), characterized by distinct peaks occurring at approximately 350 °C and 600 °C. The maximum total oil yields were recorded for II-mudstone, III-carbonaceous mudstone, and III-coal, with values of 116.37 mg/g TOC at 350 °C, 83.68 mg/g TOC at 350 °C, and 37.42 mg/g TOC at 500 °C, respectively (Table 3). The I-shale exhibited consistent total oil yield peaks, ranging from 181.23 to 201.26 mg/g TOC, within a temperature interval of 350 °C to 500 °C. Additionally, a secondary peak in total oil yield, measuring 140.04 mg/g TOC, was observed at 600 °C. Both I-Shale and II-Mudstone displayed higher conversion ratios of hydrocarbons, as indicated by their higher total oil and gas yields (mg/g TOC) than those of III-Carbonaceous Mudstone and III-Coal. However, when considering the volume of rock (Fig. 4b and d), Type III carbonaceous mudstone and coal had higher rates of oil and gas production than I-shale and II-mudstone did because of their higher TOCs. The total gas yields had a consistent trend (Fig. 4c), wherein the Type I and Type II kerogen samples demonstrated greater conversion efficiencies of total organic carbon (TOC), with I-Shale at 1378.16 ml/g TOC, II-mudstone at 1521.83 ml/g TOC, III-carbonaceous mudstone at 780.58 ml/g TOC and III-coal at 581.04 ml/g TOC. The Type III source rocks presented relatively high gas yields per unit rock.

Oil generation and expulsion

The total oil yield encompasses both bitumen production and the yield of expelled oil (Fig. 5). The expelled oil rate represents the quantity of liquid crude oil that is initially transported out of the hydrocarbon source rock during the process of hydrocarbon generation. The data presented in Fig. 5c demonstrate that the efficiency of expelled oil gradually increased as the temperature increased, reaching a level exceeding 99% at 500 °C. The percentage of oil expelled from Type I shale decreased from 38.96% at 250 °C to 13.17% at 300 °C, suggesting that shale in the early stages of maturation had a high affinity for adsorbing generated crude oil. The bitumen yield had a positive correlation with increasing temperature, reaching its peak at 350 °C and then decreasing, except for III-coal. The bitumen yield of the coal sample decreased from 8.84 mg/g TOC at 250 °C to 0.37 mg/g TOC at 500 °C, suggesting that the organic matter in the coal did not produce much oil in the early stages of maturation. After reaching a temperature of 550 °C and a fluid pressure of 72 MPa, the percentage of expelled oil exceeded 99%, whereas the residual bitumen extraction was less than 0.96 mg/g TOC. The quantification and separation of those residual bitumen traces present significant challenges and were therefore not included in the scope of this study.

Group composition

The group composition of the complete series of expelled oils from 250 °C to 650 °C was separated and analysed, as shown in Fig. 6. Figure 6 reveals that the fraction composition of the expelled oil from the four source rock samples was predominantly composed of nonhydrocarbons and asphaltenes, particularly during the hydrocarbon generation period of temperatures from 350 °C to 600 °C. The average proportion of nonhydrocarbons and asphaltenes surpassed 50%, while the concentration of saturated hydrocarbons was consistently lower than that of the other components. At temperatures ranging from 500 °C to 650 °C, asphaltenes were found to be the predominant component in the expelled oil from II-mudstones and III-carbonaceous mudstones (Fig. 6). In contrast, I-shale presented higher concentrations of nonhydrocarbon and aromatic hydrocarbon fractions in the expelled oil.

Radargram for fractions of saturated hydrocarbons (black squares), aromatic hydrocarbons (red dots), non- hydrocarbons (green triangles) and asphaltene (blue inverted triangles) yields (mg/g TOC) of I-shale(a), II-mudstone(b), III-carbonaceous mudstone(c) and III-coal (d) versus temperatures (oC).

Semiclass systems facilitate the continuous expulsion of hydrocarbons during their generation, potentially increasing the cumulative yield of oil. However, this process does not impede the aromatization and asphaltenization of oil in highly overmature stages. Therefore, while there was a peak in raw hydrocarbon expulsion at 600 °C (Table 4; Fig. 6a), the saturated hydrocarbon fraction was notably low (1.17–11.30 mg/g TOC); this suggests that the saturated hydrocarbons may have originated from the initial stages of the thermal simulation experiment. Furthermore, high fluid pressure and hydrocarbon exclusion may also exert an inhibitory influence on the process of oil cracking28. Compared with the alterations observed in the saturated hydrocarbon fraction of the expelled oil during high evolutionary stages of closed system hydrous pyrolysis experiments31, the saturated hydrocarbon fraction of the expelled oil from semiclosed system experiments exhibited significantly less variation across different temperature points (supplementary data online).

Gas generation

The total gas production rate includes nonhydrocarbon and hydrocarbon gases (Fig. 7). The primary nonhydrocarbon gases identified in our experimental observations were carbon dioxide (CO2) and nitrogen (N2), and the hydrocarbon gases were mainly C1-C5 hydrocarbon gases (Fig. 8). The II-mudstone had the highest total and hydrocarbon gas yields, with values of 1521.83 ml/g TOC at 650 °C and 1126.61 ml/g TOC at 650 °C, respectively. The III-coal had the lowest total, nonhydrocarbon and hydrocarbon gas yields, with values of 581.04 ml/g TOC at 650 °C, 119.61 ml/g TOC at 600 °C and 481.35 ml/g TOC at 650 °C, respectively. However, there was not much difference in the proportion of hydrocarbon gases among the four samples, and even at the highest maturity stage (650 °C), the III-coal sample had the highest proportion of hydrocarbon gases, with a rate of 82.84% (Table 3).

Rock Eval of residual solids

The hydrogen index (HI) values obtained from Rock-Eval pyrolysis of the pyrolyzed samples demonstrated a strong correlation, indicating a decrease in the HI with increasing temperature from 250 °C to 400 °C (Fig. 10a). We observed incremental changes in the HI at 300 °C in coal from 91 to 122 mg/g TOC (Fig. 10a). During the initial stages of low maturity below 400 °C, the hydrogen index (HI) values of source rocks containing various kerogen types had a hierarchical pattern of Type I > Type II > Type III kerogen. However, following the onset of the first oil generation peak above 400 °C, the HI values of these source rocks converged with minimal distinction among the different kerogen types. Good relationships were also established between temperature and S2 from Rock-Eval for the different kerogen types (Fig. 10C). The S2 of the pyrolyzed solid residue decreased as the temperature increased from 250 °C to 500 °C. Tmax values are frequently employed as indicators of the thermal maturity of hydrocarbon source rocks. The values of Tmax from Rock-Eval pyrolysis of the pyrolyzed samples exhibited good correlation, with Tmax increasing with increasing temperature for Type III kerogen source rocks (Fig. 10b). In Type III kerogen coal, the Tmax values ranged from 422 °C to 610 °C (Table 4). Above 550 °C, the Tmax values decreased with increasing temperature for Type I and Type II kerogen source rocks, indicating that for high-over-mature Types I and II hydrocarbon source rocks, the Tmax values are not reliable indicators of maturity.

Discussion

Hydrocarbon generation patterns

By investigating the process of hydrocarbon generation and the evolution of the group composition, gas composition and δ13C value variations for different types of kerogen, their evolutionary patterns were determined. Previous studies on hydrocarbon generation and geochemical evolution of kerogen have been carried out29,32,33. Research has indicated that liquid hydrocarbons remain present in formations with burial depths between 7544 and 9600 m when the vitrinite reflectance (Ro) reaches 3.0%7. Under ultradeep conditions, high temperatures continue to play a major role in promoting casein and crude oil cracking. The effect of high fluid pressure on organic matter evolution, on the other hand, is complex. Not all thermal evolution of organic matter in overpressured basins is anomalous, and a certain threshold of pressure is needed to inhibit the evolution of organic matter. Taking the hydrocarbon source rocks of the Cambrian Yurtus Formation in the Tarim Basin at a burial depth of approximately 10,500 m as an example, Gu23 posited that a significant factor contributing to the persistence of liquid oil in the ultradeep reservoirs of the Tarim Basin, at depths exceeding 7,500 m, is the inhibitory effect exerted by fluid pressures surpassing 60 MPa. In this study, we used the Jurassic hydrocarbon source rocks of the Qaidam Basin as an example. The results show that the Jurassic hydrocarbon source rocks still have potential for hydrocarbon production under high-temperature conditions where the fluid pressure is stabilized at more than 70 MPa. On this basis, combined with the experimental hydrocarbon generation results for organic matter under high-temperature, high-pressure conditions, a schematic representation of the hydrocarbon generation process under ultradeep conditions with overpressure is shown in Fig. 11.

A schematic representation of the hydrocarbon generation process under conditions of ultra-deep with overpressure.

Under conditions of normal pressure and fluid pressure less than 60 MPa (Fig. 11), organic matter evolution basically follows the classical theory of hydrocarbon genesis, with massive oil production occurring during the kerogen maturation stage. The high overmaturation stage is dominated by hydrocarbon gas production. During the maturation stage of Ro (approximately 0.8–1.5), the temperature (350–450 °C) range of the main hydrocarbon generation stage exhibits significant variability among the different kerogen samples31. The main hydrocarbon generation stage of kerogen Type I shale has a wide range of main hydrocarbon phases (with hydrous pyrolysis temperatures between 350 °C and 500 °C). The kerogen Type II mudstone has a peak temperature of 350 °C for mainly hydrocarbon phases. The main degradation of kerogen generates a significant amount of oil and gas from kerogen Type I shale samples, and the hydrocarbon gas content increases to more than 50%. In the Type II mudstone sample, oil predominates prior to reaching the peak temperature of 350 °C, with a low content of hydrocarbon gas. After reaching 350 °C, the quantity of oil decreases, whereas the quantity of hydrocarbon gas increases, suggesting that the hydrocarbon gas was derived from the thermal cracking of the crude oil. Both Type III kerogen coal and carbonaceous mudstone samples have low oil and gas yields when the fluid pressure is less than 60 MPa (Table 3; Figs. 4 and 5), indicating the limited hydrocarbon generation potential of Type III terrestrial organic matter under conventional geological conditions with normal pressure.

In ultradeep geological environments where fluid pressures exceed 70 MPa (Fig. 11), organic matter evolution is inhibited. At a temperature of 400–450 °C and a fluid pressure of 71 MPa, the reflectance (Ro) of kerogen is significantly inhibited, and the residual organic matter Ro values are 0.16–1.3 lower (average of 0.54) than those of conventional simulations23. After 500 °C, we observed a hydrocarbon peak in the late hydrocarbon generation stage (Table 3, Fig. 4and Fig. 5). The kerogen Type I shale, Type II mudstone and Type III carbonaceous mudstone have peak temperatures of 600 °C for the late hydrocarbon phases. However, the kerogen Type III coal sample has high oil yields at both 500 °C and 600 °C at the high-over maturity stages. The late hydrocarbon generation stage is the main hydrocarbon gas generation period, with all four samples with different kerogen types showing a significant increase in hydrocarbon gas yield and a share greater than 50% of the total gas yield.

Kerogens are characterized by intricate macromolecular structures containing diverse functional groups and specific bonds. The process of hydrocarbon generation from kerogen primarily involves the sequential cleavage of chemical bonds34,35, with the evolutionary mechanism of kerogen playing a crucial role in controlling the hydrocarbon generation process at fluid pressures below 60 MPa. Although hydrocarbon source rocks can still produce some amount of liquid hydrocarbons at high overmature stages under fluid overpressure conditions, the main oil fractions produced are nonhydrocarbons and asphaltenes, and the contents of saturated and aromatic hydrocarbons do not increase or even decrease (Fig. 6). The saturated hydrocarbons and aromatic hydrocarbons in the oil are rapidly cracked and converted into hydrocarbon gases, especially methane, in the overmature stage. This study shows that under high-temperature and high-pressure simulation experimental conditions with increasing maturity, source rock with different kerogen types has hydrocarbon potential and is dominated by the potential for dry gas reservoirs.

Geological application

The results of this study have substantial implications for hydrocarbon exploration, especially concerning deep shale gas and ultradeep gas reservoirs. The Palaeozoic organic-rich shales of the Yangtze Plate in South China demonstrate considerable potential for shale gas extraction, with particular emphasis on those shales that are in highly mature or postmature gas-generating stages (VR > 2.5% Ro)36,37. A shale gas field was discovered in the Jurassic formation on the northern margin of the Qaidam Basin38. These observations suggest that gas generation mainly occurs at VR values below 2.5% Ro. Our research indicates that with high pressure, kerogen Type I shale undergoes significant gas generation even when it reaches maturity. These findings suggest that continental Type I kerogen may hold promise for the production of deep shale gas and petroleum in the Qaidam Basin. The high-maturity natural gas deposits in the Mingfeng Sag and Baxia depression in the Bohai Bay Basin formed at temperatures ranging from 170 to 201 °C, as documented by Ping39 and Zhao40. Additionally, the presence of high-maturity oil and natural gas in the Baxia depression can be attributed to source rocks rich in continental Type II kerogen, as noted by Zhao40. Zou41 reported that Type III kerogen has the highest gas generation potential at a vitrinite reflectance (VR) exceeding 2.0% Ro. The Dongping area of the Qaidam Basin has been inferred to contain natural gas derived from oil-cracked gas from the Jurassic high-maturity to overmature stages4. In the K-2 well, ultradeep gas reservoirs in the Qaidam Basin at depths less than 7000 m were discovered. Consequently, source rocks rich in Type III kerogen mudstones and coal are deemed suitable for ultradeep gas exploration. All the source rocks with different kerogen types have not yet reached peak gas generation at 650 °C, which may indicate a continued potential for hydrocarbon gas generation in the rocks. The findings of this study indicate that continental source rock has significant potential for the extraction of deep shale gas and ultradeep petroleum, whereas coal shows promise for the production of ultradeep natural gas.

The implications of this research extend beyond the immediate findings of hydrocarbon generation in Jurassic source rocks. The methodology and results of this study provide a robust framework for understanding the complex interplay between temperature, pressure, and kerogen type in ultradeep geological environments. The increasing global energy demand and depletion of traditional shallow reserves make this especially significant. To meet future energy needs and maintain energy security, it is increasingly important to tap into ultradeep reservoirs.

Conclusions

This study assesses the hydrocarbon generation potential of various lithologies and kerogen types within Jurassic continental source rocks subjected to ultradeep conditions via high-temperature and high-pressure hydrous pyrolysis experiments. The results reveal a bimodal distribution of total oil yields with distinct peaks at approximately 350 °C and 600 °C. Fluid overpressure exceeding 70 MPa exerts a substantial inhibitory influence on hydrocarbon evolutionary processes within ultradeep hydrocarbon source rocks. These findings indicate that Jurassic hydrocarbon source rocks maintain oil and gas potential under ultradeep conditions (depths exceeding 6000 m). These insights are significant for the exploration of deep shale gas and ultradeep gas reservoirs in the Qaidam Basin, suggesting that continental Types I and II kerogen may hold promise for deep shale gas and petroleum extraction, whereas coal has potential for ultradeep natural gas production.

Previous studies have demonstrated that liquid hydrocarbons persist in geological formations at burial depths ranging from 7,544 m to 9,600 m when the vitrinite reflectance (Ro%) reaches 3.0. Numerous contemporary discoveries of ultradeep oil and gas reserves, such as those in the Tarim Basin, Sichuan Basin, and Qaidam Basin, are predominantly influenced by anomalies in temperature and pressure coefficients42–45. In these instances, excessive high pressure is frequently identified as the principal factor governing the presence of ultradeep hydrocarbons. Moreover, a lower geothermal gradient is conducive to the retention of liquid hydrocarbons within ultradeep petroleum systems.

In this study, we utilize the geological temperature and pressure coefficient conditions of the Qaidam Basin to simulate the potential for ultradeep hydrocarbon generation. These findings clearly demonstrate that the Jurassic hydrocarbon source rock strata possess significant oil and gas potential under the ultradeep conditions of the Qaidam Basin. Nevertheless, for hydrocarbon source rocks located in different basins and formations, it remains essential to integrate the specific local conditions of the hydrocarbon source rocks with the temperature and pressure coefficient parameters of the basin, particularly the pressure coefficient conditions, to accurately assess their ultradeep hydrocarbon potential.

This research offers essential insights into the hydrocarbon generation potential of Jurassic source rocks under ultradeep conditions, which is of paramount importance for the oil and gas industry as deeper reservoirs are explored. The findings not only contribute to a better understanding of the geological processes occurring at great depths but also pave the way for the development of new exploration strategies and techniques tailored to the unique conditions of ultradeep formations. By identifying the bimodal distribution of oil yields and the conditions under which maximum hydrocarbon generation occurs, this research enhances the ability of geologists to predict and locate potential deep hydrocarbon reservoirs. The detailed analysis of the oil expulsion rate and the composition of expelled oil fractions provides valuable information on the mobility and transport of hydrocarbons within source rocks, which is crucial for assessing the commercial viability of deep reservoirs. Furthermore, investigating the influence of kerogen type on hydrocarbon generation offers a nuanced perspective on the variability in source rock potential. The observation that hydrocarbon gases, particularly methane, continue to increase in yield even at temperatures above 600 °C challenges conventional theories and suggests that ultradeep conditions may harbour significant hydrocarbon resources that have previously been underappreciated.

In conclusion, this study significantly advances the scientific community’s knowledge of hydrocarbon generation in ultradeep settings and has important implications for the future of energy resource development. This study encourages further research into the exploration and exploitation of deep Earth resources and promotes the integration of these findings into the broader context of global energy security and sustainability. A thorough understanding of hydrocarbon generation under such extreme conditions is crucial not only for the oil and gas industry but also for the broader goals of energy independence and environmental stewardship.
