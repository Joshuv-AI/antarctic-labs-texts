Risk factors associated with depressive symptoms among institutionalized elderly in Lebanon
Authors: Heba Mohsen, Linda Abou Abbas, Benjamin Calvet, Pascale Salameh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDepression in long-term care facilities (LTCF) is often influenced by physical health, social support, environmental conditions, and other related factors. This study aimed to investigate the factors associated with depressive symptoms in LTCF in Lebanon. A cross-sectional study was conducted with 180 residents aged 65–97 years who were recruited through convenience sampling from 16 LTCF. Data were collected using standardized instruments, including the Nursing Home Short Depression Inventory (NH-SDI), Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI), Lebanese Insomnia Scale (LIS-18), Lebanese Anxiety Scale (LAS-10), and Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NRS). The prevalence of depressive symptoms was 46.1%. Participants had a mean age of 77 ± 8 years; most were female (68.3%), and few were married (13.3%). Multiple logistic regression analysis revealed that daily religious involvement was associated with a decreased risk of depressive symptoms (AOR = 0.3, 95% CI: 0.1–0.9, p = 0.04). In contrast, having multiple health conditions (> 2) (AOR = 3.1, 95% CI: 1.2–7.4, p = 0.02), a history of COVID-19 (AOR = 2.4, 95% CI: 1.1–5.1, p = 0.02), frailty (AOR = 3.3, 95% CI: 1.1-9.0, p = 0.03), and anxiety symptoms (AOR = 2.5, 95% CI: 1.0–6.0, p = 0.04) were all associated with an increased risk of depressive symptoms. In conclusion, depression is a multifactorial disorder that requires significant attention in residential care; elderly individuals with specific characteristics are at increased risk. Routine screening is advised to improve mental health outcomes and quality of life among elderly residents.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Psychology, Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

Depression is a complex mood disorder often characterized by sadness, decreased energy, loss of interest, disturbed sleep, and poor concentration1. It affects more than 300 million people globally and is ranked by the World Health Organization as the leading cause of disability2. Depression is considered the most common mood disorder among elderly people3. It often exacerbates other health conditions or symptoms and increases the risk of mortality4,5. Studies have consistently demonstrated a profound impact of depression on social interaction, physical functioning and quality of life6,7. Late-life depression is a psychiatric disorder with high prevalence rates in long-term care populations ranging between 26% and 60%8,9. The prevalence rates in these settings have long been recognized as greater than the rates reported in community-dwelling elderly individuals which range from 13% to 31%10,11. In a previous study conducted by our team, the prevalence of depression among residents in long-term care facilities (LTCF) in Lebanon was approximately 32%12.

Residents in LTCF often face multiple health conditions, limited mobility, and cognitive decline13. These factors not only increase care needs but also contribute to psychological vulnerability14. A crucial aspect of understanding and managing depression in long-term care is identifying the factors that place elderly residents at risk for depression. Depression in LTCFs is multifactorial, arising from the interplay of demographic, psychosocial, and health-related determinants15. Demographic characteristics such as age16, gender17, marital status18, and education level18 have been associated with depressive symptoms. Psychosocial factors, particularly lack of social support, play a significant role, as residents often experience reduced contact with family and increased feelings of loneliness following institutionalization19. Health-related factors, including the presence of medical comorbidities20, cognitive function21, sleep disturbances22, pain23, and reduced physical function24 have been consistently associated with depression.

Despite the growing body of research on depression in elderly individuals, a gap remains in identifying risk factors within long-term care settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries25. The majority of existing studies have been conducted in high-income or Western countries, limiting the generalizability of findings to regions with different cultural, social, and healthcare structures26. In Lebanon, where family support systems, religious practices, and perceptions of institutionalization differ markedly from those in Western countries, the factors associated with depression may vary. However, data on this population remains scarce, particularly in the context of global stressors such as the COVID-19 pandemic. To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine depressive symptoms (post-COVID-19) among Lebanese elderly residents in LTCF. The present study aimed to (1) describe the demographic and health-related characteristics of elderly individuals living in care homes and (2) investigate the key factors associated with depressive symptoms in institutionalized individuals.

Materials and methods

Study design, sample, and setting

The cross-sectional study recruited a convenience sample of 180 participants in LTCF during a period of 6 months. Sixteen institutions distributed across Beirut, Mount Lebanon, Kesserwen-Jbeil, and South Lebanon were visited from July 2022 till December 2022. The study sample included Lebanese elderly people aged 65 years or older, of both genders, and who were able to answer the questionnaires. On the other hand, elderly people who were unable to communicate were excluded from the study.

Sample size calculation

A power analysis using the G-power software version 3.1 determined that a sample size of 157 was necessary to detect a medium effect size via multiple regression including 18 predictors, with an alpha error of 5% and a power of 80%.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was approved by the Institute of National Public Health, Clinical Epidemiology, and Toxicology in Lebanon (Approval number: 2022REC-007-INSPECT-11-09). Permission to approach residents was obtained from the head of each care facility. Residents who agreed to take part in the study were asked to provide their written, informed consent. The study was carried out in accordance with the ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. The reporting of this study followed the STROBE guidelines.

Study variables

Dependent variable: Depressive symptoms (Yes = 1, No = 0).

Independent variables: Age, gender, marital status, education status, work history, religious involvement, frequency of visits, exercise, smoking habits, alcohol consumption, COVID-19 history, surgery history, number and types of medical conditions (cardiovascular, endocrine, musculoskeletal, neurologic, etc.), cognitive function, frailty, anxiety symptoms, insomnia, and physical pain.

Measurements

The Nursing Home Short Depression Inventory (NH-SDI) is an informant-based measure used by attending nurses to evaluate depressive symptoms among elderly residents in nursing homes27. It is short, easy to administer with dichotomous replies (absent or present), and does not require specialized training. The Arabic version of the NH-SDI, a 13-item questionnaire previously validated by our team, was utilized12. The total score ranges from 0 to 13, with a cutoff score of ≥ 3.5 indicating depressive symptoms. The NH-SDI demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.83) and good criterion validity (cutoff score = 3.5, sensitivity = 88.5%, and specificity = 69.5%). In the present study, Cronbach’s α = 0.81.

The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) is a standard screening tool for measuring the level of cognitive state28. It includes 6 subtests: orientation, registration, attention, recall, language, and constructional praxis with a total score of 30 points. The MMSE was validated among the Lebanese population, demonstrating a sensitivity of 80% and a specificity of 89.4% for a cutoff of 2329. Cronbach’s α reliability score in this sample = 0.71.

The Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI) is a screening tool for assessing frailty among older adults30. It measures the loss of functions and resources in 4 main domains: physical (mobility functions, multiple health problems, physical fatigue, vision, and hearing), cognitive (cognitive dysfunction), social (emotional isolation), and psychological (depressed mood and feelings of anxiety). The GFI is composed of 15 items with a total score ranging from 0 to 15, where higher scores indicate greater levels of frailty. It was validated among Lebanese elderly people, with a cutoff score of ≥ 4 used to identify frail individuals31. The reliability of GFI in this study was good with Cronbach’s α = 0.75.

The Lebanese Insomnia Scale (LIS-18) is a self-reported scale that was developed in Lebanon to measure insomnia in adults32. It is based on the ICD-10 insomnia criteria and includes 18 items. Each item is rated on a Likert scale from 1 to 5 (never, seldom, sometimes, mostly, always). The cutoff score used was 52.5 (sensitivity = 89.5% and specificity = 80%), given that most participants in our sample were on drug medication for insomnia. Cronbach’s α of LIS-18 = 0.65.

The Lebanese Anxiety Scale (LAS-10) is a self-reported instrument developed in Lebanon to screen for anxiety33. The 10-item questionnaire is related to common anxiety symptoms experienced by individuals during the past week, such as tension, somatic problems, fears, and intellectual problems. It is a brief, reliable tool that can be used in both research and clinical practice, with a cutoff point = 13.5, sensitivity = 77.5%, and specificity = 70.8%. In this sample, Cronbach’s α = 0.77.

The Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) is a measurement of pain intensity that has been shown to be an accurate screening test34. It rates pain over the last 24 h using a horizontal line from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain). It also helps achieve a rapid classification of pain severity, with higher values reflecting greater pain. Furthermore, the NRS is used in clinical and research settings for its simplicity and minimal intrusiveness. It has been validated in Arabic-speaking countries, including Lebanon35,36.

Procedure

Questionnaires were administered in the Arabic language and were completed by the trained PhD student in face-to-face interviews due to low literacy levels of participants and advanced age. The interviewer read each questionnaire item aloud to ensure accurate responses. Each interview lasted between 30 min and 1 h. The nurses completed the NH-SDI along with clinical information about elderly participants. The interviewer was blinded to the responses of the NH-SDI at the time of interviews.

Statistical analysis

The study data were tabulated and analyzed using the SPSS software (version 27). Missing data were handled using listwise deletion, whereby participants with incomplete responses on the NH-SDI were excluded from analyses; given the proportion of missing data was low (< 5%). Frequency distribution with percentages, median with interquartile range (IQR), and mean with standard deviation (SD) were calculated for the study variables. Due to the skewness in the data distribution, non-parametric tests were employed. Chi-square tests and Mann-Whitney tests were utilized to study the relationships between independent variables and depressive symptoms. Variables with p values less than 0.2 (Age, gender, religious involvement, frequency of visits, number of medical conditions, surgery history, COVID-19 history, frailty, anxiety, and insomnia) were fitted into the multivariable logistic regression for the final analysis. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated to check for multicollinearity; all VIFs were below the recommended threshold of 5, indicating absence of multicollinearity in the data. Multiple logistic regression (forward method) was performed to identify potential factors related to the outcome variable (a score of ≥ 3.5 on the NH-SDI). Model fitness was checked using Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness of fit test. The adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with 95% CI and standard error were reported. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and clinical profile

A total of 180 individuals were included in the study. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. The mean age was 77 ± 8 years, with ages ranging from 65 to 97 years. The majority of participants were aged 65–74 years (41.1%), followed by those aged 75–84 years (36.7%), while individuals aged 85–97 years represented the smallest proportion (22.2%). More than half of participants were female (68.3%), and less than a quarter (13.3%) were married. Less than half of residents had primary/secondary or high school education (44.4%), 27.2% had university degrees, and 28.3% were illiterate. In terms of work history, half of the participants previously held jobs (50.6%), whereas the other half never worked (49.4%). In addition, almost three-quarters of elderly people (73.9%) performed daily religious activities, whereas only 15% had no religious involvement. Regarding visits from relatives, approximately 32% reported never being visited by anyone at all. With respect to exercise, 51.7% of participants did not exercise, whereas others either exercised daily (31.7%) or once a week (16.6%). With respect to smoking habits, 53.9% never smoked, 17.8% were former smokers, and 28.3% were smokers. Moreover, the majority of participants never consumed alcohol (86.7%), 6.1% were former drinkers, and 7.2% were drinkers. Furthermore, the number of medical conditions among elderly residents varied, with 34.4% having one or two conditions and 43.9% having more than two conditions. Finally, more than half of participants had undergone surgeries (52.2%), and approximately one-third of participants were previously infected with COVID-19 (31.1%).

Depressive symptoms were found in approximately 46% of the whole study sample (Table 1), as measured by the NH-SDI. The MMSE showed that more than half of participants (61.1%) scored 23 or higher, reflecting normal cognitive function. The vast majority of participants (83.9%) were frail, as measured by the GFI assessment. Approximately 90% of residents scored less than the cutoff on the LIS-18. About a quarter (24.4%) of residents had anxiety symptoms, while the rest did not. The NRS median score was 0, with an IQR = 5.

Neurologic diseases and sensory impairments (vision and auditory) were prevalent in about 18% and 17% of the sample, respectively. Bivariate analysis

As shown in Table 1, individuals who performed daily religious activities or practices had 60% lower odds of experiencing depressive symptoms (COR = 0.4, 95% CI: 0.2–0.9, p = 0.03) than those with no religious involvement. Similarly, those who were visited by relatives several times a month were 50% less likely to have depressive symptoms (COR = 0.5, 95% CI: 0.2–0.9, p = 0.03) in contrast to those who were not visited. On the other hand, higher odds of depressive symptoms were significantly associated with the number of medical conditions (p = 0.04 for one or two diseases and p < 0.001 for more). Moreover, elderly people who previously underwent surgeries had double the risk of depressive symptoms compared to those who never had surgeries (COR = 2.0, 95% CI: 1.1–3.6, p = 0.02). Participants who were previously infected with COVID-19 were 2.1 times more likely to have depressive symptoms than those who were not (COR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.1–4.1, p = 0.02). Furthermore, frailty (GFI ≥ 4) was significantly associated with an increased likelihood of depressive symptoms (COR = 3.6, 95% CI: 1.5–9.3, p = 0.004). Residents with anxiety symptoms (LAS-10 ≥ 14) were 3.3 times more likely to experience depressive symptoms than their counterparts (COR = 3.3, 95% CI: 1.6–6.8, p = 0.001). Finally, upon comparison of NRS medians between individuals with and without depressive symptoms, pain level was not a significant factor (p = 0.31).

Residents who performed daily religious activities were 70% less likely to exhibit depressive symptoms (AOR = 0.3, 95% CI: 0.1–0.9, p = 0.04). On the other hand, having more than two medical conditions substantially increased the odds of depressive symptoms by 3.1 times (AOR = 3.1, 95% CI: 1.2–7.4, p = 0.02). Residents with anxiety symptoms (LAS-10 ≥ 14) were 2.5 times more likely to have depressive symptoms (AOR = 2.5, 95% CI: 1.0–6.0, p = 0.04). These findings revealed that daily religious involvement was a protective factor, whereas having multiple conditions (> 2), a history of COVID-19, anxiety symptoms, and being frail were risk factors for depressive symptoms, adjusting for other variables in the model.

Discussion

Depression in LTCF is a subject of concern, and to our knowledge, very few studies have been conducted on elderly residents in Lebanon. However, this is the first cross-sectional research on depression status quo and related factors post COVID-19 pandemic in Lebanon. Our study explored the factors associated with depressive symptoms among residents in both nursing homes and assisted living centers. It revealed that daily religious involvement, multiple health conditions (> 2), COVID-19 history, frailty, and anxiety symptoms were significantly associated with depressive symptoms.

There was no association between depressive symptoms and any of the following sociodemographic variables: age, gender, marital status, or education level. Similar studies revealed that age37, gender38, marital status39, and education level40 did not affect the occurrence of depression. However, other studies have reported a greater risk of depressive symptoms in younger institutionalized elderly individuals41, females42, unmarried individuals43, and higher educated residents44. These inconsistencies may be explained by differences in characteristics of study populations, settings, and cultural contexts, including variations in family support and institutionalization45.

Cognitive impairment and pain were found to be significantly associated with depression in other studies but not in our study. These findings were inconsistent with prior evidence since both cognitive impairment46 and pain47,48 are commonly identified as risk factors for depressive symptoms in LTCF. This discrepancy may be explained by the potential underrepresentation or exclusion of residents with severe cognitive impairment, who were unable to complete the assessments, thereby attenuating the observed association. Moreover, variability in pain measurement was limited, as most participants reported little to no pain, likely due to effective symptom management through medication.

Religious involvement was found to be associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms. In this study, religious involvement was reflected through activities such as prayers, recitation, and participation in religious ceremonies. This finding was in line with a study conducted in Indonesia, which demonstrated that residents receiving a religious intervention experienced a greater reduction in depressive symptoms and an improvement in quality of life scores compared to the control group49. Religion and spirituality offer individuals a sense of purpose, mitigate feelings of loneliness, and relieve stress50. In the context of LTCF, where residents often experience social isolation, loss of autonomy, and separation from family, religious practices may serve as an important coping mechanism. Engaging in religious activities can offer emotional comfort and foster social interaction within the institutional environment. In Lebanon, where religious beliefs and practices are deeply embedded in daily life, older adults may be particularly inclined to rely on faith-based coping strategies when adapting to institutionalization and age-related challenges such as bereavement and declining health51.

Elders with multiple health conditions (> 2) were at greater odds of depressive symptoms compared to their counterparts. This finding was in agreement with a study conducted in Taiwan which showed that having multiple chronic diseases (more than two) was a critical risk factor for depressive symptoms46. Similarly, an older study demonstrated that nondemented residents with more than three medical comorbidities were 2.7 times more likely to experience depressive symptoms compared to those with one or no comorbidities52. There are numerous reasons underlying the association between multimorbidity and depression, with the relationship being bidirectional53. The physical burden of managing multiple illnesses, along with accompanying symptoms and functional limitations, contributes to psychological distress54. Moreover, shared biological pathways, such as chronic inflammation, are involved in depression and certain physical disorders55.

A significant association was found between a history of COVID-19 infection and depressive symptoms, whereas other LTCF studies have only documented a rise in depression levels during the COVID-19 pandemic56. One study conducted in Spain reported a 57.7% increase in the prevalence of depressive symptoms three months into the pandemic57. Evidence suggests that COVID-19 causes systematic inflammation and disruptions in the central nervous system, which may contribute to mood disorders58. Furthermore, individuals infected with the virus have experienced various complications and persistent physical symptoms, such as loss of taste or smell, which can be distressing59. In addition, they were socially isolated from activities and visits for extended periods, which exacerbates feelings of loneliness60. Together, these factors are associated with the risk of developing depression or aggravating existing symptoms61,62.

Depressive symptoms were found among those who were frail, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies63. In Indonesia, a strong positive association was found between frailty and depression among elderly individuals in nursing homes64. Frailty is characterized by poor balance, muscle weakness, fatigue, and reduced mobility, which contribute to disability and functional dependence. In addition to these physical limitations, frailty involves psychological challenges such as emotional distress resulting from the inability to perform daily activities such as walking or using the toilet, which may contribute to a higher risk of depressive symptoms. Depression and frailty are comorbid geriatric syndromes with overlapping mechanisms65. One mechanism is chronic inflammation, which is characterized by elevated levels of inflammatory biomarkers (interleukin 6) and is linked to both depressive symptoms and frailty66,67. The complex interplay between the physical and mental components of frailty underscores the need for further investigation, particularly in LTCF population.

Residents with anxiety symptoms were more likely to experience depressive symptoms, as anxiety is often comorbid with depression in LTCF68,69. This finding was consistent with other research studies that revealed a significant relationship between both depression and anxiety among elderly residents6,70. Anxiety experienced by elderly people is closely related to age-related factors such as physical health decline, loss of autonomy, and loss of one’s spouse, which may lead to depression71,72. Moreover, the COVID-19 pandemic has introduced or intensified stressors such as worrying about becoming sick or dying, aggravating feelings of hopelessness and fear73.

Limitations

Several limitations concerning this study need to be highlighted. First, this study cannot establish causality between depressive symptoms and associated factors due to its cross-sectional design. Further studies using longitudinal or randomized control trial designs could be adopted to elucidate time-sequential associations. Second, participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach which may have resulted in selection bias and may limit the generalizability of findings. Third, most data were measurements of self-rating, which may have introduced recall bias in the findings; this information bias, which is nondifferential in nature, might drive the results toward the null hypothesis, underestimating certain associations. Fourth, the use of face-to-face interviews may have introduced interviewer bias, whereby the presence or behavior of the interviewer could have influenced participants’ responses. Fifth, certain potentially confounding variables were not adequately accounted for in the methodological design which could increase the risk of residual confounding, specifically, the different types of medication that each participant received. The analysis was also not adjusted for the treatment of depression, as all individuals diagnosed with depression were receiving antidepressant medications. Furthermore, given the number of variables studied, the sample size was relatively small since many LTCF refused to participate. Further prospective studies with larger sample sizes that consider these weaknesses are recommended.

Implications of the study

This study provides key information on the factors associated with depressive symptoms among residents in Lebanese elderly institutions. This information is essential for health professionals, especially nurses, who take care of elderly individuals and are expected to develop interventions that address depression in this population. Considering the influencing factors of depression, long-term institutions must adopt a holistic approach that addresses the physical and mental needs of elderly residents. Additionally, the Ministry of Public Health is highly encouraged to implement educational activities that teach coping strategies and stress management techniques.

Conclusion

Identifying the risk and protective factors of depressive symptoms in LTCF is an important topic to address, as depression is a prevalent mental health condition in this population. This study revealed that religious involvement, multiple health conditions, COVID-19 history, frailty, and anxiety symptoms were significantly associated with depressive symptoms among elderly. Future research should explore causal pathways and interaction effects among these variables, employing longitudinal and intervention-based designs.
