Temporal Robustness of Mental Fatigue Susceptibility: A One-Year Follow-up Study
Authors: Jelle Habay, Y Laurisa Arenales Arauz, Chao Bian, Kevin De Pauw, Nathalie Pattyn, Bart Roelands, Jeroen Van Cutsem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrior research has shown that mental fatigue, induced by prolonged cognitive loads, negatively impacts subjective and behavioral variables. A currently unanswered question is whether the susceptibility to mental fatigue (i.e., how much mental fatigue a person experiences and how much it affects performance) can be classified as a state or a trait characteristic, or combination of both. Therefore, a follow-up measurement of a large-scale experimental study was conducted to assess the temporal robustness of mental fatigue susceptibility.MethodsTwenty-five participants (10♀; 31 ± 8 y) finished a familiarization, intervention (Stroop task) and control (documentary) trial on two separate occasions (i.e., baseline and follow-up) approximately one year apart. Subjective (i.e., subjective feeling of mental fatigue (MVAS) and the rating of perceived exertion (RPE)) and behavioral (i.e., GoNoGo task reaction time and distance covered during a cycling time trial) outcomes were selected for further analysis. Temporal robustness was analyzed by comparing mixed linear models between baseline and follow-up, and performing intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) on the condition differences between baseline and follow-up.ResultsThe influence of the Stroop task on the time trial performance revealed fair temporal robustness (ICC = 0.448, p = 0.011), while also showing a diminishing effect of mental fatigue in follow-up compared to baseline. The influence of mental fatigue on RPE values was significant at baseline (β = 2.85, p = 0.022), and showed a trend towards significance at follow-up (β = 2.22, p = 0.058). GoNoGo reaction time showed limited consistency between baseline and follow-up, while there was a small significant correlation after the intervention/control task for the MVAS (ICC = 0.336, p = 0.047). There were no other significant correlations between condition differences.ConclusionsOur findings favor a state-like interpretation of mental fatigue susceptibility. The results highlight the importance of RPE in driving the negative influence of mental fatigue on physical performance, while also confirming the discrepancy in subjective and behavioral quantification of mental fatigue. Background

Mental fatigue can be defined as a psychobiological state that is induced by prolonged demanding cognitive activity [1], and has significant implications for daily functioning. It can lead to an acute feeling of tiredness, a decrease in human performance capacity, and alterations in brain activity [1–3]. Because of its impact on both healthy and diseased individuals, a multitude of researchers across various disciplines have investigated its effects and underlying mechanisms. However, a key question that still remains unanswered is whether the susceptibility to mental fatigue can be classified more as a stable trait-like or a transient state-like characteristic [4].

Mental fatigue susceptibility can be described as the extent to which an individual experiences mental fatigue and the degree to which it affects performance [4]. In essence, it characterizes how different individuals deal with the effects of mental fatigue, which can be quantified on a subjective, behavioral and even physiological level. If conceptualized as a trait, susceptibility would reflect a relatively stable, internal disposition, with little or no impact of external factors (e.g., weather, family situation, pressure at work, etc.). Two individuals would therefore be expected to respond similarly, especially compared to each other. In contrast, a state-like acute interpretation implies that the influence of mental fatigue changes depending on the specific scenario, with a large contribution of external influences. This means that differences between individuals would be relatively unstable, and susceptibility to mental fatigue would primarily hinge on factors such as sleep quality or mood changes [4–6]. A final perspective is that mental fatigue susceptibility can be seen as an almost dynamic interplay between trait and state [4]. Individual characteristics may determine how people cope with different external events, ultimately influencing the subjective level of mental fatigue throughout the day and its link with behavioral variables. As mental fatigue is considered a multifactorial and complex phenomenon, this integrative explanation is probably the most appropriate [4, 7]. However, current research often focuses more on the differences of state and trait fatigue outcome measures [8–10], while failing to address whether the susceptibility to fatigue itself can be seen as a state or trait characteristic. More extensive knowledge on the underlying nature of mental fatigue susceptibility has numerous applications for research and practice, as it could further elucidate its fundamental mechanisms, confirm the potential added value of mental fatigue susceptibility screening in healthy individuals, and give crucial information on the outcomes used to quantify mental fatigue.

A recent systematic review examined the influence of individual features (e.g., age, sex and training level) on the variability of mental fatigue susceptibility [4]. The performed meta-regression analysis found no relevant link between age, sex, training level and body mass index (BMI) and mental fatigue effects [4], providing evidence in support of acute mental fatigue being state-like in nature. However, not only is a meta-regression limited in its conclusions because of substantial between-study heterogeneity, the list of examined features was extremely limited, and other relevant features might still influence the effect of mental fatigue on subjective and behavioral variables [4]. Moreover, focusing on the influence of individual features only provides us with a partial understanding of susceptibility. Another way to assess whether susceptibility is trait- or state-like is by considering its robustness over time. A trait-like nature will be more likely if the overall effect of mental fatigue stays the same across both time points. In contrast, the opposite scenario, where the effect varies significantly between two time points with no significant changes in the individual profile, provides clear evidence for a state-like nature. The aim of the present study was therefore to examine the temporal robustness of mental fatigue susceptibility. To this end, we conducted a follow-up measurement of a previously published study on the effects of a prolonged cognitive load on human performance [11]. Mental fatigue is generally quantified across three domains: subjective, behavioral and neurophysiological [7]. The present paper includes comparisons over time in both subjective (i.e., the mental fatigue visual analogue scale (MVAS), and the rating of perceived exertion (RPE) CR-100 scale [12]) and behavioral (i.e., a cognitive (GoNoGo) and physical (cycling time trial) performance task) responses. We hypothesized that the expected negative effects of mental fatigue [1, 13] would have a limited degree of robustness over time, based on the idea that mental fatigue susceptibility can be seen as an interplay between trait and state [4]. The robustness over time was investigated in two distinct ways. First, linear mixed model outputs on the effect of mental fatigue across the different variables were compared between baseline and follow-up, with similar model output parameters indicating a consistency over time on group level, and thus evidence of a trait-like interpretation. Second, interclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated between baseline and follow-up for all measures. This measure gives a value for the consistency over time on an individual level, with a high and significant value indicating that mental fatigue susceptibility can be potentially seen as a trait.

Methods

The present article concerns a follow-up investigation of a previously published article regarding the effect of mental fatigue on cognitive and physical performance [11], and was conducted in accordance with the Settings, Protocol establishments, Confounders, Individuals, Framework, and Yield (SPeCIFY) reporting guidelines [7]. The overall study protocol and its procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the UZ Brussel (B.U.N. 1432022000084) in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05576935). This study is also part of a larger project to determine the individual response to mental fatigue (Grant number FWO: 11J6323N).

Design

The overall design can be described as a randomized crossover trial, with an added one-year follow-up measurement (Fig. 1).

Overview of the overall design of the experiment

Both the baseline and follow-up experiment consisted of three separate visits: a familiarization, intervention and control condition. The order of the baseline measurement was randomized, while the follow-up measurement was conducted in the same order as the baseline measurement. All measures were conducted at the same lab (MFYS/BLITS VUB, Brussels, Belgium) in a soundproof climate chamber (Weiss Technik Belgium, Liedekerke, Belgium) providing a controlled atmosphere (20 °C, 40% humidity). Measurements were conducted by trained researchers, with the same primary investigator (J.H.) overseeing data collection in both the baseline and follow-up phases.

Participants

The inclusion criteria consisted of individuals with no acute or chronic physical and mental health conditions, with an age range between 18 and 50 years old. One hundred and seventeen participants were included in the full baseline experiment, of which 104 completed all experimental steps [11]. Participants for the follow-up measurement were recruited from this original subject pool of 104. They were invited for this follow-up measurement between September 2023 and August 2024. Invitations were sent individually to perform the follow-up familiarization session about one year after the last experimental session of participants. This one-year time frame was chosen to mimic the external environment (e.g., specific season, weather, school holidays, etc.) as much as possible. In a similar manner to the baseline experiment, participants were first required to fill in registration and mental health screening questionnaires (i.e., the multidimensional fatigue inventory, the Burnout Assessment Tool and the Beck Depression Index-II). Information on the selected screening questionnaires as well as the cut off scores can be found in Supplemental Digital Content 1 of the published study [11]. Eventually, 70 participants were invited for follow-up, with 32 participants filling in the registration and screening questionnaires. Seven participants did not accept the invitation to attend for the follow-up familiarization visit, and 25 participants completed all experimental steps of the follow-up measurements. The invitations sent to participants were halted because we had reached the desired number of participants. Participants were unaware of the goal of this study and were told that the aim of the study was to examine cerebral activity differences linked to specific tasks over time.

Trial Procedure

The methodology of the baseline experiment is described in detail in a previous publication [11]. To evaluate the robustness of mental fatigue effects, the follow-up measurements were conducted in the exact same manner as the baseline experiment. Only the outcomes that were eventually analyzed in this paper are mentioned in this section.

Participants first reported for a basic medical checkup and maximal incremental exercise test with VO2max assessment. Subsequently, a personal profile of participants was created using multiple individual features (see Specific outcome Measures and Collection Methods). The last part of the first visit consisted of the familiarization with all study procedures (i.e., questionnaires, cognitive and physical tasks), and the completion of the Stroop max task designed to individualize the cognitive load of participants to effectively induce mental fatigue [11].

The experimental and control visit started with the completion of the MVAS. Before the intervention/control task, cognitive performance was assessed using a GoNoGo task, followed by another MVAS. In the intervention condition, participants performed a 45-min individualized Stroop task to induce mental fatigue. In this Stroop task, four color names (i.e., “rood”, “blauw”, “geel” and “groen”) were presented on screen in colored (i.e., red, blue, yellow and green) letters. Participants were required to react to the color of the word, except if the color was red. In this case, participants were instructed to respond with the meaning of the word with the corresponding color buttons on a keyboard. The Stroop task employed in the present series of experiments was based on previous references aimed at taxing participants’ inhibitory control [7]. In the control condition, participants watched a self-chosen emotionally neutral documentary of the same duration. In the follow-up measurement, participants were allowed to choose a different documentary compared to baseline. The MVAS was assessed again before the start of the post-GoNoGo task. Participants then started the physical performance task (i.e., a 20-min cycling time trial). During the cycling time trial, the MVAS and RPE were assessed verbally.

Specific Outcome Measures and Collection Methods

The utilized data file with all collected data is in supplementary file 1. The present paper focuses on the following collected outcomes, namely the collected individual features, the MVAS collected throughout the trial, the RPE collected during the physical task, the reaction time (RT) on the pre and post-GoNoGo task, and the distance performed during the cycling time trial. All of these outcomes were collected in an identical manner between baseline and follow-up.

During the familiarization trial, individual features were collected in the following order: height (using the SECA 206), weight (using the Tanita TBF 300), body mass index (calculated), fat percentage (using the Tanita TBF 300), VO2max (using the VO2Master), self-reported physical activity (using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form [14]), performance level in the performed sport (using a self-made questionnaire [15]), base level of response inhibition (using the sustained attention to response task [16]), trait anxiety (using the trait anxiety inventory [17]), self-control (using the brief self-control scale [18]), mental toughness (using a mental toughness questionnaire [19]), base level of working memory (using a 2 back task [20]), sleep quality (using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [21]), chronotype (using the single item chronotype scale [22]), base level of attention (using the psychomotor vigilance task [23]), and the self-reported weekly caffeine consumption (using the revised caffeine consumption questionnaire [24]). Further information on these features can be found Supplemental Digital Content 1 of the published study [11].

The utilized GoNoGo task was based on previous literature [25–27]. The presented stimuli always consisted of two objects: a figure (either a square or a triangle) and an arrow (either pointing right or left). Participants were instructed to react to the arrow on the screen with the appropriate buttons on the keyboard (either right or left arrow) if a square (i.e., Go stimulus) was presented along with the arrow. However, if a triangle (i.e., NoGo stimulus) was presented, participants needed to refrain from reacting and wait until the next stimulus to continue the task. The Go/NoGo ratio was 80/20 with 180 stimuli in total, which were randomly selected. The stimulus presentation time was set at 500 ms, while the interstimulus time varied between 1100 and 1700 ms, leading to a total task duration of about 6 min. The main outcome collected during this task was the Go reaction time (Go RT).

A 20 min cycling time trial was used to assess physical performance, with an included warm up (3 min) and cool down (2 min) at a set resistance. In the remaining 15 min, participants were instructed to cover as much distance as possible, and were free to change the resistance as they wanted at any point. Participants were only provided with the remaining time in every part of the cycling time trial, without any other feedback or motivational input. The main outcome of the cycling time trial was the distance performed during the 15 min part.

The self-reported feeling of mental fatigue was assessed in two distinct ways. First, by using the MVAS, which was collected on three separate occasions during the trial (i.e., before the first GoNoGo task, before the intervention/control task, and after the second GoNoGo task). Participants were given a line of 10 cm ranging from “not at all” to “completely mentally exhausted”, with the added question “How mentally fatigued do you feel?”. On the other nine occasions (i.e., two times during the intervention/control task at set intervals, after the intervention/control task, before the time trial started, during the warm up and cool down of the time trial, and every 3 min during the progression of the time trial) the feeling of mental fatigue was assessed verbally. Participants were given the exact same question (“How mentally fatigued do you feel?”), and were asked to give a score between 0 and 100, where 0 stood for “not at all”, and 100 stood for “completely mentally exhausted”. No particular definition of mental fatigue was provided to participants. The RPE was assessed using the CR-100 scale [12]. This scale ranges from 1 to 100 with set verbal indicators (i.e., “nothing at all”, “minimum”, “extremely weak”, “very weak”, “weak”, “moderate”, “strong”, “very strong”, “extremely strong”, “maximal” and “absolute maximum”), and was presented visually in front of the bike. Participants were asked to give a score from 0 to 100 with the accompanying question “How heavy does the task feel right now?” at set points during the time trial.

Data Analysis

The collected data were analyzed using R (version 4.5.1.) and R studio (version 2025.05.1). The code used in the analysis and the output of all models can be found in supplementary file 2. Normality was checked visually using Q-Q plots, and statistically using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data that were not normally distributed (i.e., more than half of data points were not normally distributed) were analyzed using non-parametric equivalents. Outliers were checked using the Z score method, and manually evaluated. Imputation errors were corrected, and only in the case of clear response errors was the data imputed as a missing value. Single missing values were interpolated using linear interpolation.

First, the individual features were compared between both time points using paired sample t tests (or Wilcoxon tests in the case of categorical or non-normal data) and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) with a two-way mixed effects model and “consistency” type (i.e., an evaluation of the consistency between measurements in relative scores). Differences in distribution between categorical variables (i.e., physical performance level, sport performance level and chronotype) were assessed using McNemar and subsequent symmetry tests.

To determine a possible difference in influence of mental fatigue between both time points (baseline vs. follow-up), separate analyses were performed. For the distance performed during the cycling time trial, paired sample t tests were conducted to investigate the influence of condition (intervention vs. control) in both time points separately. For all other outcomes (i.e., Go RT, MVAS and RPE) separate linear mixed effects models (fixed effects: condition and time; random effect: participant number) for every time point were conducted. Models were fit using restricted maximum likelihood estimation. Assumptions of homoscedasticity were examined when necessary through visual inspection of residual plots. In case of potential heteroscedasticity, we applied cluster-robust (CR2) standard errors for generalized linear models to obtain unbiased estimates and valid inference.

The robustness of responses was also analyzed using ICCs (two-way mixed effects model and “consistency” type). A delta value was constructed representing the effect of condition (= mental fatigue condition value—control condition value). In the case of multiple time points (i.e., Go RT, MVAS and RPE), multiple condition deltas were calculated. ICC values were interpreted as follows: < 0.40 = poor, 0.40–0.59 = fair; 0.60–0.74 = good and > 0.75 = excellent [28].

All data are presented using means ± standard deviation together with 95% confidence intervals (CI), unless stated otherwise. The significance value was set at 0.05 for all analyses. Different ranges were adopted to indicate the magnitude of the following effect sizes: Cohen's d (< 0.2 = trivial; 0.2–0.6 = small; 0.6–1.2 = moderate; 1.2–2.0 = large; > 2.0 = very large), and partial eta square (~ 0.01 = small; ~ 0.06 = medium; ≥ 0.1 = large). Effect size for Wilcoxon tests was calculated using the r-statistic (r = Z / √N).

Results

Comparison of Individual Features Between Baseline and Follow-Up

Of the 25 participants that performed all sessions at both time points, 15 were male and 10 were female (mean age at baseline = 31 ± 8 y). The mean time between the last visit of the baseline measurement and the first visit of the follow-up measurement was 376 ± 66 days. Table 1 features an overview of the changes and similarities in individual features and the statistical comparison between baseline and follow-up, while Table 2 displays a matrix overview of the changes in the categorical variables between baseline and follow-up. There was a significant decrease in psychomotor vigilance test (PVT) performance (W = 59; p = 0.004; 95% CI = [− 35.59; − 5.22]; r = 0.41) and a significant improvement in Stroop max task performance (t = 3.05; p = 0.004; 95% CI = [42.06; 205.94]; d = 1.03) between baseline and follow-up. There were no other statistically significant differences in individual features. All ICC values comparing baseline and follow-up measurements reached significance, except for the IPAQ values (p = 0.335). McNemar and symmetry tests showed no significant changes between baseline and follow-up in categorical (i.e., physical performance level, sport performance level and chronotype) variables (all p-values were not available). Robustness of the General Effect of Prolonged Cognitive Load on the Selected Outcome Variables

An overview of the separate influence of mental fatigue on the original and follow-up measurements can be found in Table 2.

Analyses showed no influence of the prolonged Stroop task on distance performed during the cycling time trial in the baseline (t = − 0.45; p = 0.652; 95% CI = − 1.10; 0.70]; d = 0.22) and follow-up measurement (t = − 0.10; p = 0.920; 95% CI = [− 1.06; 0.96]; d = 0.06).

However, there was a significant effect of condition (baseline: F = 7.12; p = 0.010; 95% CI = [1.62; 11.20]; ηp2 = 0.09; follow-up: F = 10.70; p = 0.002; 95% CI = [4.54; 18.8]; ηp2 = 0.13) and time (baseline: F = 4.85; p = 0.03; 95% CI = [− 10.10; − 0.50]; ηp2 = 0.07: follow-up: F = 6.98; p = 0.010; 95% CI = [− 16.5; − 2.30]; ηp2 = 0.09) on Go RT in separate models at both baseline and follow-up, with no interaction effect (baseline: F = 0.50; p = 0.481; ηp2 = 0.007; follow-up: F = 1.20; p = 0.278; ηp2 = 0.02). The RT was generally greater in the intervention condition, and also increased over time in both conditions.

There was a condition*time interaction effect for the subjective feeling of mental fatigue, in both the baseline (F = 12.02; p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.19) and follow-up (F = 14.95; p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.23) measurements. Pairwise comparisons showed extremely similar results between baseline and follow-up, with a significant effect of condition from the start of the Stroop task to the 9th minute of the time trial.

There was a significant increase in the RPE in the intervention compared to the control condition in the baseline measurement (F = 5.29; p = 0.022; 95% CI = [0.41; 5.28], ηp2 = 0.02), with a trend to significance in the follow-up measurement for the same effect (F = 3.63; p = 0.058; 95% CI = [− 0.08; 4.52], ηp2 = 0.01). There were similar increases in the RPE over time in baseline (F = 265.01; p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.83) and follow-up (F = 304.13; p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.85), with no interaction effects (baseline: F = 0.68; p = 0.641; ηp2 = 0.01; follow-up: F = 0.46; p = 0.805; ηp2 = 0.008).

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine the temporal robustness of mental fatigue susceptibility by repeating a prolonged cognitive load protocol after one year. Different stable individual features were compared between baseline and follow-up to evaluate the state or trait nature. While most features remained stable, three (i.e., Stroop max task performance, PVT performance and IPAQ-SF results) showed significant differences and/or poor correlation. The improvement in Stroop max task performance likely reflects a practice effect, which is observed in repeated or prolonged Stroop task sessions [29]. The low reliability of the IPAQ-SF, combined with no significant changes in other physical performance or health parameters, aligns with evidence that this questionnaire may not accurately capture physical activity levels, providing support for more consistent subjective questionnaires and objective physical activity measurement methods such as accelerometers [14, 30]. Lastly, PVT performance was adequately correlated, but worsened significantly over time when comparing the baseline and follow-up measures. As this cognitive task is free of practice effects with limited variability over time, significant changes might be an indication of a cognitive state difference between baseline and follow-up [23, 31]. As the participant sample is fairly diverse (with a range of men and women of different ages in different stages of their life and with a wide variety of professions), it is difficult to comment on the possible reasons behind this cognitive state difference. However, as the deterioration remained small and other relevant measures of cognitive performance and sleep quality were unaffected, it is unlikely that there would have been any major influence on the results of the present analysis.

The published paper in which we focus on the complete study population confirmed RPE and the subjective feeling of fatigue as the primary drivers behind the influence of mental fatigue on cycling time trial distance [11]. Compared to this baseline experiment (Cohen’s d: 0.20 [11]), the subpopulation showed similar effects of mental fatigue on cycling time trial performance (Cohen’s d: 0.22), which were again mediated by the RPE. However, results from the follow-up measurement seem to indicate a diminished impact over time. There was a decrease in the mean difference of the condition effect on time trial distance between baseline (0.20 km) and follow-up (0.05 km). Moreover, the regression equation indicated that the influence of mental fatigue on cycling time trial distance in the follow-up was more than halved (slope = 0.465) compared to baseline. In contrast, the influence of mental fatigue on RPE remained relatively stable between baseline (β = 2.85, p = 0.022) and follow-up (β = 2.22, p = 0.058), with only a slight reduction and still showing a trend towards significance. As both trials were conducted within the same environmental conditions with no relevant changes in individual features, one explanation could be a possible influence of external features, such as weather, family and work situation, and daily use of self-regulation [32–35]. While most of the cited references pertain to qualitative research, where participants mention possible influences of other variables based on their own opinion, there are some quantitative studies that have already assessed the influence of external factors on mental fatigue response. For instance, Martin et al. [32] showed that individuals who reported a higher degree of occupational cognitive demand were less susceptible to mental fatigue-related changes in physical performance. Moreover, studies that examine the effects of environmental conditions highlight a possible negative influence of heat [36–38] and hypoxia [39, 40] on mental fatigue effects. Together, our findings reinforce the central role of RPE in mediating physical performance decrements due to mental fatigue [1, 11, 41]. Moreover, while the mentioned external factors were not specifically assessed in this study, our results do provide us with important indications towards other possible influences on mental fatigue susceptibility besides individual features.

The findings of time trial distance and RPE seem to indicate a consistent relationship between subjective and behavioral values in the negative influence of mental fatigue. However, this relationship was not always present, as evident when comparing the subjective (i.e., MVAS) and behavioral (i.e., Go RT) outcomes collected before and after the intervention/control task. Both the graphical representations as well as the statistical analysis (significant ICC in MVAS values post Stroop/Docu) seemed to indicate a small consistency in subjective quantification. However, this consistency was notably absent in the behavioral quantification (Go RT). Van Cutsem et al. [42] showed in their fMRI study that subjective changes due to mental fatigue were related to a decrease in response-inhibition related brain activity [42]. Crucially, these changes in brain activity did not translate to negative effects of mental fatigue on cognitive performance [42]. Therefore, our findings support earlier work documenting mismatches between subjective and behavioral indices of mental fatigue [42–48], further emphasizing the importance of using multiple domains to quantify this complex phenomenon [7]. It is important to mention that these conclusions are based on only one outcome of the GoNoGo task, namely the RT. The accuracy of the Go and NoGo stimuli was not added in these analyses as these metrics portrayed a very limited amount of variation (with medians from 0.92 to 0.99, and interquartile ranges from 0.01 to 0.11). Moreover, it should be noted that, while mental fatigue did have an overall negative effect on Go RT in the original study [11], there was no interaction effect between condition and time in this sample at baseline and follow-up. This could indicate that, for this specific population, mental fatigue did not have a behavioral effect on cognitive performance, and the discrepancy between the subjective and behavioral measures could be the results of a smaller sample size. Overall, a more appropriate cognitive task and a larger participant sample could have provided further important information on the cognitive response to mental fatigue and specifically the discrepancy between these measures and the subjective values.

The results of the present study favor a predominantly state-like interpretation of mental fatigue susceptibility. These results align with earlier indications showing that this susceptibility is highly individualized, and does not seem to be impacted by any of the internal features reported [4]. However, due to the design of the study and the chosen analyses methods, we cannot entirely disregard our hypothesis that mental fatigue susceptibility could be seen as an interplay between a trait and state-like nature. First, to increase clarity, we chose to only focus on four specific outcome measures. While we believe that this was the most appropriate course of action for a comprehensive story, it does limit a complete overview of the temporal robustness response. As the data are enclosed in the supplementary material of this paper, secondary analyses of these data could provide a more complete view of the robustness of mental fatigue susceptibility. Second, it is important to address the limitations of using ICC values to demonstrate consistency in measurements over time. While negative or low ICC as in the present study could be interpreted as evidence of a state-like nature, these values could also be the result of methodological issues such as a limited sample size, measurement errors or a limited number of comparisons [49]. While our design was as tightly controlled as possible, as we used established methods to quantify and induce mental fatigue [7, 11, 25], these considerations need to be taken into account to nuance the state-like interpretation of the nature of mental fatigue susceptibility. Moreover, we only used one follow-up moment after one year, limiting our view of potential differences in response in more short- or long-term follow-up measurements. It is also important to mention that the overall recruitment for the follow-up measurement was low (= ± 35%, recruiting 25 participants out of 70 that were invited). However, as the participant sample was varied, and there were only very few participants that declined to participate with a specific reason mentioned, we are unable to provide a clear reason for this limited participant retention. Future studies using a multi-point longitudinal design could help to further disentangle possible dynamic individual patterns, which could also augment participant retention.

From a practical perspective, the partial stability across some measures suggests that it might still be possible to detect individuals with similar levels of mental fatigue susceptibility, opening the door to improved management of these individuals in daily life and sport performance settings [4, 50]. Future studies should investigate ways to identify these individuals with a higher degree of mental fatigue susceptibility [4, 51, 52]. Moreover, as our findings indicate a state-like interpretation, practitioners should remain cautious about possible mental fatigue induction in all athletes. Literature has already indicated that few practitioners provide deliberate assessment and management of mental fatigue within their respective sports [53]. A recent narrative review recommended an integrative perspective of subjective, behavioral and neurophysiological quantification methods, while taking into account possible confounders [7]. This review could be a first starting point for practitioners to organize structured assessment methods to continuously evaluate the level of mental fatigue in sports.

Conclusions

The results of this follow-up investigation showed distinct differences in the temporal robustness of mental fatigue susceptibility across outcome domains. The influence of a prolonged cognitive load on physical performance showed a notable decrease in the follow-up compared to the baseline measurement, while the overall significant negative influence on RPE remained fairly stable. However, the influence of the intervention on MVAS values showed some degree of robustness from baseline to follow-up. This degree of robustness was notably absent in the Go RT values. There were no strong correlations between condition differences when comparing baseline and follow-up. Therefore, the results indicate that mental fatigue susceptibility can be best understood as a state-like phenomenon, with response varying over time. Future research should build on these findings, further investigating the nature of mental fatigue susceptibility using a range of external features, and providing a comparison of responses over multiple time points.
