Response to the Letter to the Editor
Authors: Nickson M Karie
Published: 2026

I want to take this opportunity to thank the authors for their thoughtful letter regarding our 2019 article, “Diverging deep learning cognitive computing techniques into cyber forensics.”

On behalf of the other authors, I want to appreciate their engagement with the conceptual and practical dimensions of deep learning integration in cyber forensic workflows.

At the outset, it is important to contextualise our contribution within the temporal and technological landscape of 2019, almost 7 years ago. At the time of this publication, the application of deep learning in cyber forensics was emergent, with limited operational deployments, evolving benchmarking standards, and minimal regulatory guidance on AI-assisted evidentiary analysis. The objective of our work was to provide a structured conceptual framework to stimulate discourse and research direction, rather than to prescribe fully validated, forensic-grade implementation protocols.

I observed several concerns raised in the letter, particularly those related to data bias, explainability, validation standards, and evidential integrity. All these are well taken. However, the authors must acknowledge that substantial developments have occurred since this publication was released in 2019. More specifically, advances in explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), model auditing methodologies, reproducibility standards, and AI governance frameworks have significantly strengthened the operational viability of machine learning in forensic contexts. Again, contemporary research increasingly integrates validation pipelines, chain-of-custody safeguards within automated workflows, and human-in-the-loop architectures to mitigate risks associated with black-box inference systems.

With respect to evidence integrity, our original framework did not advocate unsupervised automation over raw evidentiary artefacts. Rather, our intention was to position deep learning as a decision-support mechanism within established forensic procedures. The principle that automated systems should augment, rather than replace, expert judgment remains central to our position and aligns with current best practices in digital forensic science.

Furthermore, the evolution toward forensic readiness and near–real-time monitoring has indeed become more pronounced in recent years, particularly in response to the scale and velocity of cyber threats. While our 2019 paper focused to some extent on post-incident investigative structuring, it laid foundational groundwork that subsequent scholarship has extended toward proactive and operationalised models. And we wanted exactly that to initiate an academic and research discussion around that topic.

Again, we agree that interdisciplinary collaboration, spanning computer science, digital forensics, legal scholarship, and regulatory policy, is essential to ensure admissibility, transparency, and accountability in AI-assisted forensic practice. The concerns articulated in the letter underscore precisely the type of critical dialogue that advances the field.

Finally, while certain limitations identified by the authors reflect valid implementation challenges, many stem from the developmental state of the field at the time of publication (almost 7 years ago). The rapid maturation of AI validation frameworks, governance standards, and explainability tools suggests that the trajectory envisioned in 2019 has evolved considerably. Ongoing empirical validation and policy alignment will continue to shape the responsible integration of deep learning within cyber forensic science.

I hope this brings context to the authors and also what we intended to do 7 years ago. More research still needs to be done in this area, and anyone is welcome to extend our work to whichever extent is achievable.
