Improving the performance of practical wavelength division multiplexing decoy-state quantum key distribution with advantage distillation
Authors: Bumil Kim, Jun Heo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuantum key distribution (QKD) is a promising method for sharing an information-theoretically secure secret key using the properties of quantum mechanics. Although QKD is the most practical field in quantum information science, its widespread adoption is constrained by high implementation costs. Despite the cost-effectiveness of propagating QKD with classical communication using wavelength division multiplexing (WDM), the noise generated by relatively strong classical signals degrades QKD performance. Even though several mitigation strategies exist, they often involve complexity or additional hardware. In this paper, we apply advantage distillation (AD) to enhance the performance of a practical decoy-state BB84 QKD system operating in a WDM environment. We perform finite-key analysis in both co-propagation and counter-propagation scenarios with three classical channels, and furthermore we examine the effects of varying the number of classical channels and the optical power per channel. A comparison of simulation results obtained with and without AD demonstrates that AD is robust to noise originating from classical channels. These results show that AD increases the secure key rate and transmission distance in the WDM-based QKD environment under both co-propagation and counter-propagation without requiring hardware changes. These findings suggest advantage distillation is a promising strategy for the widespread adoption of WDM-based QKD, including in challenging counter-propagation cases.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Quantum key distribution (QKD) is a technology that utilizes the properties of quantum mechanics to allow two parties, Alice and Bob, to share a symmetric, information-theoretically secure key while detecting the presence of eavesdropping1. Since the first QKD protocol, i.e., BB84, was proposed in 19842, many studies and implementations have been conducted to improve the practical security and the secure key rate performance3–5.

In practical implementations, it is crucial to consider side-channel attacks that arise from imperfections in hardware components, such as the source, channel, and detectors. Regarding the source, because high-speed, high-efficiency deterministic single-photon sources are not yet sufficiently mature, most practical QKD systems employ phase-randomized weak coherent pulses. Such pulses have a nonzero probability of containing multi-photon components, which makes these systems vulnerable to photon-number-splitting (PNS) attacks6. To overcome this issue, the decoy-state method was proposed7–9, in which pulses with different intensities are randomly interleaved to obtain tighter bounds on the yield and error rate of the single-photon component. This enables secure key extraction with weak coherent pulses and improves the achievable secure key rate and transmission distance. As a result, the decoy-state method has been widely adopted in experimental demonstrations3,10,11, and tighter finite-key analyses12–17 as well as security proofs for practical implementations have been developed18,19. Among these components, detectors are often regarded as the weakest link. Their complexity and role in receiving external signals render them highly vulnerable to security threats. For example, an eavesdropper can launch attacks, such as sending additional light to expose information or using intense light to disable detectors. Measurement-Device-Independent (MDI) QKD was proposed to overcome these vulnerabilities20. In MDI-QKD, Alice and Bob send their quantum states to an untrusted third-party, Charlie, for measurement. This setup eliminates vulnerabilities associated with measurement devices because the secret key is not compromised even if Charlie’s measurement results are manipulated.

As MDI-QKD generates secret keys using only two-photon interference, it possesses a relatively low key-generation rate and is limited by a Pirandola-Laurenza-Ottaviani-Banchi (PLOB) bound, which constrains its transmission distance21. To address this, Twin-field (TF) QKD was introduced, which utilizes single-photon interference to surpass the PLOB bound even without quantum repeaters22. Although TF-QKD offers enhanced security for measurement devices and overcomes the PLOB bound, maintaining global phase matching between two independent light sources from Alice and Bob over long distances is difficult. Various modifications to TF-QKD have been proposed to solve this challenge, such as Sending-or-Not-Sending (SNS) TF-QKD23, Mode-Pairing (MP) QKD24, and Phase-Matching (PM) QKD25.

Despite developments in QKD technology and commercial systems becoming available, challenges in practical implementation remain. QKD requires a dedicated optical fiber as a quantum channel; however, the high installation costs of a new fiber limit its widespread use. A straightforward approach involves integrating QKD with the existing classical communication infrastructure using wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM). WDM is also receiving increasing attention in recent studies on hybrid classical-quantum networks, such as SDN-enabled QKD networks deployed over shared communication infrastructures26 and heterogeneous quantum network architectures27.

However, the noise generated by high-power classical signals considerably degrades the weak quantum signals. In particular, backward spontaneous Raman scattering (BSpRS) noise, which does not decrease with distance, generates an error rate that is too high for secret key generation as the transmission distance increases, substantially shortening the effective range compared with a standalone QKD system.

Several strategies have been proposed for addressing this problem. One approach involves separating the QKD and classical signals into different wavelength bands (e.g., placing the QKD channel in the O-band and classical channels in the C-band)28. Other methods that operate within dense wavelength division multiplexing (DWDM) include optimized channel allocation29, separation of forward and backward channels onto different fibers30, adapted data laser power31, and time scheduling32. Another approach is to modify the fiber itself. Ultra-low-loss (ULL) fibers can improve the signal-to-noise ratio by reducing attenuation33. Hollow core fibers (HCFs), in which light propagates in an air-filled core, reduce light-matter interaction and thereby suppress nonlinear effects34. Multicore fibers (MCFs) provide spatial separation between quantum and classical channels through space division multiplexing, thereby mitigating inter-channel interference35. Additionally, a method for optimizing the node layout based on the characteristics of forward and backward noise has also recently been proposed36.

However, these approaches typically require complex scheduling or additional hardware or replacing existing fiber infrastructure. Moreover, when a WDM environment is combined with the plug-and-play method37, which is often adopted to simplify the implementation of QKD by automatically compensating polarization drifts, conventional methods are not well suited to the plug-and-play method. For example, separating quantum and classical channels into different wavelength bands and channel allocation cannot sufficiently mitigate BSpRS noise arising along the unavoidable counter-propagation path in the plug-and-play method. Reducing the power of the classical channels to suppress noise is constrained by the sensitivity of the classical receivers. Furthermore, the separation of forward and backward channels onto different fibers can weaken the automatic polarization compensation, while time scheduling reduces the secure key rate because it requires waiting for the returning pulses.

The Advantage Distillation (AD) technique, which was introduced in classical cryptography38, can serve as a practical solution to integrate QKD into WDM environments without requiring additional hardware modification. AD is a classical post-processing technique based on two-way classical communication that improves error tolerance by extracting highly correlated bits from weakly correlated raw keys. Because of this property, AD has been investigated in various QKD protocols, including BB8439,40, Device-independent (DI) QKD41, MDI QKD42, TF QKD43, and MP QKD44. Recently, experimental demonstrations have been conducted using AD to overcome hardware implementation constraints45, and rigorous finite-size analysis of decoy-state BB84 with AD has also been studied46.

Several studies have applied AD to QKD in WDM environments under asymptotic key-length assumptions, including analyses of MP QKD co-transmitted classical signals over various fiber types47 and entanglement-based QKD combined with optical amplifiers on the classical signals to suppress spontaneous Raman scattering (SpRS) noise48. However, practical implementations operate in the finite-key regime, where only a limited number of bits are exchanged.

Recently, a WDM experiment demonstrated decoy-state BB84 with AD and finite-key analysis in a configuration where a classical channel in the O-band coexisted with a single quantum channel in the C-band, providing experimental evidence for performance benefit of AD in that WDM setting49. In contrast, our work addresses a C-band DWDM environment in which the QKD channel and multiple classical channels are placed within the C-band at 200 GHz spacing, introducing crosstalk noise, cumulative SpRS noise from channels with different SpRS coefficients and additional four-wave mixing (FWM) noise from inter-channel nonlinear interactions. Furthermore, we perform finite-key analysis for both co-propagation and counter-propagation scenarios and examine the secure key rate and maximum transmission distance for different transmitted bit string lengths, numbers of classical channels, and optical powers per classical channel.

Discussion

In this paper, we performed finite-key analysis in a C-band DWDM environment with multiple classical channels and one QKD channel. We simulated scenarios involving the transmission of bit strings of different lengths. In the co-propagation scenario at , the transmission distance increased by up to 36.32 km (26.17%) compared with the non-AD case, which is approximately 27.22 km (18.40%) longer than the maximum achievable distance in a standalone QKD environment without WDM noise. In the counter-propagation scenario at the transmission distance increased by up to 30.89 km (41.28%) over the non-AD case. However, unlike in the co-propagation scenario, despite the transmission distance extension achieved through AD in the WDM environment, the distance could not exceed that of a standalone QKD system.

Our results show that AD provides a stronger relative improvement at the higher noise levels under WDM conditions. In the co-propagation scenario, under our channel arrangement, adding more classical channels increased the overall noise affecting the quantum channel. In particular, the number of FWM combinations contributing noise to the QKD signal increased and the additional channels had larger SpRS coefficients than the first three channels, enhancing the SpRS noise. Consequently, although the achievable transmission distance without AD decreased as the number of classical channels increased, the relative benefit of AD became larger, 85.84 km (109.65%) relative to the case without AD in the nine channel co-propagation cases. In the counter-propagation scenario, AD increased the maximum transmission distance by 31.05 km (72.17%) compared to the non-AD case in nine channel cases.

As the optical power per classical channel increased, the overall noise affecting the quantum channel also increased. Under our channel arrangement, in the co-propagation scenario, the 0 dBm case was strongly degraded by dominant FWM noise at short distances. Although the reachable transmission distance without AD was substantially reduced under this condition, the relative benefit of AD became much larger, reaching 105.56 km (308.72%) compared to the corresponding case without AD at 0 dBm. In the counter-propagation scenario, the degradation with increasing optical power was more gradual because the performance was limited by BSpRS noise. AD provided an improvement of 30.33 km (104.10%) compared to the case without AD at 0 dBm. This indicates that AD remains effective even when the optical power per classical channel is increased to satisfy the receiver sensitivity required for error-free classical communication.

These improvements have considerable implications for practical implementations.

In plug-and-play QKD configurations within WDM environments, bidirectional signal crossing between classical and quantum channels has traditionally imposed severe constraints, either substantially reducing transmission distance or requiring classical communication to pause until the QKD signal returns. Our results demonstrate that the AD technique can effectively mitigate these limitations under counter-propagation conditions, thereby facilitating more practical integration of WDM and QKD systems.

In this study, we did not apply any noise mitigation techniques other than a narrow bandpass filter. Therefore, reducing noise effects by adjusting the classical signal power31, optimized channel allocation to suppress FWM and SpRS noise29, and post-processing enhancements, such as employing tighter finite-key analysis methods17 or applying AD multiple times rather than a single iteration44 are expected to further improve performance.

Finally, a rigorous finite-key analysis of decoy-state BB84 with AD has recently been proposed46, in which the statistical fluctuation arising from the block-wise post-selection of AD is addressed at the security proof level. Applying such a framework to WDM-based QKD is expected to provide a more rigorous security guarantee for the resulting secure key rate.

Noise in WDM-based QKD

In this section, we discuss the noise generated by classical signals. In the co-propagation system of QKD and classical optical communication, the powers of the classical and quantum signals differ considerably, which causes the classical signal to induce noise in the quantum channel. The noise can be classified into in-band and out-band noise53. The out-band noise includes the crosstalk noise, which can be sufficiently suppressed if the isolation characteristics of the optical components are sufficient. The in-band noise includes FWM noise and SpRS noise.
