Ventilator waveform interpretation skills of attending intensivists relative to critical care fellows (VISA) study
Authors: Roxana Amirahmadi, Muhtadi Alnababteh, Michael Keller, Junfeng Sun, Kavitha Bagavathy, Sami Safadi, Bashar S Staitieh, Stephanie I Maximous, Eric Kriner, Christian J Woods, Emil Oweis, Matthew P Schreiber, Nitin Seam, Burton W Lee, the Critical Care Education Research Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMechanical ventilation can be lifesaving for critically ill patients. However, there is evidence that intensivists’ mechanical ventilation management skills are lacking, presenting a potential risk to patient safety. Ventilator waveform interpretation skills of intensivists need to be further studied to address learning gaps among intensivists.ObjectivesWe aimed to study the baseline ventilator waveform interpretation and management skills of attending intensivists compared to those of critical care fellows in academic medical institutions in the United States.MethodsThis prospective multi-institutional investigation spanned 7 US cities where the Multi-Institutional Fundamentals of Mechanical Ventilation Course (MFMVC) took place in July and August 2023. Participants included attending intensivists (study group) from academic medical centers and critical care fellows (control group) who participated in the MFMVC. A validated ventilator waveform test was administered pre- and post-MFMVC. The primary outcome was the pretest scores of intensivists compared to fellows.ResultsThirty-three attendings and 136 fellows took the pretest and 29 attendings and 135 fellows took the posttest. Mean ± SD pretest scores out of 100 points were 24.3 ± 22.3 for attendings and 16.9 ± 18.7 for fellows (P = .0503). Posttest scores for attendings (45.6 ± 25.5; delta score, 21.3; P = .0015) and fellows (43.6 ± 24.0; delta score, 26.7; P <.00001) significantly improved. There was no difference in posttest scores between attendings versus fellows (P = .69).ConclusionsIntensivists’ ventilator waveform interpretation and management skills are not different from those of critical care fellows before and after a mechanical ventilation course. These findings highlight a need for targeted educational interventions given the apparent concerning knowledge gaps among intensivists post-fellowship.Keywords: mechanical ventilation, attending intensivists, multi-institutional course, ventilator waveform interpretation

Introduction

Mechanical ventilation can be lifesaving, but if inappropriately used, it can lead to ventilator-associated lung injury, hemodynamic compromise, and patient–ventilator asynchrony, which in turn can contribute to adverse clinical outcomes, patient suffering, and even death.1,2 Respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation is primarily managed by critical care physicians in intensive care unit (ICU) settings. Therefore, mechanical ventilation is an essential component of critical care fellowship education.3 However, there is evidence to suggest that mechanical ventilation management skills among intensivists may be lacking. If so, this presents a critical safety concern for mechanically ventilated patients, as failure to address ventilator asynchronies and conditions that could lead to ventilator-induced lung injury is associated with greater patient morbidity and mortality1,2,4 and the recognition of ventilator asynchrony is a key component in mechanical ventilation training and safe practice. Both ventilator over- and underassistance can lead to eccentric diaphragmatic contractions and diaphragmatic myotrauma, which may result in patient discomfort, diaphragm weakness, prolonged ventilation, and increased mortality.1,2,5,6 Similarly, double-triggering, due to asynchronies like reverse triggering or premature cycling, may result in delivery of consecutive tidal volumes without exhalation, posing particular harm in settings such as acute distress respiratory syndrome where low-tidal volume ventilation is essential.7 Ineffective triggering, frequently seen in the setting of auto-positive-end-expiratory-pressure, has also been associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation and mortality.1,2,6 While system-level and team-based barriers may contribute to suboptimal implementation of low tidal volume ventilation, failure to recognize and address asynchronies could be due to intensivists’ limited knowledge.

Trainees are often dissatisfied with their mechanical ventilation training,8 and many critical care fellows do not achieve mastery of mechanical ventilation management skills during their fellowship.9 Prior investigations have demonstrated that a multiday course can help improve ventilator waveform recognition and management among fellows.10 However, a recent follow-up of that study found that fellows who undergo such a course experience a substantial decay in that knowledge unless they engage in a longitudinal preceptorial course.11

The opportunity for fellows to learn the needed mechanical ventilation skills during fellowship is likely tied to the knowledge and skills of the supervising attending physicians who are formally and informally teaching the fellows. Expertise in a subject is essential for a teacher to effectively relate the educational content to different scenarios and efficiently identify and address gaps in student knowledge.12 If the attending intensivists have the sufficient expertise but the fellows do not, the various traditional and novel approaches to transferring these skills to the fellows in an efficient and enduring manner seem appropriate. However, if attending intensivists lack these skills, mechanical ventilation education needs to expand beyond fellowship training to include ongoing professional development of attending intensivists. Indeed, a prior study found that the ventilator waveform interpretation skills were poor among attending intensivists and not significantly better than that of the critical care trainees.13 However, this was a single institutional study of 10 attending physicians.

A recent survey of 1832 ICU professionals (physicians, nurses, and respiratory therapists) across 31 countries found that only slightly over 60% of waveforms demonstrating patient–ventilator asynchrony were correctly identified.14 While the sample size of this study was large, only 51 participants were physicians from North America, including 31 from the United States. Furthermore, because fellows-in-training were not studied, it is unknown how the attending intensivists perform compared to fellows.

In this present investigation, we aim to conduct a multi-institutional study comparing ventilator waveform interpretation skills among attending intensivists at US academic medical centers to that of critical care fellows in training. Using a validated mechanical ventilator waveform assessment tool, we evaluated the performance of attending intensivists and critical care fellows at academic medical centers before and after a multiday mechanical ventilation course.

Methods

Subjects

The study subjects were attending intensivists from academic medical centers who voluntarily participated in the Multi-Institutional Fundamentals of Mechanical Ventilation Course (MFMVC), which took place in July and August 2023 at 7 different cities in the United States: Washington, DC; Baltimore, Maryland; Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania; Minneapolis, Minnesota; Los Angeles, California; Atlanta, Georgia; and Charlottesville, Virginia. The fellow and attending participants were from 30 different academic medical institutions in the United States. All participants expressed interest in taking the course in order to improve their mechanical ventilation skills.

The control subjects were the critical care fellows participating in the MFMVC in the same 7 locations. Most of the institutions required their first-year fellows to participate in the course, but rising second, third, and fourth-year critical care fellows were also welcome to join.

This study was exempt from institutional review board approval by the National Institutes of Health Office of Humans Subjects Research (Exempt #13430) under 45 Code of Federal Regulations 41.101(b).

Curriculum

The MFMVC is a 4-day course that has been described previously.10 In brief, MFMVC requires learners to complete preclass readings and engage in interactive lectures that present introductory information on a wide range of topics related to mechanical ventilation, with an emphasis on ventilator waveform analysis and patient–ventilator asynchrony recognition and management. Didactic sessions were followed by collaborative small group sessions in which learners participated in simulations and problem-solving exercises to solidify concepts taught during the preceding lectures. The learner-to-educator ratio during the small group sessions was approximately 6:1.10

Demographics survey

We gathered demographic information from attendings and fellows as part of the pretest and posttest assessments. From the attending intensivists, we collected the following information: the type of prior residency and fellowship training; the number of years since completing their critical care fellowship; percentage of time devoted to direct patient care, research, administrative work, and education; undergraduate major; and other graduate-level training outside of medical school. From the fellows, we collected the following information: the type of prior residency training; postgraduate year; year in fellowship and other fellowship(s) in addition to critical care if any; undergraduate major; and other graduate-level training outside of medical school.

Waveform test

A previously validated15 ventilator waveform test was administered to attendings and fellows prior to (pretest) and after (posttest) their participation in the MFMVC. Given that there is no standard mechanical ventilation waveform assessment, one of the authors (B.W.L.) with expertise in mechanical ventilation created the test in collaboration with other mechanical ventilation experts. Over several years, the test underwent iterative changes based on feedback from learners and educators. The final version of this test was pilot tested with fellows and respiratory therapists before taking on its final form, which was used in this present study. The pretest and the posttest questions are identical. In brief, the test consists of 5 questions, each with a picture of pressure-time and flow-time waveforms demonstrating common ventilator asynchronies that can cause significant patient morbidity or mortality if not adequately addressed. The test questions target one of the phases of the mechanically ventilated breath: triggering, flow, cycling and exhalation. The test requires integration of knowledge about patient–ventilator interaction and underlying physiologic principles influencing each phase. Each question asks the participant to (1) identify the waveforms from a list of 12 options and (2) select the appropriate management from a list of 11 options. Participants are asked to choose all potentially correct answers for each question. Incorrect answers that would potentially cause patient harm or worsen the asynchrony led to deduction of points while partially correct and correct responses were awarded positive points. Answers that would lead to neither harm nor benefit were awarded 0 points. Every question had the same answer choices and were weighted equally at 20 points for a total of 100 points. The test was scored by a single grader and 20% of the pretest and 100% of the posttest scores were confirmed by an independent grader using the same predefined rubric. Any discrepancies in the scores were resolved by discussion and consensus. Learners were not shown the correct answers to the test or their scores because we plan to use this test in future studies and aim to prevent compromising the validity of the test through familiarity with the specific questions and answers.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome of interest was the baseline pretest scores of the attending intensivists compared to that of the fellows. The primary outcome was compared using an unpaired t-test. We also compared the mean pretest and posttest scores for both groups using a paired parametric t-test. The difference between pretest and posttest scores (delta scores) of attendings versus the fellows were compared using an unpaired t-test.

We then explored whether test performance was influenced by gender, years in practice, percentage of time devoted to direct patient care, residency and fellowship training background, and educational background in science, technology, engineering, or mathematics (STEM) fields prior to medical school.

We report continuous variables as mean (± SD) and categorical variables as proportions with 95% confidence intervals. We used unpaired t-test for between-group differences and paired t-test for within-group differences for continuous variables and χ2 test to compare categorical variables.

We used linear regression models to investigate the relationship between the attendings’ number of years in practice post-fellowship and their pretest, posttest, and delta scores. Since other variables (gender, not being exposed to STEM-related fields prior to medical school, residency training, fellowship training, and percentage of time spent on direct patient care) could potentially confound this relationship, univariable analyses were done to investigate the relationships between each of the potential confounders and pretest, posttest, and delta scores. For potential confounders that were categorical or continuous variables, the test scores were compared using t-tests or Pearson correlation, respectively. If there appeared to be a significant relationship in the univariable analysis (defined as P ≤.1), the variable was included in the final linear regression model. Statistical analyses were completed using SAS version 9.4 software.

Results

A total of 142 fellows and 38 attending intensivists participated in the MFMVC course. One-hundred thirty-six fellows and 33 attendings took the pretest and 135 fellows and 29 attendings took the posttest; their baseline characteristics are described in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. Sixty-nine percent of the attendings were pulmonary and critical care trained and 79% devoted at least 50% of their time to direct patient care. Fifty-seven percent of the attendings and 68.4% of the fellows had completed internal medicine residencies.

Out of 100 possible points, the mean ± SD pretest score (24.3 ± 22.3) for the attending intensivists was not statistically different from the mean for fellows (16.9 ± 18.7; P = .0503). Both the attendings and fellows significantly improved their scores after the course. For the attendings, the posttest score improved to 45.6 ± 25.5 (delta score, 21.3; P = .0015), while for the fellows, the posttest improved to 43.6 ± 24.0 (delta score, 26.7; P <.00001). There was no significant difference in the posttest scores between attendings versus fellows (P = .69) (Figure 1). There was also no significant difference in the delta scores between attendings and fellows (20.15 ± 26.7 vs 26.5 ± 25.5; P = .24).

Interestingly, although the mean posttest scores improved significantly for both groups, 21 of 132 fellows and 8 of 27 attendings had delta scores less than zero, suggesting that they performed worse on the posttest compared to the pretest. There was not a significant difference between the proportion of fellows and attendings who had negative delta scores (15.9% vs 29.6%, P = .10) (Figure 2). Twenty-one participants in the MFMVC took only the pretest or posttest, so they did not have delta scores.

We also performed a post hoc analysis of the relationship between pretest scores and delta scores for all participants, and also analyzed fellows and attendings separately. Interestingly, we observed a significant negative correlation between pretest and delta scores (R = −0.44, P <.001) for all participants. A similar pattern was observed for fellows and attendings. We believe this may reflect a ceiling effect where participants who entered the course with stronger baseline knowledge of mechanical ventilation likely had less room for measurable improvement, whereas those with more limited prior knowledge may have benefited more substantially from the course content (Figures S1–S3).

Pretest, posttest, and delta score did not significantly differ by gender, years in practice, percentage of time devoted to direct patient care, residency and fellowship training background, and educational background in a STEM field prior to medical school (Tables S1–S3). However, not having a background in a STEM field prior to medical school was the only variable that yielded possible association with posttest scores and delta scores (P = .1 and .06, respectively). The slope estimates (± SE) in the final linear regression models suggest that the number of years since fellowship training has no association with the pretest (−0.58 ± 0.42, P = .18), posttest (−0.75 ± 0.47, P = .12), and delta scores (−0.20 ± 0.51, P = .70). Similarly, the slope estimates suggested that there was no association between the lack of exposure to a STEM field prior to medical school and posttest (+9.57 ± 11.45, P = .41) and delta scores (+17.57 ± 13.29, P = .20).

Discussion

In this multi-institutional study, we assessed the ventilator waveform interpretation and management skills of attending intensivists at academic teaching hospitals. Similar to prior findings,10,11 the fellows in this study had a low mean pretest score. Surprisingly, however, attendings from various academic teaching hospitals did not outperform fellows on the pretest or posttest. Although both fellows and attendings on average significantly improved their skills after taking the MFMVC course, both groups’ mean posttest scores were still below 50%. Additionally, 15.9% of fellows and 29.6% of attendings who took the course performed worse on the posttest than on the pretest, suggesting that while the course improved the performance of most fellow and attending participants, the course did not benefit all learners.

Our findings raise a potential opportunity to improve the safety concern of mechanical ventilation. Our findings also raise an interesting question about fellowship education since fellows are expected to learn about mechanical ventilation from attending intensivists formally and informally. Given that mechanical ventilation is an integral part of critical care medicine, intensivists are expected to be experts in utilizing mechanical ventilation compared to physicians of other specialties. However, neither attendings nor fellow groups in our study achieved mean scores that would suggest mastery of the content. While a multiday course can help address some knowledge gaps, the results of this study suggest that other educational modalities would be beneficial to achieve mastery of mechanical ventilator knowledge among critical care attendings and fellows. To that effect, a longitudinal preceptorial course in mechanical ventilation11 that was found to be helpful for fellows may also be needed for attendings.

Although the relationships between attendings’ number of years in practice after fellowship and pretest, posttest, and delta scores were not statistically significant, one potential reason for the lack of significance in the findings could be the low sample size of attendings, particularly among attendings who have more than 10 years’ experience. Thus, a study with a larger sample size may be warranted. Interestingly, the percentage of time devoted to clinical care did not have a strong correlation with performance on the pretest and even the attendings who spent the most time working clinically also demonstrated ventilator waveform analysis skills that were no better than those of critical care fellows.

A recent study among 1832 ICU professionals (physicians, nurses, and respiratory therapists) found that they correctly identified slightly over 60% of the waveforms demonstrating patient–ventilator asynchronies and that respiratory therapists outperformed physicians in 11 of 15 questions.14 Because the participants were recruited via social media, the denominator of potential participants is unknown. Nevertheless, this study highlights a potential need for broader improvement in mechanical ventilation education. The ability of attending intensivists to correctly identify and manage ventilator waveform abnormalities in our study was consistent with these findings, although the pretest score of 24% among attending intensivists was lower than the 60% reported by Liu et al.14 In their study, the use of multiple-choice questions, each with 5 answer choices (one of which was “I don’t know”), may have limited the potential to distinguish true mastery from contextual guessing. Moreover, only 1 of 15 questions asked about management of the waveform abnormality. In contrast there were 11 identification and 12 management answer choices for each of the waveforms in our study, making it difficult to guess the correct answer at random.

This current study has several strengths. First, subjects are attendings and fellows with diverse training backgrounds and from 30 institutions in 7 US cities. Second, we have pretest data from 33 attendings and 136 fellows and posttest data from 29 attendings and 135 fellows, which we believe is the largest assessment of ventilator waveform knowledge to date comparing attending intensivists to fellows-in-training. Additionally, we used a validated tool that has previously been used to assess ventilator waveform knowledge.10,11,15

There are also several weaknesses in our study. The study only included attendings who chose to attend the MFMVC, but do not have data on which attendings chose to participate or the specific reasons for their self-selection. We recognize that multiple reasons are possible. It may be that some attending physicians enrolled in the course due to a lack of confidence in their mechanical ventilation skills, suggesting a potentially weaker baseline knowledge or less clinical experience relative to the broader population of academic intensivists. Conversely, it is also possible that those who participated were particularly self-motivated and already highly skilled or engaged in medical education and committed to deepening their expertise in mechanical ventilation, with the goal of becoming more effective teachers for fellows and trainees. The sample size of this study might not have been large enough to fully capture the effect of years in practice on pretest and posttest performance, especially among attendings with the highest number of years in practice. We also recognize that, by design, our study was limited in only assessing ventilator waveform knowledge through a written test, but we were unable to assess the impact of the course on actual practice patterns or patient outcomes.

Our study findings suggest that there is a need and an opportunity to improve asynchrony recognition and management skills among intensivists. Since mechanical ventilation is an essential component of critical care clinical practice, this may also present a potential opportunity to improve patient safety in the ICU. Furthermore, as fellows often rely on attending physicians for their expertise and guidance, narrowing the knowledge gap among attendings may potentially impact the next generation of clinicians. Unlike fellows, attendings do not have dedicated time to attend structured and time-consuming didactic sessions, so educational modalities geared toward attendings need to be flexible and cater to their busy schedules. As demonstrated by the wide range of pretest scores among our attending cohort, the baseline knowledge of ventilator waveform identification and management among attendings is likely very diverse, so educational modalities catered to attendings must address educational needs of a wide range of learners.
