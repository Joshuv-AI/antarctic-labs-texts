Differences between breech and cephalic neonates born after planned cesarean section: a systematic review
Authors: Yi Du, Xiaoyun Zhang, Xinyu Zhou, Philip N Baker, Mark D Kilby, Chao Tong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA growing number of studies have found that breech presentation is related to preexisting diseases and may affect future fetal development. This study aimed to compare the differences between breech presentation and cephalic presentation and explore the underlying mechanisms of adverse outcomes caused by fetal station itself.Methods‘PubMed’, ‘Embase’ and ‘Cochrane Library’ were searched from inception to October 21, 2022. Comparative studies containing ‘prelabor CS’ or ‘elective CS’ of cephalic and breech presentation were included. A total of 585 studies were included, and 5 studies were eligible for final research. Review Manager 5.4.1 was used for data synthesis.ResultsThe mortality of breech group is not different from that of cephalic group (RR 0.69, 95% CI 0.09–5.13, p = 0.71). No differences in infant (< 1 year) and neonatal (0–27 days) mortality were found between the breech and cephalic presentations (RR 1.56, 95% CI 0.86–2.82, p = 0.14). However, breech group was found to have lower birth weight (p < 0.002), umbilical coiling index(p < 0.0001), and bone SOS (p = 0.03 )than cephalic group.ConclusionsThe breech presentation itself may not be related to adverse pregnancy outcomes, and more attention is needed for further exploration.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08327-z.Keywords: Mortality, Morbidity, Neurological development, External cephalic version, Apgar score, Neonates, Fetus, Light perception, Cerebral circulation, Autoregulation

Introduction

Breech presentation refers to an abnormal fetal position in the uterus, where the fetus’s bottom, knee(s) or feet present at the uterine cervix, with the head in the fundus. Of all term deliveries, breech presentation accounts for 3–4% and is always considered more dangerous than cephalic presentation, specifically due to the delivery method. In one study, fetal mortality and morbidity of vaginal breech were found to be higher than those of the vaginal cephalic group, and planned cesarean section (CS) could effectively reduce the rate of disadvantageous fetal outcomes [1]. Although CS has been widely recommended for term breech presentation since the 2000 Term Breech Trial [2, 3], there are still many debates because some studies believe that the high rates of fetal morbidity and mortality of breech vaginal delivery are partly due to the lack of delivery techniques and strict indications for breech vaginal delivery [4, 5].

On the other hand, breech presentation might be a marker of preexisting diseases, and the head-up position may have an adverse effect on fetal development [6–8]. However, a head-down position is not always good. A study by Sekulić SR et al. showed that the fetus is in a hypogravity environment in the uterus during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy [9]. Thus, the head-down position of the cephalic fetus may lead to differences in heart-cerebral circulation compared with the head-up position of the breech fetus. Head-down tilt (HDT) bed rest is a model used to imitate the effects of spaceflight in astronauts, and the change from breech presentation to head-down cephalic presentation can be considered equivalent to the process of an astronaut flying into space [10, 11]. However, neurocognitive deficits have been reported in astronauts after long-term exposure to microgravity [12]. Therefore, it is necessary to reconsider the influence of cephalic presentation on the development of the fetal brain, especially in terms of long-term outcomes. Moreover, we need to review whether the existing guidelines maximize the health benefits to both mothers and children.

The aim of this study was to perform a systematic review and meta-analysis to compare the neonatal outcomes of term singleton breech presentation and cephalic presentation after planned CS and to review the literature to summarize the possible differences caused by breech and cephalic postures during pregnancy and their mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Information source and search strategy

The PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases were searched up to October 21, 2022, with a combination of the following words: ‘breech’, ‘cephalic’, ‘vertex’, ‘cesarean section’, ‘planned cesarean section’, ‘singleton’ and ‘term’. The detailed research list is shown in Supplementary Appendix 1. The reference lists of all selected articles were independently screened for additional articles. The results are presented based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [13]. (registration number: CRD42022380479 11/12/2022).

Study selection and eligibility criteria

The reports returned from the search were screened based on the titles and abstracts by two independent reviewers (Yi Du and Xiaoyun Zhang), and then, the final decision was made after screening the full text of each study. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Data extraction

Data were extracted by two independent reviewers (Yi Du and Xiaoyun Zhang). If disagreements could not be resolved by discussion, a third reviewer was consulted (Chao Tong). Basic information, including the first author’s name, publication year, study design, gestational age and outcome items, was collected.

The primary outcomes of this study were mortality and Apgar scores. The secondary outcomes were the other short-term and long-term outcomes: Birth weight, Head circumference, Brachial plexus injury, Neurological morbidity, Trauma, Umbilical artery PH and so on. Assessment of risk of bias

The Newcastle‒Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (NOS) is a ‘star system’ used for quality assessment of case‒control studies and cohort studies in three broad aspects: selection, comparability, and exposure for case‒control studies or outcome for cohort studies. High-quality studies had 7 or more stars, moderate-quality studies had 6 stars, and low-quality studies had fewer than 6 stars [14]. The American Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), comprising 11 questions, was used to evaluate cross-sectional studies. ‘Yes’ answers were worth 1 point, and other answers were worth 0. Studies were regarded as high quality if they had scores of 8–11, moderate quality if they had scores of 4–7, or low quality if they had scores of 0–3 [15].

Data synthesis

We calculated the p value for the effect measures for each outcome. The chi-square test and the t test were applied for categorical data and continuous data separately. If no patient was reported in one group, Fisher’s exact test was applied. If the same outcome item was reported in three or more studies, a meta-analysis was conducted. For meta-analysis, risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Der Simonian and Laird’s random-effects model [16]. The heterogeneity of the pooled results was assessed using I2 and was considered significant if I2 ≥ 50%. If the heterogeneity was significant, a subgroup analysis was conducted [17]. When the number of included studies on a specific outcome measure is fewer than 2, or when the outcome data are measured using inconsistent methods, no meta-analysis will be performed, and the results are presented as a ‘narrative summary’. ClinicalTrials.gov was searched for unreported or partly reported studies. Review Manager 5.4.1 was used for data synthesis, and p < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Certainty assessment

The grade approach was applied to evaluate the certainty of evidence. Five main domains (study limitations, directness, consistency, precision and reporting bias) and three additional domains for observational studies (dose‒response association, plausible confounding, and strength of association) were considered. The results were classified into four levels: high, moderate, low and very low [18].

Results

Study selection, characteristics, and risk of bias of included studies

A total of 584 studies were retrieved after the online search, and two studies were obtained after reading the reference list. Among the 585 studies, 133 were excluded because of duplication. The title and abstract of the remaining 452 studies were screened according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and 47 studies remained. After full-text assessment, 38 studies were excluded due to (1) failure to report outcomes of babies with cephalic presentation or those undergoing selected cesarean sections; (2) inclusion of external cephalic version, (3) inclusion of maternal complications like preeclampsia and diabetes). Ultimately, 5 studies were included for further systematic review and meta-analysis [8, 19–22], including 4 cohort studies and 1 cross-sectional study. (Fig. 1).

Apgar score

Apgar scores were available in all five studies [8, 19–22], among which two [20, 21] described the number of neonates with low Apgar scores (less than or equal to 7 or 9 at 5 min and less than 7 at 1 min) and three [8, 19, 22] described the Apgar scores at 1 min and 5 min. Both studies [20, 21] that reported neonatal numbers indicated no differences in Apgar scores less than 7 at 5 min between the breech and cephalic groups, while there were more neonates who scored less than 7 at 1 min in the breech group than in the cephalic group (p = 0.0046 and p = 0.0029). There were no differences between the breech and cephalic groups according to the mean ± SD/SE scores at 1 min and 5 min in the other three studies (Table 3) [8, 19, 22].

The meta-analysis for two studies [20, 21] reporting the number of neonates with low Apgar scores < 7 at 1 and 5 min showed no statistical differences between breech and cephalic groups (RR 1.56, 95% CI 0.82–2.54, p = 0.14). However, the pooled incidence of 1-minute Apgar scores less than or equal to 7 indicated that infants born after breech presentation were statistically more likely to have low Apgar scores at 1 min than those born after cephalic presentation (RR 1.82, 95% CI 1.30–2.45, p = 0.0004) (Fig. 3). The heterogeneity results showed that I²=47%, indicating the presence of mild heterogeneity. Meanwhile, the P value of the Q test was 0.15 (P > 0.1), suggesting that the heterogeneity was not statistically significant. Taken together, the degree of variation among the included studies was small, and such variation might be attributed to random factors, with limited impact of heterogeneity on the pooled results.

Other short-term and long-term outcomes

The five included studies also reported other long-term and short-term outcomes, such as birth weight, head circumference, brachial plexus injury, neurological morbidity, trauma, umbilical arterial/venous PH and so on. Because the number of studies reporting the results or the form in which outcomes were reported varied, we only summarized them in tables (supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Five studies reported birth weight [8, 19–22]. Three of the five studies [19, 21, 22] found breech fetuses were lighter than cephalic fetuses (p < 0.01), while two [8, 20] were not statistically significant. Three studies [19, 20, 22] reported umbilical arterial or venous PH, and no difference was found. Ulander VM et al. [21]reported a lower incidence of respiratory problems in the breech presentation group than in the cephalic presentation group (p < 0.001). Ochshorn Y et al. [22] reported that the breech group had a lower umbilical coiling index (p < 0.0001). Tshorny M [8]found bone SOS(bone speed of sound) at birth in breech group was less than in the cephalic group. Other outcomes such as brachial plexus injury, neurological morbidity, trauma, intensive care, incubation, intracranial hemorrhage, hypoxia, decrease of consciousness, knee-sole length and base excess were not different between the two groups.

Discussion

Our study systematically reviewed 4 cohort studies and 1 cross-sectional study and compared neonatal outcomes of breech presentation and cephalic presentation after planned CS. Meta-analyses were performed based on neonatal and infant mortality and 5-min and 1-min Apgar scores ≦ 7. No statistical differences were found in infant and neonatal mortality between the two groups, while more breech presentation infants were found to have 1-min Apgar scores ≦ 7 than the cephalic presentation infants.

The Apgar score might be related to subsequent neurological development. One study found that a low 5-minute Apgar score (< 7) for term neonates might be a marker of higher risk for long-term and short-term neurological morbidities, such as autism, cerebral palsy, epilepsy and movement disorders [23]. Another study showed that low Apgar scores (0–3) at 1 and 5 min were associated with cerebral palsy, epilepsy and intellectual disability by four years old [24].

There are multiple factors contributing to a low Apgar score. However, according to our study, breech presentation itself does not appear to be a factor influencing the Apgar score. Although other outcomes, such as lower fetal weight, lower umbilical cord index, and reduced bone SOS, may be associated with breech fetuses, the survival rate and Apgar score of breech fetuses do not seem to be influenced. This could be attributed to the fact that infant survival rate and Apgar score are short-term outcomes, whereas factors such as weight, umbilical cord indices, and bone SOS may exert significant impacts on the long-term outcomes of the fetus [25–27].

There’s also other evidence supporting that infants in breech position don’t have worse outcomes. For example, one study suggested that early light sensation mediated by melanopsin through the ipRGC-SON-PVN oxytocin neuron circuit could promote cortical synaptogenesis and improve long-term learning ability in mice [28].Besides, breech presentation may avoid the adverse effect of low head position in utero under the force of microgravity, which may lead to orthostatic intolerance or intracranial hypertension [11, 29]. In summary, although breech position has an adverse effect on some pregnancy outcomes, it does not adversely affect most pregnancy outcomes, such as survival rate, Apgar score, neurocognitive system development and so on. Therefore, the benefits of breech presentation should be weighed against the risks, and related guidelines about correcting breech presentation should be carefully re-evaluated.

Strengths and limitations

The advantages of this study are that few studies have systematically reviewed the differences between breech and cephalic presentation and discussed the effect of breech presentation on neuromotor development compared with cephalic presentation. However, there are still some limitations: (1) two of the five eligible trials were retrospective cohort studies; (2) the numbers of included studies and patients are small; (3) the grade of the evidence is low. Therefore, more high-quality data and research are needed.

Conclusion

The influence of breech presentation on fetal development is complex, and knowledge of the influence and mechanisms is limited. By performing a systematic review of the differences between breech and cephalic presentation, this study found that cephalic presentation did not have an absolute advantage over breech presentation in multiple aspects of fetal development. Considering that the breech position does not adversely affect many perinatal outcomes, further investigations on the regulatory mechanisms of fetal development by fetal presentation are warranted.
