PDF gene family in four Cucurbitaceae species: genome-wide identification, classification and expression in response to biotic stress
Authors: Ba Dun, Sun Chengzhen, Ding Zhuo, Cui Haonan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlant defensins (PDF) represent a crucial superfamily of innate immune proteins that exhibit antifungal and antibacterial activities, significantly contributing to biotic stress. Despite being significant horticultural and economic crops, a comprehensive genome-wide analysis of Cucurbitaceae plant defensin genes (CuPDF) remains unreported.ResultsIn this study, 19 CuPDF family members homologous to Arabidopsis PDF were predicted in four Cucurbitaceae and validated based on the presence of conserved PDF-related domains. The 19 CuPDF are unevenly distributed across the chromosomes, and phylogenetic analysis reveals their classification into two distinct subgroups, consistent with those identified in Arabidopsis thaliana. The similarity in 3D protein structures, together with the conserved intron-exon organization and core motif distribution, collectively indicates strong evolutionary conservation. Synteny analysis suggests that the expansion of the CuPDF family in cucurbits is predominantly driven by tandem duplication events. Promoter analysis revealed that the upstream regions of CuPDF are enriched with numerous cis-acting elements associated with plant hormone signaling, defense responses, stress responses, and developmental regulation, including MYB (59 elements), MYC (58), ARE (37), ABRE (40), ERE (40), and AAGAA-motif (36). RNA-seq analysis showed that the expression levels of CmPDF9, ClPDF1.1/1.2/2, and CmoPDF20 were differentially upregulated or downregulated in response to various biotic stresses, including Phytophthora capsici, Fusarium oxysporum, Pseudoperonospora cubensis, Podosphaera xanthii, cucumber green mottle mosaic virus (CGMMV), and Meloidogyne incognita. These findings suggest that these genes may play regulatory roles in biotic stress responses, as further confirmed by RT-qPCR.ConclusionsIn this study, the structure, chromosomal distribution, and evolutionary relationship of PDF family members in four Cucurbitaceae were analyzed. We identified the CuPDF genes involved in biotic stress by combining promoter element analysis, transcriptome data, and RT-qPCR analysis. These findings enrich the knowledge of the molecular characteristics of the CuPDF family and provide crucial insights into their potential roles in responses to biotic stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07216-z.

Introduction

Cucurbitaceae account for 2.6% of the total global cultivated area of vegetable crops and are among the most genetically diverse crops worldwide [1]. From 2019 to 2023, China’s major Cucurbitaceae crops exhibited clear shifts in both harvested area and production [2]. The harvested area of watermelon slightly declined from 1,539,370 to 1,488,028 hectares, whereas total production increased from approximately 60.85 million to 63.87 million tonnes. For melons, including cantaloupes and related types, the harvested area increased from 383,712 to 392,183 hectares, with production rising from approximately 13.55 million to 14.46 million tonnes. In contrast, pumpkins, squash, and gourds experienced a minor decline in area from 401,559 to 398,664 hectares, accompanied by a slight decrease in production, from approximately 7.43 million to 7.37 million tonnes. Cucumbers and gherkins showed increases in both area and production, expanding from 1,258,370 to 1,324,706 hectares and rising from approximately 70.33 million to 80.18 million tonnes (http://faostat.fao.org/).

Although Cucurbitaceae crops have experienced significant growth in cultivation and economic importance, their productivity remains threatened by a wide range of abiotic and biotic stresses. The primary biotic stressors include attacks by pests, fungi, and viruses, whereas abiotic stresses are triggered by environmental conditions such as extreme temperatures, salinity, drought, nutrient deficiencies, and heavy metal toxicity. These stresses have become major limiting factors for sustainable crop production and highlight the need for improved stress resistance. To survive under abiotic and biotic stress environments, higher plants have gradually evolved sophisticated self-defence mechanisms to cope with to cope with these adversities. Defence mechanisms are broadly categorised into preformed and induced responses, which are often triggered by pathogen attack [3, 4]. The preformed defence systems include plant cell walls, a rigid cellulose-based structure that supports and is the first barrier against invading pathogens. The induced biochemical defence responses involve synthesising defence-related proteins and producing a diverse range of molecules that can inhibit the growth of microbial pathogens [5–9].

Within defence-related proteins, plant defensins (PDF) represent a class of cysteine-rich, low-molecular-weight antimicrobial peptides broadly found in plants. They are generally defined as essential peptides with a characteristic three-dimensional folding pattern that is stabilised by eight disulfide-linked cysteines and are postulated to be evolutionarily ancient elements of innate immunity [5]. PDF are small, globular, essential, cysteine-rich proteins. They have a globular three-dimensional structure that is believed to be stabilised into a compact shape by four disulfide bonds and share the CSɑβ motif that is composed of an ɑ-helix and a triple-stranded anti-parallel β-sheet [10, 11]. Despite their highly conserved structure, PDF exhibits substantial diversity at the amino acid sequence level [5, 11]. This diversity might account for the diverse biological roles of PDF [12].

As an important component of the innate immune system, members of the PDF family are considered to have diverse roles, showing constitutive expression in tissues such as seeds [13], and being induced by various abiotic and biotic stresses including cold [14], drought [15], heavy metals [16], or microbial pathogens [17, 18]. In terms of biotic stress, research indicates that PDF possesses potent and broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties. With a few exceptions where antimicrobial activity is absent [19], they effectively inhibit the growth of multiple plant pathogenic fungi, such as Fusarium oxysporum, Verticillium dahliae, and Botrytis cinerea, at extremely low concentrations in vitro [19]. Their antibacterial activity is generally mediated through alterations in membrane potential and permeability, disruption of plasma membrane integrity, or inhibition of bacterial growth [20]. Li et al. found that the expression of RsAFP2, a defensin derived from Raphanus sativus, improved wheat resistance against Fusarium and Rhizoctonia cerealis [21]. Tobacco defensin NaD1 is capable of penetrating the cytoplasm of Fusarium oxysporum hyphae, causing cytoplasmic granulation and ultimately leading to cell death [22]. Heterologous expression of MtDEF4.2 enhanced leaf rust resistance in transgenic wheat (Triticum aestivum) [23]. Transgenic tobacco plants overexpressing the pepper defensin gene (J1-1) and the maize defensin gene showed enhanced resistance to Phytophthora parasitica [24] and Phytophthora nicotianae [25], respectively. Overexpression of AtPDF1.1 enhances Arabidopsis resistance to Alternaria brassicicola [26]. AtPDF1.2 is commonly used as a marker gene for studying defence responses mediated by ethylene- and jasmonic-dependent signalling pathways [27]. Sels et al. purified AtPDF1.3 and confirmed its antifungal activity in vitro, with subsequent studies revealing that its expression pattern closely mirrors that of AtPDF1.2a [28]. Under abiotic stresses, research indicates that CADEF1 mRNA accumulates markedly in pepper leaves subjected to mechanical injury, high salinity, and drought, suggesting that various abiotic and environmental stimuli can induce CADEF1 expression [14]. Tad1 is induced specifically during cold acclimation in winter wheat, producing a defensin-like peptide with antimicrobial activity, suggesting a critical role in cold-triggered pathogen resistance [15]. In addition, certain PDF have been demonstrated to develop metal tolerance through evolution. Mirouze et al. reported that overexpression of AhPDF1.1 in the zinc-hyperaccumulator Arabidopsis thaliana enhanced tolerance to excessive zinc [16]. It has been found that PDF1.2 is markedly induced in Arabidopsis under cadmium stress [29, 30]. According to Luo et al., overexpression of AtPDF2.6 reduces cytoplasmic chromium ion content via chelation, thereby enhancing cadmium tolerance in Arabidopsis [31].

In conclusion, PDF is known to function importantly in stress responses in many species, yet studies in Cucurbitaceae are still relatively scarce. At present, there are no reports on the functional characterization of PDF family members in Cucurbitaceae species (e.g., cucumber, watermelon, melon, pumpkin) in response to biotic and abiotic stresses. Furthermore, with the adaptive evolution of specific pathogen populations and the increasing threat of global climate change to Cucurbitaceae crop growth and development, it is especially important to understand the roles of the defensin protein family in biotic and abiotic stress responses.

In this study, we performed a genome-wide analysis of four Cucurbitaceae species: melon (Cucumis melo), watermelon (Citrullus lanatus), cucumber (Cucumis sativus), and pumpkin (Cucurbita moschata), identifying 19 plant defensin genes (CuPDF). Their physicochemical properties, phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, gene duplications, and cis-regulatory elements were systematically analysed. The expression pattern analysis of CuPDF under abiotic stresses (e.g., salt, drought, cold, osmotic stress) and biotic stresses (e.g., Podosphaera xanthii, Fusarium oxysporum) revealed that most CuPDF are responsive to both stress types. Additionally, specific primers were employed in real-time RT-qPCR to validate the expression data. Overall, the results of this research provide theoretical groundwork for a better understanding of the functions of the CuPDF family and identify candidate genes responsive to biotic and abiotic stresses, offering molecular resources for developing new stress-resistant crop varieties.

Materials and methods

Identification of PDF in Cucurbitaceae

The protein sequences of the PDF family in Arabidopsis thaliana were retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and used as query sequences to identify homologous genes in Cucurbitaceae species. Genome and annotation files of watermelon (Citrullus lanatus subsp. vulgaris cv. 97103), cucumber (Cucumis sativus L. var. sativus cv. Chinese Long), melon (Cucumis melo cv. DHL92), and pumpkin (Cucurbita moschata var. Rifu) were downloaded from the Cucurbit Genomics Database (CuGenDB, http://cucurbitgenomics.org/v2/). Preliminary identification of PDF in Cucurbitaceae was performed by combining Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiles with BLASTP alignment using Arabidopsis-derived protein sequences as queries. The initially identified CuPDF was further validated using the NCBI CDD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/), SMART (http://smart.embl.de/), and Pfam (http://pfam.xfam.org/) databases to verify the presence of PDF-related domains. Sequences lacking confirmed PDF-specific domains were excluded, and the remaining were retained as definitive members for the CuPDF family. All information on CuPDF family members, including gene locus tags, chromosome numbers, exon counts, protein lengths, and start and end positions, was extracted from annotation files using Perl scripts. Additionally, the protein sequences of all family members were analyzed using the ExPASy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/) to determine physicochemical properties, including molecular weight, instability index, stability characteristics, isoelectric point, and aliphatic index. Meanwhile, the subcellular localization of CuPDF protein sequences was predicted using WoLF PSORT (https://www.genscript.com/wolf-psort.html).

The conserved gene structure and motifs prediction

Graphical visualization of gene structures provides critical insights into conserved exon-intron architectures, aiding in comparative and evolutionary analyses [32]. Accordingly, custom Perl scripts were utilized to extract exon-intron structure data for CuPDF from genome annotation files, enabling detailed comparative analysis. The analysis results were then visualized using TBtools [33]. At the same time, conserved motifs were analyzed using Multiple Expectation Maximization for Motif Elicitation (MEME, http://meme-suite.org/). The parameters were set to classic mode, permitting any number of repeats with the minimum number of motifs set to 10; all other settings remained at their default values. The results of conserved motif and gene structure analyses were visualized alongside the phylogenetic tree to reflect conserved motifs and gene organization among subgroups.

Phylogenetic analysis of CuPDF family

PDF protein sequences from Arabidopsis and Cucurbitaceae species were aligned using the CLUSTAL tool in MEGA11, and the alignment results were processed in PERL to remove unnecessary gaps. The trimmed alignment was then imported into MEGA11 to construct a phylogenetic tree using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method, with all parameters at default except for the bootstrap value set to 1000. Finally, the resulting plot was refined and visualized using Chiplot (https://www.chiplot.online/).

Chromosomal localization and evolutionary history analysis

The chromosomal positions of CuPDF were obtained from the genome annotation files downloaded from the Cucurbit Genomics Database (CuGenDB, http://cucurbitgenomics.org/v2/). Duplicate genes among CuPDF were identified using the MCscan tool. The duplicated gene pairs were analyzed for selection pressure using TBtools by calculating the nucleotide substitution parameters Ka (nonsynonymous substitution rate) and Ks (synonymous substitution rate), which were then used to estimate the duplication times of each gene pair. This information, along with the gene coordinates, was subsequently input into TBtools software to generate Circos plots of duplicated genes and gene distribution maps.

Promoter analysis of CuPDF

The promoter regions of CuPDF family members were analyzed to identify potential cis-regulatory elements. For this purpose, TBtools was used to extract the 2 kb upstream sequences of each CuPDF, and the FASTA-format sequences were submitted to the PlantCARE database (http://www.bioinformatics.psb.agent) to identify cis-regulatory elements (CREs). All identified cis-elements were visualized using TBtools, and their detailed information was compiled into a table.

RNA-sequence-based expression analysis of CuPDF family

This study utilized publicly available RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from the NCBI-SRA database to analyze the expression patterns of the CuPDF family under different tissues, biotic stresses, and abiotic stresses. The accession numbers for the RNA-seq datasets utilized in this study are detailed in the “Data Availability Statement”. The analyzed tissues included shoot apex, hypocotyl, stem, root, cotyledon, leaf, epicarp, fruit flesh, anther, and seed; Abiotic stress treatments included low temperature stress, cold stress, high temperature stress, salt stress, flooding stress, osmotic stress, and drought stress; Biotic stresses included Phytophthora capsici, Fusarium oxysporum, Podosphaera xanthii, Fusarium wilt, Meloidogyne incognita, Pseudoperonospora cubensis, and green mottle mosaic virus. Finally, the expression patterns of CuPDF family genes under these conditions were visualized as a heatmap using TBtools.

Plant materials and real-time RT-qPCR-based expression analysis

Seedlings of watermelon (Citrullus lanatus subsp. vulgaris), cucumber (Cucumis sativus var. sativus), melon (Cucumis melo), and pumpkin (Cucurbita moschata) accessions (Cl-1, CD26-5, PI124112, and Cmo-12) were sown in small plastic pots and cultivated under controlled conditions (25 °C; 16 h light/8 h dark) in a growth chamber. Fifteen plants from each accession were cultivated in soil pre-inoculated with a Fusarium oxysporum conidial suspension (1 × 10⁶ spores/mL). Root tissues were collected at 0, 12, 24, 72, and 168 h post-inoculation. Each time point included three biological replicates, and all samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), followed by genomic DNA removal. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using gene-specific primers and the SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II kit (Takara, Japan) on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Actin genes served as internal controls: CmActin (MELO3C023264) for melon, ClActin (Cla97C02G026960) for watermelon, CsActin (CsaV3_2G018090) for cucumber, and CmoActin (CmoCh07G010370) for pumpkin. All RT-qPCR reactions were conducted in three independent biological replicates.

Secondary structure and three-dimensional (3D) protein structure prediction

To investigate the structural features of the CuPDF protein, its amino acid sequence was first subjected to secondary structure prediction using the SOPMA (Self-Optimized Prediction Method with Alignment) online tool Upon uploading the FASTA-formatted sequence, SOPMA applies multiple sequence alignment and statistical optimization algorithms to estimate the proportion and distribution of secondary structural elements, including α-helices, β-strands, turns, and random coils. Subsequently, the CuPDF protein sequence was submitted to the SWISS-MODEL online homology modeling platform (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/interactive). The platform identifies homologous structural templates using BLAST and HHblits, and automatically constructs three-dimensional models, which were then downloaded in PDB format and visualized using PyMOL.

Results

Genome-wide identification of PDF family members in melon, watermelon, cucumber, and pumpkin

Based on the identification method described above and using the known PDF family members in Arabidopsis thaliana as queries, we identified members of the PDF gene family in melon (Cucumis melo), watermelon (Citrullus lanatus), cucumber (Cucumis sativus), and pumpkin (Cucurbita moschata). A total of 19 CuPDF family members were identified across these Cucurbitaceae species. The basic physicochemical properties of these genes, including gene ID, chromosomal location, molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point (pI), instability index, and predicted subcellular localization, are summarized in Table S1.

The lengths of the protein sequences predominantly fell within the range of 62 to 118 amino acids. The predicted isoelectric points (pI) spanned from 5.12 to 9.98, with approximately 79% of CuPDF proteins exhibiting pI values above 7, thereby suggesting an overall acidic nature. The molecular weights of the CuPDF proteins ranged from 6,491.4 to 12,822.6 Daltons. In addition, all 19 CuPDF proteins exhibited positive grand average hydropathicity (GRAVY) scores, indicating a uniformly hydrophobic character across the group.

Subcellular localization predictions indicated that most CuPDF family members are likely to be secreted extracellularly. However, several exceptions were noted: CsPDF1.3 was predicted to be localized in the cytoplasm, ClPDF3.2 in the nucleus, and CmPDF2 in the chloroplast. These findings may suggest potential functional divergence among CuPDF family members.

Conserved motifs and gene structures of CuPDF

A systematic analysis of the exon-intron structures of CuPDF was conducted using genome sequences and their corresponding annotation files, with the results presented in Fig. 1C. Most members contained one to two coding sequences (CDS) regions. Notably, seven genes lacked untranslated regions (UTRs) but retained CDSs. Except for CsPDF1.3, which harbors a single CDS, the majority of genes, such as CsPDF1.1, CsPDF1.4, ClPDF1.1, and ClPDF3.2, exhibited a two-CDS structure. Overall, exon-intron architectures were highly conserved within sister clades, indicating a shared ancestral origin and close evolutionary relationships (Figs. 1A, B). Nevertheless, structural variations observed in certain clades may reflect functional divergence or differences in selective pressure, suggesting that some CuPDF may have acquired novel biological functions during adaptation.

To further analyze CuPDF proteins, MEME software was used to identify conserved motifs across 19 sequences, with a total of ten motifs detected (Figure S1). Based on phylogenetic relationships and motif distribution, the 19 CuPDF were divided into two major subgroups (Fig. 1B), among which subgroup I (orange labels) showed the highest structural conservation. Most members of this group, except for ClPDF3.2, contain Motifs 2, 3, and 5. CsPDF1.1, CsPDF1.2, CsPDF1.4, and CsPDF1.5 specifically contain Motifs 7 and 8, while ClPDF3.1 contains Motif 7. Notably, CmPDF2 exclusively contains Motifs 9 and 10, which are absent from all other CuPDF members, suggesting that it may participate in biological processes distinct from those of other family members. The consistency in motif arrangement reflects gene similarity, whereas differences in motif composition may correspond to functional diversity [34].

Phylogenetic analysis of CuPDF

To gain deeper insights into the evolutionary relationships of the CuPDF family, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using 57 defensin protein sequences from five species: Arabidopsis thaliana, Cucumis melo, Citrullus lanatus, Cucumis sativus, and Cucurbita moschata (Fig. 2).

The results revealed that these genes clustered into two major groups based on their evolutionary trajectories, with an uneven distribution across species. Group I consisted of six sequences—one each from melon and watermelon, two from cucumber, and two from Arabidopsis. Group II contained the remaining 51 sequences, including 36 from Arabidopsis, two from melon, three from cucumber, and five each from watermelon and pumpkin. Most defensin genes were assigned to Group II and exhibited high bootstrap values, indicating close phylogenetic relationships and suggesting divergence from a common ancestor. Notably, CmPDF2 occupied a distinct position within the phylogenetic tree, characterized by the longest branch length. This unusual placement may be attributable to species-specific events such as gene duplication, loss, or sequence divergence. These evolutionary processes likely led to structural or sequence alterations, consequently influencing its phylogenetic placement. However, motif analysis (Fig. 1) revealed that CmPDF3 shares a similar motif composition with ClPDF3.1, supporting their classification within the same group.

Chromosomal localisation and gene duplication and collinearity of CuPDF

Using the latest version of the genome sequence and the coordinate information of all CuPDFs, the physical locations of these genes were determined (Fig. 3). The results showed that defensin genes are randomly distributed alone or in clusters on the different melon, watermelon, cucumber, and pumpkin chromosomes.

CuPDF was distributed on three chromosomes in melon, four in watermelon, one in cucumber, and four in pumpkin. A total of nineteen Cucurbitaceae members of the defensin protein family were localized on 12 chromosomes. The details indicate the presence of one CuPDF on chromosomes 2, 9, and 11 of melon, chromosomes 1, 2, 3, and 9 of watermelon, chromosome 1 of cucumber, and chromosomes 0, 4, 15, and 20 of pumpkin. The other chromosomes had at least two defensin genes. Remarkably, the defensin genes of cucumber were only observed in chromosome 1, which showed the most genes, with a maximum of 5 counts.

Plants exhibit a greater capacity to adapt to their environments owing to extensive segmental duplication and the amplification of tandem repeats within gene families [35]. An intraspecies gene duplication analysis was performed to study the evolutionary and functional diversity of CuPDF. The results identified four tandem duplication clusters within the CuPDF family: specifically, clusters comprising CsPDF1.4 and CsPDF1.5, CmoPDF4.1 and CmoPDF4.2, ClPDF1.1 and ClPDF1.2, along with ClPPDF3.1 and ClPDF3.2. A nonsegmental duplication cluster was also detected (Fig. 4 and Table S2). The analysis revealed four tandem duplication events, suggesting that fragment repeat events are a key driver of gene diversity [36].

To gain a deeper understanding of the evolutionary origins of the CuPDF, we conducted an interspecies covariance analysis among melon, watermelon, cucumber, and pumpkin (Fig. 4), which provided further insights into the evolutionary dynamics and functional divergence of the CuPDF family. We detected eight segmental duplications within 19 genes, including one pair in watermelon and cucumber, three pairs in watermelon and pumpkin, two pairs in watermelon and melon, one pair in melon and cucumber, and one pair in melon and pumpkin (Fig. 4 and Table S2). These genes may have the same function and likely originate from a common ancestor. Further analysis incorporating the previous phylogenetic tree was conducted. The fragment replication genes ClPDF2, CmPDF12, CmPDF12, and CmoPDF20 belong to the same subgroup, and the evolutionary branches are very close. We hypothesize that the replication events occurred late and functionally undifferentiated [37].

The Ka/Ks values of all the syntenic gene pairs were less than 1, indicating they might have experienced purifying selection. We also determined the duplicate divergence time by calculating the Ka/Ks ratios (Table S2). Tandem duplication events occurred approximately 236 to 684 million years ago, while segmental duplication events occurred between 104 and 464 million years ago. Comparatively, tandem duplication events occurred earlier, particularly duplications CsPDF1.4 and CsPDF1.5, which came from more ancient events.

Cis-acting regulatory elements in the promoter region of CuPDF

To further elucidate the potential regulatory mechanisms of the CuPDF gene family, we analyzed the cis-regulatory elements within the 2 kb upstream promoter regions using the PlantCARE database (Fig. 5). In total, 88 distinct types of cis-elements were identified. These elements were broadly categorized into four major functional groups: light-responsive elements, hormone-responsive elements, stress-responsive elements (including those involved in defense), and development-related elements, such as seed-specific motifs.

Among the light-responsive elements, GT1-motif (total 32, most abundant in CmoPDF4.2), G-box (total 32, enriched in CmoPDF4.1), Box 4 (total 65, highest in CmPDF11), TCT-motif (total 19), and GATA-motif (total 10) were the most prominent. These findings suggest that CuPDF genes may be transcriptionally regulated by light and participate in photomorphogenesis and light-adaptive physiological responses. For stress- and defense-related regulation, STRE (total 24), WUN-motif (total 21), DRE core (total 9), MYB (total 59, particularly enriched in CsPDF1.3 and CsPDF1.4), MYC (total 58, highest in ClPDF1.1), LTR (total 7), ARE (total 37), and TC-rich repeats (total 12) were commonly detected. Notably, MYB and MYC elements were present across all CuPDF genes and were especially enriched in CmPDF2, ClPDF1.1, ClPDF3.1/3.2, CsPDF1.1/1.3/1.4, and CmoPDF0/20, indicating that these transcription factor-binding sites may represent a conserved regulatory module within the CuPDF family. Hormone-responsive elements included ABRE (total 40, with CmoPDF20 harboring the highest number), GARE-motif (total 5), TCA-element (total 14), TGACG-motif (total 28, most abundant in CmoPDF4.1), CGTCA-motif (total 28, also enriched in CmoPDF4.1), ERE (total 40, especially present in ClPDF1.2 and CmoPDF20), and as-1 (total 28, again most enriched in CmoPDF4.1). Additionally, some CuPDF genes, such as ClPDF1.2 and ClPDF9, contained development-associated cis-elements including CAT-box (total 6), AAGAA-motif (total 36, predominantly in ClPDF9), O2-site (total 6), and RY-element (total 2). These elements may contribute to the involvement of these genes in plant developmental regulation and environmental adaptability.

Expression profiles of CuPDF under biotic stress

To investigate the functional roles of the CuPDF family in plant responses to biotic stress, we performed a comprehensive expression analysis based on publicly available transcriptomic datasets from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) (Fig. 6). This study covered a range of pathogenic challenges, including Phytophthora capsici, Fusarium oxysporum, Podosphaera xanthii, cucumber green mottle mosaic virus, Meloidogyne incognita, and Pseudoperonospora cubensis. The results revealed divergent expression profiles of CuPDF under different stress conditions, suggesting that this family plays diverse and extensive roles in plant defense regulation.

In melon, upon infection with Phytophthora capsici (Fig. 6A), CmPDF2 expression was undetectable. CmPDF9 was downregulated in the resistant line, becoming completely suppressed by day 3. In the susceptible line, CmPDF9 expression declined sharply before increasing again to a level comparable to that at 0 h. CmPDF11 was significantly upregulated in the resistant line (3.6-fold relative to baseline), whereas it was downregulated to undetectable levels in the susceptible line. Following inoculation with Fusarium oxysporum (Fig. 6B), CmPDF2 remained expressed at extremely low levels with no evident change. In contrast, CmPDF9 was strongly induced in both resistant and susceptible lines, peaking at 12 h with fold-changes of 54 and 312, respectively. CmPDF11 was suppressed to undetectable levels in the resistant line and downregulated 2.9-fold in the susceptible line. Upon Podosphaera xanthii infection (Fig. 6C), CmPDF2 and CmPDF9 remained barely expressed across all time points. In contrast, CmPDF11 exhibited dynamic fluctuations in the resistant line, with a sharp downregulation at 72 h (22.7-fold) followed by a strong upregulation at 168 h (5.2-fold). In the susceptible line, CmPDF11 showed high expression at 0 h, decreased 47-fold by 24 h, and slightly recovered at 72 and 168 h.

In watermelon, after inoculation with Fusarium oxysporum under continuous monoculture and crop rotation systems (Fig. 6D), ClPDF1.1 and ClPDF1.2 were prominently expressed under both treatments. ClPDF1.1 exhibited the highest expression under continuous monoculture (FPKM: 1525.28), while ClPDF1.2 showed substantial expression under crop rotation (FPKM: 624.72). ClPDF2 was markedly induced only under rotation (FPKM: 998.52). ClPDF3.1, ClPDF3.2, and ClPDF9 were undetectable or minimally expressed. At day 7 post-inoculation (Fig. 6E), ClPDF1.1, ClPDF1.2, and ClPDF2 were significantly expressed in both resistant and susceptible genotypes, with generally higher expression in the susceptible line. ClPDF3.1, ClPDF3.2, and ClPDF9 remained undetectable or extremely low. In leaves infected with cucumber green mottle mosaic virus (CGMMV) (Fig. 6F), ClPDF1.1 was strongly upregulated (5.6-fold), and ClPDF1.2 and ClPDF2 showed moderate upregulation (1.4-fold). ClPDF3.1, ClPDF3.2, and ClPDF9 were not detected. In fruit tissues post-CGMMV infection (Fig. 6G), ClPDF1.1 and ClPDF1.2 were downregulated 1.3-fold and 2.8-fold, respectively, whereas ClPDF2 showed a slight increase. The other three genes remained undetectable.

In cucumber, CsPDF1.1 and CsPDF1.4 remained low and stable across treatments. Upon infection with Pseudoperonospora cubensis (Fig. 6H), CsPDF1.2, CsPDF1.3, and CsPDF1.5 were strongly and persistently induced in the susceptible line, peaking at day 6 (85-fold upregulation). In the resistant line, these genes also responded but peaked earlier (day 3–4) and showed lower expression, followed by a marked decline by day 6. Upon Fusarium oxysporum inoculation (Fig. 6J), the same three genes were downregulated at 0, 24, and 48 h, then slightly upregulated at 192 h. After Meloidogyne incognita infection (Fig. 6K), the susceptible line exhibited transient induction followed by suppression of CsPDF1.2, CsPDF1.3, and CsPDF1.5, whereas these genes remained stably expressed at low levels in the resistant line. Upon infection with Podosphaera xanthii (Fig. 6L), weak expression and downregulation of these genes were observed in the susceptible line, while they were not detected in the resistant line.

In pumpkin (Fig. 6M), under control conditions (non-inoculated), CmoPDF4.1 was highly and stably expressed. In contrast, CmoPDF0, CmoPDF4.2, CmoPDF15, and CmoPDF20 were expressed at lower levels and exhibited considerable fluctuations at 24 h. Notably, CmoPDF0 decreased to undetectable levels, while CmoPDF4.2, CmoPDF15, and CmoPDF20 were downregulated 7-fold, 23-fold, and 1.95-fold, respectively. At 24 h post-Fusarium oxysporum infection, CmoPDF4.2, CmoPDF15, and CmoPDF20 were upregulated 1.9-fold, 1.02-fold, and 3.5-fold relative to the 24 h control. CmoPDF4.1 was downregulated 2.2-fold.

Collectively, the CuPDF family displayed substantial expression divergence under various biotic stresses. Several genes, such as CmPDF9, CmPDF11, ClPDF1.1, ClPDF1.2, CsPDF1.2, CsPDF1.3, CsPDF1.5, and CmoPDF20, were significantly induced by pathogen infection and exhibited genotype-specific responses, suggesting their critical roles in plant defense.

Expression patterns of CuPDF across different tissues and under abiotic stress conditions

To investigate the potential biological functions of CuPDF genes in plant tissues and under abiotic stress conditions, we analyzed publicly available RNA-seq datasets. The selected tissues included anthers, seeds, cotyledons, hypocotyls, shoot apices, stems, leaves, roots, fruit flesh, and epicarp (Figure S2). In melon (Cucumis melo) (Figure S2A), CmPDF9 was predominantly expressed in the epicarp two weeks post-harvest, whereas CmPDF11 exhibited high transcript levels in seeds soaked for 1–3 days and in the shoot apex. These patterns suggest that CmPDF9 may be involved in fruit development, while CmPDF11 could function in seedling morphogenesis. In watermelon (Citrullus lanatus) (Figure S2B), ClPDF1.1 showed the highest expression in leaves and roots, particularly at 10 days after pollination. ClPDF1.2 was broadly expressed across multiple tissues, with especially high levels in vascular tissues and fruit flesh at 10 days post-pollination. ClPDF2 exhibited its strongest expression in phloem sap, vascular tissues, and the carpopodium at 18 days after pollination. By contrast, CsPDF genes in cucumber (Cucumis sativus) displayed negligible expression across all surveyed tissues (Figure S2C). In pumpkin (Cucurbita moschata) (Figure S2D), both CmoPDF4.1 and CmoPDF4.2 were highly expressed in fruit flesh and roots. Notably, CmoPDF4.1 transcript levels increased progressively during fruit ripening, indicating a potential positive regulatory role in this process. CmoPDF15 was specifically expressed in roots, whereas CmoPDF20 was ubiquitously expressed across all sampled tissues, with markedly elevated levels during early fruit development (10 and 20 days after pollination), suggesting its involvement in early developmental regulation.

Further analysis of CuPDF expression patterns under abiotic stress conditions revealed species-specific regulatory responses (Figure S3). In melon (Cucumis melo), CmPDF transcript levels remained generally low under both cold (Figure S3A) and salt stress (Figure S3B), with only slight inductions observed at certain time points. Under waterlogging conditions (Figure S3C), CmPDF11 expression in the hypocotyl was markedly upregulated by approximately 77-fold, whereas CmPDF2 and CmPDF9 remained weakly expressed. In watermelon (Citrullus lanatus), chilling stress (Figure S3D) induced a pronounced increase in ClPDF1.1 expression (8.6-fold) in the leaves of heterografted plants, while ClPDF1.2 and ClPDF2 were downregulated. In contrast, all three genes exhibited consistently low expression levels in homografted plants under the same conditions. Following 7 h of salt stress (Figure S3E), ClPDF1.1, ClPDF1.2, and ClPDF2 were significantly downregulated in leaf tissues, with ClPDF1.1 exhibiting a 24.1-fold reduction. Under osmotic stress (Figure S3F), ClPDF1.1 expression in roots showed a modest increase, while ClPDF1.2 and ClPDF2 were downregulated by 1.3-fold and 2.8-fold, respectively. In drought-tolerant lines subjected to 4- and 8-day drought treatments (Figure S3G), ClPDF1.1 expression was reduced by 1.6- and 19.7-fold, while ClPDF2 levels declined by 3.3- and 2.9-fold, respectively. Conversely, ClPDF1.2 was upregulated by 1.6- and 4.9-fold under the same conditions. In cucumber (Cucumis sativus) (Figure S3H-K), CsPDF genes showed minimal transcriptional responsiveness to abiotic stimuli, with negligible expression changes across all treatments. In pumpkin (Cucurbita moschata), cold stress (Figure S3L) led to a 3-fold and 1.2-fold reduction in CmoPDF4.1 and CmoPDF20 expression, respectively, in cotyledons. Under salt stress (Figure S3M), both genes were significantly downregulated in fresh root apices, with CmoPDF4.1 and CmoPDF20 reduced by approximately 4-fold and 21-fold, respectively. In response to heat stress (Figure S3N), CmoPDF4.1 expression was progressively upregulated during both acclimation and high-temperature challenge, whereas CmoPDF20 was strongly repressed following exposure to 45 °C.

qRT-PCR-based expression analysis of CuPDF family

Transcriptome analysis revealed that most CuPDF are significantly induced under both biotic and abiotic stresses, with clear expression responses observed across all four Cucurbitaceae species following Fusarium oxysporum infection. F. oxysporum is a widespread soil-borne fungal pathogen that causes vascular wilt in a variety of cucurbit crops and is characterized by strong systemic infectivity. Upon colonization of the xylem, it triggers robust systemic defense responses in host plants, making it an ideal model for investigating the transcriptional regulation of CuPDF under biotic stress.

Based on RNA-seq expression profiles, we conducted qRT-PCR validation of selected CuPDF with strong transcriptional responses. Expression analysis focused on the biotic stress condition induced by F. oxysporum inoculation at 0, 24, 48, 72, and 168 h, targeting nine CuPDF members: CmPDF9, CsPDF1.2/1.3/1.5, ClPDF1.1/1.2/2, and CmoPDF4.1/20 (Fig. 7). The qRT-PCR results closely mirrored the RNA-seq data. CmPDF9 expression was upregulated and peaked at 12 h post-inoculation. CsPDF1.2/1.3/1.5 reached its highest expression at 0 h and declined thereafter. ClPDF1.1, ClPDF1.2, and ClPDF2 exhibited progressively increased expression, reaching maximal levels at 168 h. CmoPDF4.2 was downregulated, while CmoPDF20 was induced. Taken together, the RNA-seq and qRT-PCR data indicate that CmPDF11, ClPDF1.1/1.2/2, and CmoPDF20 are transcriptionally responsive to F. oxysporum, suggesting they may play pivotal roles in defense against biotic stress. These results provide evidence for the potential functions of the CuPDF family, particularly suggesting that CmPDF9, ClPDF1.1/1.2/2, and CmoPDF20 may play roles in responses to various biotic stresses.

Secondary structure and 3D modelling prediction of CuPDF proteins

We employed SOPMA software to predict the secondary structures of CuPDF proteins. The results indicate that these structures primarily comprise α-helices (H), extended strands (E), β-turns (T), and random coils (C), with α-helices, extended strands, and random coils being predominant; β-turns were undetected (Table S2). Except for CmoPDF20, all CuPDF proteins exhibited random coil proportions exceeding 50%, with ClPDF3.2 reaching the highest level at 69.35% (Table S3). Given that protein functionality is closely tied to three-dimensional conformation [38], we conducted homology modeling of CuPDF proteins using the SWISS-MODEL platform and validated the resulting models with the SAVES tool (Figure S4).

Notably, structural heterogeneity was observed even within the same species (Figure S4). In melon, for example, CmPDF10 and CmPDF12 exhibited high structural similarity, whereas CmPDF3 differed substantially, suggesting evolutionary divergence and potential functional differentiation. Structural comparison, aligned with the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2), revealed that ClPDF1.1, ClPDF2, CmoPDF20, CmoPDF4.1, CmoPDF4.2, CmoPDF0, CsPDF1.1, CsPDF1.2, and CsPDF1.4 shared notable structural similarity and were all classified under Subgroup III. However, significant structural variation was also found within this subgroup. For instance, ClPDF3.1 showed limited similarity to CsPDF1.3 and CsPDF1.5, indicating possible mutation events during evolution that may have led to functional divergence.

Discussion

Throughout evolution, plants have progressively developed sophisticated mechanisms to perceive and respond to diverse environmental stimuli, which are essential for their growth, development, and survival. Plant defensins (PDF), a class of small cationic peptides ubiquitously distributed across the plant kingdom, constitute a core component of the innate immune system. Their expression can be induced by a wide range of biotic and abiotic stresses [14–31], and they have also been implicated in regulating various aspects of plant development [39]. These conclusions are corroborated by our transcriptome data derived from different tissues and stress conditions.

Although PDF has been extensively characterized in model and crop species such as Arabidopsis thaliana [40], Musa spp [41]., and Oryza sativa [42], their functional roles remain largely unexplored in Cucurbitaceae crops, including Cucumis sativus (cucumber), Cucumis melo (melon), Citrullus lanatus (watermelon), and Cucurbita moschata (pumpkin). In this study, we identified and characterized 19 CuPDF from the genomes of four Cucurbitaceae species. Among them, CmPDF9, ClPDF1.1/1.2/2, and CmoPDF4.1/20 displayed pronounced responsiveness to both biotic and abiotic stresses, suggesting that they may function as key regulators under adverse conditions.

Phylogenetic analysis indicated that CuPDF exhibits high sequence homology with Arabidopsis PDF and can be classified into two subgroups. The unequal distribution of gene numbers among subgroups may be attributed to more frequent tandem duplication events during evolution. Gene duplication is recognized as a major force driving gene family expansion [43]. In the CuPDF family, four pairs of tandemly duplicated genes were identified, whereas no segmental duplication events were observed, suggesting that tandem duplication has played a predominant role in the expansion and evolution of this family. Although gene duplication increases gene copy number, point mutations in coding or regulatory regions may alter gene functions, thereby contributing to the expression divergence among family members [44, 45]. This observation is consistent with findings in Arabidopsis [46] and Brassica napus [47], where tandem duplication has similarly contributed to the functional diversification of PDF. Furthermore, gene expansion may also result from internal repeats and structural rearrangements common in antimicrobial peptides, which promote beneficial mutations while preserving structural stability [48, 49]. Beyond their roles in innate immunity, the expansion of PDF suggests their potential for functional diversification. Indeed, several peptides derived from duplicated or neofunctionalized PDF has been reported to fulfill novel developmental or reproductive roles independent of defense [50, 51]. Synteny analysis indicated stronger collinearity among CuPDF within Cucurbitaceae species than between Cucurbitaceae and Arabidopsis, consistent with their closer evolutionary relationship. Subcellular localization prediction further revealed that most CuPDF proteins are extracellularly localized, echoing findings from Arabidopsis [52] and Brassica napus [47], where PDF proteins function in the extracellular matrix, potentially mediating intercellular communication and defense signaling.

PDF are widely expressed in multiple plant organs and serve as the first line of defense against pathogen invasion. Seeds are considered the richest source of defensins [53], which can inhibit fungal growth and enhance seedling survival [54]. Hegedüs et al. reported that plant defense responses regulate the growth and development of roots, leaves, stems, buds, pistils, ovules, carpels, stamens, and sepals [55]. Consistent with these findings, our RNA-seq results showed that CuPDF is not only induced under stress but also expressed constitutively in various tissues. For instance, CmPDF11, ClPDF1.1/1.2/2, and CmoPDF20 showed dynamic expression changes during fruit development. CmPDF11 was highly expressed in seeds and shoot apices; ClPDF1.1/1.2 showed strong expression in leaves, roots, shoot tips, fruit pedicels, vascular bundles, and phloem; CmoPDF20 was highly expressed in roots, leaves, and stems. These results suggest that these CuPDF may be involved in the development and differentiation of multiple organs. PDF also participates in plant responses to diverse abiotic stresses, such as heat and cold [56–59]. Sasaki et al. found that wheat defensin TAD1 is cold-inducible, and its overexpression enhances resistance to snow mold and Fusarium head blight [60]. Our findings are consistent with these reports. CmPDF11 expression gradually increased under waterlogging stress. ClPDF1.1 and ClPDF1.2 showed significant expression changes under cold, salt, osmotic, and drought conditions. ClPDF2 was significantly downregulated under osmotic stress, while CmoPDF20 expression was suppressed under cold, salt, and high-temperature stress.

Previous studies have shown that the transcription of PDF is significantly affected by light and stress. For example, a low red/far-red light ratio suppresses the expression of Arabidopsis PDF1.2 [61]; in tomato leaves, Flg22-induced PDF expression depends on photoperiod and ethylene signaling [62]. We identified several light-responsive elements (GT1-motif, G-box, Box 4, TCT-motif, and GATA-motif) in CuPDF promoters, suggesting that their expression may be regulated by light signals. Furthermore, endogenous accumulation of salicylic acid (SA) and ethylene (ET) has been shown to activate PDF1.2 transcription [63, 64]. During defense against necrotrophic pathogens, PDF1.2 can be co-activated with other defense genes through the jasmonic acid (JA) and ethylene signaling pathways [65]. CuPDF promoters harbor numerous cis-elements related to hormone signaling, defense, and stress responses (e.g., TGACG-element, AAGAA-motif, ERE, WUN-motif, MYC, and MYB), suggesting regulation by stress-related hormones. Transcriptomic data further support this, as CmPDF9, ClPDF1.1/1.2/2, and CmoPDF4.1/20 were markedly induced under multiple biotic and abiotic stresses.

PDF proteins exhibit potent, broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity. For instance, overexpression of the tobacco defensin NaD1 significantly enhances resistance to Fusarium wilt in transgenic cotton [66]. Similarly, SmAMPD1 overexpression in banana confers increased resistance to Fusarium oxysporum f.sp. cubense race 1 (Foc1) [67]. Additionally, synthetic PDF peptides derived from Capsicum chinense disrupt fungal cell structures and reduce the pathogenicity of both Fusarium solani and Fusarium oxysporum [68]. Our gene expression analysis corroborates these previous findings. Specifically, CmPDF9/11, ClPDF1.1/1.2/2, CsPDF1.2/1.3/1.5, and CmoPDF4.1/15/20 were significantly upregulated or downregulated under various biotic stress conditions, particularly in response to Fusarium oxysporum infection. These observations suggest that CuPDF may play crucial roles in plant responses to biotic stress, especially in defense against Fusarium wilt.

Taken together, the persistent and robust expression of CmPDF9, ClPDF1.1/1.2/2, and CmoPDF4.1/20 across multiple tissues and stress conditions highlights their potential as key functional genes. Therefore, these six genes represent promising candidates for further genetic and functional analyses aimed at elucidating their roles in plant defense responses.
