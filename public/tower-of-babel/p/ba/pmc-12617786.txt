Addressing maternal cardiovascular risk: The impact of lactation, adverse pregnancy outcomes, and racial disparities☆
Authors: Monica Rodriguez, Michele L McCarroll, Caroline Marrs, Kamala P Tamirisa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPregnancy functions as a cardiopulmonary stress test, revealing underlying cardiovascular disease (CVD) or heightening future CVD risk following adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs). Studies consistently demonstrate strong associations between APOs, higher mortality rates, and increased risk of cardiometabolic diseases in women from racial and ethnic underrepresented groups (UREG). Social determinants of health, particularly in UREGs, contribute to increasing allostatic load, potentially driving poorer pregnancy outcomes. Breastfeeding has a protective effect on allostatic load and is linked to improved maternal cardiometabolic health. In the United States, UREGs have lower rates of breastfeeding initiation, retention, and duration—potentially influenced by both cultural factors and systemic biases within healthcare. Interventions must extend beyond healthcare settings to engage key stakeholders, including community leaders and families, to create sustainable change and reduce disparities. As maternal CVD prevalence rises among UREG populations, a collaborative, multidisciplinary approach to improving breastfeeding rates is essential to improving maternal and infant health outcomes.Keywords: Lactation, Breastfeeding, Cardiovascular disease, Women's health, Adverse pregnancy outcomes, Underrepresented racial and ethnic women

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of maternal mortality and has continued to increase, with rates more than doubling from 1999 to 2022 and disproportionately affecting women of color [1]. The increasing incidence of CVD and persistent disparities highlight the need to identify those at the highest risk [2]. The disparities in CVD result from a complex relationship of biological, social, and structural factors [3,4]. There is growing evidence that supports that adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs) such as preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, preterm birth, and others are not merely complications of pregnancy but can also serve as early markers of a higher risk of future CVD [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Additionally, lactation has demonstrated a potential protective role by reducing long-term CVD risk [5]. However, lactation initiation and duration vary significantly across racial and socioeconomic groups [6,7]. This selective review aims to discuss the current literature on maternal CVD risk, focusing on the impact of APOs, the existing disparities, and the role of lactation.

Adverse pregnancy outcomes and cardiovascular risk

Normal cardiovascular adaptations during pregnancy include increased cardiac output, decreased systemic vascular resistance, and structural remodeling of the heart and vasculature [9]. Pregnancy functions as a cardiopulmonary stress test, unmasking underlying CVD or heightening the future CVD risk following adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs) (Fig. 1) [10,11]. Multiple studies have demonstrated strong associations between APOs, higher mortality rates, women from racial and ethnic underrepresented groups (UREG), and an increased risk of cardiometabolic diseases [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. The Collaborative Perinatal Project found that APOs, including preterm premature rupture of membranes, preterm induced labor, preterm/pre-labor cesarean delivery, gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, eclampsia, and gestational diabetes/impaired fasting glucose are associated with higher CVD risk nearly 50 years post-pregnancy [16,17].

Black women, in particular, have a disproportionately higher burden of coronary artery disease related mortality, with an earlier onset compared to women of other racial and ethnic groups [18]. Black women are at a 3.2-fold increased risk of dying during pregnancy and up to six weeks post-partum compared to White women [19]. These disparities persist even when adjusting for socioeconomic factors and comorbidities, underscoring the urgent need for healthcare strategies tailored to diverse patient populations [20]. Most clinical care models still treat APOs as isolated obstetric events, and not as factors that increase CVD risk identifying those that may need long-term cardiovascular surveillance [14,15].

Lactation and cardiovascular health

The American Academy of Pediatrics, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, and the World Health Organization (WHO) recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months, followed by continued breastfeeding along with the introduction of complementary foods around six months, ideally for up to two years of age or longer [21]. In the United States, Native Hawaiian, Pacific Islander, Alaska Native, Black, and Hispanic women experience the lowest rates of breastfeeding initiation, retention, and duration. These disparities may be influenced by cultural and social factors, as well as systemic biases in healthcare [22,23]. To address these disparities, breastfeeding support should begin with early prenatal visits, education, and community engagement to build self-efficacy in breastfeeding [24]. The Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative, a joint effort by the WHO and United Nations International Children's Emergency Fund since 1991, has been instrumental in supporting breastfeeding through measures such as lactation counseling, immediate skin-to-skin contact, and rooming-in practices [25]. When supported by policymakers, healthcare providers, and communities, women are twice as likely to choose to breastfeed [26].

Lactation has emerged as a modifiable factor that may offer long-term cardiometabolic benefits. Oxytocin, a neuropeptide produced in the hypothalamus during breastfeeding, has been shown to decrease vascular tone, improve metabolic function and reduce inflammation and stress. Increased levels of oxytocin can also have anxiolytic effects [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Lactating women have been found to have more favorable lipid profiles, increased insulin sensitivity, and reduced fasting blood glucose levels [31]. Breastfeeding is also associated with reduced maternal risks of CVD-related hospitalization, and mortality, and lowers the risk of developing type 2 diabetes, and cancer of the breast, ovaries, and endometrium [32,33]. Evidence from multiple studies including the Nurses Health Study and the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) study suggest that those who breastfeed for longer durations were associated with lower rates of metabolic syndrome, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular events [[34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41]]. Breastfeeding is also inversely associated with allostatic load, or the cumulative physiologic effects of chronic stress on the body (Fig. 2) [42,43]. Initiating longer longitudinal studies on mothers who lactate should continue to improve our understanding of lactation's lifelong impact. Addressing the current inconsistencies in defining breastfeeding duration and exposure will facilitate a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between breastfeeding and CVD risk.

Peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM), a rare form of heart failure that can occur late in pregnancy or after childbirth, remains a significant cause of maternal mortality and morbidity, particularly among UREG [44]. Bromocriptine, a medication that inhibits prolactin release, may improve PPCM, but results in the cessation of lactation [45]. Recent studies have shown no evidence that breastfeeding negatively impacts myocardial recovery or exacerbates PPCM [[46], [47], [48]]. An active multicenter randomized trial, the Randomized Evaluation of Bromocriptine in Myocardial Recovery Therapy for Peripartum Cardiomyopathy (REBIRTH), is currently comparing bromocriptine against standard heart failure therapy [49]. Given the evolving body of evidence and varying guidelines, decisions regarding breastfeeding should be made on an individual basis.

Disparities in maternal CVD risk

Maternal CVD risks in UREG populations arise not only from genetic and biological factors but are also deeply rooted in structural racism and social determinants of health (SDOH) [50]. Key SDOH include economic stability, education, access to quality healthcare, social support, transportation, discrimination, and environmental safety. SDOH can significantly contribute to allostatic load by increasing stressors and contributing to dysregulated homeostasis, associated with increased incidence of chronic disease and worse health outcomes, including an increased risk of CVD [51]. Traditional models of pregnancy risk have not adequately accounted for the significant disparities in adverse outcomes experienced by UREG women, as socioeconomic factors alone account for only 21.4 % of the variance in low birthweight and preterm birth complications [52]. Despite rising levels of education and socioeconomic status among women of color, increasing allostatic load due to both acute and chronic discrimination remains a critical factor driving poorer pregnancy outcomes [53]. Experiences of racism have been shown to elevate neuroendocrine, metabolic, cardiovascular, and inflammatory biomarkers leading to dysregulated homeostasis and high allostatic load [54].

Sheehy et al. reported a higher incidence of coronary artery disease among Black women associated with frequent experiences of perceived racism in areas such as employment, housing, interactions with law enforcement, and other daily social aspects of living [55]. The stress associated with exposure to racism in UREG pregnant women can trigger cardiovascular reactivity and an endocrine response overload, potentially leading to APOs and increased CVD events (Fig. 3). Regardless of various efforts and initiatives encouraging healthy behaviors among women of color, systemic issues that place UREG individuals at a significant disadvantage necessitate broader remedies [56]. Research indicates that UREG patients, including newborns, have better patient outcomes when cared for by UREG health professionals [57]. However, only 10 % of doulas, 4.9 % of midwives, 11 % of obstetricians, and 7.9 % of obstetric and gynecology resident physicians in the United States identify as UREG [58,59]. Black doula programs, for instance, can provide vital non-clinical support and advocacy throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period, often improving outcomes for Black mothers and infants. Therefore, creating pathways that increase representation in maternity care is essential.

Conclusion

Maternal CVD remains a significant contributor to pregnancy-related morbidity and mortality. APOs such as hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, gestational diabetes, and preterm birth can serve as early indicators of increased lifelong CVD risk.

Lactation can provide cardioprotective benefits and reduce long-term cardiovascular morbidity. However, structural barriers and inequities limit access to adequate postpartum support. UREG communities face a disproportionate burden of APOs, reduced lactation support, and delays in cardiovascular risk recognition and management. This underscores the need for equitable clinical and public health strategies. Integrating more culturally competent care and structured cardiovascular risk assessments into routine postpartum care, especially for those with APOs, may be an opportunity to reduce maternal health disparities. Future research should focus on implementing equity-driven interventions to bridge obstetric and preventive cardiovascular care.
