Aquaculture-derived carbon and nitrogen fuel N2O emissions and undermine mangrove climate benefits
Authors: Lijuan Zhang, Danqing Huang, Qingxing Tan, Xinyi Wang, Ying Fan, Yuxuan Sha, Xin Zhang, Junxian Hong, Zhiqiang Lu, Chengye Wu, Danyang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study revealed the effects of aquaculture activities on sediment N2O emissions in Zhangjiang Estuary mangrove, southeastern China: (1) the annual average emission rate of N2O was 0.073 t ha−1 yr−1 (19.82 t CO2 eq ha−1 yr−1), offsetting about 43.08% of the climate benefits produced by the carbon sequestration here; (2) Aquaculture-derived NO3− directly provided substrate for sediment denitrification. Nitrogen inputs with high NH4+: NO3− ratios also led to the NO2− accumulation in the overlying water and sediment pore water, further promoting denitrification. In addition, the input of highly reactive organic matter from aquaculture could directly stimulate denitrification, or indirectly promote it by supporting CH4 oxidation; (3) Although denitrification was accelerated by aquaculture activities, the reduction of N2O was likely inhibited by the acidic conditions of the study area, thus increasing N2O emissions. This study highlights the need to incorporate sustainable aquaculture management into mangrove conservation frameworks.Keywords: Mangroves, sediment N2O emission, aquaculture activity, MixSIAR model, denitrification

Introduction

As one of the most productive ecosystems, mangroves play significant roles in mitigating global warming.1,2 It has been reported that the annual carbon sequestration flux in mangroves is 174 g C m−2 yr−1, and the carbon stored in mangroves can be retained in sediments for long periods.1 However, this climate benefit may be partially offset by the simultaneous emissions of greenhouse gas (GHG) in mangroves. Previous studies have indicated that about 8.7%–35% of the net carbon sequestration in mangroves was offset by the GHG emissions,3,4,5,6 and sediments were identified as the major source of these emissions.3,5 On one hand, the decomposition of mangrove litter produces substantial amounts of CH4.7,8 On the other hand, periodic hypoxic conditions in mangroves facilitate denitrification, thereby promoting the emission of its intermediate product, N2O. Given that the 100-year global warming potentials of CH4 and N2O are 27.9 and 273 times higher than that of CO29, a better understanding of the regulatory mechanisms governing sediment GHG emissions in mangroves is urgently needed.

Due to the growing global demand for seafood, aquaculture has expanded rapidly in recent decades.9 The prevailing high-intensity farming practices aimed at maximizing yield have led to markedly increased GHG emissions from aquaculture systems, with an estimated annual output of approximately 63.90 Tg CO2 equivalent (CO2 eq).10 Geographically, the majority of aquaculture-driven GHG emissions originate from intensive farming regions in mid- and low-latitudes, notably East Asia, Southeast Asia, and South Asia.10 These regions are also characterized by extensive mangrove distributions. In southeastern China, aquaculture ponds are frequently established in close proximity to, or even directly encroach upon, mangrove forests. This spatial adjacency can result in the substantial transport of nutrient-rich materials, including uneaten feed and metabolic wastes, from pond systems into adjacent mangrove sediments. Such exogenous inputs are likely to alter local biogeochemical conditions and may stimulate GHG production in mangroves, particularly N2O. The reasons are as follows: first, nitrogen-rich effluents from overfeeding and metabolite excretion provide substrates for N2O production;11,12 Second, aquaculture activities introduce large amounts of organic matter into mangroves.13 Furthermore, nutrient-rich effluents may stimulate the production of highly labile biogenic organic matter and accelerate the decomposition of refractory dissolved organic matter in mangrove sediments.12,14 Such organic matter can serve as electron donors for denitrification, thereby promoting N2O production. Although elevated GHG emissions in mangroves linked to aquaculture activities have been documented,15,16,17,18 the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain poorly understood. Integrating stable isotope techniques with metagenomic approaches can help address this gap. Stable isotopic mixing models enable tracing and quantification of aquaculture-derived nutrients,19 while metagenomic analysis elucidates the microbial potential for key metabolic processes in carbon and nitrogen cycling.20 Specifically, the ratio of N2O-producing genes to N2O-reducing genes can serve as an indicator of net N2O accumulation potential during denitrification.21,22 Together, these approaches enable a mechanistic understanding of how aquaculture activities regulate sediment N2O emissions in mangroves, offering important insights for blue carbon management and climate mitigation strategies.

In this study, the Zhangjiang Estuary mangrove (ZJE mangrove; 23.90°–23.93° N, 117.40°–117.50° E; 2360 ha), located in Fujian Province, southeastern China, was selected as the study area. It represents the most species-rich natural mangrove community north of the Tropic of Cancer in China, with dominant vegetation including Kandelia obovata, Aegiceras corniculatum, and Avicennia marina. Aquaculture is highly developed in this region, with the razor clam (Sinonovacula constricta) as the primary cultivated species. Although aquaculture ponds within the core zone of the ZJE mangrove have been systematically removed in recent years in the recognition of the carbon sequestration role of mangroves,23 activities in surrounding areas remain intensive and continue to exert environmental pressure.3,24,25 These pressures have been associated with various environmental issues in the region.25,26 Therefore, the ZJE mangrove represents an ideal site to investigate the regulatory mechanisms by which aquaculture activities influence sediment N2O emissions.

N2O emission incubation experiments, stable isotope mixing models, and metagenomic analysis were integrated to analyze the relationship between sediment N2O emissions and aquaculture activities. The objectives were: (1) to quantify the flux of sediment N2O emissions in the study area and evaluate their potential negative climate effects; (2) to identify key environmental factors affecting N2O emissions; and (3) to reveal the roles of aquaculture activities in regulating sediment N2O emissions. This research aims to improve understanding of anthropogenic impacts on mangrove carbon sinks and their implications for global climate, thereby informing future mangrove restoration efforts and integrated land-sea management.

Physical and chemical parameters

Sediment moisture content ranged from 38.4% to 52.7%, peaking in Jan 2025. Fresh and dry bulk densities varied from 1.35 to 1.69 g cm−3 and from 0.66 to 1.00 g cm−3, respectively. The contents of NO3−−N, NO2−−N, and NH4+−N are shown in Table S3 and Figure S1. Generally, NH4+ is the major component of dissolved inorganic nitrogen (DIN), accounting for 82.5 to 94.3% (88.6 ± 5.5%, n = 5), 89.9 to 96.3% (93.2 ± 2.1%, n = 7), and 92.0 to 95.4% (94.1 ± 1.5%, n = 7) of the total DIN in May 2024, Sep 2024, and Jan 2025, respectively. The δ15N and δ18O of NO3− are also shown in Figure S1, with higher values both observed in May 2024.

The content and isotopic values of organic matter

The total organic carbon (TOC) content in sediment ranged from 1.29% to 2.75% (1.79 ± 0.59%, n = 5), from 0.91% to 1.87% (1.24 ± 0.35%, n = 7), and from 0.96% to 3.54% (2.13 ± 0.98%, n = 7) in May 2024, Sep 2024, and Jan 2025, respectively. The total nitrogen (TN) content in May 2024, Sep 2024, and Jan 2024 ranged from 0.10% to 0.18% (0.13 ± 0.03%, n = 5), from 0.10% to 0.14% (0.12 ± 0.01%, n = 7), and from 0.12% to 0.25% (0.19 ± 0 0.04%, n = 7). Generally, higher contents of TOC and TN were observed at the core area of ZJE mangrove, with the maximum values for Jan 2025 observed at A6, while the maximum values for May 2024 and Sep 2024 were observed at A5. Higher C:N ratios of soil organic matter (SOM) (C: NSOM) were also observed at the core area of the ZJE mangrove (A5 in May 2024 and Jan 2025; A6 in Sep 2024). The contents of DOC ranged from 5.687 to 11.480 mg L−1 (7.812 ± 2.383 mg L−1, n = 5), 4.354 to 5.603 mg L−1 (5.050 ± 0.542 mg L−1, n = 7), and from 7.105 to 9.459 mg L−1 (8.168 ± 0.766 mg L−1, n = 7) in May 2024, Sep 2024, and Jan 2025, respectively. Generally, the contents of DOC in January 2025 were higher (Figure S1).

The δ13C of SOM (δ13CSOM) in May 2024, Sep 2024, and Jan 2024 ranged from −26.290‰ to −25.397‰ (−25.902 ± 0.378‰, n = 5), from −28.200‰ to −24.414‰ (−26.196 ± 1.366‰, n = 7), and from −28.369‰ to −22.580‰ (−26.001 ± 1.911‰, n = 7), respectively, with relatively less seasonal variation. The δ15N of SOM (δ15NSOM) in May 2024, Sep 2024, and Jan 2024 ranged from 1.917‰ to 4.037‰ (3.293 ± 0 0.826‰, n = 5), from 4.290‰ to 6.890‰ (6.209 ± 0 0.880‰, n = 7), and from 3.898‰ to 6.160‰ (5.132 ± 0 0.849‰, n = 7), with lower values observed in May 2024. Generally, the spatial variation of δ15NSOM was more obvious than that of δ13CSOM. The details for organic matter were shown in Table S4 and Figure S1.

Sediment N2O emission rate

The emission rates of N2O fluxes in May 2024 ranged from 0 to 0.029 mmol m−2 d−1 (0.009 ± 0.012 mmol m−2 d−1, n = 5), with less spatial variation. The rates in Sep 2024 ranged from −1.111 to 20.576 mmol m−2 d−1 (4.743 ± 8.103 mmol m−2 d−1, n = 7), which were significantly higher than those in other seasons. In Jan 2025, most of the observed N2O emission rates were negative. The details were shown in Figure S11.

Normalized abundance of key functional genes

Gene abundances presented in this section are expressed as normalized abundance values (NA, coverage depth per million reads), enabling direct comparison across samples and functional genes. Denitrification exhibited the highest genetic potential among all nitrogen transformation pathways in the study area (Figure 1). In May 2024, the NA of narG ranged from 3.59 to 8.05 (mean: 5.45), increasing to 4.89–16.88 (mean: 10.24) in Sep 2024. The napA gene (DNRA) also maintained relatively high abundance levels, with mean values of 6.92 in May and 6.69 in Sep 2024. In contrast, nitrification genes (amoA, amoB, amoC) remained at extremely low levels across all samples (mean <0.5), suggesting that nitrification is not a major nitrogen transformation pathway in this sedimentary environment. Nitrogen fixation genes (nifH, nifD) showed moderate abundances, with mean values ranging from 0.46 to 4.06. All denitrification genes showed increased abundances from May to September. The mean NA of narG increased from 5.45 to 10.24, nirS from 4.26 to 4.65, nosZ from 3.51 to 3.96, nirK from 0.85 to 1.53, and norB from 2.49 to 3.72. Spatially, A1 and A3, the stations located near aquaculture pond sluice-gates, emerged as the most active denitrification hotspots, with the highest abundances of narG and nirS during both sampling periods (Figure 1). Detailed information is provided in Table S5.

Discussion

Sediment N2O emissions offset 43.08% of mangrove carbon climate benefit

Within our study area, N2O emissions were substantially higher during periods of stronger aquaculture activity. Generally, the rates of sediment N2O emissions in May 2024 fell into the range of previously reported values from similar ecosystems, whereas those in Sep 2024 exceeded the upper limit of the reported values (Table 1). Notably, significant negative emissions were observed in Jan 2025, which were closely associated with the lower T and relatively weakened aquaculture inputs during this period. This indicates that under conditions of reduced aquaculture influence, the mangrove sediments may act as a sink for N2O rather than a source. When compared to mangrove ecosystems elsewhere that are not subjected to aquaculture disturbances, the emission rates in our study area were also considerably elevated (Table 1).

The N2O emission rates in mangrove ecosystems and coastal waters

Based on the global warming potential coefficients of N2O,35 the fluxes of N2O can be converted to CO2 eq fluxes by the following equation: Fe = Fm × M × KGWP, where Fm, M, and KGWP represent the emission flux, molar mass, and global warming potential coefficient of N2O (273).35 If only the N2O emissions of the top 30 cm sediment are considered, the CO2 eq fluxes converted from sediment N2O emissions in May 2024, Sep 2024, and Jan 2025 were 0∼0.0035 t ha−1 d−1, −0.1335–2.4715 t ha−1 d−1, and −0.7170–0.0172 t ha−1 d−1, resulting in an average value of 19.82 t ha−1 yr−1. According to previous research,36 the annual net ecosystem productivity (NEP) of the ZJE mangrove was approximately 1255 g C m−2 yr−1. Thus, sediment N2O emissions in the ZJE mangrove may offset approximately 43.08% of the climate benefits from carbon sequestration. Although this substantial offset is subject to inter-annual variability, it underscores the urgency of quantifying the impact of aquaculture activities on GHG emissions from mangroves and provides a realistic basis for subsequent mechanistic exploration.

Effects of environmental factors on N2O emission potential and flux

N2O emissions from mangrove sediments are directly regulated by multiple environmental factors, including T, pH, salinity, and the availability of nitrogen and carbon substrates.3,4 The generalized additive model (GAM) analysis in this study confirmed the coupled effects of these parameters on N2O emission rates (Figure 3). To further elucidate the underlying microbial mechanisms, the ratio RN2O = (nirK + nirS)/nosZ (based on gene NAs), which reflects the genetic potential of N2O emission,21,37 was also used to explain the regulatory role of the above factors.

Sediment T exhibited a significant positive correlation with N2O emissions (EDF = 1, p < 0.05, Figure 3A). This can be explained by the following ways: (1) elevated T can accelerate microbial metabolism, especially the first steps of denitrification and nitrification.38,39 (2) Compared to the preceding N2O-producing steps, the N2O-reducing step (N2O → N2) catalyzed by NosZ enzyme has been reported to be more sensitive or unstable under warming conditions.40,41 This imbalance may intensify N2O accumulation. (3) Increased T can also stimulate sediment respiration and oxygen consumption,42 creating more favorable anaerobic conditions for denitrification and consequently enhancing N2O production. However, the NA of nosZ showed no significant correlation with T, suggesting that the inhibitory effect of warming on N2O reductase activity may occur at the enzymatic level rather than at the gene abundance level. This also explains why the genetic potential (RN2O) was not significantly associated with T, while actual emissions responded strongly to T.

Beyond sediment T, sediment pH also exerted a significant influence on N2O emissions, which exhibited a “U-shaped” relationship with N2O flux (EDF = 2.85, p < 0.001; Figure 3B): N2O emissions decreased with increasing pH within the weakly acidic to neutral range (pH < 7), but rebounded slightly as pH entered the near-neutral to weakly alkaline range (pH > 7). This variation likely reflects shifts in the dominant denitrifying microbial groups and key functional steps across different pH intervals. Previous studies have indicated that denitrification under acidic conditions (pH < 6.8) is primarily driven by bacteria, whereas neutral to weakly alkaline conditions may favor fungal denitrification.43 Concurrently, NosZ enzyme activity has been widely reported to be inhibited under acidic conditions.44,45 Specifically, acidic conditions may disrupt the synthesis of N2O reductase by impairing copper incorporation into its CuZ and CuA structural domains.44 Furthermore, low pH promotes the accumulation of free nitrous acid, which can also inhibit N2O reductase.46 Thus, acid conditions may impede the reduction of N2O to N2. Across all samples, soil pH exhibited significant negative correlations with the NAs of narG (r = −0.679, p < 0.05, n = 10) and norB (r = −0.685, p < 0.05, n = 10), indicating that nitrate reduction genetic potential increased as pH decreased. Simultaneously, NosZ enzyme activity was inhibited under acidic conditions, hindering N2O reduction. The combination of enhanced substrate supply driven by narG and norB, together with strong inhibition of NosZ activity, resulted in higher net N2O emissions under acid conditions. As pH approached neutrality, substrate availability became limited by weakened narG and norB supply, and NosZ inhibition was partially alleviated, leading to a decline in net emissions. In May 2024, under the near-neutral to weakly alkaline conditions, denitrification is primarily carried out by fungi.43 Most fungi lack the nosZ gene; thus, the denitrification process driven by fungi typically terminates at N2O.43 As a result, the rate of N2O emissions exhibited a slight rise within this pH range.

During our investigation, the S of the overlying water was negatively correlated with the contents of NO3−−N (EDF = 1.88, p < 0.01; Figure 3C), NH4+−N (EDF = 1, p < 0.05; Figure 3D), and the emission rates of N2O (EDF = 1, p < 0.05; Figure 3E). This indicates the strong effects of terrestrial inputs on the nitrogen pool of the study area. The elevated NO3−−N and NH4+−N provide sufficient substrate for NO2−−N, thus promoting the emissions of N2O (EDF = 1, p < 0.01; Figure 3F). Apart from nitrogen, the input of external organic matter may also have a great effect on N2O emissions. In this study, N2O emission rates exhibited positive correlations with DOC (r = 0.955, p < 0.05, n = 5) in May 2024 and TOC (r = 0.815, p < 0.01, n = 7) in Sep 2024. RN2O was also found to be negatively related with C:NSOM in Sep 2024 (r = −0.855, p < 0.05, n = 6). On one hand, organic matter may directly serve as electron donors for denitrification, thus supporting N2O emissions.47 On the other hand, it may also create anaerobic conditions or alter sediment pH,18,44,45,46 thus indirectly regulating N2O emissions.

In summary, these results indicate the effects of environmental factors on the N2O emission potential and flux in the study area. Notably, the functional potential reflected by the NAs of functional genes is not always aligned with actual N2O emission rates. Functional potential represents the metabolic configuration shaped by long-term microbial community responses to environmental pressures. In contrast, actual emission rates are subject to immediate regulation by in situ conditions at the time of sampling, reflecting metabolic responses over short timescales. In this study, RN2O was not significantly correlated with actual emission rates, suggesting that future studies should integrate metatranscriptomics or enzyme activity assays to bridge the gap between functional potential and actual metabolic fluxes.

The roles of aquaculture activities in fueling N2O emissions

As noted in the introduction, aquaculture activities in the surrounding region of the ZJE mangrove remain intensive. Water enriched with organic waste and nutrients from these ponds can be introduced into the mangrove area via tidal exchange.24,25 The correlations between S and the contents of NO3−−N and NH4+−N also support this. Thus, the MixSIAR model was used to quantify the specific effects of aquaculture activities on the nitrogen and carbon pools of the study area. The results revealed that aquaculture effluent was the primary source of NO3−, accounting for about 36.28% and 28.04% of total NO3− in May 2024 and Jan 2025 (Figure 2). An obvious positive correlation between the proportional contribution of aquaculture water to the total NO3− (:NO3−Aquaculture ratio) and the emission rates of N2O was observed (N2O emission rates = −12.48 + 0.33 ×:NO3−Aquaculture ratio; r = 0.677, p < 0.05, n = 12). If the contribution of aquaculture was zero, then the N2O emission was negative in the study area (−12.48 mmol m−2 d−1).

Another important characteristic of the aquaculture-derived N was the extremely high NH4+:NO3− ratios (Table S3). Aquaculture water contains large amounts of undigested feed proteins, which can be converted to NH4+18,26 This may be the major reason for the obviously higher NH4+ in the overlying water of the study area and the neighboring area.11,26,48 As only the values of δ15N of NH4+ were determined, it is difficult to quantify the contribution of aquaculture water to the total NH4+ by the MixSIAR model. However, the δ15N values of NH4+ at most sampling stations were close to those observed at the drainage sluice of the aquaculture ponds, indicating a possible great contribution of aquaculture water to the total NH4+ in the overlying water of the study area. Nevertheless, precise source analysis needs to be considered in future investigations. Previous studies have revealed the inhibition of free ammonia on the activity of ammonia-oxidizing bacteria (AOB) and nitrite-oxidizing bacteria (NOB).49,50 In comparison, NOB is more susceptible to free ammonia inhibition,51 leading to inadequate conversion of NO2− and the accumulation of NO2− in the overlying water. This can further elevate the content of NO2−in the pore water of mangrove sediments, thus promoting the production of N2O under anaerobic conditions. Metagenomic data revealed that the NA of amoA, the functional gene for the first step of nitrification (NH4+ oxidation), was very low (Figure 1), confirming that direct nitrification is not the main source of NO2− under anoxic conditions. Therefore, aquaculture-derived nitrogen promotes denitrification through two pathways: direct NO3− supply and indirect NO2− enrichment.

In addition to N inputs, organic matter derived from aquaculture activities should also be considered. Aquaculture wastewater contains substantial amounts of plant and animal debris, residual feed, and other organic-rich substances.13,17 In this study, a negative correlation between the contribution ratio of aquaculture to the total SOM and the C:NSOM in the study area was observed (r = −0.778, p < 0.01, n = 19), indicating that the inputs of aquaculture-derived particulate organic carbon may elevate the bioavailability of SOM in the study area.17 Although no statistically significant correlation was observed between RN2O and the contribution of aquaculture activity to total SOM, RN2O showed a clear increasing trend with the rising contribution of aquaculture (Figure S2). In addition, it exhibited a significant negative correlation with the C:NSOM in the study area. Given that aquaculture activity may be the most important source of low C: N ratio organic matter in the study area, its positive influence on RN2O cannot be overlooked. Apart from particulate organic matter, aquaculture activities may also be an important source of bioavailable DOC, as most DOC in aquaculture water is biogenic.13,17 During our investigation, concurrently elevated concentrations of DOC, NO3− and NH4+ were observed at A1 (the station near the discharge outlet of aquaculture water). Such bioavailable organic matter can be rapidly degraded and then serve as electron donors for microbial denitrification. This may also alter the pH of mangrove sediments, thus indirectly altering N2O emission (Figure 4A, SEM).

In addition to direct effects, aquaculture-derived organic matter may also indirectly regulate N2O emissions by influencing methane metabolism. The coupling between methane oxidation and denitrification has been widely reported in multiple ecosystems in recent years.52,53,54,55 In this study, the ratio of methanogenic to methane-oxidizing genes (RCH4 = mcrA/pmo ratio) was used as an indicator of net CH4 emission potential.22 RCH4 was positively correlated with the content of aquaculture-derived particulate organic matter (r = 0.929, p < 0.01, n = 10), indicating that aquaculture inputs disrupted the balance between methanogenic and methanotrophic genetic potentials, thereby enhancing CH4 emission potential. This aligns with Dai et al.,16 who found that intensive aquaculture effluents provide substrates for methylotrophic methanogens, elevating methane emission risk. Notably, although the elevated RCH4 ratio implies an increased advantage of methanogenesis over methane oxidation, the abundance of pmo exhibited similar trends to mcrA. This suggests that rather than reflecting the inhibition of methane oxidation, aquaculture inputs simultaneously stimulate both methanogenesis and methane oxidation. In addition, pmo abundance was negatively correlated with C:NSOM (Figure 4B). Given that aquaculture activities serve as the primary source of labile organic matter with low C:N ratios, it is suggested that such inputs stimulate both processes, albeit to varying extents. It has been shown in previous studies that small-molecule organic compounds (such as methanol and formate) can be released by aerobic methanotrophs during metabolism,56 which may theoretically serve as electron donors for coexisting denitrifiers.57 Additionally, oxygen consumption during methane oxidation may exacerbate local hypoxia, creating more favorable microhabitats for denitrifiers. In this study, the significant positive correlation between pmo abundance and denitrification genes (nirS, nosZ, Figure 4B) can further support this. However, although the complete denitrification process may be promoted by methane oxidation, NosZ enzyme activity may be constrained under acidic conditions, leading to the accumulation of N2O.

In summary, aquaculture effluents significantly enhance N2O emissions in the ZJE mangrove through multiple interconnected pathways (Figure 5). First, aquaculture-derived nitrogen inputs directly fuel denitrification by supplying NO3− as a substrate, while the characteristically high NH4+:NO3− ratios lead to NO2− accumulation in overlying water and sediment pore water, further promoting denitrification. Second, labile organic matter from aquaculture acts as a direct electron donor for denitrifiers and simultaneously stimulates methane metabolic processes. The resulting methane oxidation then indirectly supports denitrification by supplying small-molecule organic acids. However, although carbon-nitrogen coupling promotes denitrification, acidic conditions inhibit NosZ enzyme activity, preventing N2O reduction and leading to its accumulation. This study demonstrates that aquaculture effluents influence N2O emissions through coupled carbon-nitrogen mechanisms. It also implies that future management of aquaculture effluent should shift from bulk load reduction to form-specific targeting. Specifically, efforts should prioritize reducing the NH4+:NO3− ratio and the labile organic carbon fraction over TN and TOC, so as to more effectively mitigate N2O emissions from coastal wetlands.

The regulating mechanism of aquaculture-derived nitrogen and organic matter on the sediment N2O emissions in the study area

Limitations of the study

This study provides comprehensive insights into the mechanisms by which aquaculture activities regulate N2O emissions in mangrove sediments, yet several limitations should be acknowledged. The analysis of microbial mechanisms is primarily based on functional gene abundances (genetic potential) rather than actual gene expression or enzyme activity levels. Future studies integrating metatranscriptomics, proteomics, or direct enzyme activity assays would help bridge the gap between genetic potential and actual metabolic fluxes. Furthermore, other microbial processes, such as nitrifier denitrification (suggested by high NH4+ and aerobic-anoxic oscillations) and fungal denitrification, may also contribute to N2O production. Future studies should fully consider these processes.

Method details

Sampling collection

Field samplings were carried out in May 2024, September 2024 (Sep 2024), and January 2025 (Jan 2025). The sampling stations are shown in the figure below: A1 ∼ A4 were located near tidal creeks; A5 and A6 were situated in the core zone; A7 was located on the mudflat. Surface sediments (0 ∼ 30 cm) were collected using a core sampler (15 cm diameter, 50 cm length). After removing the top 3 cm, the samples were homogenized for subsequent analysis of organic matter, DNA extraction, and N2O incubation experiments. The overlying water from each station and the water at the outflow gate of the aquaculture ponds were also collected for determining the contents of nitrogen and organic matter, as well as their isotopic values. In-situ measurements of sediment temperature (T), salinity (S), and pH were conducted using a portable analyzer, while fresh and dry bulk densities were determined via the cutting ring method. Water parameters were measured on-site with a multiparameter meter (WTW Multi 3630 IDS, Germany). All sediment samples were stored at −20 °C, water samples at 4 °C, and samples for DNA analysis at −80 °C.

Sampling stations in the Zhangjiang estuary mangrove

Contents and isotopic values of dissolved inorganic nitrogen

Water samples were filtered by precombusted (450 °C, 4 h) GF-75 filters (47 mm, 0.3 μm pore size). Concentrations of nitrate (NO3-), nitrite (NO2-) and ammonia (NH4+) were analyzed with a Lachat QuickChem 8500 autoanalyzer (Lachat Instruments, Loveland, CO., USA) following standard colorimetric methods. The isotopic values (δ15N, δ18O) of NO3- and NH4+ were determined in May 2024 and Jan 2025. The δ15N and δ18O of NO3− were measured using the “denitrifier method.”58,59 The δ15N of NH4+ was determined by the following method: NH4+ was concentrated on the glass fiber filter through a diffusion method and digested to NO3− by persulfate.60 After that, the δ15N of the converted NO3− from NH4+ was measured using the denitrifier method.58,59 The analytical precision for δ15N and δ18O were less than 0.2‰ and 1‰, respectively.

Contents and isotopic values of organic matter

Water samples (4 L) were filtered through a 200 μm mesh sieve to remove large detritus, then filtered using precombusted (450 °C, 4 h) GF-75 filters (47 mm, 0.3 μm pore size). The filtrate was acidified to pH < 2, and the content of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) was determined using a Total Organic Carbon Analyzer (TOC-L CPH, Shimadzu, Japan). Sediment samples were treated with HCl vapor (48 h) to remove inorganic carbon, then dried at 60 °C and completely ground. The contents and δ13C and δ15N values of soil organic matter (SOM) were then measured using a Finnigan Delta V Advantage isotope ratio mass spectrometer interfaced with a Carlo Erba NC 2500 elemental analyzer. The analytical precision of isotopic values was < 0.2‰.

Sediment N2O emission rate

Sediment N2O emission rate was determined by the slurry incubation method.61 Briefly, the weighted sediments were mixed with the overlying water in a volume ratio of 1:5, then transferred into two 60 mL brown serum bottles. Bottle A was designed as the control sample to observe the initial content of N2O, while Bottle B was designed to observe the final concentration. During filling, 200 μL of ZnCl2 was added to Bottle A. Both bottles were completely filled without bubbles and then incubated in the dark for 24 h. The laboratory incubation T was set to match the in situ field T at the time of sampling. After incubation, 200 μL of ZnCl2 solution was immediately added to Bottle B. During analysis, 25.0 mL of high-purity helium was injected into each sample bottle using a gas-tight syringe, displacing an equal volume of water to create a headspace. Samples were shaken thoroughly with a vortex mixer and then left to equilibrate in the dark for at least 3 h. A portion of the headspace gas was then injected into a gas chromatograph (Agilent 7890A, US) to measure the N2O concentration in the headspace (Cg, ppm). Metagenomic analysis

Metagenomic analysis was conducted in May 2024 and Sep 2024. Total DNA was extracted using the TIANMicrobe Magnetic Envir-DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China) and the concentration was measured by Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Sequencing libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina, following the manufacturer's instructions. The library construction procedure included enzymatic fragmentation, end repair and A-tailing, adapter ligation, fragment size selection and purification, followed by PCR amplification (8 cycles). After passing quality control, the libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in paired-end 150 bp (PE150) mode.

Raw sequencing reads were processed using fastp (v0.23.1) for quality control and adapter trimming, and subsequently with bowtie 2 (v2.2.4) to remove host-derived sequences. The resulting clean reads were assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT (v1.1.2), and contigs shorter than 300 bp were discarded. Genes were predicted from the contigs using MetaGeneMark (v3.26), from which a non-redundant gene catalog was constructed with MMseqs2 at 95% sequence similarity and 90% coverage. The protein sequences from this catalog were aligned against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database using DIAMOND (v0.9.29) (E-value < 1e-5) to assign KEGG Orthology (KO) identifiers.

Gene abundance was calculated as the average coverage depth of each gene (total bases mapped to the gene/gene length). To eliminate the influence of sequencing depth differences across samples, the raw depth values were normalized by dividing by the total number of clean reads for each sample and multiplying by 106, yielding a normalized abundance value (NA, coverage depth per million reads). This normalization enables direct comparison of the same gene across different samples. In this study, the normalized abundance represents the genetic potential of a metabolic pathway or the relative abundance of functional genes (e.g., nirS, nosZ, mcrA, pmoA), rather than real-time metabolic activity. The raw metagenomic sequencing data have been submitted to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (BioProject ID: PRJNA1430958).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Pearson’s correlation analysis (SPSS, V19.0) and Generalized Additive Model (GAM, R, v4.3.3) were used to analyze the correlations between N2O emission and the environmental parameters. The MixSIAR model (R, v4.3.3) was used to observe the proportional contribution of aquaculture activities to the total SOM and NO3- in the study area. The isotopic end member values of the potential sources for SOM and NO3- are shown in supplemental information (Tables S7 and S8). Origin 2018 (OriginLab, USA) and the ‘ggplot2’ package in R (v4.3.3) were used for visualizing the results. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed using IBM SPSS Amos v26.0 to explore the direct and indirect pathways through which environmental factors and aquaculture-derived organic matter influence sediment N2O emissions. The codes for the GAM and MixSIAR models were provided in Mendeley Data (https://data.mendeley.com/preview/yztzyxvxb5?a=6a9c2205-1603-445d-8009-5c183bf490f2).
