Efficacy of ultrasound-guided lateral quadratus lumborum block on postoperative pain and quality of recovery in patients undergoing open nephrectomy: A randomized controlled study
Authors: Büşra Yağbasan, Levent Özdemir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:In our study, we aimed to evaluate the effects of lateral quadratus lumborum block (QLB) on postoperative pain and quality of recovery (QoR) in patients undergoing open nephrectomy.Methods:This study was designed as a randomized, prospective study. Patients were divided into 2 groups as lateral QLB group (group QLB) and Morphine group (group M). Lateral QLB was applied to patients in the QLB group with 25 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine. Opioid consumption, visual analog scale (VAS) and QoR-15 scores of the patients in the first 72 hours postoperatively were recorded.Results:A total of 40 patients were included in the study (group QLB, n = 20 and group M, n = 20). No significant difference was observed in total morphine consumption. VAS pain scores were lower in the lateral QLB group at postoperative 0th min, 30th min, 2nd, 3rd, 12th and 48th hour (P < .05). Postoperative agitation was significantly less in patients who underwent lateral QLB (P = .01). Length of hospital stay was shorter in the QLB group (P = .02). QoR-15 score was higher in lateral QLB group only on the 3rd postoperative day (P = .02).Conclusion:In patients undergoing open nephrectomy, lateral QLB may be a safe part of multimodal analgesia because it reduces postoperative pain scores, agitation and length of hospital stay. However, it should be noted that in our study, no significant difference was found between the groups in terms of total morphine consumption and QoR.

1. Introduction

Nephrectomies are a common type of urological surgery that can be performed with laparoscopic, robotic or open surgery methods.[1] In some cases, open surgery may be preferred. It is known that open nephrectomy causes intense postoperative acute pain, high rates of chronic pain and also prolongs the hospital stay.[2] Multimodal analgesia combined with regional techniques is recommended to reduce severe postoperative pain and side effects related to opioid consumption, and there are studies showing that these approaches can positively affect patient outcomes.[3]

There are many ways to treat postoperative pain in patients undergoing nephrectomy. Medical treatment alone is always an option. Thoracic epidural analgesia has been the standard practice.[3] Other regional analgesia options include the use of intrathecal opioids, paravertebral, erector spinae plane block, transversus abdominis plane block (TAPB) and quadratus lumborum block (QLB). QLB is a relatively new regional anesthesia method and can be applied as a part of multimodal analgesia.[4] QLB can be effective in the T8-L4 dermatome area.[4] QLB can be applied in different approaches as lateral (type 1), posterior (type 2) and anterior (type 3) and there are published articles about anterior and posterior QLB in open nephrectomy cases.[4] However, the effectiveness of lateral QLB in nephrectomy cases is not clear. According to the Delphi nomenclature study, it was suggested that the posterior TAPB has similar areas of effect to the lateral QLB block, and both could be called lateral QLB blocks.[5]

To understand the effect area of QLB, it is important to define the fascial plane in the abdominal compartment and the 3-layer fascial model is the most widely used.[6] These fascial planes follow the quadratus lumborum (QLM) and psoas muscles along the medial and lateral arcuate ligaments and the aortic hiatus of the diaphragm and form the endothoracic fascia. This provides a potential route for the injected substance, such as local anesthetic, to spread from the abdomen to the thoracic and paravertebral spaces, thus achieving clinical effect.[7] In addition, sympathetic nerve fibers associated with the abdominal branches of the lumbar arteries lie posterior to the quadratus lumborum muscle and innervate the thoracolumbar fascia (TLF), which contribute to analgesic effects.[7]

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of lateral QLB on the amount of opioid consumption for postoperative analgesia in patients undergoing open nephrectomy. Secondary outcomes were to compare the effects on postoperative pain scores, quality of recovery (QoR-15 scale), and length of hospital stay compared with the control group.

2. Materials and methods

This study was designed as a prospective, randomized trial and the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) system was used for the study. The study was approved by the Mersin University Clinical Research Ethics Committee with the decision numbered 2023/06 dated April 26, 2023. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration (2013 review). Preoperative information was provided to the patients included in the study and their written consents were also obtained.

A total of 40 patients who underwent elective open nephrectomy with flank incision at Mersin University Medical Faculty Hospital between June 2023 and November 2023, were between the ages of 18 and 80, had American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III, did not use any analgesic medication during the preoperative period, had complete perioperative records, had no known allergy to local anesthetics, and signed the informed consent form were included in our study. Patients with missing perioperative records, known bleeding disorders or anticoagulant therapy, ASA physical status IV, used preoperative analgesic medication for chronic pain or another reason, and did not sign the informed consent form were excluded from the study. The patients were divided into 2 groups: lateral QLB group (n = 20) and morphine group (group M, n = 20).

The randomization of the patients was provided by another anesthesiologist who was not involved in the study and by giving random numbers generated by the computer during the preoperative anesthesia evaluation (https://www.random.org). The generated random numbers were sealed in sequentially numbered opaque envelopes. These sealed envelopes were placed in the patient file. The group that these numbers corresponded to was checked by an anesthesiologist (L.Ö.) in the operating room and applied intraoperatively. The follow-up of the patients in the postoperative period was provided by the other anesthesiologist (B.Y.) who did not know what was applied to the patient intraoperatively and the results were recorded.

The necessary information about visual analog scale (VAS) and quality of recovery scale (QoR-15) was given during the preoperative anesthesia evaluation of the patients. The patients were provided with a fasting period of 6–8 hours before the operation. Preoperatively, demographic characteristics of the patients, existing comorbidities (diabetes, coronary artery disease, essential hypertension, etc), medications used, and ASA risk classification data were recorded. The QoR-15 scale was applied to the patients both before and during the specified postoperative periods. The validity and reliability study of the Turkish version of the QoR-15 scale used was available.[8]

After both groups were taken to the operating room where the operation would be performed, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), invasive arterial pressure and electrocardiogram monitoring were applied to the patients. The patients in both groups underwent standard anesthesia induction with propofol (Propofol®, Fresenius Kabi, Melsungen, Germany) at a dose of 2 mg kg-1, fentanyl (Fentanyl Citrate®, Hospira) at a dose of 2 mcq kg-1, rocuronium (Esmeron®, Organon, Kloosterstraat, Netherlands) at a dose of 0.6 mg kg-1, respectively. Anesthesia was maintained with inhalation anesthesia with a mixture of 1%–2% sevoflurane (Sevorane®, Abbott, Chicago), 50% N₂O and 50% O₂. After endotracheal intubation, the patients were placed in the lateral decubitus position, and support gel pads were applied to the positional pressure points to prevent nerve damage. The hemodynamic data, urine output, amount of bleeding, blood transfusion requirement, intraoperative complications, duration of anesthesia and surgery of the patients were recorded during the surgery.

The patients in Group M were not given any preoperative analgesic medication or any nerve blockade before surgery. After the patients were placed in the lateral decubitus position (right or left) according to the operation side, the area to be blocked in Group QLB patients was cleaned with antiseptic solution. After the ultrasound (US) probe was covered in a sterile manner, the probe was advanced in the transverse plane from the paramedian region to the midaxillary line, between the crista iliaca and the 12th rib. The US probe provided visualization of the external oblique muscle, internal oblique muscle and transversus abdominis muscle in the paramedian region (Fig. 1). Then, by advancing laterally, the tapering of the transversus abdominis muscle on the midaxillary line and the beginning of the thoracolumbar fascia on the quadratus lumborum muscle and the QLM just below it was visualized. The probe was held fixed at this point and a 22 G 100 mm long blocking needle (Stimuplex® Ultra, Braun, Germany) was advanced using the in-plane technique until the lateral aponeurosis of the transversus abdominis muscle was punctured and the anterolateral border of the QL muscle was reached (Fig. 1). The position of the needle was confirmed by injecting 1 mL of the study injectate and the needle was repositioned if necessary. Then, 25 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine (Buvasin®, Vem, Turkiye) was injected and the appropriate distribution of the local anesthetic was monitored on ultrasound. The patients were handed over to the surgical team after the block. All patients included in the study were operated on by the same surgical team. Intravenous infusion of 10 mg kg-1 paracetamol (Parol, Atabay, Turkiye) and 15 mcq kg-1 morphine bolus were administered to the patients of both groups as a standard analgesic method before awakening.

Patients were routinely monitored in the postanesthetic care unit (PACU) for the first 1 hour postoperatively. During the first 72 hours postoperatively, the patients’ VAS scores, the amount of additional analgesics administered and the recovery quality scale (QoR-15) scores were recorded at specified intervals (postoperative 0th minute, 30th minute, 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 6th, 12th, 24th, 48th, and 72nd hours). During the evaluation of patients’ postoperative VAS scores, special attention was given to excluding catheter-related bladder discomfort (CRBD). Postoperative agitation status in patients was assessed with the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) and agitation was considered present in patients with +3 and +4 points.

In both groups, the pain of patients with VAS scores ≥ 4 was considered significant, and intravenous morphine infusion at a dose of 1 mg h-1 was started for analgesia. In addition to the morphine infusion, 1 mg morphine bolus was administered to patients with VAS scores ≥ 7. The patients were continued to be administered 1 mg morphine bolus at 10-min intervals until their VAS scores dropped below 7. Postoperative complications such as hypotension, agitation, allergy-anaphylaxis, respiratory distress, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), the need for inotropic support, and unexpected intensive care admission were recorded. In addition to the preoperative period, the QoR-15 scale was also applied to the patients at the end of the 24th, 48th and 72nd hours postoperatively and the results were recorded.

2.1. Statistical analysis

A study in which the morphine consumption for postoperative pain control in patients who underwent nephrectomy and in the control group was determined as 33.37 ± 7.36 mg (median 32.5, IQR 21.5–47) was taken as the criterion for sample size.[9] The hypothesis that QLB could reduce morphine consumption in these patients by 20%; When beta was calculated with 0.2 (80% power) and alpha 0.05, it was calculated that a total of 38 patients should be included, at least 19 in each group. Considering the possible dropouts for our study, a total of 40 patients were planned to be included in the study.

The data belonging to the patients were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for windows version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago) program. While analyzing the data; normally distributed data were calculated as mean and standard deviation. For data that did not conform to normal distribution, median and quartile values were given along with minimum and maximum values. Student t test and Mann–Whitney U test were used to compare independent 2 group means, and chi-square test was used to evaluate the relationship between categorical variables. Paired t-test was used to compare 2 repeated continuous measurement means. Repeated ANOVA test was used to compare more than 2 repeated continuous measurement means. The statistical significance level of the data was determined as P < .05.

3. Results

From June 12, 2023, to November 24, 2023, 47 patients were screened for eligibility. Of these, 45 patients were randomized to receive either QLB (n = 22) or morphine (n = 23). A total of 40 patients fully completed the study (group QLB, n = 20 vs group Morphine, n = 20) after deducting failure in the US-guided block and losses to follow-up.

4. Discussion

In our study, VAS pain scores were found to be lower in patients who underwent lateral QLB in open nephrectomy patients at 0 minutes, 30 minutes, 2 hours, 3 hours, 12 hours and 48 hours postoperatively. In addition, although the number of patients who received bolus IV morphine as rescue analgesia within the first hour of the postoperative period was found to be lower in the lateral QLB group, no difference was observed between the groups in terms of total morphine consumption. Postoperative agitation (+3 and +4 points according to RASS) was less common in lateral QLB group patients. Total hospital stay was shorter in lateral QLB group. While the QoR-15 score of the patients did not show a significant difference in the first 2 days postoperatively, it was higher in lateral QLB group only on the 3rd day postoperatively.

According to the generally accepted 3-layer fascial model for defining the posterior abdominal compartment, the fasciae surrounding the QLM anteriorly and posteriorly are the anterior TLF and middle TLF, respectively.[6,9] The posterior TLF surrounds the latissimus dorsi and erector spinae muscle posteriorly. In cadaveric studies comparing the nerves affected in different types of QLB, it was observed that lateral QLB could block the subcostal nerve (11th and 12th) as the upper limit in the cranial direction, as well as the ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric nerves.[10,11] The dermatomal area of influence of the QLB is not limited to these nerves alone and is more complex due to the relationships between fascial compartments. Clinically, it has been reported that it is usually affected in the T8-L4 dermatomes.[12] In cadaver studies, while lateral QLB causes deep staining in the middle layer of TLF, faint staining is observed in the anterior layer of TLF.[11] Pain control due to flank incision is possible with blockade of the subcostal nerve in the lateral QLB.[10] This situation suggests that even if it does not include the entire flank incision line and does not provide a completely painless condition, it may reduce the intensity and severity of pain. This may also give an idea about the lack of correlation between pain scores and total opioid requirement. The results of a systematic review and meta-analysis including cases of open nephrectomy showed that QLB provides better outcomes in terms of pain intensity and pain scores compared to TAPB and control groups.[13] The authors attribute this to the fact that the block of the ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric inferior nerve, and the lateral cutaneous branch of the thoracoabdominal nerve (T10–12) can be achieved distally at the nerve level with TAPB, whereas it can be obtained more proximally at the paraspinal space with QLB.[14] Finally, it should be kept in mind that individual structural differences in the interfascial area, adhesions and the mass effect created by the tumor may affect the distribution of local anesthetic and analgesic efficacy may be less or more than expected.

In our study, the primary outcome was defined as the total postoperative morphine consumption. The main factors influencing the primary outcome include the postoperative analgesia plan,[3] surgical technique,[15] use of retractors,[16] occurrence of rib fracture,[16] CRBD,[17] the patient’s preoperative analgesic use,[18] and anxiety level.[19] In order to standardize the surgical technique and related conditions in our study, we only included open nephrectomy cases that were performed by a single surgical team through a subcostal (flank) incision at a single center. Only one patient in the QLB group had a non-displaced rib fracture caused by retractors. However, this patient was not excluded from the study because he did not experience severe pain during the early postoperative period. Some patients suffer from urinary CRBD in the postoperative period, and this also affects pain outcomes.[17] During the evaluation of patients’ postoperative VAS scores, special attention was given to excluding CRBD. To minimize bias in terms of results, patients who had used analgesics preoperatively were not included in the study. Studies indicate that patients with high preoperative anxiety are associated with more intense postoperative pain.[19] However, preoperative anxiety scales were not applied to the patients included in our study, and this has been added to the limitations section. Secondary outcomes in our study were determined as quality of recovery and length of hospital stay. These patient-related outcomes depend on many factors beyond perioperative anesthesia management and often involve complex processes. In order to avoid bias in the results of our study, we ensured that preoperative quality of recovery baseline values were similar across all groups.

Sindwani et al[20] reported that lateral QLB applied to renal transplant recipients significantly reduced the numerical rating scale score at 1, 4, 8, 12 and 24 hours postoperatively and that it can be used effectively as a part of multimodal analgesia. Our study also partially supports this result. In our study, VAS scores were found to be lower in patients who underwent open nephrectomy at 0 minutes, 30 minutes, 2 hours, 3 hours, 12 hours and 48 hours in patients who underwent lateral QLB. In the same study, they concluded that lateral QLB reduced postoperative fentanyl consumption in renal transplant recipients. In our study, no difference was found between the groups in terms of opioid consumption. However, unlike the flank incision in our study, researchers reported that patients who underwent a paramedian and modified Gibson incision extending from T10 to L1 in kidney transplant recipients may benefit more from lateral QLB (especially due to iliohypogastric nerve blockade).[20]

Kwak et al evaluated the analgesic efficacy of unilateral lateral (type 1) QLB in patients undergoing nephrectomy and found that patients’ total opioid consumption and first 24-hour pain scores were significantly lower in the lateral QLB patient group.[21] In this study, the authors included only laparoscopic nephrectomy patients. The distribution and the size of laparoscopic trocar incisions are different from flank incision. As is known, open surgeries can cause more intense pain and complications in the postoperative period. In the same study, recovery quality was measured with QoR-15 questionnaire at the 48th hour postoperatively, but no significant difference was found between the groups. In our study, although the QoR-15 questionnaire was found to be significantly higher in the block group on the 3rd postoperative day, we think that the recovery quality scores in the first 24 hours are more important to evaluate the effectiveness of the single injection block.

Saleh et al[22] compared postoperative analgesic efficacy in 3 different groups including transmuscular (type 3) QLB, subcostal posterior TAPB and control group in open nephrectomy patients. As a result of the study, it was determined that both block types were effective and acceptable in providing analgesia but type 3 QLB was more effective. In the study, fentanyl consumption as rescue analgesia in the postoperative period was found to be similar between the QLB group and the subcostal TAPB group (190.31 ± 44.48 mg vs 164.69 ± 27.35 mg), while it was lower compared to the control group (347.50 ± 63.64 mg). Previous studies have confirmed that ultrasound-guided TAPB is an effective method of analgesia for upper abdominal surgeries,[23] lower abdominal surgeries[24] and kidney transplantation with minimal side effects.[25] According to the Delphi nomenclature study, it was suggested that posterior TAPB has a similar effect to lateral QLB and both should be named as lateral QLB block.[5] Since additionally L1–3 and genitofemoral nerve can be blocked in transmuscular (type 3) QLB, it can theoretically provide more effective analgesia, but it is important that lateral (type 1) QLB is also effective (due to its similar effect area with posterior TAP) and can be a part of multimodal analgesia.

Another study by Murouchi et al investigated the interaction between serum concentrations of local anesthetics and the efficacy of QLB and TAPB.[26] They found higher serum concentrations of local anesthetics in TAPB than in QLB, along with better analgesia in QLB compared to TAPB. One possible explanation is that some local anesthetics after QLB move from the intermuscular space into the paravertebral space, reducing the systemic absorption of local anesthetics into the blood.[26] However, local anesthetic systemic toxicity after QLB remains a potential risk due to the dose of local anesthetics used and the vascularity of the area. Additionally, in patients undergoing nephrectomy, the QLB group showed a lower incidence of PONV compared to the control group.[14] Similarly, in our study, although we observed less PONV in the QLB group (30% vs 50%, respectively), there was no statistically significant difference (P = .2). This lack of significance is likely due to our relatively small sample size.

The QLB is a deep block, especially type 3, and therefore carries a higher risk of visceral (such as kidney) and nerve injury.[6] However, the lateral (type 1) QLB is a relatively superficial and easy-to-apply fascial plane block that can be used as part of multimodal analgesia in open nephrectomy cases. This may increase the success rate of this block and offer an advantage in terms of fewer complications.[6] While we did not observe a difference in postoperative morphine consumption, it is worth noting that it is linked to a shorter hospital stay, as well as lower levels of agitation and pain scores.

Our study has several limitations. The sample size in our study is relatively small. The preoperative anxiety level, a personal factor that could affect patients’ pain scores, was not measured. CRBD, which can also influence pain scores, was not assessed. Since QLB was applied to patients under anesthesia, block success and affected dermatomal area were not evaluated with pinprick test. In addition, patients’ VAS scores were not evaluated separately as dynamic and static.

In conclusion, it was observed that lateral QLB reduced postoperative VAS scores and the number of patients requiring bolus opioids in the first 1 hour of the postoperative period in open nephrectomy cases compared with the control group. In addition, postoperative agitation was significantly lower in the lateral QLB group. Therefore, lateral QLB may be an important part of multimodal analgesia in open nephrectomy patients. However, no significant difference was found between the groups in terms of total morphine consumption and quality of recovery. Prospective randomized studies comparing different types of QLB in a larger study population undergoing open nephrectomy are needed to confirm these results.
