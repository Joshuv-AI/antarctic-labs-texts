Machine learning versus clinical scoring systems for predicting poor wound healing after posterior surgery for thoracolumbar tuberculosis
Authors: Tuotuo Xiong, SiYan Huo, Ye Zhang, Wanyuan Qin, Mingyang Fang, Yong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePostoperative poor wound healing (PWH) is a significant complication following posterior surgery for thoracolumbar tuberculosis, leading to prolonged recovery and increased healthcare costs. Traditional prognostic markers often lack precision. This study aimed to develop and validate machine learning (ML)-based models to predict PWH and compare their performance with conventional logistic regression and established clinical scoring systems.MethodA retrospective analysis of 188 patients undergoing posterior debridement and internal fixation for thoracolumbar tuberculosis was conducted. Patients were randomly partitioned into a training set (70%, n = 132) and a test set (30%, n = 56). Four predictive models were constructed: Model I (Multivariate Logistic Regression), Model II (LASSO Regression), Model III (Random Forest), and Model IV (Artificial Neural Network). The performance of these models was compared against the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) and the Naples Prognostic Score (NPS) using the Area Under the Curve (AUC) and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA).ResultsThe incidence of PWH was analyzed alongside preoperative and intraoperative variables. LASSO regression identified six significant predictors: albumin, lymphocytes (%), C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cell (WBC) count, hemoglobin, and the number of instrumented levels. Based on these features, a nomogram (Model II) was developed. In the test set, the LASSO-based model achieved the highest predictive accuracy with an AUC of 0.879, significantly outperforming the traditional logistic regression model (AUC: 0.733), the Random Forest model (AUC: 0.860), and the Artificial Neural Network (AUC: 0.840). Furthermore, the LASSO model demonstrated superior discrimination compared to clinical scoring systems (PNI: 0.760; NPS: 0.746). DCA confirmed that the LASSO-based nomogram provided the highest clinical net benefit.ConclusionThe LASSO-based nomogram demonstrated superior predictive accuracy and clinical net benefit for PWH compared to traditional regression methods and established prognostic scores. For limited clinical cohorts, penalized regression techniques effectively mitigate overfitting risks and provide robust interpretability compared to highly parameterized algorithms. This validated nomogram serves as a practical and reliable tool for early risk stratification, thereby optimizing personalized perioperative management for patients with thoracolumbar tuberculosis.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is one of the oldest known diseases, affecting humans since prehistoric times, as evidenced by characteristic lesions found in Neolithic skeletal remains [1, 2]. Spinal tuberculosis, specifically known as Pott’s disease, accounts for approximately 50% of all musculoskeletal TB cases [3–6]. It is estimated that approximately one-quarter of the global population has been infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis, extrapulmonary manifestations occur in 20% of cases, and skeletal involvement is seen in nearly 10% of active patients. The spine is the most common site of skeletal involvement [3–6]. The onset of spinal tuberculosis is slow and insidious. Patients often present with atypical back pain. Kyphosis and severe neurological complications may occur if not diagnosed and treated in time. Paraplegia is the most serious complication after nervous system damage, with an incidence of 10–30% [5, 7–9]. The thoracic spine (40–50%) and lumbar spine (35–45%) are common sites of spinal involvement [10]. In clinical practice, for patients with spinal tuberculosis who have clear surgical indications, standardized anti-tuberculosis drug treatment should be performed first, and then surgical treatment, including abscess drainage, lesion debridement, bone graft fusion, and internal fixation placement, etc. Thus, it can improve the efficacy of anti-tuberculosis drugs, strengthen the control of tuberculosis, promote bone graft fusion, and reconstruct the stability of the spine [11–13]. TB bacteria mainly attack people with poor basic conditions, such as patients with immunosuppression, malnutrition, organ dysfunction, diabetes, or a history of smoking and alcohol abuse [14, 15]. Therefore, such patients are more prone to various postoperative complications compared to patients with spinal degenerative diseases [16, 17]. Poor healing of wound (PWH) is a common but serious postoperative complication that serves as a major risk factor for surgical site infection (SSI). PWH can lead to internal fixation failure, pseudarthrosis, and osteomyelitis, often necessitating secondary revision surgery. Consequently, this prolongs hospital stays, increases economic burdens, and elevates mortality rates [18–20].

Controlling systemic toxicity and optimizing nutritional status are pivotal in the management of spinal tuberculosis. Previous studies have demonstrated that nutritional status significantly influences postoperative wound healing [21]. Following effective control of tubercular toxicity, preoperative nutritional assessment becomes critical. Therefore, patients should undergo a comprehensive preoperative nutritional status assessment, and nutritional, immune-inflammatory and surgery-related markers should be used to evaluate the possibility of poor postoperative wound prognosis. The Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002), Patient-Generated Subjective Global assessment (PG-SGA) and other assessment methods have been widely used in clinical practice. However, these methods are still limited because they include multiple subjective factors and require expertise for accurate assessment. The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) based on serum albumin and peripheral blood lymphocyte count was originally used to assess preoperative nutritional status, surgical risk, and postoperative complications in surgical patients. It has been shown to be a prognostic biomarker for postoperative spinal infection, postoperative delirium, solid tumors, and cardiovascular disease [22]. The Naples Prognostic Score (NPS), first proposed by Gennaro et al. [23], has been identified as an independent prognostic factor for patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery. It has demonstrated good prognostic value in gastrointestinal tumors [24]. However, the predictive value of PNI and NPS for wound healing outcomes following posterior instrumentation and debridement for thoracolumbar tuberculosis remains unclear.

Currently, while studies have explored risk factors associated with postoperative wound healing, there is a lack of comprehensive, individualized evaluation methods for accurate prediction. Postoperative wound healing is influenced by complex, confounding risk factors that are difficult to predict preoperatively using traditional methods alone. Standard logistic regression analysis, while common, may fail to fully capture the intricate interactions between risk factors. In contrast, machine learning (ML) techniques offer significant advantages over traditional statistical methods. ML algorithms utilize powerful computational capabilities to automatically detect complex, nonlinear relationships between features without the strict assumptions required by conventional statistics. This allows ML models to handle high-dimensional data and multicollinearity more effectively. For example, deep learning models can be used to extract abstract feature representations of the data through multi-level nonlinear transformations, enabling more efficient processing of highly correlated features. Therefore, we introduced the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), random forest (RF), and artificial neural network (ANN) to provide more powerful support for the selection of risk factors and model optimization, and more effectively analyze and model the prognosis risk of wound healing after posterior thoracolumbar tuberculosis surgery.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify risk factors for poor wound healing (PWH) following posterior instrumentation and debridement for thoracolumbar tuberculosis. We sought to establish and compare prediction models using both machine learning algorithms and traditional logistic regression. Furthermore, we compared the predictive performance of these ML models against widely used clinical scoring systems, specifically PNI and NPS. Ultimately, this study aims to facilitate the early identification of high-risk patients, enabling timely intervention and the development of individualized treatment strategies.

Method

Case data

This study retrospectively analyzed clinical data from 188 patients with thoracolumbar tuberculosis who underwent primary posterior debridement, bone graft fusion, and internal fixation at our institution between January 2019 and January 2024. All surgeries were performed by a single senior surgeon experienced in complex spinal procedures.

Inclusion criteria: (1) patients undergoing primary surgery for thoracolumbar tuberculosis; (2) availability of complete medical records, including preoperative demographics, laboratory and imaging findings, and intraoperative data; (3) confirmed histopathological diagnosis of thoracolumbar tuberculosis; (4) effective control of preoperative systemic toxicity; (5) susceptibility to the standard chemotherapy regimen (2HRZE/6H2R2Z2E2).

Exclusion criteria: (1) postoperative pathological confirmation of non-tuberculous lesions; (2) diagnosis of drug-resistant tuberculosis; (3) history of prior surgical intervention for thoracolumbar tuberculosis; (4) age < 18 years; (5) severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction; (6) coexisting hematological disorders affecting baseline laboratory parameters.

Perioperative management

Preoperative management

Based on established clinical guidelines, all patients received a standardized preoperative chemotherapy regimen (2HRZE/6H2R2Z2E2) for 2 to 4 weeks. Daily dosages included: isoniazid (300 mg), rifampicin (450 mg), pyrazinamide (1500 mg), and ethambutol (750 mg). The primary goal was to achieve effective control of systemic toxicity. Patients with comorbidities such as diabetes, coronary heart disease, and hypertension received multidisciplinary consultations to optimize their physiological parameters for surgery. Surgical intervention was scheduled once dynamic monitoring confirmed the stabilization of the patient's condition [25].

Surgical procedure

Following general anesthesia and endotracheal intubation, the patient was placed in the prone position with the abdomen hanging free. The involved level was localized using C-arm fluoroscopy. A midline incision was made centered over the lesion. Subperiosteal dissection was performed to expose the bilateral paraspinal muscles via a posterior midline approach (for unilateral lesions, a contralateral intermuscular approach was utilized). The spinous processes, laminae, and superior and inferior articular processes of the involved and adjacent healthy vertebrae were exposed. Pedicle screws were inserted into one or two healthy vertebrae adjacent to the involved segment. In cases of severe vertebral invasion, screw placement was limited to the unaffected side. Titanium rods were installed for provisional stabilization. Based on the extent of bony destruction, a transforaminal or transpedicular approach was utilized to perform thorough debridement of the anterior and lateral columns. This included the removal of pus, sequestra, residual disc material, and caseous necrosis. Debridement continued until healthy, bleeding bone was exposed and adequate spinal canal decompression was achieved. Interbody fusion was performed using structural iliac bone blocks or titanium mesh cages filled with autologous morcelized bone (or allograft). Local chemotherapy was administered by mixing the graft with isoniazid (0.3 g) and streptomycin (1 g) or rifampin (0.3 g). Kyphosis correction was achieved through posterior compression and instrumentation. Intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopy confirmed satisfactory spinal alignment and implant position, after which the instrumentation was finally locked. Bilateral drainage tubes were placed, and the incision was closed in layers. Resected specimens were submitted for histopathological and bacteriological examination.

Routine postoperative management

All patients continued the standardized chemotherapy regimen (2HRZE/6H2R2Z2E2) for a total duration of 18 to 24 months. Postoperative prophylactic antibiotics (cefuroxime sodium, 1.5 g twice daily) were administered for 3 days. Drains were removed when the 24-h drainage volume decreased to < 40 mL. Postoperative anteroposterior and lateral spinal radiographs were obtained following drain removal. Patients were encouraged to ambulate wearing a thoracolumbar brace one week postoperatively and to gradually resume daily activities.

Data collection

Poor wound healing (PWH) was defined as delayed healing occurring within one month postoperatively, characterized by wound stagnation, exudation, hematoma, or dehiscence, in the absence of surgical site infection (SSI) (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Postoperative poor wound healing

Based on literature review and clinical experience, data were categorized into three groups: preoperative demographics, laboratory findings, and surgical variables. Demographic data included sex, age, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, history of pulmonary tuberculosis), and disease duration of spinal tuberculosis.

Preoperative laboratory samples were collected within 48 h prior to surgery. Parameters analyzed included: white blood cell (WBC) count, red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (Hb), platelet (PLT) count, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), neutrophils (count and percentage), lymphocytes (count and percentage), monocytes (count and percentage), prealbumin, albumin, total cholesterol, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and C-reactive protein (CRP).

Surgical variables included operative time, number of instrumented levels, and estimated blood loss (EBL). The PNI was calculated using the formula: albumin (g/L) + 5 × total lymphocyte count (109/L). Modeling methods and processes

The cohort of 188 patients was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%, n = 132) and a test set (30%, n = 56). To ensure reproducibility, a random seed was set prior to data partitioning. Initially, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between the two sets to assess homogeneity. The absence of statistically significant differences confirmed the comparability of the groups, serving as the basis for subsequent model development and validation. The overall study workflow is illustrated in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Development and validation of models based on logistic regression and machine learning

Development and validation of models based on logistic regression and machine learning

Traditional logistic regression modeling

Within the training set, univariate logistic regression was performed to screen for potential risk factors. Variables demonstrating statistical significance (P < 0.05) were subsequently included in a multivariable stepwise logistic regression analysis to identify independent risk factors associated with PWH. Stepwise selection was performed based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The traditional prediction model (Model I) was constructed using the identified independent risk factors and validated in the test set. The discriminative ability of the model was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the area under the curve (AUC). Model calibration was evaluated using calibration curves.

Machine learning modeling

Three machine learning models were developed using the training set: (1) Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression; (2) Random Forest (RF); and (3) Artificial Neural Network (ANN). The LASSO algorithm was employed to select significant predictors and prevent overfitting through regularization, thereby enhancing model interpretability. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined via tenfold cross-validation using the glmnet package. We selected the λ value that minimized the misclassification error. Subsequently, a prognostic nomogram was constructed based on the features selected by LASSO using the rms package. For the Random Forest (RF) model, we utilized the ranger and caret packages. Hyperparameters were optimized using a grid search strategy to identify the best combination (e.g., number of trees and variables per split). The ANN was implemented using the keras package. To prevent overfitting given the limited sample size, the network architecture was intentionally kept concise, comprising an input layer, a single hidden layer utilizing Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation functions, and an output layer with a sigmoid activation function for binary classification. The model was compiled using the binary cross-entropy loss function and optimized via the Adam optimizer. Crucially, an early stopping mechanism was employed during the training phase; 20% of the training data was allocated as an internal validation split, and training was halted when the validation loss ceased to improve over a predefined number of epochs, thereby prioritizing generalizability over excessive node addition or infinite iterations.

Model performance was evaluated in the test set using: (1) the C-statistic (area under the ROC curve, AUC); (2) comprehensive diagnostic metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive likelihood ratio (PLR), and negative likelihood ratio (NLR); and (3) Decision Curve Analysis (DCA). DCA was performed to assess the clinical utility of the models by quantifying the net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities.

Statistical method

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on normality. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous data. Normally distributed variables were compared using the independent samples t-test, while non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test (or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate). Univariate and multivariable stepwise logistic regression analyses, along with machine learning algorithms, were employed to construct prediction models for PWH. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). The differences in AUCs between the prediction models were statistically compared using DeLong's test. Calibration curves were plotted to assess model calibration, and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was utilized to evaluate clinical net benefit. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.3.0.

Regarding demographics, the training and test sets comprised 81 (61.4%) and 34 (60.7%) males, respectively. The mean age was 48.6 and 53.6 years, respectively. The median disease duration of spinal tuberculosis was 4 months and 6.5 months, respectively. The mean BMI was 21.7 kg/m2 and 22.3 kg/m2, respectively.Preoperative comorbidities and lifestyle factors were comparable between groups. Specifically, a history of pulmonary tuberculosis was present in 43 (32.6%) and 25 (44.6%) patients, respectively. Smoking history was reported in 37 (28.0%) and 18 (32.1%) patients, while alcohol consumption was noted in 21 (15.9%) and 12 (21.4%) cases. Regarding comorbidities, diabetes mellitus occurred in 11 (8.3%) and 3 (5.4%) patients, and hypertension in 12 (9.1%) and 5 (8.9%) patients, respectively. Conventional logistic regression modeling (Model I)

Significant predictors identified by univariate and multivariable stepwise logistic regression in the training set are presented in Tables 3 and 4. Univariate analysis indicated statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) between the PWH and control groups for the following variables: age, white blood cell (WBC) count, hemoglobin, MCV, MCH, MCHC, platelet count, neutrophils (%), lymphocytes (%), neutrophil count, albumin, prealbumin, monocyte count, ESR, CRP, number of instrumented levels, and estimated blood loss. (Note: PNI and NPS were reserved for model comparison and were excluded from this screening). Subsequent multivariable stepwise logistic regression identified four significant independent predictors: lymphocytes (%) (OR 0.626, 95% CI 0.446–0.879, P = 0.007), albumin (OR 0.613, 95% CI 0.400–0.941, P = 0.025), CRP (OR 1.034, 95% CI 1.000–1.069, P = 0.050), and MCH (OR 0.556, 95% CI 0.363–0.852, P = 0.007). Lymphocytes (%), albumin, and MCH were identified as protective factors. Specifically, for every unit increase in lymphocyte percentage, the risk of PWH decreased by 37.4% (1–0.626). Similarly, unit increases in albumin and MCH were associated with risk reductions of 38.7% and 44.4%, respectively. Conversely, CRP was identified as a risk factor, with the risk of PWH increasing by 3.4% for every unit increase. Model I was constructed based on these four independent predictors. In the test set, Model I achieved an AUC of 0.733 (95% CI: 0.505–0.962), indicating acceptable discriminative ability for predicting postoperative wound prognosis (Figs. 6 and 9). Discussion

In this retrospective study, we employed both machine learning (ML) algorithms and traditional logistic regression to investigate risk factors associated with postoperative poor wound healing (PWH) in patients undergoing posterior debridement and internal fixation for thoracolumbar tuberculosis. We integrated comprehensive clinical data, including preoperative demographics, laboratory findings, and intraoperative surgical variables, to construct predictive models. Furthermore, we systematically compared the predictive performance of our developed ML models against the traditional logistic regression model and established clinical scoring systems, specifically the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) and the Neutrophil-to-Platelet Score (NPS).

We developed four distinct predictive models: Model I (conventional logistic regression), Model II (LASSO regression), Model III (Random Forest), and Model IV (Artificial Neural Network). Validation in the test set demonstrated that while all four models exhibited robust predictive capabilities, the LASSO-based model (Model II) yielded the highest AUC, indicating superior predictive performance. This superiority is likely attributable to the L1-regularization (L1-norm penalty) utilized by LASSO. This mechanism promotes model sparsity by shrinking the coefficients of less relevant features to zero, thereby effectively performing feature selection. By reducing model complexity and eliminating redundant variables, LASSO minimizes the risk of overfitting, which is particularly advantageous in clinical datasets with multiple potential predictors.

The predictive models established in this study provide clinicians with valuable tools for the early identification of patients susceptible to PWH, thereby facilitating timely preoperative interventions and personalized treatment strategies.

Regarding specific predictors, Model I identified lymphocytes (%), albumin, CRP, and MCH as independent risk factors. Model II expanded this list to include white blood cell (WBC) count, hemoglobin, lymphocytes (%), albumin, number of instrumented levels, and CRP. Similarly, the Random Forest algorithm (Model III) prioritized WBC count, neutrophils (%), lymphocytes (%), CRP, number of instrumented levels, and hemoglobin as top-ranking variables. Given their recurrent identification across multiple modeling algorithms and their clinical significance, the subsequent discussion will primarily focus on four key predictors: albumin, lymphocytes (%), number of instrumented levels, and CRP.

Epidemiological and clinical characteristics of spinal tuberculosis

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, remains a critical global public health challenge. As one of the oldest known diseases, it ranks among the top ten causes of death worldwide and is the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent, surpassing HIV/AIDS [26, 27]. Osteoarticular tuberculosis constitutes 1%–3% of all TB cases, with spinal tuberculosis being the most prevalent form, accounting for approximately 50% [28]. Also known as Pott’s disease, spinal tuberculosis was first described by Percivall Pott in 1771 [29]. The disease predominantly affects the thoracolumbar region, typically involving the vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs; isolated involvement of the posterior elements is rare [7]. Clinically, spinal tuberculosis is characterized by an insidious onset, slow progression, and non-specific symptoms. Coupled with the low sensitivity of culture tests, these factors contribute to high rates of missed or delayed diagnosis. Furthermore, treatment complexity is often exacerbated by patient-related factors, including malnutrition, compromised immunity, and poor adherence to anti-tuberculosis chemotherapy [30–32].

Surgical treatment of spinal tuberculosis

Although 82–95% of patients achieve favorable clinical outcomes with anti-tuberculosis chemotherapy alone, a subset remains at risk for progressive neurological impairment. Thoracic spinal tuberculosis, in particular, is associated with high rates of morbidity and mortality; consequently, surgical intervention remains a cornerstone of management for these complex cases [33]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that timely surgical intervention significantly improves the prognosis of patients with spinal tuberculosis [34].

Surgical intervention is indicated in the presence of specific complications, including: (1) intractable pain severely affecting quality of life; (2) uncontrolled progression of the tuberculous lesion (abscess); (3) spinal cord compression resulting in sensory or motor deficits; and (4) spinal deformity or segmental instability. Surgical procedures were categorized as either radical or non-radical (diagnostic). In this study, radical surgery was defined as procedures involving focal debridement/curettage, decompression, internal fixation, or bone graft fusion. Patients undergoing only non-radical (diagnostic) procedures were excluded from the analysis.

Nutritional assessment and prognostic scores

Malnutrition is a well-established risk factor for impaired wound healing and postoperative complications following orthopedic surgery [35, 36]. Spinal tuberculosis acts as a chronic wasting disease, placing a significant metabolic burden on patients. The chronic inflammatory and immune responses associated with TB infection induce a hypercatabolic state, leading to increased energy expenditure. However, nutritional replenishment is often inadequate or delayed due to a lack of routine monitoring. Consequently, preoperative malnutrition is highly prevalent in this population [37, 38], underscoring the critical need to assess and optimize the nutritional status of patients prior to surgery.

Current nutritional assessment tools are generally categorized into subjective and objective methods. Subjective assessments, such as the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002) and Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA), rely on patient-reported changes in dietary intake, functional capacity, and clinical symptoms. Previous studies have demonstrated that these scales correlate significantly with TB outcomes and serve as valuable prognostic indicators [39]. In contrast, objective evaluation methods—utilizing preoperative laboratory biomarkers and anthropometric data—minimize recall bias and provide a more quantifiable and accurate assessment of nutritional status.

The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), calculated from serum albumin and peripheral blood lymphocyte counts, was originally established by Onodera et al. [40]. to assess the immunonutritional status and surgical risk of patients undergoing gastrointestinal surgery. In recent years, PNI has emerged as a valuable prognostic indicator for various malignancies, including gastrointestinal, gynecological, and lung cancers. Furthermore, its utility has extended to non-malignant conditions, such as fractures, heart failure, and cerebral infarction.

The Naples Prognostic Score (NPS), which integrates serum albumin, total cholesterol, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), was first introduced by Galizia et al. [23] for colorectal cancer prognostication. It has since been extensively validated across a spectrum of malignancies, including pancreatic, endometrial, gastric, and esophageal cancers. However, the application of PNI and NPS in spinal tuberculosis remains limited, particularly regarding the evaluation of postoperative wound healing.

Our results indicated that the PNI achieved a slightly higher AUC compared to the NPS (0.760 vs. 0.746). While both scoring systems demonstrated moderate predictive accuracy (Fig. 10), their performance was notably inferior to the machine learning-based models. Consequently, the ML models developed in this study demonstrate superior applicability for patients with thoracolumbar tuberculosis and warrant further clinical validation.

Albumin

Albumin, synthesized exclusively by the liver, is the most abundant protein in human plasma. Functioning as a monomeric multi-domain macromolecule, it serves as the primary determinant of plasma colloid osmotic pressure and a key regulator of body fluid distribution [41]. Clinically, it is widely regarded as the most reliable marker for assessing nutritional status [42].

Wound healing is a complex biological process reliant on fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis, both of which are significantly influenced by albumin levels. Bohl et al., analyzing the NSQIP database, demonstrated that hypoalbuminemia is associated with an increased risk of wound dehiscence, surgical site infection (SSI), and urinary tract infections [43]. Consistent with these findings, our study identified albumin as an independent predictor of PWH.

Radical debridement, a critical component of spinal tuberculosis surgery, often involves significant surgical trauma, prolonged operative time, and blood loss, all of which contribute to perioperative albumin depletion. Furthermore, the physiological stress and systemic inflammatory response triggered by surgery increase capillary permeability. This leads to the extravasation of intravascular albumin into the interstitial space—a phenomenon known as the transcapillary escape rate (TER)—further exacerbating hypoalbuminemia. Hypoalbuminemia results in reduced oncotic pressure, causing tissue edema and interstitial fluid accumulation at the wound site [44]. This edema compromises local tissue perfusion and integrity, hindering incision closure. Moreover, as both innate and adaptive immune responses are albumin-dependent, low albumin levels impair local immunity, rendering the poorly healing wound susceptible to bacterial colonization.

Exogenous albumin supplementation can effectively restore colloid osmotic pressure, enhance plasma antioxidant capacity, and modulate the inflammatory response [45]. Therefore, strict preoperative monitoring of albumin levels and timely nutritional intervention are essential strategies to prevent adverse postoperative wound outcomes.

Lymphocyte percentage

The immune response to tuberculosis (TB) is primarily cell-mediated, involving T lymphocytes that play a pivotal role in host defense against Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection [46, 47]. Previous studies have demonstrated an inverse correlation between peripheral CD4+ T cell counts and disease severity; specifically, lower T cell counts are associated with more advanced disease progression. Fan et al. reported that lymphocyte subsets serve as reliable indicators of immune function in patients with spinal tuberculosis. They observed that significant reductions in CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T lymphocytes correlate with more extensive disease involvement [48].

Consistent with these findings, our study identified preoperative lymphocyte percentage as an independent predictor of PWH. We hypothesize that uncontrolled, active spinal tuberculosis drives a chronic immune response, leading to the continuous consumption and eventual depletion of lymphocytes. Therefore, strict adherence to standardized preoperative anti-tuberculosis chemotherapy protocols is essential. By effectively reducing the bacterial load, chemotherapy alleviates the systemic immune burden and promotes functional restoration. This, in turn, enhances the patient's capacity to tolerate surgical stress and minimizes the risk of postoperative wound complications.

Number of instrumented levels

The surgical strategy, including the extent of fixation, is determined by the severity of symptoms and the radiological extent of the tuberculous lesion. When lesions involve multiple vertebral levels, the number of instrumented levels inevitably increases. This necessitates a longer surgical incision and more extensive soft tissue dissection, resulting in greater surgical trauma and a consequently higher burden on wound healing.

Wound healing is a complex, dynamic biological cascade comprising hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and tissue remodeling [49]. Extensive wounds require increased fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis, thereby demanding a higher consumption of substrates, particularly albumin. The correlation between incision length and healing outcomes is well-documented across surgical disciplines. For instance, a systematic review by Noel et al. [50] involving 1,471 colorectal resection patients demonstrated the superiority of smaller laparoscopic incisions (6.9 cm) over open incisions (17.6 cm). Similarly, Wang et al. [51] reported that shorter, minimally invasive incisions for Achilles tendon rupture repair yielded better recovery prognoses compared to traditional approaches.

Minimizing incision length to reduce surgical insult is a fundamental concept in minimally invasive surgery. Notably, compared to typical degenerative spinal disorders, surgery for thoracolumbar tuberculosis is associated with prolonged operative time, substantial blood loss, and greater physiological stress. Therefore, while ensuring radical debridement and biomechanical stability, surgeons must strive to minimize the extent of surgical invasion. Limiting the number of instrumented levels, where clinically feasible, is a crucial strategy to reduce surgical trauma and mitigate the risk of PWH.

C-reactive protein (CRP)

C-reactive protein (CRP), an acute-phase reactant synthesized by the liver, serves as a highly sensitive biomarker for systemic inflammation and tissue injury. During infection or surgical stress, CRP levels rise significantly earlier than clinical signs such as fever or leukocytosis. Regarding wound healing, Cao et al. demonstrated that perioperative CRP dynamics effectively monitor abdominal wound recovery following cesarean sections; specifically, patients with sustained high CRP levels faced a significantly higher risk of PWH compared to those with normal levels [52].

In the specific context of tuberculosis, CRP serves as a reliable indicator of disease activity. A meta-analysis indicated that CRP possesses high sensitivity for detecting active TB, particularly in HIV-coinfected patients [53, 54]. Similarly, Kang et al. observed significantly elevated CRP levels in patients with active pulmonary tuberculosis, correlating these levels with disease severity [55]. Furthermore, elevated CRP has been implicated in the poor prognosis of various malignancies, including prostate cancer [56] and epithelial ovarian cancer, particularly in advanced-stage cases [57].

Consistent with these findings, our study identified preoperative CRP as an independent risk factor for PWH. Mechanistically, we hypothesize that elevated CRP reflects a hyper-inflammatory state characterized by the excessive infiltration of neutrophils at the wound site [58]. While neutrophils release Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs)—DNA–protein complexes designed to trap and kill pathogens [59, 60]—aberrant or excessive NET formation can be detrimental. Evidence suggests that dysregulated NETosis impairs cellular signaling essential for repair [61, 62]. Furthermore, the cytotoxic histones and DNA components within NETs can cause collateral tissue damage, thereby disrupting the highly coordinated wound healing process [63, 64].

Consistency and robustness of predictors

Although slight variations were observed in the specific feature subsets selected by different algorithms—likely attributable to the distinct mechanisms by which Logistic Regression, LASSO, and Random Forest handle multicollinearity—the core predictors (albumin, CRP, and lymphocyte percentage) remained consistent across all models. This recurrence across diverse methodological approaches reinforces the clinical robustness of these markers and suggests that they represent genuine pathophysiological risks rather than statistical artifacts.

Conclusion

This study successfully introduced machine learning algorithms to predict postoperative poor wound healing (PWH) in patients with thoracolumbar tuberculosis. Our results demonstrate that the ML-based models significantly outperform both traditional logistic regression and established clinical prognostic scores (PNI and NPS) in terms of predictive accuracy and discrimination. Notably, the LASSO-based nomogram (Model II) exhibited the optimal performance. This model incorporates six key predictors: white blood cell (WBC) count, hemoglobin, lymphocytes (%), albumin, CRP, and the number of instrumented levels, providing a precise tool for estimating the probability of PWH. The clinical application of this nomogram facilitates early risk stratification, thereby enabling more accurate and personalized perioperative management for patients undergoing posterior surgery for thoracolumbar tuberculosis.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, our analysis focused primarily on preoperative and intraoperative variables; the impact of dynamic postoperative changes was not assessed. Therefore, large-scale, multi-center prospective studies are warranted to further validate these models.
