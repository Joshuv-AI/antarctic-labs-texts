Ultrasound-assisted hot water extraction of yellow leaves of Pu’er tea polysaccharides: Extraction process, characterization, antioxidant and hypoglycemic activity
Authors: Ao Zhao, Lina Sun, Fu Xu, Wendie Gan, Biaoying Guo, Lianzhou Jiang, Xia Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Pu’er tea polysaccharides, Ultrasound-assisted hot water extraction, Structural characterization, Antioxidant and hypoglycemic activity

Introduction

Tea is a traditional Chinese beverage with a long 5,000-year history in China [1], which is known to be rich in polyphenols, polysaccharides, caffeine, theaflavin, alkaloids, and many other useful chemicals. Tea was first mentioned in Shen Nong's Herbal Classic, the earliest extant work on Eastern herbal medicine and the foundation of Traditional Chinese Medicine, dating from around 100 BCE. Its primary function was attributed to its ability to remove toxins from the body[2,3]. The utilisation of lower-grade tea for diabetes management has a historical precedent in East Asia, particularly in countries such as China, Korea and Japan[2,4,5]. Zhou et al. (2007) [6] found that aqueous tea extracts, unrefined tea polysaccharides (TPS), and the specific TPS portion could markedly reduce fasting blood glucose and glycosylated serum protein levels in diabetic mice induced by alloxan. Moreover, TPS have been demonstrated to possess antioxidant properties[7]. As a result, researchers have been more interested in TPS.

Yellow leaves of Pu'er tea are mature and coarse, separated during screening to meet production standards and classified as lower-grade Pu'er tea leaves. Due to their unappealing look, flavor, and scent, the yellow leaves of Pu'er tea are usually separated and sold at lower prices or used as fertilizer, resulting in low rates of usage.

The levels of key bioactive compounds (including caffeine and tea polyphenols) decline as tea leaves become coarser, whereas TPS content shows an inverse trend[8]. Coarse tea leaves contain elevated levels of TPS, making Pu'er yellow leaves a promising source for developing antioxidant and blood sugar-lowering polysaccharide products.

In the industrial extraction of natural polysaccharides, Hot Water Extraction (HWE) is widely used due to its simple operation and low equipment requirements. However, this method relies on high temperatures and prolonged extraction conditions, and its efficiency is significantly affected by factors such as extraction time, temperature, and feed/liquid ratio. This drastic extraction environment not only reduces the yield of polysaccharides, but also affects their bioactivity due to thermal degradation leading to breakage of polysaccharide molecular chains. In the last few years, Ultrasound-Assisted Extraction (UAE) has been emerged as a focal point of study. because of its high efficiency and energy saving. This method employs ultrasonic cavitation to create localized high-pressure, high-temperature conditions and micro-jets, which disrupt plant cell walls and facilitate solvent infiltration, thereby enhancing mass transfer effectiveness[9]. When compared to HWE, UAE ‌demonstrates superior performance‌ in terms of ‌shorter extraction duration‌, ‌lower energy consumption‌ (40–60 % reduction), and ‌precise temperature control‌ capabilities.

It was shown that UAE increased the yield by 10 %-30 % in the extraction of ginkgo biloba polysaccharides[10], jujube polysaccharides[11], and maca polysaccharides[12], and moreover, the antioxidative capacity of the products demonstrated a notable superiority over that of the HWE method[13]. Compared to conventional hot water extraction, the ultrasound-assisted hot water extraction (UA-HWE) was used to increase the yield of polysaccharides of Morus alba mycelium from 4.2 % to 7.8 %, which was an increase of 85.7 %[14]. In this research, the yield of yellow leaves of Pu'er tea polysaccharides was established as the evaluation criterion. A one − way experimental approach was employed to systematically optimize the parameters of the UA-HWE process. After systematic separation and purification of the Pu'er tea polysaccharides prepared under the optimal process conditions, the purified fractions were structurally characterized by modern analytical techniques. The antioxidant effect and hypoglycemic function of the purified polysaccharides were further evaluated through in − vitro experiments. This investigation is chiefly intended to provide a theoretical system that may establish UA-HWE method of coarse tea polysaccharides and expedite the advancement of antioxidants and hypoglycemic products based on polysaccharides.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

The yellow leaves of Pu’er tea was delivered by Yunnan Shuangjiang Mengku Tea Co., Ltd (Lincang, Yunnan, China). Glucose (Glc), fucose (Fuc), rhamnose (Rha), mannose (Man), arabinose (Ara), galactose (Gal), galacturonic acid (GalA), 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), α-amylase (A3176, from porcine pancreas, Type VI-B, 16 U/mg) and α-glucosidase (A7095, from Aspergillus niger, 260 U/mL, aqueous solution) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). Shanghai Bo'er Chemical Reagents Co.,Ltd. (Shanghai, China) supplied all additional reagents and chemicals of analytical quality.

Ultrasound-assisted hot water extraction

A 10 g of dried Pu'er tea powder was dispersed in deionized water at a ratio of 1:25 (w/v). Afer being stirred for 1 h, the solution was extracted by ultrasonic treatment for 40 min at an ultrasonic temperature of 70 °C and an ultrasonic power of 500 W. Then, the solution was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was collected, after that, anhydrous ethanol was added to precipitate the polysaccharide, and centrifuged again to separate the precipitate. Finally, the precipitate was subjected to freeze − drying to acquire the crude extract of Pu’er tea polysaccharide (PTPS).The concentration of PTPS was measured by the phenol–sulfuric acid assay.

Single-factor experiments

The effects of solid- to-liquid ratio, ultrasonic temperature, ultrasonic power and ultrasonic time on the yield of PTPS were determined in a one-way test by weighing 10 g of Pu’er tea powder. The one-factor test conditions were as follows: solid- to-liquid ratio of 1:10, 1:15, 1:20, 1:25, 1:30; ultrasonic temperature of 50 ℃, 60 ℃, 70 ℃, 80 ℃, 90 ℃; ultrasonic power of 200 W, 300 W, 400 W, 500 W, 600 W; and ultrasonic time of 20 min, 30 min, 40 min, 50 min, 60 min.

PTPS purification

The unrefined polysaccharide underwent many rinses with acetone and ethanol, and was subsequently combined with D101 macroporous resin. Subsequent decolorization was located in a water bath shaker (120 rpm, 50 °C, 5 h). After undergoing filtration and concentration, a solution of Saveg was introduced to the mixture (1:3, v/v). The sample was first mixed thoroughly with a magnetic stirrer for 15 min. Afterwards, centrifugation was executed at 4000 rpm for 15 min. The clear supernate obtained was then filtered (3500 Da) for 48 h, lyophilized.

In addition, 20 mL of the sample with a concentration of 25 mg/mL was purified using a DEAE-52 cellulose column with dimensions of 2.0 cm × 20 cm. The eluent consisted of NaCl solutions with different concentrations (0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.5 M), flowing at a rate of 1 mL/min. The five gained polysaccharide fractions were designated as PTPS-1, PTPS-2, PTPS-3, PTPS-4, and PTPS-5. Furthermore, PTPS-4 and PTPS-5 underwent an additional purification step using a Sephadex G-200 gel chromatograph (1.5 cm × 75 cm) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. This allowed for the collection of highly intense fractions with specific molecular weights, specifically PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A. Finally, the eluents were subjected to freeze-drying. Three analytical methods were employed to quantify different components of PTPS: overall polysaccharides were measured by the carbolic-sulfuric acid assay, the amount of protein was determined using Bradford assay, and uronic acids were analyzed via the m-hydroxydiphenyl method.

Molecular weight (Mw)

The Mw of PTPS were determined by high-performance size exclusion chromatography (HPGPC, Shimadzu LC-10A HPLC system, Shimadzu Co., Japan) with a gel chromatography column and a refractive index detector. Dextran was utilized as the benchmark standard, and the method for calculating molecular weight was constructed from the calibration curve.

Monosaccharide content

The monosaccharide content of PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A was quantitatively detected using high-performance anion exchange chromatography (HPAEC) following the procedures [15], with minor adjustments. PTPS (5 mg) was dissolved in trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, 3 mol/L, 2 mL). Following hydrolysis at 120 ℃ for 3 h, the resulting hydrolysate was subjected to a stream of nitrogen for purification. Subsequently, 5 mL of deionized water was added and thoroughly mixed. The liquid portion was collected by spinning it in a centrifuge at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The solution was ‌filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane before analysis. Reference monosaccharides (fucose, rhamnose, arabinose, galactose, glucose, mannose, and galacturonic acid) were used as calibration standards. Monosaccharide composition was determined by HPAEC on a Shimadzu LC-10A HPLC system (Shimadzu Corporation, Japan). The separation column employed was Dionex CarbopacTM PA10 with dimensions of 4 mm × 250 mm. The analysis was conducted under the specified conditions. The mobile phase comprised three components: mobile phase A (deionized water), mobile phase B (sodium hydroxide-sodium acetate mixture), and mobile phase C (sodium hydroxide). Chromatographic separation proceeded at 1.0 mL/min flow rate with constant 30℃ column temperature.

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR)

The dehydrated PTPS powder was combined with KBr powder (1:100, w/w). And the mixture was compressed into a 1 mm pellet. The FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) was applied to measure the spectra. The wavelength interval of the spectrometer was 4000–––400 cm−1.

Analysis of methylation

The methylation protocol was derived from the existing literature, with several alterations made[16]. The galacturonic acid underwent reduction using the carbodiimide-sodium borohydride technique. The PTPS solution, with a concentration of 5.0 mg/mL, was put to a test tube with a volume of 1 mL. Next, 200 µL of a 0.2 mol/L solution of 2-morpholinopropanesulfonic acid (MOPS) and 200 µL of a carbodimide solution (500 mg/mL) were introduced into the test tube. The solutions were completely blended and left to incubate for 2 h under ambient temperature, prior to the addition of 1 mL of a 4 mol/L imidazole solution. The answer was partitioned into two identical segments. Sodium borohydride solution (30 mg/mL, 1 mL) and sodium borodeuteride solution (30 mg/mL, 1 mL) was added respectively. After undergoing a reaction at room temperature for 3 h, an additional 200 µL of acetic acid was introduced to end the reaction individually. The solution underwent dialysis using deionized water for a duration of 48 h, followed by freeze drying.

The lyophilized powder (5 mg) was dissolved in 500 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution. Subsequently, 50 µL of a NaOH/DMSO solution (120 mg/mL) was introduced and mixed with 1 mL of methyl iodide for a duration of 1 h in order to perform methylation. In addition, a volume of 1 mL of ultrapure water and 500 µL of dichloromethane were used to extract methylated derivatives thrice. The dichloromethane layer was then evaporated until it was completely dry. A solution of trifluoroacetic acid (100 µL, 2 mol/L) was introduced and the reaction was closed at 121℃ for 90 min before being evaporated until dry. Ammonia water (50 µL, 2 mol/L) and sodium borohydride (50 µL, 1 mol/L) were well mixed and left to react at room temperature for 2.5 h. Next, 20 µL of acetic acid was used to halt the reaction, and the solution was dehydrated using nitrogen gas. Methanol (250 µL) was repeatedly introduced and subsequently dried using nitrogen. In addition, 250 µL of acetic anhydride was well mixed and allowed to react at a temperature of 100 ℃ for 2.5 h. Finally, demonized water and dichloromethane were added and used for several washes in order to preserve the dichloromethane phase. Subsequently, a sample of partly methylated sugar alcohol acetate (PMAAs) was acquired for examination using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS). PMAAs were identified by matching their ‌retention time and peak profiles against the established reference data[17,18].

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra

PTPS (45 mg) was dissolved in D2O. The 1H and 13C NMR spectra of PTPS were obtained by a Bruker DRX-600 NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Switzerland).

Atomic force Microscope (AFM)

A 3 μL solution of PTPS（0.5 mg/mL） was placed onto a freshly cut mica surface [19]. The solution was let to dry at room temperature and then examined by Park FX40 AFM (SCientech Instruments, Shanghai, China). During AFM examination, the scan size was set as 400 nm × 400 nm; the pixel resolution was at least 256 × 256 pixels; and the imaging mode was tapping mode (Tapping Mode).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

PTPS’ surface microstructure was examined by SEM3300 (CIQTEK, Beijing, China). The PTPS powder was adhered to the sample holder using conductive adhesive followed by sputtering gold. SEM imaging was carried out at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV[20].

In vitro antioxidant activities of PTPS

The assays for DPPH and hydroxyl radicals were conducted according to established methods [18], [21]. VC was functioned as a positive control.

In vitro hypoglycemic activity of PTPS

The inhibitory effect on α-glucosidase and α-amylase activities was assessed according to established methods [22], [23]. Acarbose was used as the positive control.

Statistical analysis

All experimental operations were repeated three times. Results were presented as means ± standard deviations (n = 3). Data analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA in SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corporation, USA) at a significance level of P < 0.05. Graphs were generated using Origin 2021 Pro (Origin Lab Corporation, USA).

Results and discussion

Results of single-factor experiments

Fig. 1 illustrates the influence of four critical parameters solid-to-liquid ratio, temperature, ultrasonic power, and treatment time on PTPS yield. As shown in Fig. 1a, the PTPS yield demonstrated a biphasic response to increasing solid-to-liquid ratios, showing initial enhancement followed by gradual decline. And the maximum yield (9.30 ± 0.02 %) was achieved at the optimal ratio of 1:25 (w/v). This may be attributed to the fact that polysaccharides were not fully dissolved at lower solvent dosage, while increasing the solvent volume appropriately could enhance the concentration gradient effect, thus promoting the dissolution of polysaccharides. As a result, a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:25 was determined to be most effective and was therefore adopted for further investigations.

The impact of the material-to-liquid ratio（a）, ultrasound temperature (b), ultrasound power (c), and ultrasound time (d) on the yield of PTPS. Note: Different letters indicate significant differences among groups (P < 0.05).

Fig. 1b clearly shows the substantial impact of ultrasonic temperature on PTPS yield. When the ultrasonic temperature rose from 50 °C to 70 °C, the yield of PTPS showed a marked enhancement, increasing from 9.21 ± 0.07 % to 13.78 ± 0.24 %. The increase in temperature accelerated the molecular motion and favored the solubilization of polysaccharides. However, considering that high temperature may lead to degradation of polysaccharide structure and loss of biological activity, 70 °C was determined as the optimal ultrasonic temperature.

The influence of ultrasonic power was examined in Fig. 1c based on the optimization of the ratio of material to liquid and ultrasonic temperature. It showed that the yield of PTPS reached the maximum value (15.71 ± 0.06 %) when the ultrasonic power was increased to 500 W, and then showed a decreasing trend thereafter. This can be explained by the fact that the moderate ultrasonic cavitation effect can effectively destroy the cell structure and promote the release of polysaccharides. However, when the ultrasonic power exceeded the critical value (500 W), the excessive mechanical effect might lead to local overheating, which in turn affected the polysaccharide stability.

Finally, Fig. 1d illustrates the pattern of the effect of ultrasonic time. The highest yield of PTPS (17.71 ± 0.07 %) was obtained at 40 min, and prolonging the treatment period resulted in a decline in the yield instead. It might be attributed to the fragmentation of polysaccharide molecular chains triggered by continuous sonication or the accelerated degradation of polysaccharides due to the cumulative thermal effect of prolonged mechanical action. Therefore, 40 min was identified as the optimal ultrasonic time.

A validation experiment performed at the optimized parameters (solid-to-liquid ratio: 1:25, ultrasonic temperature: 70°C, ultrasonic power: 500 W, ultrasonic time: 40 min) demonstrated a PTPS yield of 17.53 ± 0.68 %, with a crude polysaccharide content of 68.74 ± 0.46 %.

Physicochemical properties of PTPS

Basic components of PTPS

In Fig. 2 (a), the decolorized, protein-free, and dialyzed PTPS solution was eluted and separated using a DEAE-52 column. The uncharged polysaccharide components were first separated using deionized water, but the charged acidic polysaccharide components were progressively separated as the concentration of NaCl rose [24]. Five elution peaks are as set out in Fig. 2 (a), named PTPS-1, PTPS-2, PTPS-3, PTPS-4, and PTPS-5. Afterwards, the PTPS-4 and PTPS-5 were submitted to further purification and separation through a Sephadex gel column. The gel chromatography column was employed to isolate and purify polysaccharides relying on their diverse molecular weights. Gel chromatography columns are utilized to separate and refine polysaccharides according to their varying molecular weights [25]. PTPS with higher molecular weights eluted prior to PTPS with lower molecular weights. The elution curves of PTPS-4 and PTPS-5 are present ed in Fig. 1 (b,c), respectively. Two distinct elution peaks were designated as PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A. The PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A samples had sugar contents of 91.56 % and 90.34 %, respectively. The uronic acid concentration of PTPS-4A was 2.78 %, while PTPS-5A had 1.69 %.

The molecular weight

Polysaccharides possess a significant characteristic parameter known as relative molecular mass. The molecular weight determination findings for PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A may be shown in Fig. 3 (b). PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A, purified using Sephadex G200, have multi-peak molecular weight distribution features, characterized by varying peak area ratios. PTPS are heterogeneous molecules, making detailed analysis of their fine structure challenging at this time. The molecular weight determination findings for PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A are presented in Table 2, highlighting notable variances in the molecular weight distribution range and proportions. The molecular weight of the fraction representing 84.418 % of the peak area in PTPS-4A was 15.77 kDa, and in PTPS-5A the molecular weight of the fraction representing 68.427 % of the peak area was 16.62 kDa, which further indicated that PTPS were non-homogeneous substances. It has been discovered that tea polysaccharides having molecular weights ranging from 104 to 105 Da are often seen in numerous investigations and demonstrate enhanced biological activity[15], [27], [28]. Given that the part of 104Da constituted over 80 % of PTPS-4A and more than 68 % of PTPS-5A, our analysis primarily focused on this dominant component.

These values are comparatively lower than the molecular weights of polysaccharides (609.86 kDa and 208.28 kDa) in Biluochun tea [29]. Furthermore, they are significantly lower than the molecular weight of polysaccharides (882 kDa) in yellow tea [30]. PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A had the same molecular weights as polysaccharides (18.05 kDa) extracted from black brick tea [31]. The likely cause for this might be that the raw material utilized for extracting PTPS in this study, which was yellow leaves of Pu'er tea, falls under the category of cooked Pu'er tea. Both cooked Pu'er tea and black brick tea fall within the dark tea group. The findings suggest that tea polysaccharides, derived from tea leaves processed using the identical technique, exhibit comparable molecular weights.

Galacturonic acid was likewise present in PTPS-5A, making up a significant percentage with a molar ratio of 44.5 %. However, this ratio was lower compared to that of PTPS-4A. The levels of arabinose (17.0 %), galactose (16.7 %), and rhamnose (10.9 %) in the sample were significantly greater compared to PTPS-4A, which had levels of 11.9 %, 10.2 %, and 7.7 % correspondingly. The content of uronic acid and galactose in polysaccharides are directly related to its anticoagulant activity[33], [34], [35], [36], [37]. The presence of arabinose is associated with the control of α-glucosidase inhibitory action [38]. The regulation of α-amylase inhibitory activity by rhamnose is directly related [16].

Infrared spectroscopy analysis of PTPS

Fig. 4 displays the FTIR spectra for PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A. Both samples exhibited characteristic –OH stretching vibrations at 3392 cm–1, indicative of polysaccharide hydroxyl groups. The morphology of the wide resonance signal indicated a significant abundance of hydroxyl groups in the PTPS composition. Both components presented absorption peaks at 2931 cm−1, suggesting that the methyl or methine groups in the polysaccharide were undergoing C-H stretching vibrations. The assimilation maxima at 1740 cm−1 for PTPS-4A and 1743 cm−1 for PTPS-5A were determined to be indicative of uronic acid. A comparable absorption peak at 1743 cm−1 was identified in the polysaccharides derived from green brick dark tea [39]. Bound water caused the two components' 1615 cm−1 absorption maxima. This peak bears resemblance to the absorption peak of polysaccharides derived from coarse aged green tea [40].

FT-IR spectra of PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A.

The pronounced peak located at 1418 cm−1 in both components corresponded to the asymmetric bending vibration of the C-H bond. The infrared spectra of polysaccharides exhibited distinctive absorption peaks, such as the peaks at 1105 cm−1 and 1010 cm−1 for PTPS-4A, and the peaks at 1101 cm−1 and 1019 cm−1 for PTPS-5A. The distinctive peak of polysaccharides extracted from coarse green tea, was also detected at a wavenumber of 1054 cm−1 [41]. The presence of peaks at 958 cm−1 and 830 cm−1 in both PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A fractions suggested that they exhibited both β and α configurations [42], [43].

NMR analysis

The one-dimensional nuclear magnetic resonance (1H, 13C) analysis of PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A is displayed in Fig. 5. The 1H spectral signal peaks of PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A nuclear magnetic resonance mostly appeared between δ3.0–6.0 ppm. The most intense signal peaks at δ4.65 ppm and δ4.69 ppm were identified as originating from the D2O solvent. As shown in Fig. 5a, six signals (A–H) are observed in the δ 4.30–5.95 region, corresponding to chemical shifts of δ 5.30, 5.16, 5.08, 5.00, 4.81, 4.46, 4.32 and 4.30respectively. Based on literature data[44], signals A–D can be assigned to the α-anomeric configuration, and signals E–H to the β-configuration. In Fig. 5(b), two anomeric proton signals (I and J) are observed in the δ 4.29–4.97 region, with chemical shifts of δ 4.97 and 4.29, respectively. Based on their characteristic chemical shifts, I is assigned to the α-anomer, and J to the β-anomer. The proton signals of the sugar rings (H2 ∼ H6) in PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A corresponded to the ranges of δ3.52–4.0 ppm and δ3.27–3.97 ppm, respectively [45]. The existence of galacturonic acid in PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A [46] was confirmed by the δ4.59–4.60 ppm value, which aligned with the result of the monosaccharide composition （Fig. 5）. The δ4.59–4.60 ppm value supported the presence of galacturonic acid in PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A [46], which aligned with the findings of the monosaccharide composition.

The 1H NMR (a,b) and 13C NMR (c,d) spectra of PTPS-4A (a,c) and PTPS-5A (b,d).

The chemical shift values of δ99.11 and δ108.46–109.46 indicated that the anomeric carbon regions of PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A contained α-glycosidic bonds in PTPS-4A and β-glycosidic bonds in PTPS-5A [31]. The presence of a furan ring structure in PTPS-5A was verified by the signal peak at δ107-109 ppm (C1) [47]. The signal peaked at δ170.94–175.46 and δ171.90, suggesting that uronic acid structures were present in both PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A. This discovery aligned with the outcomes of the monosaccharide composition study and infrared spectroscopy. PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A appeared to have branching structures based on the chemical shift of δ77.71 and δ77.86 [47].

Spatial and morphology structure of PTPS

Atomic force microscopy allows for the direct acquisition of the spatial structure and shape of polysaccharides. The atomic force microscopy images of PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A are displayed in Fig. 6. PTPS manifested as flexible chains and assumed a linear spline curve suggestive of an earthworm-like conformation.

AFM topographic images (planar view (a,d) and cubic view (b,e)) and SEM micrographs (c,f) of PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A at magnification of 500 ×.

PTPS may form a cross-linked network, as evidenced by its coiled shape and chain formation [48], [49]. Interweaving polysaccharide chains into a network structure may cause tangled chains in water-based solutions [50]. The atomic force stereo images of PTPS (Fig. 6 b and e) showed a rough, uneven surface. TPS derived from black brick tea [51] and green brick black tea [39] also exhibited similar surface morphologies. This indicates that TPS with comparable structures have the capacity to function as bio-thickeners and stabilizers [51].

SEM may be utilized to visualize the form and appearance of polysaccharides. Figures (Fig. 6 c and f) demonstrated PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A's spider-web-like three-dimensional network structure. This structure combined porous entanglement, sheet-like formations, and tubular shapes. Comparable microstructures were also seen in the TPS derived from tea blossoms [52] and green tea[16]. This TPS structure enhances water retention capacity [53]. The observed porosity within the cobweb-like network is likely a result of intense cavitation-induced shear forces generated during ultrasonic irradiation.

These hydrodynamic effects appear to promote the formation of short, filamentous branched-chain segments, ultimately yielding an open, loosely-organized morphology with increased structural porosity. Such a configuration not only facilitates greater water-holding capacity but may also enhance the polysaccharide's functional properties in food applications[11].

The antioxidant activity of PTPS in vitro

DPPH radical scavenging test

As shown in Fig. 7 (a), PTPS has the capacity to scavenge DPPH radicals. A direct association was discovered between PTPS concentration and DPPH radical scavenging at 1.0–5.0 mg/mL. As the concentration of PTPS rised, its DPPH radical scavenging ability strengthened, revealing a dose-dependent effect. At a concentration of 4 mg/mL, the clearance rates of PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A were 85.41 % and 77.35 %, respectively.

PTPS-4A showed a much stronger capacity to remove DPPH free radicals compared to PTPS-5A. At a concentration of 4 mg/mL, PTPS-4A (78.02 %) exhibited a superior scavenging rate compared to a purified fraction of polysaccharide from the.

The difference in antioxidant activity between PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A can be explained by the fact that PTPS-4A has a smaller molecular weight. In most cases, polysaccharides that have a smaller molecular weight are associated with higher levels of antioxidant activity. TPS from the same tea type with a lower molecular weight exhibited stronger antioxidant effects [56]. TPS, characterized by its large molecular weight, demonstrates diminished viscosity and solubility in water, rendering it challenging to traverse the cell membrane and access the intracellular environment. Consequently, its biological efficacy is constrained [57]. Chen et al. extracted three tea polysaccharides with varied molecular weights from coarse and aged green tea [58], discovering that TPC-3 (42 kDa), a low-molecular-weight component, removed hydroxyl radicals better than the other two components (268 kDa, 118 kDa).

TPS with a higher molecular weight exhibits a higher apparent viscosity and worse water solubility, making it harder to permeate tissue barriers and enter cells, limiting its biological activity. By effortlessly crossing biological membranes, low-molecular-weight polysaccharides can avoid immune system detection and clearance, resulting in better biological activity [59].

TPS's antioxidant activity can be influenced by the type and amount of monosaccharides. Wang et al. [60] discovered that polysaccharides with elevated concentrations of uronic acid have enhanced antioxidant activity. This study found that the glucuronic acid concentration in PTPS-4A was 2.78 ± 0.16 %, above that of PTPS-5A, which was 1.69 ± 0.05 %. The carboxyl and carbonyl groups in glucuronic acid served as electron-withdrawing moieties that enhance antioxidant capacity through dual mechanistic pathways: functioning as efficient hydrogen atom donors and facilitating electron transfer processes.

Assay for scavenging hydroxyl radicals

Fig. 7b demonstrates a concentration-dependent enhancement in hydroxyl radical scavenging activity of PTPS across the tested range (2.0–10.0 mg/mL). The IC50 value for PTPS-4A was 6.42 ± 0.16 mg/mL, whereas for PTPS-5A it was 9.48 ± 0.05 mg/mL (Table 6). Remarkably, PTPS-4A possessed a significantly superior hydroxyl radical scavenging capacity compared to PTPS-5A (Table 6). This enhanced scavenging ability of PTPS-4A can result from its relatively lower molecular weight compared to PTPS-5A. Smaller molecular weight polysaccharides possess a greater number of reducing termini, enabling them to react more effectively with free radicals and exhibit quenching effects [26]. Smaller molecular weight components isolated and processed from raw green tea polysaccharides neutralized free radicals better [16]. PTPS-4A may have a greater galacturonic acid content compared to PTPS-5A, which might provide a convincing explanation. Polysaccharides rich in uronic acid are more apt to trigger the activation of anomeric hydrogen atoms, leading to their interaction with free radicals. Consequently, these polysaccharides exhibited a superior capacity to neutralize free radicals [26].

Evaluation of the hypoglycemic effects of PTPS in vitro

Suppression of α-glucosidase enzymatic function

Long-term metabolic disorder diabetes mellitus, with high blood sugar and poor insulin production, causes health hazards [62]. Following a meal, the starch present in the food is broken down by α-amylase into oligosaccharides. These oligosaccharides are then further broken down by α-glucosidase, resulting in the release of glucose. It is crucial for α-glucosidase in the development of high blood sugar levels. Increased α-glucosidase activity leads to quicker glucose absorption in the small intestinal mucosa by breaking down starch and oligosaccharides [63]. Thus, suppressing α- glucosidase is essential for regulating postprandial hyperglycemia and reducing fasting blood glucose. Fig. 8 (a) shows that PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A inhibited α-glucosidase dose-dependently at concentrations ranging from 2.0-10.0 mg/mL. All samples inhibited less than acarbose. In Table 6, the IC50 values for PTPS-4A, PTPS-5A, and acarbose were 5.22 ± 0.08 mg/mL, 6.84 ± 0.22 mg/mL, and 1.71 ± 0.16 mg/mL, respectively. PTPS-4A inhibited α-glucosidase more effectively, mostly because of its reduced molecular weight and increased uronic acid concentration. Zhu et al. studied black tea polysaccharide extraction and processing. A significant inverse connection was found between α-glucosidase inhibition and relative molecular mass. Additionally, α-glucosidase inhibition was strongly correlated with uronic acid levels [26].

Effects of PTPS on α-glucosidase activity (a) and α-amylase activity (b).

Moreover, the hypoglycemic effects of polysaccharides are often linked to their antioxidant properties, where a stronger antioxidant activity is positively correlated with improved hypoglycemic activity. PTPS-4A outperformed PTPS-5A in DPPH and hydroxyl radical scavenging. This leaded to a larger inhibitory action on α-glucosidase. PTPS-4A demonstrated potent α-glucosidase inhibitory action, significantly retarding the process of oligosaccharide degradation into glucose in the gut and reducing glucose absorption. As a result, it aids in reducing post-meal blood sugar spikes, showing significant promise in the creation of oral medications for lowering blood sugar.

Inhibitory activity against α-amylase assay

Colon bacteria ferment undigested starch to produce gas and low-molecular- weight chemicals. This aberrant fermentation can produce stomach distension, gas, diarrhea, and bloating. Thus, our objective is to determine if PTPS can reduce the activity of α-amylase. The dose-dependent inhibition of α-amylase by PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A increased from 2 to 10 mg/mL, as shown in Fig. 8 (b). Nevertheless, the inhibition of all samples remained inferior to that of acarbose.

In addition, it is important to mention that even in a dosage of 10 mg/mL, none of the samples exhibited an inhibition rate against α-amylase that exceeded 25 %. This inhibition rate was significantly lower than that of α-glucosidase at the same dosage. The TPS derived from Pu'er tea of varying ages exhibited a notable suppressive impact on the activity of α-glucosidase, whereas it had no influence on the activity of α-amylase [22]. According to the experimental results, the inhibitory impact of PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A on α-amylase activity seemed to be ordinary.

Acarbose, an α-glucosidase inhibitor commonly prescribed for type 2 diabetes, frequently causes various adverse symptoms such as stomach distension, gas, diarrhea, and bloating. Comparative analysis revealed distinct inhibition patterns between PTPS and acarbose: while acarbose significantly suppressed both α-glucosidase and α-amylase activities, PTPS selectively inhibited only α-glucosidase without affecting α-amylase function.

We evaluated the in vitro hypoglycemic efficacy of PTPS-4A and coarse green tea polysaccharides. PTPS-4A had a more potent inhibitory action on α-glucosidase compared to WE-CTPS, polysaccharides extracted by water from coarse green tea[40]. At a concentration of 10 mg/mL, PTPS-4A inhibited α-glucosidase by 89.25 % and WE-CTPS by 64.34 %. The inhibitory impact of PTPS-4A (22.36 %) on α-amylase parallels that of We-CTPS (17.72 %), exhibiting little more than 25 % inhibition at a concentration of 10 mg/mL, significantly lower than the inhibition rate on α-glucosidase.The raw materials utilized for extracting PTPS-4A and We- CTPS are both low-grade coarse teas. Therefore, it is presumably believed that the polysaccharides isolated from low-grade coarse tea have substantial inhibitory effect against glucosidase and relatively modest inhibitory activity against amylase. Nonetheless, the mechanism requires further investigation regarding enzyme reaction kinetics, the interaction between polysaccharides and enzymes, and the alterations in the secondary and tertiary structures of enzymes induced by polysaccharides.

Conclusions

The extraction parameters for PTPS were optimized via ultrasound-assisted hot water extraction ‌under the following conditions‌: solid–liquid ratio of 1:25 (w/v), temperature of 70 ℃, ultrasonic power of 500 W, and treatment time of 40 min. Under the optimized extraction parameters, the PTPS extraction process achieved a yield of was 17.53 ± 0.68 %, with the resultant crude polysaccharides exhibiting a purity level of 68.74 ± 0.46 % as assessed by the phenol–sulfuric acid method. Two indigenous polysaccharides, PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A, were obtained and refined from PTPS. An extensive analysis was conducted on their structural features, antioxidant activity, and hypoglycemic action. The findings revealed that PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A had identical monosaccharide composition, however PTPS-4A has a larger uronic acid concentration and a slightly lower molecular weight than TPS-5A. NMR analysis found α- and β-glycosidic linkages in PTPS-4A and PTPS-5A. PTPS-4A had superior antioxidant activity compared to PTPS-5A, as seen by its lower IC50 value. PTPS-4A inhibited α-glucosidase more effectively. Neither PTPS-4A nor PTPS-5A reduced α-amylase activity, although acarbose did. The subsequent investigation will focus on the hypoglycemic effects of PTPS-4A in vivo, encompassing its ability to enhance metabolic processes in the plasma and liver of diabetic rats, as well as its regulatory influence on intestinal flora in these subjects.
