Comparison of echocardiography and surgical pathology in pediatric cardiac tumor with follow-up: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Xuechen Liu, Mengqi Zhao, Lianyi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough cardiac tumor in children are clinically rare, they present significant diagnostic challenges. This study aims to characterize the echocardiographic features of pediatric cardiac tumor and to correlate these findings with surgical and pathological results, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy. A further objective is to conduct long-term follow-up of surgical patients to provide insights into the prognosis and management of primary cardiac tumors in this population.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on pediatric patients under 18 years of age with cardiac tumor confirmed by both echocardiography and surgical pathology at our institution from January 2004 to January 2025. The inclusion criterion was confirmation of the lesion by both preoperative echocardiography and postoperative pathological analysis following surgical resection. Demographic and clinical data, including gender, age, clinical manifestations, and arrhythmias, were qualitatively summarized. Echocardiographic, surgical, and pathological findings were statistically analyzed. Long-term follow-up was performed to assess tumor recurrence, postoperative complications, and overall prognosis.ResultsOf the 18 pediatric patients included, 11 (61.1%) were symptomatic at presentation, with arrhythmias being the most common manifestation, observed in 9 (81.8%) of these symptomatic patients. Lesion size ranged from 0.4 to 10 cm, and the majority were solitary. Pathological analysis identified rhabdomyoma (n = 8, 44.4%), fibroma (n = 6, 33.3%), myxoma (n = 2, 11.1%), and cystic lesions (n = 2, 11.1%). The anatomical distribution varied by tumor type. Rhabdomyomas were most frequently found in the left ventricle (n = 2), with other locations including the right ventricle, right atrium, both ventricles, aortic valve, and superior vena cava (n = 1 each). All fibromas were predominantly located in the left ventricle (n = 5), with one case in the right atrium. The two myxomas were located in the right atrium and were multifocal with scattered distribution, respectively. The cystic lesions were located subaortic and on the posterior left ventricular wall (n = 1 each). A high concordance was observed between echocardiographic and pathological diagnoses, although discrepancies were noted in a subset of cases.ConclusionThis study confirms that primary cardiac tumor in children are predominantly benign, with rhabdomyoma and fibroma being the most common histological types. Clinical presentation is highly variable, with arrhythmias representing a frequent and significant symptom. While echocardiography serves as a reliable primary diagnostic tool, our findings highlight the potential for diagnostic discrepancies with histopathology, underscoring the need for careful interpretation. Ultimately, our data support a tailored approach to clinical management, where decisions on intervention are critically dependent on a comprehensive assessment of the tumor’s specific type, location, and size. This work provides valuable real-world data to enhance diagnostic accuracy and inform optimal management strategies for these rare conditions.Keywords: Cardiac tumor, Pediatric, Echocardiography, Pathology, Treatment outcome

Background

Cardiac space-occupying lesions encompass a spectrum of entities, including primary benign and malignant tumors, metastases, and tumor mimics such as thrombi or vegetations [1]. Cardiac tumors are exceedingly rare in clinical practice, with autopsy studies reporting a prevalence ranging from 0.0017% to 0.33%. This rarity is even more pronounced in the pediatric population, where the estimated prevalence is notably lower, at a maximum of 0.08% [2, 3].

The clinical presentation of cardiac tumors is highly variable, ranging from asymptomatic cases to a spectrum of non-specific manifestations such as arrhythmias, chest tightness, dyspnea, syncope, risks of embolic events, myocardial infarction, inflow or outflow tract obstruction, and even sudden death. In malignant cases, symptoms may be compounded by tissue invasion or distant hematogenous metastasis. Ultimately, the specific clinical picture is determined by the tumor’s size, location, and histological type, with symptoms primarily arising from the obstruction or compression of adjacent cardiac structures [4]. This non-specific symptomatology, which often mimics more common cardiovascular diseases, presents a significant diagnostic challenge.

In the pediatric population, cardiac tumors are exceptionally rare. These tumors are predominantly benign, with rhabdomyoma being the most common type, followed by myxoma and fibroma, and are frequently associated with a high incidence of arrhythmias [5]. While long-term survival is achievable in many asymptomatic children with stable hemodynamics [6], the current evidence base is limited. Knowledge in this field is largely derived from isolated case reports, leading to a scarcity of robust clinical data. This evidence gap, combined with the diverse clinical manifestations and extremely low incidence of these tumors, creates significant diagnostic challenges. Consequently, clinicians face a higher likelihood of missed or delayed diagnoses [7], which can compromise timely treatment.

Obtaining a direct histopathological diagnosis for cardiac tumors is often challenging due to their unique anatomical locations. Consequently, diagnosis typically relies on imaging studies. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the primary imaging modality for the initial screening and plays a vital role in preoperative planning and postoperative monitoring. However, its effectiveness can be limited by image quality and acoustic windows, potentially restricting the assessment of a tumor’s full extent. In such cases, cardiac CT or MRI may serve as supplementary modalities. Other techniques such as contrast-enhanced echocardiography, PET/CT, or cardiac angiography may be used in selected cases, particularly when malignancy is suspected or detailed anatomical assessment is required [8, 9]. Despite the utility of these advanced imaging techniques, pathological analysis of the tissue remains the definitive gold standard for diagnosis.

The clinical management of cardiac tumors is multifactorial, with decisions guided by the tumor’s location, size, the presence of obstructive symptoms or complications, and the need for a definitive tissue diagnosis. Among these, the severity of clinical symptoms is paramount. Surgical resection is typically indicated for patients with severe obstructive symptoms, yet the optimal timing for intervention remains a subject of debate, lacking clear consensus [10]. This therapeutic dilemma is further complicated by the well-documented phenomenon of spontaneous regression, particularly in rhabdomyomas. Therefore, a conservative, watchful-waiting strategy may be more appropriate for younger children or those with smaller tumors [11].

Cardiac tumors are extremely rare, particularly in the pediatric population. Currently, information regarding pediatric cardiac tumors is primarily limited to case reports and a small number of clinical studies, with very few available references. This study aims to characterize cardiac tumors and improve clinical diagnostic accuracy by describing and comparing the ultrasound imaging features of intracardiac tumors in children with surgical and pathological findings, as well as by analyzing the correlations between ultrasound, surgery, and pathology. Furthermore, by incorporating long-term follow-up of surgical patients, this research seeks to provide crucial data on treatment outcomes and prognosis, thereby informing and optimizing clinical management strategies for this challenging patient population.

Methods and analysis

Clinical data

This study retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of pediatric patients (age ≤ 18 years) diagnosed with cardiac tumors at our hospital between January 2004 and January 2025.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients who underwent preoperative transthoracic echocardiography and surgical resection, and whose postoperative surgical specimens were confirmed as cardiac tumors via histopathological analysis, thereby achieving dual verification of imaging and pathology.

The exclusion criteria were: cases diagnosed solely by imaging without surgical pathological confirmation; cases with severe missing clinical data or ultrasound images that precluded effective analysis; cases complicated by other severe congenital heart diseases or metastasis from systemic malignant tumors to the heart; and patients lost to follow-up.

Transthoracic echocardiography image acquisition

Echocardiographic examinations were performed by experienced sonographers using a GE Vivid E95 ultrasound system. The probe frequency was adjusted according to the child’s body surface area (typically 3.5-8 MHz). During the examination, the children were placed in the left lateral decubitus or supine position. Standard views, including parasternal long-axis, short-axis, and apical four-chamber views, were routinely obtained. Tumor location, size (maximum diameter measurement), number, echogenicity (hyperechoic, hypoechoic, or anechoic), hemodynamic impact (such as the degree of valve obstruction and the presence of valvular regurgitation), and the preliminary ultrasound diagnosis were recorded in detail.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Collected data included demographic and clinical characteristics such as gender, age at diagnosis, presenting symptoms (e.g., chest tightness, syncope, palpitations), physical signs, and comorbid arrhythmias (e.g., premature ventricular contractions, atrioventricular block).

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 software. Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous data were expressed as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages (%), and between-group comparisons were performed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Follow-up evaluation

Postoperative follow-up was primarily conducted via telephone interviews combined with outpatient clinic visits. The follow-up period ended in January 2025. Follow-up content focused on the patients’ survival status, tumor recurrence (confirmed by regular ultrasound re-examinations), and long-term complications (such as arrhythmias and thromboembolic events). Event-free survival time was defined as the interval from the date of surgical resection to the first occurrence of recurrence, death, or the last follow-up. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Tsinghua University (Approval No. [2026-003-01]).

Results

The median age was 57.5 months ([IQR], 14–136 months; [range], 4-204 months), and the median weight was 18.85 kg ([IQR], 10–26 kg; [range], 6.6–60.8 kg). Intraoperative findings and pathological results showed some discrepancies with the echocardiographic diagnoses (Table 2). Final pathology confirmed that all 18 lesions were benign, with tumor sizes ranging from 0.4 to 10 cm. Regarding the number of tumors, solitary lesions were still common, found in 13 cases (72.2%), while double and triple lesions were found in 3 (16.7%) and 2 (11.1%) cases, respectively. There were 8 rhabdomyomas (male-to-female ratio 1:1), and the diagnosis was consistent with echocardiography in 5 of these cases. There were 2 myxomas (male-to-female ratio 1:1), and the diagnosis was consistent with echocardiography in both cases. There were 6 fibromas (male-to-female ratio 1:1), with the diagnosis consistent with echocardiography in 1 case. For the 2 cystic lesions (one male and one female), the diagnosis was consistent with echocardiography in 1 case. One case of myxoma in a female patient experienced multiple recurrences and underwent three surgical resections. For the intracardiac lesions, isolated left heart involvement was found in 8 cases (44.4%), and the location was consistent with the echocardiographic diagnosis in all 8 cases. Isolated right heart involvement was found in 5 cases (27.8%), and aortic valve involvement was found in 2 cases (11.1%), all of which were consistent with the echocardiographic diagnosis. Bilateral cardiac involvement was present in 2 cases (11.1%), with the diagnosis consistent with echocardiography in 1 case. Involvement of both the mitral and aortic valves was found in 1 case (5.6%). Regarding the locations of each lesion type, among the 8 patients with rhabdomyoma, 2 were located in the left ventricle, 1 in the right ventricle, 1 in the right atrium, 1 involved both ventricles, 2 involved the aortic valve, and 1 was located at the superior vena cava. Of the 2 myxomas, one was located in the right atrium, and the other was a case of multiple myxomas with scattered locations. Among the 6 fibromas, five were located in the left ventricle; of these, four involved the inferior-posterior wall and one involved the anterior wall. One fibroma was located in the right atrium. For pediatric patients who underwent surgical resection for cardiac tumors at our institution, follow-up was conducted to assess recurrence, postoperative complications, and prognosis. The median follow-up time was 10.83 years (range, 2.83–20.25 years). Among them, one female patient with myxoma experienced two recurrences at 5 and 7 years postoperatively, respectively. Postoperative complications occurred in five patients; one patient developed ventricular tachycardia secondary to a postoperative ventricular aneurysm. Two patients developed idiopathic ventricular tachycardia, and one patient experienced cardiac insufficiency 6 months postoperatively. These symptoms were well-controlled with medication. One patient developed severe mitral regurgitation at a 5-month postoperative follow-up and subsequently underwent mitral valve replacement, with a good postoperative recovery. All patients survived.

Discussion

Clinically, cardiac tumors are rare in both adults and children. Studies have shown that cardiac tumors in adults are predominantly secondary metastases, whereas in children, primary tumors are more common, with most originating from cardiac tissue [12]. It is generally considered that rhabdomyoma is the most common benign cardiac tumor, although fibromas and myxomas are also encountered. Malignant tumors include sarcomas, lymphomas, and mesotheliomas. Tumor classification is usually associated with patient prognosis [13]. Most studies indicate a gender difference in the incidence of cardiac tumors, with a higher prevalence in females than in males [4, 14]. However, other reports suggest that in the pediatric population, the incidence rates are similar between girls and boys [13]. Our study is consistent with these previous findings: among the 18 pediatric patients with cardiac tumors, rhabdomyoma was the most common, followed by fibroma. Solitary lesions were frequent, and there was no significant difference in gender distribution between males and females.

Primary cardiac tumors may be asymptomatic, with symptoms primarily related to the tumor’s location and size. They may cause obstructive symptoms, such as dyspnea and chest tightness, due to blockage from tumor growth. Alternatively, if located near the cardiac conduction system, they can lead to arrhythmias and palpitations [12]. In our study, most patients presented with palpitations, while others were discovered incidentally during routine health check-ups without obvious clinical manifestations. It is noteworthy that the presence or absence of symptoms did not influence patient survival or the development of postoperative complications [7].

Compared to adults, pediatric patients with cardiac tumors have a higher incidence of arrhythmias, such as malignant ventricular arrhythmias and even sudden cardiac death [5, 15]. In our study, children presenting with palpitations or chest tightness often had associated arrhythmias, and the occurrence of these arrhythmias was correlated with the size, location, and type of the cardiac tumor. For example, tumors located in the right atrium or at the root of the superior vena cava can lead to atrial arrhythmias, whereas tumors in the left ventricle may induce ventricular arrhythmias due to direct electrophysiological interference or mechanical irritation and traction.

Rhabdomyoma, the most common cardiac tumor in the pediatric population, is typically benign and does not affect patient survival or prognosis. While current research on the gender predisposition of rhabdomyoma has yielded inconsistent conclusions [16, 17], our study found no significant gender difference, which may be attributed to the small sample size. Studies indicate that due to hormonal fluctuations, some cardiac rhabdomyomas may regress spontaneously in early childhood. However, in the presence of obstruction or arrhythmias, they can have life-threatening consequences [18, 19]. Studies have found that most rhabdomyomas are located within the cardiac chambers, most commonly in the left ventricle, are typically asymptomatic, and exhibit self-limiting growth [20]. However, a very small number of rhabdomyomas located within the pericardial cavity can lead to severe complications and even death. In our study, pediatric rhabdomyomas were predominantly located in the left and right ventricular myocardium. Although this location is atypical, it was confirmed by pathological findings and warrants caution from clinicians. It is noteworthy that pediatric cardiac rhabdomyomas have a high correlation with tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC). The presence of multiple cardiac rhabdomyomas is a strong predictor of TSC, a genetic disorder caused by mutations in the TSC1 and TSC2 genes [20, 21]. Genetic testing can further refine the diagnostic basis for this disease. Timely diagnosis, appropriate clinical management, and monitoring are crucial for improving the prognosis of children with cardiac rhabdomyomas.

In contrast, cardiac fibromas can involve any structure of the heart and, in most cases, grow in tandem with the ventricle. They are typically associated with a poor prognosis, and in children, they may be linked to Gorlin-Goltz syndrome [7, 22].Approximately 60% of patients with fibromas present with arrhythmias. This may be attributed to a reentry mechanism between the tumor and the underlying myocardium, or to electrical signal disarray caused by the tumor’s intermingling with the surrounding myocardium. Ventricular arrhythmias are particularly common [23–25]. In our study, 50% of pediatric patients with fibromas developed ventricular arrhythmias, a finding that is largely consistent with previous research.

Myxoma, a common cardiac tumor in adults, is typically non-invasive. However, in the pediatric population, myxomas are rare and have a high potential for invasive growth and even fatal progression [26–28]. Furthermore, patients with myxoma may present with non-specific systemic symptoms, valvular obstruction, or embolic phenomena. Numbness in the fingertips or joint pain should raise clinical suspicion [29]. It is noteworthy that pediatric patients with myxoma warrant vigilance for Carney complex, a syndrome that can also manifest as myxomas in the heart, breasts, and other locations. As an autosomal dominant disorder with nearly 100% penetrance, family history and genetic testing play a crucial role in its diagnosis [30, 31]. In the pediatric population, the presence of rare and recurrent myxomas should raise a high degree of suspicion for this disease. In our study, there were only two cases of pediatric myxoma. One of these, a girl, experienced three recurrences within five years. If genetic testing had been performed, the chain of evidence for the diagnosis and management of her condition would have been more robust.

Due to the unique nature of cardiac tumors, direct tissue sampling for pathological diagnosis, unlike for other tumors, is often not feasible. Consequently, diagnosis relies heavily on medical imaging. Currently, echocardiography is the primary non-invasive modality for the initial screening and diagnosis of cardiac masses. Furthermore, echocardiography plays a crucial role even in the prenatal diagnosis of these tumors [32]. Echocardiography is a non-invasive and radiation-free technique. Compared to cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), it is simpler to perform and requires a shorter examination time. It is highly effective for evaluating intracardiac blood flow, as well as valvular stenosis and regurgitation. Furthermore, with its high spatial and temporal resolution, it demonstrates high sensitivity for detecting valvular vegetations or abnormal masses that oscillate with blood flow. Consequently, it is the most commonly used imaging modality in clinical practice [1, 33]. However, echocardiographic diagnosis is susceptible to factors such as image quality and operator experience. Consequently, some cases still show discrepancies when compared with surgical and pathological findings. MRI examination offers superior clarity and provides higher accuracy in assessing the morphology and number of masses [34]. Nevertheless, the definitive diagnosis ultimately depends on pathological analysis. A total of 18 pediatric patients were included in this study. Among them, the preoperative qualitative diagnoses via transthoracic echocardiography were completely consistent with the postoperative pathological results in 9 cases, yielding a diagnostic concordance rate of 50.0%. Although the determination of pathological type by ultrasound deviated from the final pathological diagnosis in the remaining 9 cases, echocardiography accurately indicated the presence of cardiac space-occupying lesions in all cases. Therefore, despite limitations in the precision of pathological typing, its significant value as the preferred imaging modality for cardiac tumors and for clinical early warning cannot be overlooked. For genetic syndromes associated with pediatric cardiac tumors, such as Carney complex and tuberous sclerosis complex, genetic testing is paramount for definitive diagnosis. In conclusion, clinicians should recognize that advances in modern imaging, pathological analysis, and genetic testing all play vital roles in disease diagnosis and comprehensive clinical management. These more precise methods provide significant benefits for disease assessment and subsequent management.

The majority of primary cardiac tumors in children are benign and have the potential for spontaneous regression [18]. Nevertheless, the appropriate clinical management of these tumors remains a challenge for clinicians. The management of pediatric cardiac rhabdomyomas has been gradually evolving. Research has demonstrated that mTOR inhibitors (such as sirolimus and everolimus) can effectively reduce the volume of rhabdomyomas and ameliorate hemodynamic disturbances and arrhythmias caused by the tumor [35, 36]. This approach offers a novel alternative to conventional surgical intervention, particularly for high-risk patients who cannot tolerate surgery due to large tumor size, challenging location, or poor general condition. For benign tumors without significant clinical symptoms, specific intervention is generally not required. However, if a patient develops hemodynamic alterations or obstructive symptoms, surgical intervention is indicated, and a favorable prognosis can be achieved in most cases [11, 33]. It must be noted, however, that surgery can induce myocardial injury leading to cardiac dysfunction [6]. During the procedure, care must be taken to protect crucial cardiac structures and tissues. An individualized surgical approach, based on the tumor’s type, location, and size, should be selected to minimize myocardial injury and reduce postoperative complications [37]. In this study, surgical intervention was performed in all cases following multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussions, communication with families, and comprehensive evaluation. The families chose surgical treatment primarily to rapidly relieve the tumor mass effect and alleviate symptoms. Second, surgical resection allowed for pathological analysis to confirm the diagnosis. Third, the families were concerned about the potential side effects of mTOR inhibitors, as well as the risks associated with the time to onset and recurrence after discontinuation. In summary, all families opted for direct surgical resection. Postoperative complications occurred in five cases, but all were well-managed with medication, resulting in a favorable overall prognosis.

Of course, our study has certain limitations. First, the data were derived from a single center, and the sample size is relatively small, which may not adequately represent the disease characteristics of the Chinese population. Second, this was a retrospective study with a long follow-up period, and some data and imaging records were incomplete. Third, in terms of methodology, there were discrepancies between ultrasound diagnoses and pathological results in some cases; however, this study did not perform a consistency analysis of the two diagnostic methods. In the future, once a sufficient sample size is available, we intend to conduct further supplementary research on this aspect. Fourth, limited by the retrospective nature of this study and the clinical testing conditions available for some early cases, we were unable to obtain genotype data for the patients and their families.

Overall, our study summarizes several clinical features of cardiac tumors in the pediatric population. As a rare disease, its diagnosis remains highly challenging. The non-specificity of its clinical manifestations and the diversity of its imaging findings both contribute to the diagnostic difficulties. Timely diagnosis, along with appropriate clinical management and intervention, is crucial for patient prognosis.

Conclusion

In clinical practice, primary cardiac tumors in the pediatric population are extremely rare. Their clinical manifestations are diverse, ranging from no related discomfort to palpitations, chest tightness, or even obstructive symptoms, and can be accompanied by various types of arrhythmias. However, the majority of these lesions are benign. Rhabdomyomas are the most common type, followed by fibromas, with no significant gender difference observed. Currently, the most common diagnostic method remains non-invasive and convenient echocardiography. Nevertheless, discrepancies still exist between the diagnosis in some cases and the surgical and pathological findings, pathological results are indispensable for diagnosis. Clinical intervention for pediatric cardiac tumors should be based on a comprehensive consideration of factors such as the tumor’s type, location, and size to guide clinical decision-making. In summary, the recognition and diagnosis of primary cardiac tumors in children continue to warrant attention from clinicians.
