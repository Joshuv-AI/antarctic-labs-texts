Cellular senescence and cell therapy in cardiovascular diseases
Authors: Dehua Chang, Jiaqi Wang, Shuoji Zhu, Yunchao Shentu, Yuanli Wang, Ping Zhu, Minoru Ono
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The issue of population aging presents a significant challenge for many countries, and the related physical health implications have been receiving increasing attention. Senescence impacts several aspects of the cardiovascular system, contributing to diseases such as atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction (MI), pulmonary hypertension, and heart failure (HF). In recent decades, scientists have significantly advanced in understanding the molecular and cellular processes involved in cardiovascular aging, including telomere shortening and damage, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and DNA damage. Molecules such as p53, p21, and p16Ink4a, along with enhanced signals for SA-β-gal, are commonly used to detect senescent cells. Researchers have identified pathways and factors that could be potential targets for treating or alleviating cardiovascular aging. Furthermore, the rapid advancement of regenerative medicine, including mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) and induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) transplantation, has positioned heart regeneration as a promising strategy for addressing age-related cardiovascular diseases. This review summarizes the current understanding of senescent cells, such as cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts/myofibroblasts, and vascular smooth muscle cells, and their roles in associated cardiovascular diseases. We will also discuss recent factors contributing to cardiovascular aging, including but not limited to Akt and AMPK, and emphasize the potential of heart regeneration research and insights into future regenerative therapies for cardiovascular aging.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a group of heart and vascular conditions, including atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, pulmonary hypertension, and heart failure. It is among the leading causes of death globally, claiming around 19 million lives each year, as reported by the World Health Organization. Many factors contribute to the development of CVD, including an unhealthy diet, obesity, diabetes, and a family history of heart disease, with aging being a significant risk factor [1, 2]. The aging of the cardiovascular system is characterized by alterations in intercellular communication, telomere shortening, mitochondrial dysfunction, depletion of stem cells, a decrease in the number of myocardial cells, increased fibrosis, reduced heart function, and a diminished ability of cardiac cells to proliferate, differentiate, and repair [3, 4]. These factors lead to the deterioration of tissues and organs, increasing the susceptibility of individuals to age-related mortality. Additionally, aging causes inflammation and oxidative stress in the cardiovascular system, leading to structural and functional abnormalities that heighten the risk of cardiovascular diseases such as atherosclerosis [5, 6], myocardial infarction [7], pulmonary hypertension [8], and heart failure [9]. Aging and age-related disorders develop by integrating complex biological processes. Despite significant advancements, many aspects of the relationship between senescence and the cardiovascular system remain unclear. However, the evidence indicates that cellular senescence plays a key role in developing these conditions (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of cellular senescence

Hayflick and Moorhead initially defined cellular senescence in 1961 [10]. As senescent cells become enlarged with a flattened morphology, they exhibit an irreversible loss of proliferative potential. Changes in the expression of genetic profiles in these cells result in the secretion of pro-inflammatory molecules [11]. Senescent cells accumulate in various tissues and organs associated with aging and age-related disorders, and they are believed to become pathogenic by introducing chronic inflammation and tissue remodeling. Two major senescence-associated pathways have been highlighted in the recent literature. Telomeres are situated at both ends of a chromosome and replicate incompletely during cell division, leading to telomere shortening. When telomere shortening goes beyond the physiological range, it is recognized as DNA damage and activates replicative cellular senescence, primarily through the p53 or p16INK4a signaling pathways. p16INK4a also plays a crucial role in the mitotic process. It regulates the G1/S-phase transition of the cell cycle, helping to maintain the accuracy of cell proliferation. Normal cell division requires smooth progression through the cell cycle, and p16INK4a ensures that cells halt proliferation in the presence of DNA damage or unfavorable division conditions, thereby preserving genomic stability and preventing errors or malignancies during mitosis. Another form of cellular senescence is stress-induced premature senescence, triggered by various external and internal stress signals, including oxidative stress, irradiation, oncogenic activation, and metabolic stress. Research indicates that p53/p21 and p16INK4a signaling are primarily activated in response to DNA damage and telomere dysfunction. In contrast, p16INK4a signaling is mainly associated with mitogenic and general cellular stress [12, 13]. IGFBP7 is a member of the IGFBP family. It is a stress-responsive gene that can be upregulated in response to oxidative stress and DNA damage. The IGFBP7–p53 pathway is a critical stress–senescence pathway essential for regulating cell fate, such as cell cycle arrest, senescence, and apoptosis. This pathway may be a target for anti-tumor and anti-fibrotic therapies; however, its inhibitory effect on tissue regeneration should also be considered [14]. Senescent cells exhibit various morphological and biochemical characteristics that aid their detection [15]. Currently, no single marker can definitively identify a senescent cell; instead, combinations of markers and analytical techniques are commonly employed to improve detection specificity. Table 1 displays some widely used markers for this purpose. Many stressors that induce senescence activate the p53/p21 or p16INK4a pathways. However, it’s important to note that activating these signaling pathways does not provide conclusive evidence that the cells are senescent [16]. Currently, senescence-associated ß-galactosidase (SA-ß-galactosidase) is widely used to identify senescent cells as a marker of senescence, which has a pH optimum of 6.0; however, the SA-ß-gal activity is also known to increase in fibroblasts cultured under serum starvation [17–19]. Another category of sensitive senescence indicators includes DNA damage response (DDR) gene products, which are usually visualized through immunofluorescence. The DDR protein most commonly used for this purpose is γH2AX phosphorylated at Ser-139, which accumulates at sites of double-stranded DNA breaks and facilitates the detection of proteins involved in the double-strand break repair pathway [20, 21]. DNA damage at telomeres suggests that both cardiomyocytes and various non-cardiomyocytes, including myofibroblasts, endothelial cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells, contribute to the senescence of the cardiovascular system. These cells interact within the microenvironment, altering cardiovascular function and promoting disease progression. Additionally, some studies have monitored cytokine secretion related to the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), characterized by the extensive release of pro-inflammatory compounds. Common SASP factors secreted by senescent cells include signaling molecules such as interleukins (IL-6, IL-1ß, IL-8) and other factors [22, 23]. The cell makers mentioned above are all related to senescence, but do not exist in isolation.

In summary, cells that show positive senescent markers are well recognized for their causal roles in the progression of pathologies associated with age-related diseases [24, 25]. Investigating biological markers that provide direct evidence of cellular senescence continues to be a significant area of research. In this review article, we aim to outline the role of senescence in cardiovascular disease and explore the potential of therapies targeting senescent cells.

Cardiomyocytes

Cardiomyocytes comprise 25–35% of the total number of cells in the heart [26]. Their cell cycle arrest cannot easily define the senescence of cardiomyocytes because they are terminally differentiated cells. Cardiomyocytes undergo cell cycle arrest due to the activation of the DNA damage response triggered by exposure to higher oxygen concentrations in the postnatal environment [27]. The accumulating environment indicates these cells retain proliferative capacity. It was reported that cardiomyocyte turnover was < 1% per year [28]. Senescent cardiomyocytes exhibit significant functional, morphological, and metabolic differences compared to normal cardiomyocytes. Hallmarks of senescent cardiomyocytes include mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage, contractile dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, SASP, and hypertrophic growth [29]. P53 promotes mitochondrial dysfunction and inhibits Parkin-mediated mitophagy in senescent cardiomyocytes, accumulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and telomere damage, standard features of DNA damage in rarely-dividing post-mitotic cells [30–32]. DNA damage-induced poly [ADP-ribose] polymerase 1 (PARP1) activation regulates contractile dysfunction and accumulates ER stress [33, 34]. At the same time, there were also reports that activating p53 signaling in vascular endothelial cells induces cardiac inflammation and exacerbates cardiac dysfunction and remodeling [35]. The SASP also plays a crucial role in influencing cardiac function through various mechanisms and pathological changes. Senescent cardiomyocytes include soluble signaling factors, proteases, and insoluble proteins or the extracellular matrix (ECM) components. Senescent cardiomyocytes release IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, Edn3, GATA4, GDF15, CCN1, MCP-1, TGF-β, matrix metalloproteinase (MMPs), and serine proteases [36]. SASP is crucial for communicating senescence cues from non-cardiomyocytes to cardiomyocytes, which regulates myocardial function. Dysfunction in contractility and conduction patterns in aging cardiomyocytes can lead to cardiomyopathies and ventricular arrhythmias [23, 37, 38]. Cardiomyocyte hypertrophy is associated with senescence. Anderson’s team found that in rodents, independent telomere damage in cardiomyocytes leads to a non-canonical SASP associated with pro-fibrotic and pro-hypertrophic responses factors [39]. However, SASP also offers protective effects in specific conditions. Recent research by Cui’s team has demonstrated that senescent cardiomyocytes exhibit a protective response under ischemic conditions, enhancing heart function through GATA-binding protein 4 (GATA4) -dependent cellular communication network factor 1 (CCN1) secretion, which prevents fibrotic changes and heart dysfunction following post-acute MI [40].

Cardiac fibroblasts

Fibroblasts are considered the primary cardiac cell component. Recent research indicates that they comprise around 10% of cells in the heart [39]. Studies indicate fibroblasts contribute to maintaining homeostasis in the heart under physiological and stressed conditions. Transformations in the mechanical microenvironment can induce fibroblasts to become myofibroblasts, characterized by the expression of α-smooth muscle actin (ASMA). Myofibroblasts, which share features of both fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells, contribute to tissue replacement and interstitial fibrosis following cardiac injury [40]. Senescent cardiac fibroblasts present a double-edged sword: while they can reduce collagen expression and alleviate chronic fibrosis, they also limit cardiac remodeling capabilities [41]. Studies indicate that fibroblasts play a role in maintaining homeostasis in the heart tissue, both under physiological conditions and during stress conditions. The senescence program in cardiac fibroblasts is critical for regulating cardiac fibrogenesis, relying on p53/p21 and p16/Rb pathways. Similarly to cardiomyocytes, under the influence of SASP, inducing senescence in cardiac fibroblasts via cardiotropic expression of CCN1 and GATA4 has cardioprotective effects, reducing fibrosis and improving cardiac function [42, 43].

Endothelial cells

Endothelial cells comprise 35% of cells in the heart, with over 95% consisting of vascular endothelial cells and the remainder being lymphatic endothelial cells [26]. The vascular endothelium regulates vascular system function and health by releasing cytokines to maintain vascular balance. However, dysfunctional endothelium loses this regulatory capacity, leading to a vasoconstrictive, oxidant, proinflammatory, and prothrombotic environment [42]. Endothelial dysfunction is often indicative of CVDs [43, 44]. Senescent endothelial cells exhibit conventional senescence characteristics such as DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, replication arrest, disrupted redox homeostasis, reduced NO production, and increased oxidative stress [45, 46]. Age-related eNOS dysfunction decreases tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4), and L-arginine contributes to NO reduction, impairing angiogenesis and disrupting metabolic pathways like nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) biosynthesis and sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) function [47–49]. The altered renin/angiotensin system (RAS) and eNOS uncoupling contribute to vasoconstrictive stress and vascular inflammation [50–52]. The factor P53 is also involved in endothelial cell senescence and significantly contributes to its regulatory mechanisms. A study showed that the depletion of p53 in endothelial cells alleviated capillary rarefaction, enhanced cardiac function, and reduced cardiac fibrosis remodeling [53].

Vascular smooth muscle cells

Vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) are the primary cells in the medial layer of blood vessels, crucial for vascular functions such as constriction, relaxation, blood pressure regulation, and arterial remodeling [54, 55]. VSMCs are susceptible to DNA damage, inflammation, oxidative stress, and Ang II, leading to senescence. Senescent VSMCs exhibit features similar to other senescent cells, including growth arrest, alterations in signaling pathways, and markers such as elevated SA-β-Gal, hypophosphorylated retinoblastoma protein (RB), and p16/p53/p21 expression [55, 56]. Unique to senescent VSMCs are changes in phenotype from contractile to synthetic, involving specific signaling pathway alterations [57].

Senescent VSMCs develop SASP, which is marked by the chronic release of proatherogenic cytokines and active matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9). They also produce inflammatory cytokines such as CCL2, CCL3/4, and IL-1/6/8, alongside anti-inflammatory cytokines and collagen down-regulated [58, 59]. Senescent VSMCs are present in aged vessels, advanced atherosclerotic lesions, and other vascular aging-related disorders such as abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA), hypertension, and diabetes [4]. Ang II-induced VSMC senescence is driven by increased ROS production. These senescent VSMCs contribute to various vascular injuries, necessitating further investigation.

Immune cells

Immune cells comprise 7% of non-cardiomyocytes in the heart, including 0.3% T cells, 0.8% B cells, and 7% myeloid cells [26]. The role of immune cells in cardiovascular senescence is intricate, as they identify and eliminate dysfunctional aging cells while also undergoing senescence, which results in decreased immune capacity and chronic inflammation. Immunosenescence is marked by the age-related buildup of memory T cells, decreased naïve T cells, diminished antigen response, and ongoing low-grade inflammation [60, 61]. Senescent T cells undergo genetic and epigenetic changes, loss of proteostasis, and thymic involution [61–63]. They often develop into memory T cells, producing increased pro-inflammatory cytokines that enhance inflammation. The SASP of senescent T cells includes IL-18, IFN-γ, Tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and osteopontin, which contribute to chronic inflammation. Reduced T cell receptor (TCR) repertoires [64], accumulation of memory T cells, and less-efficient T cells impair immune responses, promoting atherosclerosis and other CVDs. Additionally, the reduced ability of senescent T cells to clear pathogens and apoptotic cells contributes to the accumulation of harmful substances in the blood vessels.

Late memory B cells increase with age, showing pro-inflammatory capabilities and low proliferation capacity. They also express inhibitory receptors, which reduce B cell receptor signaling, proliferation, and antibody production [65, 66]. Dysfunction in high-affinity antibody production impairs the immune response, worsening atherosclerosis and other CVDs. Myeloid cells increase with aging, accumulating in blood, bone marrow, and secondary lymphoid organs, where they impair T-cell function and contribute to immune senescence by activating defective phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase–protein kinase B pathway (PI3K–Akt) signaling. This myeloid shift weakens adaptive immunity, including T-cell-mediated responses, and exacerbates chronic inflammation, a key driver of cardiovascular diseases [67].

In summary, senescent immune cells play a role in cardiovascular diseases by promoting chronic inflammation and impairing immune responses. Understanding their role in the cardiovascular system can inform the development of therapeutic strategies to delay or prevent age-related cardiovascular diseases.

Factors regulating senescence in cardiovascular diseases

Both endogenous and exogenous factors contribute to cardiovascular senescence, including but not limited to environment, metabolism, inflammation, and genetic factors. The aging process is characterized by a reduction in the complexity of physiological systems, which hinders the ability to respond to external stimuli and increases the risk of age-related cardiovascular diseases [68].

Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis is defined by lesions in the affected arteries beginning in the intima, followed by lipid accumulation, proliferation of fibrous tissue, and calcium deposits, resulting in progressive degeneration and calcification of the artery’s middle layer, followed by intraplaque hemorrhage, plaque rupture, and local thrombosis [69–71]. Early in the development of atherosclerosis, lipids and macrophages gather in the extra-endothelial space alongside VSMCs in the intima from the early lesions of atherosclerosis [72]. The carotid arteries show a notable increase in the thickening of vessel walls as they age [67].

VSMCs, ECs, and inflammatory cells exhibit significant variations among various senescent cells in atherosclerosis related to aging. They share several characteristics, including telomere shortening, DNA damage, and a SASP. Studies show that some cardiovascular factors contribute to the pathological process of atherosclerosis. In vivo, Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) increases the expression of IL-6 and accelerates the cycle of atherosclerosis. Reduced IL-6 signaling declines the risk of CVD, even in carriers of large CHIP clones [73]. MMPs are a family of zinc-dependent endopeptidases highly activated by inflammatory signals, including angiotensin II. Activated MMPs can degrade collagen, elastin, and other extracellular molecules, possibly contributing to aging and atherosclerotic effects in the arterial wall [74]. In a case-cohort analysis, MMP-9 levels were significantly higher in patients with atherosclerosis than those without it [75]. As mentioned above, endothelial dysfunction is strongly linked to the development of atherosclerosis. Increased MMP-1 enhances the number of senescent endothelial cells, which can be suppressed by the p53 pathway [76]. Moreover, the pro-inflammatory cytokines secreted by SASP lead to telomere shortening. Telomere shortening has been observed in vascular cells during replicative senescence as plaques form, potentially accelerating inflammatory responses [77].

Another distinctive feature of cellular senescence is the increased production of ROS. The mitochondrial respiratory chain is a major source of reactive oxygen species, and reactive oxygen species generated by mitochondria can worsen cellular senescence through DNA damage [78]. Reactive oxygen radicals produced by mitochondria can also damage the mitochondria themselves, including mitochondrial proteins, lipids, and DNA, being oxidatively modified, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, a well-known process called “ROS-induced ROS”. In a normal situation, endogenous antioxidants regulate this process of ROS. However, when oxidative stress and excessive production of ROS occur, it damages the arterial wall and initiates the process of atherosclerotic plaque formation [79, 80].

Pulmonary hypertension

Pulmonary hypertension is characterized by progressive pulmonary vascular remodeling, leading to right ventricular failure [81]. The alteration of pulmonary vascular structure and function is the primary cause of blood flow resistance and HF development. During the remodeling process, pulmonary artery endothelial cells (PAECs), pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs), and ECM play roles in proliferation and structural transformation. Under hypoxia-induced VSMC senescence in the pulmonary arteries, Nutlin-3a activated p53, reducing pulmonary vascular muscularization. This change improved right heart hemodynamics and contributed to reversing pulmonary hypertension [82]. In agreement, treating animals with pulmonary hypertension using senolytics (ABT263) to eliminate senescent pulmonary endothelial cells worsens pulmonary hypertension [83].

The activated mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway induced the senescence of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) with SASP. SASP encourages excessive cell proliferation and structural remodeling, resulting in pulmonary hypertension [84]. Similarly, the ECM is a relatively balanced and dynamic environment mainly made up of collagens and a variety of other proteins, including basement membrane (BM), elastin, laminin, collagen IV, fibronectin, and tenascin C proteoglycan [85]. SASP releases pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic factors that can attract immune cells and result in the abnormal accumulation of excess ECM, leading to cellular dysfunction in the pulmonary artery system [86]. Additionally, senescent PASMCs secrete ECM proteins called osteopontin, which mediate the stimulatory effects of senescent PASMC media and matrix on PASMC growth and migration, contributing to the progression of pulmonary hypertension [87].

Myocardial infarction

MI is a myocardial injury involving large-scale cardiomyocyte loss because of consistent ischemia [88]. After MI, the activation of p16INK4a and p53, and upregulation of SA-β-gal enzyme activity, leads to cardiomyocyte senescence. This implies that senescent cardiomyocytes participate in the process of myocardial damage and impaired heart function [89]. When MI occurs, it leads to a significant decrease in energy supplements. PARP1 and SIRT1 increase NAD+ consumption, contributing to cellular energy crises and cell death [90]. The study showed that Hypertrophic growth and contractile dysfunction are related to healing defects in aging hearts. Transforming growth factor (TGF)-β is a factor in hypertrophic and dilated ventricular processes [91]. Isolated fibroblasts from senescent hearts show a delayed response to TGF stimulation, suggesting that senescence has impaired the fibroblast response to the growth factor and has contributed to adverse remodeling [92].

Additionally, senescent cardiomyocytes hinder non-cardiomyocytes’ functions, including endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells, thereby worsening MI [93]. In agreement, after MI, the knockdown of p53 reduces fibroblast senescence and inflammation, which increases fibrosis during the early stage [94]. T cells are classic immune cells in adaptive immunity; however, their functional role in cardiac pathophysiology remains unclear. δT lymphocytes primarily produce IL-17A, IL-17A enhances cardiomyocyte apoptosis during myocardial ischemia/reperfusion [95]. The senescence of ECs also plays a significant role in MI. Senescent endothelial cells contribute to thrombogenicity by releasing endothelium-derived microparticles that attract platelets [96].

Heart failure

HF is strongly linked to aging, with preserved systolic function showing left ventricular hypertrophy, which is highly prevalent in elderly patients [97, 98]. In HF, cardiac aging is associated with left ventricular hypertrophy and fibrosis; senescent heart cells show impaired responses to cardiac injury, leading to adverse remodeling and functional decline [99, 100]. The accumulation of senescent cells inhibits the activity of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1 and adversely affects cardiac angiogenesis and systolic function [101]. The heart is under constant pressure overload, which increases the number of p53-expressing cells. Cardiomyocyte remodeling begins with p53-independent mitochondrial activation and is characterized by hypertrophy. However, volume overload stimulation resulted in p53-dependent heart failure [102, 103].

The classic characteristic of senescent cardiomyocytes, telomere length, significantly influences heart failure, and telomere shortening occurs in senescent cardiomyocytes of heart failure patients and is linked to mitochondrial dysfunction. Likewise, telomere length is clearer in patients with chronic heart failure leukocytes than in healthy individuals [104]. The shortest telomere occurs in hypertrophic hearts that exhibit a decreased ejection fraction (EF) [31]. Knockout proviral integration of moloney murine leukemia virus 1 (PIM1) mice develop HF by 6 months, and PIM1 is a kinase that provides protective effects on mitochondria and the length of telomeres, as the levels of p16INK4a, p53, and SA-β-gal increase alongside the deterioration of the mitochondrial structure and reduced cardiac function [105].

Studies have shown that aging is associated with an increased presence of senescent immune cells in the context of immune cell involvement in HF. This study claimed that the highest p16INK4a/SA-β-gal positive macrophage cell prevalence occurs in aging mice [106]. Depending on the macrophage phenotype, it plays a role in maintaining tissue homeostasis and preventing cardiovascular disease. Macrophages produce IL-10, activate fibroblasts, and stimulate collagen deposition during the development of diastolic dysfunction, indicating cardiac aging and HF. This suggests macrophages may act as a threat marker for HF [107]. In addition to macrophages, senescent-like T cells influence HF [108]. As proof, senescent CD4 + T cells exacerbate myocardial inflammation and stress, resulting in HF [109]. Interestingly, immune cells act as a double-edged sword during the pathological process of HF because they also benefit HF through their clearance function [10].

The discovery of cellular senescence begins with fibroblasts, and myofibroblasts may develop from the transdifferentiation of fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells [110, 111]. The continuous activation of myofibroblasts leads to long-term fibrotic changes and HF [112]. Senescent fibroblasts worsen cardiac fibrosis by secreting SASP components that encourage collagen deposition and activate adjacent fibroblasts through nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) signaling pathways. This self-reinforcing loop enhances extracellular matrix remodeling, resulting in ventricular stiffening and diastolic dysfunction, a hallmark of heart failure in the aging heart.

Senescence in heart regeneration: dual effects

Hearts can regenerate in zebrafish and urodele amphibians, including newts and axolotls, during the early neonatal period of mammals [113–116]. Still, adult mammalian cardiomyocytes’ regeneration capacity is relatively low, despite their limited ability to re-enter the cell cycle [28]. Over the past decades, there has been remarkable progress in heart regeneration. Heart regeneration encompasses many concepts, including the native cardiomyocyte renewal potential, cell therapy, direct reprogramming, and tissue engineering methods for repairing damaged hearts [117].

The beneficial side of senescence in heart regeneration

Senescence is often linked to age-related cardiovascular disease; however, it also plays a crucial role in the proper development and regeneration of the heart. Various molecular and cytological indicators, including the up-regulation of SA-β-gal activity and proteins like p16INK4a, p21, and p53 mark senescent cells. For instance, in adult mice, agrin, an ECM molecule, can trigger the expression of p53 in the epicardium and scar region, demonstrating a transient regenerative senescence in zebrafish and neonatal mouse hearts [118].

As we all know, senescence is a cell cycle arrest induced by stressors such as DNA damage and oncogenes. Senescent cells secrete various proteins collectively known as the SASP, which can reinforce the arrest and induce senescence in paracrine. Methods have been employed to examine whether SASP can enhance heart regeneration. In an in vitro study, they performed apical resection (AR) and collected hearts for SASP staining at various time points following AR. They found that CCN1 was highly expressed in the periresected regions 4 days post-AR. They observed that CCN1 treatment induced fibroblast senescence and triggered the expression of SASP, including IL-1α and IL-6, in vitro. Furthermore, Treatment with IL-6 and IL-1α promoted neonatal cardiomyocyte proliferation by enhancing cardiomyocyte growth and reducing cardiac fibrosis [119]. The beneficial aspects are linked to transient senescence because SASP can stimulate angiogenesis and regulate the inflammatory response to promote repair.

SASP factors such as IL-6 exhibit a “double-edged sword” effect in myocardial repair. IL-6 can stimulate the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), promoting neovascularization and facilitating reperfusion in ischemic regions. IL-6 regulates the release of chemokines, attracting bone marrow-derived stem cells or immune cells to the damaged myocardium to participate in repair. IL-6 activates the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway, enhancing the proliferation of cardiomyocytes and adult stem cells while improving their resistance to apoptosis. However, persistent overexpression of IL-6 can maintain a state of chronic low-grade inflammation, activate fibroblasts, and promote ECM deposition, leading to myocardial fibrosis and impaired diastolic function. IL-6 can form a positive feedback loop with TGF-β, enhancing collagen synthesis and further exacerbating ventricular remodeling. IL-6 may be beneficial during the acute phase, but chronic or sustained activation can aggravate myocardial injury [120].

The detrimental side of senescence in heart regeneration

On the contrary, other researchers found that senescence is detrimental to heart regeneration. Previous studies have documented the selective use of senolytic drugs (Dasatinib, Quercetin, Fisetin, and Navitoclax) to clear senescent cells from aging tissues. Using dasatinib་quercetin (D+Q) senolytics cleared the senescent CPCs and reduced the SASP, along with its effect on promoting senescence. The global elimination of senescent cells in the heart activates resident CPCs and increases their cardiomyocyte number. Therefore, eliminating senescent cells might enhance cardiac health during aging and restore the heart’s regenerative capacity [121, 122].

Specific senescent cells, such as CPCs, have a diminished capacity to promote regeneration. Conversely, suppressing cell senescence enhances the function and therapeutic capacity of transplanted or resident stem cells [121, 123]. For example, retinoblastoma 1 (Rb1)/meis homeobox 2 (Meis2) mediated cell cycle arrest plays a significant role in the senescent phenotype of adult cardiomyocytes. In this study, the researchers induced the proliferation of adult rat cardiomyocytes through small interfering (si) RNA-based repression of Rb1 and Meis2, two negative cell cycle regulators [124]. Also, statins can inhibit the senescence of endothelial progenitor cells (EPC) and promote the proliferation of EPC in vitro, representing a potential cell therapy for heart regeneration [125] (Fig. 2).

Regenerative medicine in senescence in the cardiovascular system

Cardiovascular medicine has seen significant advancements with the advent of stem cell therapy, providing new hope for treating heart diseases [126, 127]. MSCs and iPSCs have demonstrated considerable potential for cardiac repair and regeneration among the different types of stem cells. Each type of stem cell has unique characteristics and applications that render them suitable candidates for various aspects of cardiovascular therapy. These cells address cardiovascular aging through synergistic mechanisms, such as cell replacement, paracrine effects, and modulation of microenvironmental factors. This section examines their unique therapeutic roles in combating cardiac aging [128, 129].

Mesenchymal stem cells

MSCs have garnered significant attention in recent years for their suitability in cellular therapies due to their high growth potential, multipotential differentiation capacity, and ability to reduce fibrosis through TGF-β secretion and ECM remodeling via MMPs [130, 131]. They secrete growth factors such as VEGF, insulin-like growth factor 1(IGF-1), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), and cytokines that promote cardiomyocyte recovery’s survival, proliferation, and functionality by paracrine effects and immune regulation.

Their paracrine activity involves exosomes enriched with miRNAs (miRNA-21, miRNA-146a) that suppress senescence markers (SA-β-gal, p16INK4a/P21) by inhibiting the p53/p21 axis, which enhances cardiomyocyte proliferation, DNA repair, and angiogenesis while reducing oxidative stress and fibrosis [132, 133]. These exosomes also promote vascular endothelial repair via VEGF and bFGF, enhancing perfusion and reducing endothelial senescence [134]. In immune regulation, MSCs counteract SASP-driven inflammation by releasing PGE2 and IL-10, which reduces SA-β-gal + cell accumulation [135] and further inhibits NF-κB signaling to mitigate age-related cardiac inflammation [136]. Mitochondrial protection is accomplished through the secretion of antioxidants, mitochondrial biogenesis, and the direct transfer of healthy mitochondria to aging cells, thereby restoring energy metabolism and reducing ROS through the delivery of superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) and catalase (CAT) [137].

Preclinical studies show the efficacy of MSCs in MI and HF, with optimized delivery methods enhancing engraftment and functional recovery [138, 139]. Clinically, MSCs transplantation improves cardiac function in aging patients by working together to address fibrosis, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation [140].

MSCs and iPSCs exhibit therapeutic potential; however, their functional heterogeneity is significantly influenced by factors such as cell source, cellular subpopulations, and culture conditions. MSCs derived from different tissues—such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, and umbilical cord—show marked differences in paracrine profiles, immunomodulatory capacity, proliferation, and differentiation potential. Adipose-derived MSCs (AD-MSCs) secrete higher levels of pro-angiogenic and anti-inflammatory factors, including VEGF and IL-10, and are generally more effective in tissue repair models. Bone marrow-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs) provide stronger hematopoietic support and secrete paracrine factors like IL-6 and TGF-β, making them suitable for immunomodulation and anti-fibrosis purposes [141]. In addition, MSCs are not a homogeneous population. CD271⁺ MSC subpopulations are considered to have enhanced osteogenic potential and immunoregulatory function, along with more active paracrine signaling [142]. Moreover, the influence of culture conditions, such as hypoxic preconditioning, can simulate the in vivo microenvironment, thereby improving MSC survival, migration, and paracrine activity. Therefore, optimizing source selection, refining processing methods, and enriching specific subpopulations are key strategies for achieving personalized and effective cell therapy.

Induced pluripotent stem cells

iPSCs, generated by reprogramming somatic cells to pluripotency, provide personalized cardiac regeneration through direct cell replacement, paracrine signaling, and fibrosis inhibition via HGF secretion. This complements MSC-mediated TGF-β effects [130]. They differentiate into all types of cardiac cells, including cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and smooth muscle cells, which enables structural and functional repair.

Transplanted iPS-derived cardiomyocytes (iPS-CMs) integrate into damaged myocardium, restoring contractile function and improving left ventricular EF by 10–15% in infarct models [143]. Their paracrine effects include the secretion of VEGF, IGF-1, and HGF, which promote angiogenesis, inhibit apoptosis, and suppress SA-β-gal cells by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway [144]. Exosomal miRNAs (miRNA-21, miRNA-199a) inhibit senescence pathways, while upregulating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) and SOD2 improve mitochondrial function and decrease oxidative stress [145–147]. iPSCs also modulate the cardiac microenvironment by suppressing SASP and nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling, which reduces inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction [136]. As iPS-CMs possess paracrine effects and the functional properties of cardiomyocytes, they exhibit greater potential for myocardial functional replacement.

Recently, several clinical trials utilizing iPSCs to treat cardiovascular diseases have advanced, particularly focusing on heart failure and ischemic cardiomyopathy. The LAPiS Trial by Heartseed Inc. was a phase I/II, open-label study that enrolled 10 patients to evaluate the safety and efficacy of HS-001, allogeneic iPSC-derived cardiomyocyte spheroids, in patients with advanced heart failure due to ischemic heart disease. The early results demonstrate the potential to deliver functional improvement in patients with advanced heart failure, where enhancements from coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) alone were limited and presented at the European Society of Cardiology Heart Failure Congress in May 2024 [148]. The Osaka University Cardiomyocyte Sheet trial was an investigator-initiated study involving 10 patients to evaluate the safety and efficacy of iPSC-derived cardiomyocyte sheets transplanted onto the heart surface in patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy. As of June 2023, eight patients received transplants, all showing symptom improvement. They reported that the clinical symptoms improved 1 year after surgery without significant adverse events in the first three cases, suggesting that the patches were well tolerated. Left ventricular contractility and myocardial blood flow improvements were observed in two patients [149, 150].

The HiCM-188 Trial was a Phase I/IIa study focused on dose escalation and extension to assess safety, tolerability, and preliminary efficacy. This randomized, double-blind study involved 36 participants testing allogeneic iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes in patients with severe heart failure in China. Therapeutics are ongoing, and no official trial results have been published yet. The BioVAT-HF-DZHK20 trial is a groundbreaking Phase I/II clinical study evaluating the safety and efficacy of engineered human myocardium (EHM), a heart patch derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), as a novel treatment for patients with advanced heart failure in Germany. The study involved ten patients who received EHM patches containing 200 million, 400 million, or 800 million cells. The therapy was tolerated across all doses, with no significant adverse events reported.

Despite their potential for disease modeling and the development of personalized treatments for aging-related cardiac conditions, the functional heterogeneity of iPSCs primarily arises from their reprogramming techniques, originating cell types, and differentiation protocols. iPSC-derived MSCs often exhibit variable traits across different labs, underscoring the need for stricter quality control and standardization.

Many challenges are still associated with cell therapy, including immune rejection of allogeneic stem cell transplantation. Although autologous stem cells can avoid immune rejection, their collection and expansion are costly, and the quality may decline in elderly patients. Additionally, there are risks of ectopic differentiation and tumorigenesis due to the high differentiation potential of stem cells. Furthermore, after transplantation, stem cells encounter challenges such as low engraftment and survival rates, ischemia, oxidative stress, and inflammation in the microenvironment, which often leads to poor long-term survival and limited functionality. iPS cells exhibit differentiation efficiency and consistency; however, the differentiation of iPS cells into target cell types usually suffers from low efficiency and variability between batches, which impacts clinical reliability and predictability. iPS cells are considered a breakthrough in regenerative medicine due to their broad availability, self-renewal capacity, and ability to differentiate into various cell types. Nevertheless, large-scale, safe, and practical clinical applications require further advancements in genetic stability, tumor safety, differentiation technologies, and manufacturing standards.

Targeting cellular senescence

In recent years, targeted therapies for cellular senescence, also known as senolysis, have demonstrated significant potential in treating cardiovascular diseases. Research indicates the dual role of the p53 signaling pathway in regulating senescence: in natural aging models, p53 offers protective effects by suppressing pathological cardiac hypertrophy, while its overactivation exacerbates cardiac dysfunction under pressure overload. Approaches targeting p16INK4a -marked senescent cells have become a focal point. For example, the inducible p16INK4a apoptosis through targeted activation of caspase (INK-ATTAC) transgenic model reverses myocardial fibrosis and enhances stress tolerance by eliminating p16INK4a positive cells. Pharmacological agents such as ABT263 (a BCL-2 family inhibitor) and the combination of dasatinib with quercetin (D+Q) selectively eliminate senescent cells, improving left ventricular function and promoting cardiac progenitor cell regeneration in aged animal models [89].

Brief treatment with ABT263, administered intraperitoneally, reversed the PH-related hemodynamic and structural changes. The protective ability of ABT263 against PH was attributed to the suppression of growth promoters released by senescent pulmonary endothelial cells (P-ECs). Short-term therapy with ABT263 was given to rats with MCT-induced PH, resulting in decreased pulmonary arterial pressure and pulmonary vessel remodeling. However, prolonged treatment with ABT263 over 3 weeks worsened the PH, as it can be toxic to non-senescent proliferating cells. Thus, depending on the administration methods, ABT263 may exert strong antiproliferative effects capable of limiting the progression of PH. D+Q can specifically recognize and eliminate senescent cells that express anti-apoptotic pathways (such as BCL-2, PI3K/AKT, p53/p21), thereby reducing the sustained release of SASP factors and, in turn, alleviating tissue inflammation and dysfunction. This combination broadens the scope of targeted senescent cell types. However, dasatinib is a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) with anticancer activity, which may also affect normal proliferating cells. If senolytic agents indiscriminately clear senescent pulmonary endothelial cells (P-ECs), essential for vascular homeostasis, it could disrupt vascular integrity, potentially leading to pulmonary vascular remodeling or even exacerbating pulmonary hypertension.

There are still many challenges to senolytic drugs, including off-target effects. Senolytics, when administered in high doses or over extended periods, can unintentionally eliminate functional senescent cells and highlight the double-edged nature of SASP. While SASP contains many pro-inflammatory factors, some components promote tissue repair, angiogenesis, and immune modulation. Indiscriminate elimination of these cells may impair regenerative capacity. Most studies remain in the animal or early clinical trial phase, and long-term safety data in humans are still lacking. Future research and clinical applications will require the development of more precise cell identification techniques, safer delivery systems, and individualized treatment strategies to realize the therapeutic potential of these approaches fully (Fig. 3).

Conclusion

This review outlined the role of cellular senescence and cell therapy in cardiovascular disease. The study of senescence in the cardiovascular system reveals a complex interplay of beneficial and detrimental effects on heart regeneration. Several factors, such as genetic mutations, telomere shortening, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress, influence the balance of the impacts of cellular senescence. Factors that benefit heart regeneration include the secretory functions of senescent cells, modulation of inflammatory responses, and regulation of stem cell activity. Conversely, factors that hinder heart regeneration encompass excessive accumulation of senescent cells, the adverse effects of SASP, inhibition of stem cell function, fibrosis, and tissue stiffening. Cardiovascular aging plays a dual role in heart regeneration. Senescent cells’ moderate presence and function may promote heart regeneration through inflammation and stem cell activation. However, the buildup of senescent cells, excessive secretion of SASP factors, and increased fibrosis can negatively impact heart regeneration.

Additionally, common senescence markers, such as SA-β-gal, p16INK4a, γH2AX, and SASP factors, should be combined to enhance detection accuracy, specificity, and reliability. Transplanting MSCs and iPS-CMs, or using senolytic drugs to eliminate these cells, shows promise in restoring regenerative capacity and improving heart function. We should aim to grasp the complexities of senescence regulation and develop innovative interventions to enhance cardiovascular health and longevity.
