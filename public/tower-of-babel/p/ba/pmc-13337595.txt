Triparental synthetic yeast hybrids as a platform for higher 2-phenylethanol yields and stress resistance
Authors: Karolina Drężek, Zsuzsa Antunovics, Maria Krystyna Sobczyk, Agnieszka Karolina Grabiec, Jolanta Mierzejewska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract2‑Phenylethanol (2‑PE) is a valuable aromatic alcohol widely used in the cosmetic, food, and pharmaceutical industries, yet its cytotoxicity remains a major bottleneck for microbial production. In this study, we evaluated a set of previously constructed triple hybrid yeast strains (H1-H5), derived from S. cerevisiae 10‑170 and the double hybrid II/6, to assess their potential for enhanced 2‑PE biosynthesis and tolerance. Comprehensive phenotypic analysis revealed that the triple hybrids consistently outperformed both parental strains, reaching 2-PE titers of up to 3 g/L within 72 h across different cultivation media. Depending on the parental strain used for comparison and the medium composition, the hybrids showed even up to 9-fold higher 2-PE production. Increase in production was observed under all tested cultivation conditions, indicating a robust and reproducible phenotype. In addition, the hybrids displayed increased tolerance to externally supplied 2-PE; for example, strain H1 retained 61% of its growth relative to untreated controls in the presence of 4 g/L 2-PE. The hybrids effectively combined advantageous traits from both parents, inheriting high production capacity from II/6 and strong tolerance from S. cerevisiae 10‑170, in some cases exceeding both. These findings highlight multi‑parental hybridisation as a scalable, non‑GMO strategy for developing robust yeast cell factories for industrial 2‑PE production and broader biotechnological applications.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11274-026-05126-2.Keywords: Hybrids, Saccharomyces, 2-phenylethanol, Stress tolerance

Introduction

In recent years, the construction of de novo yeast hybrids has attracted increasing attention due to their value in industrial applications such as biofuel production, brewing, and winemaking (García-Ríos et al. 2019; Snoek et al. 2015). Phenotypic diversification in these strains is commonly achieved through approaches including protoplast fusion, spore-based mating strategies, or rare mating events, as comprehensively reviewed by Steensels et al. (2014). Importantly, the significance of interspecies hybridization extends beyond the simple combination of advantageous parental traits. Hybrid genomes frequently undergo extensive restructuring, including chromosomal rearrangements, sequence mutations, and gene copy number variation, which can rewire regulatory networks and metabolic pathways. These genomic alterations can drive the emergence of novel phenotypes through heterotic effects (Naseeb et al. 2021a).

Although yeast hybrid development has been widely explored for applications in the alcohol and bioenergy sectors, its potential for alternative biotechnological outcomes remains comparatively underinvestigated. Several studies have reported pronounced shifts in aroma compound production in hybrid yeasts; however, such observations are largely confined to wine and beer fermentations and are typically evaluated under alcoholic fermentation conditions (Chen et al. 2023; Ortiz-Tovar et al. 2019). This narrow focus highlights an opportunity to explore yeast crossbreeding as a strategy for generating novel lineages with tailored aroma profiles, particularly for the targeted biosynthesis of specific flavor compounds beyond conventional fermentation systems, such as 2-phenylethanol (2-PE).

2-PE is an aliphatic alcohol characterized by a distinctive rose-like floral aroma and is widely used in the food, fragrance, and cosmetic industries. Its broad applicability has driven an estimated annual market demand growth of approximately 10–15%, while the global supply of 2-PE remains insufficient to meet this increasing demand (2-Phenylethanol Market Size & Share 2022 to 2028 2022). In addition to its olfactory value, 2-PE exhibits antimicrobial activity, enabling its use as a preservative in cosmetic formulations.

Fermentation-derived 2-PE is comparable to natural extracts in quality and offers economic competitiveness with chemical synthesis, while avoiding associated environmental risks. Consequently, the development of efficient and environmentally sustainable microbial platforms for 2-PE production has become an active area of research. Among the reported microbial producers (Gu et al. 2020; Kong et al. 2020), the generally regarded as safe (GRAS) yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae has received particular attention due to its native 2-PE biosynthetic capacity, robust performance via the Ehrlich pathway, and relatively high tolerance to 2-PE-associated stress (Eshkol et al. 2009; Etschmann et al. 2002; Jin et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2025).

Beyond S. cerevisiae, other members of the Saccharomyces genus also exhibit promising 2-PE biosynthetic potential but remain comparatively poorly studied. Notably, Zhao et al. (2024) recently reported a highly efficient wild-type Saccharomyces bayanus strain capable of substantial 2-PE production. In our previous work, representatives of Saccharomyces uvarum and Saccharomyces kudriavzevii were also examined (Drężek et al. 2024). In that study, we also successfully characterized a novel interspecies hybrid (II/6) derived from S. uvarum and S. kudriavzevii. The hybrid was subjected to detailed analyses of its 2-PE bioconversion capacity and tolerance to elevated concentrations of this compound in comparison with its parental strains. The II/6 hybrid demonstrated markedly enhanced productivity alongside significantly improved resistance to high 2-PE titers, indicating that interspecies hybridization can simultaneously enhance yield while mitigating product-associated toxicity.

In the present study, we extend this strategy to the characterization of triple hybrids. Specifically, the previously described hybrid II/6 was crossed with S. cerevisiae strain. The strain selected for this work exhibited pronounced resistance to exogenously supplied 2-PE, providing a suitable genetic background for further strain improvement. By integrating advantageous traits from multiple Saccharomyces species, this approach aims to enhance the biotechnological performance of hybrid yeast strains and to further evaluate triple hybridization as a rational strategy for targeted aroma compound production.

Materials and methods

Yeast strains

The yeasts S. cerevisiae 10–170 (Antunovics et al. 2005) (MATaSc hoSc leu2Sc), interspecific hybrid II/6 S. uvarum 10-1651 x S. kudriavzevii 10-1653 (MATaSk/MATalphaSu hoSk/hoSu ADE2Sk/ade2Su trp1Sk/TRP1Su ura3Sk/ura3Su) (Sipiczki et al. 2020) and triple hybrids H1-H5 S. cerevisiae 10–170 x II/6 (MATaSc/MATaSk/MATalphaSu hoSc/hoSk/hoSu ADE2Sc/ADE2Sk/ade2Su TRP1Sc/trp1Sk/TRP1Su URA3Sc/ura3Sk/ura3Su leu2Sc/LEU2Sk/LEU2Su) were used in this study. Data on obtaining the II/6 hybrid and triple H1-H5 hybrids can be found in recent work (Antunovics et al. 2023).

Media and culture conditions

Strains were maintained on YPDA medium containing 2% (w/v) glucose (Bioshop, Burlington, Canada), 2% (w/v) bacteriological peptone (Bioshop), 1% (w/v) yeast extract (Bioshop), and 2% (w/v) agar (Bioshop), and stored at 4 °C.

Assimilation of single carbon sources

Growth on individual carbon sources was evaluated on solid media containing 0.67% (w/v) yeast nitrogen base with ammonium sulfate (Bioshop) and 2% (w/v) agar. Each medium was supplemented with a single carbon source at 2% (w/v or v/v): D-glucose (Bioshop), D-fructose (Bioshop), D-xylose (Carl Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany), galactose (Carl Roth), maltose (Bioshop), lactose (Carl Roth), sucrose (Bioshop), D-cellobiose (Fluka, Buchs, Switzerland), ethanol (POCH, Gliwice, Poland), or glycerol (Bioshop).

Temperature tolerance

Temperature-related growth tests were performed on YPDA medium.

Extracellular enzyme activity

Lipase activity was determined on Spirit Blue agar (Becton Dickinson, New Jersey, USA) supplemented with a 20% (v/v) olive oil/Tween 80 emulsion. Protease activity was assessed on nutrient agar (MERCK, Darmstadt, Germany) containing 10% (v/v) skimmed milk (Łaciate, Poland). Amylase activity was evaluated on nutrient agar (MERCK) supplemented with 2% (w/v) starch (PPZ Trzemeszno, Polska). Cellulase activity was tested on yeast nitrogen base with ammonium sulfate lacking amino acids (Conda, Madrid, Spain), supplemented with 2% (w/v) carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) (Sigma Aldrich).

2-PE tolerance assays

Cultures used to assess the effects of exogenous 2-PE on yeast growth were cultivated in YPD medium (YPDA without agar).

2-PE biosynthesis

Biosynthesis of 2-PE via the Ehrlich pathway was evaluated using NEO (Etschmann et al. 2002) and P8 (Chreptowicz et al. 2016) media, both supplemented with L-phenylalanine (L-Phe), the precursor for 2-PE production. For S. cerevisiae 10–170, P8 medium was additionally supplemented with leucine (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) due to the strain’s leucine auxotrophy.

Biochemical and physiological profiling of parental and hybrid strains

Microscopic observations

Microscopic observations were performed using overnight cultures in YPD examined under a light microscope at 1000× magnification (Nicon Plan Apo VC 100x/1,40 oil, OFN25 DIC N2). Images were captured and subsequently analyzed with dedicated image-analysis software EOS Utility, which was used to measure the length and width of individual cells. For each strain, measurements were obtained from 30 randomly selected cells to ensure representative morphological assessment.

Spore formation

Spore formation was assessed by streaking each strain onto acetate medium (1% (w/v) potassium acetate (VWR, Radnor, PA, USA), 0.5% (w/v) yeast extract (VWR), 0.25% (w/v) glucose (VWR), and 2% (w/v) agar (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA)), followed by incubation at 25 °C for 5 days. Sporulating cultures were examined by phase contrast at 400x magnification (Olympus BX40, Tokyo, Japan). Ten tetrads per strain were dissected using a Zeiss 2588 micromanipulator (Jena, Germany), and spore viability was evaluated based on colony formation after an additional 5 days of incubation at 25 °C.

Assimilation of single carbon sources and temperature tolerance

Growth on individual carbon sources at 30 °C and growth at different temperatures (4–42 °C) were evaluated on solid media. Plates were inoculated with material from a single colony and incubated for 2–4 days.

Extracellular enzyme activity

Extracellular enzymatic activities were determined using qualitative plate assays. Cultures were incubated for 2–3 days at 30 °C on media specific for each enzyme. Lipase and protease activities were confirmed by the presence of clear zones surrounding colonies. Amylolytic and cellulolytic activities were indicated by the appearance of light halos around colonies after flooding the plates with Lugol’s iodine.

Effect of exogenous 2-PE on yeast growth

The effect of exogenous 2-phenylethanol on yeast growth was evaluated as described previously (Mierzejewska et al. 2017). Briefly, overnight cultures of the tested strains were diluted to an initial OD600 of 0.15 in 50 mL of fresh YPD medium in 100-mL flasks and incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 240 rpm. When the cultures reached an OD600 of approximately 0.6–0.8, 2-PE (Merck) was added to six flasks, in triplicate, to final concentrations of 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 g/L. A seventh culture without 2-PE supplementation served as the control. Cultivation continued for 48 h, and growth was monitored by OD600 measurements. In addition, microscopic observations were performed after 24 h of incubation.

2-PE production in batch cultures

A sterile loop containing biomass from a single colony was used to inoculate 100 mL of YPD medium in an Erlenmeyer flask, which was incubated overnight at 30 °C. The resulting overnight culture was subsequently diluted into 50 mL of P8 medium supplemented with leucine (for S. cerevisiae) or NEO medium (for 2-PE production via the Ehrlich pathway) in 100 mL Erlenmeyer flasks, adjusted to an initial OD600 of approximately 0.15. Batch cultures were incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 240 rpm (LAB Companion SI-600R, Ramsey, MN, USA) for 72 h in triplicate. At designated time points, 1 mL samples were collected for determination of OD600, sugar content, and 2-PE concentration.

Phenotypic stability cultures

Cultures of strains H1 and H2 were maintained over three consecutive generations in medium NEO at 30 °C (240 rpm) and were started as described in Sect. 2-PE production in batch cultures. After each 72-hour period, 1 mL of the culture was used to initiate the next generation (the initial OD600 in Generation 2 and 3 ranged from approximately 0.15 to 0.2). During the cultures, samples were taken during the first 8 h of incubation to determine specific growth rate, doubling time, lag phase and after 24, 48 and 72 h for HPLC analyses and microscopic assessment of the culture. At the end of the cultures samples were also collected for determination of DCW and cfu/mL.

Analytical methods

The cell density in liquid medium samples was monitored by measuring OD600 using a VIS-7220G spectrophotometer (Beijing Rayleigh Analytical Instrument Corporation Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

2-PE, glucose, sucrose, and ethanol concentrations were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) as described previously (Drężek et al. 2024). Briefly, to examine the 2-PE titer an isocratic method comprising 70:30 2% formic acid/acetonitrile with 2% formic acid was used at a flow rate of 1 mL/min (Bionacom Velocity C18-2 column 250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; DAD detector set at 256 nm). Glucose, sucrose and ethanol content was determined by HPLC coupled with an RI detector and eluted isocratically at a flow rate of 1 mL/min using 9 mM H2SO4 (SETREX IEX H + column 300 × 8 mm column, Polymer IEX H form, 8 μm, temperature-controlled at 35 °C).

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean values ± SD (n = 6) obtained from three independent experiments measured in duplicate. Statistical comparisons were performed between groups using one-way ANOVA test; p value ≤ 0.05 was the criterion for statistical significance. All analyses were performed in Prism 10 software Graph Pad (Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Metabolic profiling of hybrid yeasts

As an initial step in characterizing the investigated strains, morphological, physiological, and biochemical analyses were conducted. To describe their properties, we assessed the parental strains (S. cerevisiae 10–170 (S.c 10–170) and the II/6 hybrid) together with the interspecific triple hybrids (H1-H5) for cell morphology, sporulation ability, growth at different temperatures, assimilation of individual carbon sources, and hydrolytic activity (Table 1, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

We observed that the triple hybrids generally exhibited a more oval morphology; the length‑to‑width ratio of the parental strains averaged 1.20 and 1.32, whereas in the triple hybrids it ranged from 1.37 to 1.50. Moreover, hybrids H1 and H2 were characterized by increased cell length and width. Besides, the parental hybrid strain II/6 and all triple hybrids did produce spores, but the spores were not capable to form colonies. H1-H5 strains were able to grow up to 37 °C like II/6 parent. All tested strains were glucose, fructose, sucrose, galactose, and maltose positive. Most strains, except II/6 hybrid, were also ethanol positive. When analysing the ability to produce extracellular hydrolases, only weak lipolytic activity was observed for parents and H1, H4.

2-Phenylethanol synthesis through L-phenylalanine catabolism

In this study, we investigated the biosynthetic capacity for 2‑PE via the Ehrlich pathway, using two media that differed in carbon and nitrogen composition, their concentrations, and the amount of L‑Phe supplied (Chreptowicz et al. 2016; Etschmann et al. 2004). The NEO medium contains glucose, yeast extract, and L-Phe, whereas the P8 medium consists of glucose and sucrose, YNB without amino acids or ammonium sulfate, and L-Phe, thereby providing conditions optimized for the Ehrlich pathway. Based on our previous findings, all fermentations were conducted at 30 °C, identified as the optimal temperature for 2-PE biosynthesis.

During cultivation, samples were collected at selected time points for monitoring yeast growth and calculating specific growth rate, quantification of 2-PE using HPLC and evaluating space time yields. The growth curves indicated that all triple hybrid strains and II/6 parent exhibited nearly identical growth dynamics (Fig. 1). The calculated specific growth rates were comparable among the tested strains, ranging from 0.22 to 0.25 1/h in P8 medium and from 0.23 to 0.27 1/h in NEO medium, except for H1 strain, for which the calculated specific growth rate was 0.30 1/h. In both tested media, the second parent, S.c 10–170, displayed an extended adaptation phase, which prevented reliable determination of its maximum specific growth rate under tested conditions.

In both media, all strains exhibited a steady increase in 2-PE concentration over time (Fig. 2). In P8 medium (Fig. 2a-c), the parental strain S.c 10–170 produced the lowest amount of 2-PE, not exceeding 0.3 g/L after 72 h, which is correlated to its weak growth. In contrast, the triple hybrids H2–H5 reached approximately 1.3 g/L after the first 24 h, while hybrid H1 produced slightly more (1.6 ± 0.05 g/L). After 72 h, 2-PE levels increased markedly in all triple hybrids, reaching around 2.5 g/L (34.7 mg/L/h).

A similar trend was observed in the NEO medium (Fig. 2d-f). The parental strain S.c 10–170 again produced the lowest 2-PE concentration (approximately 0.5 g/L after 72 h). After the first day, the triple hybrids H1–H5 generated comparable amounts of 2-PE, averaging around 1.2 g/L, whereas the double hybrid II/6 produced slightly less, just below 1 g/L. During the second day of cultivation, 2-PE concentrations continued to rise, approximately doubling in hybrids H1–H5 and in the parental strain S.c 10–170. The triple hybrids exceeded 2.0 g/L, while the two parental strains, S.c 10–170 and II/6, produced 0.20 ± 0.03 g/L and 1.6 ± 0.05 g/L, respectively. By the final day of the experiment, the triple hybrids reached final 2-PE concentrations ranging from 2.61 ± 0.15 to 3.20 ± 0.55 g/L. The highest production was observed for hybrid H4 (3.20 ± 0.55 g/L; 44.4 mg/L/h). Hybrids H1–H3 achieved comparable concentrations of 2.82–2.88 g/L whereas H5 reached 2.61 ± 0.15 g/L. Overall, the hybrid strains consistently outperformed both parental strains under these conditions.

2-Phenylethanol toxicity levels

In this study, we aimed to determine whether the newly generated triple hybrids, which show enhanced 2‑PE biosynthesis, also display improved tolerance to 2‑PE, thereby further confirming this phenotype.

To assess how hybridization affected yeast tolerance to increased 2‑PE concentrations, we cultivated strains H1-H5 and its parental lines, S.c 10–170 and II/6, in 48‑hour batch cultures using YPD medium supplemented with 2‑PE at final levels of 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 g/L. Growth was monitored via OD600 measurements, and cellular morphology was evaluated using light microscopy.

Across the tested strains, substantial growth inhibition (≥ 50% reduction in OD600) occurred at markedly different 2-PE concentrations (Fig. 3). The triple hybrid H1 exhibited the highest tolerance, with inhibition exceeding 50% only at 5 g/L 2-PE (60%), while at 4 g/L the reduction remained below this threshold (39%). The second most resistant strain was hybrid H2, which showed a 53% decrease in OD600 at 4 g/L 2-PE. In contrast, the remaining triple hybrids (H3–H5) displayed more than 50% growth inhibition already at 4 g/L 2-PE (75%, 70%, and 82%, respectively), with inhibition further intensifying to 87–92% at 5 g/L 2-PE. The parental strains were considerably more sensitive. Parent S.c 10–170 exhibited 21% inhibition at 3 g/L 2-PE and 91% at 4 g/L, while II/6 showed a 71% reduction in OD600 at already 3 g/L 2-PE, making it the least tolerant strain in the panel.

Across the entire range of 2-PE concentrations tested, no statistically significant changes in cell length were observed (Fig. 4a-g). Only minor exceptions were noted for individual variants of strains II/6, H1, and H4. This trend was evident even at 2-PE titers that significantly inhibited yeast growth.

Microscopic examination of yeast cultures revealed also no significant differences in cellular shape across strains under low 2‑PE concentrations (< 2 g/L). However, at titers of 2–3 g/L and above, some cells had enlarged vacuoles and the number of intracellular structures resembling stress granules increased (Fig. 4h), suggesting activation of cellular stress responses under elevated 2‑PE pressure (Grousl et al. 2022).

Taken together, among all hybrids, H1 exhibited the highest tolerance to 2-PE, maintaining robust growth up to 4 g/L 2‑PE. Hybrids H3–H5 exhibited moderate tolerance, with a safe operational range of 3–3.5 g/L. The double hybrid II/6 remained the most sensitive strain overall.

Phenotypic stability in H1 and H2 hybrids

Based on prior screening of five generated yeast hybrids, strains H1 and H2 were selected as the most promising candidates for further stability assessment due to their 2-PE production capacity and high tolerance to elevated 2-PE concentrations.

Successive subculturing of the two yeast hybrids across three generations revealed generally good physiological stability, with only moderate fluctuations in growth kinetics and product formation. The results indicate that both hybrids retained their production capacity and biomass formation potential, although some evidence of partial adaptation loss or physiological drift was observed, particularly in the second and third generations (Table 2).

Growth kinetics, biomass formation, and 2-PE production stability of yeast hybrids H1 and H2 during consecutive subculturing across three generations

For hybrid H1, the maximum specific growth rate (µ) decreased from 0.34 ± 0.01 1/h in Generation 1 to 0.28 ± 0.01 1/h in Generation 2, before recovering to 0.33 ± 0.00 1/h in Generation 3. This temporary reduction was accompanied by a marked increase in lag phase duration from 1.31 ± 0.09 h to approximately 2.3–2.4 h in later generations, suggesting a delayed adaptation to the cultivation medium after repeated transfers. Similarly, the doubling time increased from 2.05 h to 2.50 h in Generation 2 and slightly improved to 2.13 h in Generation 3, confirming transient growth impairment rather than permanent deterioration. Despite these kinetic changes, final OD600 and DCW remained relatively stable (OD600: 8.22–8.92; DCW: 6.36–6.71 g/L), indicating that the final biomass accumulation capacity was preserved. However, cell viability showed a gradual decline, with cfu decreasing from 3.56 × 10⁶ to 2.87 × 10⁶ cfu/mL.

The production of 2-PE, the key functional trait of the hybrid, showed a slight but consistent decrease across generations in H1. At 72 h, 2-PE decreased from 2.74 ± 0.01 g/L in Generation 1 to 2.55 ± 0.01 g/L in Generation 3, representing approximately a 7% reduction (with pvalue<0.0001). Similar decreases were observed at 24 h and 48 h. Since biomass production remained stable, this decline likely reflects a mild reduction in metabolic efficiency rather than impaired growth. Cell size also showed a small decrease (8.24 to 7.81 μm, pvalue>0.05), which may indicate subtle physiological adaptation. Microscopic observations also revealed that more cells had enlarged vacuoles and the number of intracellular structures resembling stress granules, representing activation of cellular stress responses.

A comparable pattern was observed for hybrid H2. The µ decreased from 0.33 ± 0.01 1/h to 0.29 ± 0.00 1/h in Generation 2 and returned to 0.33 ± 0.00 1/h in Generation 3, indicating strong recovery of exponential growth performance. Lag time increased progressively from 1.27 ± 0.10 h to 2.37 ± 0.03 h, suggesting that adaptation to fresh medium became slower with repeated subculturing. Doubling time followed the same trend, increasing in Generation 2 (2.38 h) but returning to near-initial values in Generation 3 (2.08 h). Final OD600 decreased slightly (8.65 to 8.16), while DCW remained stable and even increased slightly in Generation 3 (6.65 g/L), indicating that biomass production was not negatively affected overall. As in H1, cfu values declined more noticeably, from 3.18 × 10⁶ to 2.00 × 10⁶ cfu/mL.

The 2-PE production profile of H2 also demonstrated moderate decline over generations, suggesting progressive viability loss. Final 72 h titers decreased from 2.72 ± 0.03 g/L to 2.52 ± 0.03 g/L, corresponding to approximately 7–8% reduction (pvalue<0.0001), very similar to H1. Intermediate time points (48 h and 24 h) showed the same downward trend. Cell size decreased slightly from 8.37 to 7.94 μm, again supporting minor physiological shifts without major phenotypic instability.

When comparing both hybrids, H1 and H2 displayed highly similar stability patterns, suggesting that both hybrids maintained their core phenotypic traits during three consecutive subcultures. Neither hybrid showed catastrophic loss of growth capacity or 2-PE synthesis, indicating overall hybrid stability. The transient reduction in µ and increased lag phase, particularly in Generation 2, may reflect physiological stress associated with repeated inoculation rather than true genetic instability. The slight but reproducible reduction in 2-PE production and cfu counts suggests some degree of metabolic drift or partial segregation of hybrid traits, but the magnitude of these changes remained limited.

Discussion

Comprehensive characterization of yeast strains is a fundamental step in selecting and engineering microorganisms for biotechnological applications. Assessing physiological, metabolic, and genetic traits enables the identification of strains with desirable industrial properties, such as stress tolerance, substrate utilization, and the capacity to synthesize high-value metabolites. Within the Saccharomyces genus, natural diversity and interspecific variation provide a rich foundation for developing improved strains through classical breeding and hybridization.

The combination of gene pools from parental strains within Saccharomyces species, achieved through natural mating followed by meiotic and mitotic segregation, can yield hybrid strains with substantial biotechnological potential. However, hybridization may generate both advantageous and disadvantageous phenotypes (Sipiczki 2018).

In this study, we conducted a broad range of biochemical assays to characterize the phenotypes of triple hybrid yeast strains in relation to their parental lines. Collectively, the results demonstrate that the H1–H5 hybrids preserve most parental traits, including temperature growth range, assimilation of single carbon sources, and hydrolase activity. As part of this phenotypic assessment, we also examined their sporulation behavior.

Reduced spore viability in yeast hybrids is a well-known consequence of the genomic divergence between parental species (Delneri et al. 2003; Lee et al. 2008; Naseeb et al. 2021b). Interspecies Saccharomyces hybrids typically sporulate inefficiently because their homologous chromosomes do not pair and recombine properly during meiosis. Sequence divergence triggers anti-recombination mechanisms that suppress crossover formation, leading to mis-segregation of chromosomes and the production of aneuploid spores that cannot survive. Structural differences between parental genomes, such as chromosomal rearrangements, can further disrupt homolog alignment and increase segregation errors.

Additional incompatibility may also contribute to poor spore viability. These include mismatches between nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, which can impair energy-dependent meiotic processes, as well as divergence in meiosis-specific genes that affects checkpoint control and progression through meiotic divisions. In some hybrids, defects in spore wall formation have also been reported, further reducing the likelihood that spores will remain viable.

Together, these factors create multiple barriers to successful meiosis in Saccharomyces hybrids and explain why hybrid strains, including those examined in this study, typically show low sporulation efficiency and limited (most often none) spore survival.

Building on this phenotypic characterization, we next focused on the main objective of our work: investigating 2-PE production and tolerance in the triple hybrids. Yeasts can synthesize 2-PE either via de novo formation from glucose through the phenylpyruvate pathway or through catabolism of exogenous L-phe via the Ehrlich pathway. The Ehrlich pathway predominates when L-phe is the sole nitrogen source, whereas de novo synthesis typically prevails under conditions of low amino acid availability. Endogenous 2-PE formation is complex, as multiple metabolic branches compete for carbon flux. Moreover, 2-PE biosynthesis is strongly feedback-inhibited by L-phe, resulting in very low overall efficiency of the phenylpyruvate pathway (Dai et al. 2021). Consequently, de novo synthesis is not considered a feasible route for cost-effective bioprocess production of 2-PE.

To ensure optimal conditions for 2-PE biosynthesis via the Ehrlich pathway, we employed two literature-based media previously used for 2-PE production and known to provide favorable growth conditions for yeast. Taking together, our results clearly demonstrate that hybridization is an effective strategy for improving strains used in the targeted biotransformation of 2-PE. The triple hybrids substantially outperformed both parental strains, achieving 1.2- to 9-fold higher production in P8 medium and 1.35- to 6.6-fold higher production in NEO medium (H1–H5 vs. II/6 and S.c 10–170, respectively).

These yields are comparable to, and in several cases exceed, recent reports in the literature. Using CRISPRa-mediated triple gene activation in S. cerevisiae, Zhu et al. (2025) achieved a 2-PE titer of 214 mg/L after 48 h. In fed-batch fermentation, Yin et al. (2015) reported a 2-PE concentration of 2.61 g/L in the SPO810 strain after 60 h. Shen et al. (2016) obtained 96 mg/L 2-PE by overexpressing key enzymes of the Ehrlich pathway and deleting genes of competing pathways in S. cerevisiae. Using a combination of evolutionary and rational metabolic engineering, Zhu et al. (2021) reported a titer of 4.02 g/L 2-PE, while Wang et al. (2017) achieved 3.73 g/L in a CAT8-overexpressing strain.

Moreover, hybridization enables the development of improved strains while maintaining their non-GMO status, which represents a significant advantage for prospective industrial deployment.

During 2-PE biosynthesis, accumulation of the end product imposes substantial toxicity on yeast cells, representing a major bottleneck in the process Yin et al. (2015). Approaches such as in situ product removal, two-phase extraction, and in situ adsorption have been applied to mitigate 2-PE toxicity; however, despite their effectiveness, these strategies are generally not economically viable (Etschmann and Schrader 2006; Lukito et al. 2019; Mei et al. 2009; Santos et al. 2021). Consequently, considerable effort has been directed toward identifying more robust microbial strains. Recently, Holyavkin et al. (2023) reported a 2-PE-resistant S. cerevisiae strain obtained through in vivo evolutionary engineering, capable of tolerating up to 3.4 g/L 2-PE. In our previous work, the II/6 double hybrid exhibited approximately 20% higher tolerance compared with its parental strains at 2 g/L (Drężek et al. 2024).

In this study, we expanded our previous work by using the II/6 hybrid as one parental strain and a 2-PE-tolerant S. cerevisiae strain as the second parent, aiming to determine whether new hybrids could simultaneously achieve higher 2-PE production and improved resistance. The tolerance assays revealed pronounced variation in 2-PE resilience among the hybrids, with H1 clearly outperforming both the remaining hybrids and the parental strains. Importantly, unlike our previous study, which focused on hybrids derived from two parental species, the present work employed a sequential hybridization strategy incorporating genetic contributions from three distinct parental backgrounds. The superior performance of selected hybrids demonstrates that introducing additional parental diversity can further improve industrially relevant traits and represents a promising extension of conventional two-species hybrid breeding.

From a metabolic-engineering perspective, the superior performance of H1, surpassing even the high tolerance of its S.c 10–170 parent, demonstrates that hybridization can effectively consolidate advantageous stress-response traits while maintaining or even enhancing biosynthetic potential. In contrast, the moderate tolerance ranges of H3–H5 (just over 3 g/L) indicate that not all hybrid combinations yield synergistic improvements, reflecting the complex interplay between genomic compatibility, membrane integrity, efflux capacity, and global regulatory networks. These findings align with the broader understanding that 2-PE toxicity is multifactorial, involving membrane perturbation, redox imbalance, and protein denaturation, and that strains capable of mitigating these stresses are better suited for high-level production (Holyavkin et al. 2023). Importantly, the observation that several hybrids outperform the double hybrid II/6 suggests that expanding genetic diversity through multi-parental hybridization can unlock phenotypes not accessible through classical two-strain crosses.

Interestingly, despite its markedly lower tolerance to exogenous 2-PE, strain II/6 produces substantially higher amounts of 2-PE than S.c 10–170. This apparent paradox may result from differences in growth performance under the respective media conditions and may also reflect variation in 2-PE export efficiency, membrane robustness, and stress-response regulation, along with a trade-off between metabolic flux toward 2-PE biosynthesis and cellular resources dedicated to tolerance mechanisms (Etschmann et al. 2002; Mitri et al. 2022; Stark et al. 2003; Zhu et al. 2021).

The results presented here demonstrate also that both selected yeast hybrids, H1 and H2, maintained relatively stable physiological and metabolic performance during three consecutive generations of subculturing, indicating good short-term hybrid stability. Although a temporary decrease in µmax and an extension of the lag phase were observed, particularly in Generation 2, both hybrids recovered their growth rate by Generation 3, suggesting that these changes were more likely associated with adaptation stress rather than permanent genetic instability. Final biomass accumulation, reflected by OD600 and DCW values, remained largely unchanged across generations, confirming preservation of growth capacity despite minor kinetic fluctuations. A gradual decline in cfu counts was observed for both hybrids, which may indicate reduced cell viability or the presence of viable but non-culturable cells after repeated transfers. The slight but consistent reduction in 2-phenylethanol production (approximately 7–8% at 72 h) suggests minor metabolic drift, possibly resulting from partial segregation of hybrid traits or changes in regulation of the Ehrlich pathway. However, because biomass production remained stable, this decrease appears to be related to metabolic efficiency rather than impaired cell growth. The small reduction in cell size observed in later generations may further support the occurrence of subtle physiological adaptation during serial propagation. Overall, the results confirm that both hybrids have retained their desirable industrial phenotype and can be considered sufficiently stable for further biotechnological applications. Naturally, for strains intended for industrial application, a greater number of consecutive generations and more extensive stability assessments would be required; however, even these initial three generations clearly demonstrate the strong stability potential of the obtained hybrids.

Conclusions

Although numerous attempts have been made to identify naturally robust producers among genera such as Kluyveromyces, Pichia, Yarrowia, and Hansenula (Cordente et al. 2019; Hassing et al. 2019; Martínez-Avila et al. 2018; Romagnoli et al. 2014), current evidence still points to natural and engineered S. cerevisiae strains as the most promising platforms for efficient 2‑PE biosynthesis. The results of this study further support this view and demonstrate that classical hybridization represents a powerful and reliable strategy for enhancing yeast performance in the biotransformation of L‑phenylalanine to 2‑phenylethanol.

The triple hybrids consistently outperformed both parental strains across all tested media, reaching titers of up to 3 g/L within 72 h and achieving volumetric productivities of 34.7–41.7 mg/L/h. These improvements, ranging from 1.2‑ to 9‑fold depending on the medium and reference strain, were independent of substrate composition, indicating that the enhanced phenotype is stable and not medium‑dependent. Importantly, the advantages of hybridization extended beyond production capacity. The triple hybrids also exhibited markedly improved tolerance to exogenous 2‑PE, significantly surpassing the double hybrid II/6 and, in several cases, approaching or even exceeding the robustness of the tolerant parental strain S.c 10‑170. This demonstrates that the hybrids successfully combined beneficial traits from both parents: enhanced biosynthetic capacity inherited from II/6 and elevated 2‑PE tolerance derived from S.c 10‑170, while in some instances outperforming both.

When positioned against recent literature, the yields obtained in this work are comparable to, and in several cases exceed, those achieved through advanced metabolic‑engineering strategies such as CRISPRa‑mediated gene activation, pathway overexpression, or evolutionary engineering. Notably, hybridization enables the development of high‑performing strains while maintaining non‑GMO status, offering a substantial advantage for industrial implementation in sectors where regulatory or consumer constraints limit the use of genetically modified microorganisms.

Collectively, these findings highlight classical hybridization as a scalable, efficient, and industry-compatible alternative to genetic engineering for improving 2-PE biosynthesis. Importantly, this study extends our previous findings on two-species hybridization by demonstrating that the incorporation of a third parental genetic background can further improve strain performance. The greater production and tolerance characteristics observed in several triple hybrids indicate that multi-parental hybridization can generate phenotypes that exceed those obtainable through conventional two-parent crosses, highlighting its potential as an advanced strain development strategy. Moreover, the demonstrated enhancement of both production and tolerance underscores the broader potential of multi-parental hybridization as a versatile tool for developing robust microbial cell factories for aromatic compound production and beyond.
