Tubular ER dysfunction in neurodegenerative diseases
Authors: Md Golam Sharoar, Riqiang Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndoplasmic reticulum (ER) is the largest secretory organelle, and it regulates diverse cellular processes to support neuronal growth, development, and maintenance during the lifespan. Structural alterations to the ER network affect a multitude of functions and downstream events, which result in abnormalities in neuronal maintenance and neuronal death. Morphological and functional abnormalities in the tubular domain of ER or in tubular ER proteins have been linked to several major neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and hereditary spastic paraplegias (HSPs). Tubular ER dysfunction in neurodegenerative diseases is manifested by elevated expression, abnormal aggregation, haplo-insufficiency, or functional deficiency in multiple ER tubule-shaping proteins. This review aims to highlight the involvement of ER tubule shaping and networking proteins that cause tubular ER dysfunction in AD and HSPs. The discussion of this review will also highlight the functional necessity of an integrated tubular ER network for axonal maintenance.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Hereditary spastic paraplegia, Tubular endoplasmic reticulum, Dystrophic neurite, Tubular ER clustering, Mitochondria dysfunction

Introduction

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a critical organelle that is responsible for vital cellular functions such as synthesis of proteins and lipids, regulation of calcium levels and mitochondrial dynamics, and cellular trafficking of proteins (Schwarz and Blower, 2016; Markovinovic et al., 2022). The morphology of ER shows variability in different types, cycle phases, and metabolic states of cells. Eukaryotic ER comprises a continuous membrane system of nuclear envelope and peripheral domain (Baumann and Walz, 2001). Typically, a peripheral ER consists of a network of tubules and interspersed sheets, and is divided into two morphologically and functionally distinct domains of rough and smooth ER (Shibata et al., 2006). Rough ER is associated with ribosomes, which are known to control protein synthesis. The smooth ER is more commonly distributed along the cell periphery. In neurons, smooth ER plays critical roles in the proper organization of the microtubule cytoskeleton and in the distribution of mitochondria in the neurites (Yperman and Kuijpers, 2023). Tubular ER is a part of smooth ER that takes part in regulating vesicle transport, organelle contacts, cellular communications, and mitochondrial dynamics such as fusion and fission (Baumann and Walz, 2001; Hu et al., 2011; Friedman et al., 2011). A growing body of evidence has suggested that ER stress- and altered ER morphology associated dysfunction are linked with multiple neurological disorders (Roussel et al., 2013). In particular, genetic mutations in ER tubulation proteins, altered tubular ER protein expression, abnormality in tubular ER morphology, disrupted ER-mitochondria contact, and perturbed ER Ca+ signaling have been associated with several neurodegenerative diseases (Ozturk et al., 2020; Sharoar et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2022; Paillusson et al., 2017; Teng and Tang, 2008). However, the direct involvement of tubular ER or multiple tubular ER resident proteins in pathology constitution has been associated with two major neurodegenerative diseases, namely, Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP). Specifically, tubular ER-associated pathology is primarily found at the hippocampus area of AD patients’ brains (Sharoar et al., 2016; Hu et al., 2007). However, the abnormality was noticed in multiple brain regions due to gene mutations in various tubular ER proteins in HSP (Servelhere et al., 2021). Yet, the underlying mechanism of tubular ER dysfunction in these neurological disorders remain to be understood. In this review, we will primarily focus on the abnormal accumulation of ER tubules and tubular ER resident proteins in AD, and the functional deficiency of several tubular ER proteins in HSP.

Organization and dynamics of ER networks

Cellular ER structure is composed of inner and outer membranes that are separated by a luminal space (Schwarz and Blower, 2016; Baumann and Walz, 2001). A typical ER system is organized in a single continuous structure of interconnected sheets and tubules that protrude from the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope and form a network in numerous junctions (Schwarz and Blower, 2016; Shibata et al., 2006; Perkins et al., 2021) (Fig. 1A). ER sheets are formed by two extended membrane surfaces laying parallel to each other and connected by curved surfaces of sheet edges (Shibata et al., 2010). A cross section of ER tubules shows high membrane curvature with a diameter of ~30 nm in yeast and ~ 60 nm in mammals, which is comparable to the curved surface of the sheet edge (Hu et al., 2011; Shibata et al., 2010; Hu et al., 2008). Tubules often stem from the edges of sheets and are connected to each other by junctions that form a polygon network structure. The ER network spreads throughout the cell by a dynamic process of tubule extension and tubule fusion; these activities lead to the formation of a new polygon network (Hu et al., 2011; Perkins et al., 2021; Lee and Chen, 1988). ER networks are built by structurally diversified proteins that are grouped into multiple families based upon their unique structures and membrane topologies (Hu et al., 2011). Tubular ER resident proteins play critical roles in interconnected activities such as tubule shaping, tubule junctions, network extension, and tubule stabilization, which are necessary for a functional ER system (Fig. 1A–D).

Formation of ER tubules

tubular ER stems from the edge of the ER sheet, which is enriched with a particular protein known as Climp63 (Shibata et al., 2006; Sandoz et al., 2023). ER tubules are generated by two major family proteins known as reticulons (RTNs) and receptor expression enhancing proteins (REEPs) (Hu et al., 2011; Fan et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2021). RTNs are a diverse family of membrane-bound proteins, named for their preferred intracellular localization in ER (Voeltz et al., 2006). The mammalian RTN family includes four members, RTN1-RTN4. Among those, RTN4, commonly known as Nogo, was initially named for its critical inhibitory roles in axonal growth and regeneration (Schwab, 2004; Strittmatter, 2002). The function of RTNs in ER organization was first elucidated by identifying RTN4b (Nogo A) as an integral element for ER network formation (Voeltz et al., 2006). The sub-cellular localization of RTNs is mostly restricted to the tubular domain of ER, where they are functionally responsible for the high curvature of ER membranes. All RTNs are characterized by their common C-terminal membrane-bound region, known as the reticulon homology domain (RHD), whereas their N-terminal domains are diversified. On ER, RTNs exhibit a characteristic ω-shape membrane topology, in which two trans-membrane domains (TM1 and TM2), separated by a 66 amino acid (aa)-long loop, are attached to the membrane and both the C- and N-termini face the cytosol (Fig. 1C–D). All RTNs are highly expressed in the human and rodent brain. However, RTN3 is the most enriched protein in neurons, and it plays critical roles in axonal tubular ER organization (Yperman and Kuijpers, 2023; Hu et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2021; Deng et al., 2013).

REEPs were originally identified for their ability to enhance cell surface expression of a subset of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) (Saito et al., 2004). In mammals, there are six REEP family members, designated as REEP1–6. However, based upon structural similarity, REEPs are also classified into two sub-families, REEP1–4 and REEP5–6 (Park et al., 2010). REEP 1–4 contain a microtubule binding (MTB) domain and a conserved 14–3–3 binding site at the c-terminal region (Fig. 1C). The REEP5–6 sub-family is homolog to yeast Yop1 protein family due to their structure and sequence homology (Park et al., 2010; Schlaitz et al., 2013). The REEP1–4 sub-family contains a conserved positively charged region between two hydrophobic TM domains, while the REEP5–6 family possesses a conserved negatively-charged domain between the TM1 and TM2 regions (Fig. 1C). Regardless of c-terminal variation between sub-family members, all REEPs dictate ω-shape or hairpin membrane topology on ER (Renvoise and Blackstone, 2010).

A large body of evidence has shown that RTNs and REEPs are strictly localized on tubular ER, where they selectively interact with each other and with other ER tubule proteins, such as spastin, atlastin1, lunapark, and protruding (Sharoar et al., 2016; Hu et al., 2008; Voeltz et al., 2006; Park et al., 2010; Bjork et al., 2013). Although the exact mechanism of tubule formation is still unknown, it has been suggested that it occurs via intra-membrane interaction through TM domains, as these proteins comprise the bulk of hydrophobic regions at the outer leaflet of the phospholipid bilayer, which eventually forms a characteristic hairpin structure and generates curvature by hydrophobic wedging (Wang et al., 2021; Goyal and Blackstone, 2013; Xiang et al., 2023) (Fig. 1D). The diversified cytoplasmic N- and C- termini of these proteins possibly determine the specified localization on a particular site in the tubular domain of ER to serve other functions such as MT binding and vesicle transport (Schwarz and Blower, 2016; Markovinovic et al., 2022).

Tubule junctions and network stabilization

The function of ER is intricately linked to its stabilized tubular network architecture and physical contact between the ER and other cellular organelles (Goyal and Blackstone, 2013). Upon shaping ER tubular structures, a polygonal network of tubules is formed by membrane fusion, which requires GTPase activity (Hu et al., 2009; Orso et al., 2009). A family of tubular ER resident proteins, called Atlastins (ATLs), serve the GTPase activity to mediate three-way junctions of ER tubules (Orso et al., 2009). The ATLs and Sey1p (yeast orthologue of human atlastin) belong to the dynamin family of GTPases, and they are associated with different intracellular membranes (Praefcke and McMahon, 2004). In human, the ATL family includes three members, ATL1–3, which have similar structures and membrane topologies, but different cellular distributions (Rismanchi et al., 2008). ATL1 expression is enriched in the CNS and it strictly localizes at axonal growth cones (Zhu et al., 2006). ATL2 and ATL3 are expressed ubiquitously and they participate in ER morphogenesis (Hu et al., 2009). All ATLs contain a cytosolic N-terminal region with GTPase and helical binding (HB) domains, and a C-terminal tail with shortly-spaced TM1 and TM2 domains (Bian et al., 2011) (Fig. 1C). ATLs interact with several members of RTNs/Yop1, REEPs (Hu et al., 2009), spastin (Park et al., 2010; Evans et al., 2006), and lunapark (Chen et al., 2012) proteins, and promote tubular network formation by homotypic membrane fusion (Orso et al., 2009). Once GTP binds at C-terminal GTPase domains, ATLs form oligomers and catalyze GTP hydrolysis (Moss et al., 2011). Mechanistically, ATLs polymerization causes a drastic conformational change that pulls the ATLs toward opposite membranes, and then enzymatic activity fuses the membranes together (Faust et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2012). Fusion activity by ATLs is a continuous process of tubular extension, branching, and network formation. However, balance of this function is controlled by a Lunapark family protein, Lnp1p (Chen et al., 2012; Chen et al., 2015). Lnp1p is also localized at the three-way junction in tubular ER networks (Fig. 1B) and it interacts with RTNs and Yop1 in mammalian and yeast cells, respectively, to stabilize tubular ER network formation (Chen et al., 2012). Structurally, Lnp1p contains two TM domains and a zinc finger motif at the c-terminal region (Fig. 1C). Binding of Lnp1p to RTNs/Yop1 is negatively regulated by Sey1p in yeast. Loss of Lnp1p causes the formation of densely-reticulated defective ER (Chen et al., 2012), which suggests that Lap1p regulation of ATL function is necessary for a stabilized polygonal network formation.

Protrudin has also been shown to play critical roles in ER shaping and network stabilization (Chang et al., 2013; Hashimoto et al., 2014). Both in vitro and in vivo evidence suggest that protrudin is predominantly localized in tubular ER, and it binds to RTNs, REEPs, and ATLs (Chang et al., 2013; Hashimoto et al., 2014). Besides two hydrophobic TM domains that sort the protein to display hairpin membrane topology, protrudin exhibits a more complex structure that consists of an N-terminal Rab11 binding domain and C-terminal FFAT (two phenylalanines in an acidic tract) and FYVE domains separated by a short CC sequence motif (Saita et al., 2009; Shirane and Nakayama, 2006) (Fig. 1C). The FFAT sequence interacts with a vesicle-associated protein (VAP) and a motor protein (Kif5A) that is involved in neuronal anterograde vesicular transport (Hashimoto et al., 2014; Saita et al., 2009). Overexpression of protrudin promotes the formation and the stabilization of the ER network, and its depletion alters ER morphology and sheet-tubule balances (Chang et al., 2013; Hashimoto et al., 2014). The FYVE domain of protrudin interacts with plasma membrane, while the VAP domain is involved in ER–plasma membrane tethering and cell signaling regulation (Chang et al., 2013; Manford et al., 2012). The multiple adapter-like motifs in the protrudin structure facilitate ER communication with other organelles.

ER dynamics

The ER is an extremely dynamic organelle that continuously alters its morphology by spontaneous extension and remodeling of tubules in a polygonal network (Pendin et al., 2011). Morphological alterations in the ER network and in microtubule (MT) organization are interdependent activities (Pendin et al., 2011; Tikhomirova et al., 2022). Cold-shocked or drug-induced MT depolymerization severely affect ER dynamics (Terasaki et al., 1986). Conversely, an ER protein, spastin, regulates MT activities by acting as an ATPase to split MTs, controlling the numbers and mobility of MTs, and maintaining the distribution of the plus end of MTs throughout the cell’s peripheral domain, including the axon terminal of a neuron (Baas et al., 2006; Evans et al., 2005; Roll-Mecak and McNally, 2010; Salinas et al., 2007). There are four spastin isoforms, which are generated by a combination of differential start codons and exon splicing: a 616 aa-long full-length protein (M1 spastin), a shorter form that lacks N-terminal 86 aa (M87 spastin), and the variants of M1 and M87, each with shorter exon 4 that encode 32 aa sequence (Claudiani et al., 2005). Both M1 and M87 spastin contain two TM domains in the N-terminal region, while their C-terminal regions contains a MT-binding domain and an ATPase-associated diverse cellular activities (AAA) domain (Hazan et al., 1999; White et al., 2007) (Fig. 1C). The predominant localization of M1 spastin in tubular ER is determined by a direct interaction of this protein with ATLs, REEPs, and RTNs through an N-terminal hydrophobic region (Park et al., 2010; Hu et al., 2009) (Fig. 1C). M87 spastin lacks this hydrophobic interacting domain, which makes this isoform thoroughly distributed in cytoplasm. While the exact roles of M1 spastin in tubular ER organization remain largely unknown, MT splicing activities of M1 spastin suggest that MT organization and ER dynamics are mutually-supported activities.

There are two primary mechanisms that contribute to ER tubule movement toward the periphery of a cell. First, the sliding dynamic, in which newly-formed ER tubules extend by binding the tubules tip with existing MTs that slide ER tubules toward the plus-end direction of MTs (Waterman-Storer and Salmon, 1998) (Fig. 1B). The second mechanism involves the formation of a tip attachment complex (TAC), in which the tip of a growing ER tubule attaches to the tip of a MT plus-end at the dense ER network region, and together they extent as partners (Grigoriev et al., 2008). The TAC rearrangement is highly dynamic and associated with direct interactions between an ER protein, STIM1, and a MT protein, EB1 (Fig. 1B). TAC is sensitive to depolymerization of MTs by nicodazole treatment; however, ER sliding with nicodazole-resistant curved acetylated MTs is a more rapid event and is considered as the prevalent mechanism of ER network dynamics (Waterman-Storer and Salmon, 1998; Friedman et al., 2010). Despite the fact that both TAC and sliding extend newly-formed ER tubules only toward the plus-end direction of MTs, an actomyosin-based retrograde flow of MTs drives ER tubules inward from the cell periphery (Waterman-Storer and Salmon, 1998). In the kidney epithelial cell lineage, kinesin 1 and cytoplasmic dynein have been shown to drive ER tubules toward the cell periphery and backward from the cellular edge, respectively (Wozniak et al., 2009). However, the mechanism of ER trafficking in neurons remains largely unknown. Both the sliding and TAC mechanisms have been suggested in ER dynamics in neurons, in which a direct role of RAB10 has been demonstrated (Ozturk et al., 2020; English and Voeltz, 2013). One study also showed involvement of VAP-A and –B in axonal tubular ER movement (Lindhout et al., 2019). We have shown that the tubular ER protein, RTN3, directly interacts with a dynactin (DCTN) subunit, DCTN6, and that such an interaction likely mediates the retrograde trafficking of tubular ER in axons (Sharoar et al., 2021a). Thus, multiple mechanisms appear to be involved in bi-directional movement of tubular ER in neurons. A study has shown that bi-directional tubular ER trafficking in axons is relatively slower compared to the fast axonal transport of synaptic vesicles (Aihara et al., 2001). Despite the fact that ER dynamics are essential cellular processes, it has been suggested that ER trafficking is also involved in the transport of several human disease-causing proteins in neurons (Muresan and Ladescu Muresan, 2016).

Neuronal ER networks

Due to polarized architecture, neuron possess specialized ER networks that consist of sheet-like morphology with ribosomal attachment in the somato-dendritic compartment and a predominant smooth ER tubule in axons (Yperman and Kuijpers, 2023; Hoffmann et al., 2021). However, small cisternae at axonal branch point of premature neurons contain rough ER that is likely involved in the local protein synthesis at axonal growth cones (Deng et al., 2022; Hafner et al., 2019; Nedozralova et al., 2022). A recent study has demonstrated that tubular ER regulate axonal ribosomal distribution and local protein translation in rat hippocampal neurons (Koppers et al., 2024). On the other hand, a specialized structure that is composed of smooth ER stacks has been detected in a subset of dendritic spines (Falahati et al., 2022). It has been suggested that these partitioned ER subdomains in neurons may have differential roles in neuronal survival and functions, including local protein synthesis, Ca2+ balance, cytoskeleton maintenance, synaptic vesicle release, and neurotransmission (Hafner et al., 2019; Konietzny et al., 2022; Chanaday and Kavalali, 2022; Chanaday et al., 2021).

Tubular ER abnormality in neurodegenerative diseases

To maintain specialized architecture throughout the lifespan, neurons largely rely on a functional ER system (Yperman and Kuijpers, 2023; Renvoise and Blackstone, 2010). The neuronal ER network is highly differentiated with predominant smooth tubular ER in the neurites (axons and dendrites) and prominent rough ER sheet and cisternae in the soma (Yperman and Kuijpers, 2023; Ozturk et al., 2020; Friedman and Voeltz, 2011). Axonal ER plays critical roles in many neuronal processes, including vesicle transport, mitochondria dynamics, and Ca2+ regulation (Goldberg, 2003; Westrate et al., 2015). The functions of axonal ER critically depend upon integrated tubular network organization (Yperman and Kuijpers, 2023; Ozturk et al., 2020; Perkins et al., 2021). Abnormalities in axonal ER distribution due to overexpression, aggregation, and/or mutation in multiple tubular ER proteins are associated with AD and HSP (Blackstone et al., 2011). The involvement of tubular ER proteins in the pathological onset of neurological diseases implicates the necessity of functional ER network organization for the maintenance and survival of neurons.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD)

AD is the most common neurodegenerative disorders and is associated with severe cognitive impairment. The majority of AD cases are sporadic, where aging is the predominant factor for pathological onset and clinical phenomena. A small percentage (~5 %) of AD cases are known as early-onset or familial AD and are associated with genetic factors. The pathological features of AD include extracellular senile plaques, intracellular neurofibrillary tangles, plaque-surrounding dystrophic neurites (DNs), glia-activated neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction (Selkoe et al., 1996). Plaques and tangles are primarily constituted with progressive deposition of β-amyloid (Aβ) peptide and hyper-phosphorylated microtubule-associated tau protein, respectively. Extensive studies have suggested that the generation of Aβ peptide from its precursor, amyloid precursor protein (APP), and its subsequent deposition in specific brain regions are the earliest events during AD development. APP proteolysis is initiated by β-amyloid cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1), designated for β-secretase, which generate the APP c-terminal fragment that is 99 amino acids in length (APP-C99) (Yan et al., 1999). A subsequent cleavage by γ-secretase on APP-C99 generates Aβ peptide with variable lengths from 39 to 43 amino acids due to differential cleavage by γ-secretase (Haass, 2004). Once generated, Aβ peptide undergoes spontaneous conformational changes and deposits in certain brain regions. While a diffused form of Aβ plaques is found in normal elderly brain, an Aβ deposited-core is frequently encircled by DNs in the brains of AD patients (Joachim et al., 1989). In the following section, we will briefly discuss the molecular composition of plaque-associated DNs in AD and describe more detail on tubular ER-associated DN formation in AD and aging mouse brains.

Markers and molecular components of DNs

DNs are defined by bulbous or swelled abnormal neuritic sprouts that originate from axons or dendrites and encircle Aβ-deposited dense cores in AD brains (Dickson et al., 1988; Lenders et al., 1989; Onorato et al., 1989). In AD postmortem brain, DNs were initially recognized by various immune-reactive markers including neurofilament, tau, chromatogranin A, thioflavin S (Dickson et al., 1999; Masliah et al., 1993; Vickers et al., 1996), APP (Shoji et al., 1990), APLP2 (Crain et al., 1996), ubiquitin (Kowall and Kosik, 1987; Perry et al., 1987), GAP-43 (Masliah et al., 1992) and SNAP-25 (Ferrer et al., 1998). Our extensive studies using various protein-specific antibodies in multiple mouse models and AD postmortem/biopsy brain samples have contributed a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms of DN formation in AD brains (Sharoar et al., 2016; Hu et al., 2007; Sharoar et al., 2019; Sharoar et al., 2021b). We showed that DNs in AD brains are formed by abnormal accumulation of multiple organelles and proteins, which are sequentially clustered in layers during neuritic plaque growth (Sharoar et al., 2019). Initially, a pre-autophagy protein, ATG9A, and saposin-enriched lysosomes form the 1st layer of DNs that encircle Aβ cores, which is followed by the accumulation of tubular ER and mitochondria in the 2nd layer. Finally, autophagy vesicles and ubiquitin are clustered in the 3rd layer at later stages of disease progression. In immunofluorescence studies, most of these abnormally-accumulated organelles in plaque-associated DNs were labeled by the antibodies against multiple proteins that are associated with specific organelles. For example, lysosomes were labeled with LAMP1-, LAMP2-, and saposin-targeted antibodies (Sharoar et al., 2021b). Several tubular ER proteins, including RTN3, REEP2, and REEP5, were profoundly detected in the 2nd layers (Sharoar et al., 2016; Sharoar et al., 2019). Similarly, the autophagy and endosomal proteins, LC3 and RAB7, were ubiquitously present in the peripheral coat of Aβ cores surrounding DNs (Sharoar et al., 2019). 2-dimensional (2D), 3D, and immuno-electron microscopy studies also supported the layer formation with proteins/organelles specific for distinct types of DNs in aging and AD mouse brains (Sharoar et al., 2019; Sharoar et al., 2021b). Interestingly, our study also demonstrated that the trapped lysosomes in the 1st layer of DNs selectively disappeared in older AD mice and advanced AD postmortem brains. LAMP1 antibody strongly labeled the active lysosomes in plaque-associated microglia in those late-stage AD brains (Sharoar et al., 2021b). Chronological clustering of those organelles in DNs suggests a successive cellular dysfunction mechanism that began with the initial Aβ core formation and progresses with aging. It has been shown that quantitative and morphological features of Aβ cores remain constant throughout the clinical course of AD (Ingelsson et al., 2004; Serrano-Pozo et al., 2012), but the numbers of DNs and activated glia increase over the course of disease pathogenesis (Serrano-Pozo et al., 2016). The removal of Aβ by antibody treatment has provided controversial results in clearing plaque associated-DNs and reversing cognitive deficits in AD brains (Nicoll et al., 2006). Indeed, studies in mice showed that autonomous formation of DNs during aging significantly impairs cognitive functions (Hu et al., 2007; Shi et al., 2009). These results strongly suggest that DN formation is an irreversible process and a significant contributor of cognitive dysfunction in AD brains.

Tubular ER accumulation in DNs

The involvement of ER proteins in DN formation was first determined by identifying abnormal accumulation of RTN3 in Aβ-surrounding areas in AD brains; those RTN3+-DNs were termed as RTN3 immunoreactive DNs (RIDNs) (Hu et al., 2007). Later, spontaneous RIDN formation was observed in hippocampal CA1 in wild-type (WT) aged mice (Sharoar et al., 2016; Shi et al., 2009). Autonomous RIDN formation was accelerated when RTN3 was overexpressed under prion promoter in mice (Tg-RTN3 mice), and the severity of RIDN formation was correlated with the cognitive decline in those mice (Hu et al., 2007; Shi et al., 2009). Our further investigation using Tg-RTN3 mice and AD human brain samples has identified the existence of REEPs (REEP2 and REEP5) in RIDNs (Sharoar et al., 2016). Based upon the manifestation pattern, plaque-associated RIDNs in AD brains and autonomously-formed RIDNs in WT or Tg-RTN3 mouse brains can be classified in clustered and dispersed forms, respectively (Fig. 2). Our ultrastructural and immuno-EM studies have revealed that both types of RIDNs are formed by clustered tubular ER-filled inclusions, which are morphologically distinguishable from other types of DNs in AD brains (Sharoar et al., 2019; Richard et al., 1989). While clustered RIDNs are predominant in AD brains, dispersed RIDNs were also observed in hippocampal CA1 in older 5xFAD mouse brains (Hu et al., 2018). Interestingly, those dispersed RIDNs were irreversible when Aβ plaques were eliminated by BACE1 deletion (Hu et al., 2018). This intriguing evidence suggests that tubular ER accumulation in clustered and disperse RIDNs originate from different mechanisms. Dysfunctional tubular ER accumulation in clustered RIDNs is likely an Aβ related pathology that is caused by Aβ-induced deficits in axonal trafficking, lysosomal function, and/or ubiquitin proteome systems, which lead to abnormal autophagosome buildup and axonal swelling in AD pathology (Gregori et al., 1997; Keck et al., 2003; Lee et al., 2011; Lindsten et al., 2003). This also supported by our 3D-EM study and reconstruction of DN formation in AD mouse brain axons, in which tubular ER is associated with vesicles in DN-forming inclusions (Sharoar et al., 2019). On the other hand, dispersed RIDN formation is probably a tubular ER-associated pathology during normal aging, in which elevated protein levels and abnormal aggregation of a single or multiple tubular ER proteins and defective axonal trafficking of tubular ER are involved in which RTN3 is likely one of the key proteins (which is discussed in the following section) (Sharoar et al., 2016; Sharoar et al., 2021a). However, tubular ER accumulation in both types of RIDNs possibly originate from axons since tubular ER proteins in DNs are primarily co-localized with axonal marker, SMI31 or neurofilament-L, but not with dendrite marker, MAP2 (Sharoar et al., 2016; Hu et al., 2007). Seemingly, our ultrastructural study also showed the existence of synaptic vesicles in the inclusion body that filled with tubular ER accumulation or tubular ER-vesicle-like morphology (Sharoar et al., 2016; Sharoar et al., 2019). These suggested that somato-dendritic rough ER is likely a protected domain in neurons (Chanaday and Kavalali, 2022). Although, by definition, both axon and dendrite participate in DN formation in AD brains, whether compartmentalized tubular ER in dendritic spines perticipates in the process remains to be investigated.

RTN3 aggregation and tubular ER accumulation

tubular ER abnormalities and the development of RIDNs positively correlate with the increased RTN3 protein levels in normal aging and Tg-RTN3 mouse brains (Sharoar et al., 2016; Shi et al., 2009; Heath et al., 2010). RTN3 was not found in younger human postmortem brain, but it was easily detectable in brain samples from individuals 65 years or older (Shi et al., 2009). As a normal biochemical process, RTN3 undergoes spontaneous aggregation by inter-molecular interaction and forms high molecular weight RTN3 (HMW-RTN3) aggregates (Hu et al., 2007; Shibata et al., 2008). Monomeric RTN3 interacts with BACE1; an increased monomeric RTN3-BACE1 interaction reduces Aβ protein levels and plaque burden in AD mouse brain cortex (Hu et al., 2007). Increased levels of HMW-RTN3 aggregates were detected in Tg-RTN3 mouse hippocampus (RIDN-forming region) compared to cortex and in AD human brains compared to non-demented elderly brains. Ultrastructural morphology of both dispersed RIDNs in Tg-RTN3 mouse brains and clustered RIDNs in AD brains showed similar tubular ER clustering patterns that were labeled by RTN3-specific antibody in immuno-EM studies (Sharoar et al., 2016; Sharoar et al., 2019). Thus, elevated RTN3 aggregation or increased levels of HMW-RTN3 in aging mouse and in AD brains are likely positive regulators of abnormal tubular ER clustering. The co-existence of REEPs in RIDNs suggest the involvement of specific regions of tubular ER as an integrated unit, termed as a morphogen complex (Blackstone et al., 2011), in RIDN formation. Levels of REEPs were elevated in RTN3 knockout brains (Sharoar et al., 2016), which is likely a compensatory effect to maintain tubular ER morphology. A balance of RTN3:REEPs is possibly critical for normal tubule-shaping activities in ER. Increases expression or elevated protein levels of RTN3 during aging may alter this ratio and facilitate the formation of RTN3-RTN3/REEP2/REEP5 complexes that induce enhanced curvature of ER tubules (Sharoar et al., 2016) (Fig. 3). How this phenomenon affects the remaining domains of the tubular networks, such as the three-way junction areas or ER sheets, remains elusive. Further studies are also needed to determine the underlying mechanisms.

Diagram showing the possible cause and downstream effects of tubular ER-associated DN formation and tubular ER dysfunction in aging mouse brains and in AD brains.

Deregulation in multiple cellular processes may increase RTN3 protein levels and HMW-RTN3 in elderly and AD brains. RTN3 mRNA levels were slightly increased, but hippocampus-specific RTN3 aggregation and RIDN formation were evident in mouse brains during aging (Shi et al., 2009). We have shown that dynactin 6 (DCTN6), a subunit of the dynein-dynactin retrograde trafficking complex, is reduced in hippocampus compared to cortex in mouse brains (Sharoar et al., 2021a). DCTN6 interacts with the N-terminal domain of RTN3, and a deficiency in DCTN6 induces RTN3 aggregation in axons and increased hippocampus-specific dispersed RIDN formation in WT and Tg-RTN3 mice. Hence, weak DCTN6-RTN3-mediated retrograde trafficking of tubular ER in hippocampus may cause site-specific RIDN formation by abnormal tubular ER clustering in axons (Sharoar et al., 2021a). On the other hand, impairment of the protein degradation system during aging and in AD brains could enhance RTN3 and HMW-RTN3 levels (Hu et al., 2007). A large body of evidence suggests that Aβ deposition in human AD and in AD mouse brains causes a defective ubiquitin proteosome system (Keck et al., 2003; Lindsten et al., 2003; Keller et al., 2000; Lopez Salon et al., 2000). Fibrillar Aβ can bind with 20S subunits of proteosomes that selectively inhibit chymotrypsin-like activity (Gregori et al., 1997), which could accelerate HMW-RTN3 formation (Hu et al., 2007). Tubular ER accumulation is likely a downstream pathway of ER stress or the activation of unfolded protein response (UPR) because we notice that there is no alteration in the protein levels of multiple ER stress markers, such as GRP78, PARK/pPARK, or eIF2α/p eIF2α, in Tg-RTN3 mouse brains or in RTN3 overexpressed cells (unpublished data). However, in our recent study with AD mouse retinae, we noted an elevated RTN3 expression in correlation with the activation of ER stress via UPR in retinal Müller glia (Palko et al., 2024). We can’t exclude the possibility that this elevated ER stress is likely caused by Aβ oligomers in retinal Müller glia rather than elevated RTN3 levels. Further study is needed to reveal the underlying mechanism.

Tubular ER dysfunction and mitochondria degeneration

Degeneration of mitochondria in AD brains has been reported in many studies (Onyango et al., 2016; Perez Ortiz and Swerdlow, 2019; Sharma et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020). Mitochondria dynamics, defined by fusion and fission activities, are vital for maintaining the integrity and functions of mitochondria; deregulation of this process is associated with numerous human diseases, including AD (Archer, 2014; Detmer and Chan, 2007; Manczak et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2009). Tubular ER plays critical roles in controlling mitochondrial dynamics by determining the site of mitochondria division (Friedman et al., 2011). It has been proposed that biochemical and physical communication between ER and mitochondria at mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs) regulates mitochondrial functions and calcium homeostasis in axons (Hayashi et al., 2009). Thus, abnormal tubular ER accumulation in AD brains is likely an upstream event of the increased mitochondrial fragmentation, imbalanced mitochondrial fission-fusion, enhanced ER-mitochondria contacts, and impaired MAM functions (Manczak et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2009; Schon and Area-Gomez, 2013). We observed reduced levels of the mitochondria fusion protein, mitofusion (MFN) 2, in Tg-RTN3 mouse brains (Sharoar et al., 2016), which is indicative of impaired axonal trafficking of mitochondria and disrupted mitochondrial dynamics (fission-fusion) (Friedman et al., 2011; Misko et al., 2010). Taken together, these results clarify our understanding that an upstream functional aberration of tubular ER may clog mitochondrial dynamics and motility in axons, and the trapped mitochondria in ER clustering regions suffer irregular fusion and fission that eventually cause degeneration of mitochondria at axonal termini. Impairment of mitochondrial function and the absence of mitochondria in axons causes axonal degeneration and prolonged neuronal death (Martorell-Riera et al., 2014; Rawson et al., 2014). Hence, preserving a functional tubular ER network is pivotal for preventing mitochondrial dysfunction and neurodegeneration in AD.

Hereditary spastic paraplegia

Hereditary spastic paraplegias (HSPs) are referred to as a group of genetic neurological disorders, which are clinically characterized by progressive lower-limb spasticity and weakness (Fink, 2006; Salinas et al., 2008). Degeneration of motor sensory neurons, predominantly at the distal end of long central nervous system axons, is the major pathological feature in HSP (Fink, 2006; Salinas et al., 2008; Reid, 2003). The prevalence of HSPs vary among different ethnic groups in the world with an estimation of around 1 per 10,000 individuals (Ruano et al., 2014). Disease symptoms, such as gait impairment, may appear at any age from infancy to childhood, which may not be sustained. However, the appearance of symptoms after childhood generally progresses to a severe condition over many years. Clinically, HSP syndromes are classified as pure, when only HSP symptoms are detected, or complicated, if it is associated with other neurological abnormalities (Blackstone et al., 2011; Harding, 1981; Harding, 1983). There are over 50 genetic types of HSP, with 55 spastic paraplegia (SPG) genes and 72 spastic gait loci (SPG 1 to SPG72) contributing to pathogenesis having been described so far (Fink, 2013; Lo Giudice et al., 2014). SPG loci are identified in all manners of autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, and x-linked inheritance. The mutant gene-encoded proteins are categorized in diversified cellular functions, including ER morphology, axonal transport, mitochondrial functions, myelination, protein folding, ER-stress response, receptor trafficking, and vesicle transport (Blackstone et al., 2011). Approximately 60 % of HSP cases in North America and Northern Europe are associated with mutations in SPG genes that encode ER membrane shaping and modeling proteins (Lo Giudice et al., 2014). A number of SPG mutants have been associated with the functional deficiency of several tubular ER proteins (Table 1). Involvement of tubular ER proteins in HSPs suggests that integrated tubular ER is essential for long-term axon maintenance.

Spastin haplo-insufficiency, MT disorganization, and axonal degeneration

Mutations in the spastin coding gene, SPG4, comprise about 40 % of autosomal dominant (ATD) and 20 % of sporadic HSPs (Hazan et al., 1999). More than 200 mutations, including nonsense, frame shift, splice site, missense, and large-scale deletions, have been described in the SPG4 gene; however, a vast majority of those mutants affect the AAA domain that acts in a loss-of-function mechanism (haplo-insufficiency) (Hazan et al., 1999; Beetz et al., 2006; Burger et al., 2000; Depienne et al., 2007; Roll-Mecak and Vale, 2008). In many HSPs, haplo-insufficiency is caused by a common inactivating C448Y mutation in both full-length M1 and shorter isoform M87 spastin (Hazan et al., 1999; Fonknechten et al., 2000). Overwhelming evidence from in vitro and in vivo models and HSPs patients suggest that spastin mutations cause disorganization of MTs, impairments in axonal transport, and swelling and degeneration of axons (Evans et al., 2005; Salinas et al., 2007; Kasher et al., 2009; Tarrade et al., 2006; Trotta et al., 2004). Both M1 and M87 spastin share common MT binding and an AAA domains, which are sufficient for MT serving activities (Evans et al., 2005; Roll-Mecak and Vale, 2008). However, M1 spastin contains an extra N-terminal hydrophobic domain that interacts with REEP1, RTNs, and ATL1, facilitating oligomeric complex formation on tubular ER (Park et al., 2010; Hu et al., 2009) (Fig. 1D). M1 spastin is abundantly present in adult spinal cord, which is an axonal predominant region of the CNS, while the M87 isoform is ubiquitously distributed (Connell et al., 2009; Solowska et al., 2008). Interestingly, spastin haplo-insufiency mostly affects the axonal part, leaving the remaining neuronal compartments intact (Tarrade et al., 2006; Solowska et al., 2008). Compared to M87 mutants, M1 mutants are more detrimental to MTs dynamics, axonal maintenance, and neurite outgrowth (Tarrade et al., 2006; Solowska et al., 2008). The specific effects of mutant M1 spastin on axonal MT organization suggest that integrated tubular ER morphology is essential for specialized MT serving machinery in axons. This also implies that an integrated structural confirmation of oligomeric complex with spastin, REEPs, RTNs, and ATLs is critical to coordinate ER dynamics and MT organization (Fig. 1B and D), which is necessary for sustainable axonal growth.

Defective tubular ER shaping and networking

The functions of tubular ER largely depend upon integrated activities of tubular networking and coordinated ER dynamics with MTs (Schwarz and Blower, 2016; Terasaki et al., 1986; Waterman-Storer and Salmon, 1998). Defects in several groups of proteins that participate in ER tubule networking and dynamics have been identified due to genetic mutations in numerous HSPs cases (Table 1).

ATL1/SPG3A.

Mutations in ATL1 (SPG3A) account for approximately 10 % of autosomal dominant form and are a predominant (6.8 %) cause of early-onset HSPs (Lo Giudice et al., 2014; Namekawa et al., 2006). More than 30 mutations in SPG3A have been identified, most of which were categorized as missense mutations (Guelly et al., 2011; Zhao et al., 2001). While the link between defective tubular ER junctions in HSPs carrying SPG3A mutations is not yet fully understood, a class of SPG3A mutant showed functional deficiencies in ER network formation, REEP1 re-distribution, and membrane fusion (Byrnes and Sondermann, 2011; Ulengin et al., 2015). Purified ATL1 mutants also showed faulty protein folding, loss of dimerization, and defective GTPase hydrolysis (Byrnes and Sondermann, 2011). However, two SPG3A variants did not affect ER morphogenesis upon overexpression in non-neuronal cells (Byrnes and Sondermann, 2011; Ulengin et al., 2015). Although, this implies that ATL1 might be playing additional roles in addition to membrane junctions, this never been explored in highly-specialized neuronal cells other than the fact that depletion of ATL1 largely affects axonal elongation (Zhu et al., 2006). The requirements of neuron-specific factors that regulate membrane junction activities of ATLs could be another possible explanation. Further investigation using neuron-based systems may answer this question.

RTN2/SPG12.

Mutations in RTN2 are associated with the development of the pure form of autosomal-dominant HSP, which is assigned to SPG12 loci (Reid et al., 2000). Due to preferential neuronal expression and predominant localization on tubular ER, RTN2B isoforms have been suggested to be the prime isoforms involved in HSPs (Yang and Strittmatter, 2007). There are two SPG12 mutations; a complete deletion of RTN2, and a frame-shift change that codes truncated proteins (Montenegro et al., 2012). The complete loss of RTN2 proteins act as haplo-insufficiency in HSPs. On the other hand, the truncated mutant is unable to interact with other tubular ER shaping proteins, such as with spastin, which dislocates RTN2 from the ER and displays defective ER networking (Montenegro et al., 2012).

REEPs/SPG31 and SPG72.

Mutations in two REEP family members, REEP1 (SPG31) and REEP2 (SPG72), are associated with HSPs (Esteves et al., 2014; Zuchner et al., 2006). REEP1, the first identified member of the family, is predominantly expressed in neurons. Having the MT binding domain, REEP1 participates in coordinated activities of ER and MTs dynamics that stimulate ER networking (Park et al., 2010; Renvoise and Blackstone, 2010; Evans et al., 2006). HSP-associated mutations in REEP1 comprise missense, splice site, nonsense, and exon deletion, all of which cause loss of function and haplo-insufficiency (Battini et al., 2011). Deletion of exon 2 in SPG31 causes complete loss of REEP1 and development of a severe gait disorder in mice, which resembles human HSP (Beetz et al., 2013). The functional deficiency of REEP1 in this mouse model displayed defective peripheral ER organization and loss of neurons in the corticospinal tract. Interestingly, neuronal soma was largely preserved at axon-affected regions of the brain in REEP1-deficient mice. These findings strongly suggest that axonal maintenance largely depends upon proper tubular ER organization, in which REEP1 and MT interactions play critical roles in MT-dependent tubular ER dynamics to maintain tubular networks in axons. This is also supported by the fact that overexpression of cytoplasmic c-terminal-deleted SPG31 mutant in Cos7 cells impairs REEP1-MT interactions and disrupt ER networks (Park et al., 2010). Furthermore, fibroblasts from a patient with SPG31 mutation showed abnormal mitochondria network organization and deficient mitochondrial energy production, which links the disruption in tubular ER-regulated mitochondria dynamics and function in HSP (Friedman et al., 2011; Goizet et al., 2011).

Mutations in the REEP2 gene (SPG72) have been reported in two families with early onset of pure HSPs, in which one showed autosomal dominant and the other autosomal recessive segregation patterns (Esteves et al., 2014). In the first case, a missense mutation 107 T > A in SPG72 exhibited a dominant negative effect of REEP2 mutant, in which the mutant inhibited the binding of WT protein without reducing protein levels. Cultured fibroblasts from this patient showed abnormalities in ER sheet and ER tubule distribution with swelled ER morphology. The second case involved a missense change 215 T >A that transmits in trans and a splice site mutation 105 + 3G > T with an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern (Esteves et al., 2014). Functional analysis showed that the splice site mutation reduced protein expression by 50 %, while trans led to loss of the membrane binding properties of mutant REEP2. These findings demonstrate a diversified mechanism of REEP2 protein dysfunction and its consequences on ER tubule organization and axonal degeneration.

Protrudin/SPG33.

A single missense mutation G191V has been identified in the e tubular ER network-stabilizing protein protrudin (ZFYVE27) in a family with HSP, and the locus was assigned as SPG33 (Chang et al., 2013; Hashimoto et al., 2014; Mannan et al., 2006). The mutant SPG33 showed aberrant tubular structure upon cellular expression and inability of mutant protrudin to interact with spastin. Upon expression in corticospinal neurons, the mutant SPG33 showed abnormal intracellular trafficking and increased susceptibility to ER stress (Hashimoto et al., 2014). However, the effects of this mutant on ER network stability and ER function are not yet known. Further investigation is needed to elucidate mutant protrudin effects on ER functions and axonal degeneration.

Impact of SPG mutations on brains in HSP

SPG mutations primarily affect corticospinal tracts that connect the cerebral cortex to the spinal cord. However, enormous evidence indicated that brains are also affected by multiple SPG mutants (Servelhere et al., 2021; Tu et al., 2024; Aghakhanyan et al., 2014; Lin et al., 2020). In a study with 9 SPG mutants, Servelhere et al. reported a gene-specific susceptibility of the brain regions in HSP (Servelhere et al., 2021). SPG4 primarily affected basal ganglia, thalamus, and posterior white matter (WM). Cerebellum damage was noticed in SPG7 and SPG11 mutants. On the other hand, cortical thinning at the motor cortices and pallidal atrophy were identified in SPG8, and the abnormal brain area was similar in SPG4 and SPG8 mutants in higher SPAST and WASHC5 expression cases (Servelhere et al., 2021). Another study also reported widespread gray matter (GM) damage in the cortical region in a Chinese family with the SPG4 mutant (Lin et al., 2020). This evidence suggests that a subset of neurons in a specific brain region is likely susceptible to tubular ER abnormality in spastin mutant HSP.

Tubular ER dysfunction, Ca2+ signaling, local protein translation, and ER phagy

The tubular domain of ER and mitochondria plays critical roles in intracellular Ca2+ balancing (Hu et al., 2011; Contreras et al., 2010). Specifically, axonal ER tubules regulate neurotransmission and synaptic activities that largely rely on rapid Ca2+ influx (Kuijpers et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2021). The impaired synaptic plasticity and cognition in Tg-RTN3 mice are likely a consequence of tubular ER dysfunction in axons to maintain Ca2+ balance that is necessary for continuous synaptic activity (Hu et al., 2007; Shi et al., 2009). Deregulation of Ca2+ signaling, the impaired ability of mitochondria for Ca2+ buffering, and elevated levels of intracellular Ca2+ has been shown in AD brains (Supnet and Bezprozvanny, 2010; Tong et al., 2018). DN formation by tubular ER is possibly a contributing factor for such elevated Ca2+ levels in AD brain neurons and induction of lysosomal exocytosis, which eventually may cause a reduced LAMP1/SAP-C+ DN in plaque forming area (Sharoar et al., 2016; Sharoar et al., 2019; Sharoar et al., 2021b; Jaiswal et al., 2002; Tancini et al., 2020). Ca2+ dysregulation is also reported in spastin- and spactascin -linked HSP in which enlarged ER morphology is unable to maintain Ca2+ intake (Boutry et al., 2019; Rizo et al., 2022). Patient-derived iPSCs from another ERLIN2 variant of HSP showed reduced intracellular free calcium that triggered ER stress-mediated apoptosis (Zhu et al., 2023). The mechanisms of Ca2+ dysregulation in other SPG variants are not clearly understood yet.

Axonal tubular ER regulates local translation by frequent contact with ribosomes to support axonal development in premature neurons (Deng et al., 2022; Nedozralova et al., 2022; Falahati et al., 2022). Defective local protein translation in axons has been suggested in several neurodegenerative diseases (Nagano and Araki, 2021; Lin et al., 2021). However, the direct evidence of tubular ER involvement in the process in AD and HSP is limited. Impaired tubular ER-ribosome interaction or the deficiency of tubular ER proteins RTN1 and RTN3 negatively affects the axonal growth (Falahati et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023). Thus, functional deficit due to abnormal aggregation or haplo-insufficiency of tubular ER proteins in AD and HSP, respectively, are probably associated with defective axonal regeneration. Additionally, defective tubular ER morphology in these neurodegenerative diseases could contribute to perturbed axonal trafficking of mRNA and other organelles that is essential for local translation (Kasher et al., 2009; Boutry et al., 2019; Denton et al., 2016).

ER-phagy is a selective autophagy that involves the receptor-mediated lysosomal degradation of ER in response to cellular stress (Reggiori and Molinari, 2022). ATL3 and RTN3 are tubular ER-resident membrane-associated ER-phagy receptors (Hill et al., 2023). While ATL1 in commonly involved in HSP, the ATL3 mutation is also associated with the SPG variants (Behrendt et al., 2019). ATL3 deficiency inhibits the degradation of other tubular ER proteins, such as REEP5, REEP2, and RTN4, but it does not alter the protein levels of ER sheet markers in nutrient-deprived cells (Chen et al., 2019). Hence, axonal tubular ER network impairment, autophagy disturbance, and REEPs redistribution in ATLs-associated HSP are possibly the impact of defective ER-phagy (Byrnes and Sondermann, 2011; Ulengin et al., 2015; Behrendt et al., 2021). Long-form RTN3 directly interacts with LC3 and regulates the ER degradation (Grumati et al., 2017). We reported that RTN3+- DNs/RIDNs, or tubular ER accumulation, occurs earlier than LC3-DN formation in AD mouse brains (Sharoar et al., 2019). In our 3-dimensional electron microscopic study with Tg-RTN3 mouse brains, we notice the association of tubular ER with vesicle structures in DN forming inclusion (unpublished data). Similarly, tubular ER-vesicle association was observed in the DN forming inclusions at the axonal terminus, and the existence of various multivesicular bodies was noticed in RTN3-immunolabeled tubular ER inclusions in AD mouse brains (Sharoar et al., 2019). This evidence implicates that tubular ER clustering likely contributes to defective ER quality control. Further investigation is needed to elucidate a direct link between tubular ER dysfunction and defective ER-phagy.

Tubular ER abnormality in other neurodegenerative diseases

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease that is characterized by the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra due to intracellular aggregation of α-synuclein in Lewy bodies (LBs) and Lewy neurites (LNs) (Shahmoradian et al., 2019). Involvement of ER dysfunctions, such as elevated ER stress (Colla, 2019), disrupted ER-mitochondria tethering and Ca2+ homeostasis (Paillusson et al., 2017), and abnormality in ER lipid (Valadas et al., 2018), have been suggested in PD. However, direct evidence of tubular ER abnormality has recently been reported in WDR45 mutants that are associated with early adulthood PD (Tetsuka et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). WDR45 conditional knockout mice showed reduction of midbrain dopaminergic neurons, neuronal loss with tubular ER accumulated axonal dystrophy in the dorsal and ventral striatum, impaired motor function, emotional instability, and memory loss (Wang et al., 2024). A concentrated RTN3 and the existence of REEPs in tubular ER accumulated axonal dystrophy at the striatum region of the brain in WDR45-deficient mice suggest that RTN3-mediated tubular ER clustering is likely a common mechanism in adolescence Parkinsonism, AD, and aging brains (Sharoar et al., 2016; Sharoar et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2024).

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative disorder that is caused by gradual deterioration of upper and lower motor neurons (Feldman et al., 2022). The evidence of tubular ER clustering in VAPB-linked ALS was first reported by Teulin et al. in 2007 (Teuling et al., 2007). Overexpression of the ALS-associated VAPB-P56S mutant induced VAPB aggregation that was colocalized with ER tubule clustering in HeLa cells. Similar tubular ER clustering was found in the inclusions in motor neurons of Tg-VAPB-P56S mice brains (Kuijpers et al., 2013). However, such tubular ER accumulation in VAPB-P56S-linked ALS has been suggested to be related to a reversible defect in the ER-associated degradation (ERAD) pathway (Kuijpers et al., 2013). The tubular ER protein, RTN4/Nogo, has also been associated with the pathogenesis of ALS (Jokic et al., 2006). A correlation between Nogo-A protein level and disease severity in ALS patients (Teng and Tang, 2008) and delayed disease onset in the Nogo-ablated ALS mouse model (Bros-Facer et al., 2014) suggest that abnormal tubular ER morphology is likely associated with RTN4-linked ALS pathology.

Disrupted Ca2+ signaling has been suggested in Huntington disease (HD), a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that is inherited in autosomal dominant fashion (Mackay et al., 2018; Walker, 2007). While an exact role of tubular ER in dysregulated Ca2+ homeostasis in HD is not established yet, dysfunction in Vps13A, a lipid-transfer protein that localizes at ER-membrane contact sites, suggests possible involvement of tubular ER in HD-like pathology (Kumar et al., 2018; Ueno et al., 2001).

Conclusion and future perspective

Neuronal death, the final outcome of a neurodegenerative disease, is the impacts of a multitude upstream cellular events, the vast majority of which are involved in irregular ER functions (Roussel et al., 2013; Volgyi et al., 2015). Thus, ER systems are being investigated with a prime focus on neuronal soma and dendrites, while its functions in axons remain largely unknown. Over the past decade, a number of elegant studies have elucidated the architecture of the tubular domain of ER and its relative predominant existence in axons (Hu et al., 2011; Hu et al., 2008; Ramirez and Couve, 2011; Yperman and Kuijpers, 2023; Hoffmann et al., 2021). Involvement of several ER-shaping proteins in the pathogenesis of two prominent neurodegenerative diseases has advanced our knowledge on the essential roles of these proteins in the morphogenesis of a functional neuronal ER system and its necessity for neuronal outgrowth and lifelong maintenance.

DN formation in AD brain is a complex mechanism, and a growing list of proteins is involved in this process. The definition of tubular ER dysfunction and its link to axonal mitochondrial loss in AD opens a new platform to be explored for better understanding the mechanism of disease onset. Although the Tg-RTN3 mouse model has provided valuable insights to hypothesize a sensible machinery of tubular ER-associated DN formation in aging mouse and AD brains, the roles of other tubular ER proteins, such as REEP2 and 5, in AD remain unknown. Since tubular ER is actively involved in mitochondrial dynamics and MT organization, it is crucial to understand how dysfunction in a particular tubular ER shaping and networking protein contributes to AD pathology and clinical symptoms. Appropriate in vitro and in vivo models are currently lacking to study the roles of these proteins. Future initiatives to develop such models may elucidate the involvement of tubular ER deregulation in mitochondria dysfunction and axonal degeneration in AD.

Although large numbers of tubular ER proteins are associated with HSPs, spastin is the major contributor (Table 1). Unlike AD pathology, most of the mutant proteins in HSPs show insufficient expression or impaired function. The deficiency of spastin largely affects MTs organization due to mutations in MT-serving domain. While both M1 and M87 spastin showed similar mutations in HSPs, why tubular ER-localized M1 spastin severely affects axons compared to M87 mutant is unknown (Tarrade et al., 2006). Mutations in other tubular ER proteins largely affect ER networking in HSPs. Some of these SPG mutations have been observed only in rare HSP cases and are not extensively explored (Hashimoto et al., 2014; Montenegro et al., 2012; Esteves et al., 2014). Tubular ER-shaping proteins act as an integrated functional unit (Blackstone et al., 2011), and possibly they compensate each other in some deficient conditions (Sharoar et al., 2016). Future studies may answer whether such compensation mechanisms are involved in tubular ER-shaping proteins in HSPs.
