Advances in pharmacotherapy for vascular cognitive impairment and vascular dementia: An update of 2025
Authors: Doan‐Khanh Tran, Yi‐Chun Kuan, Li‐Kai Huang, Chaur‐Jong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVascular cognitive impairment (VCI), including vascular dementia (VaD), encompasses a spectrum of cognitive deficits resulting from cerebrovascular pathology, ranging from mild impairment to dementia. As the second leading cause of dementia worldwide, VCI remains a major therapeutic challenge. A comprehensive search of ClinicalTrials.gov and the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO ICTRP) identified randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted or completed between 2012 and 2025. Trials enrolling participants diagnosed with VCI or VaD across all disease stages and all trial phases were included. Forty‐one RCTs met the inclusion criteria encompassing diverse therapeutic mechanisms. Investigated agents included neuroprotective and vascular‐targeted compounds, neurotransmitter modulators, symptom‐focused drugs for behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD), traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) formulations, and agents acting on novel pathways. Recent trials in VCI and VaD show increasing methodological rigor and therapeutic diversity, signaling growing research momentum in the field. Nonetheless, progress is constrained by regional concentration of studies, limited publication of completed results, and inconsistent disease classification across stages. Strengthening global collaboration, ensuring transparent reporting, and standardizing disease staging will be critical to advancing pharmacological development for vascular cognitive disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Vascular cognitive impairment (VCI), including vascular dementia (VaD), is an umbrella term describing cognitive deficits that arise from cerebrovascular pathology. VCI encompasses a broad spectrum of cognitive disorders associated with cerebrovascular disease and vascular risk factors, ranging from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to severe dementia. VaD, a subset within this continuum, refers to dementia primarily attributable to vascular causes such as stroke and cerebral infarction. 1 , 2 VCI often also involves mixed pathologies that include features of Alzheimer's disease (AD). 1 , 2

Both VCI and VaD are heterogeneous conditions comprising multiple subtypes, each with distinct underlying mechanisms. The main subtypes include cortical VaD, which results from multiple cortical infarcts or a single strategic infarct, and subcortical ischemic vascular dementia (SIVD), typically caused by small‐vessel disease leading to subcortical ischemic lesions, lacunar infarcts, and diffuse white‐matter changes.s. 2 , 3 In clinical practice, mixed dementia is also common, characterized by the coexistence of vascular pathology with other neurodegenerative processes, most frequently AD. 4

VCI is recognized as the second most common cause of dementia after AD, accounting for ≈20%–40% of all dementia cases. 1 With the global population aging and the incidence of vascular diseases continuing to rise, the prevalence of VCI is expected to double over the next three decades. 5

VaD and VCI develop through a series of interconnected molecular events involving cellular homeostasis impairment, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, glymphatic dysfunction, deficits of repairment, and white matter injury. In the subclinical stage, endothelial dysfunction and subtle blood–brain barrier (BBB) leakage represent the earliest pathological changes, often accompanied by initial disruption of glymphatic clearance. During the early symptomatic phase of ischemic brain injury or stroke, BBB breakdown becomes more pronounced, promoting oxidative stress and establishing a sustained proinflammatory state. In established VCI, chronic activation of inflammatory cascades‐particularly the Nucleotide‐binding oligomerization domain (NOD)‐like receptors family pyrin domain‐containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome—together with progressive glymphatic failure leads to the accumulation of toxic metabolites. The advanced stage is characterized by irreversible structural damage, including neuronal loss, gliosis, and the breakdown of essential neural networks. 6

VaD and VCI present persistent challenges in both clinical and research settings. A major difficulty lies in their considerable overlap with AD at the pathophysiological level. Because mixed pathologies are highly prevalent, distinguishing pure VaD from AD or mixed dementia remains problematic in clinical practice, leading to inconsistencies in clinical diagnosis and research classification. 7 In addition, research efforts remain disproportionately focused on AD, with a substantially higher number of clinical trials devoted to AD than to VaD or VCI. 8 This imbalance partly reflects the long‐standing perception of AD as the predominant cause of dementia, which has historically directed more funding and research attention toward AD‐related mechanisms and therapeutics. 9

Building upon our 2022 review, 8 which examined the pharmacological landscape of VCI and VaD primarily through ClinicalTrials.gov database, this current review implements a significant methodological expansion to provide a more comprehensive global perspective. In addition to ClinicalTrials.gov, we have incorporated the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO ICTRP) into our search strategy. This addition is intended to capture a broader range of international research efforts and regional clinical trials that may not be registered on the U.S.‐based platform. Combined with an updated search timeframe between 2021 and 2025, these methodological changes allow for a more inclusive and representative synthesis of the current VCI and VaD translational pipeline. Particular emphasis is placed on the mechanisms of action, stages of drug development, and emerging therapeutic strategies. By summarizing recent advancements, this review seeks to identify existing gaps within the translational pipeline and guide future research directions in the treatment of vascular cognitive disorders.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted using ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP to identify pharmacological trials related to VaD and VCI. The search terms included “vascular dementia,” “vascular cognitive impairment,” “poststroke dementia,” and “poststroke cognitive impairment.” Eligible trials were those that had been initiated, completed, or had an estimated completion date between 2012 and 2025.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of pharmacological treatments conducted in participants diagnosed with VaD or VCI. Trials involving broader populations were eligible only if they recruited specific subgroup for the VCI or VaD populations. Interventions were required to involve pharmacological agents aimed at improving cognitive, behavioral, or functional outcomes related to VaD or VCI. Comparators included placebo, active control, or different dosages of the same investigational drug. Trials were eligible if they reported primary or secondary outcomes concerning cognition, mood, or behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD). Only human RCTs classified as Phase I–IV were included; however, studies labeled as Phase 0 or “not applicable” (N/A) were also considered if they evaluated drug efficacy in individuals with VaD or VCI. Finally, the review included trials that were initiated, completed, or had an estimated completion date between 2012 and 2025. Trials listed as “terminated” were eligible if stopped for scientific reasons such as safety or futility; conversely, studies terminated before recruitment or stopped (defined as having no status update or results for more than 2 years beyond their estimated completion date) were excluded as non‐evaluable.

This review focuses exclusively on pharmacological interventions to provide a targeted analysis of the drug development pipeline. Although non‐pharmacological strategies, such as medical devices or lifestyle modifications, are vital to treatment, they were excluded to maintain a clear focus on molecular mechanisms and translational gaps specific to pharmacological landscape.

Data extraction

For each eligible trial, the following data were extracted: trial identification number, sample size (estimated or actual), drug name, participant characteristics, intervention and control arms, outcome measures, study phase, trial status, country, and start and completion years. Trials were further categorized according to therapeutic target to enable descriptive and comparative analyses across pharmacological categories.

Quantitative results are reported as presented in the original publication; unless otherwise specified, values are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD).

The diagnostic criteria for VaD and VCI were most frequently based on or the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth or Fifth Editions (DSM‐IV or DSM‐5; n = 18; 43.9%) or the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke–Association Internationale pour la Recherche et l'Enseignement en Neurosciences (NINDS–AIREN; n = 14; 34.1%). In addition, the Hachinski Ischemic Score (HIS) or modified HIS (n = 18; 43.9%) was frequently employed to confirm the vascular nature of cognitive decline. Cognitive severity at baseline was generally determined using standardized tools such as the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE; n = 26; 63.4%) or the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA; n = 10; 24.4%). Cognitive outcomes were the most commonly designated primary endpoints and were typically evaluated using cognitive assessment tools included the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive Subscale (ADAS‐Cog; n = 9; 22.0%), MoCA (n = 9; 22.0%), and Vascular Dementia Assessment Scale‐Cognitive Subscale (VaDAS‐Cog, n = 9; 22.0%). For trials assessing BPSD, the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI; n = 2; 4.9%) was the predominant outcome measure.

Most studies adopted placebo‐controlled designs, although at least eight trials used active comparators such as donepezil or nimodipine. Notably, one Iranian study (IRCT20181210041912N1) uniquely employed a head‐to‐head comparison of four cholinesterase inhibitors without a placebo control arm.

Early‐stage pipeline (Phase N/A, 0, I–II)

Among the 41 identified trials, 23 were classified as early‐stage studies (Phases I–II or listed as Phase N/A or 0 but meeting inclusion criteria as RCTs conducted in humans). These trials focused primarily on exploring novel pharmacological pathways, establishing safety profiles, and determining optimal dosing parameters. Neuroprotective and vascular agents

Metformin and acarbose are commonly used medications for type 2 diabetes (T2D). Metformin has been shown to improve insulin resistance, reduce neuronal apoptosis, and attenuate oxidative stress and neuroinflammation in the brain. 10 Although it has been investigated for its potential to prevent dementia in T2D, findings remain controversial. Several studies have reported an association between metformin use and a reduced risk of dementia or MCI among individuals with T2D. 10 Conversely, other evidence suggests that long‐term metformin exposure may increase the risk of neurodegenerative disorders, including dementia. 11 A separate review also concluded that no significant reduction in risk was observed among patients receiving metformin monotherapy at the onset of VaD. 12 Acarbose, an alpha‐glucosidase inhibitor that delays intestinal carbohydrate digestion, has demonstrated protective effects against cognitive decline‐including improvements in spatial learning and memory‐in senescence‐accelerated mouse models (SAMP8). 13 A retrospective cohort study further reported that reduced dementia risk associated with acarbose was observed predominantly in women and in patients who were not using metformin. Moreover, the lowest risk of dementia was found among individuals concurrently using all three agents: acarbose, metformin, and pioglitazone. 14 The clinical trial ChiCTRIPR17011855 was designed to evaluate the effects of metformin on cognitive function in patients with abnormal glucose metabolism and non‐dementia vascular cognitive impairment (NDVCI). In this study, 100 participants with NDVCI and impaired glucose metabolism were assigned randomly to receive metformin plus donepezil (n = 50) or acarbose plus donepezil (n = 50). Utilizing two‐sample t‐tests for direct between‐group comparison at the 1‐year endpoint, the published results showed that the metformin–donepezil group significantly outperformed the acarbose–donepezil group in cognitive recovery. Although ADAS‐Cog scores in the metformin group improved from 16.9 ± 6.1 at baseline to 11.9 ± 3.7 at the 1‐year endpoint (p < 0.001), the difference between the two treatment arms was highly significant (p < 0.001), as no comparable benefit was observed in the acarbose–donepezil group (17.0 ± 5.9 to 15.9 ± 3.9, p = 0.295). 15

Daphnetin is a natural coumarin derivative with multiple pharmacological activities, including antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and anti‐apoptotic effects. 16 It provides “triple protection” for the blood, vascular endothelium, and ischemic tissues by inhibiting inflammation, anticoagulation, and thrombosis; preserving endothelial integrity; and regulating vasoactive substances. These properties make it particularly beneficial for cardio‐cerebrovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, and microvascular complications. 17 Evidence regarding its efficacy in dementia, particularly VaD and VCI, remains limited. Preclinical studies have shown that daphnetin protects against diabetes‐related cognitive dysfunction by regulating glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor (GLP‐1R)–mediated inflammation and oxidative stress, acting as a potential GLP‐1R agonist. These findings suggest that GLP‐1R may represent a key therapeutic target for preventing cognitive impairment associated with T2D. 16 In another study using a double‐transgenic mouse model of AD (amyloid precursor protein/presenilin 1 [APP/PS1]), daphnetin ameliorated cognitive deficits and reduced amyloid beta (Aβ) deposition, effects primarily attributed to decreased astrocyte activation and modulation of APP processing. 18 The ongoing clinical trial NCT06507488, although listed as Phase N/A, is currently recruiting participants with VCI to evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of oral daphnetin capsules administered over 6 months. The study is expected to be completed in 2026.

N‐acetylcysteine (NAC) is a well‐established antioxidant and reducing agent that scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) and indirectly replenishes cellular glutathione (GSH) levels. 19 Although evidence specific to VaD and VCI remains limited, previous studies in AD have reported reductions in Aβ plaque formation and decreased lipid peroxidation in hippocampal and prefrontal tissues following NAC supplementation. These effects were associated with slower cognitive decline and potential neuroprotection, although findings remain inconclusive. 20 , 21 , 22 NAC has also demonstrated modest cognitive benefits in individuals with MCI, supporting its potential as a therapeutic adjunct for VCI. 23 The clinical trial NCT03306979, completed in Canada in 2023 with 30 participants, compared NAC with placebo in individuals with VCI; however, the results have not yet been published.

BAC, or CHARSIRE Trauma Complex 1, is a topical anti‐inflammatory formulation containing a multi‐glycan complex (MGC) derived from soybean extract. It is applied to the nasal area, scalp, and neck, where it is thought to reduce proinflammatory cytokine activity and consequently limit neuroinflammatory processes in the brain. This mechanism represents a novel, non‐invasive approach to mitigating inflammation‐related neuronal damage in VaD and VCI. 24 The clinical trial NCT02886494, completed in the United States in 2018, investigated the efficacy of BAC in improving cognitive function among individuals with mild‐to‐moderate dementia, including a subgroup of participants with VaD. However, the results have not been published yet.

Neurotransmitter system modulators

The cholinergic precursor choline alphoscerate (alpha‐GPC) crosses the BBB and serves as a substrate for acetylcholine synthesis, a key neurotransmitter involved in memory, learning, and attention. 25 It has been prescribed for cognitive decline associated with AD and other dementias and has demonstrated cognitive benefits in patients with cerebrovascular injury. 25 Nimodipine, an isopropyl calcium channel blocker that readily crosses the BBB, may also offer cognitive benefits in VaD, although evidence remains inconsistent. 26 Despite ongoing uncertainty regarding its efficacy, nimodipine continues to be widely prescribed for cognitive impairment and dementia in several European countries. 26 The clinical trial NCT03228498 was designed to assess whether the combination of choline alphoscerate (1200 mg/day) and nimodipine (90 mg/day) administered orally is more effective than nimodipine (90 mg/day) plus placebo in reducing cognitive decline among patients with SIVD. Although this trial was completed in Italy in 2019, and its protocol has been published, the clinical results remain unavailable.

A four‐arm Iranian trial (IRCT20181210041912N1) directly compared the cholinergic enhancers donepezil, galantamine, and rivastigmine with the glutamatergic modulator memantine to evaluate their relative efficacy in improving cognition and cerebral blood flow. The first three agents act as acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, preventing the enzymatic breakdown of acetylcholine and thereby enhancing cholinergic neurotransmission, a mechanism well established in the management of mild‐to‐moderate AD. 27 A 2021 network meta‐analysis reported moderate‐ to high‐certainty evidence that donepezil (5–10 mg) and galantamine produce slight but statistically significant improvements in cognition among patients with VCI, although the overall clinical effect may be modest. Donepezil showed the greatest cognitive benefit but was also associated with a higher incidence of adverse effects, whereas evidence for rivastigmine remains inconclusive, underscoring the need for further research in VaD. 28 Memantine, a glutamatergic modulator approved for the treatment of moderate‐to‐severe AD, is also used off‐label for other types of dementia, including VaD. 29 According to a Cochrane review, memantine likely provides a small benefit for cognitive function, behavior, and mood, and may reduce agitation compared with placebo. 30 The results of this study demonstrated that only donepezil and memantine significantly improved cognitive function over a 3‐month period. Specifically, the mean MMSE scores increased from 17.09 ± 4.01 to 18.90 ± 3.64 (p = 0.001) in the donepezil group and from 16.18 ± 3.37 to 17.63 ± 3.82 (p = 0.009) in the memantine group. In contrast, no significant cognitive changes were observed for galantamine (p = 0.451) or rivastigmine (p = 0.11). 31 Although this study is designed as a randomized clinical trial, the reported improvements in cognitive function (MMSE) are based on within‐arm pre–post comparisons rather than a statistical analysis comparing the four treatment groups against one another, and therefore should be interpreted with caution.

DAOIB acts on the N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate (NMDA) receptor, 32 a key component of excitatory neurotransmission in the brain. 33 Previous research suggests that NMDA receptor enhancers may exert modest positive effects on cognitive function in dementia. 34 DAOIB remains an experimental compound currently being investigated both as monotherapy and in combination with antioxidants or transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) for early‐stage dementia, reflecting its potential for synergistic therapeutic applications. 35 , 36 The clinical trial NCT02103673 evaluated the efficacy of DAOIB at a starting dose of 250–500 mg/day compared with placebo in patients with AD or VaD presenting with BPSD. The study was based on the hypothesis that DAOIB could provide superior improvement in cognitive function and clinical symptoms in this patient population. The results of this study indicated that sodium benzoate did not surpass placebo for the total patient population regarding cognitive restoration or the reduction of behavioral symptoms (p > 0.05). However, in an exploratory post hoc analysis (described by the authors as a “precision medicine approach”), researchers used multiple linear regression to correlate baseline characteristics and post‐randomization changes. This analysis suggested a correlation between cognitive improvement and the degree of DAAO inhibition (p = 0.034), with more pronounced responses observed in a subgroup of younger, female patients with higher body mass index (BMI) and baseline BPSD severity. Notably, although the trial recruited participants with both AD and VAD, the study did not report separate results for these diagnostic groups, as the specific dementia subtype was not found to be a significant independent predictor of treatment response in the final analysis. Given the negative primary results and the post hoc nature of these findings, the authors conclude that these results are preliminary and require confirmation in larger, longer‐term trials. 37

Symptom‐targeted agents focusing on behavioral psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)

Delta‐9‐tetrahydrocannabinol(THC), the principal psychoactive compound in Cannabis sativa, has been investigated for its potential therapeutic effects on neuropsychiatric symptoms in dementia. The psychoactive properties of cannabis are primarily mediated by THC, which modulates the endocannabinoid system to influence mood, behavior, and emotional regulation. 38 Early clinical and observational studies have suggested that cannabinoids may help reduce agitation, aggression, and impulsivity in individuals with dementia, highlighting their potential as alternative treatments for BPSD. 39 , 40 , 41 The clinical trial NCT01608217, completed in the Netherlands, examined the effects of THC in patients with mild‐to‐severe dementia, including those diagnosed with VaD. Although this trial was completed in 2014, the results have not yet been published.

Cannabidiol (CBD) is a non‐psychoactive cannabinoid that interacts with the endocannabinoid system, primarily through cannabinoid receptor type 1 (CB1R), located predominantly in the central nervous system, and receptor type 2 (CB2R), which is expressed mainly in immune cells and peripheral tissues. 42 Previous RCTs have demonstrated that a broad‐spectrum cannabis oil (Avidekel, containing 30% cannabidiol and 1% THC—≈295 mg and 12.5 mg per mL, respectively) significantly reduced agitation compared with placebo in patients with dementia‐related behavioral disturbances, with minimal adverse effects. 42 In the trial RBR686kmpz, conducted in Brazil with a sample size of 30 participants, cannabidiol (300 mg/day) treatment was associated with a significant reduction in BPSD over 4 weeks, demonstrated by significant interaction effects for the BPRS total score (F = 4.02, p < 0.05) and the NPI total score (F = 3.61, p = 0.05). However, it is important to note that these findings are preliminary and have not yet been published in a peer‐reviewed journal. 43

Traditional Chinese Medicine

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) exerts its therapeutic effects through the synergistic action of multiple active metabolites, achieving clinical benefits via complex, multi‐target mechanisms. 44 For this reason, all TCM interventions included in this review are categorized as multi‐target agents. TCM has been used widely in China and is gaining increasing attention in Western countries. Previous reviews have reported that formulations such as Nao XinTong, Nao MaiTai, and TongXinLuo demonstrated potentially significant benefits when compared with no treatment or standard Western medicine, supported by relatively strong evidence. 45 In the trials conducted between 2012 and 2025, investigators commonly adopted dual diagnostic frameworks, integrating Western diagnostic criteria for vascular cognitive disorders with specific TCM syndrome classifications. Several studies are ongoing or not yet recruiting, investigating interventions such as Yizhi Annao Granule, SYD, Hengqing I Formula, WYP, SQYF, YXQN, and JYHZ. In addition, trials include SLT, Qi Zhi Tong Luo, YXQN, Naosuikang, Fufang Danshen, and TMBCZG completed between 2014 to 2018; however, their findings have not yet been published in peer‐reviewed journals.

Neuroprotective and vascular agents

Butylphthalide is another key multi‐target compound currently under late‐phase clinical investigation. Originally extracted from celery seed, it exhibits diverse pharmacological actions, including antiplatelet aggregation, mitochondrial protection, and anti‐apoptotic effects. 46 , 47 , 48 Clinically, butylphthalide has been used in the treatment of ischemic stroke, AD, and Parkinson's disease. 46 Emerging evidence supports its efficacy and safety in VaD, positioning it as one of the most promising multi‐target candidates in the current VaD/VCI treatment pipeline. 49 , 50 Two ongoing studies, both conducted in China, are further evaluating its therapeutic potential. The first, NCT03804229 (Phase III), includes 700 participants with VaD or SIVD and examines the effects of butylphthalide soft capsules (100 mg per pill, two pills per dose, three times daily). The second, ChiCTRIPR17013143 (Phase IV), targets mild VaD caused by subcortical ischemic vascular disease, enrolling 64 participants; however, the dosage regimen has not been specified.

Mexidol is a compound with both antioxidant and antihypoxic properties that protects neuronal cells from oxidative damage and oxygen deprivation—two major contributors to cognitive decline. Evidence from a meta‐analysis of 10 prospective clinical studies demonstrated that Mexidol produced statistically significant improvements in cognitive function among patients with chronic cerebral ischemia and mild‐to‐moderate cognitive impairment. 51 The clinical trial NCT06834490, a completed Phase III study targeting oxidative stress and hypoxic injury, evaluated the effects of Mexidol intravenous (i.v.) 500 mg (10 mL) once daily for 14 days, followed by Mexidol FORTE 250 mg orally (one tablet three times daily) for 60 days, compared with placebo in 318 patients with mild‐to‐moderate VCI. Although this trial was completed in Russia in 2020, the results have not yet been published.

Nicergoline acts primarily through alpha‐1 adrenergic receptor blockade, leading to vasodilation and improved cerebral perfusion. It also exhibits antiplatelet aggregation activity, further supporting microcirculatory function. 52 A meta‐analysis of 14 studies provided evidence that nicergoline may improve cognitive and behavioral outcomes in patients with chronic cerebrovascular disorders, including VaD. 53 In regions where it remains approved, nicergoline is clinically used to alleviate cognitive dysfunction, memory impairment, and to reduce the overall severity of VaD symptoms. 54 The Phase IV clinical trial ChiCTR1900026538, currently recruiting in China, is comparing nicergoline 10 mg three times daily and 20 mg three times daily with a control group in 162 participants diagnosed with NDVCI and a history of acute ischemic stroke.

Pentoxifylline is believed to enhance cerebral blood flow by reducing blood viscosity and improving red blood cell deformability, thereby facilitating oxygen delivery to brain tissue. 55 Clinical studies suggest that pentoxifylline may help slow cognitive decline in patients with VaD, particularly those with marked cerebrovascular disease. However, the existing evidence is limited by small sample sizes and methodological heterogeneity, leading to mixed conclusions regarding its efficacy. Reported benefits appear most pronounced in specific patient subgroups or when pentoxifylline is used as part of combination therapy rather than as monotherapy. 56 The Phase IV clinical trial ChiCTR2500104470, initiated in 2025, and expected to be completed in 2027, includes 50 participants with mild‐to‐moderate VCI. The control group receives basic treatment, including blood pressure reduction, glucose control, and lipid regulation, whereas the intervention group receives the same standard care plus pentoxifylline sustained‐release tablets (400 mg per dose, twice daily; total 400–800 mg per day. The study aims to evaluate improvements in global cognitive function, as measured by the MMSE.

Neurotransmitter system modulators

Memantine, currently approved at doses of 5–20 mg/day for immediate‐release tablets and 7–28 mg/day for extended‐release capsules, is indicated for moderate to severe dementia. 30 The mechanism of action of memantine, discussed earlier in the section on early‐phase trials, involves modulation of the NMDA receptor to regulate excitatory neurotransmission and prevent glutamate‐induced neurotoxicity. A Phase IV clinical trial (NCT03986424) is investigating two dosage regimens—20 mg versus 10 mg daily—in 130 patients with moderate to severe VaD, with MMSE scores of 10–20. 57 The study aims to determine the optimal therapeutic dose specific to VaD. The trial has been completed in Russia in 2022, although results have not yet been published.

Fluoxetine, a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI), has been proposed to exert neuroprotective effects through multiple mechanisms, including promotion of neurogenesis, enhancement of synaptic plasticity, and reduction of neuroinflammation and oxidative stress via activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2/heme oxygenase‐1 (Nrf2/HO‐1) antioxidant pathway and inhibition of the Toll‐like receptor 4/NOD‐like receptor pyrin domain containing 3 (TLR4/NLRP3) inflammasome signaling pathway. 58 An open‐label study reported that fluoxetine improved cognitive recovery in specific domains and increased serum brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) levels in patients with NDVCI compared with matched controls. 59 A review further suggested that fluoxetine may enhance cognitive function in both AD and VaD. 60 In the open‐label clinical trial ChiCTRTRC12002247, fluoxetine was evaluated for its effects on cognition and serum levels of BDNF in 50 participants with VaD (including both intervention and control groups). Participants in the intervention arm received fluoxetine 20 mg/day alongside standard secondary prevention of stroke. Following an intention‐to‐treat approach, all 50 randomized participants completed the 12‐week study and were included in the final analysis. Between‐group comparisons using a two‐sample t‐test demonstrated that the mean increase from baseline was significantly greater in the fluoxetine group than the control group for both MMSE scores (1.0 ± 1.2 vs 0.2 ± 1.4; p = 0.031) and Ten‐Point Clock scores (0.8 ± 0.9 vs 0.2 ± 0.9; p = 0.007). Furthermore, serum BDNF concentrations showed a significant between‐group difference in mean change (4.5 ± 3.7 ng/mL vs 0.1 ± 3.2 ng/mL; p < 0.0001), which correlated positively with the degree of MMSE improvement in the treatment arm (r = 0.413, p = 0.04). Although these findings suggest that BDNF may play an important role in cognitive recovery, the relatively small sample size warrants further large‐scale studies to confirm the therapeutic potential of fluoxetine and other SSRIs in VaD. 61

Oxiracetam, a member of the racetam family, has demonstrated significant improvements in cognitive function among patients with multi‐infarct dementia and mild‐to‐moderate AD. 62 Mechanistically, oxiracetam is thought to enhance brain energy metabolism. 63 Experimental studies using VaD rat models have shown that oxiracetam treatment upregulates p‐Akt and p‐mTOR expression, suggesting that activation of the Akt/mTOR pathway may contribute to its protective effects against VaD‐related neuronal apoptosis and autophagy. 64 The KCT0005137 trial enrolled 500 participants with VCI—patients who reported cognitive decline 3 months after stroke and were at high risk of further deterioration. Participants received either oxiracetam 800 mg or placebo, with both groups undergoing exercise intervention to evaluate potential synergistic effects on cognition. If significant differences in physical activity were observed between the groups, the interaction effect between physical activity and treatment allocation was to be examined. The study was completed in Korea in 2022; however, the results have not yet been published.

Symptom‐targeted agents focusing on behavioral psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)

Paracetamol, a non‐opioid analgesic, and buprenorphine, a synthetic opioid, were evaluated in 162 patients with dementia, including those diagnosed with VaD and comorbid depression, in the Phase IV clinical trial NCT02267057. The study was based on the hypothesis that undiagnosed pain may manifest as depressive symptoms in individuals with dementia; therefore, effective pain management might alleviate depression in this population. However, the published findings indicated that analgesic treatment with buprenorphine–paracetamol did not reduce depressive symptoms. Utilizing a linear mixed‐effects model for group comparison (incorporating fixed effects for time, intervention, and their interaction), the placebo group demonstrated significant improvement on the cornell scale for depression in dementia (CSDD), with a mean change of –3.30 (95% confidence interval [CI]: –4.68 to –1.92) compared to –0.66 (95% CI: –2.27 to 0.94) in the active treatment group, resulting in an estimated treatment effect of 2.64 (95% CI: 0.55–4.72, p = 0.013). Stratified analyses revealed that neither active paracetamol (p = 0.162) nor buprenorphine (p = 0.059) independently improved depression compared to placebo. Furthermore, although paracetamol significantly reduced pain from Weeks 6 to 13 (estimated effect: –1.11, p = 0.037), buprenorphine showed no significant effect on pain (p = 0.456). This unexpected outcome for depression was attributed to adverse effects associated with active buprenorphine administration, which led to a 52.3% withdrawal rate in that arm due to psychiatric and neurological adverse reactions. Notably, although the trial included participants with VaD, the study did not report subgroup‐specific analyses for this population, and the findings are therefore representative of a broader advanced dementia cohort rather than isolated for vascular‐specific interpretation. 65

Typical and atypical antipsychotic agents are often used off‐label to manage psychotic symptoms in dementia, regardless of the underlying subtype. Pimavanserin, a serotonin receptor modulator, has been investigated for its potential in treating dementia‐related psychosis (DRP). In a prior study involving patients with AD‐related psychosis, approximately half of whom had cerebrovascular disease, pimavanserin demonstrated a greater therapeutic effect among those with severe psychosis 66 . The Phase III randomized discontinuation clinical trial NCT03325556 evaluated pimavanserin 34 mg and 20 mg compared with placebo for the prevention of psychosis relapse in patients with DRP. While 392 patients with DRP entered the initial open‐label phase—including a subgroup of 38 participants (9.7%) diagnosed with VaD; the primary efficacy analysis focused on 217 sustained responders who were randomly assigned to continue pimavanserin or switch to placebo. Utilizing an ITT population and a Cox proportional‐hazards regression for time‐to‐event analysis, the results indicated a lower relapse risk in the pimavanserin groups compared with placebo; at the interim analysis, relapse occurred in 13% of patients remaining on the drug compared to 28% of those switched to placebo (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.35; 95% CI: 0.17–0.73; p = 0.005). 67 However, the overall treatment effect was predominantly driven by the Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD) subgroup, and no separate efficacy results were reported for the VaD subgroup, leading the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to withhold approval of pimavanserin for broader dementia‐related indications. 68

TCM

The majority of late‐phase trials in this review investigate TCMs, including SLT, DSJ, GKE, and FYP. Most of these studies are either recruiting or not yet recruiting, whereas only three trials have been completed—two evaluating SLT (NCT03789760 and ChiCTR2300072313) and one assessing TZ (NCT02453932). All were conducted in patients with mild‐to‐moderate VaD or VCI and incorporated Western diagnostic criteria for vascular cognitive disorders in their inclusion frameworks. Among these, only one trial has published results. The Phase III trial NCT02453932, which enrolled 543 mild‐to‐moderate VaD, was a three‐arm randomized controlled study comparing TZ, donepezil, and placebo. Although donepezil, a cholinesterase inhibitor, has shown modest but statistically significant benefits in cognitive function, activities of daily living, and clinician‐rated global status in patients with mild‐to‐severe AD, 69 evidence for its efficacy in VaD remains limited, with available studies indicating only small benefits that may not be clinically meaningful in daily life. The mechanism of action of donepezil has been discussed earlier in the section on early‐phase trials. Utilizing ITT analysis and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) for between‐group comparison, the published findings of NCT02453932 demonstrated that both TZ and donepezil significantly outperformed placebo on the VaDAS‐Cog. The between‐group differences versus placebo were 2.73 points (95% CI: 0.88–4.58) for TZ and 3.05 points (95% CI: 1.20–4.91) for donepezil, with least‐squares mean changes of 6.20 (95% CI: 5.31–7.09) and 6.53 (95% CI: 5.63–7.42), respectively, compared to 3.47 95% CI: 1.76–5.19) for placebo. A direct comparison between the two medication groups revealed no significant difference in cognitive improvement (p > 0.05). Global function improvement rates measured by CIBIC‐plus were also significantly higher for TZ (73.71%) and donepezil (79.82%) than for placebo (58.19%; p = 0.004), with no statistically significant difference found between the two active arms (p > 0.05). Regarding behavioral symptoms, both agents significantly outperformed placebo on the NPI, showing mean reductions of −3.03 for TZ (p < 0.001) and −2.21 for donepezil (p = 0.013) compared to −0.36 for placebo, with no statistically significant difference between the two drugs (p = 0.842). Notably, secondary analyses showed that although donepezil significantly outperformed placebo on global cognition (MMSE: +2.61 vs +1.35, p = 0.007) and executive function (Trail‐Making Test Part A [TMT‐A]: −20.60 s vs −11.92 s, p = 0.009), TZ did not produce statistically significant between‐group improvements on these specific measures (p = 0.07 for MMSE). Despite these specific differences in secondary domains, the study concluded that TZ and donepezil demonstrate comparable overall efficacy in patients with mild‐to‐moderate VaD. 70

Agents targeting novel pathways

Prospekta is proposed to exert its effects through modulation of the S100B‐related signaling pathway. S100B is one of the most abundant proteins in the brain and mediates calcium‐dependent regulation of numerous physiological processes by interacting with other proteins and modulating their activity. Overexpression of S100B has been associated with hippocampal dysfunction and dementia‐like behavioral impairments. A systematic review and meta‐analysis reported a statistically significant improvement in cognitive performance, as measured by the MoCA, among patients with VCI treated with Prospekta. 56 Despite these encouraging findings, Prospekta's use in VaD and VCI is not widely recognized or approved internationally, and access remains limited to certain regions. The Phase III clinical trial NCT04552041, which enrolled 406 patients with mild‐to‐moderate VaD and agitation, and was completed in Russia in 2022. Utilizing ITT analysis of 399 randomized patients and repeated‐measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) for group comparison, the results demonstrated that 24 weeks of Prospekta treatment significantly improved cognitive function. MoCA scores increased by 3.3 ± 3.1 points compared to 1.9 ± 3.1 in the placebo group, resulting in a significant between‐group difference of 1.41 (p < 0.0001). In addition, the treatment significantly reduced neuropsychiatric symptoms, with NPI‐C total scores decreasing by 17.1 ± 15.1 points versus 13.0 ± 15.1 for placebo (p = 0.0028). Significant clinical benefits were specifically observed in the domains of agitation (p = 0.0174), anxiety (p = 0.0329), and motor behavior (p = 0.0006), whereas the treatment also significantly reduced caregiver distress scores (−19.7 vs −13.7, p = 0.0003). 71

Published results

Among 24 completed trials identified, only 8 (33.33%) have published results in peer‐reviewed journals. In trials exclusively recruiting mild‐to‐moderate VaD patients, Shi et al. (NCT02453932) found that the Tianzhi granule significantly improved cognition (VADAS‐cog), global function (CIBIC‐plus), and BPSD (NPI) compared to placebo, 70 whereas Tkacheva et al. (NCT04552041) demonstrated that Prospekta significantly improved cognitive scores and reduced neuropsychiatric symptoms compared to placebo. 71 Regarding specific vascular populations, Lin et al. (ChiCTRIPR17011855) reported that metformin and donepezil enhanced global cognition and memory in NDVCI with abnormal glucose metabolism compared to acarbose and donepezil, 15 and Liu et al. (ChiCTRTRC12002247) showed that fluoxetine significantly improved global cognition and executive function in poststroke VaD cohort compared to placebo. 61 An Iranian trial by Iranmanesh et al. (IRCT20181210041912N1) exclusively in VaD patients, found that donepezil and memantine significantly improved cognition function, whereas galantamine and rivastigmine did not; however, the results were based on within‐arm pre–post comparisons, and no between‐group results were reported. 72 In contrast, three studies recruited broader or mixed populations including VaD or VCI as one of their subgroups, but failed to report results separately for their vascular participants. Lin et al. (NCT02103673) found that sodium benzoate did not outperform placebo for the total cohort of AD and VaD patients. 37 Erdal et al. (NCT02267057) showed that paracetamol and buprenorphine failed to reduce depression in advanced dementia compared to placebo group. 65 Tariot et al. (NCT03325556) reported that for pimavanserin significantly reduced psychosis relapse risk in all‐cause dementia, although this effect was primarily driven by the PDD subgroup rather than the vascular population. 73 Supplementary File 1 summarizes the key characteristics and published outcomes of these trials.

The results for the remaining 16 completed trials (66.67%) remain unpublished, including data for agents such as mexidol, oxiracetam, and memantine.

DISCUSSION

The current landscape of pharmacological trials targeting VCI and VaD demonstrates methodological and strategic progress, reflecting the continuous efforts and challenges in this research field. Comparative effectiveness studies directly evaluating novel, repurposed, and established agents in the real‐world scenario have also shed light on identifying more promising pharmacological options. A total of 41 RCTs were identified, with 24 completed, showing a balanced distribution across trial phases and steady development of candidate therapies. Compared with earlier reviews, the inclusion of the WHO ICTRP substantially broadened trial coverage and provided a more representative view of the global VCI/VaD research landscape.

Methodological rigor has improved considerably. Most trials now apply standardized diagnostic criteria such as NINDS–AIREN, DSM‐IV, or DSM‐5, ensuring consistency and comparability. Trials conducted in China frequently adopt a dual diagnostic framework, combining Western criteria with TCM syndrome differentiation—a unique integration that may enhance understanding of vascular cognitive disorders. Furthermore, the increasing use of validated cognitive and neuropsychiatric assessments as endpoints of outcomes highlights the field's shift toward more reliable and reproducible outcomes.

The therapeutic landscape has also diversified. Investigational drugs range from novel compounds, such as butylphthalide and Prospekta, to repurposed agents like metformin and fluoxetine, originally developed for other indications. Dose‐optimization studies (e.g., memantine for moderate‐to‐severe VaD) illustrate growing precision in intervention design. Most trials target NDVCI, MCI, or mild‐to‐moderate VaD, reflecting a research priority toward early‐stage intervention, where disease modification or prevention of dementia conversion may be more achievable.

This diversification is reflected in the temporal patterns observed across early‐ and late‐phase trials. As shown in Figures 3 and 4, the number of studies has remained modest but relatively steady, with a noticeable increase between 2015 and 2020. In early‐phase development, TCM is the most prominent category, indicating that exploratory research is largely driven by region‐specific initiatives in China. In contrast, the increase observed in late‐phase trials is attributed not only to TCM but also to neuroprotective and vascular agents and neurotransmitter system modulators. Together these patterns reveal a dual development trajectory: strong regional momentum in advancing novel TCM therapies, and a parallel, more global effort focused on repurposing or rigorously evaluating mechanistically established drugs.

Despite these advances, several limitations constrain the interpretability and generalizability of current findings. A pronounced geographical imbalance persists, with the majority of studies conducted in China and focused on TCM‐based interventions. This regional concentration limits external validity and the generalizability of findings to non‐Asian populations. In contrast, Western countries remain underrepresented in the VCI/VaD research field.

A significant publication gap also remains: of the 24 completed trials identified, only 8 (33.33%) have published their findings in peer‐reviewed journals, whereas 16 have not published results. In the landscape of drug discovery, the failure to report results for completed trials is often highly correlated with negative or non‐significant findings, a reality that likely contributes to the current lack of data for agents such as mexidol, oxiracetam, and late‐phase memantine dosing. Such omissions not only impede evidence synthesis but also delay the translation of potentially critical findings into clinical practice.

Parallel to this reporting gap is the prevalence of studies with insufficient statistical power to detect clinically meaningful treatment effects. To quantify this systematically, we estimated the minimum detectable effect (MDE) for each included trial using the formula MDE = 2.80 × √(1/n1 + 1/n2), assuming two‐sided α = 0.05 and 80% statistical power. Where allocation ratios were unavailable, equal distribution across arms was assumed. These estimates are reported for all included trials in Tables 1 and 2. MDE values ranged from 0.21 SD to 1.19 SD across included trials, reflecting the wide variability in sample sizes across the reviewed literature. The minimum clinically pertinent effect size for pharmacological interventions in VCI and VaD has not been formally established. In the absence of this anchor, the MDE values reported here should be interpreted descriptively: trials with MDEs approaching or exceeding 0.5–0.7 SD would require implausibly large treatment effects to reach statistical significance, while even the best‐powered trials in this review—with MDEs in the range of 0.21–0.30 SD—cannot be presumed adequate without knowing where the true clinical threshold lies. Null results across this literature should therefore be interpreted with considerable caution.

Moreover, several published trials, including those on paracetamol/buprenorphine and pimavanserin, enrolled participants with VaD but did not provide subgroup‐specific analyses. Such omissions limit the understanding of treatment efficacy within VaD cohorts, whose pathophysiology differs from that of AD or mixed dementias.

In addition, most studies focus on early or moderate disease stages, leaving severe VaD largely understudied despite its clinical importance. The inconsistent definition of disease severity across trials further complicates interpretation. Many studies use general terms such as “VaD” or “VCI/VaD” without specifying severity criteria, introducing heterogeneity and reducing comparability between studies. Standardized definitions and consistent reporting of disease stage are therefore essential for improving methodological clarity and facilitating meta‐analytic synthesis.

The high prevalence of mixed dementia, where vascular pathology often coexists with neurodegenerative processes like AD, represents a significant opportunity for the field that remains largely untapped. Because research efforts remain disproportionately focused on AD, 8 these larger trials likely enroll a substantial number of participants with underlying cerebrovascular pathology. Although such studies could inadvertently evaluate whether therapies are beneficial for the vascular components of dementia, they cannot be meaningfully integrated into a VCI‐specific synthesis when subgroup‐specific reporting is absent. The omission of these broader datasets due to a lack of stratified results highlights a critical “information gap” that hinders the translation of general dementia findings into specific clinical practice for vascular cognitive disorders.

Finally, this review is intentionally scoped to pharmacological treatment of established VCI and VaD, and trials targeting at‐risk populations without a confirmed diagnosis at enrollment were excluded to maintain consistency across our stratification framework. Trials focused on preventing VCI and VaD progression in at‐risk populations therefore represent an important and understudied area that falls outside the boundaries of the current synthesis and warrants a dedicated future review.

Future research should prioritize expanding geographic diversity and promoting international collaboration to enhance generalizability across different populations. Trials that include mixed dementia cohorts should be required to report VCI‐ or VaD‐specific subgroup results to clarify disease‐specific treatment responses. Furthermore, future studies must utilize adequately powered, multi‐center designs to ensure that viable pharmacological candidates are not dismissed due to a failure to detect realistic therapeutic gains. Comparative effectiveness studies directly evaluating novel, repurposed, and established agents are also needed to identify the most promising pharmacological options. Moreover, although early‐stage intervention remains vital, greater attention should be given to moderate and severe VaD. Consistent application of validated diagnostic and cognitive measures will further strengthen reproducibility and cross‐study comparability. Future trials would also benefit from power calculations anchored to the MCID of their chosen primary outcome, alongside adequate sample sizes, to more reliably evaluate the therapeutic potential of candidate interventions.

In summary, the evolving clinical trial landscape for VCI and VaD reflects growing methodological sophistication, diversity of therapeutic agents, and research momentum. However, ongoing challenges—particularly regional imbalance, limited publication of completed results, and inconsistent disease classification—continue to restrict the strength of current evidence. Addressing these issues through global collaboration, transparent reporting, and methodological harmonization will be crucial to accelerating the development of effective pharmacological therapies for vascular cognitive disorders.
