Synchronizing food availability with the natural rhythm substantially improves reproduction and extends healthspan in laying hens
Authors: Guy Levkovich, Ran Shmulevitch, Dana Almagor, Liad Reshef, German Shiklov, Mark Rusal, Ilan Halachmi, Dror Sagi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDisrupted sleep due to nighttime eating can raise various concerns, impacting both physical health and overall well-being. Nevertheless, there is a lack of direct evidence linking nighttime eating with the female reproduction system, possibly due to the absence of suitable models. Here, we use the laying hen, a diurnal animal maintained under ad libitum feeding, as a vertebrate model to quantify the impact of nighttime eating on reproduction and aging. To do this, we have built an Arduino-based setup that regulates food availability and exclusively tracks the eating events of individuals. Our data indicate that synchronizing food availability with the natural sleep–wake rhythm substantially improves reproduction and extends healthspan in hens. With reproductive aging becoming progressively more prevalent in contemporary society due to the trend of delayed childbearing, synchronizing eating with the natural rhythm could help mitigate reproductive aging in vertebrates and potentially in humans as well.Subject terms: Ageing, Ageing, Disease model, Physiology, Systems biology, Evolutionary developmental biology

Introduction

Adequate quantity and quality of sleep is important for overall health and well-being and extends to both physical and mental aspects of health1. In humans, disrupted sleep due to nighttime eating can raise several concerns, both in terms of physical health and overall well-being. For example, nighttime eating impacts hormonal regulation, including insulin and cortisol levels. These hormonal imbalances could lead to metabolic dysfunction and weight-related issues, such as obesity or diabetes2. Nonetheless, a clear causal connection between nighttime eating and female reproduction has not been established, mainly due to the lack of suitable model animals.

Laying hens, much like humans, are diurnal animals with a 24-h rhythm3,4, and as a result, they naturally follow a pattern of being active (including feeding) during the daytime and sleeping during the nighttime. Hens are also maintained under standard ad libitum feeding conditions, allowing them free and easy access to food at all times. This scheme promotes nighttime eating on the expanse of maintaining their natural eating-and-fasting cycle5. Moreover, among land vertebrates, the laying hen stands out, as it can ovulate and produce an egg daily6. This reproduction rate provides a substantial dynamic range, allowing for reliable quantification of reproduction on a monthly basis7. Lastly, laying efficiency serves as a reliable, non-invasive assay to measure not only reproduction but also overall health. This is because the daily egg production, constituting 3% of the bird's body weight, presents a significant challenge to the animal’s health and metabolism8,9.

Here, we use the laying hen as a unique diurnal model to study the influence of nighttime eating, stemming from unrestricted food availability, on reproduction and overall health. To do that we have built a designated setup that can regulate food availability and exclusively track eating events in individuals.

Our data show that synchronizing food availability with the natural sleep–wake rhythm, substantially improves reproduction and extends healthspan. Further, our setup revealed that even though hens accumulate food in their crop before nighttime, the average hen exhibits nighttime eating events every hour of the night. Lastly, using the extreme reproductive efficiency of laying hens and by following individual reproduction well beyond the lifespan of commercial flocks, we observed a switch-like behavior in egg production; individual reproduction exhibited an abrupt decay from > 70% to < 15%, without any intermediate ovulation states. This observation implies that reproduction in hens is governed by a linear cascade of multiple components rather than multiple pathways, each contributing independently to egg production.

Taken together, our data provide direct evidence that synchronizing food intake with the natural sleep–wake rhythm mitigates reproductive aging in hens and thus may have potential as a useful strategy in other vertebrates as well10.

Results

Synchronized eating dramatically improves reproduction

Synchronized eating with the natural rhythm was achieved using programmable, Arduino-based, clock-regulated covers for the mangers (see “Methods”). These covers were programmed to be closed during nighttime (7 pm to 7 am). Control animals were fed ad libitum.

Figure 1 illustrates the effect of nighttime eating on reproduction (A), food consumption (B), body and egg weight (C, D), and mortality (E) relative to controls. Consistent with published data, the decline in reproduction in control hens in old age is expected8. However, the plateau in the reproduction per hen at about 80% due to synchronized eating even above 36 months is unprecedented. Synchronized eating also reduces mortality (Fig. 1E); at each time point, the synchronized eating cohort had a higher or equal number of live animals. However, daily food consumption and body and egg weight were not affected by nighttime eating.

Synchronized eating improves reproduction and health, in particular in old age. (A) Reproduction per hen as a function of age. Significance: p-value < 0.01 for 31 months and p < 0.001 for 32 months and older (t-test, calculated according to the number of eggs laid per hen in the corresponding cohort over a given month; mean, and STD). Error bars are represented by STD. (B–D) Food consumption, body weight, and egg weight are not significantly different between groups. Feeding was via common mangers, so data for food consumption was available for the whole flock but not for individual birds. Consumption per hen is calculated by dividing by the total number of hens (panel B), but error bars for each month are not available here. (E) Survival curves (Kaplan–Meier) of control vs. synchronized eating cohorts. The initial number of animals was 20 for each cohort. We emphasize that the animals were not subjected to any deliberate disturbances during the nighttime, including exposure to lighting, noise, or human intervention within the facilities.

Taken together, our data indicate that nighttime eating due to unlimited food availability accelerates reproductive aging, increases mortality, and worsens overall health in hens.

Ad libitum feeding results in consistent nighttime eating

To exclusively track eating events of individual control animals, we constructed a sensor-based setup positioned parallel to the mangers (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Figure 2 illustrates that nighttime eating has a very small contribution to the daily food intake: daytime eating (7–19) accounts for 85% of the total daily food intake (Fig. 2A). This data is consistent with synchronized and control groups having the same amount of daily food intake (Fig. 1B), body weight (Fig. 1C), and with hens being diurnal animals. However, Fig. 2A also shows that, despite being diurnal, the average hen is awakened and engages in several eating events per hour throughout the night under ad libitum conditions.

Patterns of eating events. (A) A typical representation of the daily eating events. Average number of eating events per hour of the day per individual hen. Data represent a weekly average of over 10 animals. Each bar represents a daily average per hen, based on 1 week and 10 animals. Error bars are SEM based on these 10 animals. (B) Nighttime eating events of the deep night hours, between 20-to-03, are presented as a histogram. 56 (animals × nights) were considered for the histogram spanning all animals, and consecutive and non-consecutive nights. The median number of events per night is 28.5. (C) Analysis of video recordings of control and synchronized eating cohorts reveals significant differences in the quality of sleep (p = 0.015, t-test). Nighttime recordings were randomly taken to capture typical differences between groups. Each recording session captures simultaneously the two groups to allow unbiased comparison. Each dot represents the average awakening time per hen per hour under different recording sessions. An animal was considered awakened upon gaining a full standing position for at least 5 s, as they sleep in a sitting pose.

Individual eating behavior during the deep nighttime, between 20 to 03, namely after the hens prepare for the night by filling their crop and before dawn when they become active, is shown in Fig. 2B as a histogram. The histogram illustrates quite a homogenous behavior that is heavily weighted towards low-eating events during this 8-h period. The median number of nighttime events is 28.5, or 3-to-4 events per hour, vs ~ 120 events (or 8 g) per hour during daytime. This ratio provides further support that nighttime eating reduces reproduction and health by disturbing the hormonal rhythm11,12 rather than excessive caloric intake. We note that in 10% of the cases, animals refrained from eating for the whole 8 h or exhibited only one nighttime eating event. This finding is consistent with hens having enough body mass to spontaneously avoid food intake during nighttime, even under ad libitum conditions.

Lastly, we evaluated differences in the quality of sleep between the synchronized cohort and controls. To do this, we used video recordings of nighttime behavior and found that, on average, control hens exhibit about a threefold increase in awakening time relative to synchronized eaters per nightly hour (1107.33 ± 261.89 vs. 352.67 ± 181.33, p = 0.015, t-test, Fig. 2C).

Taken together, our findings indicate that under ad libitum feeding the typical hen displayed eating events throughout each hour of the night. However, nighttime eating is minor and does not contribute to an individual’s total caloric intake. This suggests that the accelerated reproductive aging observed in control animals is not attributed to a higher caloric intake compared to synchronized animals.

Individual reproduction exhibits a unidirectional switch-like behavior between high-to-low states

The daily laying of an egg, weighing about 55 g, offers a simple and reliable assay to quantifiably monitor individual reproduction, as depicted in Fig. 3 for middle-aged and old animals. As young-to-middle-aged hens, the birds display a homogenous and high egg production, which results from the intensive selection for efficient egg laying. However, in old age, when the flock becomes commercially unsustainable, the flock becomes heterogeneous, where animals are either strong or weak layers without any intermediate states. For example, at 36 months of age, animals in both groups illustrate digital reproduction, as they are either highly productive or unproductive. Subsequently, the difference between the cohorts relies on the number of productive animals rather than integrating egg production for the entire population (Fig. 3A). A more robust illustration of the digital nature of reproduction is shown through the longitudinal tracking of individuals in Fig. 3A. Here, each row corresponds to an individual bird, and the data clearly demonstrate a unidirectional, switch-like transition from high to low reproduction, regardless of the cohort. We further quantified this switch in individuals and found a ratio of 22:1 between the duration of high egg production to the decay time (Fig. 3B). Since the transition to low laying efficiency is driven by aging, this switch is unidirectional—animals do not recover once they hit the low state.

Individual reproduction exhibits a unidirectional switch-like behavior from high to low reproduction. (A) Longitudinal tracking of individual reproduction. At a young-to-middle age (left) all animals are alive and demonstrate high and homogenous reproduction of > 80%. However, old animals exhibit substantial variability (right): animals are either productive or unproductive. There is no intermediate reproduction rate, as illustrated by the column's color map; black squares indicate dead animals. In addition, controls and synchronized-eating animals exhibit a digital-like reproductive behavior, which is characterized by a sharp transition from high production to very low production, as illustrated by the color mapping of the rows. (B) A histogram covering the animals in (A) depicts, for each bird, the ratio between its decay time to low egg production to its high reproduction period. Since low reproduction results from aging, it is irreversible, making the switch unidirectional. The median ratio is 1/22, corresponding to 22 months of high reproduction vs 1 month of decay towards low egg production.

In summary, laying hens display a digital, switch-like behavior characterized by a sharp decay from high to low reproduction, with the absence of intermediate rates in the range of 25-to-65%. Subsequently, animals are in either “on” or “off” states, and the persistence of reproduction in the synchronized eating group is due to an increased number of animals in the “on” state.

Discussion

In this study, we used the diurnality and the exceptional reproduction of laying hens to exclusively quantify the effect of nighttime eating on the reproductive span of individuals.

Our data show that even though eating during night hours is sporadic and does not contribute to overall daily caloric intake, it dramatically accelerates reproductive aging (Fig. 1A). The intensive breeding selection for high laying efficiency in commercial hens6,8 further highlights the strength of the data and the subsequent importance of synchronizing food consumption with the natural rhythm.

One possible explanation for such a substantial effect relies on the importance of proper regulation of natural circadian rhythms. For example, sleep disruption has been linked to impaired function of the female reproductive system and a range of negative health outcomes13. Indeed, studies in multiple species, including hens, have shown that continuous eating results in circadian misalignment, which in turn induces dysregulation of metabolic cycles and consequently worsens overall health5,14. Furthermore, in mammals, there are data on the benefits of alternative fasting associated with health benefits and extended longevity without a reduction in caloric intake11,12,15. For example, a recent study in humans was designed to assess the benefits of intermediate fasting while aligning food intake with the circadian rhythm and matching calorie intake and body weight with the control group11. This synchronized fasting scheme resulted in improved insulin sensitivity, β cell responsiveness, blood pressure, oxidative stress, and appetite in men with prediabetes. Furthermore, studies of shiftworkers suggest that consuming food during the night shift is associated with greater body fat and can impair postprandial glucose and lipid tolerance16. In rodents, the metabolic benefits of time-restricted feeding (TRF) appear quite profound, where TRF mice display reduced adiposity and liver steatosis, as well as improved glucose tolerance and reduced cholesterol levels. These metabolic benefits of synchronized chrononutrition protected against the harmful metabolic consequences of diverse nutritional challenges, including high-fat and high-sugar obesogenic diets17.

To the best of our knowledge, there are currently no direct experimental data on the effect of synchronized intermediate fasting in humans on reproductive aging, as demonstrated here for hens. However, given the established benefits of synchronizing food intake with circadian rhythms on metabolism in humans and rodents, our findings appear to extend the importance of chrononutrition to reproductive aging using hens as a vertebrate model.

One key extension to this study is testing whether individual’s eating pattern could predict its reproduction. The sample size in this study, along with the interaction between neighboring hens that resulted in the creation of collective “patches” of interacting animals engaged in nighttime eating, did not allow us to draw a significant conclusion on this matter. It has not escaped our notice that extending the persistency of lay, namely the reproductive span, in laying hens could have significant implications for our nutrition and the environment, as chicken eggs are the most affordable source of animal protein available8.

Next, we found that the animals display a switch-like behavior18 by either ovulating and producing at a high level, above 70%, or exhibiting a low level of reproduction, below 15% (Fig. 3A). This behavior indicates that reproduction in hens is not a result of parallel processes, each contributing its share to ovulation; multiple processes that contribute to egg production would convey a gradual age-dependent loss of reproduction, as malfunction in one pathway would not affect the contribution of the other pathways. Rather, egg production is driven by a linear cascade, where disruption of any of its components results in cessation of reproduction. Due to multiple of elements in the cascade, even a small aging-driven increase in the probability of malfunction per component would result in a significant increase in the overall probability of egg cessation, as the total probability of successful egg production drops by the power of n, the number of elements in the cascade19. Why did these birds develop such a fragile strategy for reproduction? We speculate that the progressive breeding selection for high laying efficiency favored the animals with the highest ovulation rate, subsequently narrowing down to a single linear cascade. In a more natural environment, the development of multiple processes that contribute to reproduction is favored, as it is more robust to failures in an individual element.

With the increasing age of first-time parents and the trend of postponing childbearing, reproductive aging becomes progressively more challenging in contemporary society20. The substantial extension in reproductive span achieved by synchronizing food intake with the natural rhythm, suggests that synchronized eating could potentially mitigate reproductive aging in humans as well.

Methods

Ethical approval

The experimental protocols were approved by the ethical committee of the Volcani Institute, approval number 942/22, under institutional approval VOL-IL-2203-106-2.

Subsequently, all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Animal husbandry

White Leghorn (Lohman) laying hens, all females, were purchased from commercial husbandries (Hasolelim, Israel) at the age of 1 day and raised in the poultry farm of the Volcani Center, Israel. Maintenance conditions and feeding formulas were according to the Lohman guidelines (https://hylinena.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Lohmann_LSL-Lite16-2.pdf), with free accesses to food and water.

Throughout the study hens were accommodated in Individual cages (40 × 40 × 43 cm), one hen per cage, to allow individual and longitudinal tracking of body weight, egg production, egg weight and egg quality. Food consumption was measured per manger of 10 animals monthly: at the first day of month N we weighted the amount of food left in the manger. This number was subtracted from the total amount (in grams) of supplied mixture during the month (N−1). We then divided by the number of hen-eating days in month (N−1) to have the amount of food intake per hen per day. For example, in a months of 30 days if all 10 animals fed from a corresponding manger were alive then the net consumed food per hen per day was the total net consumed mixture (in grams) divided by 30 × 10. If one animals died after 15 days the total net consumed mixture was divided by 30 × 9.5 because there were (30 × 9 + 15) hen-eating days.

Synchronized eating with the natural rhythm was achieved using programmable, Arduino-based, clock-regulated covers for the mangers. These covers were programmed to be closed during nighttime (7 pm to 7 am).

On the biological front, we followed a young flock until 12 months of age, verifying that all animals were at full health and peak reproduction. The animals were then randomly assigned to two cohorts, each consisting of 20 animals: a control cohort under ad libitum conditions and a cohort whose food availability is synchronized with the natural wake-sleep rhythm using the clock regulated setup. We emphasize that the animals were not subjected to any deliberate disturbances during the nighttime, including exposure to lighting, noise, or human intervention within the facilities.

Autonomic setup to regulate food availability

Autonomic covers that can regulate food availability.

To have control over feeding time, we have built a programmable clock-regulated covers for the mangers. Electrical motor coupled to Arduino-based electrical control unit opens and closes Polygal covers at the preprogrammed time window. The closing procedure includes a safety mechanism that prevents pinching of the animal's head in the closed state: any resistance during the closing course due to some hens having their heads still within the mangers, results in reopening the cover by some degrees and then proceeding with the closing procedure. This opening–closing cycles ensured that all hens were outside the mangers in the closed state.

Opening the covers is away from the cages, so no safety issues are associated with the opening procedure.

Processor unit

The task sensor management, data collection and storage had been handled be an Arduino Mega 2560 board. A Real-Time-Clock (RTC) DS3231 with a back-up power supply was used in order to provide accurate Time-Of-Day (TOD) information and to retain the timing in spite of any power shortages. The data was stored on a Secured Digital (SD) card media.

Algorithm

The algorithm monitored 10 sensors in sequence. For each of the sensors, once an event was detected, a debounce algorithm had been latched. The debounce latching was used in order to filter out stray LOS obstructions by insects etc. The debounce timer was set to 200 ms. Once an event elapsed the debounce timer filter, it had been accounted as a valid event. Upon the end of each event, the data was recorded on the SD. Prior to each write event the SD was opened for writing and once written the SD was closed. This action ensured that no data would be corrupted in the event of sudden power failure. The recorded data for each event included: sensor number, start time and duration of event. This sparing format allowed efficient data saving and posttest analysis.

The 200 ms debounce timer was in the found to be adequate in order not to miss any valid event, as test results reveled that all valid eating events were in excess of 1000 ms.
