Shape-Dependent Interactions of Gold Nanoparticles with Microalgae: Distinct Cellular and Molecular Responses
Authors: Can Wang, Bhaskar Sharma, Ruonan Peng, Xin Yong, Louis S. Santiago, Ke Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ecotoxicological effects of engineered nanoparticles in aquatic environments are influenced not only by composition and size but also by particle morphology, yet shape-dependent interactions with primary producers remain poorly understood. In this study, we evaluated the cellular and molecular responses of freshwater algae (Chlamydomonas reinhardtii) following exposure to 100 nm silica-coated gold nanospheres (AuNPs) and nanostars (AuNSs) across multiple concentrations. Exposure to 10 mg/L AuNSs for 72 h results in significantly stronger inhibition of algal growth and photosynthetic activity compared to the same concentration of AuNPs. Morphometric profiling reveals that AuNSs induce pronounced structural injury, including cell enlargement, debris production, and disruption of subcellular organization, more than AuNPs. Confocal imaging suggested that this heightened toxicity may stem from two distinct internalization patterns: AuNPs primarily adhered to chloroplast surfaces, whereas AuNSs caused more extensive membrane disruption and were observed deeper within intracellular compartments. RNA sequencing identified 9 upregulated and 38 downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the 10 mg/L AuNP-treated cells, impairing photosynthesis and energy storage via the photosystem II subunit S1 (PSBS1)/early light-inducible protein (ELI3) pathway. In contrast, the AuNS group exhibits 246 upregulated and 145 downregulated DEGs, affecting membrane integrity and nitrogen metabolism through the nitrate reductase (NIT1)/aminomethyl transferase (AMT1)/protein kinase domain-containing protein (A0A2K3CRU5) pathway. These results demonstrate that the nanoparticle morphology can drive divergent toxicity mechanisms in algal cells. Our findings highlight the necessity of incorporating NP morphology into environmental risk assessments and suggest that safer nanomaterial design should consider shape-dependent interactions with aquatic microorganisms.

Introduction

Nanoparticles (NPs) are tiny materials with a size range of 1 to 100 nm. Their high surface area and nanoscale dimensions confer unique physical and chemical properties, making them widely utilized in a variety of commercial and research applications, such as catalysis, medical applications, energy-based research, and environmental applications. The distinctive morphological characteristics of NPs have attained great interest since morphology influences most of the physicochemical properties. For instance, the morphology of NPs plays a critical role in modulating the rate and mechanism of cellular uptake and intracellular transport, and the shape of NPs also influences the drug delivery, cell–nanoparticle interaction, and the internalization rate, as well as the behavior or differentiation of stem cells. These properties prompted research on precisely controlling the shape of NPs to understand and customize these nanostructures for desired applications.

In the process of NP production, usage, transportation, and disposal, they inevitably enter the environment through migration and transformation. The aquatic environment, which receives runoff and wastewater from domestic, medical, and industrial sources, is the primary medium through which NPs enter and diffuse into other environmental resources. Current estimates indicate that 66,000 t of NPs are released into surface waters annually. Numerous studies have reported that the shape of NPs influences systemic toxicity, biodistribution, and biological activity, manifesting as shape-induced directed differentiation, cellular death via apoptosis, gene transfection and transfer, and metabolism alteration. These effects arise from differences in surface areas, uptake level, protein corona, physical disruption of the cell membrane, and particle wettability and surface curvature. Consequently, the wide usage and massive discharge of NPs with different shapes make it imperative to determine their toxic effects in aquatic environments. However, limited information remains about the ecological hazards posed by NPs of different shapes.

The World Health Organization (WHO) highlighted the concept of ecological public health, calling attention to the threats to human health caused by global ecological environment changes, such as climate change, biodiversity loss, alterations in hydrological systems, freshwater depletion, and contamination. Microalgae play a crucial role in mitigating these eco-environmental changes. They contribute significantly to regulating climate change by producing up to 60% of the world’s oxygen through photosynthesis and controlling atmospheric carbon dioxide levels. Additionally, as the foundation of the aquatic food chain, the abundance and quality of microalgae are essential for maintaining biodiversity. Moreover, the health of microalgae directly influences water quality and ecological resilience. Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, a model organism extensively studied due to its widespread distribution in soil and freshwater, was selected for this study to investigate the varying impacts of NPs with different shapes on the ecological environment.

In recent decades, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) of different shapes have been widely used for specific biomedical applications due to their distinct optical properties, including drug delivery, gene therapy, disease diagnosis, and bioimaging. Many studies have demonstrated that the toxicity of AuNPs toward microalgae is multifactorial, influenced by particle size, surface coating, concentration, exposure time, and morphology. For instance, exposure of C. reinhardtii to 42 nm AuNPs at 5 mg/L for 24 h results in slight growth inhibition, whereas a concentration of 20 mg/L induces oxidative stress in approximately 15% of cells. Raphidocelis subcapitata exposed to 30 nm AuNPs for 72 h exhibits an EC50 of approximately 350 mg/L. The EC50 value after 30 min of treatment was 114 mg/L polyamidoamine-coated AuNPs for C. reinhardtii. Despite these insights, studies addressing the role of the nanoparticle shape in algal toxicity remain limited. Silica is frequently employed as a stabilizing surface coating for AuNPs, as the silica shell can stabilize the core of AuNPs , by preventing nonspecific interactions with biomolecules. Both silica and gold are considered biocompatible, inert, and nontoxic materials, thereby minimizing intrinsic material toxicity to cells such as microalgae and making silica-coated AuNPs (Si-AuNPs) ideal for studying shape-dependent algal toxicity.

In this study, 100 nm star (AuNS) and spherical (AuNP) Si-AuNPs were used as the representative shapes of NPs and exposed C. reinhardtii at varying concentrations to investigate their differential effects on algal growth and primary photosynthetic processes. Confocal and transmission electron microscopy were employed to detect the interaction and accumulation of different shapes of AuNPs and C. reinhardtii. Furthermore, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) quantifies genome-wide gene expression, which is essential for interpreting the functional elements of the genome and understanding disease development. Therefore, RNA-seq was employed to elucidate the unique toxicity mechanisms of these differently shaped AuNPs in microalgae.

Materials and Methods

Nanoparticle Characterization

AuNPs and AuNSs (100 nm in size) were purchased from CD Bioparticles (NY, USA). AuNPs were monodispersed in DI water to form a suspension with a concentration of 1, 10, and 50 mg/L for exposure experiments. The detailed production information and number of particles in each group are provided in Supporting Information Table S1. The AuNP solution was ultrasonicated for at least 10 min before use. All AuNP suspension samples were stored at 4 °C. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to observe the size and shape of the AuNPs. Zeta potentials of AuNPs were measured by phase analysis light scattering (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS), using disposable cuvettes. Zeta potentials were averaged over 3 runs; for each calibration step, at least 300 particles were measured with typical particle concentrations of 1012–1013 particles/L. The surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peaks were measured using a spectrophotometer (BioTek, CA, USA). AuNPs were also characterized by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and zeta potential measurements using a Zetasizer after incubation in a Tris-Acetate-Phosphate (TAP) medium for 0 and 72 h.

Algal Cultivation and AuNP Exposure

Wild-type C. reinhardtii (CC-125) was obtained from the Algae Research Supply Company (Carlsbad, CA, USA). The strain was grown in the TAP medium. C. reinhardtii was cultivated at 25 ± 1 °C, 4800 l× light intensity, and a 12–12 h light–dark ratio. When algal culture was in the exponential growth phase, an aliquot having a cell density of 1 × 109 cells/L was used for treatments. Cell density was correlated with optical density at 680 nm (OD680). All flasks were shaken three times each day, and their locations were randomly shifted. The algae were exposed to varying concentrations of AuNPs (1, 10, and 50 mg/L) or deionized water (vehicle control). Given that exposure to 42 nm AuNPs for 24 h induced oxidative stress of C. reinhardtii, while exposure to 30 nm AuNPs for 72 h produced an EC50 of approximately 350 mg/L of C. reinhardtii, we established 1 mg/L (≈1/100 EC50) as a low dosage, 10 mg/L (≈1/50 EC50) as a medium dose, and 50 mg/L (≈1/10 EC50) as a high dose. These concentrations were selected to generate an appropriate dose–response relationship and to capture a comprehensive range of toxicological effects across hierarchical exposure levels.

Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry

ICP-OES was used to quantify the cellular burden of gold in different AuNP exposure concentrations (Supporting Information Method S1).

Cell Viability

Algal cell density was counted using a blood counting chamber under microscopy. The cell growth rate was measured using a spectrophotometer (BioTek Cytation 5 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader, Agilent, CA, USA) at a wavelength of 680 nm at 0 h and every 24 h after culturing in the TPA medium and represented by OD680 values. OD680 primarily reflects chlorophyll content; it was employed as a relative parameter to calculate growth rates by monitoring changes in optical density during the experimental time frame within the same treatment group. The growth rate was calculated using the following equation Growthrate(%)=(OD24h×n−OD0h)/OD0h×100%

Determination of Photosynthetic Pigment Concentration

After centrifuging the C. reinhardtii suspension, the algae pellet was resuspended in the same volume of 80% acetone. Then, the tube was heated in a water bath at 55 °C in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance values of the supernatant were measured at 470, 645, and 663 nm. The formula for calculating photosynthetic pigment content is as follows Chlorophylla(Ca)=12.21A663−2.81A645 Chlorophyllb(Cb)=20.13A645−5.03A663 Carotenoids(Cc)=(1000A470−3.27×Ca−104×Cb)/229

Measurement of the Electron Transport Rate

The ETR indicates the photosynthetic capacity of algae and is a crucial metric for assessing their physiological health and productivity. It is adapted from the method developed in a previous study, with detailed procedures provided in Supporting Information Method S2.

Flow Cytometry

Flow cytometry was used to observe the size, granularity, and debris production of algae at different AuNP exposure concentrations (Supporting Information Method S3).

Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy

To observe the interaction of microalgae and AuNPs, we employed CLSM to examine the accumulation of AuNPs in C. reinhardtii after 72 h of exposure (Supporting Information Method S4).

Transmission Electron Microscopy

TEM was used to observe the size and shape of the AuNPs and the morphology and subcellular structure of C. reinhardtii after exposure at 72 h (Supporting Information Method S5).

mRNA Sequencing

We collected algae after 72 h of exposure to 10 mg/L AuNPs and AuNSs for RNA-seq to analyze mRNA expression (see Supporting Information Method S6). In brief, following total RNA isolation, poly­(A) RNA was purified, fragmented into small pieces, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and then amplified using PCR. Peak calling and differential peak analysis were performed using the R package DESeq2, and peaks were annotated by intersection with gene architecture using the R package ANNOVAR.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical differences were studied using a Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons or one-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons with a Student–Newman–Keuls test. Data are presented as mean ± SD, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Properties of AuNPs and AuNSs

In this study, two representative morphologies of gold nanoparticles were utilized: 100 nm-diameter silica-coated gold nanospheres (AuNPs) and 100 nm-diameter silica-coated gold nanostars (AuNSs). For fluorescence observation, fluorophores (Ex/Em: 488/514 nm) were conjugated to bare AuNP and AuNS with a core diameter of 80 nm. To enhance particle stability, a silica coating was applied, rendering a final particle size of 100 nm (Figure A). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM)-measured morphology of the particles was consistent with the supplier’s specifications. TEM imaging confirmed successful functionalization and high homogeneity of the AuNPs (Figure B). Particle size distribution obtained from the TEM image analysis was fitted to the log–normal distribution. The peak values of the fit were taken as the average particle size of the corresponding samples. Two kinds of NPs, as a result, had a similar size, in the range of 100 ± 14 nm (Figure C). Notably, AuNSs possess protruding branches that extend beyond 100 nm along their longest axis, resulting in a greater tip-to-tip dimension compared to spherical particles. This anisotropic geometry represents the primary structural distinction between AuNSs and AuNPs and constitutes the key morphological parameter investigated in this study. The AuNPs and AuNSs displayed shape-dependent surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peaks at approximately 580 and 800 nm, respectively (Figure D). Both particle types exhibited negative zeta potentials, with AuNPs exhibiting −23.2 mV and AuNSs exhibiting −16.8 mV.

(C) Particle size distribution of AuNPs obtained from the TEM image analysis. The data were fitted to the log-normal distribution. The peak values of the fit were taken as the average particle size of the corresponding samples. (D) UV–vis-absorption spectra of AuNPs and AuNSs demonstrate absorbance peaks. We further performed Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and zeta potential measurements for both AuNPs and AuNSs in the TAP medium at 0 and 72 h to characterize the properties during coculture with algae (Table S2). At 0 h in the TAP medium, the hydrodynamic diameters were 137.97 ± 2.99 nm for AuNPs and 148.07 ± 4.85 nm for AuNSs, indicating slight increases compared to those in DI water (∼100 nm), likely due to ionic screening. The corresponding zeta potentials were −20.8 ± 1.15 mV (AuNP) and −18.2 ± 1.10 mV (AuNS), indicating moderate colloidal stability at the start of exposure. After 72 h, aggregation was observed for both particle types. AuNPs exhibited a marked increase in the hydrodynamic size to 461.93 ± 40.37 nm, whereas that of AuNSs increased to 212.87 ± 2.74 nm. Concurrently, the zeta potential decreased to −4.16 ± 0.50 mV (AuNPs) and −3.16 ± 0.61 mV (AuNSs), consistent with ionic compression of the electrical double layer in the TAP medium.

ICP analysis was performed to quantify the cellular burden of gold, and the results are presented in Table S3. At 1, 10, and 50 mg/L exposure levels, the intracellular gold concentrations (ppm) were 0.111 ± 0.008, 0.623 ± 0.0016, and 0.995 ± 0.0018 for AuNPs and 0.035 ± 0.0072, 0.547 ± 0.0029, and 0.741 ± 0.0023 for AuNSs, respectively. The results showed that intracellular gold levels increased with increasing exposure concentration for both particle types. Notably, at comparable exposure levels, the intracellular gold content of AuNP-exposed algae was generally higher than that of AuNS-exposed algae.

Various Degrees of Toxicity of AuNPs and AuNSs to C. reinhardtii

To evaluate the impact of different shapes of Si-AuNPs on the growth of C. reinhardtii and to determine whether there are differences in toxicity between various concentrations of AuNPs and AuNSs, we measured the growth rate by recording optical density (OD) values every 24 h for 5 days. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in the growth rate compared to the control group following exposure to different shape and concentration of AuNPs for 48–96 h, with more severe inhibition of C. reinhardtii growth observed at higher concentrations of AuNPs. Notably, at the same concentration, AuNSs caused greater growth inhibition than AuNPs at each time point. Statistically significant differences were observed between the 10 mg/L AuNP and AuNS groups at 72 and 96 h. (Figure A).

(D) Maximum quantum yield of photosystem II at 72 h. Data are presented as mean ± SD, n = 3. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.005, and ****p < 0.001 compared with the control group or between the two groups with lines.

To further verify the impact of AuNPs with different shapes on C. reinhardtii, we measured the concentrations of three main photosynthetic pigments, namely, chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids. Due to the significant differences in growth inhibition observed between the 10 mg/L AuNP and AuNS groups at 72 h, we selected 72 h as the effective time point for measuring photosynthetic pigment concentrations. As shown in Figure B, chlorophyll a decreased significantly across all exposure groups compared to the control group, except for the 1 mg/L AuNP group. Chlorophyll b showed significant reductions across all concentrations, while carotenoids displayed a significant decline in the 10 and 50 mg/L groups for both AuNPs and AuNSs. These results indicate that higher concentrations of AuNPs lead to more pronounced inhibitory effects on the photosynthetic pigments of C. reinhardtii. Similar to the growth rate findings, exposure to AuNSs results in a more pronounced inhibitory effect on pigments compared to AuNPs, with statistically significant differences observed in the chlorophyll a concentration in both the 10 and 50 mg/L dosage groups.

We also assessed the ETR and Y­(II) across all AuNP concentration groups at 72 h. Both parameters were reduced compared to the control group (Figure C,D), indicating the inhibitory effect of AuNPs on algal photosynthetic efficiency. Statistically significant differences in ETR were observed between the 10 and 50 mg/L AuNP and AuNS groups, while Y­(II) showed differences between the 10 mg/L groups. Therefore, exposure to 10 mg/L AuNPs for 72 h appears to be an effective concentration and a sensitive time point for assessing the toxicity.

Effects of AuNP and AuNS Exposure on C. reinhardtii: Size, Granularity, and Debris Production

Figure A shows the FSC and SSC signals of algae cells following exposure to AuNPs with different shapes. In the control group, microalgal cells appear as homogeneous and elliptical populations centered in the plot (56.24% in the P3 region), indicating no cell aggregation, morphological changes, or damage. Debris is observed in the bottom-left corner. Upon exposure to AuNPs, the proportion of events in the P3 region decreased to 51.25, 50.37, and 46.39% for the 1, 10, and 50 mg/L AuNP groups and to 47.91, 37.93, and 32.51% for the 1, 10, and 50 mg/L AuNS groups, respectively. Significant differences were observed in the 50 mg/L AuNP group and the 10 and 50 mg/L AuNS groups compared with the control (Figure B). Interestingly, significant changes in the shape and position of the main populations (P3) were noted in the 10 and 50 mg/L AuNS groups, which we designated as the P2 regions. Events in P2 accounted for 48.19 and 40.49% of the total number of events, respectively. Additionally, after exposure to AuNPs, there was an emergence of events on the left side (P4), interpreted as debris resulting from cell damage and death, with numbers increasing with AuNP concentration. Similar to P3, there were significant differences in the 50 mg/L AuNP group and the 10 and 50 mg/L AuNS groups compared with the control. Furthermore, the percentage of debris was significantly different between the 50 mg/L AuNP and AuNS groups (Figure C). To rule out the influence of nanoparticles themselves on these results, we conducted FC experiments with different concentrations of AuNP solutions only (Figure S1), which confirmed that debris in P4 was not affected by AuNP concentration. Thus, exposure to AuNPs results in increased cell size, enhanced internal complexity, and greater amounts of cellular debris following cell damage or death. Notably, differences in the proportion, shape, and localization of P3/P2 and P4 events between AuNP and AuNS groups indicate varying impacts of differently shaped AuNPs on microalgae, with those exposed to AuNSs displaying larger cell bodies, more complex internal granularity, and increased debris.

Given that the FC results indicated cell enlargement and increased debris upon exposure to AuNPs, we conducted further observations of cell morphology and quantified the major axis and cell area by using TEM. We selected 10 and 50 mg/L AuNP exposure groups at 72 h, as the 1 mg/L groups showed negligible effects on the microalgae. As depicted in Figure D–F, both the major axis and the area of the cells in the AuNP exposure groups were significantly larger than those in the control group, with an increase observed as the concentration rose. Notably, at equivalent exposure concentrations, cells in the AuNS group were larger than those in the AuNP group. This difference was particularly significant for the cell area in the 50 mg/L group. To further investigate the increased granularity observed, we examined the subcellular structure of C. reinhardtii to confirm internal structural changes.

Different Accumulation and Disruption of Algal Subcellular Structures by AuNPs

First, we employed confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) to examine the accumulation of AuNPs in C. reinhardtii after 72 h of exposure (Figure A). AuNPs were labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 (AF488) dye to determine their localization and accumulation within the algal cells. Chlorophyll autofluorescence (magenta) indicated the position of chloroplasts, while AuNPs were visualized via green fluorescence from the AF488 label. Manders’ colocalization coefficient analysis was performed on images displaying fluorescent signals from AF488-labeled AuNPs and chloroplast autofluorescence.

Both types of nanoparticles, spherical (AuNPs) and star-shaped (AuNSs), were internalized by the algal cells, with their localization confirmed in the chloroplasts. Overlay images of AF488 (green) and chloroplasts (magenta) revealed white regions in the merged channels, indicating colocalization at both 10 mg/L and 50 mg/L concentrations for AuNPs and AuNSs. Orthogonal projections of confocal images (Figure B) showed that a portion of the AuNPs uniformly adhered to the surface of the chloroplast membrane, forming a thin green fluorescent ring surrounding the magenta chloroplast autofluorescence.

Manders’ colocalization coefficients between AuNPs (AF488) and chloroplasts (Figure C) were approximately 0.5 at 10 mg/L and 0.76 at 50 mg/L, both significantly higher than the negative control (NC) group. In contrast, the green fluorescence of AuNSs was not evenly distributed within the chloroplasts. Instead, AuNSs tended to aggregate on the chloroplast surface, forming bright fluorescent spots, or they disrupted the chloroplast membrane and accumulated in other intracellular compartments (Figure A), a pattern also evident in the orthogonal projections (Figure B). Notably, statistical analysis (Figure C) showed that in the 10 mg/L AuNS group, the colocalization coefficient was ∼0.73, similar to that of the 50 mg/L AuNP group. However, in the 50 mg/L AuNS group, the colocalization coefficient decreased to ∼0.6. This reduction may be attributed to the sharp angular features of AuNSs, which likely facilitate membrane disruption, leading to partial escape from the chloroplasts and subsequent accumulation in other intracellular regions.

Then, we used TEM to scan the subcellular structures of C. reinhardtii. As Figure D shows, in the control group, the morphology of C. reinhardtii cells was intact, with the cell membrane closely adhering to the cell wall and no signs of plasmolysis. The internal structures, including the chloroplasts, pyrenoids, mitochondria, and starch granules, were intact, and the vacuoles were clear and free of impurities. Upon exposure to 10 mg/L AuNPs, the cell structure remained largely intact, but signs of plasmolysis were evident. AuNPs were observed within the cytoplasm and vacuoles. In the 10 mg/L AuNS group, the cell structure showed mild disruption, with the absence of pyrenoids, fewer starch granules in the chloroplasts, and a thickened cell wall. AuNSs were observed in the wall membrane space, cytoplasm, vacuoles, and other organelles. Further, cells exposed to 50 mg/L of AuNPs resulted in severe structural damage. Significant plasmolysis was observed, accompanied by the absence of pyrenoids and a decrease in starch granules. Numerous AuNPs were found within the cells. In the 50 mg/L AuNS-exposure group, cellular aberrations were evident. The normal cell structure was almost entirely disrupted, with severe plasmolysis, no pyrenoids or starch granules, a thick and blurred cell wall, and substantial accumulation of AuNSs throughout the cells.

Effect of AuNPs and AuNSs on Algal Gene Expression Profiles and Function Enrichment

Given the distinct types of damage observed in C. reinhardtii after exposure to AuNPs and AuNSs, we hypothesize that the varying shapes of AuNPs lead to differential gene expression and distinct injury mechanisms. Considering the severe cellular damage noted in the groups exposed to 50 mg/L AuNPs and the significant toxic effects observed in the 10 mg/L exposure groups between AuNPs and AuNSs, we conducted RNA-seq to analyze gene expression in microalgae exposed to 10 mg/L AuNPs and AuNSs for 72 h. The raw data required to reproduce the above findings are available to download (from reserved DOI: 10.17632/x3gyskppmp.1). Quality control (QC) for raw data is presented in Table S4. A total of 15,087 genes were identified when comparing the NC vs AuNP groups, with 9 genes upregulated and 38 downregulated. In contrast, the NC vs AuNS comparison revealed 246 upregulated and 145 downregulated genes among the 15,175 detected genes (Figure A). There were 34 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) codifferentially expressed in both the AuNP and AuNS exposure groups (Figure B). The top 18 major Gene Ontology (GO) terms (p < 0.05) are presented, including ten terms related to biological processes and eight terms related to cellular components identified under 10 mg/L AuNP and AuNS exposure groups.

In the NC vs AuNP group, DEGs were associated with various aspects of photosynthesis, gene expression regulation, osmotic stress processes, and material transport. The most enriched term with the highest enrichment factor among biological processes was nonphotochemical quenching (NPQ), which contributes to photosynthesis. Seven of the eight cellular component terms were involved in photosynthesis, including the chloroplast thylakoid membrane, plastid thylakoid membrane, chloroplast thylakoid, plastid thylakoid, thylakoid membrane, photosynthetic membrane, and plasma membrane. Biological processes, including the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade, receptor guanylyl cyclase signaling pathway, enzyme-linked receptor protein signaling pathway, guanosine 3′,5′-cyclic monophosphate (cGMP) metabolic process, and cell surface receptor signaling pathway, were implicated in the regulation of osmotic stress. Gene expression regulation processes, such as the cyclic nucleotide biosynthetic process, purine ribonucleotide biosynthetic process, and ribonucleotide biosynthetic process, along with the cellular component transcription factor II D (TFIID) complex, were identified. Nitrate transmembrane transport was associated with material transport (Figure C).

In the NC vs AuNS group, DEGs participated in different functions. In addition to osmotic stress processes (cellular components: cAMP-dependent protein kinase complex, serine/threonine protein kinase complex, and protein kinase complex), all biological process terms related to material and transmembrane transport were identified. This included purine nucleobase transport, acetate transmembrane transport, ammonium homeostasis, nitrate transmembrane transport, potassium ion transmembrane transport, inorganic anion transmembrane transport, carboxylic acid transport, potassium ion transport, inorganic anion transport, and organic anion transport. Five cellular component terms related to cytokinesis were also identified, including the actin filament, plasma membrane, cell periphery, membrane, and cytoplasm (Figure D).

Differential Toxic Mechanisms of AuNP and AuNS Effects on C. reinhardtii

Figure A illustrates the expression profiles of the 34 codifferentially expressed genes (co-DEGs) across the control, AuNP, and AuNS groups. The patterns of upregulation or downregulation were consistent within the two groups; however, the fold changes were more pronounced in the AuNS group compared with the AuNP group. This observation aligns with the results on cell growth inhibition, morphological alterations, and subcellular structural damage, indicating that the cytotoxicity induced by AuNSs was consistently more severe than that caused by AuNPs. Despite this, STRING analysis of these 34 genes revealed no functional connections (Figure S2), suggesting that the co-DEGs might not contribute significantly to the primary mechanism of toxicity.

Subsequently, we conducted functional connectivity analysis using STRING for genes individually altered in either the AuNP or the AuNS groups. In the AuNP group, STRING analysis of 13 individually altered genes, along with four introduced interactors, revealed interactions among three genes forming a cluster centered around the photosystem II subunit S1 (PSBS1), with early light-inducible protein (ELI3) and A8JHP3 also showing increased expression (Figure B,C). In the AuNS group, STRING analysis of 357 individually altered genes, excluding noninteracting ones, identified four prominent clusters (Figure D). Key genes within these clusters, identified based on their interaction strength and functional relevance, included nitrate reductase (NIT1), aminomethyl transferase (AMT1), uricase (A0A2K3D311), urea amidolyase (DUR2), RING-type domain-containing protein (A0A2K3DCN5), cyclin-like domain-containing protein (A0A2K3D7Z0), protein kinase domain-containing protein (A0A2K3CRU5), and phosphate transporter (PTB12).

Discussion

The inhibition of algal growth can be primarily attributed to two factors: (1) NPs disrupt the photosynthetic process in microalgae by blocking or scattering light depending on their sizes, thereby reducing the efficiency of photosynthesis and impacting the microalgae’s energy production capabilities; (2) chemical damage: NPs damage cell membranes by inducing excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which in turn impairs chlorophyll synthesis, thereby diminishing the microalgae’s capacity to absorb and convert light energy. The initial impact of these damages is the disruption of photosynthesis, given that photosynthetic pigments are crucial for capturing sunlight energy for photosynthesis in algae. Accordingly, our results indicate that the growth rate of C. reinhardtii decreased upon exposure to both AuNPs and AuNSs across all concentrations, with photosynthetic pigment inhibition. Monitoring photosynthesis is a useful approach to gain general information on plant performance in growth and reproduction. Both ETR and Y­(II) decreased compared to the control group, signifying an inhibitory effect of AuNPs on algal photosynthetic efficiency. The reduced values of ETR and Y­(II) indicate plant stress as they are indicators of photosynthetic activity. Notably, all these results show statistically significant differences between the 10 mg/L AuNP and AuNS groups, with AuNSs exhibiting a more severe inhibitory effect. It can be inferred that star-shaped AuNPs were more damaging to microalgae than their spherical counterparts.

Our flow cytometry results show that postexposure to AuNPs, C. reinhardtii cells exhibit increased size, enhanced internal complexity, and a greater quantity of cellular debris, indicative of cellular damage or death. Notably, the proportion, shape, and localization of the P4 area in Figure A varied between AuNP and AuNS groups, suggesting that the shape of AuNPs distinctly impacts microalgae. Specifically, cells exposed to AuNSs display larger cell bodies, more complex internal granularity, and increased debris compared to those exposed to AuNPs. TEM images at low magnification further confirmed that exposure to AuNSs caused larger cell volumes than exposure to AuNPs. It is well established that NPs can inhibit photosynthesis in microalgae by blocking or scattering light, leading to reduced photosynthetic efficiency, cellular swelling, and increased debris production. Thompson et al. found that C. reinhardtii strains dramatically swell and ultimately disintegrate when grown in the dark, which can result in the leakage of cellular contents due to the destruction of the cell wall and membrane from swelling. This phenomenon may explain the observed cell enlargement, increased internal complexity, and the elevated debris levels.

NPs can physically interact with microalgal cells and be taken up from the surrounding medium. This process may block or damage cell membranes, thereby compromising cellular integrity and function. Plasmolysis observed in C. reinhardtii post-AuNP exposure might be attributed to this mechanism. AuNSs may more easily traverse cellular barriers, potentially exacerbating damage due to their structural characteristics. Once internalized, NPs can accumulate within various organelles and subcellular structures, disrupting normal cellular functions. Most cells exposed to AuNPs exhibited reduced internal structures, with significant membrane deformations and the absence of clearly identifiable vacuoles compared to control groups. Specifically, cellular structures in the AuNS groups were notably more disorganized than those in the AuNP groups at the equivalent exposure concentrations. Moreover, the severity of the internal structural damage was most pronounced in the 50 mg/L groups. The occurrence of chloroplasts containing starch granules and the central pyrenoid reveals high photosynthetic activity and energy storage capacity. Notably, AuNSs (10 mg/L) and AuNPs (50 mg/L) resulted in fewer starch granules and a reduced occurrence of pyrenoids, indicating that a lower AuNS concentration might affect photosynthetic and metabolic activity in C. reinhardtii. Cell-wall thickening is one of the primary indicators of nitrogen deficiency. Under nitrogen-limited conditions, cells redirect metabolic flux from protein synthesis and cell division to the accumulation of carbon-rich storage compounds. This metabolic reprogramming is often accompanied by reinforcement of the cell wall, increased wall thickness, and enhanced mechanical strength as adaptive strategies to withstand stress. Nitrogen deprivation can result from nutrient deprivation or changes in light intensity. This may explain the observed cell-wall thickening in the AuNS group. In addition, nitrogen limitation affects the growth of microalgae, which also accounts for the lower growth rate of cells in the AuNS group compared to the AuNP group. Through cytotoxicity assays and observations of cell morphology and subcellular structures, we conclude that AuNSs exhibit greater toxicity toward C. reinhardtii compared to AuNPs.

The 10 mg/L concentration allowed us to detect clear differences in internalization behavior and subcellular injury between AuNPs and AuNSs. Therefore, we selected this concentration for further mechanism investigation. Based on RNA-seq analysis, DEGs in the NC vs AuNP group and the NC vs AuNS group were enriched in distinct biological processes and cellular components. It has been shown that AuNPs in leaves can lead to fluorescence quenching, as the excited electron of chlorophyll molecules can be transferred to AuNPs, inducing the increase of NPQ in plants. Saison et al. found that copper oxide NPs covered with polystyrene had a deteriorative effect on chlorophyll by inducing the photoinhibition of photosystem II, and the inhibition of photosynthetic electron transport induced a strong dissipation of energy by NPQ processes. Furthermore, the reorganization of photosynthetic machinery, especially in the range of alternative energy-dissipation pathways, has been reported as a response to osmotic stress. Our GO enrichment analysis indicates that AuNPs primarily affected photosynthesis and osmotic stress in C. reinhardtii, resulting in inhibited cell growth, cell death, and damage to subcellular structures. In contrast, AuNSs primarily affected material transport and cytokinesis as their main toxic mechanisms. Substance transport and transmembrane transport are involved in all biological processes, primarily concerning nitrogen and related metabolites, such as purine and ammonium, which are the main reasons for growth inhibition of microalgae. The actin filaments, plasma membrane, cell periphery, membrane, and cytoplasm play crucial roles in cytokinesis. Therefore, despite AuNSs appearing more severe in their effects on microalgae compared with AuNPs, the underlying mechanisms differ significantly.

According to our co-DEGs and individually DEGs results, the toxic effects of AuNPs on C. reinhardtii appear to stem from alterations in specific genes rather than co-DEGs. In the AuNP group, the cluster’s main biological process, molecular function, and cellular component were nonphotochemical quenching, chlorophyll binding, and photosystem II, respectively. These findings align closely with the GO analysis results for the AuNP group (Figure C), suggesting that the toxic effects of AuNPs on C. reinhardtii may primarily result from alterations in these individually affected genes rather than the co-DEGs. PSBS1 plays a pivotal role in NPQ, where its expression increases during NPQ to modulate chlorophyll fluorescence quenching in photosystem-depleted membranes. ELI3, located in thylakoid membranes, is known to protect photosynthetic machinery from various environmental stresses in plants, with its expression heightened under photo-oxidative stress conditions. A8JHP3 is the major protein complex of the chloroplast photosynthetic apparatus, and the increased expression also contributes to abiotic stress tolerance. The elevated expression of these three genes in the AuNP exposure group suggests that the PSBS1/ELI3 pathway is pivotal in impairing the photosynthetic capacity of C. reinhardtii, thus serving as the primary toxic mechanism induced by AuNP exposure.

In the AuNS group, the largest cluster was associated with nitrate and ammonium transport, encompassing processes such as ammonium homeostasis, nitrate assimilation, and ammonium transmembrane transporter activity. The other three clusters pertained to protein kinase activity, cytokinesis (involving plasma membrane and cell periphery), and inorganic molecular entity transmembrane transporter activity (including cellular responses to inorganic substances). These functions of the individually altered genes in the AuNS group are consistent with the GO analysis results (Figure C). NIT1 functions as a crucial enzyme involved in the initial step of nitrate assimilation in plants. Nitrate reductases, classified as molybdoenzymes, catalyze the reduction of nitrate to nitrite, a pivotal reaction in protein production in most crop plants, where nitrate serves as the primary nitrogen source in fertilized soils. AMT plays a central role in the uptake of reduced nitrogen for biosynthetic and energy metabolism. A0A2K3D311 is essential in ureide formation, while DUR2 catalyzes urea conversion to ammonium, the key step in utilizing urea as a nitrogen source. The elevated expression of these four genes represents increased nitrogen metabolism and transport.

Although previous studies have suggested that AuNPs could contribute to the enhanced nitrogen metabolism in plants, our experimental results indicate that the upregulation of nitrogen metabolism-related genes observed here is likely compensatory. This compensatory regulation may result from significant energy loss caused by photosynthetic inhibition, prompting microalgae to rely more on nitrogen metabolism to bolster nutrition. Functional studies of A0A2K3DCN5 and A0A2K3D7Z0 are currently limited; however, UniProt annotations indicate that the gene clusters in which they serve as key components are associated with cytokinesis. Thus, their reduced expression may reflect impaired cytokinesis and, consequently, decreased cell proliferation. A0A2K3CRU5 is a structurally conserved protein domain containing the catalytic function of protein kinases. These enzymes can move a phosphate group onto proteins, inducing phosphorylation. Protein phosphorylation is a reversible modification that plays a crucial role in plant signaling transduction and environmental adaptation. The exposure to foreign substances, such as AuNSs, may have stimulated a cellular response leading to the upregulation of genes within this cluster. PTB12 plays a fundamental housekeeping role in phosphate transport, critical for plant phosphate homeostasis mediated by membrane-localized transporters. Dysregulated expression of genes in this cluster suggests membrane transport disturbances possibly induced by AuNSs that compromise membrane integrity. The aberrant expression patterns of these genes in the AuNS exposure group imply multiple pathways of damage, particularly affecting nutrient metabolism, transmembrane transport, and cell proliferation. The NIT1/AMT1/A0A2K3CRU5 pathway emerges as a potential primary toxic mechanism induced by the AuNS exposure.

Our findings indicate unique mechanisms by which different shapes of AuNPs interact with C. reinhardtii. Specifically, AuNPs predominantly impact photosynthesis and induce cellular damage, whereas AuNSs primarily disrupt the cell membrane structure, impairing transmembrane transport and substance absorption. These effects can be attributed to their respective shapes: AuNPs, being spherical with smooth surfaces, are prone to adhering to the surfaces of microalgae cells and chloroplast membranes. The adherence of AuNPs of this size likely causes light scattering, attenuating photosynthesis, leading to NPQ, cell swelling, nutrient deprivation, and eventually cell death. In contrast, AuNSs, characterized by their star-shaped and irregular protrusions, are more likely to disrupt cell membrane structures. This membrane disruption results in extensive membrane damage, hindering the transmembrane transport of materials, causing cell rupture, disintegration of internal structures, and cell death. The observed differences in toxicity are influenced not only by particle shape but also by specific surface area (SSA), which is inherently shape-dependent. The increased surface area of AuNSs may enhance cellular contact, membrane interaction, and overall biological reactivity, and cause more disruption compared with AuNPs. However, the precise interactions between different shapes of AuNPs and cells require further investigation. Future studies could benefit from employing artificial intelligence to simulate the motion and interaction pathways of these nanoparticles, offering deeper insights into their cellular dynamics. The exposure concentrations used in this study were selected to induce a quantifiable but nonlethal level of stress, enabling the identification of shape-specific internalization patterns and subcellular structural alterations. These concentrations were employed to investigate hazard mechanisms rather than to directly assess the environmental risk. Extrapolation to real-world environmental scenarios requires the consideration of environmentally relevant concentrations, chronic exposure conditions, and environmental transformation processes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that AuNSs exert more severe effects on C. reinhardtii cells compared to AuNPs, including growth inhibition, photosynthesis suppression, cell enlargement, debris production, and subcellular structure disruption. This heightened toxicity can be attributed to distinct mechanistic interactions driven by the nanosphere and nanostar. AuNPs tend to adhere to the membrane surfaces of C. reinhardtii through occlusion, leading to diminished cellular photosynthesis and reduced energy storage via the PSBS1/ELI3 pathway, which results in moderate cell damage and death. In contrast, AuNSs compromise membrane structure and disrupt cellular metabolism through the NIT1/AMT1/A0A2K3CRU5 pathway, severely affecting the physiological functions of C. reinhardtii and causing extensive cell damage and death. This study elucidates the unique toxic mechanisms by which different nanoparticle shapes interact with microalgae, underscoring the importance of the nanoparticle shape in assessing ecotoxicity. Our findings suggest that in the development and production of nanoparticles, consideration should extend beyond application performance stability and functional enhancements to include potential ecological and human health impacts. Emphasizing nanoparticle morphology optimization to maintain safety and stability while preserving functionality is essential for minimizing adverse environmental effects.
