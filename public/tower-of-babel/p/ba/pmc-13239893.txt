A Diagnostic Challenge: From Suspected Ischemic Stroke to Confirmed Herpetic Encephalitis
Authors: Mafalda Dias Neves, Miguel Gonçalves Pereira, Natacha Silveira, Susana Barbosa, José Maria Matos Sousa, Eduarda Pereira, Elisabete Monteiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHSVE mimics large‐vessel stroke with focal deficits and altered consciousness, challenging early diagnosis: Hemorrhagic lesions outside vascular territories warrant urgent lumbar puncture and empiric acyclovir initiation; Malignant edema may require decompressive craniectomy as a life‐saving intervention. Outcomes vary, but prompt neurosurgical evaluation is essential when intracranial hypertension persists.Keywords: acyclovir, Decompressive craniectomy, herpes simplex encephalitis, Neurocritical care, stroke mimic

Introduction

Herpes simplex virus encephalitis (HSVE) remains the most common cause of sporadic viral encephalitis worldwide, predominantly attributed to Herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV‐1). HSVE continues to carry a high risk of long‐term neurological sequelae, despite the declining mortality achieved through early acyclovir administration [1, 2]. The clinical presentation is typically acute and includes altered mental status, fever, seizures, and headache. However, the absence of fever or prodromal symptoms may occur and has been associated with diagnostic delay, particularly when patients present with focal neurological deficits resembling acute ischemic stroke [3, 4, 5]. Although cerebrovascular complications such as ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke are increasingly recognized in the context of central nervous system (CNS) infections, their occurrence in HSVE is poorly characterized. Despite a recent systematic review [6], high‐quality prospective data remain limited, with most evidence derived from case reports and small case series. The overlap in clinical presentation between HSVE and acute cerebrovascular events poses a significant diagnostic challenge with potential implications for patient management and outcome [7, 8].

We present a case of HSVE initially misinterpreted as acute ischemic stroke. This case highlights how cognitive bias—particularly diagnostic anchoring in afebrile patients presenting with abrupt focal deficits—may delay recognition of infectious etiologies and timely antiviral therapy.

Case History

A 55‐year‐old woman with poorly controlled arterial hypertension, major depressive disorder, chronic tobacco use, and cervical foraminal stenosis was found conscious on the floor, reporting sudden‐onset left‐sided weakness earlier that day. No prodromal symptoms—fever, headache, behavioral changes, or seizures—were identified. The patient lived alone, and collateral history was limited at emergency department (ED) presentation, contributing to initial diagnostic uncertainty.

On examination, she was alert but exhibited left hemiparesis, left hemi hypoesthesia, rightward gaze deviation, moderate dysarthria, and anosognosia. The NIHSS score was 16. She was hypertensive (BP 192/121 mmHg), tachycardic (HR 108 bpm), mildly hypoxic (SpO2 93% on room air), dehydrated, and with poor hygiene—suggesting she had been on the floor for a prolonged period prior to discovery. Initial blood work showed hemoglobin 16.4 g/dL, WBC 12,000/μL, CRP 3.5 mg/L, mild elevation of hepatocellular and cholestatic liver enzymes (ALT 74 U/L, AST 75 U/L, ALP 55 U/L, GGT 47 U/L), normal electrolytes, INR 1.01, NT‐proBNP 144 pg/mL, and positive benzodiazepine screening. Non‐contrast brain CT revealed extensive right fronto‐parieto‐temporal and insular cortico‐subcortical hypodensity with mass effect, 5 mm midline shift, right lateral ventricular compression, and early uncal herniation. Brain and neck CTA showed no intracranial or supra‐aortic occlusions or stenoses, and contrast‐enhanced CT excluded abnormal enhancement—findings initially favoring extensive right MCA infarction.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Given clinical deterioration and refractory intracranial hypertension, an urgent right frontotemporoparietal decompressive craniectomy was performed on June 15, 2025. Intraoperative findings included a markedly tense, herniated brain with diffuse venous congestion and an incidental highly vascularized dural lesion of the sphenoid wing, consistent with a possible meningioma but without emergent surgical indication. An ICP monitor was placed (initial ICP: 4 mmHg). Postoperatively, the patient was admitted to the Neurocritical Care Unit, sedated, intubated, and ventilated under multimodal brain monitoring, with seizure prophylaxis initiated with levetiracetam.

Despite initial stabilization, follow‐up CT on June 16 revealed a new right lateral temporal hemorrhagic lesion within the previously infarcted area, with features atypical for ischemic hemorrhagic transformation and lacking correlation with a specific vascular territory—raising suspicion of viral encephalitis. Lumbar puncture showed 57 WBC/μL (predominantly mononuclear), 200 RBC/μL, protein 1.55 g/L, lactate 2.8 mmol/L, and a CSF/serum glucose ratio of 58% (serum glucose 184 mg/dL). Empirical intravenous acyclovir (10 mg/kg every 8 h) was initiated immediately. HSV‐1 DNA was subsequently confirmed in the CSF by PCR, establishing the diagnosis of herpes simplex encephalitis; a full 21‐day course of acyclovir was completed.

Sedation was gradually weaned and the patient was successfully extubated 72 h after admission, initially requiring high‐flow nasal cannula oxygen due to respiratory compromise. Neurological examination post‐extubating showed severe left hemiplegia and anosognosia, with incomplete recovery of consciousness. During the ICU stay, she developed ventilator‐associated tracheobronchitis with Enterobacter cloacae complex isolated from respiratory secretions, treated empirically with ceftazidime, later switched to meropenem, for 5 days. Aspergillus fumigatus was also isolated from respiratory secretions; chest CT on June 23 revealed bronchial wall thickening, scattered micronodules, and ground‐glass opacities in the right lower lobe and lingula, consistent with inflammatory rather than invasive fungal changes. No further workup for Aspergillus was pursued given the absence of features consistent with active infection, and clinical evolution remained favorable.

As part of the etiological workup, cervical Doppler ultrasound revealed a non‐hemodynamically significant atherosclerotic plaque at the left carotid bifurcation; transcranial Doppler showed mild‐to‐moderate hemodynamic stenosis in the right MCA M1 segment (velocity 167/55 cm/s, PI 2.0). Following completion of the acyclovir course, the patient was transferred to the Infectious Diseases Department, hemodynamically stable, afebrile, and requiring only nasal cannula oxygen. Neurologically, severe left hemiplegia, anosognosia, and residual dysarthria persisted, with no further decline in consciousness or new epileptic activity under ongoing levetiracetam prophylaxis.

On July 18, 2025, after 34 days of hospitalization, the patient was transferred to a Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation Centre. At admission she was clinically stable, oriented, with fluent but mildly slowed speech, short‐term memory impairment, mild left prefrontal cortex weakness, and dysphonia. Motor function was largely preserved, with only mild bilateral knee flexion weakness, and she was able to walk independently and perform basic daily activities autonomously.

Conclusion

HSVE can closely mimic the presentation of acute ischemic stroke, particularly in cases complicated by malignant cerebral edema and secondary intracerebral hemorrhage. Prompt recognition is critical, as delays in diagnosis can lead to devastating neurological outcomes.

This case highlights the diagnostic challenge posed by HSVE presenting as an afebrile stroke mimic in the absence of prodromal symptoms, which may contribute to cognitive bias and diagnostic anchoring toward a vascular etiology. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion when hemorrhagic lesions appear outside typical vascular territories; such findings should trigger immediate lumbar puncture and initiation of empiric intravenous acyclovir without waiting for confirmatory imaging.

In severe cases with life‐threatening cerebral edema, early consideration of decompressive craniectomy may be lifesaving, emphasizing the need for rapid neurosurgical evaluation. Importantly, surgical decision‐making should not rely solely on intracranial pressure measurements, as clinical deterioration and radiological evidence of malignant mass effect may better reflect the need for urgent intervention in selected patients with HSVE. Optimal patient outcomes hinge on the combination of timely antiviral therapy, continuous specialized neurocritical care and advanced multimodal neuromonitoring—including intracranial pressure and cerebral perfusion monitoring—to guide both medical and surgical interventions. Awareness and swift action can significantly reduce morbidity and improve functional recovery in this otherwise highly morbid condition.

Maintaining diagnostic flexibility in atypical stroke presentations is essential to minimize delays in treatment initiation and improve neurological outcomes.

Discussion

Herpes simplex virus encephalitis (HSVE) remains a leading cause of acute infectious encephalopathy and carries significant morbidity and mortality if not treated promptly. Intravenous acyclovir substantially reduces mortality and improves neurological outcomes; however, the main determinant of prognosis remains the timeliness of treatment initiation. Diagnostic delay continues to be the principal challenge, particularly when HSVE presents atypically.

In the present case, encephalitis was not initially suspected due to the absence of fever, abrupt onset of focal neurological deficits, lack of identifiable prodromal symptoms, and early CT findings suggestive of a large territorial infarction with mass effect. These features collectively contributed to diagnostic anchoring toward an ischemic stroke etiology. This scenario illustrates the impact of cognitive bias—specifically anchoring bias and premature diagnostic closure—in acute neurocritical care, where time‐pressured decision‐making may reinforce an initial vascular hypothesis.

This diagnostic pitfall is clinically relevant, as delayed initiation of acyclovir is consistently associated with worse neurological outcomes and increased mortality [1, 3]. Current guidelines therefore advocate empiric intravenous acyclovir in patients presenting with unexplained encephalopathy or focal deficits that are atypical for a vascular syndrome, even before virological confirmation [7, 9, 10]. A low threshold for lumbar puncture is essential unless contraindicated.

Neuroimaging further complicated the diagnostic process. Non‐contrast CT, typically the first‐line imaging modality in suspected stroke, may demonstrate extensive cortico‐subcortical hypodensities with mass effect that closely resemble large ischemic infarcts. In this case, the absence of contrast enhancement on the initial CT likely reflected the very early phase of the disease, prior to significant blood–brain barrier disruption. Additionally, CT has limited sensitivity for detecting early inflammatory parenchymal changes when compared with MRI, increasing the risk of misclassification in the hyperacute setting.

MRI, particularly diffusion‐weighted and FLAIR sequences (Figure 1C–F), provides superior sensitivity for the frontotemporal and insular involvement characteristic of HSVE. The presence of hemorrhagic foci that were multifocal, subcortical, and not confined to a single arterial territory was atypical for hemorrhagic transformation of ischemic stroke and more consistent with necrotizing viral encephalitis. Recognition of this non‐territorial hemorrhagic pattern was pivotal in prompting CSF analysis and initiation of antiviral therapy.

Multimodal neuroimaging from admission to postoperative MRI in biopsy‐proven HSV‐1 encephalitis mimicking malignant MCA stroke. (A) Non‐contrast CT at presentation showed extensive right fronto‐parieto‐temporo‐insular cortico‐subcortical hypodensity with mass effect—appearance initially interpreted as a large right MCA infarct. (B) Early postoperative CT after right frontotemporoparietal decompressive craniectomy demonstrates the craniectomy defect and a new lateral temporal intraparenchymal hemorrhagic focus within the previously hypoattenuating territory. The morphology and location (non‐territorial, subcortical) were atypical for hemorrhagic transformation of ischemia and raised concern for an alternative etiology. (C) Axial FLAIR MRI reveals asymmetric hyperintensity involving the right mesial and lateral temporal cortex with extension to the insula and adjacent subcortical white matter, consistent with encephalitis rather than a vascular territorial pattern. (D) Axial T2‐weighted imaging showed concordant high signal in the involved fronto‐temporo‐insular cortex and subcortical white matter, with associated swelling. (E) Diffusion‐weighted imaging demonstrates prominent cortical diffusion restriction in the right temporal lobe (bright DWI signal), supporting an acute infectious process; these changes aligned poorly with a single arterial territory and, together with the hemorrhagic focus, favored HSV encephalitis. (F) Axial T2* (susceptibility‐weighted) images highlight blooming at the lateral temporal hemorrhagic lesion, confirming parenchymal hemorrhage within the encephalitic involvement. These imaging features prompted CSF analysis, which confirmed HSV‐1 by PCR; the patient completed a 21‐day course of acyclovir with subsequent clinical improvement. Abbreviations: CT, computed tomography; DWI, diffusion‐weighted imaging; FLAIR, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery; HSV‐1, herpes simplex virus type 1; MCA, middle cerebral artery.

Malignant cerebral edema represents a rare but life‐threatening complication of HSVE. In such cases, mortality is high without intervention. Decompressive craniectomy has been described in small case series and reports as a salvage strategy in patients with refractory intracranial hypertension [11, 12]. Outcomes remain heterogeneous, with some patients achieving meaningful recovery while others survive with significant disability.

In the present case, the indication for decompressive craniectomy was based on neurological deterioration and imaging evidence of life‐threatening mass effect rather than intracranial pressure (ICP) values alone. The initial ICP measurement of 4 mmHg likely reflected post‐decompression physiology and local measurement limitations, and therefore did not accurately represent the severity of intracranial hypertension prior to surgical intervention. This emphasizes that surgical decision‐making in malignant HSVE should be driven primarily by clinical and radiological progression rather than isolated ICP values.

Hemorrhagic complications further increase complexity. Unlike hemorrhagic transformation of ischemic infarction, HSVE‐associated hemorrhage results from necrotizing parenchymal destruction and viral vasculopathy leading to vessel fragility and microhemorrhage [4, 6, 8, 13]. Although hemorrhagic HSVE is associated with increased mortality, it does not contraindicate antiviral therapy and may still justify aggressive supportive and surgical management in selected cases.

Virological confirmation through CSF polymerase chain reaction (PCR) remains the diagnostic gold standard. However, false negatives may occur if testing is performed very early (< 72 h) or after prolonged antiviral exposure. In patients with strong clinical suspicion, repeat lumbar puncture and PCR testing after 48–72 h should be considered [2, 5].

Finally, long‐term morbidity among survivors remains substantial. Persistent cognitive impairment, epilepsy, focal motor deficits, and neuropsychiatric sequelae are common [14, 15]. Early integration of multidisciplinary neurorehabilitation is therefore crucial.

This case adds clinical insight by illustrating how HSVE may present as an afebrile stroke mimic with malignant edema, reinforcing the need for diagnostic flexibility in acute stroke pathways. Early consideration of infectious etiologies in atypical stroke presentations, prompt initiation of empiric acyclovir, and timely neurosurgical evaluation when mass effect progresses are essential steps to optimize outcomes.
