Population Pharmacokinetic Modeling Practice of Time‐Varying Clearance: Insights from a First‐in‐Human Study Case
Authors: Yongjun Hu, Lan Gao, Ronald Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate characterization of time‐varying clearance (CL) is critical during early drug development, as it informs clinical study design and dosing strategies for future trials. However, detecting and modeling time‐varying clearance is challenging due to limited data availability in early clinical development. Using pharmacokinetic data from a well‐controlled first‐in‐human (FIH) study with single and multiple ascending doses, this study explored time‐varying clearance, dose proportionality, and meal effects on pharmacokinetics (PK) of utreloxastat. Initial data analysis revealed time‐varying systemic clearance, more‐than‐dose‐proportional kinetics, and meal‐dependent PK alterations, which informed the development of a population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) model. A systematic evaluation of twelve distinct time‐varying clearance models identified an exponential time‐varying clearance model within a two‐compartment framework, incorporating first‐order absorption with eight transit compartments, as the best fit to the data. Covariate analysis confirmed that sex, age, and body weight were not significant predictors of variability. This study is the first to showcase a comparative evaluation of time‐varying clearance for a small molecule. It highlights the innovative application of time‐varying clearance modeling during the FIH study as a pivotal step in optimizing model‐informed dosing strategies. This approach underscores its importance in addressing non‐linear pharmacokinetics and enhancing drug development practices for future clinical trials.

Introduction

Accurately characterizing pharmacokinetics (PK) of investigated drug is a primary objective of first‐in‐human (FIH) study during the early phases of drug development, which is also essential for guiding subsequent clinical trials. 1 , 2 To achieve this, FIH studies often include single ascending doses (SAD) and multiple ascending doses (MAD) studies. These FIH studies allow researchers to systemically explore the PK profiles and safety to optimize dose range that ensures sufficient drug exposures while minimizing potential adverse effects, thereby informing subsequent clinical development. 3 The outcome from FIH studies, is vital for optimizing a drug's therapeutic dose regimen in future clinical trials and eventual real‐world use. 4 , 5 The key characteristics of PK include absorption, distribution, clearance, food effects and dose proportionality. Among these, time‐varying clearance is often overlooked due to the difficulty in distinguishing it from the accumulation effects and the challenge of quantitatively describing it. Time‐varying clearance, that is the observed changes in drug clearance over time often following multiple doses, is usually a result of physiological or pharmacological conditions variations such as time‐varying enzyme induction or inhibition, changes in transport activity, and disease progression. The FIH study is the optimal and perhaps the only opportunity to identify time‐varying clearance by analyzing rich pharmacokinetic data, such as changes in drug concentration–time profiles, alterations in half‐life or deviations from expected clearance rates over multiple dosing intervals. Recognizing time‐varying clearance during FIH studies is crucial, as it allows for the planning and incorporation of an appropriate PK sampling schedule in subsequent clinical development to further characterize it. If overlooked, subsequent clinical studies, such as clinical pharmacology studies of drug–drug interaction, organ impairments, and phase 2 and 3 patient's studies, which generally involve single‐dose or sparse PK sampling, are insufficient to reveal the presence of time‐varying clearance.

Over the past decades, pharmacometrics has become one of the most powerful approaches in drug discovery and development through quantitatively characterizing drug pharmacokinetic exposures and their corresponding efficacy. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 This approach can efficiently bridge the gap between preclinical and clinical phases, comprehensively describe PK profiles of drugs and effectively optimize dose regimens for subsequent clinical practice. Global regulatory agencies have underscored the significance of pharmacometrics by publishing relevant guidelines that detail the best practices in both non‐clinical and clinical studies from start to finish. In 2025, ICH proposed new guidelines on model‐informed drug development (MIDD) to foster a wider application of modeling and simulation, and should initiate modeling analyses as early as possible for clinical development. 13 These modeling approaches commonly include population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) modeling, physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling, exposure‐response (ER) model, disease modeling, and so on.

Among these approaches, PopPK modeling is the highly regarded methodology frequently employed in MIDD. Beyond its descriptive purpose, the foremost aim of PopPK modeling is to accurately and precisely predict the PK profiles under various clinical scenarios to guide later clinical studies, such as optimizing dose regimens. 14 , 15 These practices require the establishment of a pharmacokinetic mathematical model as soon as initial clinical PK data is available. During this process, identifying and appropriately characterizing time‐varying clearance poses extreme challenges, as it requires that PK characteristics of the drug be identified and appropriately captured in the initial model when PK data are still limited.

In this study, we outlined essential steps and considerations for developing a population PK model. This included evaluating and incorporating potential time‐varying clearance as a structural model component. Using FIH PK data of a small molecular drug Utreloxastat, we showcased the challenges and approaches to address them. This FIH case study data consisted of three parts: SAD, MAD, and FE. 16 Utreloxastat is a 15‐lipoxygenase inhibitor in development for the treatment of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Utreloxastat leads to ferroptosis and hence reduces oxidative stress and blocks depletion of reduced glutathione by inhibiting 15‐lipoxygenase. 16 The elimination of utreloxastat is primarily through metabolism, with a negligible amount through renal clearance. In vitro metabolism of utreloxastat involved multiple cytochrome P450 enzymes CYP1A2, 2B6, 2C8, 2C19, 3A4, and 4F2. 16 In the FIH study, the drug was administered once a day (QD) in FE studies and twice a day (BID) during the MAD study. The maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) was observed at 4 h post dose, and the area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) increased slightly more than dose proportionality. However, following repeat doses, the apparent clearance decreased, and the terminal phase half‐life prolonged to over 33 h on Day14, compared to 23 h on Day1, suggesting the involvement of time‐varying clearance. Coadministration the drug with food increased its Cmax and AUC, though a difference was noticeable between high‐fat and low‐fat diet, the effects between low‐fat and medium‐fat diet (a customized diet provided by the investigation site with fat content and total calories in the middle of standard low‐fat (11–14 g fat) and high‐fat diet (55–65 g fat)) were not statistically distinguishable. Hence, both low‐fat and medium‐fat conditions were categorized as low‐fat in this study for simplicity. In this study, it is the first time to report that special challenges were encountered to identify time‐varying clearance, as well as efforts were made to incorporate it with appropriate mathematical functions into the PopPK model to accurately describe the phenomenon and reflect the underlying biological mechanism involved.

Materials and Methods

Software

Nonlinear mixed effects modeling was conducted using NONMEM (v7.5, ICON, US). Data processing and graphical analysis were performed in R (version 4.5.0 or higher, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics

This study is a retrospective analysis based on data from a previously published FIH study. 16 All clinical studies involved in this retrospective research adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, with protocols and consent forms receiving approval from regulatory agencies and local ethics committees or institutional review boards. These studies were conducted in accordance with the study protocols and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All participants provided their signed, informed consent.

PK Data Collection

The dataset for this study was obtained from a FIH clinical study. Details of the study and non‐compartmental PK analysis results have been reported earlier. 16 Briefly, in the SAD part, participants received a single dose of the investigational drug with a low‐fat breakfast. In the FE part, participants received 3 single doses orally administered under fasted status, with a high‐fat high‐calorie diet, and with a low‐fat low‐calorie diet in separate treatment periods. In the MAD part, healthy adults received QD or BID of the investigational drug with low‐fat meals for 14 days. Intensive blood samples were collected following oral administration of the investigational drug up to 72 h post dose in SAD and FE studies, and up to 24 h postdose following the initial dose on Day1 (the evening dose was administered 12 h post the morning dose if BID) and up to 72 h following the morning dose on Day14 in MAD cohorts, and an additional sample was harvested at predose on Day7 in MAD cohorts.

Exploratory Data Analysis and PopPK Model Development

The analysis dataset includes all quantifiable PK data from participants who received the drug. PK data that were missing due to assay sensitivity, specifically those below the quantitation limit (BQL), were ignored following the “M1” method. 17 , 18 , 19 Exploratory data analyses (EDA) were conducted first to guide the construction of the compartmental model. PK concentration–time profiles were stratified by relevant study conditions, such as dosing time, or covariates, like meal status, to identify the key components of the PopPK model. During the model development, a comprehensive set of diagnostic plots was generated to assess the adequacy of the model fit. 20 , 21

In the model, the observed drug concentration (Y) is expressed by the following equation: LnY=Lnfθ,η,t+ε

In this equation, θ denotes the fixed‐effect parameter, η represents the random‐effect parameter, t denotes longitudinal Time, and ε accounts for the additive residual error.

Selected demographics and characteristics of individuals were summarized, and their effect on PK exposure was investigated. A power model was used for continuous covariates, and a binominal model was used for categorical covariates as described in publication. 21

Once a base PopPK model was established, the final PopPK model was identified using a stepwise process methodology briefly described in the literature. 22 In forward addition, covariates were added if the change in objective function value (ΔOFV) was ≥ 3.84 (p < .05, df = 1). In backward elimination, covariates were removed if the change in ΔOFV was ≤ 10.83 (p < .001, df = 1), repeating until all covariates met the criteria.

Final Model Evaluation and Covariate Effect Illustration

A visual predictive check (VPC) approach was applied to evaluate the final model. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 Particularly, after 1000 replications of simulation were performed using PK parameters from the final model, the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles of predicted drug concentrations with 90% confidence interval (CI) were compared to the corresponding percentiles of observations for a visual assessment of the predictive performance of the final PopPK model.

Additionally, the impact of individual covariate in the final model on the drug exposure was illustrated using forest plot approach. 27 Briefly, one thousand sets of population parameters were generated using a smoothed parametric bootstrap procedure from parameters matrix of the final model. After simulations were completed, maximum concentration (Cmax,ss), minimum concentration (Cmin,ss), and AUC0–12 h,ss values at steady state were calculated and presented in tabular format, as well as in forest plots.

Exploration of Concentration–Time Profiles

The exploration of drug concentration–time profiles is a critical step in the development of PopPK models. 22 The distribution of PK data and the overall PK concentration–time profiles are depicted in Figure S1, Supporting Information. The PK data point number by study cohort, visit, data sampling time, and dose levels are tabulated in Table S3, Supporting Information. As shown in Figure 1a, the peak concentrations do not align consistently across dose levels when normalized by dose following a single dose in the SAD cohort. Specifically, higher dose levels (e.g., 500 and 1000 mg) demonstrated elevated dose‐normalized peak concentration compared to lower dose levels (e.g., 100 and 250 mg). Meanwhile, the terminal phase most likely consists of a rapid distribution stage, followed by a slower elimination stage, with the parallel curves across dose levels (Figure 1b). These findings suggest that the PopPK model, used to characterize the pharmacokinetics of the drug following oral administration, adheres to at least a two‐compartment structure, with an elimination process not saturated by concentrations and a nonlinear absorption process influenced by varying dose levels.

Furthermore, in comparison with the fasted condition, the FE study shows both low‐fat and high‐fat meals can enhance drug absorption, as illustrated by the observed average peak concentration (Figure 2a), but with a large variability. 16

In the MAD cohort, with a BID dosing frequency, comparing PK profiles on Day1 reveals a flatter slope in the terminal phase for the evening dose compared to the morning dose (Figure 2b). Furthermore, on Day14, the terminal phase shows a lower elimination rate than Day1 across all dose levels after the morning dose, particularly at the 150 and 250 mg dose levels (Figure 2c). Collectively, these observations suggest that: (1) the absorption fraction is dose‐dependent and (2) clearance varies over time. Consequently, this leads to complex more‐than‐dose proportional PK profiles of the drug, which aligns with the result from NCA linearity analysis. 16

Model Structure and Pharmacokinetic Parameters

After a thorough iterative evaluation and differentiation assessment, exploring one‐, two‐, and three‐compartment models, goodness‐of‐fit (GOF) plots, PK parameter estimate plausibility, the OFV, changes in OFV, Akaike information criterion (AIC), and Bayesian information criterion (BIC), a two‐compartment model was demonstrated to be adequate in characterizing the concentration–time profiles of the drug following oral administration and was selected as the final structural model. This model incorporates eight transit compartments to represent the time‐delayed absorption process, with a mean transit time of 2.20 h.

The final PopPK model incorporated additional factors such as dosage levels and meal status, which influence the relative absorption fraction, as well as time‐varying clearance dynamics. Beyond these, no other intrinsic or extrinsic factors, such as age, sex, ethnicity, race, body weight, BMI, and hepatic and renal function biomarkers (e.g. albumin, alkaline phosphatase [ALP], alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], total bilirubin [BILI], and creatine [CREAT]), were identified as significant covariates influencing the PK exposures, which was not a surprising, as all participants were healthy adults as specified by the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the clinical study. 16

In evaluating time‐varying clearance, we compared the conditional models regarding the effects of dosing time (e.g., evening dosing vs morning dosing), total dose number (e.g., single dose vs multiple doses), and treatment duration thresholds (e.g., before Day14 vs after Day14). Furthermore, time‐varying clearance models were assessed by analyzing both absolute and natural log‐transformed longitudinal data across various models, incorporating minor modifications as referenced. 28 , 29 A total of twelve potential mathematical functions, including exponential, sigmoidal time‐varying clearance, and conditional clearance functions and their alternatives, to describe time‐varying clearance have been explored. After a comprehensive comparison based on predefined quality‐fit criteria—such as the percentage of relative standard error (RSE%) of PK parameter values not exceeding 30%, 90% CI of these values not including zero, model stability (conditional number not exceeding 500), inter‐individual variability (IIV) not exceeding 0.3, %CV of IIV not exceeding 60%, shrinkage of IIV not exceeding 30%, changes in OFV, changes in AIC values, changes in BIC values, diagnostic plots of GOF, the limitation of analysis dataset, and, most importantly, pharmacological and biological plausibility and rationality—the model with exponential time‐varying clearance was deemed to be superior. This model was preferred due to its stronger biological and pharmacological plausibility, acceptable and reliable parameter estimate values, as well as its better fit compared to other models. Eventually, the time‐varying clearance (CLTV,t) over natural log‐transformed time (ln(t)) in the final model was described using an exponential function, as outlined in Model 8 (refer to Table 2). The key model metrics were summarized and presented in Table S1, Supporting Information. The PK parameter estimates for the selected final PopPK model were presented in Table 3. Briefly, the presence of food increases drug absorption by up to 36% (90%CI: 25 % to 48%) for low‐fat meals (θ7 = 0.36), and 57% (90%CI: 42% to 72%) for high‐fat meals (θ8 = 0.57). Additionally, the positive power value for dose levels on relative absorption fraction, θ9 = 0.21, indicated that the absorption fraction increased at higher dose levels. The coefficient value for time‐varying clearance (KTMCL), which is 0.096, suggested that the clearance decreases with treatment duration, eventually reaching a plateau after approximately four weeks (referred to the plot of Model 8 in Table 2).

Evaluation of the Final PopPK Model

Selected GOF plots, which demonstrate the adequacy of the model's description of the observed PK data, are shown in Supplemental Figure S2, Supporting Information. Furthermore, as demonstrated in Figure 3, the model's predictability was validated using the VPC methodology. Overall, 96.6%, 94.5%, and 93.2% of PK observations fall within the 95% prediction interval (PI) for the SAD, MAD, and FE cohorts, respectively. Notably, for the MAD cohort, which was stratified by dose levels and PK sampling times on Day1 and Day14, the 95% CI of predicted concentrations includes most observed Cmax and AUC0–12 h across dose levels, except 50.0% within 95% PI for 150 mg BID dose regimen on Day14 (referred to Table 4). Additionally, the accumulation ratios of AUC0–12 h are comparable between predictions and observations, with observations ranging from 1.3 to 2.2 and predictions from 1.4 to 1.8 across dose levels.

Covariate Impact on PK Exposures

During the process of the stepwise approach analysis, covariates—such as dosing time, age, sex, body weight, hepatic functional biomarkers, and renal function biomarkers—did not significantly reduce the objective function value (ΔOFV ≥ 3.84, P < .05, df = 1). Conversely, impacts from dose levels and meal conditions (low‐fat meals, high‐fat meals vs fasted status) on relative absorption fraction (Frel) were found to be significant. Hence, dose levels and meal conditions were covariates retained in the final model (ΔOFV ≥ 10.83, P < .001, df = 1). In addition, a Forest Plot analysis was performed to elucidate the clinically significant effects of these retained covariates on PK exposures at steady state, as presented in Figure 4, focusing on Cmax,ss, Cmin,ss and AUC0–12 h,ss post morning dose following a BID dosing regimen. Notably, the analyses indicated that the 5th (150 mg) and 95th (500 mg) percentiles of doses most likely influenced exposure levels, resulting in a slightly more than dose‐proportional Cmax,ss, Cmin,ss, and AUC0–12 h,ss when compared with the reference of 250 mg BID and fasted condition, as detailed in Table 5. Meal status of low‐fat and high‐fat, however, did not demonstrate a clinically meaningful impact on the PK exposures at steady state relevant to the clinical dose regimen (Figure 4 and Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, a PopPK model was constructed to characterize the PK profiles in an FIH clinical trial. Notably, this work represents the first comprehensive and systemic assessment of time‐varying clearance models for small molecules using PopPK modeling methodology. Key findings from the study include: (1) evidence of more‐than‐dose‐proportional kinetics in the absorption process; (2) identification of a two‐compartment model with eight transit compartments; (3) identification and characterization of clearance with exponential declination with time; (4) determination that meal conditions influenced PK exposures, but were clinically irrelevant based on Forest plot evaluation; and (5) confirmation that sex, age, and body weight were not significant covariates. This research highlights the importance of critically evaluating time‐varying clearance models during FIH studies as a fundamental step in optimizing model‐informed dosing strategies and guiding future clinical trial PK sampling schedules. Furthermore, it is the first time to compare the utility of time‐varying modeling strategies in addressing non‐linear pharmacokinetics.

Effective decision‐making of dose regimen optimization is a pivotal yet complex challenge faced within pharmaceutical companies at each juncture of modern drug development. 30 To avoid suboptimal decisions, implementing a precise, accurate, and efficient analysis of preclinical and clinical pharmacokinetics, safety, and efficacy data, while incorporating multi‐stakeholder perspectives and advanced methodologies, is essential for guiding the drug development journey forward. 31 As one of these approaches, modeling and simulation are vital in drug development, offering substantial support for informed decision‐making. 6 , 11 , 32 Since 2012, Mould et al. and Bauer have consistently provided a comprehensive outline of best practice and examples in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics modeling and simulation, with a particular focus on the fundamental concepts 33 , 34 , 35 and workflow framework in NONMEM program, 36 , 37 respectively.

Generally, the PK characteristics of a drug, such as absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination, along with related factors like drug accumulation and dose proportionality, are thoroughly investigated during the FIH studies. However, time‐varying clearance often receives insufficient attention, despite its critical importance. This oversight can be attributed to the challenges in identifying the presence of time‐varying clearance, differentiating it from drug accumulation, and the lack of appropriate tools for its accurate characterization. For example, it is highly challenging and complicated to determine the appropriate model structure for the non‐linear dynamics disposition process, particularly when the disposition process is mingled with time‐varying clearance. 28 , 29 , 38 , 39 Usually, time‐varying clearance is often noticed for antibodies, generally introduced by target‐mediated drug degradation (TMDD). 40 These processes lead to the formation of immune complexes that are cleared more rapidly from circulation, thereby impacting clearance in a time‐varying manner. The time‐varying clearance modeling strategy has been comprehensively summarized in previous literature. 28 , 39 In contrast, reports of time‐varying clearance for small molecules were much less frequent. 41 This could be due to the shorter dose interval (generally daily or less than daily) and PK sampling period, which often prevent capturing the terminal elimination phase post multiple dosing (frequently only up to 24 h post last dosing). Time‐varying clearance for small molecules typically arises from the induction or inhibition of CYP450 enzymes, such as CYP3A4, or through mechanisms like auto‐induction, as observed with lorlatinib. 42 These changes subsequently alter the clearance rate over time. 42 , 43 Nevertheless, a comprehensive and comparative comparison of time‐varying clearance models for small molecules has not been reported previously.

Clearly characterizing time‐varying clearance is as essential as other PK characteristics, as it significantly impacts later clinical study design, including selection of dose regimens and the timing for PK and PD sample collection. Although the limited data available in FIH studies frequently restricts the ability to characterize time‐varying clearance, these FIH studies provide an optimal opportunity to address it. If overlooked during FIH studies, it is hard to plan the appropriate sample collection timing in the following clinical trials to distinguish time‐varying clearance, since the general assumption is that the clearance is stable from multiple dosing. In later clinical pharmacology studies, such as drug interaction, relative bioavailability, or bioequivalence trials, single‐dose designs and sparse PK sampling are often used and will likely fail to capture time‐varying clearance, if it is not appropriately captured during the FIH study. Modeling based on FIH data offers the best chance to identify and characterize time‐varying clearance. Once identified, appropriate PK sampling strategies can be planned and incorporated into subsequent clinical development to confirm and quantify the observations.

In practice, thorough EDA of PK concentration‐time profiles is an essential initial step. An appropriate PopPK model capable of accurately characterizing the PK features is based on successful EDA analysis. 22 Based on available PK data, various EDA approaches can be applied, including comparisons of PK profiles across specific covariates such as sex, race, age, body weight, disease state, hepatic and renal function impairment, formulation and food conditions, as well as dosing time and treatment duration.

In the present study, a dose‐normalized concentration‐time profile comparison across dose levels from the SAD cohort on Day1 showed a lower peak concentration at lower doses, indicating that the drug absorption is more than dose‐proportional (Figure 1a). It aligns with the conclusion from the dose linearity analysis. 16 Furthermore, as illustrated in Figure 1b, the semi‐logarithmic plots of PK profiles after the initial morning dose on Day1 showed a parallel curve during the terminal phase of PK profiles across different dose levels. These observations collectively indicate that: (1) the structure model likely consists of at least two compartments, for instance, a fast tissue distribution stage and a slow elimination stage; (2) the elimination process is not concentration‐dependent within the current dose range; and (3) the more than dose‐proportional absorption is attributed mainly to the absorption stage rather than the drug disposition stage. The enhanced drug absorption at higher dose levels may result from altered physiological conditions in the gut. This could include induction of influx transporters or inhibition of efflux transporters or metabolic enzymes in the small intestine, for example, P‐gp. 44 , 45 , 46 However, this is unlikely to occur after only one single dose. Although the specific proteins involved in this drug have not been identified yet, the mechanism warrants further investigation.

Furthermore, when assessing PK profiles with the BID dosing frequencies, it has been consistently observed that the evening dose resulted in higher concentrations and a more gradual terminal phase compared to the morning dose at both 150 and 250 mg dose levels on Day1, as illustrated by Figure 2b. This suggests that the drug's PK profiles could be influenced by changes in drug disposition. For instance, higher concentrations could be linked to the compositions of meals, the diurnal effect of clearance, including intestinal motility, renal functions, and hepatic functions. 38 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 Upon reviewing Figure 2c, the concentration–time curves on Day14 also exhibit a flatter terminal phase compared to Day1 across all dose levels, while the terminal phase of PK curves is parallel after the morning dose (Figure 1) on Day1 in the SAD cohort. These observations align with previous findings reported by Gao et al. 16 Putting all together, these discoveries suggest a time‐varying clearance associated with this drug, rather than a concentration‐dependent clearance. As mentioned previously, the variation in systemic apparent clearance over time may be quantified by using an empirical PopPK modeling approach with an appropriate time‐varying model.

Table 2 presents twelve empirical models used to explore the time‐varying clearance associated with this drug to identify the best fit based on observed PK profile analysis results and potential underlying biological and pharmacological mechanisms. These models are categorized into conditional clearance models (models 1 to 4), time‐varying clearance models and their alternatives (models 5 to 12) against absolute or natural log‐transformed longitudinal time course. Some of these empirical models are more theoretical and difficult to interpret when considering physiological variations. For instance, the “binary wave” pattern in Model 1 alternates every 12 h with high and low values. For this drug, the conditional clearance models effectively captured and described the PK observations (data not shown). However, some uncertainties remained, especially for continuous predictors, such as time (t) and responders, such as systemic clearance.

Among these time‐varying clearance models, Model 8 with the exponential function was identified as the best to describe the time‐varying clearance, and it was integrated into the final PopPK model. Other time‐varying models exhibited either an excessively rapid reduction in the fraction of time‐varying clearance within the first 24 h by 100% (such as models 7 and 12) or had unacceptable PK parameter estimates or model stability. Model 8 not only demonstrated statistical success in modeling performance, meeting predefined quality‐fit criteria outlined in the results section, such as low RSE% (<60%) and shrinkage values (<30%), but also offered insights into potential biological and pharmacological mechanisms. As shown in the plot of clearance change over time (referred to Model 8 in Table 2), the clearance drops rapid‐relatively in the first 24 h then decreases at a much slower rate, eventually reaching a plateau after 4 weeks. The initial rapid decrease in clearance following the first dose indicated the clearance drops of the evening dose compared to the morning dose on Day1, while the subsequent slower decrease in systemic clearance likely reflected changes due to protein amount turnover, which was due to long‐term steady drug effects. 16 In this study, there was some uncertainty in confirming the clearance decreases for the evening dose on Day1 due to far fewer time points post‐ evening dose, which was restricted by the clinical setting. Additional time points post Day1 evening dose or additional intensive PK profile on Day2 or 3 can further confirm the initial rapid clearance change in the first 24 h described by Model 8. In biological mechanisms, the timing of systemic clearance changes may depend on clearance‐related protein expression pathways and the turnover rate of these proteins. 54 The mechanism for rapid change in clearance may involve immediate protein expression, which is directly translated from mRNA reserves, enabling an instantaneous response. In contrast, the mechanism for slower change in clearance may involve post‐transcriptional regulation, which can take several days to achieve a steady state. For instance, the time required to reach maximal induction and subsequent disappearance of CYP3A4 induction was 14 days for rifampicin and efavirenz, as well as at least 7 days for rifabutin. 55 , 56 The model was selected based on empirical evidence and our collective expertise. However, the possibility of clearance influenced by other factors, such as auto‐inhibition and transporter modulation, cannot be ruled out.

We noticed that the appropriate incorporation of a time‐varying model, either as a conditional clearance model or a time‐varying clearance model, is highly dependent on the quality and adequacy of data availability. In this study, intensive PK data were collected only on Day1 and Day14. Between Day1 and Day14, only a predose sample was collected on Day7. There was a lack of data to fully define the curvature of the clearance change. Hence, the time‐varying clearance was assumed to change gradually over time and reach a plateau by Day14. The limited sample size and the extended interval between PK sampling points between Day1 and Day14 made it challenging to accurately parameterize a more suitable time‐varying model. Some models excluded were likely due to data insufficiency. For example, Model 5 (Table 2) was excluded because the 90% CI of KTMCL (−0.0018 to 0.018) included a slightly negative value and the range contained zero, while Model 5 could potentially be an appropriate model with additional data to tighten the 90% CI. It was believed that additional intensive PK data between Day3 to Day7 would be more instrumental in identifying the most suitable time‐varying model for this drug. To address this, an ad hoc simulation‐based sensitivity example test was performed by adding two simulated data points around peak (4 h post dose) and trough concentrations on Day3 and/or one simulated data point around peak on Day7, using the time‐varying clearance model 8. As illustrated in the supplemental Table S2, Supporting Information, all three tested analysis datasets showed a significant reduction in OFV, AIC, and BIC when compared with the reference analysis dataset. Notably, the change in BIC (ΔBIC) decreased by at least 29.69 points for the dataset (Analysis Dataset 3) with a slight improvement in PK parameter estimates. We also noticed a limitation in the selected time‐varying clearance model (Model 8). Statistically, applying a natural logarithm transformation of predose time (time = 0) results in undefined values (approaching infinity). Therefore, it is advisable to remain open‐minded toward exploring various potential time‐varying models during the modeling practice and to refine the PopPK model when more data becomes available.

Finally, through the application of the PopPK modeling approach, time‐varying clearance for this drug was successfully identified and accurately characterized. This advancement enhanced the accuracy of predictions for future clinical study designs, while suggesting that certain key findings from previous research may warrant reevaluation. 16 For example, the effective half‐life— used to justify the dose frequency—was previously calculated based on accumulation under the assumptions of constant clearance and steady state achievement. These assumptions are invalidated with the discovery of time‐varying clearance, rendering the commonly used formula unsuitable for time‐varying clearance. Furthermore, the time required to reach a steady state may differ from earlier predictions due to the changes observed in systemic clearance over time. The limitations to fully define the curvature of clearance changes from this practice suggest the importance of additional intensive PK samplings before steady state achievement in a clinical study. Further simulations based on the current PopPK model would provide an optimal PK sample collection scheme. Moreover, insights into time‐varying clearance may aid in identifying potential mechanisms responsible for this change.

Put all together, in the final PopPK model, the Frel increase was estimated to be 36% (90% CI: 25% to 48%) for low‐fat meals and 72% (90% CI: 42% to 72%) for high‐fat meals (Table 3). These results were consistent with previous NCA PK analysis. 16 The low‐fat diet condition compared to the fasted condition showed Cmax, AUC0‐t, and AUC0‐inf increases of 80.25%, 48.66%, and 45.90%, respectively. Under a high‐fat diet, the increases for Cmax, AUC0‐t, and AUC0‐inf were 141.30%, 82.45%, and 79.07%, respectively. During the SAD and MAD cohorts, the investigational drug was administered with a standardized meal, which consisted of fat providing at least 25% calories and was provided as the standard meals at the clinical site under controlled well. This composition was very close to the standard low‐fat meal as defined by FDA guidance; it is not surprising to observe the similar concentration‐time profiles between the standardized and low‐fat meal conditions. The effect of the standardized diet was not distinguishable from the low‐fat meal. Hence, the standardized meals were categorized as the low‐fat meals in the final PopPK model. In addition, further Forest analysis indicated that the ratio of drug systemic exposures, including Cmax,ss, Cmin,ss, and AUC0–12 h,ss, between fed and fasted states fell within the range 0.8 to 1.25 (see Figure 4 and Table 5). This suggests that the meal status may not be a significant extrinsic factor in clinical practice and drug labelling. 4 This serves as a reminder for clinical pharmacologists to exercise caution when drawing conclusions, despite observable differences in clinical trials and estimable values through PopPK modeling.

Conclusions

Adhering to the practice outlined in this study regarding the modeling of dynamic clearance over time, we successfully developed a PopPK model incorporating time‐varying clearance based on underlying biological and pharmacological plausibility as well as statistical success in modeling performance. Careful analysis of PK characteristics from the FIH study is the optimum time to identify time‐varying clearance. Only with successful recognition of time‐varying clearance from FIH, appropriate PK sampling will be planned and incorporated in later clinical trials to further confirm and quantitatively assess it. This model effectively characterized the concentration‐time profiles observed in this FIH clinical trial. The model demonstrated robustness and effectiveness in capturing the drug's pharmacokinetic behavior, particularly in relation to drug elimination influenced by time‐varying clearance.

During model development, dose levels and meals emerged as covariates significantly affecting pharmacokinetics, while sex, age, and body weight did not have a significant impact. However, forest plot analysis demonstrated that dose levels were the only covariate with significant clinical relevance. These findings collectively underscore the necessity of a thorough modeling assessment, particularly for time‐varying clearance models.
