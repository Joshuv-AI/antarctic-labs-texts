Longitudinal sleep duration patterns and their association with cognitive decline in older Chinese adults
Authors: Deqing Zeng, Wei Zhang, Yuqi Yang, Yijing Chu, Yuge Yan, Ping Zhu, Jing Guo, Xianqu Feng, Li Kang, Jiaoling Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAssociations between sleep duration and cognitive decline are inconsistent, and the value of longitudinal changes versus static measurements remains unclear.METHODSWe identified longitudinal sleep patterns in 4780 older adults from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) using K‐means clustering. Linear mixed‐effects models and Cox models assessed cognitive decline and incident dementia risk over 10 years.RESULTSThe “Consistently Long” pattern showed the fastest cognitive decline (β = −0.027 standard deviation/year; p < 0.001) and highest cumulative dementia incidence. Conversely, patterns trending toward healthy duration slowed decline. However, adjusted Cox models found no significant association between sleep patterns and incident dementia risk.DISCUSSIONThe trajectory of sleep duration, particularly the direction of change, is a powerful correlate of cognitive decline. Monitoring long‐term dynamics outperforms static assessments for identifying high‐risk older adults.Keywords: Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), cognitive decline, dementia, K‐means clustering, longitudinal study, older adults, sleep duration, trajectory analysis

INTRODUCTION

Cognitive decline is a progressive, age‐related neurodegenerative process that can culminate in dementia, such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related dementias (ADRD), representing a severe public health challenge that impacts quality of life and increases mortality risk in a rapidly aging global population. 1 In 2015, the total socioeconomic cost of AD in China was estimated to be $167.74 billion, consisting of direct medical ($54.53 billion), direct non‐medical ($26.20 billion), and indirect costs ($87.01 billion). 2

Sleep health is a key modifiable risk factor for dementia. 3 Sleep disturbances such as insomnia and hypersomnia are common early symptoms, 4 and are often measured by sleep duration. While 6 to 8 hours of sleep is considered optimal, studies linking non‐optimal sleep duration to cognitive health are inconsistent. 5 Some find long sleep more detrimental, 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 others short sleep, 11 , 12 , 13 and some find both are unfavorable. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Adding to this complexity, some researchers propose that the change in sleep duration over time might be a more critical predictor of cognitive outcomes than any single measurement. 19 , 20 , 21

These inconsistent findings may stem from two key methodological limitations in previous studies assessing sleep patterns. First, most research has relied on sleep data from a single time point, 6 , 7 , 9 , 11 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 which inherently treats sleep as a static characteristic and overlooks its dynamic trajectory over time. Second, many studies used predefined, threshold‐based categorizations to identify sleep patterns (e.g., < 6 hours for short sleep, > 8 hours for long sleep). This approach, applied to both single measurements 6 , 7 , 9 , 11 , 15 , 16 , 17 and longitudinal changes, 8 , 10 , 12 , 13 , 19 , 20 , 21 is subjective and threshold dependent, failing to capture complex, naturally occurring sleep patterns and risking inaccurate classification.

To address these limitations, this study uses longitudinal data from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) to link distinct sleep duration patterns to cognitive decline in older Chinese adults. Using 10 years of sleep and neuropsychological data, we assessed these long‐term patterns’ associations with both the rate of cognitive change and incident dementia risk. We aim to provide new insights for early dementia prevention and intervention.

METHODS

Participants

The data for this study were sourced from the CLHLS, a prospective cohort study of individuals aged ≥ 65 in China (see Methods S1 in supporting information for details). Our analytical cohort was derived from the 2008 wave, from which we excluded participants with baseline cognitive impairment, missing data, or insufficient follow‐up until at least 2014. We defined sleep trajectories up to 2014 to minimize survival bias and preserve sample size (n = 4780 vs. n = 2332 if extended to 2018), while incorporating 2018 cognitive data into the linear mixed‐effects models (LMMs) to extend the outcome observation. This resulted in a final sample of 4780 participants for K‐means clustering, LMM, and Cox proportional hazards models. The detailed sample selection process is illustrated in Figure 1.

Sleep measures

To measure sleep duration, the following question was used: “How many hours do you normally sleep?” 22 To interpret our findings, we used 6 to 8 hours as a clinical benchmark for optimal sleep in older adults. Based on this, durations <r 6 hours and > 8 hours were conceptually labeled as short and long sleep, respectively. Importantly, these descriptive thresholds did not dictate participant grouping for our subsequent statistical analyses.

Cognitive function assessment

Cognitive function was assessed at each wave using the Chinese Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), with scores ranging from 0 to 30. Dementia was defined as an MMSE score < 18. We analyzed the total MMSE score and five cognitive subdomains: orientation, registration, attention and calculation, recall, and language. The reliability and validity of these tests have been well established in previous studies, 14 , 23 and further details regarding the MMSE are provided in Table S1 in supporting information.

To facilitate comparison across tests and over time, all raw scores were standardized into Z scores based on the baseline cohort's mean and standard deviation (SD). A global cognitive Z score was also generated. 24 The detailed methodology for the Z score calculation is provided in Methods S1.

Covariates

We collected information on sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, and health‐related variables through the questionnaire. In our analyses, we adjusted for a range of potential confounding factors associated with sleep patterns or cognitive function. These covariates, measured at baseline (2008), included: age, sex, education level, residence, economic status, smoking status, drinking status, exercise status, labor status, body mass index (BMI), history of hypertension, and history of diabetes. Detailed definitions and classifications for all covariates are provided in Methods S1.

The proportion of missing data for all covariates was assessed (see Table S2 in supporting information). To handle missing data for baseline covariates, we performed multiple imputation using chained equations (MICE), 25 assuming the data were missing at random (MAR). We generated 20 imputed datasets, and the estimates from the analyses on each dataset were pooled using Rubin rules.

K‐means clustering

To identify distinct longitudinal sleep duration patterns, we performed K‐means clustering in R software on self‐reported sleep durations from the first three waves (2008, 2011, and 2014). Prior to clustering, the three sleep duration variables were standardized to ensure each wave contributed equally to the cluster assignment. The detailed procedure for determining the optimal number of clusters and the specific parameters used for the final analysis are described in Methods S1.

Statistical analysis

First, we conducted descriptive analyses of the baseline characteristics of the study population, stratified by the identified sleep pattern groups. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD, and between‐group differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. 26 Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (n, %), with between‐group differences assessed using the chi‐squared test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate.

Our primary analysis used LMMs to assess the association between the identified sleep patterns and the trajectory of cognitive change. Our secondary analysis used Cox proportional hazards models to examine the association between these patterns and the risk of incident dementia.

Detailed descriptions of all statistical procedures—including the characterization of sleep pattern trajectories, the full specifications of the LMM and Cox models, and all sensitivity analyses—are provided in Methods S1.

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2), and a two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 4780 participants were eligible for the analysis. The mean (SD) age at baseline was 78.07 (9.48) years, and 51.2% of the participants were female. Other baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 1. During the longitudinal follow‐up, the cumulative incidence of dementia increased from 5.9% in 2011 to 18.6% in 2014, and reached 23.6% in 2018 among the continuing participants (Table S3 in supporting information). The overall incidence rate for dementia in our cohort was 32.95 per 1000 person‐years.

Based on their longitudinal trajectory characteristics, and in conjunction with results from LMM (see Table S4 in supporting information), we defined and named these three patterns “Sleep Pattern 1: Decreasing Sleep Duration,” “Sleep Pattern 2: Consistently Long Sleepers,” and “Sleep Pattern 3: Consistently Short Sleepers.” A detailed description of the trajectory for each pattern is provided in Results S1 in supporting information.

Cluster demographic and clinical characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the 4780 participants, stratified by the three identified sleep patterns, are presented in Table 1.

Significant differences were observed among the groups. Participants in Sleep Pattern 2 were the oldest, while those in Sleep Pattern 3 were the youngest (p < 0.001). The Sleep Pattern 3 group also had a higher proportion of females, city residents, and individuals with higher education but poorer economic status (all p < 0.01).

Regarding health and lifestyle, the groups differed significantly in exercise habits and the prevalence of hypertension and diabetes (all p < 0.01). Notably, Sleep Pattern 3 had the highest proportion of persistent exercisers but also the highest prevalence of hypertension and diabetes. No significant baseline differences were found for smoking, drinking, labor history, or BMI.

Importantly, significant differences in global cognitive Z scores were already present at baseline, with Sleep Pattern 2 showing the lowest cognitive level (Figure S1 in supporting information). This pattern of lower cognitive performance for the Sleep Pattern 2 group persisted and remained significant in 2011 and 2014, though the differences were no longer significant by 2018.

The longitudinal analysis revealed that Sleep Pattern 2 consistently showed the highest cumulative incidence of dementia, while Sleep Pattern 3 demonstrated the lowest rates throughout the follow‐up period, with statistically significant between‐group differences observed across study waves (Table S3).

For global cognition, a significant overall difference was observed among the three sleep patterns (p for comparison < 0.001). Specifically, Sleep Pattern 2 was associated with the fastest rate of decline, with its trajectory deviating from the cohort's average decline by −0.027 SD per year (95% confidence interval [CI], −0.039 to −0.015; p < 0.001). In contrast, both Sleep Pattern 1 (β = 0.013; 95% CI, 0.0004 to 0.025; p = 0.01) and Sleep Pattern 3 (β = 0.014; 95% CI, 0.002 to 0.026; p = 0.01) showed significantly slower rates of decline compared to the average.

As detailed in Table 2, this pattern of accelerated cognitive decline in the Sleep Pattern 2 group was consistent across all five specific cognitive domains analyzed, as the overall differences among the groups were statistically significant in each domain (all p for comparison < 0.05). The effect was particularly pronounced for orientation (β = −0.027, p < 0.001) and language (β = −0.025, p < 0.001). A significantly faster rate of decline for the Sleep Pattern 2 group was also observed for the domains of registration (β = −0.012, p = 0.003), attention and calculation (β = −0.009, p = 0.03), and recall (β = −0.009, p = 0.01). Conversely, Sleep Pattern 1 and Sleep Pattern 3 showed positive deviations in most cognitive domains. The predicted trajectories, visualized in Figure 3, graphically confirm these findings, illustrating a consistently steeper slope for the Sleep Pattern 2 group across all cognitive domains.

Subgroup analysis

We conducted a series of subgroup analyses to explore whether baseline characteristics modified the association between sleep patterns and the rate of cognitive decline.

First, stratified analyses confirmed that the accelerated cognitive decline associated with Sleep Pattern 2 was a robust finding across most subgroups, including strata of sex, age (≥ 70 years), lower education, and residence (all p for comparison < 0.05, Table S5 in supporting information).

Given that Sleep Pattern 2 consistently emerged as the highest risk group, we then tested for three‐way interactions to identify which factors specifically exacerbated the rate of decline within this group (Figure 4). These tests revealed significant effect modification by sex (p for 3‐way interaction = 0.020) and residence (p for 3‐way interaction < 0.001). Specifically, among individuals in the Sleep Pattern 2 group, the rate of cognitive decline was significantly faster for females than for males, and for city residents compared to those in towns or rural areas. No other significant three‐way interactions were observed.

Sensitivity analyses

To test the robustness of our primary findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by repeating all LLMs using raw MMSE scores instead of Z scores (Figure S2 in supporting information). The results did not substantively alter our conclusions, as the association patterns between sleep patterns and the rates of cognitive decline remained highly consistent with the primary analysis (Table S6 in supporting information); Sleep Pattern 2 was still associated with the fastest rate of decline. Furthermore, stratified analyses using raw scores confirmed that the accelerated cognitive decline associated with Sleep Pattern 2 was a robust finding across most subgroups, with significant overall differences among the sleep patterns observed in key strata such as sex, age (≥ 70 years), and residence (all p for comparison < 0.05, Table S7 in supporting information). Similarly, the forest plot for the subgroup analysis of Sleep Pattern 2 based on raw scores also confirmed significant interaction effects for sex and residence (Figure S3 in supporting information). Overall, these sensitivity analyses enhance the reliability of our main findings.

Sleep pattern and dementia risk

We used Cox proportional hazards models to assess the association between the three identified sleep patterns and the future risk of incident dementia. After adjusting for all baseline covariates, we did not find a statistically significant association between any sleep pattern and the risk of incident dementia (Table S8 in supporting information).

Given that Sleep Pattern 1 is characterized by a return to the healthy sleep range, it was selected as the reference group to evaluate the relative risks of other trajectories. In Model 1, which defined dementia events using cognitive Z scores, the hazard ratio (HR) for Sleep Pattern 2 was 0.83 (95% CI, 0.47–1.47; p = 0.526) and for Sleep Pattern 3 was 1.30 (95% CI, 0.75–2.25; p = 0.342), compared to Sleep Pattern 1 (reference). In the sensitivity analysis (Model 2), which used raw MMSE scores to define the event threshold, the results remained non‐significant, with an HR of 0.85 (95% CI, 0.46–1.56; p = 0.598) for Sleep Pattern 2 and 1.11 (95% CI, 0.60–2.05; p = 0.737) for Sleep Pattern 3.

DISCUSSION

In this large, community‐based cohort of older Chinese adults, our data‐driven analysis identified three distinct long‐term sleep duration patterns. We found that the direction of change in these long‐term trajectories, not just static measurements, is a critical predictor of cognitive outcomes. Our core finding shows that a trajectory of consistently long and increasing sleep was robustly and independently associated with accelerated cognitive decline over a decade. Conversely, trajectories trending toward a healthy, moderate duration—whether by decreasing from a long baseline or increasing from a short one—were associated with more favorable cognitive outcomes. From a public health perspective, these findings establish that monitoring long‐term sleep dynamics is an effective, non‐invasive strategy for identifying older adults at high risk for cognitive decline. Specifically, individuals exhibiting a pattern of persistently long and increasing sleep warrant closer cognitive monitoring and further health assessments.

Our cohort, with a mean baseline age of 78.07 years, had an overall dementia incidence rate of 32.95 per 1000 person‐years. This rate is comparable to findings for populations aged ≥ 80 in some studies, including a rate of 30.4 in another Chinese cohort and 32 in a US‐based study for the 75 to 84 age group. 1 , 27 It is important, however, to acknowledge the considerable heterogeneity in reported incidence rates across populations. For instance, our rate is higher than those from the Global Burden of Disease 2019 data for China (≈ 19 per 1000 person‐years for the 80–84 age group). 28 These variations likely reflect key differences across studies, including cohort‐specific mean age and the diagnostic criteria used for dementia.

Many previous studies have identified sleep patterns using methods that differ fundamentally from our approach. They often relied on subjective, predefined thresholds—an approach that can lead to classification bias by failing to capture more complex, naturally occurring patterns—and were frequently based on only two sleep assessments, which can generate spurious associations due to insufficient sampling. 8 , 13 , 14 , 19 , 20 , 21 In contrast, our study used an objective, data‐driven clustering method using multi‐wave data to provide a more authentic reflection of long‐term sleep dynamics. This approach identified three distinct sleep trajectories: Sleep Pattern 2, characterized by a trajectory moving progressively away from the healthy 6 to 8 hour range, presented the most unfavorable clinical profile (oldest age, lowest baseline cognition, highest dementia incidence). In contrast, both Sleep Pattern 1 and 3 trended toward this healthy range from opposite extremes.

The core finding of our study centers on the longitudinal association between these patterns and the rate of cognitive decline. Our results show that consistently long sleep, particularly with an increasing trend, is significantly associated with accelerated cognitive decline. This finding is consistent not only with numerous studies based on single time‐point sleep data 6 , 7 , 9 but also resonates with the few studies that have used longitudinal data analysis. 8 , 10 For instance, Westwood et al. 8 found that transitioning to long sleep in old age was more strongly associated with dementia than having a stable long sleep habit. Similarly, Cavaillès et al. 10 concluded that individuals who maintained or became long sleepers had a significantly increased risk of dementia, and notably, that sleep duration began to increase as early as 12 years before a clinical diagnosis. Furthermore, our results indicate that this detrimental association is not confined to global cognition but is consistent across all five specific cognitive domains analyzed, aligning with studies that link long sleep to declines in specific domains like registration and recall. 29 However, it is important to note that when we used Cox proportional hazards models to assess the risk of incident dementia as a clinical endpoint, we found no significant association for any sleep pattern. Even after adjusting for all baseline covariates, the dementia risk for the “Consistently Long Sleepers” group did not reach statistical significance. A potential explanation for these findings is that persistently long sleep may not be a direct cause of cognitive decline, but rather an early preclinical manifestation driven by underlying neurodegenerative pathology. 9 , 30 , 31 This hypothesis is also biologically plausible, as existing evidence has linked long sleep with brain atrophy and early degenerative changes in key sleep‐regulating centers of the brain, pathological changes that could collectively lead to a compensatory increase in sleep need. 8 , 32 , 33 Methodologically, this discrepancy also likely reflects the higher sensitivity of LMMs in capturing gradual cognitive deterioration as a continuous process compared to the dichotomous diagnostic endpoints used in Cox models. 34 Additionally, significant findings in previous analyses based on predefined sleep duration thresholds are often driven by extreme sleep durations. Because our data‐driven trajectory evaluates the continuous dynamic process of sleep lengthening over time, rather than artificially isolating individuals with extreme sleep durations, the strong association typically driven by these extreme cases might be attenuated in our survival analysis. Furthermore, given that the “Consistently Long Sleepers” were the oldest group, the competing risk of mortality might have further attenuated the observed dementia risk by censoring high‐risk individuals before diagnosis. 35

Furthermore, the significant cognitive decline in the “Consistently Long Sleepers” group may be confounded by underlying sleep disorders. Because self‐reported data primarily capture “time in bed” rather than restorative sleep, individuals experiencing sleep fragmentation due to sleep disorders often compensate by extending their time in bed, which artificially prolongs their reported sleep trajectories. Importantly, sleep disorders (particularly obstructive sleep apnea) may also independently accelerate cognitive decline. 4 Therefore, these unmeasured sleep disorders likely contribute to both the observed prolonged sleep duration and the detrimental cognitive outcomes.

In contrast to the detrimental association observed for Sleep Pattern 2, our LMM analysis revealed that both the Sleep Pattern 1 and Sleep Pattern 3 exhibited a protective effect on cognitive decline relative to the cohort average. A possible explanation for this is that while these two groups started at opposite extremes, their longitudinal trajectories shared a common, favorable feature: a dynamic trend toward the 6 to 8 hour range widely considered to be healthy. This “dynamic regression” toward a healthy duration may reflect sound physiological regulation or positive lifestyle changes. For instance, such regulation could benefit cognitive function by restoring disrupted circadian rhythms, thereby improving the clearance of neurotoxic proteins and reducing neuroinflammation. 36 This finding suggests that the trajectory of sleep duration, particularly its direction toward or away from a healthy range, is a more informative correlate of cognitive outcomes than either single time‐point measurements 14 , 15 , 16 or simple classifications of change as “increasing” or “decreasing.” 37

Furthermore, our subgroup analyses revealed additional complexity. While stratified analyses confirmed that the harm of Sleep Pattern 2 is a generalized risk factor across diverse demographic groups, three‐way interaction tests identified sex and residence as key modifiers. Within the Sleep Pattern 2 group, cognitive decline was significantly faster for females and for city residents. The stronger association in women is consistent with prior research and may be related to sex‐specific differences in hormone levels, genetic susceptibility, or mental health status. 20 , 38 , 39 , 40 Interestingly, our finding that urban residents experienced faster decline contrasts with most studies reporting urban living as a protective factor. 41 , 42 We speculate this paradoxical result could reflect complex factors like the competing risk of mortality, by which the least healthy individuals in resource‐limited rural areas may die earlier, leaving a relatively healthier group of survivors.

The strengths of this study include its large‐scale prospective cohort design, a long follow‐up period of 10 years, the data‐driven identification of longitudinal sleep patterns, and the use of LMMs capable of precisely analyzing trajectories of change. The prospective design helps establish the temporal sequence between sleep patterns and cognitive decline. The 10‐year follow‐up allowed us to capture long‐term trends rather than short‐term fluctuations. Moreover, our use of a data‐driven K‐means clustering method avoids the subjectivity of arbitrary thresholds and better reflects the data's underlying structure. Finally, the LMMs leveraged all available longitudinal data, providing more precise estimates by handling missing data and simultaneously accounting for individual differences in both baseline levels and rates of change. The supplementary use of Cox models in our dual‐analysis approach allowed us to not only quantify the impact on the rate of decline but also to assess the predictive value for a clinical endpoint, providing more comprehensive evidence for our inferences regarding causal direction.

This study has several limitations. First, our measurements were limited. Regarding the exposure, sleep duration assessment was based solely on a single subjective question (“How many hours do you normally sleep?”), which is subject to recall bias and fails to capture sleep structure, and precludes adjustment for underlying sleep disorders. 43 , 44 Similarly, for the outcome, dementia diagnosis relied on the MMSE screening questionnaire rather than comprehensive clinical diagnostic criteria, risking misclassification, 45 and the absence of biomarkers (e.g., amyloid beta, tau) precluded mechanistic investigation. 46 Second, residual confounding may exist from unmeasured variables like depression or apolipoprotein E genotype, 47 and from not modeling time‐varying covariates. 48 Third, restricting the cohort to participants who survived until 2014 could introduce survival bias, potentially underestimating the association. 49 Finally, our findings from an older Chinese cohort require replication in diverse populations before generalization. 50
