Severe Multi-organ dysfunction secondary to Shigella flexneri encephalopathy (Ekiri Syndrome): a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Ali Alishvandi, Masoud Keikha, Maryam Barancheshemeh, Cena Aram, Raha Azaryan, Fariba Mahmoodi, Chista Sarira Arfaee, Asiyeh Dezhkam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractShigella infection is a major global cause of diarrhea, particularly among children in low- and middle-income countries. While most cases are self-limited, certain strains such as Shigella flexneri can lead to severe systemic complications, including encephalopathy. Among the rarest and most fatal forms is Ekiri syndrome, primarily reported in East Asian literature, characterized by fulminant neurotoxicity, high fever, seizures, and rapid progression to coma or death. In this study, we report the case of a previously healthy 14-year-old girl who presented with vomiting, diarrhea, and low-grade fever, initially treated conservatively. Her condition worsened with progressive lethargy and a generalized seizure, leading to ICU admission. Brain imaging showed diffuse cerebral edema, and her temperature intermittently spiked to 44–45 °C. Laboratory findings revealed marked leukopenia, and stool culture confirmed Shigella flexneri. Despite supportive management including anticonvulsants, empiric antibiotics, and antivirals for suspected CNS infection, her neurologic status continued to deteriorate. As per family request, she was referred to a tertiary center, where her condition rapidly worsened. She developed multi-organ failure with acute kidney injury requiring dialysis, elevated liver enzymes, cardiac dysfunction, and pneumothorax. Despite intensive care, she succumbed to complications after nearly ten days of hospitalization. This case illustrates a fulminant presentation of Shigella-associated encephalopathy consistent with Ekiri syndrome. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in pediatric patients with gastrointestinal and early neurological symptoms. Introduction

Shigellosis is a highly contagious intestinal infection caused by Shigella species, and it remains a major public health concern in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly among young children [1, 2]. Globally, shigellosis is responsible for an estimated 80 million cases of bloody diarrhea and approximately 700,000 deaths each year. Alarmingly, nearly 99% of these infections occur in LMICs, where children under the age of five are most vulnerable (roughly 70% of cases and 60% of related deaths). Despite its severity, fewer than 1% of cases are managed in hospital settings, which may contribute to delays in identifying and treating life-threatening complications [3].

Among the rare but devastating complications of shigellosis is lethal toxic encephalopathy, also known as Ekiri syndrome, which is a fulminant neurologic disorder first recognized in Japanese pediatric populations [4, 5]. This condition is marked by the abrupt onset of high-grade fever, generalized seizures, and altered consciousness, rapidly progressing to cerebral edema, coma, and often death, even in the absence of common triggers such as dehydration, electrolyte imbalances, or hypoglycemia [6]. Although its exact pathophysiology remains incompletely understood, current hypotheses suggest a role for overwhelming systemic inflammation, cytokine-mediated neurotoxicity, and immune dysregulation [7, 8].

Here, we report a fatal case of Shigella flexneri-associated Ekiri syndrome in a previously healthy adolescent girl. Unlike some reports of survival in older children and adults, she developed rapidly progressive multi-organ failure involving the brain, heart, liver, and kidneys despite early antimicrobial and supportive therapy (Fig. 1). This case highlights that Ekiri syndrome can present as a severe systemic emergency, not limited to neurological involvement, and underscores the need for prompt recognition and aggressive management across different age groups.

Case presentation

A 14-year-old previously healthy girl presented to the emergency department with two days of vomiting and abdominal pain followed by non-bloody diarrhea. Her symptoms began suddenly two nights earlier, initially with episodes of vomiting, abdominal pain, then progressing to diarrhea and low-grade fever. She had visited the emergency department once before during this illness, where she was evaluated and received outpatient treatment, resulting in partial improvement. However, as her symptoms worsened over the next day, she returned for further assessment. On arrival, she appeared mildly dehydrated but was fully alert and oriented, with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15. Her vital signs were as follows: blood pressure 90/60 mmHg, heart rate 120 bpm, respiratory rate 28 breaths per minute, temperature 37.8 °C, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. Given her persistent abdominal complaints, she was admitted to the pediatric ward and she underwent routine blood tests, stool exam, stool culture, chest X-ray, and abdominopelvic ultrasonography.

The abdominal ultrasound showed an enlarged appendix diameter, raising suspicion for appendicitis. A surgical consultation was then requested, and a contrast-enhanced CT scan ruled out appendicitis. Empiric intravenous antibiotics, including ceftriaxone, were started early due to the clinical presentation and concern for bacterial infection. Despite appropriate fluid resuscitation to correct mild dehydration, the patient remained febrile and gradually developed increasing lethargy. On day two, she experienced a generalized seizure lasting less than 15 min, characterized by upward gaze and limb rigidity. The seizure episode was followed by a sudden deterioration in mental status, with a GCS score of 6 documented at the intensive care unit (ICU) admission. In consultation with the anesthesiology team, the patient was intubated for airway protection and levetiracetam was initiated for seizure control. On arrival to the ICU, her blood pressure was 90/60 mmHg, and she was started on an epinephrine infusion to maintain adequate perfusion. Brain CT imaging revealed cerebral edema consistent with toxic encephalopathy (Fig. 2). To control intracranial pressure, head-of-bed elevation to 30°, normocapnia-targeted ventilation, and intravenous mannitol were applied as clinically indicated. Lumbar puncture was not performed due to the risk of brain herniation in the setting of cerebral edema, and additionally, the patient’s family declined the procedure due to strong local beliefs. Given the concern for CNS infection and meningitis, ceftriaxone was discontinued and broad empiric coverage with meropenem, vancomycin, and acyclovir was initiated. Notably, although her body temperature at ICU admission was 37.3 °C, the nursing chart documented intermittent spikes reaching as high as 45 °C during the first 24 h. Laboratory tests showed leukopenia with a white blood cell count of 1.7 × 10^3/µL (Table 1). Of note, coagulation testing demonstrated an INR of 2.2, and aPTT of 58 s. Accordingly, the patient received 400 mL of fresh frozen plasma (FFP). A follow-up sample obtained later the same day, after plasma transfusion, showed partial correction with INR 1.7. Given persistent coagulopathy, she was administered a second unit of 400 mL FFP the following day. No active bleeding or invasive procedure was present during this period. Physical examination revealed sluggish pupillary light reflexes, normal lung and heart sounds, normal capillary refill time, and a soft, non-distended abdomen without guarding. Liver and spleen were not palpable. Urine output was approximately 2.2 mL/kg/h.

Despite intensive supportive care, the patient’s neurological status failed to show meaningful improvement. On the third day of her ICU stay, and at the request of her family, she was transferred to a tertiary pediatric referral center. Prior to transfer, norepinephrine was added to epinephrine for additional hemodynamic support, and both were continued at the tertiary center. Following transfer, she developed oliguria with urine output declining to approximately 0.5 mL/kg/h, and serum creatinine rose to 4.7 mg/dL, necessitating acute hemodialysis. Over the course of several days, she developed multiorgan involvement, including acute liver dysfunction, alongside progressive azotemia, and marked elevations in creatine phosphokinase (CPK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) (Table 3). She required daily hemodialysis for acute renal failure. Eventually, she experienced cardiac decompensation (with moderate pleural effusion managed with furosemide), and her clinical course was further complicated by the development of a pneumothorax. Despite all efforts by the multidisciplinary team, the patient died approximately ten days after transfer. Given the fulminant course marked by acute onset seizures, encephalopathy, extreme hyperpyrexia, leukopenia, and eventual multiorgan failure in the context of Shigella flexneri infection, the overall clinical picture was highly suggestive of Ekiri syndrome, which is a rare but fatal encephalopathic complication of shigellosis.

Discussion

Shigella infections are a leading cause of pediatric diarrhea globally, yet severe neurological complications like encephalopathy remain uncommon and often overlooked. When these complications arise, they tend to progress rapidly and can be challenging to manage despite timely diagnosis and treatment. Our case demonstrates that even with early antibiotic therapy and supportive care, Shigella flexneri-associated encephalopathy can follow an aggressive course. Such severe manifestations underscore the limitations of current management approaches and emphasize the need for heightened clinical awareness and timely intervention. However, early identification may not always alter the ultimate outcome.

In terms of the pathophysiology of Shigella-associated encephalopathy, the exact mechanisms remain unclear. Interestingly, the production of Shiga toxin is not considered essential in all cases [9, 10]. While Shiga toxin is produced by Shigella dysenteriae type 1, other species such as S. flexneri and S. sonnei do not generate this toxin, yet they can still cause severe neurological manifestations. One consistent finding in affected patients is the early onset of cerebral edema. This is accompanied by a marked increase in inflammatory mediators, including cytokines like tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 (IL-1), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), both in the bloodstream and cerebrospinal fluid during the acute phase [8, 11]. Experimental studies in animal models support the hypothesis that an exaggerated host immune response, rather than direct bacterial toxins alone, plays a pivotal role in driving the brain inflammation and injury observed in these cases [7].

While Ekiri syndrome is typically associated with a fulminant and fatal course, rare instances of survival have been documented. One such case involved a 3-year-old child who presented with seizure, drowsiness, and diarrhea, but without dehydration. Early brain imaging revealed diffuse cerebral edema, yet the patient showed remarkable neurological recovery within 48 h following anticonvulsant therapy and empiric antibiotic treatment with ceftriaxone. Stool culture confirmed Shigella flexneri, and follow-up CT imaging demonstrated significant resolution of the edema [12]. This exceptional outcome highlights the potential reversibility of neurologic manifestations in Ekiri syndrome when timely intervention is initiated. In contrast, our patient deteriorated rapidly despite similarly early empirical treatment, suggesting that factors such as age, immune status, virulence of the strain, or extent of initial CNS injury may critically influence the prognosis. Notably, survival has also been documented in an adult patient with Shigella-associated encephalopathy, suggesting that favorable outcomes may hinge less on age and more on timely diagnosis and targeted therapy. In a report by Schuster et al., a 45-year-old man with acute onset of fever, diarrhea, and neurological dysfunction recovered fully following the identification of Shigella flexneri in stool culture and the initiation of appropriate antibiotics [13]. Despite the rarity of such cases in adults, this example highlights that early clinical suspicion and prompt antimicrobial intervention may play a more critical role in prognosis than demographic factors alone.

Recent evidence suggests that Shigella encephalopathy can involve more than just the CNS. A study reported cardiac involvement in nearly 75% of affected children, with one fatality linked to shock and arrhythmia. Notably, shock was identified as a significant risk factor for cardiac complications (P = 0.024) [14]. In our patient, the progression to multi-organ failure and clinical suspicion of cardiac dysfunction mirrors these findings. These observations emphasize that in severe cases of Shigella infection, especially those with neurologic symptoms, clinicians should remain vigilant for systemic inflammatory responses and perform early cardiac monitoring.

To better frame our case within the existing literature, we focus on the youngest and oldest reported patients with Ekiri syndrome. The youngest, a 2.5-year-old girl from Albania infected with S. flexneri, presented with seizures, high fever, hyponatremia, and cerebral edema. She required anticonvulsants, hyperosmolar therapy, corticosteroids, and supportive ventilation, and ultimately recovered [5]. The oldest, a 71-year-old woman from the U.S. with S. sonnei, developed altered mental status, diarrhea, abdominal tenderness, and progression to shock, necessitating intensive care with hemodynamic support and empiric antimicrobial coverage, and she also survived [15]. This study has several important limitations. Firstly, although cerebrospinal fluid analysis would have provided critical diagnostic information, lumbar puncture was contraindicated due to signs of increased intracranial pressure and decreased level of consciousness, which increase the risk of brain herniation. Having said that, we initiated broad-spectrum antibiotic and antiviral treatments. Secondly, advanced cardiac evaluations and detailed immunological studies were not performed because of urgent clinical priorities and resource limitations, which restricted a full understanding of its pathophysiology and the extent of multi-organ involvement.

Conclusion

This case emphasizes how difficult it can be to diagnose Ekiri syndrome early, and shows the need for careful clinical assessment and timely tests to catch this rare but serious complication of shigellosis. Interestingly, our patient developed leukopenia only after being admitted to the ICU, highlighting the importance of closely watching laboratory trends over time. When a patient suddenly develops very high fever, seizures, and altered mental status following gastrointestinal symptoms, Ekiri syndrome should be on every clinician’s radar. Being aware of these red flags can help doctors spot the diagnosis earlier and provide the targeted supportive care.
