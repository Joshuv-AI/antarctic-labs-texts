Reversible, Noninflammatory ST-Segment Elevation in Cardiac Tamponade
Authors: Zhong-Qun Zhan
Published: 2026

Acute concave ST-segment elevation is usually ascribed to either myocardial infarction or pericarditis. Ng et al1 described a 44-year-old man who presented with global ST-segment elevation 5 months after losing a hypodermic needle during intravenous drug use. Emergency angiography revealed patent coronaries but a radiopaque 25-mm needle abutting the right ventricular free wall, respiratory systolic swings approximately 18 mm Hg, and a large hemorrhagic pericardial effusion. Aspiration of 500 mL of blood produced instant normalization of blood pressure and ST segments.1 The case crystallizes a key teaching point: mechanical tamponade can generate reversible, noninflammatory ST-segment elevation that perfectly mimics acute myocardial infarction or pericarditis.

The proposed mechanistic spectrum includes the following:

1.Reduced coronary driving pressure: elevated intrapericardial pressure lowers the aortic-ventricular diastolic gradient, causing coronary artery perfusion drop.2

2.Mechanical epicardial constraint: focal compression of the thin-walled right ventricular produces local stretch currents.3

3.Catecholamine surge (in profound shock) may precipitate multivessel vasospasm.4

4.Atrial wall stretch by rapid effusion causes PR-segment deviation and Spodick’s sign without true inflammation.1,5

The heterogeneous electrocardiographic distribution (inferior versus diffuse) may reflect the balance between global perfusion deficit and local compression: when compression dominates, inferior leads are preferentially affected; when global driving pressure falls, multiple territories are involved.

Whenever coronary angiography shows patent epicardial vessels, take a second look at the fluoro screen for radiolucent pericardial bands and roll the ultrasound probe to the bedside immediately. Hold off on heparin or dual antiplatelet therapy until a definite occlusive lesion is demonstrated—unnecessary antithrombotics only add bleeding risk in a patient who may need urgent drainage. Pericardiocentesis doubles as diagnosis and therapy: watch the ST segments flatten and the blood pressure rise as the needle exits the pericardium, proving the mechanism in real time.

The case of Ng et al1 epitomizes a growing literature: acute pericardial hypertension, whether from blood, pus, or external compression, can produce rapidly reversible ST-segment elevation by purely mechanical-ischemic means. Recognizing this entity protects patients from unnecessary antithrombotic therapy and expedites life-saving drainage.
