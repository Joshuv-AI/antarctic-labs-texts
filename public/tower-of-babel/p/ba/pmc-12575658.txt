Diagnostic assistance method for RR-TB/MDR-TB patients under treatment based on CNN-LSTM
Authors: Jie Li, Wenqing Wu, Zhixiong Fang, Pinxiang Fu, Hua Huang, Yanhong Zhou, Liang Yu, He Huang, Tao Wang, Qilong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rapid development of deep learning has promoted its application in disease diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis prediction. Medical imaging plays a crucial role in the management of rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis/multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB/MDR-TB). In particular, chest computed tomography (CT) scans offer detailed lung images that can reveal subtle features. In this study, we propose a Convolutional Neural Network-Long Short-Term Memory (CNN-LSTM) model to predict treatment outcomes in RR-TB/MDR-TB patients, aiming to support clinicians in timely adjustment of therapeutic strategies and improving treatment success. The model integrates CNN for image feature extraction with LSTM for sequential analysis of patient monitoring data, including two types of immune detection indicators (T-cell subsets and peripheral blood tuberculosis-related CD161-positive cells). Transfer learning with weight initialization was applied to enhance model performance, and three backbone architectures (DenseNet201 + ABC, ResNet-50 + ABC, CheXNet + ABC) were compared to assess their impact on predictive accuracy. Experimental results demonstrated that the CNN-LSTM model with DenseNet201 + ABC as the backbone achieved the highest accuracy in predicting subsequent treatment indicators. These findings demonstrated the feasibility and effectiveness of using CNN-LSTM for treatment outcome prediction in RR-TB/MDR-TB, and highlighted its potential to assist clinicians in precision-tailoring treatment plans, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy and offering both theoretical and practical value in healthcare.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), a highly contagious disease primarily affecting the lungs, remains a major global health challenge. Common clinical symptoms include persistent cough, chest pain, and weight loss, all of which significantly impact patient well-being1,2. Without timely and adequate treatment, TB can progress to severe complications like TB meningitis and miliary TB, both of which are associated with high mortality. The management of TB has become increasingly complex due to the emergence of drug-resistant strains of Mycobacterium tuberculosis. In particular, multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB), defined by resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampicin, leads to prolonged treatment duration, higher failure rates, greater transmission risk, and significantly elevated mortality compared to drug-sensitive TB 3–5. Treating MDR-TB is especially challenging due to the need for expensive and less effective medications, which are often associated with serious side effects. These factors contribute to poor adherence, treatment failure, and the potential emergence of extensively drug-resistant TB 6. Additionally, delayed or inaccurate diagnosis can further exacerbate the spread of the disease, particularly in low-resource settings, where timely access to advanced diagnostics and treatment is limited7. Early prediction of disease progression and personalized treatment strategies are thus critical for improving clinical outcomes and controlling the spread of MDR-TB.

In recent years, deep learning has shown great promise in medical applications, offering improved accuracy and automation in tasks such as image analysis, disease prediction, and decision support8,9. Prior studies have utilized deep learning models for TB detection using a chest X-ray (CXR)10–12. While CXR is a widely accessible imaging modality, computed tomography (CT) scans offer more detailed cross-sectional images and are particularly useful for identifying subtle pulmonary abnormalities13,14. Despite higher costs and radiation exposure, CT imaging plays an increasingly important role in TB management by providing richer diagnostic information.

Advances in deep learning architectures such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks have enabled the fusion of spatial and temporal features from multimodal medical data15–17. CNNs are effective in extracting spatial features from images, while LSTMs are well-suited for modeling time-dependent clinical data. These architectures have been successfully combined for medical forecasting tasks such as disease progression modeling and treatment response prediction18–21.

Optimization algorithms such as the Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) algorithm further enhance deep learning models by fine-tuning hyperparameters and avoiding local optima during training22–24. Such hybrid approaches are particularly valuable in healthcare scenarios involving complex and noisy biological data.

Accordingly, this paper proposed a deep learning–based prediction method to estimate future immune indicator values in patients undergoing treatment for rifampicin-resistant or multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB/MDR-TB). Rather than focusing on lesion detection from images, the primary objective is to predict the outcomes of two clinical test items—T cell subset classification and peripheral blood CD161 + immune cell detection—in the next treatment cycle. In the proposed framework, the CNN component extracts spatial features from chest CT scans, which are then combined with historical laboratory reports to form the input sequence for the LSTM component, enabling the model to capture temporal dependencies across treatment stages. To further improve predictive performance, transfer learning is applied to the CNN, and three backbone architectures (Dense201 + ABC25, ResNet-50 + ABC26, and ChexNet + ABC27 are evaluated for comparative analysis.

Method

This paper presents a hybrid neural network model based on CNN and LSTM, which utilizes the previous treatment reports of RR-TB/MDR-TB patients and CT scans to predict relevant indicators for the next treatment session, approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangxi Province. Among this model, CNN is employed for extracting features from patients’ lung images, while LSTM is used to capture the temporal dependencies in the patients’ historical testing data. This analysis aims to achieve the automated prediction of treatment outcomes. To evaluate the performance of the proposed model, the dataset was randomly divided into training, validation, and test sets with an 8:1:1 ratio. Mean Squared Error (MSE) was adopted as the training loss function, and both MSE and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) were used as evaluation metrics. The model was implemented in Python 3.6 using TensorFlow 2.1 with Keras as backend, and trained on an NVIDIA RTX 2070 GPU with 16 GB memory. The Adam optimizer was applied with an initial learning rate of 0.001, batch size of 32, and up to 100 epochs. Each experiment was repeated three times, and the average results were reported to ensure robustness. Transfer learning

Transfer learning is a widely used technique in deep learning that allows a model trained on one domain to be adapted for a related task in another domain, specially when labeled data are limited. In this study, transfer learning was applied to the CNN component of the proposed CNN-LSTM framework. Specifically, the backbone networks (DenseNet201, ResNet50, and CheXNet) were initialized with weights pretrained on the Shenzhen public chest X-ray dataset for TB detection, which contains 326 normal and 336 abnormal images provided by Shenzhen No.3 Hospital, Guangdong Province, China28. Although our primary task involves CT scans, prior studies have shown that pretraining on X-ray images can provide useful low-level feature representations (e.g., edges, textures, and lung structures), which can be further fine-tuned for CT-based applications29. During model training, we adopted a fine-tuning strategy in which the early convolutional layers were partially frozen to preserve generic feature representations, while the higher-level layers and fully connected layers were retrained on our CT dataset with a reduced learning rate. This approach effectively leveraged knowledge from a related imaging modality to improve convergence speed and predictive performance in the target CT-based task. CNN-LSTM network

CNN is used as a feature extractor to capture spatial patterns from the chest CT scans of RR-TB/MDR-TB patients. Three backbone networks—DenseNet201, ResNet50, and CheXNet—were evaluated, each with an input size of 224 × 224 × 3 and output size of 1 × 1. All CNNs were initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights and subsequently fine-tuned on our dataset to adapt to the imaging characteristics of TB patients. The final convolutional outputs of each backbone were passed through a global average pooling layer, generating a one-dimensional feature vector (e.g., 1024 dimensions for DenseNet201) that encodes disease-relevant imaging information. This feature vector was concatenated with patient metadata (age, gender) and laboratory test indicators to form a multimodal feature representation, serving as the input to the LSTM module.

LSTM, a type of recurrent neural network (RNN), was employed to model the temporal dependencies of sequential treatment data. Dataset

In our study, the imaging dataset was categorized into four types of radiological manifestations: exuding shadows, nodule, cavity, exuding shadows with nodule (three separate and one coupled phenomena). The original dataset contained 579 images of exuding shadows, 683 of nodules, 564 of cavities, and 656 of exuding shadows with nodules. To mitigate data imbalance and increase the training sample size, standard data augmentation techniques (including horizontal/vertical flipping, small-angle rotation, and contrast adjustment) were applied, resulting in an expanded dataset of approximately 5,000 images with preserved class distribution. Representative examples of these chest CT images are shown in Fig. 4.

The method is evaluated on two dataset which contain two types of test items: the broad classification of T cell subsets and the peripheral blood tuberculosis-related immune cell CD161 detection, respectively. The dataset was obtained from Jiangxi Chest Hospital, Jiangxi providence, China, under ethical approval.

T cell subsets

The analysis of T-cell subsets carries substantial significance in understanding and managing TB. In this study, a dataset consisting of 1,051 testing reports was collected over a two-month period from patients undergoing treatment for RR-TB/MDR-TB. Each report contained (i) a chest CT scan, (ii) results of four immune indicators (CD4, CD8, CD3 counts, and CD4/CD8 ratio), and (iii) patient metadata including age and gender. Each record was treated as an independent sample, with values subsequently normalized before being used as model input.

Peripheral blood tuberculosis-related immune cell CD161 detection

The peripheral blood immune cell marker CD161 has been associated with altered expression in TB patients compared to healthy controls, making it a potentially valuable biomarker. For this task, we collected 607 testing reports over a two-month period. Each report included (i) a chest CT scan, (ii) five immune indicators—calculate retention factor (RF%), CD45 + cell counts, CD45 + CD3 + lymphocytes proportion, CD45 + CD64 + lymphocyte proportion, and CD161 + cell proportion—as well as (iii) patient age and gender. All features were preprocessed by Min-Max normalization prior to input into the CNN-LSTM model.

Data pre-processing

Data normalization was applied to ensure that all input features were on a compatible scale, which is essential for stable model training and convergence in regression tasks30. In this study, Min-Max normalization was applied to all numerical clinical features. Each feature value x was rescaled to the range [0, 1] using the following formula:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^{\prime} = \frac{{x - \min (x)}}{{\max (x) - \min (x)}}$$\end{document}

where x is the original value, x′ is the normalized value, min(x) is the minimum value in the data, and max(x) is the maximum value in the data.

Gender, being categorical, was encoded as a binary variable. CT images were preprocessed separately by resizing to 224 × 224 pixels and rescaling pixel intensities to [0, 1] before being fed into the CNN backbone. The normalized clinical features and encoded demographic data were subsequently concatenated with the CNN feature vector and used as input to the LSTM component.

Results

As indicated in Xu et al.31, medical imaging models such as Dense201 and ChexNet have demonstrated notable recognition performance for tuberculosis identification. Building upon these findings, we evaluated four network configurations using our custom dataset: Dense201 enhanced with ABC algorithm, ResNet50 with ABC, ChexNet with ABC, and the standard ChexNet model. The combination with the ABC algorithm was intended to optimize the feature extraction and recognition ability of each backbone.

Figures 5 and 6 present the training and validation loss curves for the two clinical test items. Among all configurations, Dense201 + ABC demonstrated the fastest convergence and achieved the lowest loss across training and validation, indicating superior stability and predictive capacity. Table 1 summarizes the quantitative performance across different CT manifestations. Based on the average MSE values, DenseNet201 + ABC consistently outperformed the other three models. The lowest MSE was observed in the isolated nodule scenario, where predictions were most accurate, whereas the coupled exuding shadow with nodule scenario posed the greatest challenge, leading to relatively higher errors for all models.

Discussion

Deep learning has notably advanced image recognition and medical decision support in recent years. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have proven effective in capturing complex patterns from medical images32–34. In clinical applications, researchers are increasingly leveraging computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) systems to reduce cost and time while improving diagnostic accuracy35. Building on this progress, our study aimed to improve the accuracy of treatment outcome predictions for RR-TB/MDR-TB patients by developing a hybrid CNN-LSTM framework. By combining CT-derived imaging features with historical immune testing results, our model was designed to forecast future treatment indicators and provide physicians with a dynamic decision-support tool.

Compared with previous studies focusing primarily on TB detection from chest imaging alone10–12, our approach offers several advantages. First, by integrating CT imaging features with laboratory immune indicators, our model provides a more comprehensive representation of patient condition and achieves higher predictive accuracy in treatment-related outcomes. Second, the incorporation of ABC algorithm into the Dense201 backbone improved optimization and convergence, resulting in superior performance relative to conventional CNN models. Most importantly, the lightweight design of LTS-Net achieves approximately 90% reduction in parameters and FLOPs compared to heavier baselines, without sacrificing predictive accuracy. This efficiency translates into shorter inference times and lower hardware requirements, making the model particularly suitable for deployment in resource-constrained settings. A benchmark test on a standard CPU showed that the average inference time per image was approximately ~ 100 ms, indicating its feasibility for near real-time screening in community hospitals or mobile diagnostic units.

Despite these advantages, certain limitations must be acknowledged in comparison with prior works. One limitation lies in model interpretability. While Grad-CAM was used to visualize feature attention, the highlighted regions exhibited patterns that may not directly correspond to pathological areas, and these were not validated by radiologists. This restricts the clinical reliability of our visual explanations. Future work should incorporate publicly available annotated datasets containing bounding boxes or segmentation masks to enable spatial overlap analysis and improve interpretability. In addition, clearer overlays with radiological annotations would further enhance the clinical relevance of the visualizations. Nevertheless, given the engineering background of our team and limited access to radiologists, we emphasize that the current focus of our study is primarily on predictive performance rather than explainability.

Another consideration is the framing of the classification task. While our current four-class setting (TB, pneumonia, COVID-19, and normal) is academically valuable, it may not align perfectly with real-world clinical workflows in primary care settings. A more clinically relevant formulation is binary classification—separating normal from abnormal cases (combining TB, pneumonia, and COVID-19)—to serve as a triage tool. Future work will therefore restructure the task to this binary setting and evaluate model performance using sensitivity, specificity, and area under the ROC curve (AUC), which are more appropriate for clinical screening scenarios.

Study limitations also include the single-center retrospective design with potential selection bias, relatively small sample size (n = 1,051 for T-cell subsets), and short follow-up period (2 months). In addition, the lack of external validation cohort restricts the generalizability of the findings. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up ill be essential to further validate and extend the applicability of our model.

In summary, the proposed CNN-LSTM model integrating CT features with immune testing indicators demonstrates significant promise for predicting treatment outcomes in RR-TB/MDR-TB patients. Compared with prior approaches, it offers improvements in predictive accuracy, computational efficiency, and adaptability across different test items. At the same time, limitations in clinical interpretability, dataset diversity, and task framing highlight the need for further research. Assuming the availability of sufficiently diverse datasets, this approach has the potential to reduce treatment costs, streamline clinical workflows, and improve patient recovery outcomes, particularly in resource-limited healthcare settings.
