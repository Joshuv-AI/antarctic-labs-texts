A qualitative exploration of the factors influencing physical activity engagement among Black individuals with heart failure in South Florida
Authors: Tamira B Prince, Kathryn E Roach, Neva J Kirk-Sanchez, Meryl I Cohen, Lawrence P Cahalin, Sannisha K Dale
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlack people in the United States have the highest rates of heart failure, and the worst outcomes of any racial/ethnic group which has been linked to both sociostructural (e.g. racism, poverty, food deserts) and behavioral factors. Physical activity is vital in managing heart failure, yet little is known about the perceptions of the Black community with heart failure. The purpose of this study was to understand and interpret physical activity engagement and the factors that may influence it in Black individuals with heart failure.MethodsThis study used a thematic analysis approach. One-on-one semi-structured interviews of eight Black individuals with heart failure addressed physical activity habits, health beliefs, health literacy, medical mistrust, depression, self-efficacy, health disparities, social support, barriers and facilitators. Line-by-line coding was conducted, and themes were developed.ResultsThe four key themes identified were 1. I don’t understand what heart failure is; 2. I was told that physical activity would not hurt; 3. I struggled when treated by providers from different cultures; 4. Having heart failure means I don’t have long to live. All eight participants acknowledged that physical activity was important but reported being underactive. Most could not describe what heart failure was and thus could not explain the importance of physical activity in managing heart failure. Physical activity instruction was not emphasized by healthcare providers, which likely contributed to an undervaluation of physical activity and to underactivity. Cultural differences between patients and clinicians had potential to result in non-adherence to recommendations.ConclusionsHealthcare providers should strive for adequate patient literacy about heart failure, and educate on the importance of physical activity, ensuring the appropriate level of instruction is provided so that Black individuals with heart failure are informed to best address their health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24945-w.Keywords: Heart failure, Physical activity, Black individuals, Barriers, Health beliefs

Background

Heart failure (HF) is a condition with significant impact on individuals, society and the healthcare system. Prevalence in the United States (US) was approximately 6.5 million people in 2014 and is projected to rise to more than 8 million people by 2030 [1, 2]. HF disproportionately affects older adults with markedly higher prevalence in adults 60 years and older as compared to younger adults and children [2]. In 2019, HF resulted in over eight million physician office visits [2]. In 2021, HF was the primary reason for over 1.2 million hospitalizations [2]. HF-associated medical costs were estimated at over $24,000 per patient, annually [2]. It is estimated that 20–25% of HF patients are readmitted within thirty days of discharge after a HF hospitalization [1, 3–7]. Individuals with HF, who have a lower functional status have an increased risk of hospital readmission [1].

Physical activity is important for maintenance of function and for patient self-management of HF [8]. Physical activity can improve lung function and circulation, increase exercise capacity to allow for better daily functioning and thereby improve quality of life and longevity [6, 9]. Despite this knowledge, many patients with HF have been shown to be less physically active than recommended [6, 10–12]. There are many factors that impact the participation in physical activity by patients with HF. Over the course of the condition, exercise intolerance and other factors may cause patients with HF to become more sedentary and experience greater functional mobility deficits and limitations in activities of daily living, creating a cycle of HF exacerbations due to lack of physical activity [6, 13, 14]. Promotion of physical activity in this group through education and training in all phases of the condition is an imperative.

Engagement in physical activity by this population is likely in part mediated by health beliefs. The Health Belief Model is one theoretical framework that can be used to explain why a person may decide to engage or not engage in a health behavior [15]. Core concepts of this theory can be modified from assessing attitudes toward disease prevention, to instead evaluate attitudes toward adopting positive health behaviors, like physical activity for HF management, as demonstrated in Table 1.

Modification of the health belief model

Perceptions and self-efficacy in this model are all influenced by modifying factors/variables that can be demographic, like age or education level, or sociopsychological, like personality type or susceptibility to peer pressure [15]. One study utilized the Health Belief Model to tailor education on self-care, including physical activity, in HF with positive outcomes [16].

In the US, several studies have demonstrated that HF disproportionally impacts Black people. Black people experience the highest prevalence and incidence of HF [2]. African Americans tend to be younger than their White counterparts by almost 10 years [17]. Early HF presentation, which is onset at less than 50-years-old, is 20 times higher in African Americans than it is in White people [17]. Death rates from HF in the US also display racial differences. Black men and women have higher mortality rates than their White counterparts [18]. After a hospitalization, Black men perform worse on functional performance measures than White men [19]. They also score lower on quality-of-life measures [19, 20].

Some of these differences between the races can be explained at a biologic/genetic level but social determinants also contribute significantly to the disparities seen in HF prevalence and outcomes [21–23]. Structural racism, medical racism, food deserts, poverty, neighborhoods without safe spaces to exercise, and environmental pollution are examples [21]. Culture, education and health literacy, depression, self-efficacy, health beliefs and social support networks may also influence how the patient with HF values or recognizes the importance of physical activity in managing HF [24–33]. Also important is the recognition that in the HF population, there is a fear that physical activity will lead to an exacerbation of symptoms [34]. Economic factors, such as income level and time constraints due to need to work multiple jobs, and severity of the condition are likely to contribute to a person’s ability to engage in physical activity as well [35].

Trust in the healthcare provider has been positively correlated with positive health behaviors [36], however high levels of medical mistrust have been reported by Black individuals [37–39]. Minoritized and marginalized groups may demonstrate greater mistrust due to a history of injustices [37, 38, 40]. Alternatively, Black individuals may have experienced discrimination personally or have been ill-served due to systemic racism within healthcare institutions [39]. One recent study reported that racial and gender congruence between patient and physician resulted in improved patient satisfaction [41]. This congruence is likely an important factor in the development of trust in the healthcare provider and therefore will positively influence patient adherence to health recommendations, like physical activity. Unfortunately, there is an underrepresentation of Black people in health professions, with Black people comprising over 12% of the US population but only 5% of physicians and physical therapists [42–44]. This imbalance may result in less opportunities for culturally congruent care and more challenges for Black persons to adhere to physical activity recommendations. Because of the multifactorial nature of the ability to adopt a physically active lifestyle, it is important to hear and learn from Black persons living with HF to understand the issue in-depth.

Historically, scientific publications classified Black people into one category. However, there is growing evidence and recognition that African Americans, African immigrants and Afro-Caribbean individuals may have differing risk profiles and thus different health outcomes, for HF, as well as other conditions [22, 23]. South Florida, due to its location, has a high prevalence of foreign-born residents from Latin America, which according to the US Census, includes the Caribbean. In Miami-Dade County, which comprises the largest population within the South Florida region, 54.8% of the population is foreign born, with 93% of those being from Latin America [45]. This locale thus provides a unique opportunity to explore the experiences of both African Americans and Afro-Caribbean individuals.

Prior studies have provided some information about physical activity in the HF population, but they utilized a quantitative approach, collecting information via medical chart reviews, demographic questionnaires, and surveys [12]. Prior qualitative works in this patient population reflect themes focused on the participants’ history of physical activity, with less emphasis on contributors to the behavior [46]. A qualitative approach can provide a richer understanding with opportunities for more open communication with an interviewer who is like the participants racially. A greater understanding of the motivators and barriers to consistent engagement in a physically active lifestyle may help to bridge the divide between patient and healthcare provider perspectives on the issue. Additionally, it may allow healthcare providers to use some of the motivators identified to support their patients in their physical activity efforts and help the providers to ask the right questions to identify and eliminate barriers to engaging in this health behavior. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to describe the lived experience of physical activity engagement and the factors that may influence it in community-dwelling Black individuals with HF.

Methods

Research design

This study adopted a thematic analysis approach, as described by Braun and Clarke [47, 48]. This method allows for exploration to understand experiences and behaviors of groups [48]. It was of particular interest to understand what value this population placed in physical activity to manage their HF. The data was interpreted using a social constructivism framework [48, 49]. In this study, physical activity was defined as any movement of the body that requires energy expenditure greater than that used in the performance of basic activities of daily living (e.g., bathing, dressing, eating), modifying the standard definition to capture moderate to vigorous intensity activities as recommended for the HF population and exclude low intensity activity [50].

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the University of Miami’s Institutional Review Board (IRB #20220462).

Researcher stance

The first author was also the sole interviewer and the primary data analyst. She has worked as a physical therapist in hospitals in South Florida for over 16 years and became interested in this topic because she noticed that patients with HF were at times being readmitted to the hospital within a short time. It made her begin to wonder if there are things that healthcare providers, can do better to help patients avoid readmissions. Her professional training as a Doctor of Physical Therapy provided her with knowledge about the benefits of physical activity in managing HF, and her experience provided her with some insight into some of the reasons why people report not being physically active. The COVID-19 pandemic made it publicly evident that there are significant disparities in health and health services in this country based on race, and review of the epidemiology of HF revealed that Black people experience disparities in health related to HF as well. As a person from the Afro-Caribbean community, this prompted the first author to want to interview patients in the Black community to understand their perspective on this issue. This was disclosed to participants in the interview preamble. See Additional file 1.

At the time the study was conducted, the interviewer was a PhD candidate, who had taken graduate-level coursework in qualitative study methods.

Study population and recruitment

The target sample size for the study was 24, representing 4 subgroups: African American men, African American women, Caribbean men and Caribbean women. The researcher had no prior relationship with the participants. Participants for the study were first recruited through the University of Miami Health System (UHealth) Registry. This registry is sourced from the electronic medical record system and consists of people who have had any medical care performed though UHealth and agreed to be contacted for research purposes. UHealth represents one of the largest health systems in the South Florida region and serves the local and global community. After IRB approval was obtained, a contact list was requested that was tailored based on the study inclusion and exclusion criteria. The contact list was furnished via database system (REDCap ™, Nashville TN). Purposive nonprobability consecutive sampling was used on the contact list of 124 potential participants. Participants were contacted via telephone and screened for eligibility using a standard script. If unable to reach the potential participant with 3 call attempts on different days and times, the investigator excluded that contact from further recruitment efforts. See Additional file 2. Recruitment via this method occurred between April 3, 2023, and October 3, 2023.

Secondarily, once the recruitment pool via the registry was exhausted, recruitment was conducted through the UHealth Cardiac and Pulmonary Rehabilitation Department. The manager of the department reviewed the files of active and past patients seen in the department and provided a list of potential candidates based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Active patients were approached by department staff who were oriented to the nature of the study, and patients were given a study flyer with contact information. All the potential participants identified were called by the investigator and screened to determine their interest and eligibility in the same manner as was done for the participants sourced from the UHealth Registry. Recruitment via this method occurred between October 20, 2023, and November 6, 2023.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were included if they were community-dwelling, 60–85-year-old Black cisgender males and females with a medical diagnosis of HF, could communicate in and understand verbal and written English, and willing to participate in one audio-recorded interview. Participants were excluded if they had difficulty recalling HF or physical activity history, were a resident in a nursing home or currently hospitalized or had co-morbidities that may preclude them from participating in regular physical activity. Comorbidities that would exclude someone from participation included severe osteoarthritis or other joint pathology, neurological condition resulting in motor/sensory weakness, limb amputation, or a condition that significantly limits respiratory capacity. The investigator used scripted screening questions and asked relevant follow-up questions as needed to determine eligibility.

Interview development

The interview protocol was designed to use a one-on-one approach to address the research question and last approximately 60 min. It was a semi-structured interview format comprised of 15 open-ended questions, with probes utilized only when needed for clarification and to elicit more detail. The interview questions were developed to address the aims of this study and were not derived from any prior interview protocol. In addition to determining current and prior physical activity levels, questions were designed to address key components that could influence participation in the health behavior of physical activity, based on prior knowledge of the researcher and on review of previous literature. Health beliefs, health literacy, medical mistrust, depression, self-efficacy, health disparities, social support and other barriers and facilitators were discussed. See Additional file 1.

Study procedures

Once eligibility was determined via the telephone screening protocol, potential participants were emailed a link to the E-consent via REDCap. After the e-consent was signed, intake information was gathered either via the participant entering responses into an online intake form in REDCap, or via telephone call with the investigator inputting the information. Intake information included basic demographics like gender, age, ethnicity, cultural background, educational attainment, employment, income, zip code, and insurance status; and health information like age at time of the HF diagnosis, hospitalizations, cardiac rehabilitation participation status, and comorbidities that can limit physical activity and their severity. See Additional file 3.

One-time Interviews were then scheduled and conducted by the first author remotely via a secure telehealth platform (Doximity Dialer ™, San Francisco, CA). The decision to have remote interviews was in consideration of the possible burden of travel, as well as concern for possible COVID-19 exposure in this high-risk group. No other individuals were present during the interview. Consent to be audio recorded was obtained as part of the e-consent and was verbally restated on the recording. The researcher avoided using the participant’s name during the interview. The interviews were audio-recorded (Microsoft Voice Recorder ™, Seattle WA) for later transcription. At the conclusion of their interview, participants were sent a $25 electronic gift card.

During the interview, brief notes were taken by the researcher to ensure appropriate follow-up questions were asked during the interview. Immediately following each interview, the researcher performed memo-writing to reflect on the content and quality of the interview. These memos were stored and maintained using a qualitative data analysis program, NVivo Release 1 (NVivo ™, Burlington, MA).

Analysis

Audio recordings of participant interviews were transcribed using NVivo Transcription service (NVivo ™, Burlington, MA). Data management and analysis were done using NVivo qualitative software. Line-by-line coding of each transcript was performed. Data-rich statements were highlighted, and comments were made with simple code. The interview transcripts were analyzed multiple times. Emerging themes were identified based on the codes.

Additional steps to enhance rigor

Interview questions were designed collaboratively by the first and senior authors, with input from the other co-authors to ensure that they were appropriately structured to avoid creating bias. Interviews were conducted remotely via a secure telehealth platform, in accordance with HIPAA requirements. Audio recordings were stored on the University’s secure server prior to transcription. Once the transcripts were returned and verified against the audio recording, the recordings were deleted as per the IRB-approved study protocol. The first author/interviewer was the only study member with access to the transcripts, which were also stored on the University’s secure server.

The first author engaged in reflexivity to identify biases in the data analysis and coding of the transcripts. Additionally, the first author held frequent consultations with other senior members of the research team during the thematic analysis, providing excerpts of the transcripts and having discussions about the interpretation of the data to ensure trustworthiness. Member checking was used to validate the analysis once themes were determined. Participants were provided via e-mail with a written interpretation explaining the theme along with excerpts from their transcripts that captured the theme. In the email, participants were instructed that they had two weeks to respond if they had differing views on the interpretation. Approval of the researcher’s interpretation was assumed if there was no response from the participant after two weeks.

Results

Nine participants initiated the e-consent process, however one participant, Subject 6, never completed the consent and became unreachable by telephone. Of the 8 participants interviewed, 7 were female. Two of the females identified as Afro-Caribbean while the others identified as African American. The one male in the study was African American. Interviews lasted 45 min on average. The themes presented are believed to have achieved saturation with participants reporting similarities in experiences and attitudes regarding health beliefs, health literacy, and challenges in healthcare interactions. Thematic analysis

I don’t understand what heart failure is.

Most of the participants in this study demonstrated limited understanding of HF. They all acknowledged awareness of having a heart problem, but they were unaware of the mechanism by which HF impacts their bodies and of whether they had HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) or HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), a distinction which impacts their medical treatment. Subject 8 appeared cautious about seeking additional knowledge beyond understanding that she was not at imminent risk of death:

“You know what? My doctor told me that I have heart failure. I told him, I say, ‘So, what that mean? I was going to die?’ So, he said, ‘no’. So, you know, and I can't remember what else he said behind that because I guess after he said no, you know, I just know that, you know, I'm okay.”

When asked about her current understanding of the condition, Subject 8 stated:

“I still don't understand what it is. I really don't understand what it is. 'Cause I've been going to my cardiologist and he never after that time when he told me that we never discussed it ever again.”

Only Subject 4 described the condition medically and knew her HF type. Subject 4 worked in a healthcare setting and after an acute event that impacted her heart, she attended cardiac rehabilitation where she received substantial patient education. She saw her job as a healthcare worker as a positive influence on her health literacy:

“That's a really big advantage. That's a big advantage. You know, you know what's going on. You know what is going on in your body. You know what you're supposed to do that can help, that it's a big help.”

I was told that physical activity would not hurt.

Subject 2 had a background in athletics, and Subject 4 received exercise training in cardiac rehabilitation. Subject 9 reported having a personal trainer in the past and was actively enrolled in pulmonary rehabilitation, where she received exercise education as well. The others reported no formal instruction in exercise. All but Subject 4 denied knowing how to monitor their vital signs during exercise, which also speaks to self-efficacy in this group.

All the participants in this study regarded physical activity as important for their health yet they all reported being underactive. Dialogue with participants revealed that physical activity was not as emphasized by healthcare providers as was medication adherence and dietary changes, and this may have impaired health literacy on this topic. All the participants reported taking their medications as the first thing they did to improve their health. Three participants reported receiving written handouts on diet. Most participants were told to walk or exercise, but no further details were explained, nor were any handouts provided.

“A doctor mentioned one time that [physical activity] would not hurt… Well, he gave me some paper with what types of food to eat and what not to eat, and the best, you know, what he would prefer me to eat and stuff like that. But no, he didn't tell me what type of exercises and all of this.” (Subject 2)

“[Doctors] always tell me to walk…. They say as much as you can, as much as you're able to, you should do it.” (Subject 3)

“Told me I need to do a little bit more exercise and taking me a little walk, taking me a little walking and stuff. Like, you know, usually when they tell me that I don't pay that much attention, 'cause I don't really have the energy to do that. I can only walk so far and I'm so tired…. Yes. [My doctors told me] I should do a little bit more exercise, but I don't have the energy.” (Subject 7)

“No, he didn't give me no specific exercises. He will always tell me to exercise. You need to exercise. You need to.” (Subject 8)

Regarding patients understanding the importance of physical activity, Subject 9, who was actively enrolled in pulmonary rehabilitation, stated eloquently:

“I would urge [healthcare providers] to tell [patients] how important the exercise is to keep stepping it up and to do it as frequently as possible. Because if [patients], for instance, when they finish the [rehabilitation] program, if they stop, they go back to zero. So, this is a lifetime exercise mandate. And I don't think it's as bluntly presented as that.”

I struggled when treated by providers from different cultures.

Half of the participants reported experiencing discrimination in a healthcare encounter based on ethnic, cultural, and language differences. Subject 3, a self-identified Afro-Caribbean, reported conflict with Haitian doctors when seeking care for herself and on another occasion for her mother. She perceived a callousness on the part of the Haitian healthcare providers toward her and her family members. Subject 4, also Afro-Caribbean, reported feeling prejudged as a Black Haitian woman.

“They have the view that as uh, they have the view [that] as a Black, as a Black woman, you don't have the appropriate diet. It is something, it's because you, you, you have the...As soon as they saw you they say, ‘Oh you is the fattest’. Surely with the Haitian background, they talk about, ‘Oh, you need to stop eating griot’. You know, it's like a judgement they have against you, that's been you don’t eat properly. It is like, you don't have the appropriate diet and then it is not true, sometimes it's not true because, uh, the griot, yes, it's part of our culture, the fried food is part of our culture, but it's not something we eat everyday.”

African American participants also reported differential treatment and barriers to engaging with care systems, due to linguistic mismatch. Subject 5 retold the experience below.

“Well, when I call, my insurance right away, 'cause I felt like the lady was rude and everything because I wasn't Spanish. I told her I didn't understand what she was saying. And she got mad. But I don't understand 'cause you not speaking properly for me to understand, you know. Why you can't just get somebody who understands English more, because you [are] claiming that you can't speak but so much English. Well, that's not my fault. And then she didn't want to help me. So, I called my insurance. I said, ‘you shouldn't send us to places where people can't speak English. You know, I know you don't want me to go. You don't want to pay for me to go where I want to go. You know, for me to get my health care because y'all want me to go where y'all going to pay less. But the truth of it is, you need to have people there, that can help you there that can speak English.’”

Subject 7 also shared that she was unable to get information about her own care because the providers were not native English-speakers:

“They would give me a doctor that speak Spanish and nurse speak Spanish. I don't understand nothing that they saying. And they asked me, “well, what the doctors say?” I say, ‘I don't know.’”

Subject 7 also explained that when there was a mismatch between her ethnic background and that of the care team, the differing expectations around communication impacted her willingness to cooperate with the advice of the healthcare providers, which can impact health outcomes:

“Because when I when I was at --- Hospital and I had, I had just, almost... The doctor had saved my life. And the nurses that was there, they was more like... They [were] Haitian people. They used to treat me so bad. But I guess they was really trying to save my life. But it was the way they was talking to me. It was the way that they was hounding me and everything, you know? I really. You know, I really felt some type of way. Yeah. I felt they didn't have no patience. [It made me feel] very angry. Very, very angry. 'Cause it was like they was demanding me to do things that I knew I couldn't do. And then when they made me feel like that it made me feel I didn't want to do it. I just wanted to get out.”

Those participants that denied having experienced discrimination tended to have more assertive personalities and asked many questions.

“Well, when I started getting health care, everything was good. Yeah. I mean, I wasn't treated badly, you know? And, you know, ‘cause I ask a lot of questions. I ask a lot of questions because I be wanting to know, you know? And, um, everybody answered my questions, what I want to know. And everybody was really friendly, you know, professional and, you know.” (Subject 8)

“Let me say that I'm a very assertive person… So, I don't, you know… If you might seem to be having a problem with my presence, I can put you at ease, immediately.” (Subject 9)

“I've had good experiences. Very good. Because I'm a very boisterous person.” (Subject 1)

When asked to speculate on differences in physical activity levels between Black and White persons with HF, the participants offered several possible reasons. A popular belief was that Black individuals experienced greater financial hardship and responsibilities that allowed them less free time to be physically active. Subject 2 stated, “Some of the Blacks probably have more other pressing situations worrying about the food and the rent or whatever like that than the Whites, you know?” Subject 9 agreed, stating:

“I would say they don't have the time. Maybe they're pretty busy making life work. It depends on the income level, I think. Yes, I would say that the income is a major part of it and the time, too. You know, sometimes you have Black people working two jobs. To make ends meet.”

Other participants suggested that the issue may be due to the role of physical activity in daily life and also feeling patronized when told to change habits. Subject 8 speculated, “I think that African American people. I don't know. I don't think they feel as though exercise plays an important role in your life because, you know, I didn't feel that way either.” Subject 3 added:

“We don't take it seriously. Even for diabetes, you know, you tell them you can't eat this, this this, They still... like my mom. She wants to eat what she wants to eat. And you tell her you can't. And we have a tendency to get upset. You know, we have a tendency to say, ‘don't treat me like a child’. When others say, ‘OK. I can't have it? I can't have it’. If it's a gene thing or is just the cultural thing. It is a cultural thing because Black is, um Black Caribbean, American. A lot of the Black. They'll tell you, ‘don't treat me like a child’. You know, we have been like that for years. Don't treat me like a child.”

Having heart failure means I don’t have long to live.

Many participants, rather than describing the condition from a medical perspective, described it more from an emotional or fatalistic perspective. Subject 9 reported, “It means I can die at any minute… It makes me get my house, my affairs in order.” Subject 3, who had a congenital heart condition that led to her HF, simply states, “That means I have a broken heart.” Subject 7 said, “At one point I thought that I wasn't gonna be able to make it for a while. I thought that I was gonna… I didn't think I had long to live.” Subject 8, as previously quoted said, “You know what? My doctor told me that I have heart failure. I told him, I say, ‘So, what that mean? I was going to die?’”.

Some participants viewed their diagnosis of HF as inevitable, suggesting that their locus of control was external.

“I always knew it, though, you know. I knew I've come from a family that has bad hearts and cancer, so. It's really no secret, but I know that I have to take care of myself. I just accept it for what it is.” (Subject 2)

“I was born with many malfunctions in my, in my heart. Some of them was fixable, but the main thing I was born with an enlarged heart that can't be fixed.” (Subject 3)

“I think this is something that's hereditary. All the old girls, all the old girls had it. My mother, my two aunts.” (Subject 1)

Most participants denied concerns that physical activity can worsen their HF, seeing it only as beneficial, although details about the benefits were not stated. The benefits of physical activity were at times related to an activity that they can no longer perform, like dancing, or walking several blocks. Concerns expressed regarding physical activity being harmful were related more so to the risk of muscular injuries rather than HF exacerbation. Although Subject 9 reported a belief that exercise was important and beneficial, she expressed a fear of exercising alone, preferring to have a family or friend present due to experiencing palpitations intermittently.

The potential influences on physical activity engagement in Black patients with HF are depicted in Fig. 1. This conceptual figure was derived from the analysis of the participant responses in this study. For instance, limited understanding about HF and no detailed exercise prescriptions were described by participants and related to health literacy as reported in the thematic analysis. The limited physical activity instruction would contribute to their health beliefs and thus may become a barrier to physical activity due to lack of understanding and knowledge. Therefore, although the influences are represented as distinct factors, there may be some overlap and interconnectedness in the concepts. The healthcare provider has the potential to positively or negatively impact all the factors that can contribute to physical activity engagement by the nature of their interactions with patients with HF [51].

Potential influences on physical activity engagement conveyed by Black patients with heart failure

In this study, looking at participant physical activity through a Health Belief Model lens, we can assume that the perceived seriousness and perceived susceptibility are low, as all the participants report being underactive. This may be due to inadequate health literacy. A lack of understanding of the pathophysiology of HF would make it challenging for patients with HF to understand why they need to adopt physical activity and that there may be negative consequences if they do not [52, 53]. The participants all acknowledge that physical activity would be beneficial, although they fail to express the benefits they anticipate. The cues to action also were insufficient to mobilize toward the adoption of physical activity, and in some cases may have indirectly discouraged physical activity. Two participants reported having other family members with HF. The quality and quantity of physical activity education received by most participants from the health care providers was inadequate and does not emphasize the importance of this self-management tool. Discussion

This study sought to gain insight into the lived experiences of Black persons with HF relating to physical activity behaviors. The four main themes that emerged from the analysis were 1. I don’t understand what heart failure is; 2. I was told that physical activity would not hurt; 3. I struggled when treated by providers from different cultures; and 4. Having heart failure means I don’t have long to live. What became apparent during the interviews with the participants was that their reported experiences with healthcare providers did not empower them to engage in physical activity. The themes reported above capture known influences of physical activity behaviors and will therefore be discussed in that context below. To what degree these factors “cause” the physical activity behavior for each participant is not explained in this study.

Health literacy is foundational to establish behavior change [52, 54]. In attempting to recruit participants for this study, several potential participants were excluded because they denied having HF, despite having that diagnosis in their electronic medical record and despite some of them even describing classic symptoms of HF. This was quite surprising. While the HF diagnosis may have been an error in some people’s medical records, there may have been some individuals who were unaware of their HF diagnosis possibly due to absent or ineffective communication from the physician at the time. Most of the enrolled participants in this study, regardless of educational attainment, lacked an understanding of their HF and consequently did not grasp how they can use physical activity safely and effectively to manage the condition. This is consistent with existing literature that suggests that as much as 39% of all individuals with HF have low health literacy, and that Black people with HF are more likely to be in that category [55]. Future studies that continue to investigate health literacy in this population are warranted.

The participant with the highest level of health literacy was the one who attended cardiac rehabilitation and worked in healthcare. Patients with HF can benefit greatly from supervised exercise and patient education as is provided in a cardiac rehabilitation program [1]. However, in the US, cardiac rehabilitation services for Medicare recipients and those with other insurance plans are covered only for those with a diagnosis of HFrEF, even though more than half of HF patients have HFpEF [56, 57]. Additionally, only 10% of eligible hospitalized HF patients are referred to cardiac rehabilitation at discharge [58]. Thus, most patients with HF are provided with little guidance on how to exercise safely and effectively to address their condition [59].

In addition to deficits in health literacy, there were multiple reports of inadequate physical activity instruction from the healthcare providers. This may not be an experience limited only to Black patients. Existing literature reported limited instruction in physical activity with White patients as well [59]. In the absence of a formal, structured patient education program, the responsibility falls on the individual healthcare provider to provide this instruction, which lends itself to wide variability in the education provided. In the provision of physical activity education to patients with HF by physical therapists, there was variability in patient education provided based on attainment of specialist status[60]. Even within a formal patient education program there can be deficiencies [29]. This lack of time and detail in the physical activity instruction may subconsciously suggest to patients that engaging in physical activity is not that important. In alignment with existing evidence, being told to walk is not enough and does not emphasize the importance of physical activity as a self-management tool [61]. Additionally, if health professionals do not explain effectively why they are asking patients to be physically active, but rather simply instruct them to do it, patients may feel infantilized, an experience few adults appreciate.

In South Florida, where this study was performed, the patient population is richly diverse [45]. Despite the benefit that diversity can bring to a community, diversity can also result in conflict and miscommunication if not handled carefully. Consistent with existing evidence, our study supports that healthcare interactions negatively influenced by a clash of cultures, linguistic mismatch or discrimination may result in lack of trust or low value placed on the information provided and poor adherence to recommendations, like physical activity [27, 62]. Participants in this study reported perceived discrimination in healthcare, primarily caused by cultural and linguistic differences. Biases by the health providers may have contributed to the poor quality of physical activity education received by participants, and conversely, participant biases may have also limited their ability or willingness to receive information. Wider structural and systemic issues resulting in lack of access to or use of translation services within the healthcare setting, and insurance plans that limit policyholder ability to select where they receive care may compound the challenge of navigating the healthcare system for the participants and patients in general [63, 64].

Participants also speculated that barriers primarily related to income and health beliefs that understate the importance of physical activity may be contributors to worse physical activity adherence in the Black population with HF. These factors have been previously identified in the literature [30, 65]. This again speaks to the importance of improving health literacy, which in turn will positively influence health beliefs.

In the introduction, we reported on the current underrepresentation of Black people in health professions. This lack of Black health professionals that can educate effectively on physical activity may mean that many patients will not be receiving this information from someone who is racially or ethnically congruent with them, which may affect patient satisfaction and buy-in as was found in similar studies [37, 41]. Continued efforts should be made to recruit and train health professionals from underrepresented groups to address this discordance as part of a wider strategy to reduce health inequities.

Implications

If empowered with an understanding of HF, patients may better appreciate their ability to influence their health outcome through physical activity, modify health beliefs and behavior, increase self-efficacy and reduce fear [53]. They may also be better equipped to identify questions to ask their healthcare providers to optimize their health management. This knowledge may also improve the patient’s outlook, with fewer negative emotions associated with the diagnosis [66]. Therefore, clinicians should ensure that this education is provided to their patients with HF in the format that best suits their learning needs.

Physical activity instruction should be provided in greater detail, utilizing various modes of learning tailored to the patient to ensure understanding. Patients should be educated on how to monitor their own vital signs during exercise and be prescribed individualized exercise plans based on their preferences and resources to improve adherence. This depth of education and training is unlikely to occur in one session, so there should be follow-up sessions and referral to cardiac rehabilitation or physical therapy as needed, where such instruction can be reinforced over multiple sessions to ensure self-efficacy of the patients and/or their caregivers [67]. Opportunities exist for future research on the efficacy of this approach.

Clinicians should recognize that their interactions with a patient can strongly influence behavior adoption [62]. Healthcare providers should evaluate their cultural competence and inherent biases and be able to identify disengagement that may be due to patient biases. Clinicians should develop strategies to bridge the divide between themselves and the patients who may differ from them racially, ethnically, linguistically or culturally [63]. Sometimes acknowledging each other’s differences helps to build rapport. Clinicians should utilize language translation resources, if available. Finally, clinicians should maintain focus on their goals of care for the patient and do what they can to facilitate a positive outcome, regardless of any differences.

Changing health beliefs may be the most challenging task a clinician will face. Using the Health Belief Model as a framework as done in Fig. 2 may prove useful in highlighting some of the considerations the individual may have in the adoption of physical activity. Open discussion with patients, challenging them to reflect on their health beliefs may prove useful in identifying a path to changing behaviors. The Health Belief Model, although helpful, places much of the onus to change on the patient. The clinician should be mindful that there may be structural and systemic factors outside of the control of the patient that influence the patient’s ability to adopt the changes demanded [21]. Public policy, like insurance that dictates that only patients with HFrEF have benefits for cardiac rehabilitation enrollment, is an example of a condition that is out of the control of the patient. Therefore, health care providers should evaluate the entire context that may impact physical activity adherence and work to navigate those with the patient. Advocacy by individual clinicians and by health profession associations is vital to advance measures for policy change that improve care and health outcomes.

Limitations

There were several limitations of this study. First, the first author’s position as a physical therapist may have influenced how a participant chose to respond to questions regarding physical activity behaviors, as he/she may have wanted to provide responses that would portray himself/herself in a better light. Additionally, while minoritized communities often have collective experiences as well as vicarious experiences through family, friends, and others, that may well equip them to reflect on the group perspective, questions that ask individuals to speak about how a group may behave or think may unintentionally reinforce stereotypes.

There were several challenges with recruitment. Based on the existing literature, our sample size of 8 was adequate to achieve saturation and provide meaningful contribution to the existing literature on this topic [68, 69]. However, the small sample size limited our ability to perform analysis based on the desired subgroups of participants. With the proliferation of spam calls, recruitment via telephone calls may have made potential participants less trusting of the researchers’ intentions. Also, this study required that participants have an email address and internet access to sign the online consent form and to complete intake survey instruments. This may have posed a challenge for participants with lack of comfort with technology and thereby negatively impacted recruitment.

We recruited patients through UHealth, a system that does not serve the uninsured. Therefore, the uninsured HF population was excluded from participation. They may have different perspectives and challenges in physical activity adherence that are not represented. Another limitation is that the interview was only conducted with English speakers, which excluded several potential participants from our diverse, multi-lingual South Florida community. Additionally, due to connectivity issues, most of the interviews were performed without video cameras. Doximity is a web-based telehealth platform and given the inconsistency of service at the participant locations, there were issues with signal interruption, intermittent loss of picture, lags and freezes when trying to use the video feature. We decided to use audio only to allow for better quality recordings for the transcription. We therefore were unable to evaluate the participant’s non-verbal body language, which may impair the ability to interpret the communication fully. Finally, the responses to this qualitative inquiry are dependent on the recall of the participants and therefore may not accurately reflect their true lived experience.

Conclusion

In this study we investigated the beliefs and physical activity behaviors of Black individuals with HF in South Florida. While all considered physical activity important, they all reported being underactive. The reasons described for this discrepancy were unique to each participant, but low health literacy about the condition and limited physical activity instruction provided by their healthcare providers were common findings. The limited education about physical activity likely contributed to a belief that it was not an important component of the management of HF. Future research that captures the experiences of Black patients with HF from different genders, cultural groups, different language proficiencies and health insurance statuses will provide greater insight into the challenges of this population in adoption of regular physical activity and may help to illuminate the need for practice and policy change. A direct recruitment strategy with in-person interviews will likely be more favorable now as the risks and impacts of COVID-19 exposure for this population are better understood.
