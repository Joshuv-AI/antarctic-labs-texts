Labor analgesia in India: A study on awareness, utilization, and perceptions among pregnant women
Authors: Yuvasri Srinivasan, KalyaniPraba PremSundar, M. Anuradha, S. Subramanian, D. Gunaseelan, A. Saravanan, K. Thamarai Selvi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The concept of labor analgesia remains largely ambiguous to expectant women and pregnant individuals within the Indian healthcare system. The act of parturition is recognized as one of the most excruciating experiences in a woman’s existence. Inadequately managed labor pain yields numerous detrimental ramifications for both the mother and the fetus. Insufficient levels of cognizance and prevailing attitudes toward labor analgesia among pregnant women constitute a significant impediment that adversely influences both maternal and fetal wellbeing.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional study anchored within an institutional framework was executed. A total of 109 consenting pregnant women were enlisted for participation in the study. Data collection was facilitated through the utilization of semistructured questionnaires, and subsequent analysis was conducted employing the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 24.RESULTS:A mere 20.2% of participants exhibited awareness of labor analgesia. Among this group, only 18.3% had previously availed themselves of labor analgesia during their past deliveries. A substantial 77.1% of respondents expressed a desire for labor analgesia in their forthcoming labor experiences. Conversely, 22.9% of expectant mothers indicated a refusal to receive labor analgesia. In addition, it was reported that 47.7% exhibited reservations about the financial consequences related to the procedure.CONCLUSION:Awareness of labor analgesia among Indian prenatal women is significantly low. Obstetricians should provide education during prenatal and antenatal care, especially during labor, to minimize adverse maternal and fetal outcomes. Bridging this gap requires comprehensive health education and improved accessibility to these services.

Introduction

Labor pain constitutes one of the most intense sensations that an individual may experience, and it can produce physiological consequences for both the mother and the fetus.[1] Although all females undergo the experience of labor pain, the modalities of perception, interpretation, and response to such pain exhibit significant variability. A woman’s perception of labor pain is influenced by a multitude of factors, encompassing cultural, social, mental, environmental, and psychological dimensions.[2] The progression of labor pain management techniques has transpired incrementally since James Young Simpson’s notable discovery of chloroform for labor analgesia in 1847. Presently, a diverse array of pharmacological and nonpharmacological approaches are employed for labor analgesia, each demonstrating varying levels of efficacy.[3] The onset of labor is characterized by consistent, distressing uterine contractions that lead to progressive cervical effacement and dilation.[1] A multitude of factors influences the perception of labor pain, including prior childbirth experiences, educational background, and religious beliefs.[4] Cognitive, social, and environmental determinants significantly contribute to the manner in which individuals interpret labor pain.[5] Numerous women across the globe opt for cesarean delivery rather than vaginal birth due to the intense physical suffering associated with labor. Research conducted in India indicates that approximately 50% of cesarean deliveries were executed on women in labor as a result of previous adverse encounters with labor discomfort.[6] Pharmacological interventions play a pivotal role in the provision of labor analgesia.[7] Women undoubtedly possess the inherent right to experience childbirth devoid of pain and anxiety.[8] Labor analgesia, or the mitigation of pain through various medical interventions, is essential in improving women’s birthing experiences by reducing discomfort, stress, and anxiety.[9] The Society for Obstetric Anesthesia and Perinatology was founded in 1968. Epidural analgesia (EA) is the most prevalent form of labor analgesia, accounting for 65%, followed by combined spinal EA.[10] EA constitutes a central nerve blockade technique that entails the administration of a local anesthetic into the lower back region adjacent to the nerves responsible for transmitting pain associated with uterine contractions and the birth canal.[11] The World Health Organization (WHO) promotes a diverse array of pain relief modalities for parturient women, encompassing epidural analgesia, systemic opioids, relaxation techniques, and alternative therapies such as massage, depending on the preferences of the expectant mothers.[1213] With epidural analgesia as the gold standard of labor pain relief, and pain relief seen as not only good practice but also patients’ right, the question becomes, ‘how far has epidural analgesia for labor evolved in the tertiary care hospital?’[3] The deficiency of knowledge and awareness regarding labor pain management options among women, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, is a significant issue.[14] Consequently, this research endeavor sought to elucidate the extent of knowledge, perceptions, and acceptance regarding the utilization of labor analgesia among pregnant women.

Materials and Method

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional investigation was executed within a tertiary care medical facility between November 2024 and January 2025. This study targets pregnant women, mostly in their second and third trimester. A structured questionnaire is given to the participants in understandable languages.

Study participants and sampling

Hundred and nine individuals participated in this study, comprising pregnant women in their second and third trimester attending an obstetrical clinic for customary antenatal evaluations.

Data collection tools and techniques

The authors constructed a structured questionnaire utilizing Google Forms, which was informed by prior discussions and previous research. The questionnaire encompassed four distinct sections (sociodemographic data, knowledge assessment, perception evaluation, and acceptance measurement). A total of 24 questions were formulated, each accompanied by multiple-choice response options. In instances where a patient exhibited unfamiliarity with labor analgesia, educational information was provided. To mitigate bias, the interviewer meticulously documented the respondents’ answers.

Inclusion criteria

Any pregnant woman above the age of 18 years who gives consent to participate in the survey.

Exclusion criteria

Expectant mothers who are not willing to take the survey, who are scheduled for elective cesarean section, and who are in active labor.

Ethical consideration

This investigation was executed in perfect compliance with the ethical principles established in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was secured from the Institutional Ethics Committee before the initiation of the research (Approval No: IEC No ST1124-1831).

All participants received comprehensive information about the study’s objectives, methodologies, risks, and advantages. Written informed consent was acquired from each participant prior to data collection. Participation was optional, and participants maintained the choice to withdraw from the study at any time without repercussions to their care.

Confidentiality and anonymity were rigorously upheld during the research process. Data were securely stored and accessible exclusively by the principal investigators. No identifiable information was revealed in any publications or presentations. This study did not encompass any physical intervention or experimentation. The study concentrated exclusively on knowledge, usage patterns, and attitudes of labor analgesia, presenting minimal risk to participants. The authors declare no conflicts of interest and confirm adherence to institutional and international ethical norms for research involving human participants.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected from all 109 patients and analyzed based on the data type. Microsoft Office Excel 2019 was used to analyze the data. Ordinal and nominal data were presented as numbers and percentages. Bar diagrams and pie charts were used for graphical representation.

Results

A total of 109 antenatal participants who expressed a willingness to engage in the study were administered a questionnaire within the obstetrics and gynecology department. The response rate attained was 100%, with all inquiries adequately addressed. The sociodemographic characteristics of the participants are delineated in Table 1. The average age of the respondents was determined to be 26.65 ± 3.21; individuals under the age of 25 constituted 26 (24%), those aged between 25 and 30 comprised 64 (58%), and participants over the age of 30 accounted for 19 (18%). A majority, specifically 63 (58%), resided in rural areas, whereas 46 (42%) hailed from urban settings. A significant proportion of the participants were identified as primigravida (46.8%), followed by those classified as second gravida (43.2%), and a minority as multigravida (10%). Among the multigravida cohort, the antecedent mode of delivery was reported as vaginal for 31.1% and cesarean for 28.5%. The educational attainment of the participants revealed that 78 (71.6%) had completed their graduation, 28 (25.6%) had achieved an education level up to secondary school, and 2 (1.9%) reached primary education. A considerable number of the participants, totaling 62 (56.9%), were unemployed; 21 (19.2%) were engaged in the medical field, 24 (22%) were employed within the private sector, and 2 (1.9%) were affiliated with the government sector. Among the participants, 51 (46.8%) were classified as primigravida, 47 (43.2%) as second gravida, and 11 (10%) as multigravida (refer to Table 2); a predominant 61 (56%) expressed apprehension regarding labor pain when queried. Furthermore, 83 (76.1%) of the participants believed that vaginal delivery would be unattainable without the experience of pain.

Awareness of labor analgesia

The majority of participants (76.1%) indicated that their knowledge regarding labor analgesia was predominantly acquired from medical practitioners, thereby highlighting the essential function of healthcare professionals in the transmission of pertinent information. The references provided by friends or family comprised 13.8% of the surveyed individuals, while 10.1% cited the Internet, highlighting a relatively low reliance on digital resources regarding this specific issue. In relation to distinct pain alleviation techniques, a substantial proportion (77%) recognized an injection administered in the lower back (commonly known as epidural or spinal analgesia), whereas a mere 15.6% acknowledged intravenous (IV) methods and only 4.6% referred to intramuscular (IM) alternatives. A scant 2.8% identified inhalation-based analgesia, indicating a restricted awareness of less traditional modalities. Notwithstanding a certain degree of familiarity with various techniques, only 18.3% of respondents had personally experienced labor analgesia, while 81.7% had not, implying that the practical implementation of these methods remains insufficient. When inquired about the providers of labor pain relief, over half (51.4%) accurately recognized anesthetists, followed by 39.4% who believed obstetricians to undertake this responsibility, and 9.2% who attributed it to nursing staff. Concurrently, only 20.2% reported having friends or relatives who utilized labor pain relief, with an equivalent percentage (20.2%) stating awareness of labor analgesia themselves. The remaining 79.8% demonstrated unfamiliarity with the subject, thereby highlighting a significant knowledge deficit [Table 3].

Awareness of labor analgesia

Attitude of labor analgesia

A significant fraction of respondents (63.3%) indicated a lack of awareness regarding labor analgesia, signifying a notable deficiency in knowledge within the community. Merely 36.7% acknowledged their awareness, implying that information pertaining to pain alleviation options during parturition may not be extensively distributed or readily available. Additionally, a majority of participants (79.8%) had not observed any acquaintances or family members utilizing labor pain management, while only 20.2% were familiar with individuals who had. This restricted vicarious exposure corresponds with the observation that only 18.3% of participants had personally employed any modality of labor analgesia, in contrast to the predominant majority (81.7%) who had not [Figure 1]. In aggregate, these findings underscore the imperative for enhanced educational initiatives and awareness campaigns concerning the available strategies for pain management during childbirth.

Awareness and utilization of labor analgesia: a survey analysis

When confronted with the question of whether mitigating labor discomfort contradicts the fundamental essence of labor, 86 respondents (78.9%) articulated disagreement, whereas 23 respondents (21.1%) conveyed agreement. A significant proportion of the respondents (63.3%) did not subscribe to the notion that labor analgesia is an obligatory intervention for all women undergoing labor, whereas 36.7% of the respondents perceived it as a requisite. Upon questioning whether it is advisable to alleviate labor pain, 97 respondents (88.9%) affirmed the necessity, in contrast to 12 respondents (11.1%) who negated this proposition. Roughly 77.1% of the respondents indicated a preference for pursuing labor analgesia during their ongoing or future pregnancies, and 88.1% expressed a willingness to recommend labor analgesia to their social circle and relatives, whereas conversely, 22.9% did not favor labor analgesia for their present or impending pregnancies, and 11.9% were reluctant to suggest it to their social circle and relatives. While 52.3% of participants demonstrated a readiness to incur costs associated with labor analgesia, 47.7% exhibited a reluctance to do so [Figure 2].

Attitudes toward labor analgesia: a survey perspective

The predominant factor that affected the participants’ decision to utilize labor analgesia was the aspiration to mitigate pain during the delivery process, identified by 53 participants (48.6%), followed by the intent to alleviate stress and anxiety, reported by 25 participants (22.9%), the objective of conserving energy, indicated by 21 participants (19.3%), and prior adverse obstetric experiences, mentioned by 10 participants (9.2%). Among those who articulated their rationale for opting against labor analgesia, 43 participants (39.5%) expressed apprehensions regarding potential side effects, 25 participants (22.9%) cited familial or guardian opposition, 23 participants (21.1%) indicated a preference for experiencing an unmedicated birth, and 18 participants (16.5%) acknowledged a lack of awareness regarding labor analgesia [Figure 3a and b].

The survey data illustrated in Figure 2 offer significant insights into the perceptions and attitudes concerning labor analgesia among the participants. The results reveal a prevailing acceptance of pain relief during labor, although there is a divergence of opinions regarding factors such as economic implications and the necessity of making it a compulsory practice. A notable majority of respondents (88.9%) assert that labor pain ought to be alleviated, underscoring a widespread acknowledgment of the advantages associated with pain management during childbirth. Nevertheless, a minority (11.1%) articulated the view that labor pain should remain unaddressed, potentially influenced by cultural convictions or individual preferences pertaining to natural childbirth. When queried about their inclination to select labor analgesia for their present or forthcoming pregnancies, 77.1% of respondents indicated a preference for employing pain relief techniques. In contrast, 22.9% exhibited reluctance to pursue labor analgesia, suggesting apprehensions regarding safety, adverse effects, or personal beliefs about experiencing labor in its natural state. A robust endorsement for labor analgesia was apparent as 88.1% of respondents indicated they would advocate for it to friends and family members. Only 11.9% hesitated to recommend labor analgesia, potentially due to a lack of knowledge or varying views on childbirth experiences. One of the central issues concerning labor analgesia pertains to its financial implications. The survey findings reveal that 52.3% of respondents are willing to incur the costs associated with labor analgesia, while 47.7% are not. This narrow margin highlights the economic burden that may affect a woman’s decision to seek pain relief during labor. When inquired whether labor analgesia ought to be obligatory for all women experiencing labor, opinions were polarized. Merely 36.7% endorsed the notion that it should be mandated, whereas a greater segment (63.3%) opposed such an idea, accentuating the significance of personal autonomy in childbirth. This indicates that while labor pain relief is esteemed, many contend that women should possess the agency to determine their preference regarding its utilization. A minority of respondents (21.1%) perceived that alleviating labor pain contradicts the inherent process of childbirth. However, the majority (78.9%) refuted this perspective, suggesting that a predominant segment of the population regards labor analgesia as a constructive medical advancement rather than a disruption of natural processes [Figure 4].

Awareness in different labor pain relief methods

A considerable proportion of survey participants (77%) recognized the administration of injections in the lumbar region as a method for alleviating labor pain. This approach likely pertains to epidural or spinal analgesia, which is among the most frequently employed and well-established modalities for pain management during labor. The elevated acknowledgment of this technique implies that healthcare practitioners or public awareness initiatives have prioritized this method over alternative options. Approximately 15.6% of participants were cognizant of the availability of intravenous (IV) analgesics as an alternative for managing labor pain. IV pharmacological agents, including opioids, are occasionally utilized to alleviate pain, although they may not offer comprehensive relief in comparison to epidural analgesia. The comparatively lower recognition of this modality indicates that it is not as extensively addressed as epidural analgesia within the discourse surrounding labor pain management. Merely 4.6% of respondents noted intramuscular injections as a viable pain relief strategy during labor. This technique, albeit less prevalent, entails the administration of analgesic agents directly into a muscle. The limited awareness surrounding this option suggests that it is not routinely emphasized in patient education or prenatal counseling sessions. The least acknowledged method of labor pain relief was inhalation-based analgesia, with a mere 2.8% of respondents recognizing it as a potential option. This approach, commonly utilizing nitrous oxide (“laughing gas”), is less prevalent in certain geographical locales, yet it is a widely adopted technique in various healthcare environments. The scant awareness of this alternative underscores the necessity for enhanced educational efforts to inform expectant mothers regarding its availability and efficacy.

The majority of the participants expressed a desire for a labor process devoid of pain. Among these individuals, 22.9% indicated a reluctance to pursue labor analgesia in their present or future pregnancies, primarily due to apprehensions regarding the potential side effects associated with analgesic medications; additionally, some (22.9%) cited familial disapproval, while others (21.1%) expressed a preference for experiencing the natural childbirth process [Table 4]. This finding is in stark contrast to the investigations conducted by Naithani et al., Olayemi et al., where the predominant rationale was the desire to undergo a natural childbirth experience.[15] Numerous similar studies have been executed in various developing countries. In an investigation by Nabukenya et al.,[15] the level of awareness regarding labor analgesia was recorded at a mere 7%, which is significantly lower in comparison to the findings of our study. Among the expectant mothers surveyed, 63.3% held the belief that labor analgesia is not a requisite for all women, whereas 36.7% contended that it is essential for every woman. The results of this study clearly indicate that expectant mothers are willing to embrace labor analgesia despite the evident lack of information. The participants articulated a desire to acquire knowledge from their obstetrician during their initial antenatal outpatient consultations. Furthermore, the economic circumstances of the pregnant woman significantly influence the utilization of labor analgesia as 47.7% reported an inability to afford the expenses associated with labor analgesia.

Discussion

Pain is an inherent aspect of human existence from the moment of birth and is predominantly viewed as a deleterious occurrence. Indeed, it constitutes a crucial component of our lived experiences. It serves as an indicator of deteriorating health conditions in both pathological and physiological contexts.[16] Labor pain is widely recognized as one of the most severe forms of discomfort that women may encounter during their reproductive years.[17] Labor pain constitutes a multifaceted phenomenon encompassing both physiological and psychological dimensions, intricately linked to uterine ischemia and a myriad of complex neurohormonal processes.[16] This pain results from the rhythmic contractions of the uterus and the dilation of the cervix, which are conveyed via the sympathetic nervous system from T10 to S1, as well as through perineal stretching that is mediated by S2 to S4.[17] The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists posits that the labor room is a unique environment wherein both the patient and the physician coexist under conditions of pain.[18] Participants underscored the significance of a supportive and empowering environment in facilitating their labor experience. Various social and cultural factors can significantly shape women’s perceptions, experiences, and responses to labor pain.[19] A woman’s disposition toward labor may be influenced by her insufficient comprehension of the childbirth process and the intensity of pain encountered during labor and delivery.[20] The American Society of Anesthesiologists, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, along with the National Institute of Clinical Excellence in the United Kingdom advocate for the education of expectant mothers regarding the possibilities and accessibility of effective pain management strategies during labor.[2122] The evolution of pain management techniques during labor has seen substantial advancements since 1847, when Simpson discovered that chloroform could alleviate the pain experienced by women in labor. Currently, there exists a multitude of pain relief methodologies for managing labor pain, encompassing both nonpharmacological and pharmacological approaches.[23] The provision of analgesia during labor and delivery is occasionally a topic of contention. Insufficient knowledge concerning the childbirth process can adversely affect a woman’s perspective on pain relief options.[24] A lack of awareness among pregnant women substantially contributes to the underutilization of analgesic techniques, resulting in the enduring suffering associated with labor pain due to ignorance, unfounded anxieties, and limited understanding of the available analgesic services. A study conducted in India indicated that 50% of cesarean deliveries were the consequence of maternal requests stemming from prior negative experiences with labor pain. During the course of pregnancy, it is imperative that women are informed about the advantages and potential adverse effects of the various pain control methods available.[23]

In the course of our investigation, a predominant proportion of participants originated from rural regions and belonged to a lower socioeconomic class. In addition, the majority of these mothers were aligned with the Hindu religion and were categorized within the age group of 25 to 30 years. In the present investigation, the level of awareness regarding labor analgesia was observed to be inferior to that reported by Khan et al.,[24] which indicated an awareness rate of 37.7%. The potential factors contributing to this diminished awareness may include geographical location, educational attainment, and the cultural beliefs held by the women. Notwithstanding this, a substantial 77.1% exhibited a favorable disposition toward labor analgesia during both their present and prospective pregnancies, which surpasses the findings of the study conducted by Workie et al.[23] A research endeavor by Sadawarte and Bhure indicated that primigravida women in a rural healthcare facility in India possessed limited knowledge regarding the labor process.[25]

The findings of the current investigation reveal that 36.7% of pregnant women possess awareness of labor analgesia, a figure that is lower than the 56.3% awareness rate reported by Mugambe et al.[26] The discrepancies observed may be attributable to variations in the geographic study location and the accessibility of information. In the current research, the primary source of information regarding labor analgesia was identified as healthcare professionals.[27] Nevertheless, this statistic is significantly lower in comparison to data emerging from developed countries, where the awareness rate hovers around 80%. This underscores the necessity for increased public enlightenment concerning the availability of such services within tertiary care institutions. Within our study cohort, a mere 0.9% were classified as illiterate, while the majority were individuals of primary, secondary, and tertiary educational backgrounds. Although the percentage of the aware population was modest (36.7%), the proportion of patients expressing a desire for painless labor was significantly greater at 77.1%. This suggests that if provided with the opportunity, parturients would prefer to undergo childbirth without pain.[28] Our acceptance rate is comparatively higher than that documented in other studies, as per the existing literature. A study has established a statistically significant correlation between socioeconomic status, gravida, pain perception, and awareness of labor analgesia.[28] In the present investigation, the majority expressed a willingness to obtain information regarding labor analgesia through their physicians (76.1%).

Upon inquiry regarding their willingness to accept labor epidural analgesia for their ongoing pregnancy after being educated about labor epidural analgesia (LEA), it was observed that among 109 parturients, a significant majority (77.1%) expressed a readiness to embrace labor epidural analgesia, whereas a minority (22.9%) maintained the belief that childbirth is an inherently natural process; furthermore, many exhibited misconceptions and apprehensions that aligned with the findings of the study conducted by Ibach et al.[29] This divergence in the awareness levels and the inclination to embrace labor epidural analgesia may be ascribed to the prevalent misconceptions and anxieties surrounding the procedure.[30] In the present study, it was revealed that 39.5% of parturients harbored concerns regarding the implications of labor analgesia, while nearly 21.1% of parturients expressed a preference for experiencing a natural delivery. A research study conducted in India similarly indicated that a considerable number of parturients believed that epidural analgesia could lead to chronic back pain, adversely affect the fetus, prolong the duration of labor, and ultimately result in cesarean deliveries or instrumental interventions.[30] In high-income nations, the discourse surrounding labor analgesia predominantly revolves around the selection of methods and associated complications; conversely, in low-income countries, the primary challenges pertain to the levels of awareness and the acceptance of analgesia during labor. Antenatal consultations are identified as the most opportune moments for disseminating such critical information. This assertion is corroborated by research indicating that the information disseminated regarding pain management during the antenatal phase is deemed particularly beneficial.[24]

Limitations and suggestions

The study, limited to a specific region and healthcare facility, may not represent the broader population of pregnant women in India. Participants were recruited from antenatal clinics, potentially excluding non-prenatal care women. Data on perceptions and awareness were self-reported, and the study did not evaluate changes in perception or postpartum outcomes. Perform research across many states and healthcare environments to enhance generalizability. Monitor pregnant women from early gestation through birth and postpartum to evaluate the evolution of awareness, utilization, and satisfaction. Integrate perspectives from obstetricians, anesthesiologists, and midwives to comprehend obstacles from the provider’s standpoint. Evaluate the effect of organized educational initiatives on enhancing awareness and acceptability of labor analgesia. Analyze the impact of cultural ideas, familial influence, and traditional practices on the decision-making process for labor pain treatment. Examine the availability, accessibility, and institutional preparedness for labor analgesia in both public and private healthcare facilities.

Conclusion

Pregnant women show a considerable gap in their knowledge and awareness about labor analgesia. A substantial proportion of the population anticipates that labor pain will be exceedingly severe, and upon receiving information regarding labor analgesia, a significant majority of respondents expressed favorable attitudes. An elevated level of educational attainment was correlated with enhanced awareness and acceptance of labor analgesia. Labor analgesia is gaining increasing popularity due to its substantiated benefits. Nonetheless, persistent misconceptions and a lack of comprehension regarding the physiological ramifications of labor pains and their advantages continue to prevail. Enhancing communication between obstetric practitioners and anesthesiologists is imperative, alongside the dissemination of information regarding the various options and benefits associated with labor analgesia. The absence of a dedicated labor analgesia team constitutes a considerable obstacle, and the establishment of specialized clinics warrants attention. It is essential to educate pregnant women about the advantages of these interventions to mitigate misconceptions and alleviate apprehensions regarding potential complications.
