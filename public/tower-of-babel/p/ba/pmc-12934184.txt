A mixed inflammatory peripheral signature defines clinical outcomes in a phase II trial combining pembrolizumab with paclitaxel and carboplatin in melanoma
Authors: Caroline Lambert, Rahima Jamal, Paméla Thébault, Eftihia Cocolakis, Karl Bélanger, Jeanne Dionne, Jean-François Cailhier, Huy Le, Caroline Letendre, Stéphanie Lepage, Yelena Shechtman, Réjean Lapointe, Wilson H Miller Jr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCheckpoint blockade of PD-1 with pembrolizumab provides long-term survival to a significant proportion of patients with metastatic melanoma. Pembrolizumab has been successfully used in combination with chemotherapy in non-small-cell lung cancer to increase the response rate. This phase II trial combined pembrolizumab with carboplatin/paclitaxel (CP) to assess its safety and efficacy, and to identify correlates of responses. Thirty patients without prior immunotherapy for unresectable/metastatic melanoma were treated with pembrolizumab and CP. Peripheral blood was collected at baseline and after 2 cycles to characterize systemic immune activity by multiplex assays and flow cytometry. Seventy percent of patients received all 4 cycles of CP; 87% received pembrolizumab for 2 y or until progression. Grade 3 and higher adverse events (AEs) occurred in 50% of the patients. The overall response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR) by irRC criteria were 43% and 53%, respectively. Median overall survival (OS) was 23.8 months. Objective response was associated with a lower frequency of naive CD8 T cells and low plasma CCL3 at baseline, along with a larger proportion of mature NK cells and of CD4 T cells expressing BTLA or LAIR-1. Survival rate was higher for patients with lower baseline of IL-6, IL-8, and CD4+CD39+ T cells. Following treatment, pro-inflammatory soluble factors increased in both responders and non-responders. Addition of CP to pembrolizumab in this study did not appear to result in a response or survival advantage and was less tolerable than immunotherapy alone. Correlative data point to peripheral signals to investigate further as potential biomarkers. Trial registration: clinicaltrials.gov, NCT02617849, registered on December 1st, 2015.Keywords: Melanoma, pembrolizumab, NK cells, paclitaxel, carboplatin, PD-1, predictive biomarkers, immunotherapy, interleukin-6, interleukin-8

Introduction

The past two decades have seen important improvements in the outcomes for patients with metastatic melanoma, made possible with the development of the checkpoint inhibitors ipilimumab, nivolumab, pembrolizumab and relatlimab as well as drugs targeting the MAPK pathway. Pembrolizumab is a humanized antibody that blocks the interaction between PD-1 and its ligands, PD-L1 and PD-L2. It was first approved for the treatment of refractory unresectable or metastatic melanoma in a multitude of countries based on the results from a Phase 1b clinical trial demonstrating an overall response rate (ORR) of 26%.1 Over time, additional indications for pembrolizumab in melanoma have been approved, including its use in both adjuvant and neoadjuvant settings. Yet, immunotherapy with pembrolizumab is still ineffective for a significant proportion of patients, with an overall response rate of less than 50% in metastatic disease, leaving over half of melanoma patients with a need for additional treatment.2

Pembrolizumab when combined with chemotherapy has shown success in improving the response rate across several types of malignancies, including uterine, cervical, and triple-negative breast cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and non-small-cell lung cancer, where it has become a standard of care. Cytoreduction by carboplatin/paclitaxel (CP) has the potential to expose tumor antigens to immune cells and enhance overall response rate and clinical benefit rate of pembrolizumab. Chemotherapy alone demonstrates limited efficacy in treating melanoma. Carboplatin/paclitaxel itself yields response rates of approximately 11%, with a median overall survival (OS) of around 10.5 months.3 Previously, the combination of ipilimumab and CP in patients with metastatic melanoma was found to be safe, well-tolerated and revealed novel characteristics associated with patients likely to benefit from treatment.4 A pre-existing systemic inflammatory state characterized by elevation of selected chemokines and advanced B cell differentiation was strongly associated with poor patient outcomes, revealing potential predictive circulating biomarkers. Similar to the ipilimumab/CP study, the current study sought to examine the safety and efficacy of combining pembrolizumab with CP and characterize features of the host immune system that may predict response either before or early after starting treatment.

Materials and methods

Study design and treatment

The study was designed by the investigators as an open-label, single-arm, prospective phase 2 study and was conducted in two university hospital centers in Montréal (Quebec, Canada, for CONSORT checklist, see S_Table 1). Eligible patients were men and women ≥18 y with a histologic diagnosis of unresectable Stage III or metastatic melanoma (based on the 7th edition of AJCC) and adequate organ function. Patients with uveal melanoma, as well as those with concomitant autoimmune conditions requiring immunosuppression were excluded. Prior systemic neo-adjuvant/adjuvant treatment was permitted. In the advanced/metastatic setting, only prior treatment with BRAF/MEK inhibitors was permitted. Patients with prior treatment for melanoma at any point with a checkpoint inhibitor were excluded. The complete list of inclusion and exclusion criteria are available on clinicaltrials.gov. Patients received carboplatin (AUC 6)/paclitaxel (175 mg/m2) for 4 cycles and pembrolizumab (200 mg) every 21 d for up to 34 cycles or until unacceptable toxicity or disease progression. On the day of treatment, patients received pembrolizumab first, followed by standard premedication with ondansetron (8 mg), dexamethasone (10–20 mg) and diphenhydramine (50 mg) before receiving paclitaxel and carboplatin. A run-in phase was performed in order to ensure the tolerability/safety of the combination of pembrolizumab and CP in the first 3 patients. These patients were observed for dose-limiting toxicities (DLT) for one cycle.

Tumor assessments were performed at screening, weeks 8, 16, 24, and approximately every 12 weeks thereafter. Safety assessments included vital signs, physical examinations, laboratory tests, and adverse event reporting (AEs). AEs were graded using the Common Toxicity Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 4.0. All SAEs were recorded following consent signature up until 90 d from discontinuation of investigational products. Non-serious AEs were collected from the first dose of the study treatment until 30 d post-last dose. The primary objective was to determine the anti-tumor efficacy, ORR, and clinical benefit rate (CBR, ORR + SD ≥ 24 weeks) of pembrolizumab when given with CP by immune related response criteria (irRC, relying on bidimensional measurements and allowing for new lesions) and RECIST 1.1. The secondary objectives included the safety and tolerability as well as the OS and PFS of patients receiving pembrolizumab with CP. Putative early cellular and/or predictive molecular biomarkers of the immune response in patients receiving the combination of pembrolizumab with CP, which may serve as a predictive tool for the treatment in metastatic melanoma, were also explored.

All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion in the study. The study received approval from the Research Ethics Board of the CIUSSS West-Central Montréal (approved as project 15-141) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable regulatory requirements.

Correlative studies

Blood samples were collected from patients in heparinized vacutainers at baseline and after 2 cycles (6 weeks) of treatment; a time early in treatment prior to first follow up imaging and at which marked changes in peripheral blood were observed in a prior study with ipilimumab.4 Peripheral blood collection and processing were done as previously described.5 Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) suspended in human serum albumin solution were stored in liquid nitrogen. Cell surface staining was performed as described previously, using antibodies (BD Biosciences and Biolegend, antibody panels are listed in S_Table 2).4 Dead cells were stained with the Live-or-Dye™ 787/808 fixable viability staining kit (Biotium). Flow cytometry data were acquired using a BD Symphony A5 instrument (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo Software version 10.10.1 (Becton Dickinson). Peripheral soluble cytokines/chemokines/soluble receptors were measured from cryopreserved plasma. Three different human kits from MesoScale Discovery Systems were processed as per the manufacturer's instructions: V-PLEX Proinflammatory Panel 1 (IFNγ, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8 (CXCL8), IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-13, and TNFα), V-PLEX custom (GM-CSF, IL-5, IL-7, IL-17A, VEGF, MIP-1β (CCL4), IP-10 (CXCL10), and MIP-1α (CCL3)) and R-Plex (MIG (CXCL9), TNFRI, and TNFRII). Electroluminescent data were analyzed with four-parameter logistic curve fit using MSD Discovery Workbench.

Statistical analyses

Calculated sample size for the trial of 44 participants was obtained by estimating that the overall response rate (ORR) of pembrolizumab in ipilimumab-naïve melanoma patients would be 34%, while hypothesizing that pembrolizumab and CP would increase the ORR rate to 56% (one-sided type I error rate of 5% and power of 90%). Recruitment was closed after 30 patients were enrolled onto the study due to a slow accrual rate, with the understanding that the treatment landscape for melanoma was evolving. Baseline soluble factors concentrations comparisons between responders and non-responders were first Log2-transformed, then compared using multiple t-test; p-values were adjusted with the Holm‒Sidak method. Dynamic changes in post-treatment samples vs pre-treatment (baseline) were done using paired t-tests; p-values were adjusted with the Holm‒Sidak method. Cox regression model was applied to identified potential predictive factors, with patients dichotomized into the high or low groups using the mean soluble factor concentration or mean percentage of the cell population value as a threshold. GraphPad Prism version 10 and SPSS version 29 were used for analyses and graphical displays.

Results

Cohort and trial design

We performed a clinical trial with advanced melanoma patients combining pembrolizumab with CP to assess its safety, efficacy, and to search for correlates of responses. Between June 2016 and September 2018, a total of 30 patients with a median age of 66 y (range 20–82) were enrolled in the study. No DLTs were observed in the 3 participants enrolled in the run-in phase. The baseline patient characteristics are described in Table 1. Ninety percent of patients had stage IV disease at study entry, 50% had M1c and 17% had M1d (CNS metastasis) disease, and 33% had elevated LDH levels and a baseline tumor burden (BTS) greater than 10 cm. The primary site of melanoma was cutaneous (77%), mucosal (3%) and unknown (20%). Forty-three percent of patients had melanoma harboring a BRAF mutation. None of the patients had received prior treatment with checkpoint inhibitors or chemotherapy prior to entering the study. Twenty-two patients were naive for systemic treatment. Among those who had received prior therapy, four patients (13%) had received adjuvant interferon-α and another four patients (13%) had received a BRAF/MEK inhibitor as a first line for advanced disease. Allocation and follow-up are shown in the flow diagram in Supplementary Figure 1.

Efficacy

All participants were evaluable for the best overall response. The overall response rate (ORR, CR + PR) was 43% by irRC and 40% by RECIST 1.1. The clinical benefit rate (CBR, including patients with CR, PR, and SD of at least 24 weeks) of 53% by irRC and RECIST 1.1 (Table 2). The rate of complete response by irRC was 10% versus 6.7% by RECIST. The median time to objective response for both criteria was 57 d (range, 54–766). The median OS was 23.8 months, and the median PFS was 5.6 months. One patient was lost to follow up after 170 d. 1-y, 3-y, and 5-y overall survival rates were 65.9%, 31.3%, and 24.2%, respectively. No significant association with overall survival was seen in patients with a BRAF mutation (S_Figure 2A). While the difference did not reach statistical significance, patients with a high BTS demonstrated a trend toward poorer survival outcomes in this study (S_ Figure 2C).

Toxicity

All participants reported at least one adverse event (AE; Table 3). In terms of treatment exposure, patients received a median of 10 doses (range, 2–35) of pembrolizumab, and 21/30 patients completed all 4 cycles of CP. Four patients discontinued pembrolizumab due to an immune-related adverse event. Three patients discontinued carboplatin, whereas 4 patients discontinued paclitaxel due to toxicity. Grade ≥3 AEs attributed by the investigators to the study drugs occurred in 50% of the patients. The most common treatment-related AE that affected 60% of the patients was alopecia, followed by fatigue (57%), nausea (50%), and rash (47%). Forty-seven percent of the participants experienced an SAE. A total of 7/30 patients had at least 1 SAE related to pembrolizumab as per the investigator. Two patients had an SAE related to CP. An SAE of multi-organ failure secondary to sepsis resulting in death occurred in one patient. This was assessed as unrelated to pembrolizumab but possibly related to CP in the context of neutropenia.

Events attributed to pembrolizumab by the investigator and reaching at least grade 2 within 12 months of the first dose of pembrolizumab were designated as significant immune-related adverse events (irAEs). A total of 20 irAEs were identified in 13 patients. A total of 7 patients developed a grade 2 or 3 rash and 5 patients developed grade 2 hypothyroidism that required treatment with hormone replacement therapy. One patient with grade 2 colitis was treated with corticosteroids and 2 patients with grade 3 colitis were also treated with infliximab. Additionally, the following irAEs were also identified: grade 3 hepatitis (2 patients), grade 2 adrenal insufficiency (1 patient), and grade 3 pneumonitis (1 patient). All cases were treated with corticosteroids. No significant association with overall survival was seen in patients with or without immune-related adverse events (irAEs, S_Figure 2B).

Baseline peripheral blood immune cells are linked to response and survival

To find predictive blood biomarkers, we first assessed the abundance of the main PBMCs populations prior to initiating treatment (S_Figure 3, S_Table 3). The baseline levels of most common immune cells remained unchanged in non-responders (PD or SD by irRC) and responders (PR or CR by irRC), including T cells (CD3, CD4, and CD8), B cells (CD19), and NKT cells, and various myeloid-lineage cells, such as classical and intermediate monocytes, granulocytic MDSCs (gMDSCs), and plasmacytoid and myeloid dendritic cells (pDC and mDC), assessed by irRC (S_Figure 4A). When analyzing different B lymphocyte subsets, only Bm3-4 population linked to plasma cells were found in higher proportion in the blood of responders than in non-responders (S_Figure 4B).

Interestingly, we observed higher proportion of CD56dimCD16hi cells in the blood of responders compared to non-responders, markers associated to natural killer (NK) cells with cytotoxic activity (Figure 1A, left panel), which was positively associated with survival (Figure 1B, left panel). While the association was not statistically significant, having high levels of monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (mMDSC) or non-classical monocytes, seemed to be linked to a lack of objective response and shorter survival (Figure 1B, middle and right panels).

Abundance of several T cell subsets, including naive, activated, regulatory T (Tregs) or memory T cells, were evaluated and associated with clinical outcome (S_Tables 4 and 5). The proportions of CD4+CD25+ T cells, encompassing both activated and regulatory CD4 T cells, were on average more abundant in responders, but their frequency did not correlate with survival (Figure 2A). More specifically, the proportion of putative Tregs (CD4+CD25+CD127dim) did not correlate with objective response or survival (Figure 2B). We also noted a higher level of naive CD8 T cells (CD8+CD45RA+CD62L+) in the blood of non-responders, although this did not translate into a difference in survival (Figure 2C).

Expression of several checkpoint and co-stimulatory receptors were compared against objective response and overall survival, namely LAG-3, PD-1, LAIR-1, TIGIT, ICOS, OX-40, BTLA, and 4-IBB on both CD4 and CD8 T cells. Proportions of CD4 T cells expressing BTLA (CD272) or LAIR-1 (CD305) were on average higher in responders compared to non-responders, although no significant correlation was observed with survival (Figure 2D and E). Finally, a lower frequency of CD8+CD39+ T cells correlated positively with survival but not with response (Figure 2F).

In summary, the distribution of immune cell populations prior to treatment differed in patients who eventually gained an objective anti-tumor response, while certain immune populations were more associated with higher probability of survival in the long term.

Baseline peripheral blood soluble factors linked to response and survival

The abundance of soluble factors, including chemokines, cytokines and soluble receptors, were measured from blood plasma prior to and after 2 cycles of treatments. (S_Tables 6 and 7). Lower levels of CCL3 were associated with an objective response (Figure 3A and B) but not with OS. Other cytokines/chemokines were similar before treatments between responders and non-responders (S_Figure 5). The soluble factors associated with a lower probability of OS may include higher levels of IL-6 and IL-8 (CXCL8) (Figure 3C and D). Blood samples collected after 2 cycles of treatment showed that the levels of several factors (TNFRII, TNFα, IL-10, IL-5, CXCL10, CXCL9, and IL-17A) increased after treatment in most participants, with CXCL10 and CXCL9 and IL-17A most evident, indiscriminately from the future response status (Figure 4 and S_Figure 6). In summary, while an increase in pro-inflammatory soluble factors was detected post-treatment with CP + pembrolizumab, clinical outcomes were linked to having lower levels of CCL3, IL-8, or IL-6 prior to treatment.

Discussion

Immune checkpoint inhibitors have revolutionized cancer therapy, particularly for melanoma patients. As a first-line treatment, they offer sustained efficacy with manageable toxicity, although only a subset of patients responds. The mechanisms behind resistance to checkpoint blockade remain unclear, and robust predictive biomarkers for patient selection are still lacking. In this clinical trial, the combination of CP and pembrolizumab achieved a 43% ORR by irRC. Additionally, we observed circulating cell subsets and soluble factors associated with treatment and patient outcomes, including NK cells and specific T lymphocyte subsets exhibiting both activated and exhausted phenotypes. Pre-treatment blood samples also revealed that cytokines such as IL-6, CCL3, and IL-8 correlate with clinical outcomes.

Several clinical trials have demonstrated that combining chemotherapy with immunotherapy is clinically feasible and can yield impressive outcomes across various malignancies. Notable examples include the KEYNOTE-868 trial for endometrial carcinoma6 and the KEYNOTE-407 trial for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).7 While comparisons across trials are to be approached with caution, the results of our single-arm limited size study do not point towards an improvement of efficacy, when contrasted with the ORR and PFS observed in single anti-PD-1 arms in landmark melanoma trials such as CheckMate 0678 and KEYNOTE-006.9 First-line randomized trials adding anti-CTLA-48 or anti-LAG310 to an anti-PD-1 regimen have also yielded higher response rates, making them desirable upfront options. In our study, 26/30 patients were first-line in the advanced setting; the remaining 4 patients had failed a first-line treatment with BRAF/MEK inhibitors. Our study was limited to patients who had no prior exposure to checkpoint inhibitors or chemotherapy. It remains possible that subgroups of patients, such as those with disease progression after immunotherapy, may benefit from a combination approach, perhaps as a result of chemotherapy resensitizing tumors by reducing immune-resistant cell populations and enhancing the overall immune response. Retrospective studies combining chemotherapy with immunotherapy in metastatic melanoma in patients who have already received immunotherapy11,12 have shown encouraging results. In terms of the safety profile, the toxicity of the CP + pembrolizumab combination was consistent with other studies, such as KEYNOTE-B10 in head and neck cancer, with no novel or unexpected toxicities observed.13

In this cohort of participants with advanced stage melanoma, elevated levels of mature CD56dimCD16+ NK cells prior to systemic treatment correlated positively with subsequent objective response and overall survival. The prognostic significance of NK subsets cells remains to be clarified. While several groups observed lower levels of CD56dim NK cells in the peripheral blood of melanoma patients compared to normal donors prior to treatment,14–16 others observed no difference in NK cell proportions in patients vs. healthy donors or differences across different stages of melanoma.17 Our findings could be compatible with NK cells' role in cancer immunosurveillance, where having availability of cytotoxic NK cells could contribute to the anti-tumor response following CP and anti-PD-1 treatment.

Responders also had higher baseline levels of CD4+BTLA+ and CD4+LAIR-1+ and CD4+CD25+ T cells, in the absence of Treg subset enrichment. The predictive/prognostic value of circulating Tregs in solid tumors is still debated, as findings vary with disease stage and tumor histology. While Tregs have previously been associated with poorer outcomes,18 high levels of Tregs did not hinder the response to anti-PD-1 therapy in NSCLC patients and were even associated with longer overall OS and PFS.19,20 BTLA and LAIR-1 vary in function and regulation. The expression of BTLA and LAIR-1 may indicate an activated T cell status, which could contribute to the anti-tumor response following PD-1 treatment. The BTLA-HVEM complex, while a promising target in immunotherapy, has produced divergent data in previous studies, possibly due to variations in detection methods, localization, and cancer types.21 Studies that have examined circulating T cells expressing BTLA in cancer remain limited. Interestingly, in hepatocellular carcinoma, BTLA has been shown to mark dysfunctional PD-1-expressing CD4+ T cells.22 Finally, while the abundance of the aforementioned T cell subsets correlated with the response to study treatment, they did not correlate with overall survival. This may be attributable to significant advancement of available treatments for melanoma. Many participants who discontinued the trial intervention due to disease progression later received additional treatments, including MEK inhibitors and additional immunotherapy.

A lower prevalence of CD4+ expressing the ectonucleotidase CD39 at baseline was positively associated with the probability of survival in our study. CD39 may identify a subset of exhausted T helper cells. This feature would support the development of combination therapies targeting both PD-1/PD-L1 and the adenosine-generating axes.23,24

Notable differences exist in studies seeking to identify predictive biomarkers with respect to both the patient populations (prior to patient exposure to checkpoint inhibitors, use of anti-PD-1 alone or in combination with other treatments, measure of clinical outcome in terms of response rate, PFS or OS) and sampling design (time weeks to months after treatment, plasma or serum collection vs. in vitro cell stimulation of PBMCs to induce cytokine production).25-29 As highlighted by others, it may be that a durable change in peripheral immune signatures follows checkpoint inhibition29 and that signatures associated with treatment with single anti-PD-1 or in combination with anti-CTLA-4 differ from one another.26 We also observed different immune signatures in patient cohorts treated with ipilimumab and chemotherapy in a prior study.4

Despite differences in studies, our results converge with other published work. In agreement with other studies, elevated levels of IL-8 and IL-6 at baseline were associated with poorer OS in this cohort of patients. Specifically, elevated circulating levels of IL-8, IL-6, and MDSCs were found to predict worse outcomes in melanoma patients.30-32 IL-8 (CXCL8) may promote melanoma growth, survival and invasiveness as well as angiogenesis. CCL3 (MIP1α) levels were higher at baseline in non-responders compared to responders. This was also noted in our prior work with patients treated with CP and ipilimumab. Higher serum CCL3 in stage I-II melanoma was associated with worse OS in analyses carried out by Wang et al.33 However, CCL3 levels did not correlate with survival in our metastatic cohort.

When comparing the levels of circulating soluble factors in individual participants before and after 2 cycles of treatment in a paired analysis, we observed a shift in pro-inflammatory cytokines. The most marked changes were in CXCL9, CXCL10, and IL-17A, which increased in patients regardless of response outcome. Increases in CXCL927,34 and CXCL1026,27,29,34 have also been reported in patients treated with anti-PD-1 with or without anti-CTLA-4. Chow et al. found that CXCR3, which binds CXCL9 and CXCL10, in the tumor is essential for anti-PD-1 tumor responses. They also observed that plasma levels of CXCL9 and CXCL10 increased more markedly in responders compared to non-responders weeks after treatment with anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4.34 Similar to our results, increased levels of IL-10, TNFα, and IFNγ in melanoma patients after treatment with anti-PD-1 alone or dual checkpoint immunotherapy have also been reported, although individual fold change in these were not predictive of longer PFS.26 Others, using in vitro stimulation, have suggested that IL-17A production is involved in the response to checkpoint inhibitors.25 Our results also suggest that CP + pembrolizumab induces a proinflammatory peripheral response, the presence of which may be insufficient by itself to fully overcome tumor-associated immune suppression, which is not in opposition with a key role for the receptor-ligand pairs in the tumor microenvironment.

We are aware of limitations of this study, namely its non-randomized design and small sample size. Biomarker studies were exploratory in nature and would require assessment in a validation cohort. Given the scarcity of melanoma patients treated with chemotherapy and our results, such a cohort is unlikely to be assembled. It would be relevant to collect samples at the time of response assessment and at the end of chemotherapy, as paired analyses can be affected by attrition due to disease progression. A deeper understanding of both the tumor microenvironment and peripheral immune dynamics will be essential for developing and selecting personalized treatment with augmented efficacity and reduced toxicity burden. Our study nonetheless adds to the growing body of work to gain insight into immune pathways involved with treatment using minimally invasive methods. It may inform future studies seeking biomarkers in other malignancies treated with chemotherapy and PD-1 and/or identifying the common immune patterns associated with anti-PD-1 alone or used in combination. Until such biomarkers are validated, clinicians should exercise caution when considering the use of chemotherapy in combination with immunotherapy for melanoma, particularly as a first-line treatment.
