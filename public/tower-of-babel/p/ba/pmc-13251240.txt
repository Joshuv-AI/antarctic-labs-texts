Efficacy and safety of telitacicept in patients with progressive interstitial lung disease associated with antisynthetase syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, or Sjögren’s syndrome: a prospective observational study
Authors: Xue Chen, MingFang Sun, Ying Zhou, Fei Xiao, MengShan Li, YanLong Yang, HuanZi Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterstitial lung disease (ILD) is a severe complication of antisynthetase syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, and Sjögren’s syndrome, contributing significantly to patient mortality. Standard treatment options remain limited. This study evaluated the efficacy and safety of telitacicept, a dual-target inhibitor of B-lymphocyte stimulator and a proliferation-inducing ligand, in patients with these specific ILD subtypes.MethodsThis prospective observational study included 18 patients with ILD associated with antisynthetase syndrome (n = 5), rheumatoid arthritis (n = 5), or Sjögren’s syndrome (n = 8). Participants either demonstrated resistance to standard immunosuppressive regimens or presented with other connective tissue diseases (CTDs) and complex multisystemic manifestations. All patients received subcutaneous telitacicept (160 mg weekly) combined with glucocorticoids. Primary outcomes were absolute changes in forced vital capacity percentage (FVC%) and diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide percentage (DLCO%) at 24 weeks. Secondary outcomes included exercise capacity (6-minute walk test), radiographic changes (Warrick score), and disease activity indices. Comparisons between baseline and 24 weeks were performed using paired t-tests.ResultsAfter 24 weeks, lung function significantly improved across all groups. FVC% increased by 20.94% in antisynthetase syndrome-ILD, 10.83% in rheumatoid arthritis-ILD, and 12.19% in Sjögren’s syndrome-ILD (all P < 0.05). Similarly, DLCO% improved by 30.61%, 8.61%, and 9.73%, respectively (all P < 0.05). Exercise capacity significantly increased, with 6-minute walk distances improving by over 20% in all subtypes. Radiographic assessment showed a significant reduction in lung lesions, with Warrick scores decreasing by 44.73%, 27.04%, and 30.12%, respectively. Systemic disease activity also decreased significantly, and patients successfully reduced their daily prednisone dose to 5–7.5 mg. No adverse reactions were observed during the study period.ConclusionsTelitacicept demonstrated significant clinical efficacy and a favorable safety profile in patients with ILD associated with antisynthetase syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, and Sjögren’s syndrome. These findings suggest telitacicept may be a promising therapeutic option for refractory or complex connective tissue disease-associated ILD.

Background

Connective tissue diseases (CTDs) comprise a heterogeneous group of autoimmune disorders characterized by immune dysregulation and multisystem involvement. When interstitial lung disease (ILD) develops as a complication of CTDs, it is termed CTD–associated ILD (CTD-ILD) [1]. Among the various subtypes, antisynthetase syndrome (ASyS)–associated ILD, rheumatoid arthritis (RA)–associated ILD, and Sjögren’s syndrome (SJD)–associated ILD are relatively common in clinical practice [2]. Patients typically present with progressive dyspnea and declining pulmonary function, and in severe cases, the disease may advance to respiratory failure, leading to substantial mortality and impaired quality of life.

The primary goal of treatment in CTD-ILD is to control both the underlying CTD and the pulmonary manifestations, slow disease progression, prolong survival, and improve quality of life. However, therapeutic evidence remains limited, with most data derived from studies of systemic sclerosis–associated ILD [3]. Emerging evidence has also been reported for RA–associated ILD and idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs)–related ILD. Current treatment strategies include glucocorticoids; conventional immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide (CTX), calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), and azathioprine; biologic therapies including rituximab, tocilizumab, and tofacitinib; plasma exchange; hematopoietic stem cell transplantation; and antifibrotic agents [4, 5]. However, given the marked heterogeneity of clinical presentations across CTDs and the variability in ILD patterns, therapeutic approaches must be individualized according to patient characteristics and disease phenotype.

B-lymphocyte stimulator (BLyS) and a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL) are key cytokines involved in the maturation and differentiation of B cells and plasma cells, playing essential roles in B-cell survival, activation, and differentiation [6, 7]. Dysregulation of BLyS/APRIL signaling promotes the expansion of autoreactive B cells and sustained autoantibody production, leading to immune complex-mediated inflammation and activation of downstream cytokine pathways. This pathogenic process contributes to the development and progression of multiple autoimmune diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), RA, and primary SJD [8–10].

Telitacicept is a dual-target biologic agent designed to inhibit both BLyS and APRIL, thereby modulating B-cell function, reducing plasma cell differentiation, and suppressing aberrant immune responses [11]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that telitacicept effectively decreases autoantibody levels and ameliorates autoimmune inflammation. In randomized clinical trials, telitacicept significantly improved disease activity in B cell–driven autoimmune disorders such as SLE and primary SJD. For example, treatment with telitacicept reduced anti–double-stranded DNA antibody levels and overall disease activity in SLE [12, 13], and improved the EULAR SJD Disease Activity Index (ESSDAI) in patients with primary SJD [14]. Collectively, these findings underscore the immunomodulatory efficacy of telitacicept and support its potential as a therapeutic strategy for B cell–mediated autoimmune diseases.

Studies have demonstrated that circulating levels of BLyS and APRIL are elevated in patients with pulmonary fibrosis and in corresponding animal models [15–17]. In dermatomyositis, patients with ILD exhibit higher BLyS levels than those without pulmonary involvement [7]. Furthermore, individuals with CTD–associated ILD have significantly increased serum BLyS and APRIL concentrations compared with healthy controls, and elevated BLyS levels have been shown to correlate with disease severity [15]. These findings suggest that BLyS/APRIL signaling plays a contributory role in the initiation, progression, and fibrotic remodeling of ILD.

Based on this mechanistic rationale, we hypothesized that dual inhibition of BLyS and APRIL with telitacicept may suppress B-cell maturation and differentiation, reduce pathogenic autoantibody and immunoglobulin production, and thereby attenuate immune-mediated inflammation and fibrotic processes within the pulmonary interstitium. Guided by this biological premise, we administered telitacicept to patients with ILD whose conditions progressed despite conventional immunosuppressive therapy or who presented with concomitant CTD and multisystem involvement. In this prospective real-world study, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of telitacicept in CTD-associated ILD and to provide preliminary clinical evidence to inform future therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Regulatory status and indications of telitacicept

Telitacicept is the first BLyS/APRIL dual-target fusion protein independently developed in China. It has been formally approved by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) for the treatment of adult patients with SLE who continue to exhibit high disease activity despite standard therapy [18, 19], as well as for patients with moderate-to-severe RA who have had an inadequate response to methotrexate and for those with generalized myasthenia gravis [20]. Clinical trials evaluating telitacicept in IgA nephropathy (IgAN), SJD, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders, and multiple sclerosis have been completed or are ongoing, and several additional indications are currently under regulatory review.

Our group has previously reported favorable clinical efficacy and safety of telitacicept in patients with IIMs-ILD and SJD-ILD [21, 22]. However, telitacicept has not yet been approved for the treatment of CTD-ILD. Therefore, its use for ILD-related outcomes in the present study represents off-label administration. Treatment decisions were made by experienced clinicians following comprehensive evaluation of disease activity, prior therapeutic responses, and the pharmacological mechanism of telitacicept. All patients provided written informed consent, and treatment was conducted in accordance with institutional oversight and ethical approval.

Study design

This study was designed as a single-center, prospective, single-arm observational investigation. Patients were consecutively recruited from the Department of Rheumatology and Immunology at our institution between March 2023 and March 2025, provided they met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

No concurrent or historical control cohort receiving conventional therapy alone was prespecified or assembled; therefore, all analyses were conducted exclusively within the telitacicept-treated cohort. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of our hospital and was conducted in strict accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Standard-of-care treatment failure was defined as meeting at least two of the following criteria despite receiving standardized therapy with glucocorticoids and conventional immunosuppressive agents for a minimum of 6 months. Given our enrollment timeline and follow-up schedule, this framework was operationalized using a minimum 6-month assessment period after initiation of standard therapy. Definition of refractory patients

Patients with refractory ILD were defined as those diagnosed with ASyS-ILD, RA-ILD, or SJD-ILD whose ILD continued to progress despite treatment with glucocorticoids combined with conventional immunosuppressive agents and who fulfilled the criteria for failure of standard-of-care therapies as defined above.

Treatment-naive patients with complex ILD were defined as those diagnosed with ASyS-ILD, RA-ILD, or SJD-ILD, with no prior glucocorticoid or immunosuppressive therapy, and accompanied by other systemic involvement or connective tissue diseases.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met al.l of the following criteria: age ≥ 18 years; a diagnosis of ASyS according to the 2011 Solomon classification criteria [24], RA according to the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatology (ACR/EULAR) classification criteria [25], or SJD according to the 2016 ACR/EULAR classification criteria [26]; presence of ILD confirmed by chest high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT); patients presenting with treatment-refractory ILD despite receiving standard immunosuppressive regimens; or treatment-naive patients with complex ILD. In addition, patients were required to be able and willing to comply with scheduled follow-up assessments, including pulmonary function testing and HRCT.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: severe infection, active malignancy, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction (Child-Pugh class C or estimated glomerular filtration rate < 30 ml·min− 1·1.73 m− 2), known drug allergy, or a history of severe adverse drug reactions; pregnancy or lactation; participation in another clinical trial within 3 months prior to enrollment; requirement for additional biologic agents due to disease-specific indications; severe psychiatric disorders that could interfere with study evaluation or anticipated poor compliance; or failure to provide written informed consent.

Overlap case of primary SJD and SLE

One patient fulfilled both the 2016 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for primary SJD and the 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria for SLE. This case was classified as overlap primary SJD-SLE and was analyzed within the SJD-ILD subgroup according to the predefined grouping strategy.

Rationale for telitacicept initiation

In this study, telitacicept was evaluated as an immunomodulatory strategy in two predefined clinical scenarios: patients with refractory ILD following standard-of-care therapy and treatment-naive patients with complex ILD. This approach was based on the recognized role of B-cell–driven autoimmunity in the pathogenesis of both CTDs and CTD-ILD. By simultaneously neutralizing BAFF/BLyS and APRIL, critical factors for B-cell survival, activation, and plasma cell differentiation, telitacicept may attenuate autoreactive B-cell activity, suppress autoantibody production, and reduce immune-complex–mediated inflammation.

Treatment protocol

Glucocorticoids constituted the backbone of therapy for CTD-ILD in this study. All enrolled patients received continuous systemic glucocorticoid treatment throughout the study period. A standardized tapering regimen was implemented for all participants. Treatment was initiated with prednisone acetate at a starting dose of 0.8–1.0 mg/kg/day. Subsequent dose reduction followed a unified tapering schedule guided by the 2024 KDIGO recommendations for lupus nephritis, with gradual adjustment based on clinical response. By week 24, the glucocorticoid dose was tapered to a maintenance range of 5–7.5 mg/day [23]. Telitacicept was administered at a dose of 160 mg by subcutaneous injection once weekly in combination with glucocorticoid therapy.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes

The co-primary outcomes were the absolute changes in FVC% and DLCO% from baseline to week 24. These endpoints were selected a priori to capture complementary domains of ILD severity, with FVC% reflecting ventilatory capacity and DLCO% reflecting gas exchange impairment. Both measures are widely accepted and clinically meaningful indicators of ILD progression and therapeutic response. Changes in FVC% and DLCO% over 24 weeks were used to evaluate the effect of telitacicept on pulmonary function.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included exercise capacity assessed by the 6-minute walk test (6MWT); changes in oxygenation measured by the PaO2/FiO2 ratio; radiologic extent of ILD evaluated using the Warrick score on chest HRCT; inflammatory and serologic markers, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels; and changes in disease activity indices, including ESSDAI and EULAR Sjögren’s Syndrome Patient Reported Index (ESSPRI) for SJD-ILD, the disease activity score index of a 28-joint count (DAS28) for RA-ILD, and Manual Muscle Testing 8 (MMT8) and creatine kinase (CK) for ASyS-ILD. The glucocorticoid-sparing effect was assessed as the change in daily prednisone-equivalent dose. Safety outcomes included the incidence of adverse events and serious adverse events during the 24-week follow-up period.

Follow-up process

A screening team comprising two senior rheumatologists and pulmonologists was established to evaluate eligibility in strict accordance with the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible patients were fully informed about the study objectives, treatment protocol, follow-up schedule, and potential risks prior to enrollment. After providing written informed consent, participants were formally enrolled, and dedicated study records were created to document baseline clinical data. These included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, prior treatment history, organ involvement, laboratory findings, and imaging results (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Time frame

Patient recruitment was conducted between March 2023 and March 2025. All enrolled participants were followed for 24 weeks, with final follow-up completed by September 2025. Scheduled study visits occurred at baseline and at 4, 12, and 24 weeks after initiation of treatment.

Clinical data were obtained primarily from the hospital’s electronic medical records (EMR) system. Collected variables included demographic characteristics, medical and treatment history, symptom assessment, disease activity indices (DAS28, ESSPRI, ESSDAI), MMT8, pulmonary function parameters (FVC% and DLCO%), chest HRCT findings, 6MWT distance, Liver Stiffness Measurement (LSM) where applicable, and laboratory parameters, including renal function, urine protein, inflammatory markers, IgG, complement levels, ferritin, CK, and autoantibody profiles. Adverse events were systematically recorded throughout the study period. Data extraction was performed independently by two investigators in a blinded manner and cross-verified to ensure the accuracy and completeness of the data collection process.

HRCT pattern classification

Baseline HRCT scans were reviewed to determine the predominant ILD pattern. Radiologic patterns were classified as usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), organizing pneumonia (OP), lymphoid interstitial pneumonia (LIP), or diffuse alveolar damage (DAD) according to established radiologic criteria for CTD-ILD [27]. UIP-related features were evaluated using consensus HRCT diagnostic criteria. Pattern classification was performed through multidisciplinary discussion involving specialists in respiratory medicine and thoracic radiology. In cases demonstrating mixed radiologic features, categorization was based on the predominant pattern.

Warrick score assessment

The chest HRCT images were independently evaluated by three senior radiologists who were blinded to clinical data. The extent of interstitial involvement was quantified using the semi-quantitative scoring system proposed by Warrick [28]. In cases of discrepant interpretations, consensus was achieved through joint review and discussion. The mean score derived from the three radiologists was recorded as the final Warrick score for analysis.

Handling measures for confounding by indication

Given the observational single-arm design, several measures were implemented to mitigate potential confounding by indication. First, strict and predefined inclusion criteria were applied to ensure a clinically homogeneous population. Only patients with progressive ILD despite prior standard therapy or newly diagnosed complex ILD with systemic involvement were enrolled, thereby reducing selection bias related to disease severity and treatment history. Second, treatment responses were systematically assessed at prespecified follow-up time points over the 24-week study period to ensure consistent outcome evaluation. Third, stratified analyses were not conducted due to the limited sample size, as subgroup comparisons would have resulted in insufficient statistical power and potentially unreliable estimates. Despite these precautions, residual confounding by indication cannot be excluded, particularly given the absence of a control group and non-randomized treatment allocation. Therefore, causal inferences should be interpreted with caution.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between baseline and week 24 were conducted using paired t-tests. An available-case analysis approach was adopted, and no imputation was performed for missing data. In accordance with the 2024 KDIGO recommendations, glucocorticoid doses were tapered to a maintenance range of 5–7.5 mg/day during the study period. Owing to the absence of detailed longitudinal prednisone-equivalent dose data, the glucocorticoid-sparing effect was evaluated descriptively, and no formal statistical testing was applied for this outcome. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 18 patients were enrolled in the study, including 5 with ASyS-ILD, 5 with RA-ILD, and 8 with SJD-ILD. Among the ASyS-ILD patients, four were positive for anti–Jo-1 antibodies and one for anti–PL-12 antibodies. All five patients had experienced ILD progression despite prior treatment with glucocorticoids, MMF, CTX, and tacrolimus. In the RA-ILD group, articular symptoms had improved under treatment with glucocorticoids, methotrexate, leflunomide, CTX, and tofacitinib; however, ILD continued to progress. Within this subgroup, one patient had concomitant IgAN with renal insufficiency, and another had primary biliary cholangitis (PBC). Among the eight patients with SJD-ILD, one had tubulointerstitial nephritis, and one fulfilled criteria for overlap primary SJD and SLE (SJD classified according to the 2016 ACR/EULAR criteria and SLE according to the 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria). The remaining six patients demonstrated ILD progression despite treatment with glucocorticoids, MMF, and CTX. Baseline characteristics of the entire cohort are summarized in Table 1.

During follow-up, changes in selected extrapulmonary manifestations and comorbid autoimmune conditions were recorded at the individual patient level. LSM decreased by 38.6% in one patient with RA and PBC. In one patient with RA and IgAN, renal function normalized and proteinuria resolved. In a patient with overlap primary SJD-SLE, the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2 K) score decreased to 0. No cases of allergy, infection, leukopenia, gastrointestinal adverse events, or neurological complications were observed during the 24-week follow-up. Reductions in IgG levels were mild and did not result in clinically significant events, supporting the favorable safety profile of telitacicept in this cohort.

Discussion

Telitacicept simultaneously neutralizes BAFF/BLyS and APRIL, thereby providing a mechanistic rationale for modulating B cell-driven immune dysregulation. Previous randomized controlled trials in systemic CTDs have demonstrated efficacy at established disease activity endpoints. For example, a phase IIb trial in SLE reported improved SRI-4 response rates at 48 weeks, and a subsequent phase III study confirmed that the modified SRI-4 response at 52 weeks was superior to placebo [13, 29]. In primary SJD, phase II studies showed improvement in ESSDAI at 24 weeks, and phase III data further demonstrated that both 80 mg and 160 mg doses significantly reduced ESSDAI at 24 weeks, with sustained efficacy through 48 weeks and no new safety concerns [14]. In RA, a phase III randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial presented at the ACR annual meeting reported improved clinical response and an acceptable safety profile in patients with inadequate response to MTX [30]. In contrast, evidence regarding ILD-specific outcomes remains limited and is currently derived primarily from small CTD-ILD cohorts and observational studies in IIM-ILD [21, 22]. Against this backdrop, the present study provides prospective real-world data on 24-week pulmonary outcomes, offering preliminary evidence regarding the clinical effectiveness and safety of telitacicept in ILD.

According to institutional registry data, ASyS-ILD, RA-ILD, and SJD-ILD represent the three most prevalent CTD-ILD subtypes at our center. These subgroups provided sufficient case numbers to permit meaningful descriptive analysis within the constraints of a single-center cohort. In contrast, other CTD-ILD subtypes, including dermatomyositis, systemic sclerosis, and mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD), were less frequently encountered during the study period, and case numbers were insufficient to support subgroup evaluation. Consequently, this study focused on the three most common subtypes and did not include other CTD-ILD phenotypes.

Our findings suggest that telitacicept was associated with favorable therapeutic responses in ASyS-ILD, RA-ILD, and SJD-ILD. No treatment-related adverse events were observed during the 24-week follow-up, and all patients successfully tapered prednisone to a maintenance dose of 5–7.5 mg/day. Improvements in exercise capacity were clinically meaningful: 6MWT distance increased by 88.9 m in ASyS-ILD, 77.1 m in RA-ILD, and 69.0 m in SJD-ILD, exceeding the reported minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of approximately 29–34 m for diffuse parenchymal lung diseases [31]. These gains indicate perceptible improvements in functional status and daily activity. Pulmonary function also improved across subgroups. FVC% increased by 20.94% in ASyS-ILD, 10.83% in RA-ILD, and 12.19% in SJD-ILD, each meeting or surpassing the ≥ 10% threshold commonly regarded as clinically meaningful in CTD-ILD outcome studies [32]. DLCO% increased by 30.61%, 8.61%, and 9.73% in the respective groups. The pronounced improvement in ASyS-ILD suggests substantial recovery of diffusion capacity, whereas the more modest changes in RA-ILD and SJD-ILD were directionally consistent with concurrent improvements in oxygenation, exercise tolerance, and imaging findings. Notably, improvements in FVC% and DLCO% were accompanied by parallel gains in 6MWT and radiologic scores, supporting a multidimensional treatment effect rather than isolated changes in single parameters. Systemic disease activity also decreased. In RA-ILD, DAS28 reached clinical remission (< 2.6) [33]. In SJD-ILD, ESSDAI improved by ≥ 3 points and ESSPRI decreased by ≥ 1 point or ≥ 15%, achieving a patient-acceptable symptom state (ESSPRI < 5) [34]. In ASyS-ILD, muscle strength improved, as reflected by a 19.4% increase in MMT8 and normalization of CK levels, consistent with enhanced functional performance. Nevertheless, given the absence of a control group and the concomitant use of glucocorticoids, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and confirmation in controlled studies is warranted.

Beyond pulmonary endpoints, improvements were also observed in selected extrapulmonary manifestations in individual cases. Although these changes were not prespecified study outcomes, they are biologically plausible given telitacicept’s mechanism as a TACI-Fc fusion protein that neutralizes BAFF/BLyS and APRIL. By attenuating aberrant B-cell activation, plasmablast and plasma cell survival, autoantibody production, and immune complex–mediated inflammation, telitacicept may exert broader immunomodulatory effects across multiple organ systems. Nevertheless, given the uncontrolled and non-randomized design of this study, the consistency of positive findings across domains should be interpreted with caution.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, this was a small, single-center observational study that included only ASyS-ILD, RA-ILD, and SJD-ILD; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to other CTD-ILD subtypes or to a broader CTD-ILD population. Second, the absence of randomization, protocol-driven treatment allocation, and a control group—particularly a conventional-therapy comparator—substantially limits causal inference. As a result, the observed improvements cannot be definitively attributed to telitacicept, and potential sources of bias, including confounding by indication and regression to the mean, cannot be excluded. In addition, this study did not incorporate ILD-specific patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures, such as the King’s Brief Interstitial Lung Disease questionnaire (K-BILD) or the St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire for Interstitial Lung Disease (SGRQ-I). Although disease activity instruments including ESSDAI, ESSPRI, DAS28, and MMT8 were used to capture certain patient-reported and functional aspects, these measures do not fully reflect the impact of ILD on health-related quality of life, respiratory symptoms, and daily functioning. The absence of validated ILD-specific PROs limits comprehensive assessment of patient-centered outcomes.

Despite these limitations, our findings suggest that telitacicept was associated with improvements in both systemic disease activity and ILD-related parameters in patients with ASyS-ILD, RA-ILD, and SJD-ILD, without significant safety concerns during the 24-week follow-up. These preliminary results indicate potential clinical benefit and support further investigation of telitacicept as a therapeutic option in CTD-ILD. However, larger prospective studies are required to validate these findings. Future research should include broader CTD-ILD subtypes, incorporate randomized controlled designs, ideally in multicenter settings, and adhere to predefined treatment allocation protocols. Integration of ILD-specific PROs alongside clinical, physiological, and radiologic endpoints will be essential to more comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of telitacicept in CTD-ILD.
