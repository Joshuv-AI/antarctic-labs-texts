Preventive and therapeutic effects of cepharanthine on acetaminophen-induced acute liver and kidney injury in male laboratory mice
Authors: Neda Askaripour, Ali Akbar Oroojan, Mohammad Amin Behmanesh, Pouya Imanpour, Marjan Aliabdi, Soraya Khajerezaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim: This study aimed to evaluate the preventive and therapeutic effects of cepharanthine on APAP (N-acetyl-para-aminophenol )-induced liver and kidney injury in an experimental model.Background:Acetaminophen (APAP) is widely used as an analgesic and antipyretic drug; however, its overdose can cause acute liver and kidney injury. Cepharanthine, a natural alkaloid with antioxidant properties, has been proposed as a potential protective agent. Methods:In this experimental study 40 Male NMRI mice were divided into five groups: Control, Sham, APAP-treated, APAP + preventive cepharanthine, and APAP + therapeutic cepharanthine groups. Liver and kidney function markers, including ALT, AST, BUN, creatinine, total antioxidant capacity (TAC), and malondialdehyde (MDA), were measured. Histopathological analysis was performed to assess tissue damage. Statistical analysis was conducted using ANOVA with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.Results:APAP administration significantly increased ALT, AST (p < 0.001), and MDA (p < 0.01) while reducing TAC levels (p < 0.01) in liver and kidney tissues. Cepharanthine, particularly in the therapeutic group, reduced ALT levels (p < 0.05) and improved liver histology but did not significantly alter AST or TAC in the prevention group. In contrast, cepharanthine failed to improve kidney function markers and worsened histological damage, leading to increased tubular casts and hemorrhagic areas.Conclusion:Cepharanthine exhibited partial hepatoprotective effects against APAP-induced liver injury but did not improve kidney damage. So, this natural alkaloid does not confer a significant protective effect against acute kidney injury. Key Words: Acetaminophen, Cepharanthine, Liver, Kidney, Injury

Introduction

Acute liver injury is characterized by the rapid loss of liver cell function in a patient without preexisting liver disease. Acute liver failure is classified into acute, subacute, and hyperacute types based on the time interval between the onset of jaundice and the development of hepatic encephalopathy, which serves as a crucial prognostic factor in the disease progression (1).

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common clinical syndrome characterized by a sudden decline in kidney function over a short period. The pathogenesis of AKI is complex and involves multiple pathological processes such as inflammation, apoptosis, and necrosis. A large-scale, multicenter epidemiological study of critically ill patients revealed that drug-induced nephrotoxicity accounts for 19% of AKI cases (2).

Acetaminophen (APAP: N-acetyl-para-aminophenol) is one of the most widely used analgesics and antipyretic drugs worldwide. While it is safe and effective at therapeutic doses, it has a narrow therapeutic window, and overdose can be highly toxic. Due to its widespread availability and accessibility, APAP hepatotoxicity has become the most common cause of acute liver failure in the United States and other Western countries. Acetaminophen poisoning leads to approximately 70,000 hospitalizations annually in the U.S. Overall, APAP overdose is recognized as a significant public health concern (3). Symptoms such as right upper quadrant abdominal pain, vomiting, hepatomegaly, and encephalopathy indicate severe APAP intoxication and toxicity (4). Excessive APAP consumption depletes sulfate and glutathione reserves in the liver and kidneys, leading to increased production of N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI) via the cytochrome P450 (CYP2E1) metabolic pathway. This reaction initiates lipid peroxidation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and the formation of other free radicals, ultimately triggering oxidative stress and tissue damage in the liver and kidneys (5-7).

Cepharanthine is a bisbenzylisoquinoline (BBI) alkaloid extracted from Stephania cepharantha, known for its antioxidant properties (8). Previous studies have demonstrated that cepharanthine exhibits significant antioxidant activity, comparable to that of standard antioxidants like α-tocopherol, in reducing oxygen radicals and oxidative stress. Thus, cepharanthine may be used to minimize or prevent lipid oxidation and reduce the formation of toxic oxidative products (9). Cepharanthine has also been reported to mitigate ischemia-reperfusion-induced injuries in the lungs and kidneys. Additionally, it inhibits the proliferation of various cancer cells, including human hepatocellular carcinoma and kidney carcinoma cells. Importantly, cepharanthine has been identified as a compound that prevents neuronal cell death and suppresses inflammatory cytokine production. In one study, cepharanthine inhibited lipid peroxidation in the liver of mice with lipopolysaccharide-induced liver damage (10). Cepharanthine significantly alleviates oxidative stress in a mouse model of transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO). Also, administration of this alkaloid at a concentration of 10 µM effectively reduces the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and mediators—including IL-6, IL-1β, iNOS, and COX-2—in LPS-stimulated macrophages, while simultaneously increasing GSH levels and decreasing ROS production. These results highlight the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of cepharanthine (11, 12).

Given the increasing use of acetaminophen, along with the rising incidence of overdose-related toxicity and the limitations of current treatments, there is a growing need to explore novel therapeutic approaches. The development of new strategies—such as the use of antioxidant compounds with fewer side effects—could help address these challenges and ultimately improve the management of acetaminophen poisoning. So, according to the high prevalence of acetaminophen use, its role in inducing acute liver and kidney injury through oxidative stress, and the antioxidant potential of cepharanthine, this study aims to evaluate its protective and therapeutic effects against acetaminophen-induced acute hepatic and kidney injury in male mice.

Methods

Animal preparation

In this experimental study, 40 male NMRI laboratory mice, weighing between 25 and 30 grams, were obtained from the Animal Breeding and Maintenance Center of Dezful University of Medical Sciences. The mice were housed under a 12-hour light-dark cycle with free access to food and water. To induce acute liver injury, the animals were fasted for 12 hours before receiving an intraperitoneal (IP) injection of acetaminophen (300 mg/kg) (13).

For the prevention of acetaminophen-induced liver injury, cepharanthine (10 mg/kg) was administered via gavage 2 hours before the IP injection of acetaminophen. For the therapeutic approach, cepharanthine (10 mg/kg) was administered 6 hours after the acetaminophen injection. Accordingly, the animals were divided into five groups (8 mice per group):

1. Control group – received no treatment.

2. Vehicle group – received an intraperitoneal injection of normal saline and gavage of DMSO.

3. Acetaminophen group – received an intraperitoneal injection of acetaminophen (300 mg/kg) (13, 14).

4. Cepharanthine-prevention group – received cepharanthine (10 mg/kg) 2 hours before acetaminophen injection (300 mg/kg).

5. Cepharanthine-treatment group – received cepharanthine (10 mg/kg) 6 hours after acetaminophen injection (300 mg/kg) (10, 15).

Considering that the serum effects of acetaminophen begin within 2 hours and its full tissue effects manifest after 6 hours, cepharanthine was administered 2 hours prior to acetaminophen injection in the preventive group, and 6 hours after injection in the therapeutic group (16).

Biochemical and antioxidant assessments

Twenty-four hours after drug administration, the animals were deeply anesthetized using a ketamine-xylazine mixture (70-10 mg/kg). Blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture, and serum was separated for the biochemical evaluation of blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) using specific biochemical assay kits (Pars Peyvand Co, Iran).

Additionally, the right kidney and a portion of the liver were excised, homogenized in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 minutes. The supernatant was used to measure malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), and total antioxidant capacity (TAC) levels (17).

Histological analysis of the liver and kidneys

At the end of the experiment, liver and kidney tissue samples were rapidly collected after deep anesthesia and fixed in 10% formalin. The tissues were processed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5–7 μm thick slices. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under a light microscope to assess histopathological changes. At least six slides per animal were prepared for both liver and kidney tissue analyses (18). Specific features evaluated included: inflammatory cell infiltration, disruption of normal tissue architecture, necrosis, fibrosis, vascular congestion, and edema. Each of these parameters was scored semi-quantitatively on a scale from 0 to 4, as follows: 0 = Absent / Normal condition; 1 = Mild; 2 = Moderate; 3 = Severe (For certain features such as necrosis or fibrosis, a score of 4 was assigned for extensive involvement). The total histopathological score was calculated by summing the individual scores to provide an overall index of injury severity for each sample. This scoring system allowed for consistent and reproducible comparison between experimental groups.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for statistical comparisons. Results were expressed as Mean ± SEM, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effect of cepharanthine on serum ALT and AST levels

The results of liver enzyme analysis showed that acetaminophen significantly increased serum ALT levels compared to the control group (P < 0.01). However, ALT levels were significantly reduced in both the cepharanthine + acetaminophen and acetaminophen + cepharanthine groups compared to the acetaminophen group (P < 0.01; Figure 1). Serum AST levels were significantly elevated in the acetaminophen group, as well as in the cepharanthine + acetaminophen and acetaminophen + cepharanthine groups, compared to the control group (P < 0.05; Figure 2).

Effect of cepharanthine on serum BUN and creatinine levels

Serum BUN levels were not significantly elevated following acetaminophen administration compared to the control group. However, in the acetaminophen + cepharanthine group, serum BUN levels significantly decreased compared to both the control group (P < 0.05) and the acetaminophen group (P < 0.01; Figure 3). Serum creatinine levels significantly decreased in the acetaminophen group (P < 0.05), cepharanthine + acetaminophen group (P < 0.01), and acetaminophen + cepharanthine group (P < 0.001) compared to the control group. Moreover, creatinine levels in the acetaminophen + cepharanthine group were significantly lower than in the acetaminophen group (P < 0.05; Figure 4).

Effect of cepharanthine on hepatic MDA, GSH, and TAC levels

Lipid peroxidation levels, measured by MDA, significantly increased in the acetaminophen group compared to the control group (P < 0.05). Additionally, MDA levels in both cepharanthine-treated groups (before and after acetaminophen administration) were significantly higher than in the control (P < 0.01) and acetaminophen (P < 0.05) groups. The measurement of hepatic GSH levels showed no significant differences among the control, vehicle, acetaminophen, and cepharanthine-treated groups. TAC levels in the liver significantly increased in the acetaminophen, cepharanthine + acetaminophen, and acetaminophen + cepharanthine groups compared to the control group (P < 0.05). Moreover, TAC levels in the acetaminophen + cepharanthine group were significantly higher than in the acetaminophen group (P < 0.05; Table 1).

Effect of cepharanthine on acetaminophen-induced liver and kidney histopathology

Histopathological analysis of liver tissues revealed increased inflammation, sinusoidal dilation, central vein dilation, and hepatocyte necrosis in the acetaminophen group. Cepharanthine administration improved acetaminophen-induced acute liver injury, with a more pronounced protective effect observed in the group that received cepharanthine 6 hours after acetaminophen injection (Figure 5).

Liver

The present study showed that acetaminophen led to a significant increase in liver enzymes ALT and AST. Additionally, the drug increased MDA levels, indicating enhanced lipid peroxidation in the liver. The absence of significant GSH changes and an increase in TAC following acetaminophen administration suggest that the liver injury in this model was acute. The rise in MDA likely triggered the antioxidant defense system, leading to an increase in TAC. However, with prolonged liver injury, this increase may become ineffective, and TAC levels might return to normal over time, as seen in previous studies (19).

Both prophylactic and therapeutic administration of cepharanthine demonstrated its protective effects. Although cepharanthine had no significant impact on AST levels, it significantly reduced the elevated ALT levels induced by acetaminophen. The antioxidant defense system analysis suggests that, similar to acetaminophen, cepharanthine treatment increased MDA, which in turn stimulated the antioxidant response. This was evident by the significant increase in TAC levels in the cepharanthine-treated groups, potentially contributing to ALT normalization. While this effect was more pronounced in the therapeutic group, the prophylactic administration of cepharanthine did not show a significant TAC increase compared to the acetaminophen group. This suggests that cepharanthine may act through alternative pathways to reduce ALT levels, which warrants further investigation to determine its precise mechanism.

TAC plays a crucial role in suppressing excessive ROS production and protecting cells from oxidative stress. In cancer patients, elevated TAC levels are often attributed to adaptive responses to increased oxidative stress. Additionally, increased free radicals frequently stimulate the production of antioxidant molecules (20). Based on the current study, cepharanthine and acetaminophen, by inducing oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, triggered an increase in TAC to counteract acute liver damage. This effect was more pronounced in the acetaminophen-treated group receiving cepharanthine.

ALT and AST are sensitive markers of liver cell damage. ALT is highly concentrated in liver cells but is present in very low amounts in other tissues. In contrast, AST is found in multiple tissues, including the kidneys, brain, heart, skeletal, and smooth muscles. Thus, ALT is considered a more specific marker for liver injury. AST exists in both the cytoplasm and mitochondria of hepatocytes, whereas ALT is found only in the cytoplasm. Approximately 80% of AST activity in the liver is mitochondrial, while ALT activity primarily comes from the cytosolic isoenzyme. Consequently, any process leading to hepatocyte necrosis or cell membrane damage results in the release of AST and ALT into the bloodstream (21). Given this, cepharanthine may have stabilized hepatocyte membranes, preventing ALT release into the bloodstream. However, the increase in AST might be due to its release from non-hepatic tissues, necessitating further investigation into the effects of cepharanthine on other organs.

Histological analysis in the present study showed that acetaminophen-induced liver injury was associated with increased inflammation, sinusoidal dilation, central vein enlargement, and hepatocyte necrosis. Prophylactic cepharanthine administration reduced inflammation and other pathological changes caused by acetaminophen, while therapeutic administration had an even more pronounced effect. This suggests that cepharanthine may be more effective in treating acetaminophen-induced liver toxicity rather than preventing it. Supporting this, Şener G et al. (2006) demonstrated that acetaminophen induces inflammatory necrosis in the liver through oxidative stress. However, resveratrol, an antioxidant compound, alleviated acetaminophen-induced liver damage and protected the liver from further oxidative stress and fibrosis (22). Similarly, a study by Bae GH et al. (2017) on mice confirmed that acetaminophen-induced acute liver injury led to significant inflammation, sinusoidal dilation, and tissue destruction, along with increased ALT and AST levels, which aligns with our findings (23). ALT is most concentrated in periportal hepatocytes and least in centrilobular hepatocytes. In contrast, AST is distributed more evenly throughout the liver. Centrilobular hepatocytes receive the lowest oxygen supply, making them more vulnerable to oxidative stress and acute hepatic ischemia, such as that caused by acetaminophen toxicity. This susceptibility results in elevated ALT levels. Additionally, centrilobular necrosis leads to a rapid increase in aminotransferases, with AST levels rising higher than ALT in the early stages of liver injury (21), as observed in the histopathological findings of the present study.

Kidney

The present study showed that acetaminophen administration led to a decrease in blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine, and total antioxidant capacity (TAC) in kidney tissue. On the other hand, cepharanthine, particularly in therapeutic use, did not improve acetaminophen-induced acute kidney injury (AKI) and even appeared to sustain the damage.

Creatinine is an endogenous compound derived from muscle creatine, freely filtered by the glomerulus, and commonly used as a marker of kidney function. Its concentration is influenced not only by glomerular filtration rate (GFR) but also by non-GFR factors, such as muscle mass, dietary intake, tubular secretion, and extrarenal elimination via the gastrointestinal tract. Additionally, liver disease can reduce creatinine production (24). The decrease in serum creatinine levels observed in acute hepatic toxicity is primarily due to reduced creatinine production. The liver plays a pivotal role in the final step of creatine biosynthesis, particularly through the methylation of guanidinoacetate by S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to form creatine. In the context of acute hepatocellular injury, this biochemical pathway is disrupted, resulting in insufficient creatine availability for normal creatinine generation. Consequently, the serum creatinine level is reduced during this metabolic alteration (25). Since acetaminophen-induced acute liver injury is one of the most commonly used experimental models in mice (13), it is plausible that the reduction in serum creatinine observed in this study resulted from impaired liver function.

The body's antioxidant defense system protects against reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress. When this defense system is overwhelmed by toxins, free radicals, ROS, and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), oxidative stress develops (26). In alignment with this, Hussain Z et al. (2019) reported that acetaminophen significantly reduced kidney TAC levels at 4, 8, and 24 hours after administration in mice (27). A study by Kao MC et al. (2015) demonstrated that cepharanthine improved antioxidant defense and reduced lipid peroxidation in mice subjected to kidney ischemia-reperfusion injury after 24 hours (28). In the early phase of acute hepatic injury, hepatocytes attempt to protect themselves by enhancing their antioxidant defenses. As a result, the total antioxidant capacity (TAC) of the liver may transiently increase as a compensatory mechanism. In contrast, the kidneys are highly sensitive to the toxic and reactive metabolites of acetaminophen, such as N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI). Unlike the liver, the kidneys have a limited capacity for rapid regeneration of glutathione and antioxidant enzymes. Therefore, in most cases, the renal TAC—particularly in the cortex and proximal tubules—decreases during acetaminophen toxicity. This reduction contributes to oxidative damage, tubular necrosis, and ultimately, acute kidney failure (29, 30). However, no prior studies have examined its effects in acetaminophen-induced AKI models. This study, being the first to do so, produced conflicting results compared to previous findings. This discrepancy may stem from multiple factors, including antioxidant type and dosage, treatment duration, and animal species used (31). Future research should explore cepharanthine at different doses and in chronic kidney injury models or alternative AKI models.

BUN is the end product of hepatic ammonia detoxification and can sometimes serve as a liver function marker. BUN levels may decrease when hepatic conversion of ammonia to urea is impaired (32), as seen in liver disease (33). After glomerular filtration, a large portion of solutes, including urea, is reabsorbed in the proximal tubules to prevent excessive loss of essential metabolites. This reabsorption is mediated by specific transporters. Dysfunction in proximal tubule function leads to impaired solute transport (34). Acetaminophen-induced kidney injury not only damages the kidney structurally but also disrupts proximal tubule function (35). In this study, therapeutic cepharanthine further reduced BUN levels compared to both healthy and acetaminophen-treated mice. This suggests that cepharanthine, in this acute acetaminophen nephrotoxicity model, was not beneficial and instead exacerbated acetaminophen-induced kidney damage. However, considering the dose- and time-dependent effects of antioxidants, it is recommended that cepharanthine be tested in longer-duration studies.

Histological analysis revealed that acetaminophen-induced kidney injury led to increased inflammation, tubular necrosis, and glomerular damage. Cepharanthine, when used prophylactically, failed to reduce inflammation, and its therapeutic use did not improve acetaminophen-induced damage. Instead, it worsened kidney injury, leading to hemorrhage and increased tubular casts in treated animals. These findings suggest that cepharanthine may not be suitable for treating acute acetaminophen nephrotoxicity. Given the dose-dependent and time-sensitive nature of antioxidants, future studies should investigate cepharanthine in chronic kidney injury models. Supporting these results, Şahin S et al. (2024) reported that high-dose acetaminophen causes tubular necrosis, inflammation, hemorrhagic areas, glomerular damage, and epithelial shedding in kidney tissue (36). The most common type of casts observed in kidney biopsies is hyaline casts, which are homogeneous, rounded structures filling the loop of Henle and distal tubules. These casts are composed of Tamm-Horsfall protein, a glycoprotein secreted by tubular epithelial cells (37). Similar to the effects of vancomycin and cisplatin in previous studies, the present study suggests that cepharanthine, by reducing kidney TAC levels, may have contributed to the formation of hyaline casts in the kidney tubules.

Limitations

Considering the different results of this study investigating the effect of cepharantine on the kidney and liver in the acute model of acetaminophen-induced toxicity, it can be stated that acute models induced in animals have several limitations when compared to chronic models. Acute models reflect only short-term effects, typically within hours, which fail to capture the long-term pathophysiological processes seen in many chronic conditions. In contrast, chronic models allow the evaluation of progressive, adaptive, and cumulative changes. Secondly, the high doses and non-clinical routes of administration often used in acute models (e.g., intraperitoneal injections) do not accurately replicate human exposures. Moreover, chronic models are more suitable for evaluating standard therapeutic regimens and long-term safety profiles (38-40). So, it could be suggested that chronic models of liver and renal injury may better reflect induced toxicity and cepharantine responses in future studies.

Conclusion

Overall, the administration of acetaminophen induces acute liver injury by increasing liver tissue damage and elevating hepatic enzymes ALT and AST. This effect is mediated through enhanced lipid peroxidation and increased MDA levels in the liver. Additionally, acetaminophen causes acute kidney injury by increasing kidney tissue damage and reducing TAC levels in the kidney. The use of Cepharanthine as both a preventive and therapeutic agent demonstrated its potential in mitigating liver tissue damage and reducing ALT levels. While the therapeutic effects of Cepharanthine may be suggested to be linked to an increase in TAC levels, the enzymatic changes observed in the preventive group are likely mediated through alternative cellular pathways, necessitating further investigation. Moreover, given that acute liver injury impairs hepatic production of BUN and creatinine, leading to reduced serum levels of these markers, the observed decreases in BUN and creatinine in this study may be attributed to impaired hepatic synthesis rather than improved kidney function. Furthermore, Cepharanthine, particularly in its therapeutic application, failed to reverse the detrimental effects of acetaminophen on kidney function, suggesting that it does not confer a significant protective effect against acute kidney injury. So, the findings of this study do not support the use of Cepharanthine in the management of acute liver and kidney injury. Given the dose- and time-dependent nature of antioxidant effects, further research using chronic injury models with extended treatment durations is recommended to explore the potential therapeutic and preventive benefits of Cepharanthine more effectively.
