Non-coding RNAs-mediated regulation of the MAPK pathway in gastric cancer: translational perspectives
Authors: Darmadi Darmadi, Komeil Aghazadeh-Habashi, Sona Ayvazyan, Arshia Bozorgnia, Elnaz Khodabandehloo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) have been acknowledged as master regulators of various cellular behaviors, and their dysregulation contributes to the development, progression, and therapeutic resistance of various cancers, including gastric cancer (GC). The mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) signaling pathways are frequently dysregulated in GC, leading to tumor initiation, progression, cell death resistance, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Emerging evidence supports the critical role of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), including microRNAs (miRNAs) and long ncRNAs (lncRNAs), in the regulation of MAPK pathways. These molecules can act as oncogenes or tumor suppressors by affecting components of the MAPK pathway or regulating their upstream regulators/downstream effectors. MAPK-regulating ncRNAs can drive various behaviors of malignant cells, including tumor growth and progression, angiogenesis, metastasis, immune evasion, and drug resistance. Understanding the regulatory network of MAPK-related ncRNAs can help develop novel biomarkers for diagnosis, predict prognosis, and stratify patients before treatment. Moreover, MAPK-regulating ncRNAs can serve as potential therapeutic targets in GC. In this review, we present an overview of current insights into ncRNA-mediated regulation of the MAPK pathway in GC, emphasizing the clinical relevance of these molecules. Moreover, the paths and challenges of the clinical use of MAPK-regulating ncRNAs as diagnostic/predictive biomarkers and therapeutic targets are comprehensively discussed. By elucidating the mechanistic roles of MAPK-regulating ncRNAs in GC, this review provides valuable insights into novel molecular biomarkers and innovative ncRNA-based therapies in GC management.Keywords: Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), MiRNAs, lncRNAs, MAPK signaling pathway, Gastric cancer, Stomach cancer

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks as the 5th most common cause of mortality due to cancer globally [1]. Known for its aggressive nature, GC presents a low survival rate in advanced stages [2]. According to the recently released GLOBOCAN report, approximately 968,000 new cases of GC were diagnosed in 2022. Also, the GC was responsible for 660,000 deaths [1]. As shown in Fig. 1, a significant portion of annual cancer incidence and mortality belongs to GC, highlighting its high prevalence and invasive nature. At the early stages of GC, symptoms are usually atypical, making it difficult to diagnose the disease in its early stages. Hence, most GC cases are diagnosed when the cancer is already advanced, which correlates with an unfavorable prognosis [3]. Despite advancements in cancer treatment, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and molecular targeted therapy, the overall survival of GC remains poor [4]. Therefore, overcoming the limitations of current diagnostic and therapeutic approaches requires a deeper understanding of GC’s underlying biology to identify novel biomarkers for earlier detection, accurate prognosis, and more effective treatment strategies.

Research consistently indicates that MAPK signaling is crucial for regulating division, differentiation, migration, apoptosis, inflammation, and immunity—processes whose dysregulation is fundamental to carcinogenesis [7, 8]. The MAPK pathway can be dysregulated in GC due to the change in the expression of signaling components. Dysregulation of MAPK components can result from gene mutations or alterations in gene expression at the DNA, RNA, and protein levels [9]. Mutations in genes that encode MAPK signaling kinases are associated with the development of different forms of GC. For instance, mutated forms of Ras and Raf kinases are commonly detected in gastrointestinal cancers and gastric polyps [10–14].

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) regulate epigenetic, transcriptional, and post-transcriptional events [15], and the aberrant expression of them is found in a broad spectrum of malignancies, such as GC [16, 17]. Based on their length and function, ncRNAs are primarily classified into two distinct categories: small (sncRNAs) and long (lncRNAs). sncRNAs are fewer than 200 nt long and include microRNAs (miRNAs), short-interfering RNAs (siRNAs), and piwi-interacting RNAs (piRNAs). lncRNAs are characterized by a length exceeding 200 nt and include regulatory subclasses of circular RNAs (circRNAs), long-intergenic RNAs (lincRNAs), enhancer RNAs (eRNAs), and natural antisense transcripts (NATs) [18]. The ncRNAs mediate their biological functions through mechanisms such as guiding chromatin-modifying proteins, acting as enhancers, forming RNP complexes, acting as a co-factor of transcriptional factors, post-transcriptional regulation of mRNAs, and regulating translational factors and signaling proteins [19–22].

miRNAs and lncRNAs are modulators of the MAPK pathway; their dysregulation can trigger constitutive MAPK signaling, which promotes oncogenesis and tumor progression [23, 24]. Mounting evidence reveals that the MAPK cascade participates in cell proliferation and differentiation, metastasis, invasion, angiogenesis, and immune responses during the development of GC [25–28]. Therefore, investigating the ncRNA signature in the MAPK pathway can lead to finding potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for GC. Furthermore, targeting MAPK-related ncRNAs can be a promising therapeutic strategy for attenuating MAPK signaling and thereby controlling tumor-associated mechanisms in GC.

This review comprehensively discusses the role of ncRNAs in regulating MAPK signaling in GC to decipher the intricate interaction between ncRNAs and the MAPK pathway, which is implicated in tumor development. Understanding the underlying mechanism of ncRNA-mediated MAPK regulation in GC can help to develop valuable therapeutic targets for the treatment of this prevalent cancer.

MAPK signaling pathways

Each cascade of MAPK is composed of three kinases, which are turned on sequentially: a MAPK kinase kinase (MAPKKK), a MAPK kinase (MAPKK), and a MAP kinase (MAPK) [29]. External stimuli induce phosphorylation of MAPKKKs by receptor-associated kinases and/or interaction with a small GTPase [30]. MAPKKK phosphorylates and activates MAPKK, which then triggers phosphorylation of MAPK on both Thr and Tyr amino acids. To initiate biological functions, MAPKs activate their downstream signaling molecules, which are phosphorylated in the cytoplasm and nucleus to change the expression of the target molecules [31]. There are three main MAPK modules in mammalian cells: ERK/MAPK, JNK/MAPK, and p38/MAPK (Fig. 2).

Different modules of the MAPK signaling pathway in mammalian cells. GF: growth factor; GPCR: G protein-coupled receptor

ERK1 is the first discovered MAPK, and it has been found that ERK1 is phosphorylated on tyrosine and threonine amino acids in response to growth factors (GFs) [32, 33]. Two main isoforms of ERKs are ERK1 and ERK2, which are 83% identical in structure [34]. Growth factors, cytokines, mitogens, and G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) agonists initiate the ERK/MAPK pathway by binding to surface receptors, including receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), GPCRs, and integrins [35]. In the ERK/MAPK module, Raf isoforms are the major MAPKKKs, MEK1/MEK2 act as MAPKKs, and ERK1/ERK2 act as MAPKs. In a few cell types, in response to some specific stimuli, Mos and Tpl2 act as MAPKKKs [31]. The substrates of the ERK1/ERK2 module are located in various cell organelles, including the cytoplasm (such as death-associated protein kinase (DAPK), tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (TSC2), RSK, and MNK) and the nucleus (such as NF-AT, Elk-1, myocyte enhancer factor 2 (MEF2), c-Fos, c-Myc, and STAT3). Also, some of the substrates of the ERK1/ERK2 module are associated with membranes (such as CD120a, Syk, and calnexin) and the cytoskeleton (such as neurofilaments and paxillin) [36]. The ERK/MAPK signaling pathway is the principal signaling network for controlling cell division, growth, development, and death [37–39]. Over the past few decades, our understanding of the physiological and pathological roles of MAPK/ERK signaling has undergone substantial improvement. Consequently, the RAS/RAF/MAPK pathway has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for various disorders, particularly tumors harboring mutations in the KRAS and BRAF genes. To date, multiple RAS/RAF/MAPK inhibitors have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA); they are primarily used for the treatment of melanoma, colorectal cancer, and non-small cell lung cancer that carry specific mutations (Fig. 3).

FDA-approved drugs for targeting the RAS/RAF/MAPK signaling pathway for cancer therapy [40, 41], and the mutation frequency of involved genes in RAS/RAF/MAPK across some gastrointestinal cancers [42–44]. NSCLC: non-small cell lung cancer; CRC: colorectal cancer

The JNK (also known as SAPK) family members are JNK1-3, which contain a TPY motif at their activation site. Like ERKs, stress stimuli trigger phosphorylation of JNKs on tyrosine and threonine amino acids. Various extracellular stresses, such as ionizing radiation, UV irradiation, heat stress, and DNA damage, can activate the JNK/MAPK module [45]. Also, the JNK module can be initiated by inflammatory cytokines, GFs, and GPCR agonists. In the JNK/MAPK module, MAPKKKs include MEKK1/MEKK4, MLK3, ASK1, TAK1, and Tpl2. MKK4 and MKK7 act as MAPKKs in the JNK module [46, 47]. At the downstream of the MAPK/JNK module, JNKs positively regulate the expression of c-Jun [48]. Although c-Jun is the primary substrate for JNK, other transcription factors such as p53, ATF-2, NF-ATc1, Elk-1, HSF-1, STAT3, c-Myc, and JunB are phosphorylated by JNKs [35, 49]. Ultimately, the activation of MAPK/JNK is implicated in various biological functions, including apoptosis, inflammation, cytokine production, and metabolism [50–52].

The kinases of the p38 family have a Thr-Glycine-Tyr (TGY) motif at their activation segment, and p38α/β/γ/δ are the prominent members of this family [53]. While p38α and p38β are expressed in most of the tissues, p38γ and p38δ isoforms are limited to a few tissues, and they are responsible for special functions. Like the JNK/MAPK pathway, external stresses and inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1 and TNF-α, activate the p38/MAPK module by triggering the phosphorylation of p38 on tyrosine and threonine amino acids. In p38/MAPK module, the MAPKKKs are MLK2/MLK3, MEKKs, ASKs, TAK1, and TAO1/TAO2. MKK3 and MKK6 act as MAPKKs [54]. Unlike MKK6, which phosphorylates all isoforms of p38, the function of MKK3 is limited to p38α, p38δ, and p38γ isoforms [53]. Activation of p38 isoforms results in phosphorylation of various substrates in the cytosol (cPLA2, MNK1/2, MK2/3, HuR, Bax, and Tau) and the nucleus (ATF1/2/6, MEF2, Elk-1, GADD153, Ets1, p53, and MSK1/2). The p38/MAPK pathway regulates crucial biological processes, including cell cycle, apoptosis, cell differentiation, and inflammation [53, 55].

Dysregulation of the MAPK pathway in gastric cancer

Dysregulation of the MAPK pathways is a key characteristic of various cancer types, including GC. This is associated with disease onset, development, invasion, metastasis, and response to treatment. In GC, the MAPK pathways can be dysregulated by trans-regulating elements that are indirectly associated with the pathway or as a result of genetic modifications/mutations in the MAPK pathway components [56].

The ERK/MAPK pathway plays an important role in regulating cell motility in both GC and normal epithelia [56]. Alterations in the RTK-RAS signaling pathway and BRAF mutations have been reported in about 37% and 11% of GC cases, respectively [57, 58]. In GC, the MAPK/ERK pathway can influence cell migration and invasiveness by regulating cell adhesion, EGFR-mediated focal adhesion disassembly, and the activity of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [25, 59]. Elevated p-ERK1/2 is considered an unfavorable prognostic factor in GC patients [60]. It has been revealed that in patients with scirrhous GC, hypoxia-induced angiopoietin-like-4 (ANGPTL4) induces anoikis resistance and peritoneal dissemination by activating FAK/Src/PI3K-Akt/ERK signaling [61].

Overexpression of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) is associated with the proliferation and invasion of GC cells. Research has demonstrated that the dysregulation of the p38 pathway in GC leads to epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Activation of the EGFR/Ras/MAPK signaling pathway by ROS results in the NF-κB-mediated induction of COX-2 expression, which facilitates cancer cell proliferation and cell death resistance [56]. Research findings have underscored the significant role of the p38-MAPK pathway in enhancing multidrug resistance in human GC cells [62]. While several studies indicate that p38 plays a role as an oncogenic factor, numerous studies support the function of p38-MAPK as a tumor suppressor. Research suggests that p38 plays a crucial role in suppressing tumor growth. It accomplishes this by activating p53 to induce apoptosis and by counteracting the ERK and JNK signaling pathways to induce cell cycle arrest in tumor cells [63–65].

The role of JNK across various cancer types is controversial. It has been shown that in the case of skin cancer, JNK functions as a tumor suppressor; however, in the cases of breast, ovarian, and prostate cancers, it promotes tumor growth [66–69]. The role of JNK in GC is also not yet well understood. Numerous studies support the crucial role of JNK activation in both the onset and progression of GC [70, 71]. In a study by Shibata et al., it was found that mice lacking JNK1 exhibited a significant decrease in N-methyl-N-nitrosourea-induced GC compared to wild-type mice [72]. On the contrary, numerous studies have demonstrated that JNK activation leads to a decrease in GC cell survival and proliferation while enhancing their apoptosis [73–78].

Non-coding RNAs

In humans, approximately 80% of the genome is capable of transcription activity. Nonetheless, only 2% of the human genome encodes proteins. A large segment of the human transcriptome comprises the non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), which are functional molecules but do not typically encode functional peptides [79]. In recent years, several characteristics of these molecules, including their origin, biogenesis mechanism, structure, and function, have been uncovered, and their crucial role in the biological process has been acknowledged. Based on their roles, ncRNA can be classified into two distinct groups: housekeeping ncRNAs, which are consistently expressed by all cells and are vital for mRNA translation, and regulatory ncRNAs, which control gene expression levels at various stages, encompassing epigenetic, transcriptional, and post-transcriptional levels [80]. Based on their length, ncRNAs can be subclassified into small ncRNAs (sncRNAs) with a length of less than 200 nucleotides and long ncRNAs (lncRNAs), which are longer than 200 nucleotides [81]. Each regulatory sncRNA and lncRNA comprises several subtypes with different structures and mechanisms of function.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are the most well-known members of the sncRNA family. MicroRNAs are a group of conserved sncRNAs with less than 25 nucleotides, which are expressed in all eukaryotic cells. In 1993, Lee et al. identified the first miRNA, named lin-4, in a nematode. It was found that the lin-4 gene mediates the development of this nematode by suppressing the LIN-14. The transcribed RNA from lin-4 did not code for any protein. Later studies showed that the lin-4 RNA is matured in multiple steps, and the mature lin-4 miRNA decreases the level of LIN-14 by attaching to the 3′ UTR of its mRNA [82, 83]. The discovery of the magic molecules, miRNAs, revolutionized the field of molecular biology. In particular, many studies have been conducted to discover miRNA biomarkers for cancer prognosis, diagnosis, and therapeutic targets. According to the latest release of miRBase (https://www.mirbase.org) [84], the number of mature miRNA sequences in humans has reached 2,654. Currently, identifying new potential miRNAs as biomarkers for cancer is an exciting research topic in biology [85].

miRNAs are located in different regions of the genome. MiRNAs can be found between two genes (intergenic) or within a sequence of genes (intragenic). Approximately 50% of miRNAs are intergenic, and their expression is independent of a host gene because they possess their own promoter. Other miRNAs with intragenic position are co-transcribed with their host gene. The synthesis of miRNAs occurs by two different pathways: canonical and non-canonical [86, 87].

MiRNAs are mainly processed by canonical pathways. The canonical path begins with the transcription of pri-mRNA, followed by processing to pre-miRNA within the nucleus. The processing is done by a microprocessor complex that Drosha and DGCR8 constitute. DGCR8 recognizes pri-miRNA, and Drosha cleaves the pri-miRNA’s duplex [88–90]. The delivery of pri-miRNA into the cytosol is done through an exportin 5/RanGTP complex. In the cytosol, Dicer, an endonuclease, eliminates the terminal loop of pre-miRNA and converts it into a mature miRNA duplex. Both mature ss-miRNAs link to the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). The RISC complex consists of proteins, including the argonaute (AGO) family of proteins (AGO1-4) and TRBP. RISC complex mediates AGO2-dependent slicing or translational inhibition of target mRNAs [91–93].

Some protein components of the non-canonical miRNA synthesis pathway are similar to those of the canonical pathway. However, they differ in some other proteins. Generally, the non-canonical pathways of miRNA biogenesis are categorized into Drosha/DGCR8-independent and Dicer-independent. Mitrons are transcribed from the introns of the mRNAs during splicing, and shRNAs are examples of Drosha/DGCR8-independent and Dicer-independent processed miRNAs, respectively [94–96]. AGO2 completes the maturation of these pre-miRNAs in the cytoplasm [96].

The seed region at the 5’ end of mature miRNAs recognizes the specific mRNA targets by binding to their complementary sequence. The complementarity level of miRNA-mRNA is a crucial determinant for the employed mechanism of RNA interference. When the complementarity level of miRNA-mRNA is partial, the miRNA suppresses the expression of mRNA by reducing its stability. Additionally, partial complementarity can result in translational inhibition of the target mRNA. On the other hand, sufficient complementarity between the seed region and the target mRNA induces AGO2 endonuclease activity, which cleaves the target mRNA [97]. However, there are instances in which near-perfect complementarity results in translational suppression instead of cleavage [98]. Also, evidence has been revealed that miRNA binding can result in faster deadenylation of mRNA, which reduces its stability and accelerates its degradation [99]. It has also been observed that under specific conditions, miRNAs can stimulate gene expression levels [100].

miRNAs regulate a variety of biological processes, and it has been shown that they are fundamentally dysregulated during tumorigenesis. Various mechanisms, including chromosomal abnormalities, epigenetic changes, uncontrolled transcription, and defects in miRNA production pathways, may underlie the abnormal expression of miRNAs in human tumors [101]. Cancer cells acquire specific capabilities during development, including sustaining proliferative signaling, resisting cell death, deregulating cellular energetics, inducing angiogenesis, enabling replicative immortality, evading growth suppressors, activating metastasis, and promoting invasion [102]. Given the role of MAPK signaling in controlling these processes and the dysregulation of miRNAs in cancers, it is supposed that miRNAs can control these processes by regulating MAPK signaling during the development of GC. Depending on their target genes and whether they induce or inhibit the MAPK pathway, they can act as an oncogene or a tumor suppressor.

lncRNAs are large members of the ncRNA category, which consists of circRNAs, lincRNAs, NATs, and eRNAs. Although some circRNAs have been found with a length of less than 200 nucleotides, they are historically classified as lncRNAs. circRNAs are covalently closed RNAs without a 5′ cap and a 3′ polyadenylated tail produced by back splicing, a non-canonical splicing mechanism. These molecules play a regulatory role in gene expression levels, cell proliferation, and programmed cell death, as well as being involved in miRNA sponging, increasing the stability of other RNAs, and regulating the localization of RNA-binding proteins (RBP) [103–105]. The tissue- and pathologic condition-specific expression pattern of circRNAs makes them favorable biomarkers [103, 104]. Long intergenic ncRNAs (lincRNAs) are another subtype of lncRNAs, exhibiting specific expression patterns in relation to cell types, tissues, stages of human development, and pathological disorders [106]. They are called intergenic because they originate from non-coding regions of the genome that do not overlap with coding segments [107]. Although the biological significance of most human lincRNAs remains elusive, it has been revealed that they are involved in transcription regulation and chromatin remodeling. They can also serve as decoys to suppress the function of miRNAs, mRNAs, and proteins, or as a scaffold for multi-protein complexes [106]. NATs are generated by the transcription of the opposite DNA strand and can interfere with the corresponding sense transcripts, inhibiting their translation or function. Although some NATs are shorter than 200 nucleotides, they are generally classified as lncRNAs. NATs can interact with sense transcripts from the same or different genomic loci, referred to as “cis” and “trans” activity. eRNAs are the last members of lncRNAs with a length of 50–2000 nucleotides, transcribed from active enhancers. Although previous studies considered eRNAs as transcriptional noise, increasing evidence indicates they are essential players in transcriptional regulation [108].

NcRNAs and their regulatory roles in gastric cancer

Emerging evidence has uncovered the critical role of ncRNAs in the onset, progression, and response to treatment of GC. Both oncogenic and tumor-suppressor ncRNAs can be dysregulated in GC and change the behavior of malignant cells by regulating the expression level of oncogenes (encompassing EZH2, EGFR, STRA6, FSCN1, TRIM24, PDK4, LYN, Bcl-2, VEGF, VE-cadherin and PPP2R2A) and tumor suppressor genes (encompass PTEN, RUNX1, PDCD4, TRAIL and YPEL1). Through controlling the expression level of these target genes, ncRNAs govern several cancer-related processes, including proliferation, invasion, metastasis, angiogenesis, and apoptosis [109].

The expression pattern of ncRNAs in GC cells determines the sensitivity or resistance to drugs and radiation therapy. The dysregulation of ncRNAs has been demonstrated to induce resistance to anticancer agents, including Adriamycin, platinum-containing antineoplastic agents (including cisplatin, oxaliplatin, and carboplatin), 5-fluorouracil, Vincristine, Paclitaxel, Etoposide, Mitomycin, and Capecitabine. miRNA expression patterns in GC cells have also been shown to affect the response of targeted therapy agents, including anti-EGFR, anti-HER2, anti-VEGF-A, and anti-VEGFR-2 monoclonal antibodies, as well as tyrosine kinase inhibitors [110]. It has been revealed that ncRNAs govern therapeutic resistance in GC in different ways. They have the ability to affect programmed cell death, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, drug efflux, and stemness of GC cells by regulating the expression level of key genes of these processes [16]. NcRNAs can also induce multidrug resistance (MDR) in GC cells by affecting MDR-associated signaling pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K/PTEN/Akt/mTOR, TGF-β, and MAPK, as well as by increasing exosome secretion activity of GC cells [109].

Despite the recent progress, GC still presents a significant risk of mortality, mainly due to the absence of a reliable early diagnosis approach and prognostic factors. Currently used biomarkers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), and cancer antigen 72-4 (CA72-4), have low accuracy in GC diagnosis. Involvement in various stages of GC and their differential expression make ncRNAs potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers in GC. The presence of ncRNAs in circulating extracellular vesicles and body fluids suggests that they could serve as non-invasive diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. Considering the crucial impact of ncRNA dysregulation in the occurrence and progression of GC, they can also be used as therapeutic targets, specifically in patients resistant to conventional anti-cancer drugs [16, 111]. In the subsequent sections, we will explore the regulatory mechanisms of MAPK pathways by miRNAs and lncRNAs in GC.

NcRNA-mediated regulation of MAPK pathways in GC

Recent studies highlight the involvement of MAPK-regulating ncRNAs in GC development and progression. Downregulation of tumor suppressor ncRNAs and upregulation of oncogenic ncRNAs causes dysregulation of the MAPK pathway, resulting in tumor initiation, uncontrolled proliferation, and progression of malignant cells, as well as angiogenesis, metastasis, and drug resistance (Fig. 4).

MAPK-regulating LncRNAs

Recent evidence strongly supports the significant influence of lncRNAs on GC through their impact on the MAPK pathways. LncRNAs can regulate the expression levels of MAPK pathway-related genes through various mechanisms, including mRNA decay and miRNA sponging. MAPK-regulating lncRNAs can function as either tumor suppressors or oncogenes in GC. MAPK-regulating LncRNAs and their role in gastric cancer

It has been shown that the dysregulation of various lncRNAs is involved in carcinogenesis and tumor initiation via deregulation of MAPK pathways. TINCR is an lncRNA with various genomic variants and tissue-dependent function. While its expression is reduced in glioma, retinoblastoma, and prostate cancer, it is upregulated in gastric, esophageal, bladder, and breast cancer. This lncRNA is the master regulator of various pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin, ERK1/2-SP3, and MAPK pathways, positioning it as a possible contributor to early carcinogenesis. Although the essential role of TINCR in GC makes it a potential therapeutic target, its opposite role in different tissues suggests that targeting TINCR should be performed in a tissue-specific or even personalized approach [112]. Likewise, Hsa-circ-0052001 is overexpressed in GC and contributes to carcinogenesis. While the detailed mechanism by which Hsa‐circ‐0052001 activates the MAPK pathway is not clear, it has been shown that its cellular level correlates with tumor size, TNM stage, and metastasis [113]. TM4SF1-AS1 is another lncRNA that is involved in GC initiation. Kitajima et al. found that at the initial stages of GC, the cellular level of TM4SF1-AS1 becomes upregulated, leading to the elevation of stress granule formation and sequestration of RACK1 within them. Under stress conditions, RACK1 activates the p38 and JNK MAPK (SAPK) pathway and induces apoptosis. Thus, TM4SF1-AS1 inhibits the programmed cell death of GC cells by sequestering RACK1 within stress granules [114]. H19 is the first lncRNA reported to have a regulatory role in GC. It has been shown that upregulation of H19 and its mature product, miR-675, is involved in the initiation and progression of multiple types of cancer, including melanoma [56, 115–117]. Although it has been shown to exert oncogenic effects in GC via the ADD/Caspase 8/Caspase 3 apoptotic pathway [116, 118], its direct involvement in MAPK signaling within GC remains unconfirmed. Nonetheless, miRNA-675-mediated activation of the RAS-MAPK pathway has been reported in colon cancer [119], suggesting a potential similar role in GC that warrants further investigation for therapeutic development. These data highlight the involvement of lncRNAs in the early stages of gastric tumorigenesis via the modulation of MAPK pathway components. By providing a deeper understanding of these early acting lncRNAs, novel strategies can be developed for diagnosing and preventing GC.

It has also been shown that dysregulation of several MAPK-regulating lncRNAs can affect cell proliferation and survival by modulating apoptosis and cell cycle checkpoints. Cancer susceptibility candidate 2 (CASC2), P53-inducible cancer-associated RNA transcript 1 (PICART1), GAS5, circMAPK1 (hsa_circ_0004872), and KCNKI5-AS1 are five tumor suppressor MAPK-regulating lncRNAs consistently downregulated in GC. CASC2 in GC cells can inhibit disease progression by decreasing the cellular level of p-ERK1/2 and p-JNK. The overexpression of CASC2 with RK1/2 or JNK suppressor can potentially be investigated as a novel therapeutic approach in GC [120]. It has also been shown that the transcription factor E2F6 is upregulated in GC and promotes tumor progression by inhibiting the expression of lncRNA CASC2, which correlates with an unfavorable prognosis in GC patients [121]. PICART1 can inhibit tumor proliferation and induce apoptosis in GC cells by preventing the activation of PI3K/AKT and ERK/MAPK pathways. Downregulation of PICART1 in GC is associated with an unfavorable prognosis. Thus, the overexpression of PICART1 offers a promising therapeutic strategy in GC [122]. GAS5 plays a protective role against GC, which is mediated by preventing proliferation and promoting cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in both p53-dependent and p53-independent manners. GAS5 can increase the cellular level of tumor suppressor miR-34c by downregulating E2F1 (the suppressor of miR-34c) and/or ATM/p38 MAPK signaling [123]. The GAS5/miR-34c axis could serve as a promising novel therapeutic target in future research [124]. KCNKI5-AS1 is another notable tumor suppressor that impedes proliferation by sponging miR-21. It effectively inhibits cell proliferation and encourages apoptosis by decreasing the levels of BCL-2, matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-2, and − 9 while simultaneously boosting the level of Bax [125]. Interestingly, its downregulation in GC results from epigenetic silencing driven by the MAPK and AKT pathways, forming a feedback loop that further enhances cell growth. Zhang et al. have revealed that the level of DNA methylation and histone deacetylation is increased in GC, which accounts for the downregulation of KCNKI5-ASI. They revealed that MAPK signaling and AKT signaling lead to the increase of DNA methyltransferase (DNMT1) and Histone Deacetylase 1 (HDAC1), respectively, which both are involved in the downregulation of KCNKI5-ASI. The authors stated that DNMT1 inhibitors (such as 5-azacytidine) and HDAC1 inhibitors (such as Chidamide) could be potential treatment strategies in GC. Moreover, considering that the expression level of KCNKI5-ASI is reduced in advanced stages of GC, this lncRNA could be a reliable prognostic factor in individuals with advanced GC [125].

On the other hand, upregulation of various oncogenic lncRNAs enhances GC cell proliferation through activation of MAPK pathways. For example, CARLo-5 is upregulated in GC and promotes GC cell proliferation and invasion by increasing the phosphorylation of ERK and p38 [126]. The high cellular level of CARLo-5 is associated with the advanced TNM stage of GC and can be an independent predictor of overall and relapse-free survival [127]. The cell cycle-regulating role of CARLo-5 has also been reported in other malignancies, such as colon cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma [128, 129]. It has been reported that overexpression of CARLo-5 in hepatocellular carcinoma promotes tumor growth by reducing the miR-200b expression [129]. Downregulation of miR-200b in GC and its correlation with unfavorable prognosis has also been reported [130, 131]. Nonetheless, the effect of CARLo-5 on miR-200b in GC has yet to be validated. MAP3K1-2 is another lncRNA whose upregulation is linked to the unfavorable prognosis in GC. This molecule has been shown to enhance the phosphorylation of p38, ERK1/2, and JNK, preventing programmed cell death and promoting cell proliferation and survival [132]. BCAR4, LINC00152, ERICH3-AS1, and Linc00483 similarly activate ERK or other MAPK branches to drive cell growth. BCAR4 boosts cell proliferation while also suppressing apoptosis. In xenograft models, it has been revealed that the overexpression of BCAR4 can promote in vivo tumor formation by activating the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway [133]. LINC00152 is an 828 bp lncRNA that is differentially hypomethylated and overexpressed in various cancers [134–136]. LINC00152 silencing has been shown to result in significant reductions in p-ERK1/2, p-MEK1/2, and c-fos levels, leading to the inhibition of GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [137]. Elevated expression of ERICH3-AS1 in GC is a poor prognostic factor and negatively correlates with patients’ overall survival and DFS. It has been shown that the increased expression of ERICH3-AS1 in GC results in the enrichment of MAPK, Wnt, p53, mTOR, and JAK/STAT pathways, promoting cell proliferation and tumor growth [138]. Linc00483 mediates its oncogenic effect by sponging the tumor suppressor miR-30a‐3p, leading to an elevation in the expression level of SPAG9 (the target of miR‐30a‐3p). SPAG9 serves as a scaffold for MAPK pathway components, facilitating their activation [139]. Collectively, these findings underscore the dual roles of MAPK-regulating lncRNAs in GC cell proliferation and survival. While the expression level of these lncRNAs has prognostic value, targeting them can be investigated as a potential therapeutic intervention.

Downregulation or upregulation of various MAPK-regulating lncRNAs is also linked to GC cell invasion and metastasis. For example, upregulation of DUSP5P1 aligns with poor prognosis (particularly in early-stage GC) and increased risk of GC metastasis [140]. DUSP1 binds to the promoter region of the ARHGAP5 gene, inducing its expression. ARHGAP5 enhances the invasion and metastasis of GC by activating the MAPK pathways and focal adhesion [141].

LINC01138 is another oncogenic lncRNA that is upregulated in various malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma and prostate cancer [142, 143]. In GC, the upregulation of LINC01138 increases the expression levels of GFR, FGF1, FGF23, MAP2K6, and MAP3K7 by sponging miR-1273e, ultimately leading to the hyperactivity of the MAPK pathways, cell invasion, and metastasis [144]. AOC4P is an lncRNA first identified as a tumor suppressor element in hepatocellular carcinoma, preventing disease invasion and metastasis [145]. Conversely, Qu et al. revealed that compared with normal tissues, the expression of AOC4P is elevated in GC and is linked to shortened overall survival and disease-free survival. They showed that AOCP4 enhances GC cell invasion, migration, and metastasis by increasing the expression levels of ERK1, JNK, and p38 [146]. Recently, Gao et al. described a novel oncogenic lncRNA called LINC02253. They revealed that the expression level of LINC02253 is significantly upregulated in GC and closely correlates with advanced TNM stage, tumor size, and metastasis. They showed that LINC02253 mediates its oncogenic role by increasing the stability of Keratin 18 (KRT18) mRNA through its N6-Methyladenosine (m6A) modification, and indirectly increases ERK/MAPK pathway activity and promotes tumor progression and metastasis [147]. KRT18 is one of the members of the intermediate filament family of cytoskeletal proteins and is aberrantly upregulated in various cancer types [148]. Upregulation of KRT18 is linked with a poor prognosis. KRT18 increases cancer cell proliferation and metastasis by activating the ERK/MAPK signaling pathway [149]. FAM87A is another oncogenic lncRNA that becomes upregulated in GC, modulating MAPK and transforming growth factor (TGF) signaling cascades. In vitro analysis has shown that knockdown of this molecule significantly inhibits GC cells’ migration and invasion ability. On the other hand, analysis of clinical data indicates that FAM87A can serve as an independent factor for predicting the overall survival of patients with GC [150]. HOXA10-AS is also upregulated in GC and enhances the invasion and migration of GC cells by activating the p38 MAPK/STAT3. HOXA10-AS interacts with miR-29a/b/c-3p, leading to the upregulation of HOXA10, an activator of p38 MAPK/STAT3 signaling pathway [151].

SNHG12 and LINC00924 are two oncogenic lncRNAs that facilitate peritoneal metastasis by modulating MAPK pathways. SNHG12 is associated with poor prognosis, increased tumor invasion, and metastasis across a range of cancer types [152, 153]. In GC, the upregulation of SNHG12, which acts as a sponge for miR-129-5p, leads to the elevated expression of its target, E2F. High expression of SNHG12 in GC is accompanied by activation of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, mesothelial-mesenchymal transition, and apoptosis of human peritoneal mesothelial cells (HPMCs). SNHG12 is deployed in GC cell-derived extracellular vesicles and facilitates peritoneal metastasis [154]. LINC00924 supports peritoneal metastasis by reprogramming lipid metabolism. This molecule promotes matrix-detached GC cell survival and spheroid formation through increasing fatty acid uptake and oxidation. LINC00924 regulates alternative splicing of Mnk2 pre-mRNA to downregulate the formation of Mnk2a (the isoform with a MAPK-binding site). Reduced formation of Mnk2a isoform minimizes the level of p-p38, which ultimately results in the activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors α (PPARα), which accounts for the alteration of fatty acid metabolism [155]. AK025387, another metastatic biomarker, is correlated with lymph node metastasis. AK025387 mediates its oncogenic effect by increasing the expression level of Raf-1, MEK2, and ERK genes [156].

It has been shown that circRNAs also contribute to GC cell invasion and metastasis via modulating MAPK pathway components. For instance, circRNA-0044301 sponges miR-188-5p, leading to the elevated expression of its target gene, DAXX. DAXX enhances cell proliferation, invasion, and migration by inducing the expression of ERK1/2 [157]. CircPDIA4 facilitates GC metastasis by binding to ERK1/2 and preventing their dephosphorylation, leading to the hyperactivation of the MAPK pathway [158].

Various studies have also revealed that MAPK-associated lncRNAs are involved in modulating therapeutic response and immune evasion of GC. Understanding these regulatory pathways can help to design novel combinational therapeutic strategies to enhance the efficacy of currently used therapies. For example, it has been shown that DUSP5P1 not only promotes metastasis but also induces resistance to chemotherapy with platinum-based drugs. Combining platinum-based chemotherapy with the downregulation of DUSP5P1 has a synergic effect in GC treatment [141]. CircPDIA4 sustains ERK1/2 phosphorylation, contributing to resistance against ERK inhibitors. It has been shown that depletion of circPDIA4 increases GC cell response to ERK inhibitors [158]. It has been revealed that circRNA-0044301 could decrease the therapeutic effects of GDC-0994 (ERK1/2 inhibitor) and 5-FU, and the combination of the circRNA-0044301 silencing with GDC-0994 and 5-FU promotes response to treatment [157]Subsequent studies should focus on validating the role of the above-mentioned lncRNAs in GC therapeutic resistance and identifying other responses to treatment-associated lncRNAs to design novel combinational treatments with higher efficacy.

MAPK-regulating MiRNAs

The unrestrained growth and division of cells are a fundamental attribute of tumors. The MAPK/ERK signaling pathway plays a crucial role in regulating cell growth and division. Consequently, dysregulation of this pathway can lead to uncontrolled cell division and inhibit apoptosis, resulting in tumor growth and progression. Mounting evidence indicates that miRNAs closely interact with the MAPK/ERK pathway, thereby triggering or inhibiting cell proliferation. Thus, aberrant expression of these miRNAs can lead to continuous proliferative signaling from the MAPK/ERK pathway, resulting in increased proliferation in tumor cells [159]. In recent decades, considerable research efforts have been devoted to investigating the cross-talk between miRNAs and the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, as well as how this interaction regulates the proliferation of malignant cells. Li et al. indicated that suppression of miR-130b inhibits proliferation and induces cell cycle arrest in glioma cells in vivo. Additionally, miR-130b knockdown caused inactivation of MAPK/ERK in glioma cells, suggesting a role for miR-130b in the progression of malignant cells by controlling MAPK/ERK [160]. The interaction between miR-21 and the MAPK/ERK can be considered a clear example of the reciprocal relationship between miRNAs and the MAPK/ERK signaling cascade. Blocking MAPK/ERK1/2 by either shRNA-ERK1/2 or U0126 (a MEK inhibitor) lowers miR-21 in the human esophagus carcinoma cell line. In parallel, suppressing miR-21 reduced the proliferation and migration of malignant cells, and increased their apoptosis by inhibiting the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway [161]. Ras GTPase superfamily member RERG reduces tumor cell proliferation by regulating the Ras/ERK signaling cascade. Ho and colleagues indicated that miR-382-5p targets RERG, reduces its inhibitory effect on Ras/ERK signaling, and triggers proliferation, survival, migration, and invasion of malignant cells [162]. The up-regulation of miR-335 in colorectal carcinoma cells is associated with increased cell proliferation in vitro and higher tumor growth in vivo. miR-335 increases malignant cell proliferation by targeting RASA, a negative regulator of the MAPK pathway, and thereby promotes the Ras/ERK signaling cascade [163]. Similarly, miR-31 can trigger the proliferation of colorectal cancer cells by targeting RASA1 and activating MAPK signaling [164]. Wang et al. reported the downregulation of miR-378-5p in colorectal cancer tissues and cell lines. The overexpression of miR-378-5p reduces proliferation and increases apoptosis in malignant cells by targeting the BRAF gene. Therefore, miR-378-5p can serve as a tumor suppressor, and its downregulation causes tumor growth and progression [165]. This evidence suggests that the dysregulated interaction between miRNAs and the MAPK/ERK pathway has a critical influence on tumor cell proliferation.

Mirroring the findings in other malignancies, studies focusing specifically on gastric cancer have identified several key miRNAs that regulate cell proliferation by directly or indirectly targeting components of the MAPK/ERK signaling cascade. It is evident that miR-129 regulates related genes to tumor development, tumor progression, cell division, metastasis, and chemoresistance in GC [166]. The expression of MiR-129-5p is diminished during GC, and its reduction is associated with lower overall survival in GC patients [167]. Chen and colleagues reported that the SLC2A3 gene is one of the targets of miR-129-5p, and the miR-129-5p/SLC2A3 axis regulates the glucose metabolism of GC cells [168]. SLC2A3 (GLUT3) is a high-affinity glucose transporter; various tumors, including GC, express this transporter [169, 170]. Compared to normal cells, tumor cells need more energy due to their higher rate of proliferation and metabolism [171]. Thus, tumor cells increase glucose uptake by up-regulating glucose transporter (GLUT) proteins [172]. Overall, miR-129-5p/SLC2A3 lowers the energy production and proliferation of GC cells by reducing glucose absorption, glucose utilization, and lactate synthesis. Additionally, evidence suggests that the miR-129-5p/SLC2A3 axis influences the activation of the PI3K-Akt and ERK1/2 pathways. Therefore, miR-129-5p reduces energy production and proliferation of GC cells by targeting SLC2A3 and influencing PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling [168]. Similarly, the tumor-suppressive role of miR-129-5p in controlling key MAPK components has been investigated in other tumors. Overexpression of miR-129-5p reduces proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis in glioma by targeting Wnt5a and blocking PCK/ERK/NF-κB and JNK pathways [173]. In liver cancer, miR-129-5p can inhibit proliferation and trigger apoptosis via targeting CAMK4 and blocking the activation of MAPK signaling [174]. Also, miR-129-5p can target the 3′ UTR of PAX6, inhibiting the proliferation, migration, and invasion of retinoblastoma cells by affecting the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [175]. Overall, miR-129-5p acts as a crucial tumor suppressor by controlling multiple oncogenes and key signaling pathways, making its restoration a promising therapeutic strategy for GC treatment.

The down-regulation of miR-27b has been reported in GC patients and GC cell lines, including AGS, MKN-45, NCI-N87, and SNU-1, compared with normal controls. In silico analysis confirmed that multiple key genes involved in MAPK/ERK signaling, including GRB1, SOS1, KRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1, and MAPK1, are target genes of miR-27b [176]. Zhang et al. reported the down-regulation of miR-27b in lymphoma cells compared with normal B cells; this down-regulation was associated with increased proliferation, invasiveness, and decreased apoptosis in malignant cells. Restoring the expression of miR-27b leads to the inhibition of proliferation and invasiveness, and triggers apoptosis in lymphoma cells by targeting the MET/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [177]. Moreover, miR-27-3p can inhibit the proliferation and reverse the resistance of breast cancer cells to chemotherapy by targeting CBLB and GRB2, and inhibiting the PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways [178]. The consistent downregulation of miR-27b across diverse cancers, which typically inhibits key oncogenic pathways such as MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT, strongly underscores its crucial tumor-suppressive role in controlling cell proliferation, invasion, and survival.

Contamination with Helicobacter pylori is a fundamental risk factor for the development of GC, creating a specific inflammatory microenvironment that can alter gene expression. H. pylori triggers gastric epithelial cells to express Rev-Erbα, subsequently activating ERK signaling pathways and causing tumorigenic proliferation within these host cells [179]. This infectious context directly influences ncRNA regulation, as evidenced by the decreased expression of miR-1298-5p in human and mouse GC cells following H. pylori infection. In this specific context, the reduced levels of miR-1298-5p alleviate the inhibition of its direct target, MAP2K6, thereby activating the downstream p38 MAPK pathway. Consequently, in the presence of H. pylori, the downregulation of miR-1298-5p leads to increased cancer cell division, metastasis, and aggressiveness. This serves as a clear example of how an external factor can dictate the function and pathological significance of an ncRNA in gastric carcinogenesis. Also, the tumor-suppressor activity of miR-1298-5p is observed in other tumors like breast cancer [180], cervical cancer, non-small-cell lung carcinoma [181], and glioma [182].

The role of miR-183-5p in triggering apoptosis and regulating autophagy has been demonstrated in GC [183]. Wang et al. investigated the targets of miR-183-5p and their association with the survival of GC patients using a bioinformatics approach. Their results showed that miR-183-5p can regulate MAPK, mTOR, Wnt, and PI3K-Akt pathways in GC through WNT2B, NGFR, and NTRK2 genes [184]. It is important to note that these targets were identified through bioinformatics analysis, and experimental validation of these specific interactions in GC is still required to confirm their biological relevance. Some other studies have explored the mechanisms by which miR-183-5p regulates tumor development. It has been demonstrated that miR-183-5p targets Erbin expression, thereby enhancing the proliferation of acute myeloid leukemia cells through the activation of the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK and PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a pathways [185]. Feng et al. reported that miR-183-5p, an upregulated miRNA in prostate cancer tissues, triggers the invasion and migration of prostate cancer cells by targeting TET1 [186]. Conversely, Kudo et al. demonstrated that miR-183-5p exerts a tumor-suppressive role by targeting PLK1 (a proto-oncogene) and increasing apoptosis in breast cancer cells [187]. These conflicting oncogenic and tumor-suppressive roles highlight a crucial concept in ncRNA biology: their function is highly context-dependent. The double-edged sword activity of miR-183-5p likely stems from its ability to target different genes in different cellular environments. For instance, its oncogenic role in prostate cancer is mediated by targeting tumor suppressor TET1, while its tumor-suppressive function in breast cancer is achieved by targeting the proto-oncogene PLK1. Therefore, the effect of miR-183-5p on tumor progression is dictated by the specific cancer type and the relative expression of its various downstream targets. This underscores the necessity for further studies to elucidate the precise context-dependent mechanisms governing miR-183-5p’s function, particularly in GC, before it can be considered a therapeutic target. The interaction between miRNAs and signaling pathways, such as the MAPK pathway, can control tumor metastasis and invasion. Multiple miRNAs are investigated for their role in regulating malignant cell metastasis and invasion. For instance, miR-592 triggers epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), metastasis, and invasion of gastric cancer cells by affecting E-cadherin, Vimentin, and N-cadherin [188]. miR-181a has an anti-metastatic effect on adenoid cystic carcinoma by targeting MAP2K1, MAPK1, and Snai2 [189]. miR-302a is another negative regulator of cell invasion, as it reduces Erk1/2 and Akt phosphorylation, thereby suppressing the MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling cascades. The suppression of MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling can reduce the expression of MMP-2, MMP-9, β-catenin, fibronectin, and vimentin, and decrease malignant cell invasion [190]. Besides, miR-455 and miR-622 have been demonstrated to inhibit the invasion of colorectal cancer cells by targeting Raf-1 [191]. The downregulation of miR-633 has been proven in GC-related tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues. Also, abnormal expression of miR-633 has been observed in other cancers [192, 193]. The expression of miR-633 can reduce tumor metastasis, invasion, division, and cell cycle arrest, and increase cell death in GC. MiR-633 targets MAPK1 and MAPK13 molecules and reduces their expression [194]. In GC, it has been found that miR-633 regulates the expression of MAPK1. Unlike its anti-oncogene role in GC, in other cancers, such as melanoma, an oncogene role has been demonstrated for miR-633 [193].

It has been revealed that low RASSF6 levels are a marker of a poor prognosis in GC patients [195]. Also, RASSF6 is recognized as a tumor suppressor in other cancers, including leukemia [196] and retinoblastoma [197]. Mezzanotte and colleagues found that the expression of RASSF6 is suppressed in melanoma due to extraordinary methylation of its promoter. Also, it has been demonstrated that the loss of RASSF6 triggers the invasion and metastasis of melanoma cells, as RASSF6 suppresses the BRAF/MAPK pathway in melanoma. However, the mechanism by which RASSF6 suppresses BRAF/MAPK is still unclear [198]. Mi et al. discovered miR-181a-5p as a regulator of RASSF6, revealing that overexpression of miR-181a-5p triggers metastasis and cell division in GC. Moreover, they demonstrated that miR-181a-5p induces tumorigenesis by inhibiting RASSF6 and consequently activating MAPK [199]. Ouyang et al. demonstrated that miR-181a-5p inhibits migration, invasion, and proliferation, while enhancing apoptosis in retinoblastoma cells by targeting NRAS [200]. Conversely, miR-181a-5p reduces the migration and proliferation of glioblastoma cells via downregulating MET, MAP2K1, MAPK1, MAPK3, and AKT3 [201]. Consequently, miR-181a-5p exhibits a context-dependent role in cancer migration and invasion, acting as both an inducer and a suppressor, depending on the specific cancer type and its molecular targets.

Abnormal Drosha expression has been seen in different tumors, and it plays a role as either a tumor suppressor or tumor inducer based on the cancer type. For example, Drosha expression is downregulated and is related to poor prognosis in gallbladder cancer patients [202]. Conversely, overexpression of Drosha in non-small-cell lung cancer is related to metastasis, pathological stage, and worse prognosis [203]. It has been found that aberrant expression of Drosha is related to tumor aggressiveness and differentiation [204]. As mentioned, Drosha is a crucial enzyme for the canonical biogenesis of miRNAs. Therefore, the altered expression of Drosha is followed by the dysregulation of different miRNAs [205]. Xu et al. revealed that a higher level of Drosha is related to higher metastasis and invasion in GC. Also, they showed that overexpression of Drosha increased miR-197 and decreased miR-622, consequently leading to the downregulation of CD82 and upregulation of LAMC2 in malignant GC cells. A temporary decrease in the expression of LAMC2 or miR-197 in the MGC-803 GC cell line led to lower tumor cell invasion. Therefore, Drosha can trigger the aggression of GC via the regulation of miR-197 and miR-622, and their corresponding targets, LAMC2 and CD82, resulting in the stimulation of the EGFR-ERK1/2-MMP7 signaling [206].

Drug resistance is a significant hurdle in the effective treatment of almost all cancers. miRNAs can induce multidrug resistance (MDR) by modulating signaling pathways involved in MDR, such as MAPK [207]. Increasing research has explored the roles of miRNA and MAPK signaling in the development of MDR. For example, Zhao et al. demonstrated that miR-302a/b/c/d cooperatively suppresses P-glycoprotein by targeting MEKK1, thereby sensitizing breast cancer cells to Adriamycin [208]. He et al. revealed the downregulation of miR-27a-5p and upregulation of its target, APEX1, in GC [209]. APEX1 is an endonuclease that plays a role in DNA repair through apurinic/apyrimidinic function [210]. The dysregulation of APEX1 is observed in different cancer types, and its upregulation is related to MDR in tumor cells [211, 212]. During GC development, APEX1 contributes to tumorigenesis and resistance to Doxorubicin through RAS/MEK/FOS and PTEN/AKT/SMAD2 pathways. Deletion of APEX1 can suppress the MAPK pathway and sensitize GC cells to chemotherapy. Therefore, modulation of the APEX1/miR-27a-5p may reduce the development of GC and increase its response to chemotherapy treatment [209]. In some cases, signaling molecules can modulate the miRNA expression and induce MDR in tumor cells. For instance, Sun et al. demonstrated that ERK activation can globally reduce miRNAs by phosphorylating Exportin-5 at T345/S416/S497. Among down-regulated miRNAs, reduced expression of miR-122 can enhance chemoresistance to Taxol in liver cancer cells by up-regulating Septin-9 [213]. Although the role of ncRNAs in the regulation of the MAPK pathway in gastric cancer is increasingly explored, a comprehensive understanding of how these molecules collectively orchestrate MAPK dysregulation in gastric cancer remains largely elusive. The majority of studies have focused on isolated ncRNA–target interactions, with limited evaluation of how multiple ncRNAs converge on shared signaling components, or how their regulatory effects might interact synergistically or antagonistically. Although Fig. 4; Tables 1 and 2 provide a categorized summary regarding the regulatory roles of ncRNAs in MAPK pathway activity, there remains a need for systems-level studies to unravel ncRNA cross-talk, feedback loops, and co-regulatory modules within the MAPK network. Future studies should employ integrative approaches, such as co-expression network analysis, multi-omics profiling, and CRISPR-based functional screens, to uncover the higher-order architecture of the ncRNA-MAPK axis in gastric cancer.

The paths and challenges of the clinical use of MAPK-regulating NcRNAs

Diagnostic/prognostic applications

The cancer stage-specific expression pattern of ncRNAs makes them attractive candidates for the development of predictive and diagnostic biomarkers. As discussed in the previous sections, several MAPK-related ncRNAs are aberrantly upregulated or downregulated in GC tissues, and their expression patterns are associated with various clinicopathological features, including tumor stage, lymph node metastasis, peritoneal metastasis, drug resistance, and overall survival. The expression level of ncRNAs can be detected in GC tissues and body fluids, including gastric juice, blood, and urine. In body fluids, ncRNAs are stable and resistant to RNase-mediated degradation, particularly when encapsulated within tumor-derived extracellular vesicles or bound to proteins [214]. The use of GC tissue samples for ncRNA detection directly reflects the ncRNA expression patterns in the tumor microenvironment, enabling the interpretation of the ncRNA expression profile alongside histopathological features. Nonetheless, this method is very invasive and has limited feasibility for serial monitoring [215]. The use of serum or plasma offers a minimally invasive approach suitable for continuous monitoring. Extracellular vesicles or RNA-binding proteins protect ncRNAs within the bloodstream from enzymatic degradation. Nonetheless, ncRNAs within the peripheral blood may reflect systemic changes rather than tumor-specific alterations. Moreover, the low concentration of tumor-derived ncRNAs within peripheral blood necessitates highly sensitive detection methods. There is also the risk of contamination from hemolysis or other circulating cells [216]. The use of circulating extracellular vesicles is also associated with isolation and purification challenges, and lacks a standard method for quantifying and normalizing ncRNA content [217]. Compared to peripheral fluids, gastric juice has a greater specificity for stomach-derived ncRNAs and has a relatively higher concentration of ncRNAs. Oncogenic alterations in the ncRNA expression pattern may be reflected in gastric juice earlier than in peripheral fluids. However, sample collection requires endoscopy, which is semi-invasive. On the other hand, results can vary significantly due to variations in pH and enzymatic activity, as well as the lack of a standard method for sample collection and storage [218, 219].

It should also be noted that there are significant variations in the results of currently available RNA sequencing techniques that make it difficult to make a clinical decision [220]. On the other hand, currently, standardized threshold values for ncRNA expression levels required for clinical decision-making are lacking [221]. Most of the discussed ncRNAs have been identified and validated in preclinical studies or small retrospective studies. Generalization and clinical application of ncRNAs require their validation in large, independent, and prospective patient cohorts. Moreover, given the unreliability of decision-making based on a single ncRNA, combining multiple molecules to diagnostic/prognostic ncRNA signatures can increase precision. Leveraging multi-omics approaches, artificial intelligence, and machine learning can also facilitate the development of ncRNA-based diagnostic/prognostic approaches in GC.

Therapeutic applications

Overexpression of tumor-suppressive ncRNAs and downregulation of oncogenic ncRNAs are promising therapeutic strategies that can inhibit tumor growth and enhance the efficacy of conventional therapies. In recent years, several methods have been developed for restoring tumor-suppressor ncRNAs and inhibiting oncogenic ncRNAs (Fig. 5).

Various approaches for restoring the function of the tumor suppressive ncRNAs or inhibiting oncogenic ncRNAs in GC. A Downregulated tumor suppressive ncRNAs can be restored via gene addition using viral or plasmid vectors. Synthetic miRNA mimics can also be used to replace downregulated natural miRNAs. CRISPRa system is another applicable approach that induces the expression of the genes of ncRNAs. B Oncogenic ncRNAs can be downregulated using RNA interference (RNAi) and anti-sense oligonucleotides (ASO). Permanent knock out of ncRNA genes can be achieved using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. The CRISPRi system enables transient repression of the expression of ncRNA genes

Tumor-suppressor ncRNAs can be restored transiently or permanently using viral or non-viral approaches. In viral methods, lentiviral vectors, adenoviral vectors, or adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors are used to transfer the gene of the intended ncRNAs into GC cells, resulting in the permanent or long-term expression of ncRNAs. Despite high efficiency, clinical use of viral vectors faces regulatory hurdles, safety concerns, and manufacturing complexities [222]. Plasmid vectors, synthetic miRNA mimics, and CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) systems can be utilized for transient restoration of tumor suppressive ncRNAs [223–225]. MiRNA mimics are chemically synthesized double-stranded RNA molecules designed to mimic the function of their natural counterparts. These molecules can be easily designed and synthesized on a large scale, thereby simplifying their clinical use. However, they are very prone to enzymatic degradation and require efficient delivery systems [224]. CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) system is a derivative of the traditional CRISPR/Cas9 system capable of inducing gene expression without altering the genome. In this system, epigenetic modulators are fused to a mutant form of Cas9 known as dead CAS9 (dCas9), which can recognize the target site but cannot cut it. Using this strategy, epigenetic modulators are guided to the ncRNAs’ genomic loci and induce their transcription [225].

Plasmid vectors, miRNA mimics, and CRISPRa components can be delivered into GC tissues using various non-viral delivery tools, including synthetic nanoparticles and exosomes. These tools can be equipped with intended ligands, aptamers, or monoclonal antibodies to deliver their cargo into GC cells in a targeted manner while avoiding off-target delivery. Nonetheless, these non-viral delivery tools have not yet been widely adopted in clinical settings as much as viral methods [226, 227].

Silencing oncogenic ncRNAs is another therapeutic strategy for inhibiting tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis. Inhibiting the oncogenic ncRNAs involved in drug resistance and immune evasion could also be combined with conventional therapies like chemotherapy, MAPK inhibitors, and immunotherapy to enhance their efficacy. Several methods are available that enable knockout or knockdown of oncogenic ncRNAs. NcRNA genes can be permanently disrupted using CRISPR/Cas9 pathways to prevent their oncogenic functions. Although CRISPR/Cas9 technology is highly effective for knocking out genes of ncRNAs, it carries the risk of off-target activity and double-strand break (DSB)-induced chromosomal abnormalities [228]. In recent years, several high-fidelity variants of CRISPR systems have been developed, offering a reduced risk of off-target cleavage. Some other CRISPR variants, such as base editors and prime editors, can permanently disrupt the genes of oncogenic ncRNAs without generating DSB, reducing the occurrence of chromosomal abnormalities [229].

Oncogenic ncRNAs can also be transiently inhibited using various systems. CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) can repress the transcription of oncogenic ncRNAs with a similar mechanism to that of the CRISPRa system. Replacing Cas9 nuclease with Cas13 in the traditional CRISPR system allows direct editing of ncRNAs instead of their genes [230]. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and antisense oligonucleotides (ASO) are other strategies for the transient downregulation of ncRNAs. While limited in vivo persistence of RNAi and ASO molecules is a main limitation for their clinical applications, several chemical and structural modifications have been developed in recent years to increase their stability. In recent years, various RNAi and ASO products have been approved for multiple diseases, raising hope for similar efficacy for downregulating oncogenic MAPK-regulating ncRNAs in GC [222, 231].

Tissue- or cancer-type-dependent function and multi-targeting function of ncRNAs are additional factors that should be considered in the clinical translation of ncRNA therapeutics. For example, TINCR and AOC4P act as oncogenic ncRNAs in GC, while they have a tumor-suppressive role in other types of cancers [112, 146]. In this regard, targeted delivery of therapeutic molecules into GC tissues or the use of tissue-specific promoters and inducible expression systems are crucial [232, 233]. On the other hand, many ncRNAs act on multiple targets or pathways, increasing the risk of off-target consequences [234]. Leveraging in silico prediction tools, artificial intelligence, and machine learning can help identify high-confidence ncRNA targets. Moreover, due to intra- and inter-tumoral heterogeneity, developing universally applicable therapeutic strategies can be challenging. In this regard, personalized profiling may be required to design patient-specific therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

GC is one of the most prevalent types of solid tumors, and its late-stage diagnosis is linked to an unfavorable prognosis and low overall survival. Biomarkers offer a cost-effective approach to diagnosing cancer and predicting its prognosis, and may aid in the development of novel therapeutic strategies. Nonetheless, the currently used biomarkers are not exclusive to GC and lack adequate sensitivity and specificity. NcRNAs are important regulatory axes of biological processes, and their dysregulation is a hallmark of various pathological conditions, including cancers. MAPK signaling pathways are among the most critical pathways whose dysregulation can initiate tumors or facilitate their progression. Although the underlying mechanism(s) of MAPK pathway dysregulation in GC have not yet been fully understood, the results of several studies indicate the crucial role of ncRNAs in this way. Herein, we discussed the ncRNA-mediated regulation of the MAPK signaling pathway and its role in the occurrence and progression of GC. We discussed how ncRNAs can directly or indirectly affect the MAPK pathway components. Although most of the discussed ncRNAs are potentially applicable as diagnostic, prognostic biomarkers, and therapeutic targets, further preclinical and clinical research involving large populations is needed to validate the clinical significance of MAPK-regulating ncRNAs. Future studies should be focused on: (i) elucidating the ncRNA regulatory network of the MAPK pathway by conducting bioinformatic analysis and preclinical validations. (ii) Validation of diagnostic/prognostic MAPK-regulating ncRNAs in large, multicenter GC cohorts. (iii) Identifying the best ncRNAs as therapeutic targets and their potential combination with other therapies. (iv) Targeted delivery of ncRNA therapeutics into GC tissues and ensuring their tissue-specific function. iv) leveraging multi-omics approaches, artificial intelligence, and machine learning to facilitate clinical translation of MAPK-regulating ncRNAs.
