Langerhans cell histiocytosis in the adult lower gastrointestinal tract
Authors: Xiaozhi Yang, Mohammad Alomari, Robert Raskin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryA female in her early 80s presented with chronic anal fissures which were unresponsive to conservative therapies. Examination was concerning for possible perianal Crohn’s disease, but subsequent colonoscopy and biopsies revealed Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) involving the colon and anus, diagnosed per 2022 WHO Classification of Haematolymphoid Tumours criteria and confirmed by BRAF V600E mutation. Systemic staging identified two unrelated malignancies of the breast and kidney. The patient was treated with cladribine as systemic chemotherapy, resulting in complete remission of LCH symptoms and lesions.This case illustrates key diagnostic and therapeutic considerations for clinicians: LCH can mimic inflammatory bowel disease and should be included in the differential for atypical or refractory perianal disease, especially in older adults. Histological confirmation and molecular profiling are essential for accurate diagnosis and treatment planning. Additionally, cladribine is a well-tolerated and effective option for adult LCH with gastrointestinal tract involvement. Early recognition and systemic staging are critical to guide therapy and identify potential coexisting malignancies.Keywords: Gastroenterology, Chemotherapy, Immunology, Haematology (incl blood transfusion), General surgery

Background

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare neoplastic disorder characterised by clonal proliferation of dendritic cells derived from myeloid precursors. These cells typically express CD1a, CD207 (Langerin) and S100 and commonly exhibit dysregulation of the mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) signalling pathway, often due to BRAF V600E mutations identified in over 50% of cases.1–3

Although LCH predominantly affects children, the rare occurrence in adults often presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to its broad clinical manifestations involving bone, skin, lungs and the central nervous system (CNS).2 3 The disease course in adults is also often heterogenous, ranging from indolent, localised lesions, to aggressive multisystem disease. Prognosis for isolated gastrointestinal (GI) LCH in adults is generally favourable, but multisystem involvement may require systemic therapy and close follow-up due to risk of progression or relapse.2 4

GI tract involvement of LCH in adults is exceptionally rare, with only 2%–3% of cases in larger cohorts.5 6 This leads to the lack of current published literature as they are mostly limited by sample size, heterogenous reporting and a lack of prospective studies. When GI lesions are present, they may be asymptomatic or manifest as abdominal pain, discomfort or, less commonly, bleeding.5–8 Endoscopic findings are also variable, including submucosal bulges, polyps, erosions, coarse granular mucosa and mucosal discolouration.5 7 8 Histopathological diagnosis relies on identification of characteristic Langerhans cells typically displaying reniform (coffee bean-shaped) nuclei with abundant pale eosinophilic cytoplasm and immunohistochemical positivity for CD1a and S100.8 9 The stomach is the most commonly affected site, followed by the oesophagus, colon and rectum.5–8 Manifestation involving the anus has been poorly elucidated with only one case report describing an adult patient with perianal disease and fissures in the context of multisystem LCH involving the biliary tract.10 To our knowledge, there are currently very few known cases of adult LCH in the GI tract presenting as isolated anal fissures or on initial presentation.

Clinical presentation of these cases often mimics more common disorders such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), infection or malignancy, thus complicating timely and appropriate diagnosis.11 Emerging evidence also indicates occasional coexistence or synchronous presentation of LCH with solid organ malignancies, including renal and breast cancer, potentially linked to shared oncogenic pathways such as the MAPK signalling cascade.3 12 The presence of BRAF V600E mutations is common in GI LCH, supporting its neoplastic nature.7 13 Accurate diagnosis and management require molecular and immunohistochemistry testing, comprehensive staging and targeted therapy.2 12 Given the rarity of GI-confined LCH, there is no standardised diagnostic or management algorithm. Therefore, additional case reports are warranted to further inform the accurate diagnosis and management of GI-confined LCH, including the role of molecular and immunohistochemistry testing, comprehensive staging and targeted therapy.

Case presentation

A female in her early 80s was referred to general surgery with over 1-year history of severe anal pain, secondary to anal fissures and perianal swelling. The pain was initially only associated with defecation but progressed to pain even while sitting. Conservative treatments were extensively trialled with limited benefit, including dietary fibre supplementation, various over-the-counter topical agents, compounded diltiazem ointment and corticosteroid creams. She denied rectal bleeding and reported having mostly regular daily bowel movements. She denied any other significant upper or lower GI symptoms. Despite adequate appetite, the patient unintentionally lost 140 lbs (from 280 lbs) over 5 years from food avoidance due to fear associated with painful bowel movements.

Her medical history is significant for Graves’ disease, hypothyroidism, hypertension, type 2 diabetes (controlled by diet), gout, recurrent ischaemic stroke, mild dementia and a remote history of ductal carcinoma in situ of the left breast, previously treated with lumpectomy and radiotherapy. Other surgical history included total abdominal hysterectomy, bilateral total knee arthroplasty and thyroidectomy. She is a non-smoker and does not consume alcohol.

On physical examination, the patient appeared frail but was haemodynamically stable. Abdominal examination was benign. Perineal examination revealed multiple deep anal fissures in non-classical locations at 2:00, 7:00 and 11:00, with evidence of chronic features and surrounding granulomatous-like changes (figure 1). Digital rectal examination was unremarkable.

Given the chronicity, poor response to medical therapy and atypical location of the fissures, the initial working diagnosis was that of complex fissure of non-ischaemic origin, though an underlying inflammatory or neoplastic process was also suspected. Given that her only surgical option was a diverting loop colostomy, the plan was to proceed with colonoscopy to rule out IBD or other mucosal pathology and to perform biopsies of the fissure to evaluate for malignancy or atypical ulcerative pathology.

During her initial colonoscopy, non-classical patterns of inflammatory mucosa were seen throughout the colon (figure 2). Gastroenterology was consulted and agreed that the colonic mucosa was less suggestive of Crohn’s, while the perineal appearance was more consistent for possible Crohn’s disease. The decision was made to proceed with biopsy of the anal and colonic mucosa for histopathological evaluation and to send faecal calprotectin to assess for occult IBD. The working diagnosis remained broad as the patient lacked other GI symptoms. Further investigations were pursued to delineate the cause.

Investigations

The patient initially underwent routine investigation for IBD and malignancies. Baseline testing showed a mildly elevated faecal calprotectin (64 µg/g). Colonoscopy demonstrated non-classical, patchy mucosal inflammation with scattered ulceration throughout the colon, with ulcerated lesions at the sites of the chronic anal fissures (figures 1 and 2); several polyps were removed during the procedure.

Surgical pathology reported that polyps removed from the cecum and transverse colon were tubular adenomas without high-grade dysplasia. One polyp removed from the descending colon contained aggregates of lymphocytes and eosinophils. Random colonic biopsies showed fragments of colonic mucosa with patchy involvement by LCH. Biopsies of anal fissures showed ulcerated anal mucosa with LCH involvement.

On H&E staining of biopsied colonic tissue, infiltrates of patchy aggregates of histiocytic cells along with some eosinophils within the submucosa can be seen (figure 3). On immunohistochemistry, the lesional histiocytes from random colonic and anal fissure biopsies stained positive for CD1a and S100 (figure 4). Specimen was also positive for cyclin D1 and CD43, negative for CD34, CD68, cytokeratin, CD3, CD30, SOX10, CD20 and PRAME. The Ki-67 proliferative index was approximately 20%. Molecular testing confirmed BRAF V600E in LCH tissue. The biopsy from the anal fissure showed ulcerated squamous mucosa with focal areas of granulation tissue formation and aggregates of histiocytic cells (figure 5). The diagnosis underwent internal peer review by a second local pathologist and was then reviewed at two external institutions (University Health Network-Toronto); all reviewers concurred with the diagnosis of LCH.

Following histological confirmation of LCH, systemic staging was performed prior to therapy to evaluate for extracolonic involvement. Cross-sectional imaging with CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis (CT-CAP) showed stable pulmonary nodules, a left adrenal adenoma and a 4.3×4.0×2.7 cm enhancing solid mass in the left kidney. Targeted renal ultrasound confirmed a solid, exophytic lesion with peripheral vascularity and percutaneous biopsy established papillary renal cell carcinoma (nucleolar grade 2). Screening mammography and targeted ultrasound identified a spiculated left breast mass (BI-RADS 5); core biopsy confirmed invasive ductal carcinoma (Nottingham grade III). Given the absence of further radiographic evidence of multisystem LCH, distant metastatic disease or osseous involvement on CT-CAP, positron emission tomography-CT was considered but was not deemed necessary by the oncology team before initiating management.

Routine haematological and biochemical profiles including complete blood counts and neutrophil counts were within normal ranges through evaluation. No additional sites of LCH were identified on staging, and there were no findings of distant LCH involvement. The breast and renal lesions were therefore considered unrelated, incidentally detected malignancies that warranted their respective work-ups and management independent of LCH. This case of LCH was therefore classified as single-system unifocal disease confined to the GI tract.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnoses considered were chronic idiopathic or ischaemic anal fissures, IBD (Crohn’s disease), anal malignancy and infectious aetiologies. LCH was not initially considered on the differential as it lacked clinical suspicion due to the rarity of its presentation in the adult GI tract. Chronic idiopathic or ischaemic anal fissures typically present in the anterior midline due to compromised blood supply; however, this patient’s multiple non-midline fissures made this diagnosis unlikely. Crohn’s disease was strongly considered due to perianal involvement. However, the absence of abdominal pain, characteristic granulomas, bloody diarrhoea and only mildly elevated faecal calprotectin also reduced its likelihood. Malignancy, specifically squamous cell carcinoma, was also considered due to persistent, refractory ulcerations but was excluded by biopsies. Infectious aetiologies, including herpes simplex virus, cytomegalovirus and syphilis, were also ruled out by histology and absence of other clinical risk factors. Ultimately, histological and immunohistochemical findings confirmed the rare diagnosis of GI LCH.

Treatment

Following histological confirmation of LCH, systemic chemotherapy was initiated with cladribine, a purine analogue that selectively induces apoptosis of proliferating dendritic cells.14 15 The patient received six cycles of intravenous cladribine at a dose of 6.51 mg daily over five consecutive days each cycle. No dose adjustments were required for this case.

Cladribine is typically administered at 0.14 mg/kg/day intravenously for 5 days every 4 weeks, for up to 4–6 cycles, with dose adjustments based on toxicity.16 17 Toxicities include grade 3 to 4 neutropenia and infection risk, warranting prophylaxis.17 18 Several systemic treatment options are available for adult LCH, and selection is guided by disease extent, organ involvement, clinical status and molecular profile.2 Prednisolone as monotherapy or vincristine combined with prednisolone represent historically used regimens, though evidence supporting their use in adults is limited and outcomes are generally less favourable compared with nucleoside analogue-based therapy such as cladribine and cytarabine.2 Cytarabine-based regimens, administered alone or in combination with cladribine, have demonstrated favourable outcomes in larger cohorts of adults and are considered alternatives for multisystem or refractory disease.5 19 For patients with confirmed BRAF V600E mutations, targeted therapy with the BRAF inhibitor vemurafenib has demonstrated efficacy in refractory or high-risk multisystem LCH, while MAPK/ERK kinase (MEK) inhibitors such as trametinib represent an emerging option for BRAF wild-type or refractory cases.2 20 Cladribine as a single agent has demonstrated an overall response rate of 84% in adult LCH with extrapulmonary involvement in one series, with subsequent studies reporting consistent efficacy outcomes, and was selected for this case given the patient’s clinical status, GI involvement and favourable tolerability profile.16–18

In this case, prophylactic treatment with acyclovir and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole was also administered to the patient. The patient tolerated treatment well, with marked clinical improvement along with complete resolution of anal pain and fissures including normalisation of bowel functions. Repeat colonoscopy also showed complete resolution of the atypical inflammatory mucosal changes and the scattered ulceration in the colon.

Concurrently, newly diagnosed invasive ductal carcinoma of the left breast was managed surgically via bilateral mastectomy, followed by adjuvant endocrine therapy with anastrozole based on standard treatment guidelines for hormone receptor positive breast cancer. With regard to left papillary renal cell carcinoma, interventional radiology and urology are likely favouring radiofrequency ablation over surgical intervention with the procedure currently under planning.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient completed the full six cycles of cladribine chemotherapy over 6 months followed by haematology-oncology. The treatment was well-tolerated, with no major haematologic or infectious complications. She experienced complete resolution of her perianal fissures and normalised bowel habits with no perianal pain. A repeat colonoscopy at 2 months post-treatment confirmed endoscopic remission, with no evidence of residual mucosal ulceration or histologic disease.

Approximately 8 months after LCH diagnosis, the patient underwent a bilateral mastectomy for newly diagnosed invasive ductal carcinoma of the left breast. She recovered uneventfully and was started on anastrozole as part of adjuvant endocrine therapy.

The patient’s renal mass, confirmed on biopsy as papillary renal cell carcinoma, remains under the care of urology and interventional radiology. Given her age, frailty and comorbidities, radiofrequency ablation is likely to be favoured over nephrectomy. This procedure is currently being planned, and the patient will continue to be followed by urology.

From a haematologic standpoint, the patient achieved clinical remission of LCH and is under routine surveillance every 3 to 6 months, including clinical examination, blood counts and cross-sectional imaging. At 11 months following initial diagnosis, the patient is clinically stable, with no recurrence of GI or perianal symptoms and no new systemic concerns. Surveillance imaging and specialist monitoring continue as planned to assess for recurrence or progression across all three disease processes of invasive ductal carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma and LCH.

Discussion

LCH is a clonal neoplasm of dendritic origin, driven by dysregulated MAPK signalling.1 2 Adult LCH is rare and often underdiagnosed, particularly in patients presenting with isolated or non-classical manifestations. The disease exhibits great heterogeneity, with multiple different organ involvement including bone, lung, skin, CNS and, more rarely, the GI tract.3 20 This case further underscores the importance of histologic confirmation of chronic perianal lesions and malignancy screening in older patients with rare presentations.

This case exemplified several important points regarding the diagnosis and management of adult onset LCH. First, the GI presentation of LCH in adults is exceedingly rare, often resulting in diagnostic delay due to clinical overlap with IBD, malignancy and infection.11 12 Comprehensive immunohistochemical staining and molecular testing, specifically for BRAF mutations, are essential for definitive diagnosis and targeted treatment selection.2

Second, the presence of synchronous solid tumours, such as renal and breast cancers, is noteworthy, suggesting a possible underlying shared oncogenic mechanism involving MAPK pathway dysregulation.3 20 21 It is evident that multidisciplinary coordination is crucial to ensure optimal staging and tailored therapeutic management strategies for all concurrent conditions of the disease.2 21

Cladribine, chosen as the systemic treatment in this case, is a purine analogue that selectively incorporates into the DNA of proliferating cells, causing strand breaks and subsequently inducing apoptosis in actively dividing histiocytes.14 15 Cladribine has demonstrated efficacy as a single agent in adult LCH with extrapulmonary and multisystem involvement, yielding overall response rates of 75%–91% and remissions reported in retrospective series and pooled analyses.16–18 Currently, there are no other known published studies or case reports showing the use of cladribine for LCH involving the GI tract in adults where the disease presents as or involves anal fissures. One cohort specifically documented anal fistulas with the patient undergoing successful surgery.5

Cladribine is favoured due to its excellent efficacy, favourable toxicity profile and rapid symptomatic improvement, which was evident in this case, demonstrating complete clinical and endoscopic remission.14 15 22 Alternative evidence-based treatment options for adult LCH include cytarabine, BRAF inhibitors such as vemurafenib and MEK inhibitors, each of which are chosen based on specific clinical scenarios and molecular profiling.2 22–24 Regular surveillance post-treatment is recommended every 3 to 6 months for at least 5 years, especially in BRAF mutation cases due to increased relapse risk and potential for secondary malignancies.2 4

Given the limited literature on adult LCH with GI involvement, additional accumulation of clinical reports and experiences is necessary. This report highlights the importance of identifying and further investigating atypical perianal disease while illustrating the effective use of cladribine as systemic therapy in adult LCH involving the colon and perianal region.
