Bridging the gap between policy and practice in adolescent health services: evidence from Indian primary public health centers
Authors: Ramesh Kumar Sangwan, Praveen Suthar, Simran Arora, Ritu Choudhary, Deependra Kajla, Mukti Khetan, Parul Kumawat, Diksha Chaudhary, Kamayani Mahawar, Pankaj Bhardwaj, Sumit Aggrawal, Naresh Midha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndia’s 243 million adolescents (21% of the population) face nutritional deficiencies, sexual and reproductive health concerns, mental health disorders, and rising non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Despite policies like Rashtriya Kishor Swasthya Karyakram (RKSK), major implementation gaps remain across public health facilities.MethodsThis scoping review, guided by Arksey and O’Malley’s framework and PRISMA-ScR guidelines, assessed adolescent health services at Health and Wellness Centers, Primary Healthcare Centers, and Sub-Health Centers from 2015 to 2025. Three electronic databases were searched, with standardized data extraction, expert consultation and thematic analysis mapped RKSK’s four pillars. Study quality was appraised using the MMAT (2018 version).ResultsOut of 1,371 records, only 11 met the inclusion criteria. Just 12.5% of facilities provided dedicated adolescent services. A study by Prasad et al. in 2024 reported that only 12.5% of assessed facilities provided dedicated adolescent-specific services and 6% of HWCs offered adolescent care. Barriers comprised lack of privacy, staff shortages, provider bias against unmarried youth (25%), supply chain issues, weak referral systems, poor intersectoral coordination, cultural stigma, and the need for parental consent requirements. Opportunities exist through digital health (78% smartphone use among adolescents), Stakeholder consultation participants estimated adolescent smartphone access at about 78% in the Sikar district. Public-Private Partnerships (PPP), community engagement, and innovative service models.ConclusionsDespite strong policy frameworks, persistent gaps are evident, with malnutrition (27.4% stunted growth) and rising obesity rates highlighting the urgency. Strengthening services demands political commitment, adequate financing, and culturally sensitive approaches to transform adolescent health and leverage India’s demographic potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15085-x.

Introduction

Adolescence, as defined by the WHO as the age between 10 and 19 years, is a crucial time of rapid physical, cognitive, and psychosocial change that connects childhood and adulthood [1–3]. India has around 243 million adolescents, making up about 21% of the country’s population [3, 4]. This is a pivotal period, marked by both opportunities and challenges, as many lifelong habits related to diet, physical activity, substance use, and reproductive health are often formed during this time [1].

The health issues faced by Indian adolescents are diverse and significant. Common problems include nutritional disorders (malnutrition and obesity), reproductive and sexual health problems, mental health issues (depression, anxiety, and substance abuse), and a rising prevalence of non-communicable diseases (hypertension and diabetes) [4, 5]. Social factors, such as peer pressure, violence, injuries, and gender-based inequities, further complicate their health scenario [5]. Although this age is often considered relatively healthy, many of these issues, if left unaddressed, have significant long-term effects on both individual and societal health [4].

Adolescents need not only healthcare but also supportive environments [6]. The Government of India has adopted a multi-layered approach involving HWCs, PHCs, and SHCs [7]. The AB-HWCs serve as platforms that provide comprehensive primary healthcare, including promotive, preventive, curative, and counselling services to meet adolescents’ needs [7]. At the community level, programmes such as the School Health and Wellness Programme involve teachers as Health and Wellness Ambassadors to discuss and educate students about mental health, sexual health, nutrition, and substance abuse. Teachers and the broader community play a key role in early identification and referral of behavioural and emotional issues, thereby strengthening adolescent health services’ impact [8].

At the national level, RKSK, which prioritised six dimensions, was launched in 2014: nutrition, sexual and reproductive health, mental health, injury and violence prevention, substance misuse prevention, and non-communicable diseases [9, 10]. RKSK conducts both community and facility-based interventions, including AFHCs at PHCs, CHCs, and District Hospitals that offer services such as counselling, health education, and referrals for specialised care [9, 10]. Activities like AHDs, now re-conceptualised as AHWDs, are organised in the community and brought to villages and schools to raise awareness and increase service utilisation [11, 12].

Despite such activities, several obstacles still hinder the effective implementation and use of adolescent healthcare services [13]. Reach and impact remain limited by geographic barriers, cultural stigma, lack of privacy and confidentiality, and insufficient awareness and involvement of adolescents in program designs [13, 14]. Health service providers sometimes lack the training or motivation to offer adolescent-friendly services, and confusion regarding legal or policy frameworks (including sexual and reproductive health) further restricts access [15]. Adolescents’ freedom of choice is often limited, especially for girls, while teachers and other community members are not always prepared or able to address underlying psychosocial issues [16, 17].

In the given context, a scoping review of the implementation status, barriers, and opportunities for adolescent healthcare programs in Indian public healthcare facilities is timely and necessary. Previous reviews explored adolescent health programs in India, but significant operational gaps remain unexamined. A study by Bahl et al. examined benchmark compliance in AFHCs, while study by Jain et al. evaluated broader national programs [13, 14]. There is limited evidence on how adolescent health services are actually implemented across decentralized systems like HWCs/AAMs, PHCs, and SHCs. This review fills that gap by integrating evidence from the four RKSK pillars: service delivery; community outreach; intersectoral convergence; and capacity building, covering the period from 2015 to 2025 [10]. This scoping review aims to address the what evidence exists regarding the implementation status, barriers, and opportunities related to adolescent health services delivered through primary public healthcare settings (HWCs/AAMs, PHCs, and SHCs) for adolescents (aged 10–19 years) in India?

Methodology

We selected a scoping review design to capture the breadth of evidence on the implementation, challenges, and opportunities related to adolescent health services in India. This review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA-ScRguidelines and followed the six-stage methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley (2005), further refined by Levac et al. (2010) [18–20]. The stages included: (i) specifying the research question, (ii) identifying relevant studies, (iii) selecting studies, (iv) extracting and charting data, (v) collating, synthesising, and reporting results, and (vi) integrating expert consultation [19]. Levac et al.’s refinements were applied to ensure that the research question was clearly defined, study screening was conducted independently by two reviewers, data charting was iterative and flexible, findings were synthesised thematically, and expert consultation was incorporated as a core step to strengthen the validity and relevance of the review [20].

Eligibility criteria

Studies and reports were eligible for inclusion if they focused on adolescents aged 10–19 years in India and examined the implementation, challenges, or opportunities related to adolescent health services, delivered through HWCs, PHCs, or SHCs. Peer-reviewed publications published between January 2015 and January 2025, were considered. Studies were excluded if they were not conducted in India, did not focus on adolescents, or did not cover health facility-based services.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search was undertaken from 2015 to 2025 across three electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science in English language.

The search strategy combined keywords and MeSH related to adolescent health and service delivery. Key terms included “adolescent health”, “Health and Wellness Center”, “Primary Health Center”, “Sub-Health Center”, “RKSK”, “implementation”, “barriers”, “opportunities”, and “India”. Boolean operators and truncation were applied to optimise retrieval.

To retrieve the literature, the search string was used with the Boolean operators AND and OR combined population terms (“Adolescents” OR “Adolescence” OR “Adolescents, Female” OR “Adolescent, Female” OR “Female Adolescent” OR “Female Adolescents” OR “Adolescents, Male” OR “Adolescent, Male” OR “Male Adolescent” OR “Male Adolescents” OR “Teens” OR “Teen” OR “Teenagers” OR “Teenager” OR “RKSK”) AND study descriptors (“Health and Wellness Centre” OR “HWC” OR “Ayushman Arogya Mandir” OR “AAM” OR “Primary Health Centre” OR “PHC” OR “Sub-Health Centre” OR “SHC” OR “Public Healthcare Facility” OR “Healthcare Services”) AND specific descriptors (“Implementation” OR “Barriers” OR “Opportunities”) AND geographic limitors (“India” OR “Asia, Southern” OR “Asia”).

Selection of studies

All search results were imported into the Rayyan software for management and screening. Duplicate records were identified and removed [21]. Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records against the eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant studies were then assessed for inclusion. Disagreements at either stage were resolved through discussion with the review team until consensus was reached. The overall selection process was documented using a PRISMA flow diagram [18].

Data extraction

A standardised data extraction form was developed in Microsoft Excel to capture key information from each study, including year of publication, author(s), study design, study setting, study objectives, and main findings. Extracted results were further categorised into three domains: implementation status, barriers, and opportunities. Barriers were organised according to the four pillars of the RKSK programme: (i) service delivery-availability and quality of AFHC/clinic and outreach services, privacy and confidentiality, respectful care, commodities and stock, referral pathways, and IEC at the point of care; (ii) community outreach-mobilisation activities, coverage, transport issues, and stigma; (iii) intersectoral convergence-linkages with education, ICDS, WASH, DMHP, and other sectors; and (iv) capacity building-training, supervision, mentorship, skill mix, and communication [9, 10]. A fifth category, other challenges, was included for issues beyond the RKSK pillars but relevant to programme delivery, such as policy, financing, monitoring, or socio-cultural barriers [9–11].

Although the review primarily examined facility-based adolescent health services, it also considered community outreach methods like schools, peer educators, ASHAs, NGOs, and digital platforms, since these elements serve as integrated parts of the RKSK service delivery framework [10].

Data extraction was carried out independently by two reviewers, and discrepancies were discussed with review team members until agreement was reached.

Data synthesis

Findings were grouped into predefined domains. The implementation status was summarised based on the current status of adolescent health services reported in the studies. Barriers and opportunities were organised under the RKSK pillars, with overlapping themes noted across categories. Opportunities were presented alongside barriers to highlight practical strategies for strengthening adolescent health services.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT, 2018 version) [22]. Two reviewers independently appraised each study, and differences were resolved by discussion until consensus was achieved. An overall percentage quality score was calculated and interpreted as ≤ 50% (low quality), 51–75% (average quality), or 76–100% (high quality). No studies were excluded based on quality scores; however, the appraisal findings informed the interpretation of results and helped identify areas where the majority of included studies were rated high quality in the MMAT appraisal.

Result

Our scoping review used Arksey and O’Malley’s six-stage framework (Fig. 1) [19]. We started with a broad research question on implementing adolescent health services at HWCs, PHCs, and SHCs, considering implementation, barriers, and opportunities, shaped by literature insights. We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for relevant studies and set inclusion criteria focusing on facility-based adolescent health implementation studies in India, excluding other countries and non-adolescents. Data were reviewed iteratively by theme implementation, barriers, and opportunities and validated with healthcare professionals and stakeholders. Results were synthesised thematically, mapping service coverage regionally, grouping barriers by level (individual, provider, facility, system, community), and categorising opportunities into training, integration, community engagement, and monitoring.

Following Stage 6 of the Arksey and O’Malley framework, a stakeholder consultation was held to verify initial findings and evaluate operational feasibility. Six stakeholders were purposively selected from various regions across India, including public health clinicians, reproductive and child health officers, and frontline program managers. The consultation took the form of a semi-structured, interactive workshop with prior consent from participants. Responses were carefully recorded through detailed notes and later organized thematically based on the RKSK implementation pillars.

Through database searches, we found a total of 1,371 records. We removed 86 duplicate records and screened 1,285 for eligibility. Of these, 1,232 were excluded during screening, and we retrieved 53 full-text reports. However, we could not access the full text for 12 articles. We assessed 41 reports for full-text eligibility, excluding 30 for various reasons: 8 involved the wrong population, 10 did not focus on adolescent health services, and 12 were excluded due to inappropriate study design, such as policy reviews, study protocols, or review papers. Ultimately, 11 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final review (Fig. 2).

It reveals that Indian adolescents are affected by dual malnutrition, with 27.4% being stunted, 24.4% underweight, 4.8% overweight, and 1.1% obese. Stunting is more common among girls and older adolescents, while underweight is more prevalent in boys and younger adolescents. Factors like gender, wealth, and maternal education have a significant impact on these outcomes. To address adolescent malnutrition, targeted interventions and integrating growth monitoring with school and national nutrition programs are crucial in breaking the cycle of poor nutrition across generations.

Table 2 shows the barriers to adolescent healthcare services at Ayushman Arogya Mandir and Primary Health Centres in India. The studies reviewed point out key obstacles, including concerns about privacy, staff shortages, poor infrastructure, and disrupted supply chains, which particularly affect the use of sexual and reproductive health services. Community outreach is limited by low awareness, stigma, parental opposition, and minimal involvement of NGOs. Coordination among sectors like health, education, and social welfare is weak, hindering comprehensive care. Efforts to build capacity through programs like RKSK reveal gaps in training and supervision, especially on sensitive topics like mental health and contraception. Cultural taboos, gender biases, and logistical challenges further limit adolescent access to services. These findings highlight the pressing need for improved infrastructure, training for providers, community engagement, and collaboration across sectors to improve adolescent health outcomes.

Barriers to adolescent healthcare services at ayushman arogya mandir and primary health centres in India

Table 3 shows the potential opportunities for adolescent healthcare services in Ayushman Arogya Mandir and Primary Health Centres in India. Key areas for improving adolescent health services include upgrading clinics to provide private, female-friendly spaces, offering free micronutrient supplements, and providing integrated counselling on substance use and menstrual hygiene. Enhancing school health services and forming partnerships with NGOs and community leaders can increase demand and access. Setting up adolescent-friendly health clinics with trained counsellors, standardised training, supportive supervision, and youth-friendly approaches can improve service quality and outreach. Using digital tools, expanding peer educator programmes, improving commodity supplies, and engaging communities and parents are also crucial strategies. These measures align with national programmes like RKSK and Ayushman Bharat, aiming for comprehensive, culturally sensitive, and accessible adolescent healthcare.

Discussion

Our scoping review reveals several barriers that impede effective adolescent health service delivery within India’s healthcare system. Studies have highlighted ongoing challenges in service delivery, community outreach, intersectoral collaboration, and capacity building, underscoring the complex relationship between system weaknesses and socio-cultural factors that collectively impact adolescent health outcomes.

Implementation concerns

Systemic challenges and operational barriers

The evidence reveals significant implementation concerns that transcend individual program components and reflect deeper systemic challenges within India’s healthcare infrastructure. Multiple studies document the persistent gap between policy formulation and ground-level implementation, with the RKSK exemplifying both the potential and pitfalls of large-scale adolescent health programming [33, 34].

Resource allocation and sustainability issues

Implementation studies consistently identify inadequate resource allocation as a fundamental barrier to program sustainability. The evidence suggested that while policy frameworks exist, insufficient financial and human resources are mobilised to ensure maximum impact [34]. States report significant variations in RKSK implementation, with some districts starting “virtually from scratch” while others build upon existing functional programs. This heterogeneity in baseline capacity creates uneven implementation outcomes and highlights the need for differentiated support strategies. The sustainability challenge is particularly acute for community-based interventions. Studies document how funding uncertainties and resource discontinuities risk program stability, especially for peer educator programs that depend on consistent training and supervision [34]. The temporary nature of many implementation support mechanisms, such as NGO-facilitated initiatives, raises concerns about long-term program viability once external support is withdrawn.

Training and capacity development gaps

The evidence shows significant differences in training quality and implementation fidelity across various states and models. Healthcare providers regularly request refresher training, mentioning that initial training was often provided “long-back” and was too short to cover all parts thoroughly [35]. The Madhya Pradesh model illustrates the success of structured, participatory training using standardised materials, whereas other states depend on traditional methods with inconsistent quality results. Major gaps exist in training healthcare providers to address sensitive topics, with many feelings uncomfortable discussing sexual and reproductive health issues with adolescents [36]. This discomfort is worsened by misunderstandings about legal frameworks, with some providers wrongly believing it is illegal to serve unmarried youth [28].

Monitoring and evaluation challenges

Implementation studies highlight significant weaknesses in monitoring and evaluation systems. While national programs have robust monitoring frameworks, ground-level data collection and quality assurance mechanisms remain inadequate. The absence of standardised reporting systems in some states undermines program monitoring and continuous improvement efforts. The lack of outcome evaluation beyond process indicators represents a critical gap in understanding program effectiveness [14]. Most implementation assessments focus on input and process measures rather than health outcome improvements, which limits the evidence for program impact and refinement.

Service delivery barriers

Research shows significant gaps in service delivery infrastructure and provider readiness in adolescent health programs. Several studies have consistently found inadequate privacy in healthcare facilities, with many AFHCs lacking essential confidentiality measures such as private consultation rooms and protocols to prevent interruptions [36]. This basic lack of privacy is a significant obstacle to engaging adolescents, particularly when it comes to sensitive issues related to sexual and reproductive health, where confidentiality is vital [37].

Attitudes and skills of healthcare providers are a major hurdle, with nearly a quarter of them hesitant to serve unmarried young people due to moral biases or misconceptions about the law [36]. When providers make judgmental comments or offer limited birth control advice, it goes against providing services that are friendly to adolescents and creates unequal care environments. These findings align with broader research suggesting that providers’ attitudes significantly influence young people’s use of healthcare services.

Resource constraints exacerbate challenges in service delivery, as studies indicate that essential commodities such as sanitary pads, contraceptives, and diagnostic tools often face inconsistent availability across health facilities [28, 36]. This issue is particularly severe for menstrual hygiene management programs, which suffer from irregular supply chains and inadequate inventory management, undermining their effectiveness and sustainability [38].

There are significant gaps in mental health services for adolescents, with most primary healthcare facilities lacking dedicated mental health support or trained staff [28, 36]. Healthcare workers say they feel unprepared and uncomfortable managing adolescent mental health issues, which often leads to missed opportunities for early intervention and support [39]. This is particularly concerning, given the rising number of mental health problems among Indian adolescents and the existing treatment gap in this age group.

Community outreach

At the community level, barriers highlight the significant impact of socio-cultural norms on health-seeking behaviours among adolescents. Research has consistently shown that stigma surrounding adolescent sexuality, particularly premarital sex, creates major obstacles to both generating demand and accessing services [36]. Furthermore, restrictions on the mobility of adolescent girls based on their gender limit their access to health information and services, leading to different access patterns among male and female adolescents. Community outreach efforts have limited reach and impact, posing a major challenge [40]. Research reveals that health workers have minimal direct contact with unmarried youth, resulting in information gaps that are often filled by unreliable peer networks and social media [32, 41]. This underscores the need for more proactive and structured community engagement strategies that can respect cultural sensitivities while providing accurate health information.

Intersectoral convergence

A lack of coordination exists between the health, education, and social sectors, which hinders the success of comprehensive adolescent health initiatives. Inadequate connections between school health programs and youth-friendly health clinics result in missed opportunities for integrated services and health promotion. The fragmented approach to adolescent health across various government departments leads to duplication of effort and gaps in service provision [42]. Referral systems highlight certain flaws, with studies showing inconsistent referral practices and poor communication between facilities that affect the continuity of care. The absence of standardised referral protocols and follow-up procedures also lowers the quality of service and erodes adolescent trust in the healthcare system [43].

Research consistently shows that inadequate training and supervision are significant obstacles to providing effective health services for adolescents. Multiple studies reveal that healthcare providers often request additional training on adolescent-specific issues, highlighting their lack of preparation to address sensitive topics like sexual health and substance use [44–46]. The absence of standardised training curricula and quality assurance mechanisms leads to inconsistent service quality across different facilities and regions.

Supportive supervision is a significant gap, with little evidence of regular, effective monitoring of adolescent healthcare services. This lack of supervision leads to persistent quality issues and missed opportunities for ongoing improvement in service delivery [47, 48]. Even with targeted efforts, menstrual health management programmes still face significant challenges, including inadequate disposal facilities, inconsistent supply of sanitary products, and strong taboos surrounding menstruation [49–51]. Research shows that despite knowledge-building initiatives, infrastructure gaps and social norms remain, hindering effective menstrual hygiene practices among adolescent girls [17].

Digital health opportunities and challenges

According to the review, digital platforms present both opportunities and challenges for adolescents accessing health information. Although social media and online sources are essential channels for adolescents to obtain information, concerns about the accuracy and reliability of health information on these platforms emphasise the need for verified digital health content and digital literacy programs [52, 53].

Potential opportunities

Innovation and strategic partnerships

Despite implementation challenges, the evidence identifies several promising opportunities for strengthening adolescent health service delivery through innovative approaches and strategic partnerships.

Digital health and technology integration

The COVID-19 pandemic has accelerated India’s digital health transformation, creating unprecedented opportunities for adolescent health programming. Telemedicine platforms have shown significant potential for reaching adolescents in remote areas and providing confidential consultations for sensitive health issues [54]. Evidence indicates that teleconsultation can overcome privacy concerns and geographical barriers that traditionally limit adolescent access to healthcare [55–57]. Digital platforms offer promise for mental health interventions, where stigma and provider shortages create significant access barriers [58]. Telepsychiatry programs have successfully increased mental healthcare access in rural areas, providing models that can be adapted for adolescent-specific needs [59]. The integration of digital literacy programs with health education can also combat online health misinformation and empower adolescents to access reliable health information [60].

However, the evidence also shows that most digital health initiatives focus on general populations rather than addressing youth-specific needs, highlighting opportunities for adolescent-focused digital solutions [61]. Creating youth-friendly digital health platforms that address sexual and reproductive health, mental health, and lifestyle diseases offers a significant chance to improve service accessibility and acceptability [62].

Public-private partnership potential

Evidence shows a genuine willingness among stakeholders to strengthen PPP in adolescent health. Private sector involvement presents opportunities to improve service access, introduce new technologies, and ease the pressure on public health systems [63–65].

PPP shows significant potential for addressing sensitive health issues where embarrassment or stigma prevents access to public services [63, 65].The private sector’s focus on preventive and promotive measures can complement the public sector’s emphasis on therapeutic care, leading to integrated service delivery models. Key recommendations for successful PPP implementation include establishing clear partnership foundations, defining roles and incentives for all stakeholders, ensuring sector coordination, providing training and capacity building, planning for sustainability, and implementing robust monitoring and evaluation systems [64, 66].

Community engagement and social innovation

The evidence highlights community engagement as a key opportunity to break down cultural barriers and increase demand for adolescent health services. Effective models show the importance of involving parents, community gatekeepers, and adolescents themselves in designing and implementing programs [67]. Innovative community engagement approaches involve linking adolescent health initiatives with established community events like Village Health Nutrition Days and Monthly Vaccination Days [24, 68].Employing youth-friendly communication styles, interactive techniques, and gamification can effectively enhance adolescent participation and improve knowledge retention [60].

The evidence also emphasises opportunities to leverage existing social networks and digital platforms that adolescents frequently use, such as coaching centers, educational institutions, and online spaces [69, 70]. Building formal connections between these informal networks and health systems can enhance outreach effectiveness and improve access to services.

Intersectoral convergence opportunities

The Learning Districts Initiative illustrates the potential for stronger intersectoral collaboration when supported by suitable mechanisms and resources. Successful convergence models demonstrate improved coordination among health, education, and social welfare sectors, resulting in more comprehensive adolescent health programs [14, 33].The School Health and Wellness Programme under Ayushman Bharat offers a valuable opportunity for structured adolescent health promotion through educational institutions [8].Training Health and Wellness Ambassadors in schools creates a foundation for ongoing health promotion activities and early detection of health issues [71].

Implementation science and adaptive management

The evidence indicates opportunities for using implementation science methods to enhance program effectiveness and scalability. The Learning Districts Initiative model of mentoring support, collaborative learning, and adaptive management suggests potential for strengthening district-level implementation capacity [34, 48]. Participatory problem identification and problem-solving approaches effectively address local implementation barriers while fostering local ownership and capacity. Utilising local NGOs and academic institutions for technical support and mentorship bridges the gap between policy formulation and ground-level implementation [68].

Innovation in service delivery models

The evidence highlights opportunities for new service models that overcome traditional barriers to adolescent health access. Mobile health clinics, outreach programs, and flexible schedules demonstrate potential for reaching hard-to-reach adolescent groups [72]. Integrating adolescent services with existing health programs and platforms can enhance efficiency and decrease stigma [12, 60, 64–66]. The evidence indicates that incorporating adolescent health components into broader health initiatives can boost acceptability and use.

Strategic recommendations for enhancing implementation

Based on the identified opportunities and implementation concerns, several strategic recommendations emerge:

Strengthening implementation infrastructure

Building effective monitoring and evaluation systems, standardizing training curricula, and ensuring sufficient resource allocation to sustain the program; Leveraging digital innovation: Integrating adolescent-focused digital health solutions, expanding teleconsultation services, and addressing digital literacy gaps to maximize technology’s potential; Fostering Strategic Partnerships: Building comprehensive public-private partnership frameworks, engaging community organisations, and establishing formal linkages with educational institutions; Building adaptive capacity: establishing mentoring and supportive supervision systems, promoting collaborative learning across districts, and applying implementation science methods for ongoing improvement; Addressing Cultural and Social Barriers: Creating culturally sensitive engagement strategies, fostering community ownership, and reducing provider bias through comprehensive training programs.

Implications for policy and practice

Based on these findings, several key implications emerge for adolescent health systems: Privacy and confidentiality require systematic infrastructure investments and standardized procedures [37]; Provider bias necessitates comprehensive training addressing technical skills and attitudes [73]; Poor inter-sectoral coordination demands formal integration mechanisms across health, education, and social welfare [74]; Community resistance requires culturally sensitive engagement strategies targeting social barriers [75].

Limitations and future research directions

Our review’s findings emphasise significant geographical and methodological diversity, which complicates applying the results across India’s varied contexts. Future studies should focus on mixed-methods evaluations that combine quantitative data with qualitative insights on adolescent health service delivery. Long-term studies assessing the success of multi-sectoral interventions in overcoming these complex barriers would also offer valuable guidance for enhancing programmes. Additionally, the review was limited to peer-reviewed literature indexed in selected databases. Consequently, relevant implementation evidence from government reports, program evaluations, policy documents, and other gray literature may not have been captured. Full-text articles could not be accessed despite institutional database searches and attempts to contact corresponding authors. Their exclusion may have introduced selection bias and limited the comprehensiveness of the synthesis.

Similarly, the review emphasises the need for more research on effective ways to reach hard-to-access adolescent groups, especially those who are out of school and live in rural areas. Understanding the unique needs and preferences of these groups is crucial for developing inclusive adolescent health programmes.

Conclusion

While India has strong adolescent health policy frameworks, it still faces significant challenges in delivering services, engaging communities, coordinating sectors, and building workforce capacity. These challenges are interconnected and require coordinated solutions that address both health system and socio-cultural barriers. Nevertheless, the growing adoption of digital health, smartphone access, and increased awareness of mental health now present unprecedented opportunities for transformative change. To achieve meaningful progress, India needs sustained political will, investment in resources, and innovative, culturally sensitive programs that meet the diverse needs of its 243 million adolescents [3, 4]. Strengthening adolescent health services is crucial for the country’s social and economic future, and this review offers emerging evidence and guidance for turning policy ambitions into effective practice.

This scoping review integrates findings from a focused yet significant literature base, identifying implementation gaps, contextual barriers, and potential opportunities to strengthen adolescent health services within Indian primary healthcare settings.
