Introduction

Left atrial appendage aneurysm (LAAA) is a rare structural abnormality of the left atrium, with just over 177 cases reported in the literature.1 It may be congenital —usually due to pectinate muscle dysplasia— or acquired, in association with chronic elevation of left atrial pressures, pericardial defects, myocarditis, or infectious diseases such as tuberculosis.2 The condition can remain asymptomatic for years, leading to delayed or incidental diagnosis.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old woman with no relevant medical history attended the emergency department due to a 2-week history of palpitations and exertional dyspnoea. She also described orthopnea and easy fatigability, without chest pain, syncope, or other systemic symptoms.

On examination, she was alert and haemodynamically stable. Heart rate was 110 beats per minute with an irregular rhythm, respiratory rate was 23 breaths per minute, and peripheral oxygen saturation was 98% on room air. Blood pressure was 104/68 mmHg. Bibasilar crackles were audible, and mild hepatomegaly, a positive hepatojugular reflux, and trace bilateral ankle oedema were observed.

The electrocardiogram revealed atrial fibrillation with a slightly rapid ventricular response (100–110 beats/min). Chest radiography demonstrated a markedly prominent upper left cardiac border suggestive of an abnormal left atrial contour (Figure 1, panel A).

(A) Chest radiograph demonstrating an abnormal cardiac silhouette with marked prominence of the upper left cardiac border (arrowheads). (B) Transthoracic echocardiogram, apical four-chamber view, showing a large ‘fifth’ cardiac chamber—larger than the left ventricle (LV)—corresponding to the left atrial appendage aneurysm (LAAA). (C) Apical two-chamber view clearly delineating the neck of the aneurysm. Abbreviations: LA, left atrium; LAAA, left atrial appendage aneurysm; LV, left ventricle; RA, right atrium; RV, right ventricle.

Transthoracic echocardiography showed a large, thin-walled cavity adjacent to the left ventricle (LV), which corresponded to a congenital LAAA [Figure 1, panels B and C; Supplementary material online, Video S1 (Supplementary material)]. The aneurysm exceeded the LV in maximal dimension. The LV was normal in size but exhibited moderate systolic dysfunction, with a biplane Simpson ejection fraction of 35%–40%. No intracavitary thrombi were visualized. The right-sided chambers and valves were normal, and no pericardial effusion was present.

Laboratory testing was unremarkable except for mildly elevated natriuretic peptides. Thyroid function and inflammatory markers were normal.

The patient was initiated on intravenous furosemide (40 mg every 8 h) and enoxaparin (80 mg every 12 h). After clinical stabilization, oral bisoprolol (5 mg/day), empagliflozin (10 mg/day), lisinopril (5 mg/day), and spironolactone (12.5 mg/day) were introduced. By hospital day 2, she was euvolemic and expressed a preference for conservative management over surgical intervention. By day 5, she was asymptomatic and was discharged with rivaroxaban (20 mg/day) as long-term anticoagulation.

At outpatient follow-up, she remained asymptomatic and maintained normal functional status. No recurrent arrhythmias, other than atrial fibrillation, were documented.

Discussion

Clinical presentation

Although many patients are asymptomatic, the most commonly reported symptoms include palpitations, dyspnoea, and thromboembolic events.3,4 Atrial arrhythmias, particularly atrial fibrillation, may arise due to atrial remodelling or mechanical distortion from the aneurysm. In the present case, atrial fibrillation likely contributed to acute decompensation in a patient whose LV systolic dysfunction was probably secondary to longstanding structural abnormalities.

Daralammouri et al.1 reviewed 177 cases (from articles published between January 1940 and November 2022), finding a slight female predominance (50.9%), a mean age of 29.7 years, and symptomatic presentation in approximately three-quarters of the patients. These findings reflect the variable onset and clinical expression of the condition. Diagnosis

Diagnostic confirmation relies on imaging. Transthoracic echocardiography is usually the first-line modality and may suggest the diagnosis, particularly when a left-sided mediastinal prominence or an unexpected cavity adjacent to the LV is observed. However, transesophageal echocardiography, cardiac CT, and cardiac MRI offer superior anatomical definition and are particularly useful in assessing size, neck morphology, and the presence of thrombus.3–5 In our patient, the diagnosis was established by transthoracic echocardiography, which showed clear delineation of the aneurysmal cavity and its communication with the left atrium.

Histopathological analysis is not diagnostic, as reported findings —endocardial fibrosis, myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis, and fatty infiltration— are nonspecific.3,6

Management considerations

Aneurysmectomy is generally considered the preferred and curative treatment, especially for symptomatic patients or for those with large aneurysms at risk of compression of adjacent structures or thromboembolism. Surgical results are typically excellent, with low rates of recurrence.

However, in asymptomatic patients or in those who prefer nonsurgical approaches, conservative management may be considered.1,2 Oral anticoagulation is frequently used, although its indication in asymptomatic patients without documented thrombus or atrial fibrillation remains uncertain.5 Evidence comparing vitamin K antagonists and direct oral anticoagulants is lacking. The theoretical efficacy of factor Xa inhibition in preventing thrombus formation within the LAAA has not yet been validated in this context.2,3

In the present case, the patient opted for conservative therapy. Given her atrial fibrillation at presentation and the large aneurysm size, long-term anticoagulation was considered appropriate.

Prognosis

Prognosis depends largely on symptom burden and complication risk. Patients undergoing surgical correction generally have an excellent outcome. Those managed conservatively require close follow-up to monitor for arrhythmias, thrombus formation, aneurysm enlargement, or the development of heart failure.
