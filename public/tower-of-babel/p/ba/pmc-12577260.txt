The effect of strabismus surgery on the quality of life in children and adolescents
Authors: Betül Dertsiz Kozan, Mehtap Savar Çağlayan, Mesut Sari, Mehmet Fuat Alakuş, Hasan Öncül, Umut Dağ
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWe aimed to examine the effect of strabismus surgery on quality of life levels in children and adolescents.MethodsThe Health-Related Quality of Life Inventory version 4.0 (HRQOL 4.0), consisting of 23 questions and 4 subscales (physical functioning, emotional functioning, social functioning, and school problems), was administered to the children and adolescents who underwent strabismus surgery and their parents between December 2024 and June 2025, at preoperative and postoperative 1st and 3rd months. The postoperative deviation angle of the cases was 10 Prism Diopter (PD) or less. Strabismus surgery was performed by an Ophthalmologist, and the scales were evaluated by a Child and Adolescent Psychiatrist.Results27 cases were included in our study. 17 (62.9%) of the cases were girls and 10 (37.1%) were boys. The average age was 9.0 ± 3.9 (7–17) . In child and adolescents, the change in the physical functionality parameters of the cases was not statistically significant (p >0.05), but the change in the parameters of emotional functionality, social functionality and school-related problems between the preoperative, postoperative 1st month and postoperative 3rd month was statistically significant (p < 0.05). In the parents’ proxy opinion of the child, the change in physical functionality, emotional functionality, social functionality parameters between preoperative, postoperative 1st month and postoperative 3rd month was statistically significant (p < 0.05), but in the parameters of school-related problems, preoperative, postoperative 1st month and postoperative 3rd month were statistically significant. The change between was not statistically significant(p >0.05) (Table 2).ConclusionSuccessful surgery for strabismus results in measurable improvement in the quality of life in children and adolescents and in the parents proxy opinion of the child’s quality of life.Keywords: Strabismus surgery, Children, Adolescent, Quality of life

Introduction

Strabismus surgery is not performed solely to bring the eyes into a straighter position, elimination of diplopia, or improvement in stereo acuity. One of the most important indications for strabismus surgery is to improve the quality of life and improve psychosocial symptoms, especially in school-age and adolescent children [1–5]. It has been reported that strabismus surgery is quite beneficial for patients for psychosocial reasons and improves their quality of life [2, 3]. Patients with strabismus experience psychological problems, being ridiculed, low self-esteem and self-confidence, lower chances of finding a job, avoiding social situations or activities, difficulty in making eye contact, and problems in interpersonal relationships. While patients with strabismus may achieve improvements in social avoidance, daily functioning, and psychological adjustment after strabismus surgery, patients with psychosocial symptoms achieve the greatest improvement [6]. Scales that assess the quality of life of children, adolescents, and parents of patients were defined many years ago [7–9]. Quality of life scales have been updated over time and finalized to include more parameters [10, 11]. However, there are more studies on quality of life in adults and a limited number in children and adolescents. Our study aimed to examine the effect of strabismus surgery on the quality of life of children and adolescents, as well as on the parents’ proxy opinions of their child’s well-being.

Material and method

Our study was conducted prospectively and descriptively between December 2024 and June 2025. Ethics committee approval was obtained from Gazi Yasargil Training and Research Hospital (dated 22.11.2024 and numbered 224). Written voluntary consent forms were obtained from the parents of the cases. The principles of the 2013 Helsinki Declaration were applied throughout the study. Cases who underwent surgery due to strabismus (esotropia or exotropia) and whose postoperative angle of deviation was 10 PD or less were included in the study. Single parent of each case was included in the study. The fact that there was the same parent was required to fill in all of the questionnaires at each stage to ensure continuity and better evaluate the change in parameters measured. The demographic characteristics of the cases, uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) and best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) measured with the Snellen chart, dilated autorefractometer measurements were obtained using Sikloplejin® 1% (cyclopentolate hydrochloride 10 mg/mL; Abdi İbrahim Pharmaceuticals, Istanbul, Türkiye), and eye movements were examined. In our study, 2 scales were used: child (Scale 1) and parent (Scale 2). The child (Scale 1) consisted of questions in which the patient evaluated himself/herself, and the parent (Scale 2) consisted of questions in which the parent evaluated his/her child. Both were health-related quality of life version 4.0 (HRQOL) scales, consisting of 4 subgroups (physical functioning, emotional functioning, social functioning, school-related problems) and a total of 23 questions [10]. The scales were applied preoperatively, at the 1st month postoperatively, and at the 3rd month postoperatively. The answers to each question (never: 0, rarely: 25, sometimes: 50, often: 75, almost always: 100) were given, and a score between 0 and 100 was made. The 0-100 scale for each section is explained as better / worse functioning. The total score obtained in each subgroup was calculated by dividing it by the number of questions in the subgroup. Higher scores indicated better functioning, lower scores indicated worse functioning. If more than 50% of the items in a scale were missing, no score was calculated. The internal reliability of each dimension was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha after data collection; values ​​≥ 0.7 were considered acceptable. Strabismus surgery was performed by an Ophthalmologist, and the scales were evaluated by a Child and Adolescent psychiatrist. In our study, we included children aged between 7 and 17 years because this age range corresponds to the school-age period, when psychosocial interactions and academic challenges become more apparent. Including younger children might have introduced variability related to developmental and cognitive differences. Inclusion criteria for the study were: Cases who underwent strabismus surgery between the ages of 7 and 17, had a preoperative angle of deviation greater than 10 PD, came for postoperative 1st and 3rd month follow-up and had angle of deviation of 10PD or less postoperatively. Cases dealing with family problems (parental divorce, separation, and single-parent families) or known psychological or chronic ophthalmological/systemic disorders that could affect the outcome of the surgery or psychosocial behavior were not included in the study. Successful surgery was defined as a postoperative deviation angle of 10PD or less.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS, Version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) package program was used to analyze the data. The suitability of the data for normal distribution was evaluated with the Shapiro wilk test. Numerical variables are given as mean ± SD. When comparing preoperative and postoperative 1st and 3rd month scores with the Friedman test, P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

A total of 27 cases and their 27 parents were included in the study. Of the cases, 17 (62.9%) were girls and 10 (37.1%) were boys, with a mean age of 9.0 ± 3.9 years (range, 7–17).

There was no statistically significant difference in UCVA and BCVA between the preoperative and first postoperative month (p > 0.05).

Among the cases, regarding quality of life outcomes (Table 1), emotional functionality, social functionality, and school-related problems showed statistically significant improvements between the preoperative and first postoperative month (p < 0.05 for emotional functionality; p < 0.001 for social functionality; p < 0.05 for school-related problems). In contrast, physical functionality did not change significantly (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Discussion

In our study, significant improvements were observed in emotional, social, and school-related domains of HRQOL among the cases, whereas physical functionality remained unchanged. The HRQOL quality of life scale can be used to evaluate the effects of strabismus surgery on physical functioning, emotional functioning, social functioning, and school-related problems. Strabismus surgery may improve all measures of quality of life in children and adolescents except physical functioning. Parents may report that their child’s academic and related problems do not improve because of limited opportunities to observe their child. Parents may report that their child improves in all other measures of quality of life.

Strabismus causes a wide variety of physical and psychosocial problems and reduces the quality of life in affected patients [12–14]. Children and adolescents notice their different appearance due to their peers, suffer from aesthetic appearance and cause environmental isolation [15, 16]. Therefore, Kothari and colleagues believe that the correct age for strabismus surgery is before the age of four. Additionally, most children begin to interact with their peers at this age, so four years of age is considered the critical age. The reason for this is to make the necessary cosmetic corrections before starting school and to prevent bullying by their peers [16]. In our study, we examined the 7–17 age group, which we think is most affected by the results of strabismus. Since the number of cases was limited, the results could not be evaluated according to age groups and this was one of the factors that limited our study. However, despite this limitation, according to our results, a significant and distinct improvement in the quality of life of the cases was observed in terms of emotional and social functioning and school-related problems, except for physical functioning. We think that the lack of a statistically significant difference in physical functioning is due to the fact that strabismus does not affect the movement and balance system when it is not acute or cannot be detected. Nouraeinejad et al. reported in their study on the effect of strabismus on posture that posture was affected in acute situations [17]. We think that there is no deterioration in the movement and balance system in long-term strabismus. The impact of strabismus on psychosocial and quality of life extends to children’s families. It has been reported that parents of children with strabismus have an increased sensitivity to depression or anxiety and have difficulty establishing relationships with their children [18, 19]. Moreover, the psychological state of parents may affect children’s attitudes towards treatment and their level of knowledge about the disease [19]. Parents’ and children’s concerns may not be the same. In the parents, a significant and meaningful improvement was observed in physical, emotional and social functioning, excluding school-related problems. We think that the lack of significant improvement in school-related problems is due to parents not being able to make adequate observations because they are not in the school environment like their children. In our study, all cases were selected from postoperative orthophoric patients. Successful surgery was defined as a postoperative deviation angle of 10 PD or less. The reason for this selection was the assumption that the higher the postoperative surgical success, the greater the improvement in quality of life scales. Ziaei et al. showed that most quality of life measures improved after surgical intervention and reported a positive correlation between the degree of ocular alignment correction and certain subdomains of quality of life [20]. Similarly, in our study, it is possible that varying preoperative deviation angles might have influenced the extent of improvement in quality of life scores. Patients with higher preoperative deviations may have experienced greater subjective improvement compared with those with smaller deviations. However, due to the limited sample size, subgroup analysis based on preoperative deviation magnitude could not be performed, which constitutes one of the limitations of our study. Archer et al. reported an improvement in quality of life scales was detected even in surgeries with a deviation angle greater than 10 PD, which were considered unsuccessful, but as reported in this study, the small number of cases reduced the power of the study [21]. In contrast to these results, McBrain et al. reported that improvements in quality of life were not related to how clinically successful the surgery was. This contrasts with other findings suggesting small improvements in functional quality of life after unsuccessful surgery and larger improvements after both partial and successful surgery. However, this difference could be explained by the difference in the criteria used to define success and failure in the studies [22]. In addition, Wen et al. reported that there was no change in the quality of life after strabismus surgery [23]. It should be noted that the follow-up intervals in the studies cited vary. Some studies evaluated quality of life only preoperatively and once postoperatively, while others did not specify the timing of postoperative assessments. In contrast, our study assessed patients at both the 1st and 3rd postoperative months, which allows for a more detailed observation of short-term changes. The impact of strabismus may be related to the patient’s judgment of the problem rather than its severity. Therefore, more studies are needed in orthophoric and non-orthophoric groups where the number of cases is higher. The incidence of strabismus has been reported to be between 2% and 4%, and despite such a high prevalence, patient-reported gains after corrective surgery in children with strabismus have not been adequately investigated [24]. Strabismus surgery has been reported to improve overall quality of life and specific quality of life domains such as depression and self-esteem [4, 25]. Our study supports the literature. A multidisciplinary approach with ophthalmologists, psychiatrists, or psychologists can help reduce excessive anxiety and educate parents and children about the disease. Little is known about the use of psychosocial interventions to improve quality of life in patients with strabismus, and more research on this topic is needed [26].

Limitations of the study

The current study is limited by the lack of a control group, meaning that the changes observed before and after surgery cannot be directly attributed to the surgery. Another limitation of the current study is the inclusion of only postoperative orthophoric patients. This selection may have introduced a potential bias toward better quality of life outcomes, as patients with successful surgical alignment are more likely to report higher satisfaction. Future studies including patients with varying postoperative deviations would provide a more comprehensive assessment. Studies with larger deviation angles and wider age ranges with longer-term outcomes are needed.

Conclusion

Successful surgery for strabismus results in measurable improvements in the quality of life in children and adolescents, in the parent’s assessment of the child’s quality of life, and in the parent’s quality of life.
