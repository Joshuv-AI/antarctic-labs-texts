Background

Placement of implants in the posterior maxilla has always been challenging due to its low bone density and limited bone mass. Sinus lift or vertical bone augmentation is commonly performed to overcome these limitations and allow axial insertion. However, bone grafting increases the time of operation and incurs additional costs; moreover, it results in increased surgical trauma. In 1974, Linkow proposed a new implant strategy known as the pterygoid implant to preclude the need for bone grafting [1]. Following an inclined insertion path, pterygoid implants are placed through the maxillary tuberosity and into the pterygoid plate. The placement of a 15–20 mm long angulated implant through the pterygoid process into the fossa can preclude the need for bone grafting and easily obtain cortical bone anchorage of 8–9 mm, thereby ensuring good primary stability, even for cases of immediate loading [2, 3].

Despite being technique-sensitive, a recent systematic study demonstrated the survival rate of modern roughened-surface dental implants in the pterygoid region to be comparable to that of implants in other regions of the maxilla and mandible, thus supporting the use of pterygoid implants in certain cases [4]. Most pterygoid implants do not invade the maxillary sinus; however, in certain clinical cases with large sinus cavities, pterygoid implants must pass through the sinus to reach the pterygoid plate [5]. Surgery is much more challenging in these situations, and additional operative factors should be considered.

However, when the maxillary tuberosity exhibits ideal bone mass and density, the implant need not extend to the pterygoid plate to ensure primary stability or long-term success. Tuberosity implants help avoid complicated surgical steps, thereby reducing surgical complications [6].

Despite clear definitions, certain terms, such as “pterygoid implants,” “tuberosity implants,” and “pterygomaxillary implants,” are still used interchangeably in the literature to describe implants in the maxillary retromolar region following a graftless approach. Clinicians lack guidance and an evidence-based approach for implant placement in this area.

Therefore, this study aimed to provide a novel classification for all implants in the maxillary retromolar region to simplify surgical design, reduce surgical risks, and guide clinicians in clinical decision-making. In this study, the term “pterygomaxillary implants” was chosen as the collective term to describe all the implants in the retromolar region following the suggestion of Balshi et al. [7]; the implants were classified according to the anatomic structures the implants passed through.

Methods

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) examinations of patients and pterygomaxillary implant sites

This retrospective cohort study met the criteria for medical research involving human participants according to the ethical principles described in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University(approval no.: K2024-194–01).

In this study, CBCT was performed using the New Tom VGi CBCT system (QR SRL Co., Verona, Italy). The patients were positioned with the Frankfort horizontal plane parallel to the floor. The sagittal and coronal images were acquired at 110 kV and 10 mA. The exposure volume was 120 mm in diameter and 80 mm in height. The slice thickness of the multiplanar reconstructed images was 0.2 mm.

In total, CBCT data from 180 patients were included. The study inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) CBCT images were taken before the dental implant surgery, and (b) patients presenting with bilateral partial or completely edentulous atrophic posterior maxillae for more than half a year (vertical residual bone height < 6 mm in the posterior maxilla). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) CBCT images with motion artifacts or metal artifacts, and (b)impacted third molar in the tuberosity.

New classification

The pterygomaxillary implants were divided into three different types based on the anatomical structures the implants passed through. As distinguishing the pyramidal process of the palatine bone is challenging using CBCT data, this anatomical structure was not considered while guiding the classification. To prevent cracking of the tuberosity, the distal part of the implant neck should be designed at least 5 mm mesial to the distal margin of the maxillary tuberosity. To sustain ideal implant strength, all pterygomaxillary implants should exhibit a diameter of at least 4 mm. Moreover, at least 2 mm of bone thickness should remain on each side of the implant after placement. All the implants (mentioned below) follow these rules:

Type I, Tuberosity implants: The entire body of the implant is embedded in the maxillary tuberosity. To ensure the ideal primary stability of the implant, the minimal implant length should be 10 mm, and the bone density around the implant should be ideal.

Virtual implant placement at the pterygomaxillary region

The Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format of the CBCT raw data was uploaded to the SimPlant Pro v.15 software (Densply, Mannheim, Germany) for virtual planning using an implant that was 4.3 mm in diameter (Nobel Active, Nobel Biocare, Sweden). When the bone mass of the tuberosity was optimal, we initially placed a 10 mm implant adjacent to the second molar to achieve an ideal prosthesis platform. The implant’s tip could be oriented posteriorly or palatally, depending on the anatomy of the tuberosity. If the tuberosity could fully accommodate the implant body and the bone density around the implant exceeded 400 Hounsfield units (HU) [8], Type I would be the preferred choice, allowing for an increase in implant length in accordance with the tuberosity’s mass. When the bone density around the implant was below 400 HU, Type IIa would be selected by adjusting the angulation and implant length to ensure the implant tip was anchored in the pterygoid plate. Conversely, if the bone mass of the tuberosity was suboptimal, the implant neck would be positioned more distally to maintain sinus integrity. If a placement is identified that harmonizes sinus integrity with tuberosity strength, Type IIb would be the preferred choice. If not feasible, priority would shift away from preserving sinus integrity in favor of Type III. Nonetheless, a minimum of 4 mm of vertical residual bone height around the implant neck must be preserved, or the site would be deemed unsuitable for a pterygomaxillary implant.

Statistical analysis

Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to determine the inter-rater reliability. Twenty CBCT image data were randomly selected to assess inter-examiner error. Two experienced dentists, both with over 10 years of experience in dental implants, were recruited to test the inter-observer agreement. Descriptive statistics of the virtually planned implant values were calculated.

Results

Patient demographics

The average age of the 180 patients was 69 (range: 39–89)years; 99 were men and 81 were women.

Distribution of the types and subtypes

All the patients exhibited 360 pterygomaxillary implant sites. However, during mimic implantation, 14 implant sites were excluded due to severe resorption of the tuberosity, very small pterygoid plates, or variations in the descending palatal artery configuration.

The ICC value was found to be 0.956, indicating good inter-rater reliability, which also suggests that this new classification is easy to understand and replicate.

Of the 346 pterygomaxillary implant sites, 24.0% (83/346), 40.7% (141/346), 22.0% (76/346), and 13.3% (46/346) were classified as Types I, IIa, IIb, and III, respectively.

Surgical implications for each type of pterygomaxillary implant

Type I, tuberosity implants

In cases of ideal bone mass and density, tuberosity implants (diameter ≥ 4 mm, length ≥ 10 mm) should be considered the first choice for the least operational difficulty and risk of surgical complications.

However, unlike conventional implant designs, an inclined implant (the implant tip is slightly remote and slightly offset to the palatal side) should be considered to allow a longer implant and more medial implant neck, thus ensuring primary stability and facilitating the prosthodontic phase.

In some borderline cases, satisfactory initial implant stability can be achieved without engaging the pterygoid plate by fully using the cortical bone of the posterior wall of the maxilla. An implant penetrating the posterior wall of the maxillary sinus without engaging the other bony structures is also classified as a Type I implant (Fig. 2).

Type I pterygomaxillary implants. a Preoperative radiographic examination shows ideal bone mass and bone density in the tuberosity. b Ideal bone density around the implant socket. c Preoperative radiographic examination reveals that the whole body of the implant is embedded in the tuberosity

Type II, Pterygoid implants.

When the bone mass in the maxillary tuberosity is insufficient to accommodate the ideal length of the implant or the bone density is low, the implant tip must enter the pterygoid plate to achieve ideal initial stability.

Type IIa, Pterygoid implants with adequate bone mass and low bone density of the tuberosity.

In this situation, the implant design should allow the placement of the implant neck close to the second molar to facilitate the prosthodontic phase. By choosing a long implant (≥ 15 mm) and adjusting the angulation of the implant (mostly 45–55° to the Frankfort-Horizontal plane), the tip of the implant can engage the pterygoid plate for ideal primary stability (Fig. 3).

Type IIa pterygomaxillary implants. a Preoperative radiographic examination showing ideal bone mass and low bone density in the tuberosity. b Thin cortical bone around the socket neck. c Preoperative radiographic examination shows that the tip of the implant is embedded in the pterygoid fossa, and the implant neck is in the most medial position

Type IIb, Pterygoid implants with limited bone mass of the tuberosity.

In this situation, the implant should be designed to maintain the integrity of the maxillary sinus for easy drilling control and less bleeding. The implant length should be decreased accordingly during distal movement of the implant neck. However, a 5-mm safe distance between the distal part of the implant neck and the distal margins of the maxillary tuberosity should always be maintained. Otherwise, a Type III design should be considered (Fig. 4).

Type IIb pterygomaxillary implants. a Preoperative radiographic examination shows limited bone mass in the tuberosity. b The implant socket is designed in a distal area with an approximately 5-mm safe distance. c Preoperative radiographic examination shows that the tip of the implant is embedded in the pterygoid fossa, and the implant neck is in the most distal position to ensure the integrity of the sinus

Type III, Pterygoid implants with maxillary sinus engagement.

When the maxillary tuberosity is severely atrophied and cannot guarantee complete coverage of the implant when maintaining a 5-mm safe distance, the implant must invade the maxillary sinus while reaching the pterygoid fossa.

In this situation, although the design of the implant path is unrestricted, at least 4 mm of vertical residual bone height around the implant neck should be retained, which would aid in guiding the drilling sequence, ensure transmission of the bite force, and prevent the occurrence of sinus fistula after implant failure. Thus, the implant neck can be moved medially to facilitate the prosthodontic phase (Fig. 5).

The fluid level in the sinus is noted. d Stable implant crestal bone level and no sinus complication observed at the 6-month follow-up

Discussion

With the rapid advancement of full-arch implant rehabilitation and the popularity of the graftless concept [9], the use of implants in the retromolar area of severely atrophied posterior maxillae is being considered a good treatment option to eliminate prosthodontic cantilevers for more predictable long-term results [10]. To assist in surgical design, many researchers have proposed classifications of implants in the retromolar area. In 1998, Reiser proposed a classification of the posterior maxillary implant based on anatomic location, including tuberosity and pyramidal and pterygoid processes. However, as it is difficult to distinguish the pyramidal and pterygoid processes in CBCT data, the significance for clinical practice is greatly reduced. Additionally, this classification does not consider the morphology of the maxillary sinus [11]. In 2016, Luis et al. proposed the classification of diagnostic prediction pterygomaxillary anatomical ridge and placement for implantology in the pterygomaxillary region. However, their classification places too much emphasis on the sinus while neglecting the tuberosity implant and provides little guidance for surgical implications [12].

The new classification we propose here summarizes all types of implants placed in the retromolar area and also fully considers the maxillary sinus. Furthermore, surgical techniques for each type of design are demonstrated to help reduce surgical complications.

Among the three types in the new classification, Type I implants are considered the first choice due to their simple surgical procedure and lower risk of surgical complications, as they are fully embedded in the tuberosity.

When the situation is not fit for Type I implants, Type II or III implants should be chosen based on the mass of tuberosity and the pneumatization of the maxillary sinus. The common ground of Type II and III implants is that they engage the pterygoid plate to achieve good primary stability. However, whether the implant tip should penetrate the pterygoid fossa remains disputed. When the implant tip penetrates the pterygoid fossa, the thickest cortical bone is engaged, resulting in the highest implant primary stability. However, damage to the medial pterygoid muscle could occur when using this approach; thus, further studies are warranted to evaluate muscle function following damage to the medial pterygoid muscle. Therefore, we recommend that the pterygoid fossa should not be penetrated when the bone density around the implant neck is acceptable to avoid muscle damage (Fig. 6).

Relationship between the tip of the pterygomaxillary implant and the pterygoid plate. a Tip of the pterygomaxillary implant penetrating the pterygoid plate to ensure best primary stability. b Tip of the pterygomaxillary implant not penetrating the pterygoid plate to avoid muscle damage

The critical difference between the Type II and III classifications is whether the pterygoid implant engages with the sinus. In the Type III classification, the implant should engage the sinus to reach the pterygoid plate, which will inevitably cause damage to the integrity of the Schneiderian membrane. Though it may lead to local bleeding, few studies have reported the side effects of sinus invasion on the long-term prognosis of pterygomaxillary implant placement. Based on our current clinical experience, Type III pterygomaxillary implants can also achieve an ideal success rate with no related complications. Nevertheless, similar studies on traditional or zygomatic implants that damaged the Schneiderian membrane during insertion have also demonstrated no adverse effects on sinus health or implant survival [13–20].

However, to minimize postoperative complications in patients who need to undergo Type III pterygomaxillary implant placement, the health status of the maxillary sinus and patency of the nasal sinus ostium complex should be thoroughly evaluated preoperatively. In case of pathological changes, such as large cysts in the maxillary sinus, severe hyperplasia of the maxillary sinus mucosa, acute maxillary sinus inflammation, or obstruction of the nasal sinus complex, specialized treatment or adjustment in the surgical plan should be considered before implantation to avoid the risk of acute or chronic maxillary sinus inflammation. In addition to the routine use of antibiotics pre- and postoperatively, oral corticosteroids and nasal drops containing ephedrine/pseudoephedrine and antihistamines for nasal drip should be administered postoperatively to control the edema and hematoma of the damaged maxillary sinus mucosa, ensure patency of the nasal sinus complex, maintain normal drainage of the maxillary sinus, and prevent the occurrence of acute maxillary sinusitis.

The present study had some limitations. First, this new classification for pterygomaxillary implants is based on a relatively small sample size from the Chinese population. Second, further research is warranted to evaluate the complications and long-term stability of Type III pterygomaxillary implants.

Conclusion

This new classification system provides treatment recommendations for implants placed in the maxillary retromolar regions. Of all patients with severely atrophic posterior maxillae, 96.1% were considered suitable for receiving pterygomaxillary implants. Type II pterygomaxillary implants were the most commonly used. Different types of pterygomaxillary implants should follow specific designs and surgical strategies to achieve optimal outcomes.
