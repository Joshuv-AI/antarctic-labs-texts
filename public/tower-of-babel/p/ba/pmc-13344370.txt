Parallel anterior cingulate cortex pathways underlie dissociable social and attentional outputs
Authors: Feidi Wang, Mingyue Chen, Mi Lyu, Ying Zhang, Huan Sun, Yu Lu, Dongqi Cui, Wenting Xu, Yixuan Lyu, Yifang Zhai, Jiao Han, Jun Li, Han Xu, Wenting Wang, Li Chen, Xiancang Ma, Yan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The neural mechanisms coordinating social behavior and attentional control are often disrupted together in individuals with neuropsychiatric disorders such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD); however, how distinct circuits within a shared brain region separately regulate these functions remains unclear. Here, we identified two functionally distinct subpopulations of glutamatergic neurons in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) that project to different downstream targets: the zona incerta (ZI) and the limbic sector of the thalamic reticular nucleus (lTRN). These two ACC subpopulations differentially mediate social interaction and attentional allocation. Deficits in both behaviors were observed in Shank3b mutant ASD model mice. Optogenetic activation of the ACC→ZI circuit rescued social impairments, whereas activation of the ACC→lTRN pathway restored attentional performance. Our findings reveal dissociable ACC subpopulations and their downstream circuits for social and attentional behaviors, providing circuit-level insights that may inform future research on modulating related symptoms in patients with psychiatric disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Social behavior and attention processing are two evolutionarily conserved functions that are essential for survival and reproduction (1, 2). These adaptive functions enable animals to navigate their environments and facilitate interactions that are crucial for forming social bonds, cooperating, and responding to various stimuli immediately. Accumulating evidence has indicated that a large percentage of children with autism spectrum disorders (ASDs), a group of highly prevalent neurodevelopmental disorders characterized by substantial social deficits, also exhibit comorbid deficiencies in attentional processing (3, 4). These findings suggest a shared modulation of social and attentional control within the brain. Although the neural networks underlying each function individually have been mapped in previous studies (5–7), little concrete evidence is available on how higher brain centers exert concerted control to tune social interactions and attentional control.

The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) is considered one of the principal regions that generates top-down signals, integrates inputs from various brain regions, and transfers information to a wide array of subcortical regions. These diverse outputs endow the ACC with highly heterogeneous functions, including attentional control, sociability, pain regulation, and anxiety (8–11). Previous studies in humans and primates have demonstrated the critical roles of the ACC in social behavior (12–14) and attention processing (15, 16), findings that are consistent with the results of subsequent research on modulating ACC activity in rodents (9, 10, 17). Although the roles of the ACC in regulating social behavior and attention have been preliminarily identified (10, 18), whether distinct or overlapping representations in the ACC respond to social stimuli and attentional signals remains unknown. Furthermore, the organizational dynamics of information within the ACC and its transmission to downstream nuclei, specifically those involved in social behavior and attentional control, need to be determined.

In this study, we systematically elucidated how distinct neuronal subpopulations within the ACC organize and transmit top-down information to mediate social and attentional behaviors. We also identified attentional and social deficits in Shank3b−/− mice, a well-established animal model of autism, and how the comorbid phenotypes could be ameliorated by targeting ACC pyramidal neurons.

RESULTS

ACCCaMKII neurons are activated in response to social investigation and attentional processing

We first asked whether the ACC is engaged in social behavior or attention by monitoring calcium activity at the population level in the ACC (fig. S1A). Given that a substantial portion of neurons in the frontal cortex are glutamatergic neurons (19), we injected an adeno-associated virus (AAV) carrying GCaMP6s, a genetically encoded calcium indicator, under the control of the calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) promoter into the ACC (Fig. 1, A and B). Using a fiber photometry system, we were able to record neural dynamics by measuring fluorescence signals in freely moving mice (Fig. 1C). We synchronously monitored the naturally occurring behaviors and calcium signals of the GCaMP mice after the introduction of the social stimulus (Fig. 1D). On the basis of previous studies (20–22), we defined nine types of social and nonsocial behaviors (fig. S1B). Frame-by-frame annotation of the behaviors revealed that social episodes accounted for 21.8 ± 1.9% of the total time engaged in behaviors (Fig. 1E). The calcium signals aligned with social episodes, including social interaction and retreat, exhibited significant alterations compared with calcium activity during ambulation. When the mice interacted with the social stimulus, the transient activation of ACC neurons was recorded; in contrast, an obvious decrease in ACC activity was detected upon retreat (Fig. 1, F to H). In addition, the ACC neural activity did not show significant alterations across the preonset time window of these conditions (fig. S1C). The calcium activity of ACC neuronal populations during other nonsocial behaviors—such as digging, rearing, and self-grooming—did not show a significant difference compared to ambulation (fig. S1, D to F). Therefore, ACC activity was specifically responsive to interactions with conspecifics during the social interaction test (SIT).

Next, we examined the dynamic pattern of activity in the ACC during attentional processing. We used the five-choice serial reaction time task (5-CSRTT), a widely used behavioral paradigm with high construct validity for accessing sustained attention (23, 24) (Fig. 1I). In the task, water-restricted mice were trained to allocate attention by selecting the poking hole in which a visual stimulus was presented. The animals underwent a six-step training schedule, with each phase defined by specific criteria to ensure full mastery of the task. Upon meeting the criteria, the mice underwent the attentional test, with performance divided into correct responses, incorrect responses, premature responses, and omissions. Correct/incorrect responses were annotated on the basis of the selection of the correct/incorrect poking hole, premature responses reflected poking before the visual cue was presented, and omissions represented failures to poke within a defined time span. During the test, the mice voluntarily concentrated their attention both before the cue light was illuminated [intertrial interval (ITI) phase] and when the cue light was illuminated (cue phase), which enabled them to swiftly make accurate choices. The neural activity of ACC neurons was assessed throughout the attention sessions by detecting the expression of the calcium indicator GCaMP6s. We observed transiently increased calcium signals corresponding to both the ITI (Fig. 1, J to L) and the cue (Fig. 1, M to O) phases. Notably, this transiently increased calcium signal in ACCCaMKII neurons was detected only in trials where the mice poked correctly (scored as a correct response) and not in those with incorrect responses or omissions (scored as error responses). The incorrect responses and omissions were together scored as errors, and these two errors are both thought to indicate disturbances in attentional processing (25, 26). Here, not all mice exhibited incorrect trials; therefore, in a subset of animals, the average error trace represented solely omissions, whereas in the remaining animals, it included both omissions and incorrect responses. In addition, no significant changes were detected in the population activity of ACCCaMKII neurons preceding the ITI phase in either correct or error conditions (fig. S1G). Together, these results suggest the essential role of the ACC in allocating attention.

Overall, these results demonstrate that the activation of ACC glutamatergic neurons is elicited during both social and attentional encounters, positioning the ACC as a potential top-down control hub that mediates both social and attentional behaviors.

ACCCaMKII neurons are required for both social and attentional behaviors

We subsequently sought to determine whether the increased activity of ACC pyramidal neurons during social interaction and attentional allocation is necessary for these behaviors. We performed optogenetic inhibition to counteract the activation of ACC pyramidal neurons (fig. S2A). This inhibition was achieved by expressing halorhodopsin (NpHR), a photosensitive ion channel, on the cell membrane via the delivery of AAV2/9-CaMKII-NpHR-EYFP (enhanced yellow fluorescent protein) into the ACC, while AAV2/9-CaMKII-EYFP was used as a negative control. Optical fibers were implanted aboved the ACC and transmitted yellow light, which triggered neural hyperpolarization via chloride ion influx (Fig. 2A and fig. S2, B and C).

We first investigated whether sociability was inhibited following yellow light stimulation in male mice. The three-chamber test (TCT), a widely accepted behavioral test in which the sniffing duration around an empty cup and a cup containing a novel mouse is used as an indicator of internal readiness to engage with the social stimulus, while mice are prevented from engaging in physical contact to avoid confounding factors, was performed and sociability was measured (27–29). The NpHR mice receiving yellow light stimulation during the sociability stage (stage 2, Fig. 2B) exhibited less sniffing of the cup containing the mouse (Fig. 2, C and D). Consistently, the inhibition of ACCCaMKII neurons resulted in a notable decrease in the discrimination index in stage 2 (Fig. 2E). These results indicate that these neurons are required for sociability. In addition, the optogenetic inhibition of CaMKII+ neurons in the ACC (ACCCaMKII neurons) significantly attenuated the total time spent sniffing the social stimulus in the SIT, which was conducted in a more natural environment (Fig. 2F), indicating an impairment in general social behaviors. We further investigated whether ACCCaMKII neurons are required for social behavior in females. Consistent with the findings mentioned above, female mice with ACC inhibition showed a significant decrease in social behavior, both in the TCT (Fig. 2, C to E) and SIT (Fig. 2F). Moreover, we conducted a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with sex and manipulation as factors on these combined datasets, and the analysis did not reveal a significant sex bias in the ability of ACCCaMKII neurons to regulate social behavior (table S2). Together, these results indicate that mice no longer have a high willingness to interact with other conspecifics after the inhibition of ACCCaMKII neurons.

Given the increased calcium activity in ACC neurons during the 5-CSRTT, we next tested whether these neurons contribute to attentional processing. The male mice with NpHR expression in ACCCaMKII neurons were well trained to execute the 30-min task, which consisted of three 10-min epochs, including an initial light-off (OFF) epoch, followed by a light-on (ON) epoch with continuous 589-nm yellow laser stimulation, and concluded with a return to another OFF epoch. The average performance indices in the first OFF and last OFF epochs were calculated to represent the baseline attentional levels. We observed that continuous yellow light application throughout the session led to decreases in both the number of correct responses (Fig. 2G) and the percentage of accurate responses (Fig. 2H). The number of correct responses reflects the absolute number of successfully completed trials, whereas the percentage of accurate responses represents the overall task efficiency and is calculated on the basis of all trial outcomes, including correct, incorrect, omission, and premature responses. This observation suggested the elicitation of attentional impairment, while this light-induced inhibition had no effect on the number of omissions (fig. S2D). Notably, the percentage of premature responses was significantly increased (Fig. 2I), consistent with the established role of the ACC in impulse control (30–32). While prior studies have begun to delineate phase-specific contributions of the ACC-visual cortex (VIS) circuit to attention (33–35), the precise temporal dynamics of ACC engagement during attention tasks in freely moving subjects remains less defined. We further aimed to determine more specifically the attentional time windows in which the ACC was involved. The pptogenetic inhibition of ACC neurons event-locked to either the ITI phase (Fig. 2, J to L, and fig. S2E) or the cue phase (Fig. 2, M to O, and fig. S2F) elicited attentional deficits similar to those observed in the aforementioned continuous inhibition experiment, indicating a general engagement of the ACC in attentional processing. Moreover, performance accuracy was also impaired in female mice upon the inhibition of ACC neurons (Fig. 2, G to O, and fig. S2, D to F). Notably, a significant sex difference was detected for impulsivity (Fig. 2I and table S2; P = 0.0389), alongside trending effects for attention behavior (Fig. 2H and table S2; P = 0.0519) and impulsivity (Fig. 2L and table S2; P = 0.0897), indicating that the extent to which inhibiting ACCCaMKII neurons activity affected these behaviors may differ between male and female mice, although this modulation influenced both sexes.

Considering the complexity of both social behaviors and attentional processing, which are theoretically influenced by other basal behaviors, we tested whether ACCCaMKII neuron suppression affects other basal behaviors. Similar levels of locomotor activity (fig. S3, A to C), novelty-seeking behavior (fig. S3D), repetitive self-grooming behavior (fig. S3E), object memory (fig. S3, F and G), and anxiety level (fig. S3, H to K) following ACC inhibition ruled out possible alterations in attention and sociability via changes in other basal neural functions. Collectively, these data indicate that ACCCaMKII neurons contribute directly to both social interactions with conspecifics and attention.

Social encounters and attention allocation are assigned to distinct ACC neuronal subpopulations

The ACC serves as a top-control hub, integrating information and innervating functional axon terminals across an array of cortical and subcortical regions (36), thereby orchestrating numerous functions (37, 38). We therefore sought to identify the circuit mechanisms through which subpopulations of neurons in the ACC control social and attentional behaviors. We mapped a range of downstream nuclei of the ACC by injecting an AAV carrying enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) (fig. S4A), which in turn labeled the somas and axons of CaMKII+ neurons (fig. S4, B and C) after anterograde virus expression, to obtain a broader understanding of the neural circuitry underlying these behaviors. We further investigated the target outputs of ACC neurons transmitting either social or attentional signals in these candidate regions in an activity-dependent manner. The cells engaged in social interactions or attentional processes were selectively labeled with mCherry, the expression of which was induced by the administration of 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) in TRAP2 mice following the delivery of AAV2/9-DIO-mCherry into the ACC and subsequent exposure to social or attentional environments. The activation scores, which were calculated from the fluorescence intensities of the labeled and control groups in the candidate regions, indicated that the zona incerta (ZI) received dense projections from ACC neurons associated with social behaviors (Fig. 3, A to C). Mapping the projections of the activated cells during the attentional task also revealed that the limbic sector of the thalamic reticular nucleus (lTRN) but not the sensory sector of the TRN showed an increased proportion of mCherry+ axons (Fig. 3, D to F).

The next question is whether distinct neural ensembles project into the ZI and the lTRN or whether the same population sends axons into these two regions (Fig. 3G). We addressed this question by identifying ZI- and lTRN-projecting ACC neurons through the infusion of cholera toxin subunit B (CTB) conjugated with Alexa Fluor 555 (CTB 555) and 488 (CTB 488) into both ventral and dorsal sectors of the ZI and the lTRN (Fig. 3, H and I, and fig. S5, A to D). Strikingly, the distribution profiles of ACC neurons projecting to the ZI and lTRN displayed only a small percentage of overlap (1.61 ± 0.30%; Fig. 3, J and K), suggesting the presence of dissociable circuit mechanisms underlying ACC-mediated social and attentional behaviors.

ACCZI and ACClTRN neurons exhibit distinct electrophysiological and morphological features

We then aimed to identify the electrophysiological, morphological, and molecular characteristics of ZI-projecting ACC neurons (ACCZI neurons) and lTRN-projecting ACC neurons (ACClTRN neurons). We performed whole-cell recordings of ACC neurons projecting to the ZI and lTRN retrogradely labeled with AAV carrying either mCherry or EGFP in both male and female mice (Fig. 4A). Although the resting membrane potential (RMP) of the subpopulations of ACC neurons was similar (Fig. 4B), the ACClTRN neurons presented a lower rheobase (Fig. 4C) and higher membrane resistance and spike counts in response to depolarized currents (Fig. 4, D and E). In addition, optogenetically evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (oEPSCs) recorded from ZI neurons and lTRN neurons, which were elicited by optical stimulation of the axonal terminals originating from ACC neurons in both male and female mice, were substantially blocked by the bath application of 2,3-dihydroxy-6-nitro-7-sulfamoylbenzo(f)quinoxaline (NBQX) and D-(-)-2-Amino-5-phosphonovaleric acid (APV) (fig. S6, A to C), suggesting functional control of the ZI and lTRN neurons receiving glutamatergic inputs from the ACC. Together, these results suggest that compared with those projecting to the ZI, ACC neurons projecting to the lTRN are intrinsically more excited. We also reconstructed the biocytin-filled cells in both male and female mice after whole-cell recordings (Fig. 4F). Compared with ACClTRN neurons, ACCZI neurons exhibited more complex dendritic arborizations (Fig. 4G), longer total dendritic lengths (Fig. 4, H and I), greater numbers of total intersections, comparable dendritic branch depths (fig. S6, D to G) and larger soma volumes (fig. S6H). These results suggest morphological differences between these neuronal subpopulations.

We subsequently assessed the transcriptional profiles of ACCZI and ACClTRN neurons by performing fluorescence-activated cell sorting and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) (Fig. 4J). A comparison of the expression profiles of these two distinct neural ensembles revealed a total of 142 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 4K and table S3). Specifically, numerous genes associated with either attention or social behavior—such as Ctnnd2, Nrxn1, Trim9, Nrcam, Nr4a3, and Il1b—were identified among the DEGs (Fig. 4L). In addition, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) annotations of the DEGs revealed significant differences in the expression of genes associated with several pathways between ACCZI neurons and ACClTRN neurons (Fig. 4M). Together, these results indicate that ACCZI and ACClTRN neurons are two distinct neuronal subpopulations associated with social behavior and attention processing, respectively.

The ACCCaMKII→ZI circuit modulates social behaviors

Thus far, our findings indicate that social behavior–associated neurons predominantly project to the ZI. However, these observations alone are insufficient to conclusively demonstrate that axon terminals in the ZI originating from the ACC are required for social behaviors. Similarly, experiments labeling attention-related neurons are subject to analogous limitations in the ACC→lTRN circuit. Therefore, we determined the function of ACC neuron axon terminals in the ZI and lTRN more precisely by optogenetically inhibiting these ACC circuits after the injection of AAV harboring the CaMKII promoter and NpHR in the ACC and the implantation of optical fibers above either the ZI or the lTRN (Fig. 5A). Post hoc histological validation (Fig. 5, B and C, and fig. S7, A to D) and whole-cell recordings revealed the abolishment of action potentials in response to yellow light stimulation (Fig. 5D), confirming the efficacy of this approach. In the TCT, the inhibition of the ACCCaMKII→ZI circuit induced a significant decrease in sniffing of the cup containing the mouse (Fig. 5, E and F) and the discrimination index of the cup containing the mouse and the empty cup (Fig. 5G). Consistent with the findings in the TCT, mice in which the ACCCaMKII→ZI circuit was inhibited spent less time interacting with the stimulus mouse in the SIT (Fig. 5H). In parallel, similar experiments on the ACCCaMKII→lTRN circuit were conducted. We did not observe significant alterations in behavior in the TCT (Fig. 5, I to K) or SIT (Fig. 5L) following yellow light stimulation, suggesting that synaptic transmission between the ACC and the lTRN is not indispensable for social behavior. As a control, AAV2/9-CaMKII-EYFP was injected into the ACC of another mouse cohort, with optical fibers being implanted above the ZI (fig. S8A). These EYFP mice spent a similar amount of time sniffing the mouse cage (fig. S8, B and C) and showed a comparable preference for it, regardless of whether the laser was on or off (fig. S8D). Similarly, laser stimulation did not affect the sniffing duration of EYFP mice in the SIT (fig. S8E).

In addition, we assessed the locomotor activity (fig. S9, A to C), novelty-seeking behavior (fig. S9D), repetitive grooming behavior (fig. S9E), object memory (fig. S9, F and G), and anxiety level (fig. S9, H to K) of the mice and found that these behaviors remained comparable after the ACCCaMKII→ZI circuit was inhibited. Therefore, these results indicate a regulatory role of the ACCCaMKII→ZI circuit, but not the ACCCaMKII→lTRN circuit, in specifically shaping social behaviors.

The ACCCaMKII→lTRN circuit modulates attentional processing

We injected ACC2/9-CaMKII-NpHR-EYFP into the ACC and implanted optical fibers above either the ZI or the lTRN to determine whether either of the two circuits described above drives attention-related information processing (fig. S10A). Afterward, the NpHR mice were subjected to the 5-CSRTT. In accordance with the above behavioral results obtained following ACC inhibition, when yellow light was applied to the ACC axon terminals in the lTRN, these mice presented decreases in both the number of correct responses and the percentage of accurate responses of hole poking (Fig. 6, A to C), suggesting a compromised ability to process attentional cues. Moreover, mice with ACCCaMKII→lTRN circuit inhibition attempted to poke the holes prior to the onset of the light cue (Fig. 6D), reflecting heightened impulsivity upon exposure to visual cues. As a method to further estimate the temporal dynamics of attention-related information processing, we shortened the photostimulation window to target either the ITI or the cue phase specifically. Yellow light application during the ITI resulted in significant decreases in the numbers of correct responses and accurate responses (Fig. 6, E to G), suggesting that the ACCCaMKII→lTRN circuit plays a role in attentional allocation. In addition, the inhibition of this circuit during the ITI promoted premature responses (Fig. 6H). Consistently, when laser stimulation was applied specifically during the cue delivery phase, the correct responses and accuracy of these mice were significantly decreased (Fig. 6, I to K), coupled with an increase in premature responses (Fig. 6L). We conducted similar experiments in EYFP mice to exclude potential confounding effects of laser stimulation. No significant changes were observed in the number of correct responses, percentage of accurate responses, or number of premature responses (fig. S10, B to M). Conversely, the subject mice exhibited normal attentional and impulsive levels upon the inhibition of the ACCCaMKII→ZI circuit, irrespective of whether optogenetic modulation was applied throughout the trial (Fig. 6, M to P) or only in one specific phase of attentional processing (Fig. 6, Q to X), excluding a role for the ACCCaMKII→ZI circuit in shaping attention processing. Together, these results suggest that the ACCCaMKII→lTRN circuit exerts a nonphase-specific regulatory effect on both attention and impulsivity.

Moreover, locomotor activity (fig. S11, A to C), novelty-seeking behavior (fig. S11D), repetitive grooming behavior (fig. S11E), object memory (fig. S11, F and G), and anxiety level (fig. Together, these results underscore the exclusive role of the ACCCaMKII→lTRN circuit in mediating attentional function, with no detectable contribution from the ACCCaMKII→ZI circuit.

Shank3 mutant mice exhibit comorbid behavioral deficits and impaired ACC synaptic connections

Shank3 encodes a scaffolding protein capable of mediating postsynaptic signaling at excitatory synapses and has also been implicated in several highly heritable neuropsychiatric disorders, such as ASDs (10, 39–41), schizophrenia (42), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (43, 44), and bipolar disorder (43, 45). Genetic deletion/mutation studies have revealed that a deficiency in the Shank3b gene results in autistic-like behaviors, such as social interaction abnormalities and repetitive behaviors (39). Considering that attentional impairments are observed in children with ASDs, we sought to evaluate the attentional capabilities of Shank3b−/− mice. Through the TCT and SIT, we confirmed the social deficits in Shank3b−/− mice (Fig. 7, A to D). We subsequently performed the 5-CSRTT to investigate the attention levels of these mice. Notably, during the initial 10 min of the task, no significant difference in the percentage of accurate responses was observed between the WT and Shank3b−/− mice. However, as time elapsed, the prolonged duration of the task led to an increasing attentional load. Ten minutes after the task began, this heightened attentional demand became evident, and the Shank3b−/− mice exhibited a lower percentage of accurate responses, suggesting impaired attentional maintenance (Fig. 7, E to G). Consistent with the findings observed after ACC inhibition, the Shank3b−/− mice also exhibited a significant increase in premature choices during this time window (Fig. 7H). These data provide an evolutionarily conserved insight into the cooccurrence of social interaction deficits and attentional control impairments, suggesting that Shank3b−/− mice can serve as viable model animals for investigating the mechanisms underlying comorbid social and attentional deficits.

Given that the ACC drives the comodulation of social and attentional behaviors, we recorded the calcium signals of neuronal activity in the ACC when Shank3b−/− mice interacted with other conspecifics or concentrated on visual cues in the 5-CSRTT. Consistent with our findings described above, a robust increase in calcium activity within the ACC was elicited when WT mice encountered conspecifics. However, the activity of the ACC in Shank3b−/− mice following social encounters was lower than that in WT mice (fig. S12, A to C). The calcium activity of the ACC neuronal population in Shank3b−/− mice during the 5-CSRTT was lower than that in the WT group, regardless of whether the mice were under a heavy attentional load (ITI phase; fig. S12, D to F) or received stimulus cues (cue phase; fig. S12, G to I). These results suggest a low activity state of ACC pyramidal neurons in Shank3b−/− mice during social and attentional behaviors.

Next, we investigated the functional implications of Shank3 deficiency for synaptic transmission within ACClTRN and ACCZI neurons. Retrograde AAV carrying mCherry/EGFP reporters was injected into the ZI and the lTRN of WT and Shank3b−/− mice of both sexes to specifically label these neuronal subpopulations. Spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) were significantly attenuated in both ACCZI (Fig. 7, I and J) and ACClTRN (Fig. 7, K and L) neurons in male and female Shank3b−/− mice, suggesting a decrease in excitatory synaptic inputs to these populations. Furthermore, we investigated the strength of connectivity in the ACCCaMKII→lTRN circuit and ACCCaMKII→ZI circuit in Shank3b−/− mice using whole-cell recordings. We injected an AAV delivering channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) under the control of the CaMKII promoter into the ACC of both male and female mice to specifically target glutamatergic neurons (Fig. 7M). We then recorded the synaptic responses of lTRN/ZI neurons to 3-ms 473-nm light pulses. The photostimulation of ACC-lTRN inputs elicited oEPSCs in 32 of 50 recorded ZI neurons in WT mice and 40 of 57 recorded cells in Shank3b−/− mice (Fig. 7N). Notably, 20 of the 32 recorded lTRN neurons in WT mice responded to photostimulation, whereas only 20 of the 59 recorded lTRN neurons in Shank3b−/− mice exhibited oEPSCs (Fig. 7O), suggesting significantly weakened synaptic connectivity between the ACC and lTRN (χ2 = 5.08, P = 0.02) but not between the ACC and ZI (χ2 = 0.46, P = 0.50) in the Shank3b mutant mice. In contrast, measurements of the paired-pulse ratio (PPR) revealed that ZI neurons (Fig. 7, P and Q), but not lTRN neurons (Fig. 7, R and S), in Shank3b−/− mice exhibited a greater PPR for oEPSCs than ZI neurons in WT mice did. Therefore, the ACC→ZI and ACC→lTRN circuits exhibited deficiencies in different aspects of connectivity strength.

We reconstructed biocytin-filled ACCZI and ACClTRN neurons in both male and female mice after the electrophysiological experiments to further characterize the potential morphological alterations underlying this functional deficiency. We assessed morphological alterations in spine maturation by calculating the total spine density and mushroom spine density based on the general understanding that mushroom spines represent a more mature status. With respect to the morphology of ACCZI neurons, both the total spine density and the mushroom spine density of both male and female Shank3b−/− mice were significantly sparser (Fig. 7, T to V), and similar results were obtained for ACClTRN neurons (Fig. 7, W to Y). These results revealed the morphological basis underlying the synaptic transmission deficiencies in Shank3b−/− mice.

Overall, we found that Shank3b−/− mice exhibit aberrations not only in social behavior but also in attentional control that are potentially driven by weakened synaptic connections from the ACC to the ZI and to the lTRN.

Targeting diverse ACC circuits to rescue hyposociability and inattention in Shank3b−/− mice

We subsequently hypothesized that activating the ACCCaMKII→ZI circuit and ACCCaMKII→lTRN circuit could strengthen these pathways and potentially reverse the social interaction deficits and attention impairments observed in Shank3b−/− mice, respectively. We bilaterally injected CaMKII-ChR2 viral constructs into the ACC of Shank3b−/− mice and implanted optical fibers in either the ZI or the lTRN to test this hypothesis (Fig. 8, A to C, and fig. S13, A and B). The delivery of 473-nm stimulation elicited reliable action potentials in ACC neurons (Fig. 8D). Shank3b−/− mice exposed to blue light illumination of the axon terminals in the ZI originating from ACC neurons tended to interact significantly more with the stimulus mouse in both the TCT (Fig. 8, E to G) and the SIT (Fig. 8H), suggesting the rescue of social behaviors. We subsequently investigated whether activating the ACCCaMKII→lTRN circuit within the time window during which Shank3b−/− mice displayed attentional deficits could reverse this impairment. Shank3b−/− mice in which the ACCCaMKII → lTRN circuit was activated presented substantial improvements in correct number and accuracy probabilities, regardless of whether blue light illumination was applied throughout the trial (Fig. 8, I to L) or event-locked to the ITI phase (Fig. 8, M to P) and cue phase (Fig. 8, Q to T). These findings further underscore the therapeutic potential of activating the ACCCaMKII→lTRN circuit to reverse attentional impairments. Collectively, these findings reveal a potential intervention strategy within specific circuits for treating neuropsychiatric disorders characterized by cooccurring social deficits and inattention, further highlighting the critical role of the ACC and its associated circuits in these behavioral processes.

DISCUSSION

Both social behavioral and attention processing deficits are frequently observed in individuals with ASD. Furthermore, patients with a range of psychiatric disorders—such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and obsessive–compulsive disorder—also exhibit deficiencies in these two behaviors (3, 4, 46–51). The current understanding of the mechanisms governing these two behaviors is hindered by the lack of knowledge of the circuit-specific roles of ACC neuronal subpopulations. In this study, we identified two dissociated neuronal subpopulations in the ACC with distinct electrophysiological properties, morphological features, molecular profiles, and projection patterns that coordinate attention and social behaviors. Moreover, these ACC projections, which synapse on the lTRN and ZI, are differentially engaged in attention and social behaviors and are selectively effective at rescuing comorbid symptoms in ASD mice (fig. S14). These findings suggest a top-down processing mechanism that contributes to the comodulation of social and attention behaviors, which could guide future investigations into potential therapeutic strategies for neuropsychiatric disorders associated with these impairments.

Although some studies have provided insights into social and attentional neural networks, to our knowledge, little is known about the extent to which and how these two behaviors are orchestrated within a single region. Previous studies have reported a role for the ACC as a higher brain center in attentional control (17, 52, 53). Building on this foundation, we further pinpointed the specific temporal window during attentional processing in which the ACC is actively engaged. The population activity of ACC neurons markedly increased during the ITI phase of the trials in which the mouse prepares to choose the correct hole, suggesting early, transient recruitment of ACC neurons to augment subsequent attentional processing. Consistent with the present findings, previous studies on ACC→VIS neurons also demonstrated that the suppression of this ACC subpopulation throughout 5-CSRTT sessions or specifically during the late ITI phase induced attentional impairments (33–35). The calcium signal further increased upon the appearance of a visual cue following a correct choice but not an incorrect choice, indicating that ACC activity is associated with internal attention levels rather than stimulus perception. The neural activity pattern in the ACC during attentional tasks differs significantly from that in other brain regions. For example, parvalbumin-positive neurons in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) are significantly activated during both the ITI (25) and the cue phases of correct trials (54), whereas the neural activity of the nucleus accumbens (NAc) in correct trials is greater than that in incorrect trials but suddenly switches to a lower level upon cue presentation (55). Thus, the modulation of different brain regions has distinct effects on various aspects of attentional processing. The present findings support a regulatory role for the ACC in conveying general attentional signals throughout the attention process. Our data also indicate that the ACC is required for social interaction, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies exploring other aspects of social behaviors, including social decision-making (56), social transfer of pain and fear (57), and helping behavior (58).

The ACC is involved in a large circuit that projects to and receives functional afferents from numerous cortical and subcortical regions (36, 59). Therefore, the involvement of the ACC may increase the flexibility of various behaviors. Although the outputs of the ACC have been extensively studied (60), the precise downstream targets that convey social and attentional information remain unidentified. We identified ACC glutamatergic synapses on lTRN and ZI neurons as key mediators of attention and social behaviors, respectively. In general, the ACC can integrate information to form specific social or attentional signals, as evidenced by the distinct calcium increases observed during social investigations or highly attentive situations. The lTRN has long been implicated as a “searchlight” of attention (61) and is activated when primates pay attention to various stimuli (62, 63). Since we observed that the ACC sends excitatory projections into the lTRN, the ACC may serve as one of the main sources triggering lTRN activation through top-down control. Notably, the suppression of the ACC→lTRN circuit impaired attentional processing but did not affect social behaviors, suggesting that this circuit contributes more to top-down (goal-directed) sustained attention (64) rather than social context-driven bottom-up (stimulus-driven) attention (65). Through activity-dependent anterograde tracing, we determined that the ZI served as a strong candidate for receiving projections from social behavior-associated ACC neurons. The ZI was identified as a key node regulating various forms of social behaviors, such as infant social behaviors (66) and hunting behaviors (67, 68), and the mPFC→ZI circuit was indicated in social investigation behavior (69). In addition, other nonsocial emotional behaviors, such as anxiety and fear, are also modulated by the ZI and its related circuits (70–72). Our findings that optogenetic modulation of the ACCCaMKII→ZI circuit significantly affects social behavior provide evidence indicating how the ZI participates in the social neural network.

The top-down mechanism of the ACC that underlies the comodulation of social behavior and attentional control represents a potential therapeutic target for various neuropsychiatric disorders, including ASDs, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder, as social and attentional dysfunctions are extremely prevalent in individuals with these diseases (3, 4, 46, 73, 74). Moreover, varying degrees of ACC structural or functional alterations in individuals with these diseases have been observed in clinical trials (75–77), further supporting the involvement of the ACC in the pathogenesis of these disorders. Notably, the present study is limited to basic science, and the translational implications remain to be determined. Future studies should further explore the potential clinical relevance and therapeutic value of the ACC and its related circuits.

The current understanding of the mechanisms governing social behavior and attention processing is hindered by the lack of knowledge of model mice that exhibit deficits in both of these behaviors. Notably, we found that Shank3b−/− mice, a well-known ASD mouse model, exhibit behavioral impairments not only in social interaction but also in attentional control, along with decreased synaptic connections of the ACCCaMKII→ZI and ACCCaMKII→lTRN circuits. In addition, BTBR mice, which are considered an ASD model, also exhibit concomitant social and attentional deficits (78, 79). The present findings and previous clinical imaging evidence (80, 81) suggest an evolutionarily conserved role for the ACC in concurrently mediating these behaviors. Furthermore, the activation of ACCCaMKII→lTRN and ACCCaMKII→ZI circuits was sufficient to rescue attentional and social deficits, respectively. These findings expand our understanding of ACC function and highlight the ACC as a potential treatment target rather than just a regulatory node.

Although the present findings support the role of the ACC in coordinating social and attentional processes, several limitations remain to be addressed in future studies. First, while the current study has validated the involvement of the ACC in regulating these behaviors in both male and female animals, it remains to be determined whether the ACC→ZI and ACC→lTRN circuits regulate social and attentional behaviors similarly in females, and whether ACC manipulation can rescue related deficits in female Shank3 mutant mice. Second, although evidence from ASD animal models points to the ACC as a potential therapeutic target, future clinical studies will be necessary to evaluate the translational value of ACC-targeted interventions for mental disorders, particularly ASD.

In summary, we identified two distinct neural ensembles in the ACC that display significant heterogeneity in electrophysiological, morphological, and molecular characteristics and mediate social behaviors and attentional control through projections to the ZI and lTRN, respectively. Our study provides a previously unknown neural framework for social and attention, underscoring the ACC as a potential therapeutic target in individuals with neuropsychiatric disorders who present with these cooccurring impairments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

The mice used in this study included C57BL/6J (Charles River Laboratories, stock no. 219), TRAP2 (a gift from C. Xu’s laboratory), and Shank3b−/− (a gift from G. Feng’s laboratory) mice. Heterozygous gene knockout mice were bred to obtain homozygous mice. The mice were weaned and housed separately after identification for genotyping at P21. Most of the experiments were performed using 8- to 12-week-old male mice, with the following exceptions: ACC neuron–targeted optogenetic inhibition experiments (Fig. 2, A to O, and fig. S2, D to F), electrophysiological recordings, and postrecording biocytin staining (Figs. 4, A to I and 7, I to Y, and fig. S6, A to H), where both 8- to 12-week-old male and female rodents were included, and each sex accounted for ~40 to 60% of the entire dataset. All the animals were chosen randomly for all the experiments and housed in the same laboratory environment with groups of two to five mice per cage (12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle, lights on at 08:00, 22° ± 1°C, 40 to 60% humidity, and ad libitum access to food). For the 5-CSRTT behavioral training, the water consumption and physical condition of the mice were strictly monitored, and supplemental water was provided if the weight of the mice dropped below 80% of their initial weight. For the animal cohorts, a between-subjects design was used across social and attention tasks.

All experimental procedures conformed to the principles of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals established by the National Institutes of Health and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University (approval ID: XJTUAE2023-2318).

Virus injection

The mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% for induction and 1.5% for maintenance), and the fully anesthetized mice were mounted on a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Life Science, China) at a controlled temperature maintained at 37°C until the surgical procedure was complete. The eyes of the mice were moistened with an eye ointment, and the surgical area was shaved and disinfected with iodine. The skin was removed with surgical scissors. For virus delivery, AAV was injected using a motorized stereotaxic injector and a Hamilton microsyringe coupled with a glass pipette. The rate was fixed to 50 nl/min for 1 min, followed immediately by a delay of 1 min. The glass pipette was left for 10 min and then withdrawn slowly after the injection. At each injection site, 150 nl of virus was injected. The injection coordinates were as follows [in mm; bregma anteroposterior (AP), mediolateral (ML), dorsoventral (DV) and injection angle from lateral to medial]: ACC (+0.98, ±0.72, −1.17, and 22°), lTRN (−0.70, ±1.42, −3.25, and 0°), and ZI (−1.82, ±1.50, −4.20, and 10°). After the virus injection, an optical fiber [0.37 numerical aperture (NA), optical density at 200 μm, purchased from Inper Co. Ltd., China] was fixed onto the targeted regions using dental cement for fiber photometry or optogenetics. The scalp was sutured if optical fiber implantation was not needed. AAV titers ranged from 2 × 1012 to 5 × 1012 viral genomes (vg) ml−1. Fiber photometry experiments, behavioral assays, ex vivo electrophysiological experiments, and histological analyses were performed after 3 to 4 weeks of recovery.

AAV2/9-CaMKIIa-GCaMP6s-WPRE-hGH polyadenylation (pA) (BrainVTA, PT-0110) was bilaterally injected into the ACC of C57BL/6J mice, and optical fibers were implanted above the injection site to monitor the calcium activity of ACCCaMKII neurons. AAV2/9-CaMKIIa-eNpHR3.0-EYFP-WPRE-hGH pA (BrainVTA, PT-0008) or AAV2/9-CaMKIIa-EYFP-WPRE-pA (BrainVTA, PT-0107) was bilaterally injected into the ACC, and the optical fibers were implanted 150 μm above the injection site to optogenetically inhibit the activity of ACCCaMKII neurons. For the optogenetic inhibition of the ACC→ZI and ACC→lTRN circuits, AAV2/9-CaMKIIa-eNpHR3.0-EYFP-WPRE-hGH pA (BrainVTA, PT-0008) or AAV2/9-CaMKIIa-EYFP-WPRE-pA (BrainVTA, PT-0107) was bilaterally injected into the ACC, and the optical fibers were implanted 150 μm above the ZI or lTRN. For anterograde tracing of ACCCaMKII neurons, AAV2/9-CaMKIIa-EGFP-WPRE-hGH pA (BrainVTA, PT-0959) was bilaterally injected into the ACC of C57BL/6J mice. AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyadenylate [poly(A)] (BrainVTA, PT-0115) was injected into the ACC of TRAP2 mice to detect activity-dependent ACC circuits. For retrograde tracing of the ZI and the lTRN, CTB 555 and CTB 488, respectively, were injected into these regions in C57BL/6J mice. For the electrophysiological recordings of ZI-projecting and lTRN-projecting ACC neurons, AAV2/retro-hSyn-mCherry-WPRE-hGH pA (BrainVTA, PT-0100) and AAV2/retro-hSyn-EGFP-WPRE-hGH pA (BrainVTA, PT-1990) were injected into the ZI and lTRN, respectively. For electrophysiological experiments investigating the connection properties, AAV-hSyn-hChR2 (H134R)-mCherry-WPRE-hGH poly(A) (BrainVTA, PT-0150) was injected into the ACC. For electrophysiological experiments in which the PPR and optogenetic activation of ACC neurons were recorded, AAV2/9-CaMKIIa-hChR2 (H134R)-mCherry-WPRE-hGH poly(A) (BrainVTA, PT-0297) or AAV2/9-CaMKIIa-mCherry-WPRE-hGH poly(A) (used as a negative control for optogenetic manipulation; BrainVTA, PT-0108) was injected into the ACC.

Fiber photometry

A multichannel optical fiber photometric system (Thinker Tech Nanjing Biotech Co. Ltd., China) was used to record the GCaMP6s fluorescence signal and analyze it at the desired time points. Specifically, the blue light in the 470-nm band and the isosbestic autofluorescence signal in the 405-nm band were emitted by the light-emitting diode (LED) light source and collimated into the dichroic color mirror (MD498, Thorlabs) with a bandpass filter (MF525-39, Thorlabs) via a ×10 objective lens (0.3 NA; Olympus) and connected to an optical fiber. The tip of the fiber transmitted light to a specific brain area at a power of 20 to 40 μW. The intensity of protein fluorescence in neurons is regulated by the calcium ion concentration. After bandpass filtering, the fluorescence signal was transmitted back to the detection light path through the same fiber and then transformed into a voltage signal by an amplifier for display and recording. The voltage signal was digitized at 100 Hz and recorded using a Power 1401 digitizer and Spike2 software (Cambridge Electronic Design, UK). A video camera mounted above the testing apparatus was used to record the tracks of the mice at the same time. For the SIT and 5-CSRTT, behavior-related calcium dynamics were recorded and analyzed artificially according to the type of behavior. The signals associated with interaction, retreat, allogrooming, digging, rearing, ambulation, self-grooming, scratching, and resting were recorded to investigate the changes in ACC activity during the SIT. Scratching, allogrooming, and resting behaviors were not included in further analyses because all subject mice did not engage in these behaviors. An interaction was described as the subject mouse approaching and sniffing the stimulus mouse within 2 cm, whereas retreat was defined as the subject mouse drawing back from the stimulus mouse and ending the social interaction. For attentional behaviors, we categorized trials into three types across different attentional states: correct trials (in which mice poked the illuminated hole and reflecting strong attentional allocation) and incorrect trials (in which mice poked an incorrect hole) and omissions (in which mice did not respond to the light cue). Given not all mice showed incorrect trials, we pooled the incorrect and omission trials together as the error trials to reflect weak attentional allocation as previous studies (25, 26). Thus, the average trace of error represents solely omissions in a subset of animals and omissions + incorrects in the remaining animals. Calcium signals in each category were quantified. For social behaviors, the peak ΔF/F during social and nonsocial behaviors was quantified using calcium signals recorded during ambulation as a control. The time zero was used by considering the onset of every type of behavior to calculate the values of transient calcium change (ΔF/F) as previously suggested (69). The raw signal was defined as Fraw and was, on average, the 3-s signal after the start of every type of behavior. The baseline signal was defined as Fbaseline and was on average the 2-s signal before the start of every type of behavior. The ΔF/F was calculated as follows: (Fraw − Fbaseline)/Fbaseline. The peak value during each trial was averaged to obtain the peak ΔF/F of each mouse.

Optogenetic manipulations

On the testing day a fiber optic patch cord with a protective tube was connected to the optical fiber during optical stimulation. The patch cord was connected to a 473- or 589-nm laser (Thinker Tech Nanjing Biotech Co. Ltd., China) with a ferrule connector/physical contact (FC/PC) adaptor. The optical inhibition of ACCCaMKII neurons consisted of sustained illumination with 589-nm light using an optical power output of 5 to 7 mW from the fiber tip. For the optical stimulation of ACCCaMKII neurons, constant 5 Hz, 5-ms pulses of 473-nm light with a 3-mW power output were applied. For optogenetic modulation in the TCT and SIT, the mice were randomly divided into two groups: one group underwent optogenetic stimulation first, followed by a light-off control experiment 3 days later; the other group underwent the experiment in the reverse order. For measurements of attention, the task consisted of three 10-min epochs, including an initial light-off (OFF) epoch, followed by a light-on (ON) epoch with 589-nm yellow laser stimulation, and concluded with a return to another OFF epoch.

Animal behavioral tests

Handling and habituation

The mice were transferred to the behavioral apparatus in the testing room for at least 30 min for three consecutive days before environmental habituation at the end of each day, followed by 2 min of handling. After 24 hours, the subject mice were transported to the testing room 1 hour before testing. All the behaviors were performed in testing rooms under normal lighting conditions (~100 lux) and isolated from sound. The tracks of the subject mice were recorded using a SMART v.3.0/I.O. video tracking system (Panlab Harvard Apparatus).

Three-chamber test

The opaque white three-chamber apparatus (60 cm long–by–40 cm wide–by–25 cm tall) was divided into three isometric chambers by two transparent walls (40 cm long–by–0.5 cm wide–by–25 cm tall), which held a 6-cm-diameter hole in the middle to enable the subject mice to shuttle freely between different chambers. Two identical mesh cups were placed at opposite corners of the apparatus. The stimulus mice were placed in the cups for 10 min each day for three consecutive days before habituation. The test was divided into three stages. For habituation, a subject mouse was placed in the middle compartment and allowed 10 min to freely explore the apparatus [stage 1 (S1)]. A previously unmet stimulus mouse was then placed into one of the cups randomly, with the location balanced between different subject mice. The two doors were then removed, and the subject mouse was allowed 10 min to explore the apparatus and interact with the empty cage (EC) or mouse cage (MC) for social contact (S2). Active sniffing was considered contact of the nose of the subject mouse within the 2-cm area surrounding the cup. The durations of grooming and climbing the cup wall were excluded. The discrimination index for sociability was calculated as followsDiscrimination index=DurationMC−DurationECDurationMC+DurationECwhere DurationMC denotes duration of sniffing the cage with a stimulus mouse and DurationEC denotes duration of sniffing the empty cage.

Social interaction test

The SIT was conducted in a clean cage with bedding. The subject mouse was first allowed to explore the home cage freely for 5 min for habituation. Afterward, a previously unmet stimulus mouse weighing 2 to 3 g less than the subject mouse was introduced into the cage, and their actions were recorded for 5 min. Active social behaviors were defined as the subject mouse sniffing or directly contacting the stimulus mouse within 2 cm and ending when the distance exceeded 2 cm.

Open field test

The test was performed in a white opaque box (50 cm long–by–50 cm wide–by–40 cm tall), and the subject mouse was placed in the center area and allowed to explore freely for 30 min. The center zone was defined as a central area of 30 cm–by–30 cm. The total distance traveled and the time spent in the center area were calculated.

Novelty-seeking behaviors

A white opaque box (50 cm long–by–50 cm wide–by–40 cm tall) was used in this experiment. A novel object with a specific geometric shape was fixed to the center of the arena. Then, the subject mice were free to investigate after being carefully placed in the center of the space, and the mice were free to investigate the objects. The time spent sniffing the new object within 2 cm in a 5-min period, but not climbing on or grooming near the object, was used to assess their novelty-seeking ability.

Grooming behaviors

The mice were habituated to the experimenter and a transparent case (40 cm long–by–20 cm wide–by–20 cm tall) at the same time for three consecutive days before the experiment, and the mice were allowed to explore freely for 20 min every day. On the testing day, mouse behavior was recorded for 20 min. The time spent grooming for the last 10 min of the test was calculated. Grooming behaviors were defined as licking; pawing the area around the nose, mouth, or face; and pawing the head or ears.

Novel object recognition test

The test was conducted in a white opaque box (50 cm long–by–50 cm wide–by–40 cm tall), which was separated into three 5-min training sessions and one 5-min test session with a 5-min interval subsequently. First, the subject mouse was placed in the center of the box and allowed to explore freely. Two objects with different appearances were then placed along the centerline of the box 10 cm away from the wall in opposite directions. Following three consecutive training sessions and the interval, during which mice were allowed to habituate fully to the set of familiar objects (FOs) with the position exchanged with each other at random, one of the objects was randomly replaced by another novel object (NO) in the last 5-min test session, and the position of replacement was counterbalanced across the tests. The duration of sniffing was defined as close sniffing or physical contact with the first object the FO or NO within 2 cm. The time spent grooming near the objects or climbing on the wall of the objects was excluded from the analysis. The discrimination index was calculated as followsDiscrimination index=DurationNO−DurationFODurationNO+DurationFOwhere DurationNO is the sniffing duration when exploring the NO and DurationFO is the sniffing duration when exploring the FO.

EPM test

The elevated plus maze (EPM) apparatus consisted of two open arms (25 cm long–by–5 cm wide), two closed arms (25 cm long–by–5 cm wide) and a central area (5 cm long–by–5 cm wide). The subject mouse was placed in the center area and allowed to explore for 15 min. The time spent and distance traveled in different arms were analyzed.

Five-choice serial reaction time task

The test was performed in a soundproof, ventilated cubicle housing a five-hole chamber (Panlab Harvard Apparatus). The mice were trained to develop a conditioned reflex between correct nose-poke responses to rewards (water pellets). Initially, the water intake of the mice was restricted, and the mice were monitored daily until their weight reached 85 to 90% of their baseline weight. The mice were subsequently provided with 2% lemonade for 10 min daily to maintain weight during training and testing. Before testing, the mice were subjected to two habituation stages and six training stages. In the first habituation stage, the mice underwent a 15-min session in a chamber with an illuminated house and a regularly dripping water dispenser. After drinking of the water droplets was reliably established, the mice passed to a second 30-min habituation stage with a similar light system but a different reward rule, requiring the mice to poke any hole to obtain water. After 50 nose pokes were completed, the mice moved to the training stage, where they needed to meet a passing standard in 30-min sessions to advance. In training stages 1 and 2, the mice needed at least 30 correct responses, whereas in stages 3 and 4, a minimum of 50 correct responses was required. The stimulus cue duration, ITI, and limited hold (LH) were set as previously recommended (24). In the fifth training stage, the stimulus duration was 2.5 s, with a 5-s LH, and the ITI was randomized to 3, 4, or 5 s. After achieving 50 correct responses, 80% accuracy, and less than 40% omission within 30 min, the mice progressed to the final stage, in which a 2-s stimulus duration, a 5-s LH, and a randomized ITI of 3, 4, or 5 s were maintained. In the sixth stage, testing was permitted only after the mice achieved stable performance, with 30 correct responses and 80% accuracy within 30 min.

Mouse responses were categorized as correct, incorrect, omission, or premature, as defined in previous trial sequences of the 5-CSRTT (24). A correct response was defined as a nose poke into the illuminated hole during the cue duration and the LH, which triggered the release of a single water droplet, whereas a nose poke into any other hole was defined as an incorrect response. Omission was deemed the failure to poke the nose into any hole within the cue duration and the LH. A premature response during the ITI occurred before the presentation of the cue stimulus. Correct nose pokes led to a reward with a drop of water, whereas incorrect, omission, and premature responses resulted in a 5-s time-out period of punishment of delivering no water and extinguishing the house light. For the fiber photometry experiment, the error trials were defined as the trials with incorrect responses and without responses (omissions), whereas correct trials were those with correct responses. For the optogenetic experiments, the light was delivered in the ITI phase (the time from the start of the trial to cue onset), the cue phase (one of the stimulated display holes with an internal LED light on), or the entire trial. The evaluation of attentional performance was based on the following parameters: the number of correct responses and the number of omission responses, which refer to the cumulative total number of correct and omitted responses, respectively. For the designated period, response percentages were calculated as the number of each response divided by the total number of correct, incorrect, omission, and premature responses (55, 82, 83).

TRAP labeling

Two weeks after the Cre-containing virus injection, the singly housed TRAP2 mice were habituated to intraperitoneal injections for 3 days before the test; then, subject mice were intraperitoneally injected with 4-OHT (50 mg kg−1; Sigma-Aldrich, #H6278) before performing the 5-CSRTT or SIT. After 3 weeks, the mice were euthanized. For the analysis of downstream fluorescence intensity, the total fluorescence intensity (IntDen) in different brain regions was measured using Fiji/ImageJ software. Immunofluorescence staining and image acquisition

For biocytin staining, brain sections were collected into 4% ice-cold paraformaldehyde (PFA) after whole-cell recordings. For other experiments validating virus-expressing sites, mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg kg−1) and transcardially perfused with saline followed by 4% PFA. Brains were postfixed for 24 hours with 4% PFA and then transferred to a 30% sucrose solution and incubated at 4°C until they sank. Subsequently, the brains were sectioned into 30-μm-thick sections. For biocytin staining, free-floating sections were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with streptavidin conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific, S32354) or Alexa Fluor 647 (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific, S32357) overnight at 4°C. For the validation of injection sites, brain sections were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and washed with PBS. Images were captured with a confocal microscope (A1-1024, Nikon, Japan). Cells were counted with ImageJ/Fiji software. The relative fluorescence intensity in different brain areas was normalized to the fluorescence intensity of the ACC.

Ex vivo electrophysiological recordings

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with ice-cold dissection solution containing 110 mM choline chloride, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 10 mM MgSO4, 25 mM NaHCO3, 11 mM d-(+)-glucose, 20 mM Hepes, 5 mM sodium ascorbate, and 3 mM sodium pyruvate saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 (pH 7.4, 308 to 312 mOsm). Coronal slices (300 μm; containing the target region) were cut using a vibratome (VT1200S, Leica) in oxygenated ice-cold dissection solution. The slices were subsequently removed to the recording artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) containing 126 mM NaCl, 26 mM NaHCO3, 10 mM d-(+)-glucose, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 1.2 mM MgCl2, and 2 mM CaCl2 saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 (pH 7.4, 305 to 315 mOsm) at room temperature. The cells were visualized with an Olympus microscope (BX51W1) equipped with infrared differential interference contrast optics. The recording protocols from our previous study were refined (84). The intracellular solution contained 130 mM k-gluconate, 20 mM KCl, 10 mM Hepes, 4 mM Mg–adenosine triphosphate, 0.3 mM Na–guanosine triphosphate, 10 mM disodium phosphocreatine, 0.2 mM EGTA, and 0.5% biocytin. Picrotoxin (100 μM) was added to the bath solution to record the EPSCs. We performed whole-cell recordings of sEPSCs in ACCZI and ACClTRN neurons with mCherry and EGFP expression after injecting a retrograde virus delivering mCherry to the ZI and EGFP to the lTRN, respectively. A voltage clamp was applied, and sEPSCs held at −70 mV were recorded for a total of 300 s. Current-clamp recordings were used to determine the intrinsic excitability of ACCZI and ACClTRN neurons. We recorded the voltage changes in the cells in response to a series of 400-ms current steps from 0 to 250 pA in 10-pA increments. The intrinsic properties of each cell—such as the rheobase, resting potential, and membrane resistance—were characterized simultaneously. For optogenetically oEPSC and PPR recordings, light pulses (473 nm, 3-ms pulse width) were delivered to stimulate ChR2-positive axon terminals originating from the ACC. Twenty sweeps were recorded and averaged for each recorded cell. NBQX (10 μM) and APV (50 μM) were added to the bath aCSF to assess the glutamatergic connections of the ACC circuits. The same optostimulation protocol as that applied in the behavioral tests was used to validate the functions of the halorhodopsin (NpHR) and ChR2 viruses. Whole-cell recordings were obtained using an Integrated Patch-Clamp Amplifier (Sutter Instrument, USA) controlled by Igor Pro 8 software (WaveMetrics, USA), filtered at 5 kHz and sampled at 10 kHz.

Morphological reconstruction

Imaris software (Oxford Instruments, version 9) was used to reconstruct the neuronal dendritic branches and dendritic spines. The total lengths of the basal and apical dendrites and the volume of the cell body were automatically calculated. For the Sholl analysis, spheres were constructed continuously from the center of the soma with increasing radii at increments of 5 μm. The number of intersections between each sphere and the dendrites was calculated for comparison. Segments located 100 μm from the soma were included to analyze the spine density on basal dendrites. For the analysis of apical dendrites, segments extending 100 μm from above the bifurcation node were selected. For dendritic spine identification and categorization, three-dimensional reconstructions were performed using Imaris software (Oxford Instruments, version 9), and the established criteria for spines and mushroom spines [a head/neck width ratio ≥ 1.5 (dh/dn ≥ 1.5)] were used (85). For protrusions longer than 5 μm, filopodia but not spines were considered. dh and dn reflect the head width and neck width, respectively.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Isolation of ACClTRN and ACCZI neurons

AAV2/retro-EF1a-DIO-EGFP (BrainVTA, PT-0795) and AAV2/retro-EF1a-DIO-mCherry (BrainVTA, PT-0013) were injected into the lTRN and ZI, respectively, of wild-type mice to differentiate the neurons in the ACC that project to the lTRN and ZI. After 2 weeks of EGFP and mCherry expression, the mice were euthanized, and the brains were quickly removed and submerged in ice-cold ACSF to separate the ACC subregion. Single-cell suspensions of ACC tissues were subsequently prepared using the Adult Brain Dissociation Kit, mouse and rat (RWD DHABE-10). Neurons were index-sorted into 96-well plates containing 3 μl of Smart-seq3 lysis buffer and 0.125 μM oligo(dT) primer overlaid with 4 μl of silicon oil (5 cSt; Sigma-Aldrich) using a CytoFLEX SRT FACS Melody sorter (BECKMAN COULTER).

scRNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

scRNA-seq libraries were generated using an early version of the Smart-seq3xpress protocol. Reverse transcription was performed in 4 μl using 0.75 μM Smart-seq3 template switching oligonucleotide (TSO). The preamplification of cDNA was performed in 10 μl using SeqAmp polymerase (Takara Bio) for 18 cycles. Subsequently, the amplified cDNA was diluted with 90 μl of H2O, and we used 1 μl for downstream tagmentation using an in-house Tn5 enzyme. Last, the indexed libraries from each 96-well plate were pooled, cleaned, and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X platform.

Data processing and analysis

zUMIs version 2.9.7d was used to process the raw FASTQ files and obtain clean reads. Reads were mapped with STAR version 2.7.10a using the mouse reference genome (mm10). We used the R package Seurat (v5.1.0) to conduct further analyses. First, low-quality cells, defined as those with more than 10% of the reads mapped to mitochondrial genes or fewer than 1000 detected genes, were removed. The data were normalized using the “LogNormalize” function, and the top 2000 variable features were selected. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to identify group DEGs, with a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P value threshold of 0.05 and a cutoff of |log2FC| > 0.5. KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on filtered DEGs using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.12.6), and pathways with P values < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Statistics

All the experiments and data analyses were performed by a researcher who was blinded to the experimental groups. Animals showing missed virus injection and incorrect optical fiber placement were excluded from the analysis. For experiments in one or more animal cohorts, sample sizes (n) indicated in graphs and figure legends were set based on prior references (10, 25, 26) in the same field. All n values represent biological replicates. All the data were first subjected to the Shapiro-Wilk test to assess normality, and statistical tests were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad software). All tests used were two-tailed. For normally distributed data, paired t tests, unpaired t tests, one-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparison post hoc test or two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparison post hoc test were applied. For data with a nonnormal distribution, nonparametric tests, including the Mann-Whitney test and the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test, were applied. P values and statistical details are provided in the figure legends and tables S1 and S2. All the data are presented as the means ± SEMs. The statistical significance threshold was set at α = 0.05 (n.s., P > 0.05, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001).
