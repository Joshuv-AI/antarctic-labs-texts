Functional characterization of a CFEM domain-containing protein in the mycoparasitic fungus Clonostachys rosea reveals antimicrobial activity and a role in conidiation
Authors: Isaak Iliopoulos, Anastasios Samaras, Susmita Sigdel, Linnéa Forslund, Magnus Karlsson, Georgios Tzelepis, Mukesh Dubey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommon fungal extracellular membrane (CFEM) domain-containing proteins are small cysteine-rich proteins exclusive to fungi. They are shown to contribute to fungal virulence by promoting appressorium development and suppressing plant immune response. This study aimed to investigate the role of CFEM-domain-containing proteins in fungal antagonism and beneficial fungus-plant interactions using the mycoparasitic fungus Clonostachys rosea IK726, a biocontrol agent against several fungal pathogens. Gene expression analysis of 21 C. rosea IK726 CFEM-encoding genes during in vitro interactions with fungal hosts Botrytis cinerea and Rhizoctonia solani showed that their expression patterns depend on the host and interaction stage. CFEM10, predicted to have antimicrobial activity, was expressed in Escherichia coli and purified. An in vitro assay using purified CFEM10 protein revealed its antimicrobial activity against E. coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Functional analysis of CFEM10 using gene deletion strains showed a significant difference (P = 0.01) in conidial production between the WT and Δcfem10 strains. However, no significant difference was found in fungal antagonisms against B. cinerea, Fusarium graminearum or R. solani, root colonization ability and biocontrol of fusarium foot and root rot between the WT and Δcfem10 strains. Similarly, transient expression of cfem10 in tobacco leaves failed to suppress hypersensitive response (HR) induced by Avr4/Cf4 complex. In summary, our results demonstrated the antimicrobial activity of CFEM10 and its involvement in fungal conidiation. Introduction

Small Secreted Cysteine-rich Proteins (SSCPs) have been reported in fungi with diverse lifestyles, which play essential roles in fungal adaptation to ecological niches and interactions ranging from mutualism to parasitism (Kim et al. 2016). Certain SSCPs produced by plant-associated fungi have antimicrobial properties and contribute to interference competition (Snelders et al. 2020, 2023). The CFEM (cysteine-rich fungal extracellular membrane) domain-containing protein is a fungus-specific SSCP found exclusively in fungi of the phyla Ascomycota and Basidiomycota (Kulkarni et al. 2003; Zhang et al. 2015). CFEM domains are typically 60 amino acids long with eight characteristically spaced cysteine (Cys) residues with the consensus sequence P-x-C-[AG]-x(2)-C-x(8,12)-C-x(1,4)-D-x(2,5)-C-x-C-x(9,14)-C-x(3,4)-C-x(15,16), where x is any residue with its range is indicated (Kulkarni et al. 2003; Zhang et al. 2015). CFEM domain-containing proteins can comprise one or multiple CFEM modules, often located at the N terminus, one or several transmembrane domains, a GPI-anchor (glycosylphosphatidylinositol) and/or signal peptides (Kulkarni et al. 2003; Zhang et al. 2015). The role of CFEM proteins in fungal virulence is shown; however, their role in fungal antagonisms and beneficial fungus-plant interactions remains elusive.

CFEM-encoding genes have been identified in several fungal species, ranging from one gene in Saccharomyces cerevisiae to ten genes in the saprotroph Neurospora crassa and up to 20 genes in haemibiotrophic plant pathogenic species Magnaporthe oryzae (Zhang et al. 2015). Similarly, the CFEM gene count in necrotrophic plant pathogenic fungi, such as Fusarium graminearum, F. verticillioides, and F. vanetteni, is higher, with 18–23 CFEM genes reported. A study by Zhang et al. suggested that plant pathogenic fungi possess more CFEM genes than nonpathogenic fungi (Zhang et al. 2015). Similarly, the predicted CFEM gene copy number in mycoparasitic fungi, such as those in the genus Trichoderma (e.g., T. atroviride, T. virens, and T. reesei; 14–17 genes), is comparable to the CFEM gene numbers found in plant pathogenic fungi (Piombo et al. 2023). While the number of predicted CFEM genes in the genus Clonostachys ranged from 20 genes in Clonostachys sp. CBS 192.96 to 32 in C. chloroleuca (Piombo et al. 2023).

Many CFEM domain-containing proteins contribute to fungal virulence by supporting processes such as host adhesion and penetration, modulating plant hormone production and immune suppression in both plant- and human-pathogenic fungi, and are recognized as effectors (Wang et al. 2022; Shang et al. 2024; Li et al. 2025). In addition, CFEM proteins contribute to conidiation, appressorium formation and development, and cell wall stress tolerance (Kou et al. 2017; Zhu et al. 2017; Li et al. 2025). This study aims to investigate the biological functions of CFEM genes in fungal antagonistic interactions and beneficial fungus-plant interactions.

To achieve the aim, we used the filamentous fungus Clonostachys rosea IK726 (Ascomycota, Sordariomycetes, Hypocreales, Bionectriaceae), a necrotrophic mycoparasite recognised as an effective biocontrol agent against a wide range of plant diseases caused by fungal and nematode pathogens (Karlsson et al. 2015; Iqbal et al. 2018, 2020; Chaudhary et al. 2024, 2025). The biocontrol ability of IK726 is attributed to its interference-competition through antibiosis and mycoparasitism, as well as its ability to induce plant defence responses (Jensen et al. 2021; Piombo et al. 2024a, b, c). For interference competition against fungal hosts, IK726 produces a range of hydrolytic enzymes, antifungal secondary metabolites, and membrane transporters (Dubey et al. 2014, 2016; Fatema et al. 2018; Nygren et al. 2018; Broberg et al. 2021; Piombo et al. 2021). Additionally, IK726 employ SSCPs, such as hydrophobins and LysM-domain-containing proteins, and small non-coding RNAs in antagonisms and fungus-plant interactions (Dubey et al. 2014, 2016, 2020; Jensen et al. 2021; Piombo et al. 2021, 2022).

IK726 genome contains 22 putative CFEM genes, which is relatively higher compared to closely related mycoparasites T. atroviride, T. reesei, and T. virens, as well as fungal pathogens F. graminarum and F. verticillioides (Piombo et al. 2023). We hypothesize that increased CFEM gene copy number in IK726 may play a vital role in fungal antagonisms. In this study, we examined the gene expression of 21 CFEM-encoding genes in IK726 during antagonistic interactions. We functionally characterised a putative effector, CFEM10, by generating gene deletion strains and performing transient expression in Nicotiana benthamiana. Additionally, using purified CFEM10 proteins, we demonstrated their antimicrobial activity against bacteria and fungi.

Materials and methods

Fungal strains and culture conditions

C. rosea strain IK726 wild type (WT) and mutants derived from it, Botrytis cinerea strain B05.10, F. graminearum strain PH-1 and Rhizoctonia solani strain BBA 69,670 were maintained on potato dextrose agar (PDA; Oxoid, Cambridge, UK) medium at 25 °C, in darkness.

CFEM sequence analysis

The C. rosea IK726 genome version 2 (Broberg et al. 2018) was used to extract the CFEM nucleotide and amino acid (aa) sequences. The presence of conserved domains was analysed using the Conserved Domain Search Service (CDS) (Marchler-Bauer et al. 2011). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using UGENE (Okonechnikov et al. 2012), and WebLogo analysis of CFEM domain aa sequences was conducted according to previously described procedures (Crooks et al. 2004; Schneider and Stephens 1990). The prediction of antimicrobial activity for the three putative secreted effectors CFEM9, CFEM10, and CFEM11 was investigated using the software package available at https://github.com/fantin-mesny/amapec (Mesny and Thomma 2024).

Gene expression analysis

Gene expression analyses of 21 CFEM genes were conducted in IK726 during in vitro dual culture plate interaction with the fungal hosts, B. cinerea (Cr-Bc) or R. solani (Cr-Rs). The dual plate confrontation assay was performed on agar plates, and the growing front (7–10 mm) of IK726 was harvested at the mycelial contact (C; early physical contact between the mycelia) and at 24 h post-mycelial contact (AC; close physical contact) stages of interaction (Dubey et al. 2020). IK726 confronted with itself (Cr-Cr) was used as a control treatment.

RNA was extracted using the Qiagen RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and reverse-transcribed with the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Transcript levels were quantified by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) using gene-specific primer pairs (Table S1), and the relative expression levels for the target gene were calculated compared to actin from threshold cycle (Ct) values using the 2-ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Gene expression analysis was performed in five biological replicates, each with two technical replicates.

CFEM10 heterologous expression, protein purification and antimicrobial assay

The pET-22b vector containing cfem10 was transformed into Escherichia coli SHuffle T7 Express cells (New England Biolab, MA, USA). A single colony was used to inoculate LB medium with 100 µg/mL carbenicillin and grown overnight at 30 °C with shaking. The culture was then diluted 1:100 into fresh LB with 100 µg/mL carbenicillin and incubated at 30 °C until OD₆₀₀ reached ~ 0.5. Cultures were cooled to 18 °C, induced with 0.4 mM IPTG after 2 h, and incubated for 16 h at 18 °C before harvesting by centrifugation. The cell pellet was resuspended in ice-cold buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, 20% sucrose), incubated on ice for 30 min, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in ice-cold water containing 1 mM MgCl₂, incubated for 30 min, and centrifuged again under the same conditions to obtain the periplasmic fraction. The supernatant was clarified by centrifugation and applied to Ni²⁺–NTA resin (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The resin was washed sequentially with a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl, followed by a buffer containing 25 mM sodium–potassium phosphate, pH 7.0, 500 mM NaCl and 10 mM imidazole. Bound proteins were eluted in the same buffer containing 500 mM imidazole. The eluate was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 16/60 column equilibrated in 20 mM sodium phosphate, pH 8.0, and 150 mM NaCl. The protein eluted as a monomer, and the monomeric fractions were pooled and concentrated for downstream applications.

We used a 96-well microtiter plate to assess the antimicrobial activity of purified CFEM proteins in LB medium supplemented with CFEM protein at a final concentration of 8 µM (Snelders et al. 2020, 2023). Strain grown in LB medium mixed with the same volume of protein buffer was used as a control. Plates were incubated under shaking conditions at 37 °C for E. coli and Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 and at 30 °C for S. cerevisiae. Growth was monitored by measuring optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) using a microplate reader. For E. coli and DC3000, OD₆₀₀ readings were taken every two hours for 12 h, while S. cerevisiae was monitored for 24 h at the same interval.

Generating gene deletion strain and phenotypic analyses

CFEM10 gene deletion strains in IK726 were generated using Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation and validated by PCR using primers listed in Table S1 (Utermark and Karlovsky 2008). Phenotypic analyses were performed using IK726 WT and single spore purified independent Δcfem10 strains (Δcfem10 A, Δcfem10 B, Δcfem10 C). Each experiment included three to five biological replicates, depending on the phenotype.

The growth rate, colony morphology, and conidiation were evaluated on PDA medium (Fatema et al. 2018). Growth under stress conditions was determined on PDA medium supplemented with 0.5 M NaCl, 1 M sorbitol, or 0.1% caffeine (Dubey et al. 2016). Antagonistic behaviour against the fungal hosts B. cinerea, F. graminearum, and R. solani was tested using an in vitro plate confrontation assay on PDA medium (Fatema et al. 2018). The biocontrol potential of IK726 against fusarium foot rot caused by F. graminearum on wheat was assessed through a sand seedling test. Plants were harvested three weeks post-inoculation, and disease symptoms were scored on a 0–4 scale (Fatema et al. 2018). The experiment comprised five biological replicates, with 15 plants per replicate. The root colonisation ability of IK726 WT and cfem deletion strains was measured using RT-qPCR (Piombo et al. 2024a). IK726 inoculated roots were detached from the plant five days post-inoculation (dpi), washed with water and used for DNA extraction. To quantify the DNA of IK726 in wheat roots, the actin gene (act) was used as the target gene for C. rosea, and tubulin (tub) gene was used as the target gene for wheat in RT-qPCR. Root colonization was expressed as the ratio between act and tub (Piombo et al. 2024a). The experiment was performed in five biological replicates.

Construction of overexpression vectors and hypersensitive response suppression assay

The cfem10 coding sequences were codon-optimised, custom-synthesised, and inserted into the pUC19 vector by Genewiz (Azenta Life Science, Leipzig, Germany). To create an overexpression cassette, cfem10 was amplified from pUC19 and cloned into destination vector pGWB602 (cfem10::pGWB602), driven by the constitutive CaMV35S promoter, using Gateway cloning protocols (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). The destination vector was then transformed to the A. tumefaciens strain C58C1. Nicotiana benthamiana plants (kindly provided by Wageningen University) harboring the Cf-4 receptor protein from tomato plants and the Cladosporium fulvum effector protein Avr4 were used in the hypersensitive response (HR) assay. To assess the role of CFEM10 in suppressing HR, the A. tumefaciens strain C58C1 carrying the cfem10::pGWB602 cassette was infiltrated into the leaves of 4-week-old N. benthamiana plants (grown under 18 h light/6 h dark at 23 °C) to transiently express CFEM10. The HR was triggered 24 h after infiltration with the C. fulvum Avr4 effector protein at an OD600 of 0.03. Agrobacterium infiltration with an empty vector and only buffer was used as a control.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on gene expression and phenotype data using a General Linear Model approach implemented in Statistica version 13 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA). Pairwise comparisons were made using Fisher’s or Tukey-Kramer method at the 95% significance level. Student’s t-test was also performed on gene expression.

Results

Sequence analysis of predicted CFEM proteins in IK726

The amino acid sequence analysis of 22 IK726 CFEM domain-containing proteins identified 11 proteins with predicted transmembrane domains, eight with a GPI anchor, and CFEM9, 10, and 11 were predicted to be effectors (Fig. 1A). The CFEM domains typically contain eight Cys residues spaced characteristically (Kulkarni et al. 2003). A multiple sequence alignment and Weblogo analysis of IK726 CFEM domains, along with previously known CFEM domains from B. cinerea (BC1G_15201), Sclerotinia sclerotiorum (SS1G_07295), T. gamsii (TGAM01_v206178), and F. graminearum (FGSG_03599), showed that the distribution of Cys residues in IK726 agrees with previously reported CFEM domains (Fig. 1B, C) (Kulkarni et al. 2003; Zhang et al. 2015).

Expression analysis of CFEM genes

Gene expression analysis of 21 CFEM genes was carried out in IK726 during dual plate interaction with fungal hosts B. cinerea or R. solani. The IK726 mycelia were harvested from the interaction zone at ‘’before contact’’ (BC) and ‘’after contact’’ (AC) stages of fungal-fungal interactions and compared with the BC stage of Cr-Cr self-interactions. Gene expression analysis revealed a basal expression of all CFEM genes. Among these, cfem3 expression was increased (P ≤ 0.05) at both interaction stages against both the prey and even during the AC stage of self-interaction (Fig. 2). In contrast, the expression of cfem7, cfem12, and cfem15 was not affected compared to Cr-Cr BC. The expression of six CFEM genes (cfem2, cfem3, cfem5, cfem9, cfem10, cfem11) was increased at the AC stage of Cr-Cr self-interaction compared to Cr-Cr BC, while the expression of cfem19 was significantly (P ≤ 0.05) decreased. Additionally, gene expression analysis identified 9 CFEM genes with host-specific expression. For example, the expression of cfem2 was increased during Cr-Bc BC but downregulated during Cr-Rs Bc, while the expression of cfem5 and cfem10 increased during Cr-Rs AC but decreased during Cr-Bc AC (Fig. 2).

IK726, confronted with itself, was used as control (Cr-Cr). Relative expression level based on RT-qPCR was calculated as the ratio between the target cfem and actin gene using 2−ΔΔCt method [20] and compared with the Cr-Cr BC. During interaction with B. cinerea, the expression of 12 CFEM genes was decreased or remained unchanged. The expression of cfem6, cfem8, cfem10 and cfem16 was decreased (P ≤ 0.05) at both stages of interactions, while the expression of eight CFEM genes was decreased at the AC stage compared to Cr-Cr BC (P ≤ 0.05), exhibiting interaction stage-specific expression (Fig. 2). During interactions with R. solani, 12 CFEM genes were differentially expressed, with increased expression of cfem3 and decreased expression of cfem19 at both interaction stages (P ≤ 0.05). Gene expression revealed a stage-specific interaction of six genes, with increased expression of cfem1, cfem13, cfem14, and cfem18 (P ≤ 0.05) at the AC stage, and decreased expression of cfem2 and cfem4 at both the BC and AC stages, respectively, compared to Cr-Cr BC (Fig. 2).

CFEM10 showed antimicrobial activities under in vitro conditions

The antimicrobial activity prediction for putative effector candidates showed a higher antimicrobial probability (0.83) for CFEM10. In contrast, the probabilities for CFEM9 and CFEM11 were very low (0.08) and (0.02), respectively, indicating a low likelihood of antimicrobial activity. To investigate the antimicrobial activity, the coding sequences of cfem10 was synthesized and cloned. Heterologous expression of codon-optimised CFEM10 in SHuffle T7 Express E. coli cells resulted in the production of proteins with the expected size (10.8 kDa; Fig. 3A, Fig. S1). The predicted antimicrobial activity of CFEM10 was validated against bacterial strains E. coli and P. syringae pv. tomato DC3000 and yeast S. cerevisiae by measuring their growth in LB medium supplemented with 8µM purified CFEM10 proteins or buffer as a control. The result showed a reduced optical density (OD600) of E. coli after 10 h of incubation with CFEM10 (p = 0.009) (Fig. 3B). In contrast, the growth of P. syringae pv. tomato DC3000 was not affected (Fig. 3C). The antimicrobial effect was more pronounced against S. cerevisiae, with a significant difference in growth rate observed between CFEM10-treated and control treatments starting 8 h post-inoculation (Fig. 3D). This is the first report demonstrating antimicrobial potential of a CFEM effector protein. However, a deeper investigation is required to understand the mode of action of CFEM10 as an antimicrobial protein.

Deletion of cfem10 affects IK726 conidiation

To investigate the role of CFEM10 in different aspects of fungal physiology, including antagonisms, cfem10 deletion strains were constructed. Deletion of cfem10 did not affect the mycelial growth of IK726 on PDA or PDA supplemented with NaCl (salt stress), sorbitol (osmotic stress), or caffeine (cell wall stress) (Fig. S2). In contrast, conidial production in Δcfem10 strains was significantly reduced by 25% − 52% (P = 0.001) compared with the WT on PDA at 10 dpi (Fig. 4).

Deletion of cfem10 did not affect antagonism, root colonization and biocontrol efficacy

During dual-culture plate confrontation, IK726 can grow over B. cinerea, F. graminearum and R. solani and conidiate on their mycelia (Dubey et al. 2014; Piombo et al. 2021, 2022). No differences in growth rate (overgrowth) on B. cinerea, F. graminearum and R. solani were recorded during in vitro dual-plate confrontation between the WT and deletion strains (Fig. S2). Similarly, the biocontrol tests against fusarium foot rot disease showed no significant difference in disease severity on wheat seedlings from seed coated with Δcfem10 strains compared with seedlings from seeds coated with the WT (Fig. S2). To analyse the contribution of CFEM10 in root colonization, the biomass of IK726 strains on wheat roots was measured five dpi by quantifying the ratio between IK726 DNA and wheat DNA with RT-qPCR. No significant differences in IK726/wheat DNA ratio were found between WT and Δcfem10 inoculated wheat roots (Fig. S2).

Transient expression of cfem10 in tobacco did not influence plant immune response

CFEM proteins are shown to suppress HR in plants and thereby contribute to fungal pathogenesis (Wang et al. 2022; Shang et al. 2024; Li et al. 2025). Since IK726 suppresses plant defence responses during interactions with wheat roots (Piombo et al. 2024a), we evaluated whether CFEM10 can suppress plant immunity, thereby contributing to IK726 root colonisation ability. An A. tumefaciens strain containing cfem10 overexpression vectors was generated and co-infiltrated with the known fungal effector Avr4 into N. benthamiana leaves carrying the Cf-4 Pattern Recognition Receptor (PRR) that specifically recognises Avr4, triggering the HR. Tobacco leaves co-infiltrated with Avr4 proteins, and only buffer (IM), or empty vector (EV) were used as controls. Co-infiltration of cfem10 overexpression vectors with Avr4 resulted in HR symptoms similar to those observed in the IM and EV controls. This indicates that CFEM10 does not interfere with or suppress the Avr4-induced HR in N. benthamiana (Fig. S3). However, further experiments using other known HR-inducing proteins are needed to validate these findings.

Discussion

The role of CFEM domain-containing SSCPs in fungal biology and pathogenic fungus-host interactions are shown. However, their role in fungal antagonisms and beneficial fungus-plant interactions is elusive. In this study, we used functional genomics approaches, including gene expression analysis, heterologous expression, and gene-deletion strains, to investigate the biological functions of a CFEM domain-containing protein in the mycoparasitic C. rosea IK726, known for its potential as a biocontrol agent against fungal and nematode pathogens (Jensen et al. 2021).

The differential expression of CFEM genes during antagonistic interactions in C. rosea suggests their potential roles in sensing fungal hosts and in signal transduction during these interactions. Although investigation of CFEM gene expression in antagonistic fungi during interactions with hosts is limited, our results align with previous findings in the mycoparasitic fungus T. atroviride, where differential expression of a CFEM gene, tacfem1, was reported during in vitro dual-culture interactions with R. solani (Guzmán-Guzmán et al. 2017). Similarly, a transcriptome analysis of nematode-trapping fungi Arthrobotrys flagrans during in vitro interaction with host nematode Caenorhabditis elegans showed increased expression of eight CFEM domain-containing genes, and decreased expression of another six CFEM domain-containing genes (Shi et al. 2025). Interestingly, disruption of AfCFEM1 and AfCFEM3 genes led to a significant increase in A. flagrans proteins associated with fungal pathogenicity, resulting in a denser adhesive layer on the trap surface and ultimately increasing nematode mortality. However, disrupting AfCFEM2 produced the opposite effect (Shi et al. 2025).

The transcriptional response of mycoparasitic fungi, specific to fungal hosts and interaction stages, has been previously demonstrated in C. rosea during interactions with B. cinerea and F. gramineaum (Nygren et al. 2018; Piombo et al. 2021, 2022). The effect of fungal hosts B. cinerea and R. solani and the interaction stages on the expression of specific CFEM genes revealed in this study align well with previous results. Additionally, differences in tacfem1 expression, influenced by the fungal anastomosis group, have been reported in T. atroviride during interaction with R. solani (Guzmán-Guzmán et al. 2017), indicating specificity in host recognition and transcriptional responses in mycoparasitic fungi. The difference in transcriptomic response is linked to inherent differences among fungal hosts, such as variations in cell wall composition and their ability to produce different sets of enzymes and metabolites in response to mycoparasites (Nygren et al. 2018; Piombo et al. 2021, 2022). The fungal hosts used in this study differ in taxonomic classification and lifestyle; B. cinerea is an ascomycete, and R. solani is a basidiomycete, and they cause different diseases with distinct host ranges.

Differential expression of 21 CFEM genes at Cr-Cr AC compared with Cr-Cr BC, which is consistent with the expression patterns of hydrophobin genes, another Cys-rich effector, in C. rosea and T. atroviride (Dubey et al. 2014; Mikus et al. 2009). The increased expression suggests a potential role for CFEM proteins in mycelial protection against secreted products from self- and/or intra-specific recognition, signalling, or hyphal fusion.

Effector proteins with antimicrobial activity have been identified and characterised in fungal pathogens, for example, VdAve1 and VdAve1L2 in the plant-pathogenic fungus V. dahliae (Snelders et al. 2020, 2023). The specificity of the antimicrobial effect of CFEM10 aligns with previous findings for VdAve1 and VdAve1L2 (Snelders et al. 2020, 2023), where VdAve1 exclusively affected bacteria but not fungi, and VdAve1L2 suppressed Actinobacteria but not Sphingomonadales. The mechanism of antimicrobial activity of CFEM-domain-containing proteins is unknown. However, based on the reported mode of action of other antimicrobial peptides, we speculate that the antimicrobial activity of the CFEM domain proteins is attributed to their potential interaction with microbial cell wall and cell membrane components, including their composition and permeability, thereby causing membrane disruption and inhibiting biosynthesis (Talapko et al. 2020). This is plausibly supported by the reported specificity of CFEM proteins for fungal and bacterial hosts, and for the Gram-positive Actinomyces and the Gram-negative Sphingomonadales, as these groups of microbes have different cell wall compositions (Snelders et al. 2020, 2023). A similar explanation can be speculated for the differential antimicrobial activity reported in this study. Although both E. coli and P. syringae pv. tomato DC3000 are Gram-negative, the composition of their outer membrane, especially fatty acids and membrane permeability, is different (Sathesh-Prabu et al. 2025). However, the role of CFEM proteinsas inhibitors of DNA, RNA, and protein synthesis cannot be ruled out as a potential mechanism for their antimicrobial activity (Talapko et al. 2020). A deeper analysis is required for future research to understand the antimicrobial properties of CFEM-domain-containing proteins and their underlying mechanisms.

The reduced conidiation of Δcfem10 strains indicates a role for CFEM10 in conidiation, confirming previous reports in the plant-pathogenic fungi C. gloeosporioides and B. cinerea (Zhu et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2024). Conidiation is a complex process, and the mechanism by which CFEM proteins contribute to it remains unknown. Fungal conidiation requires cell wall remodelling (Rocha et al. 2020). In Aspergillus fumigatus, for example, the cell wall integrity pathway (CWIP) components RlmA and MpkA directly activate the conidiation cascade by promoting the transcriptional activation or phosphorylation of critical proteins involved in asexual development (Rocha et al. 2020). Since CFEM proteins contribute to cell wall remodelling and appressorium formation by mediating signalling pathways, a similar mechanism for reduced conidial production in Δcfem10 strains can be speculated.

Proteins with the CFEM domain function as cell-surface receptors, signal transducers, and adhesion molecules in host-pathogen interactions and contribute to fungal virulence and suppression of host defence responses (Wang et al. 2022; Shang et al. 2024; Li et al. 2025). No significant difference in antagonisms, biocontrol of fusarium foot and root rot, and root colonisation between the Δcfem10 strains and the WT indicates no role for CFEM10 in these phenotypes. However, the functional complementation of CFEM10 by other CFEM genes in C. rosea cannot be ruled out. These findings contrast with previous reports on the roles of hydrophobins and LysM-domain-containing effectors in C. rosea, where deletion of hyd1, hyd3, lysm1, or lysm2 significantly affected antagonism, biocontrol, and root colonization (Dubey et al. 2014, 2020).

Transient expression of CFEM10 in N. benthamiana did not influence its immune response. The results support the root-colonisation phenotypes, in which no difference was observed between the IK726 WT and Δcfem10 strains. Similar results are reported in V. dahlae, in which seven out of nine CFEM domain-containing proteins failed to suppress HR caused by the known V. dahlae effector VdEG1 (Wang et al. 2022). In summary, the data presented in this study demonstrate, for the first time, the potential antimicrobial activity of CFEM10. However, the lack of results from the antimicrobial activity of CFEM10 against C. rosea fungal hosts, including the plant pathogens such as B. cinerea and F. graminearum, is a limitation of this work and highlights the importance of key future directions. Although CFEM10 contributes to conidial production, its role in fungal antagonism, biocontrol potential, and plant root colonization is redundant in C. rosea IK726 or complemented by other CFEM genes. These findings lay the foundation for future research on the functions of CFEM proteins in mycoparasitic fungi, especially their potential as antimicrobial agents, and warrant further exploration of their biological roles in fungal interactions.
