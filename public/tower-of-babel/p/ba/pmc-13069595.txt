Prevalence of Delayed Breastfeeding Initiation and Its Risk Factors Among Reproductive Aged Women in Somalia: A Cross‐Sectional Study Using Somalia Health and Demographic Survey
Authors: Nigussie Adam Birhan, Kefale Tilahun Getahun, Mahad Ibrahim Ali, Belsti Atnkut, Kindu Kebede Gebre, Tilahun Yemanu Birhan, Denekew Bitew Belay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and aimsAlthough the advantages of breastfeeding are well‐documented, many mothers struggle to meet the World Health Organization's recommendation for early initiation of breastfeeding. Improved cognitive development and intelligence in children are linked to early breastfeeding. As far as we are aware, no previous studies have looked at the initiation of breastfeeding in Somalia. Thus, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the prevalence and contributing factors of delayed breastfeeding initiation among women in Somalia.MethodsThis study used data from 2020 Somalia Health and Demographic Survey, including a weighted samples of 7,965 mothers of children. Multilevel logistic regression was used to identify factors linked to delayed breastfeeding initiation. Statistical significance was determined using adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsThe prevalence of delayed breastfeeding initiation was 32.73%. Mother age group 25–34 (AOR = 0.85; 95%CI:0: 0.73, 0.98), cesarean section birth (AOR = 2.45; 95%CI: 1.74, 3.44), ANC visit (AOR = 0.80; 95%CI: 0.71, 0.91), did not have immediate skin to skin contact (AOR = 0.32; 95%CI: 0.28, 0.37), high community media exposure (AOR = 0.73; 95%CI: 0.58, 0.92), having breast feeding counseling (AOR = 0.73; 95%CI: 0.59, 0.89) and large birth size (AOR = 0.65; 95%CI:0.53, 0.79) were factors associated with delayed breastfeeding initiation.ConclusionIn Somalia, the rate of delayed breastfeeding initiation is still high. Maternal age, antenatal care visits, mode of delivery, birth size, breastfeeding counseling, skin‐to‐skin contact, geographic region, residence, and community media exposure are important factors. Therefore, important interventions to encourage early breastfeeding initiation, including breastfeeding education programs, increasing access to ANC services, offering prompt counseling, and assisting mothers during cesarean deliveres. Moreover, implement facility promoting immediate skin‐to‐skin contact and expanding community‐based education and media campaigns to improve awareness in rural areas are vital interventions to encourage early breastfeeding initiation in Somalia.Keywords: delayed breastfeeding initiation, early breastfeeding, multilevel logistic regression, Somalia

Introduction

Breastfeeding is widely recognized as a vital source of nutrition that protects children from both infectious and chronic diseases globally [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations International Children's Emergency Fund (UNICEF) advise initiating breastfeeding within the first hour after birth [2]. Breastfeeding significantly enhances a child's intelligence and cognitive growth, with benefits extending beyond infancy, benefiting both the child and the mother throughout their lives [3]. Early breastfeeding offers advantages to mothers by promoting breast milk production and triggering the release of oxytocin, which aids in uterine contractions and minimizes the risk of postpartum bleeding. Additionally, starting breastfeeding soon after birth allows infants to receive colostrum, the initial breast milk, which is rich in nutrients and contains antibodies that shield the newborn from illnesses [4].

Globally, 60% of newborns experienced a delayed breastfeeding initiation, leading to increased risk of mortality and adverse health outcomes [5]. In 2019, the mortality rate for children under five was 37.7 per 1000 live births, leading to 5.2 million deaths before their fifth birthday and 2.4 million were neonates with a mortality rate of 17.7 per 1000 live births [3].

Early breastfeeding initiation is recognized as a key strategy to accelerate progress toward Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2, which aims to reduce neonatal mortality to 12 deaths per 1000 live births by 2030 [6]. The burden of neonatal mortality is particularly high in fragile health system settings such as Somalia. According to the 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey, the neonatal mortality rate is 40 deaths per 1000 live births significantly higher than SDG 3.2 target [7]. In low and middle‐income countries, the prevalence of delayed breastfeeding initiation was 53.8%, varying from 15.0% in Burundi to 83.4% in Guinea [8]. Globally, although 116 countries have made significant progress in reducing child mortality, projections indicate that 63 countries are still expected to miss the SDG neonatal mortality target. Therefore, it is essential to focus on reducing neonatal, infant, and child mortality rates to prevent an estimated 48.1 million under‐five deaths from 2020 to 2030 [9, 10, 11, 12].

Previous studies have identified several factors contributing to the delayed initiation of breastfeeding, defined as starting breastfeeding after the first hour of life. Mode of delivery [8, 13], place of delivery [8, 14], media exposure [14], frequent ANC visits [15], maternal education [16], maternal age [16], wealth index [14, 16], skin to skin contact [14], women occupation [17] were significant association with delayed initiation of breastfeeding. However, no studies investigated the prevalence and risk factors of delayed breastfeeding initiation in Somalia using the 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey data. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of delayed breastfeeding initiation and risk factors in Somalia using 2020 SDHS data. The 2020 SDHS is a nationally representative survey providing comprehensive data on maternal and child health, making it appropriate for studying breastfeeding practices and informing national health policy. The findings of this study will provide critical information for international agencies, public health professionals, policymakers and program managers working in the field of nutrition and breastfeeding to guide target intervations, strengthen child nutrition programs that helps to reduce malnutrition among children in Somalia.

Methods and Materials

Study Design and Data

Secondary data based on cross‐sectional study design from Somalia Health and Demographic Survey was used. The data did not collected in the Lower Shabelle and Middle Jubba, due to the disruptions of al‐Shabaab. This survey is the first and nationally large scale dataset that was conducted by the cooperative of the Government of Somalia, through the Ministry of Planning, Investment, and Economic Development, Somali National Bureau of Statistics and the Ministry of Health, together with united nation food population fund (UNFPA) from 2018 to 2019. A total of 16,360 households were selected for the sample, of which 15,870 were occupied. Of the occupied households, 15,761 were successfully interviewed. All women aged 15–49 years were eligible to be interviewed [7, 18]. Detailed sampling procedures can be found in the SDHS 2020 final report. The study population consisted of biological mothers aged 15–49 years who had at least one living child under 59 months at the time of the survey. In this study, a weighted sample 7965 mothers was participated.

Study Variable

In this study, the outcome variable was delayed breastfeeding initiation, which is dichotomized as “1” delayed initiation and “0” timely initiation. According to WHO guidelines, delayed breastfeeding initiation is defined as not starting breastfeeding within 1 h of birth [19]. A range of independent variables potentially associated with delayed breastfeeding initiation was identified based on a review of relevant literature [20, 21, 22]. These were categorized into individual‐level and community‐level variables. Individual‐Level Variables: sex of child, age of mother, education status, place of residence, marital status, wealth index, media exposure, ANC visit, place of delivery, mode of delivery, type of birth, parity, birth order, skin‐to‐skin contact and community level variable includes: residence, region, community poverty level, community media exposure level and community level literacy. In this study, mass media exposure was considered by combining the access of television or radio or both and it was categorized as “exposed”. But, if they do not have any exposure from these outlets, we coded it as “no media exposure”. Community media exposure was generated by aggregating individual‐level access to radio and television within each cluster (enumeration area). Then, the variable was dichotomized into high with proportions above the median and those at or below the median as low category based on the national median value. Community‐level literacy is defined by the proportion of mothers within the cluster who attended primary, secondary, or higher education. The second community‐level factor is the community poverty level, which reflects the proportion of poor mothers within the cluster; this proportion is aggregated to indicate the overall poverty status within the cluster [23].

Data Management and Analysis

STATA version 18 was used to clean, and analyze the data. The study participants’ socio‐demographic and health‐related attributes were compiled using descriptive statistics. A multilevel model was used to identify the potential risk factors for delayed breastfeeding initiation. A multilevel logistic regression model was used because of the data's hierarchical structure, which shows that children are nested within communities (clusters/districts). This modeling technique enables precise estimation of both fixed and random effects while taking intra‐cluster correlation into account.

First, the crude association between each explanatory variable and delayed breastfeeding initiation was evaluated using bi‐variable multilevel logistic regression analyses. Variables with a p‐value < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were kept for the multivariable model [24]. This is more liberal threshold commonly used in epidemiological research to avoid excluding potentially important variables at an early stage. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor, and all variables were below the threshold of 10. Model comparison and fitness were assessed based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), likelihood ratio test (LRT) and deviance (−2LLR) values. The model with the minimum deviance, BIC and AIC value is considered as a better fit [25]. Finally adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with a 95% confidence interval was reported for the best‐fitted model. A total of four models were fitted:

Model 0 (Null Model): Contained no explanatory variables; used to assess the extent of variation in delayed breastfeeding initiation attributable to community‐level clustering.

Model 0 (Null Model): Contained no explanatory variables; used to assess the extent of variation in delayed breastfeeding initiation attributable to community‐level clustering.

Model I: Included only individual‐level variables.

Model I: Included only individual‐level variables.

Model II: Included only community‐level variables.

Model II: Included only community‐level variables.

Model III (Full Model): Included both individual‐ and community‐level variables, providing adjusted estimates for all relevant factors.

For fixed effects, results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) to determine the strength and direction of association between predictor variables and delayed breastfeeding initiation.

For random effects, the following measures were used:

Intra‐Class Correlation Coefficient (ICC): To quantify the proportion of total variation in delayed breastfeeding initiation attributable to differences between communities.

Intra‐Class Correlation Coefficient (ICC): To quantify the proportion of total variation in delayed breastfeeding initiation attributable to differences between communities.

Median Odds Ratio: To express the magnitude of cluster‐level variation in terms of odds ratios.

Median Odds Ratio: To express the magnitude of cluster‐level variation in terms of odds ratios.

Proportional Change in Variance (PCV): To assess the reduction in unexplained variance when explanatory variables were added to the model.

This multilevel approach allowed for a more accurate estimation of the individual and contextual factors influencing breastfeeding practices in Somalia. When logistic regression model is used the residual at level one (child level) are assumed to follow the standard logistic distribution with mean 0 and variance π23=3.29 [26]. It is expressed as: ICC=σμ02σμ02+π23, σμ02is the variance of the higher level (Community).

The MOR is defined as the median value the odds ratio between the area at the highest risk and the area at the lowest risk when randomly picking out two area [27]. It is computed by; MOR=exp⁡((2xσμ02∗0.6745)) Where; σμ02 is the area level variance, and 0.6745 is the 75th percentile of the cumulative distribution function of the normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 1.

PCV is the proportion of the total observed individual variation of delayed breastfeeding initiation i.e. attributable to between‐cluster variations [28]. It is calculated as

PCV = [(VA−VB)VA∗] 100; Where VA = variance of the null model, and VB = variance of the model with more terms.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

This research involves a secondary analysis of publicly available Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data for Somalia. The DHS Program obtained ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of ICF International and the National Ethics Committee of Somalia. Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants prior to the survey interview. For children under the age of 18, consent was obtained from a parent or guardian. All data are anonymized and de‐identified before public release. Permission to use the data was obtained through the DHS Program website. Because this study was based on secondary analysis of publicly available anonymized data, no additional ethical approval was required. We used the demographic and health survey dataset, which adheres to ethical standards consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki. The data collection was approved by relevant ethical review boards and informed consent was obtained from all participants https://dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm.

Analysis of Random Effects

The ICC in the null model was 0.11, meaning that variations among Somalia's clusters (enumeration areas) accounted for 11% of the variation in delayed breastfeeding initiation. According to the final model's proportional change in variance (PCV), both individual‐ and community‐level factors accounted for 4.17% of the variation in delayed breastfeeding initiation. Significant variation in delayed breastfeeding initiation across communities was confirmed by the null model's median odds ratio, which was 1.50 and showed a significant clustering effect. Model III was found to be the best‐fitting model based on a comparison of models using deviance, AIC, BIC and log‐likelihood values, which had the lowest deviance, AIC, BIC, and highest log‐likelihood (see Table 2).

Mother's age groups 25–34 had 0.85 (AOR = 0.85; 95%CI0: 0.73, 0.98) times lower odds of delayed breastfeeding initiation as compared to 15–24 year age group. Mothers who gave birth through cesarean section were 2.45 times (AOR = 2.45; 95%CI: 1.74, 3.44) more likely to delayed breastfeeding initiation as compared to vaginal delivery. Mothers who had ANC visit were 20% less likely to delayed breastfeeding initiation than the reverse group (AOR = 0.80; 95%CI: 0.71, 0.91). Mothers living in Nomadic areas were 2.21 times (AOR = 2.21; 95%CI: 1.89, 2.57) more likely to delay initiate breastfeeding.

The odds of delayed breastfeeding initiation were 68% lower for women who had immediate skin‐to‐skin contact with their newborns compared to those who did not (AOR = 0.32; 95%CI: 0.28, 0.37). Likewise, women with high exposure to community media experienced a 27% reduction in the odds of delayed breastfeeding initiation (AOR = 0.73; 95%CI: 0.58, 0.92). Mothers who received counseling about breastfeeding were 0.73 times less likely to delay breastfeeding initiation than those who did not receive counseling (AOR = 0.73; 95%CI: 0.59, 0.89). Mothers from the Sanaag, Mudag, Hiraan, and Middle Shabelle regions were 0.69 times (AOR = 0.69, 97%CI: 0.50, 0.95), 0.70 times (AOR = 0.70; 95%CI: 0.49, 0.99), 0.46 times (AOR = 0.46; 95%CI: 0.31, 0.68), and 0.68 times (AOR = 0.68; 95%CI: 0.47, 0.98) less likely to delay breastfeeding initiation compared to those from Awdal, respectively. Conversely, mothers from the Banadir region were 1.56 times more likely to experience delayed breastfeeding initiation than those from Awdal (AOR = 1.56; 95%CI: 1.13, 2.16). The odds of delayed breastfeeding initiation were 18% and 35% lower for infants with average and large birth sizes, respectively, compared to those with small birth sizes (AOR = 0.82; 95%CI:0.74, 0.92 and AOR = 0.65; 95%CI:0.53, 0.79) (Table 3).

Discussion

In Somalia, the prevalence of delayed initiation of breastfeeding was 32.73%, which is aligns with the study in East Africa (31.33%) [3] and Arsi Zone (32.7%) [29]. However, it is lower than the study conducted in Gunchire town (19.5%) [30] and higher than study in Ethiopia (24.56%) [31]. These variations may be attributed to differences in the livelihood of the mothers, infant feeding styles, execution and strengthening of the health extension program and the enhancement of healthcare accessibility and socio‐cultural practices across the communities. Moreover, breastfeeding practices in Somalia are strongly shaped by the country's prolonged armed conflict, fragile health system and ongoing insecurity, which has disrupted maternal and child health services, limiting access to antenatal care, skilled birth attendance, and postnatal counseling that are essential for promoting timely initiation of breastfeeding. The null model suggests that 11% of the variation in delayed breastfeeding initiation is attributable to differences between communities. The final model's proportional change in variance showed that both individual‐ and community‐level factors explained 4.17% of this variation. The median odds ratio of 1.50 in the null model confirmed significant clustering, indicating that mothers within the same community were more likely to exhibit similar breastfeeding practices. These findings highlight the importance of community‐level interventions such as region‐specific education campaigns, media outreach, and local health worker engagement alongside individual‐level strategies to address delayed breastfeeding initiation effectively.

The multilevel logistic regression analysis showed that mother age, ANC visit, mode of delivery, birth weight, counseling about breastfeeding, skin‐to‐skin contact, region, residence and community media exposure level were associated with delayed breastfeeding initiation in Somalia. The study results suggest that mothers aged 25–34 years were less likely to have delayed initiation of breastfeeding as compared to mothers aged 15–24 years in line with research conducted in Ethiopia [32]. The possible explanation for this might be maternal age increases, the mother's experience in infant's care, higher autonomy in decision‐making, increased prior exposure to health services, and more established knowledge of cultural and family norms around infant feeding which can facilitate the timely initiation of breastfeeding.

Mothers who had delivered their child by cesarean section were more likely to delay breastfeeding initiation as compared to those who had vaginal delivery. This is consistent with the study in Nepal [33]. This might be explained due to mothers who deliver by cesarean section often face delays in initiating breastfeeding due to longer recovery times, postoperative pain, and difficulty finding comfortable positions. Infants may also experience respiratory distress and be separated from their mothers for intensive care. In developing countries, inadequate pain management worsens these challenges, limiting skin‐to‐skin contact and timely breastfeeding [3, 34]. Strengthening facility readiness, staff training, postoperative breastfeeding support and clear protocols to facilitate early skin‐to‐skin contact can help ensure early breastfeeding even after cesarean delivery.

Furthermore, women who experienced immediate skin‐to‐skin contact were 0.32 times less likely to delay the initiation of breastfeeding compared to those with postponed skin contact, aligning with findings from studies in Tanzania [35], and Nigeria [36] This could be skin‐to‐skin contact with the mother promotes early breastfeeding by releasing prolactin and oxytocin, which stimulate lactation and encourage mother‐infant attachment, leading WHO and UNICEF to endorse it as a key component of immediate newborn care that creates an optimal environment for breastfeeding [2]. Promoting this practice through facility protocols and provider training can help ensure timely breastfeeding initiation.

Mothers who received ANC were less likely to delay initiating breastfeeding compared to those who did not, consistent with findings reported in India [37], Uganda [38] and Ethiopia [30, 39]. A possible explanation is that mothers who attended ANC during critical periods may have received counseling and support services about recommended feeding practices for neonatal and infants from health extension workers, health professionals from health posts, maternal and child health service [40]. These findings highlight the importance of enhancing ANC services through standardized breastfeeding counseling, consistent provider training and supervision, and supportive national maternal health policies, alongside community outreach and media campaigns to promote ANC attendance and timely breastfeeding initiation.

Infants with average or large birth weights were less likely to practice delayed initiation of breastfeeding compared to small birth weight in line with the study conducted in Ethiopia [41] and United Arab Emirates [42]. This finding could be explained by the fact that babies born with large birth weight should have skin‐to‐skin contact with their mothers to get early breastfeeding and avoid hypothermia [15]. In addition, small‐sized neonates have worse suction ability breast seeking reflex and deglutition‐respiration cycle [43]. Moreover, health system practices, such as Neonatal Intensive Care Unit admission or temporary mother‐infant separation, and maternal perception of infant size can delay breastfeeding. Facility counseling should support early initiation for low birth weight infants, ensuring timely breastfeeding. Similarly, mothers residing in the community with media access had a lower likelihood of delayed breastfeeding initiation compared to those without media, in agreement with studies conducted in Ghana [44], Jordan [45] and Ethiopia [21]. One possible explanation is that access to information through media can improve community knowledge and practices regarding breastfeeding, promoting newborn health and survival which provide an efficient way to reach large populations within a country [46].

The present study found that mothers who received breastfeeding counseling immediately after childbirth were more likely to delay starting breastfeeding compared to those who did not receive such counseling right after delivery. This finding aligns with research conducted in Ethiopia [22] and India [47]. This is because immediate, focused advice and information from health care providers about the importance of timely initiation of breastfeeding and its importance to both newborn and maternal health might lead them to be more likely to initiate breastfeeding within 1 h of birth [48]. These findings suggest the need for policy measures that integrate breastfeeding counseling into national maternal health guidelines, health system interventions such as provider training and structured postnatal counseling, and community‐based strategies, including health workers and media campaigns to raise awareness and support timely breastfeeding initiation.

Limitations and Strengths of the Study

The primary strengths of the study lie in its large sample size and the use of data that is representative on a national scale. Additionally, the study employs an advanced statistical technique, multilevel analysis, which effectively assesses the influence of cluster effects on the delayed start of breastfeeding. Nonetheless, the study has certain limitations, such as its dependence on self‐reported data from the DHS survey, which may introduce bias due to the potential for inaccurate recall of past events. Furthermore, the study does not establish the fundamental causal relationships and residual confounding cannot be excluded due to unmeasured cultural, behavioral, or health service‐related factors. Moreover, the exclusion of Lower Shabelle and Middle Jubba regions may limit full national representativeness, as breastfeeding practices in conflict‐affected areas may differ due to disrupted health services and population displacement.

Conclusion

The research identified that 32.73% of breastfeeding initiations were delayed, suggesting a persistently high rate and underscoring crucial areas for intervention. The study found that age of mother, ANC visit, mode of delivery, birth weight, counseling about breastfeeding, skin‐to‐skin contact, region, residence and community media exposure level were found to be significantly association with delayed initiation of breastfeeding. Strengthen health system capacity through standardized provider training, supportive supervision, and integration of early breastfeeding indicators into routine monitoring. Additionally, implement facility protocols promoting immediate skin‐to‐skin contact, structured ANC and postnatal counseling, targeted cesarean‐delivery support and expand community‐based education and media campaigns to improve awareness in rural areas are vital interventions to encourage early breastfeeding initiation in Somalia. However, important data gaps remain regarding the quality and timing of breastfeeding counseling and other contextual health system factors influencing maternal practices. Future studies using longitudinal and mixed‐methods approaches are recommended to better understand counseling effectiveness and barriers within healthcare systems to inform more targeted interventions.
