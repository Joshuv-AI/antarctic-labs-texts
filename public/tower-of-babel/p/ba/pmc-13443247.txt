Open 4-Compartment Fasciotomy for Chronic Exertional Compartment Syndrome of the Lower Extremity
Authors: Tomás F. Vega, Thomas E. Moran, Napatpong Thamrongskulsiri, Gabriel Octavio Pérez Lloveras, Felipe Casanova, Jacob T. Morgan, Logan D. Moews, Jorge Chahla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Chronic exertional compartment syndrome (CECS) is an underrecognized cause of lower extremity dysfunction in athletes. Open 4-compartment fasciotomy remains the gold standard for surgical management.Indications:Operative intervention is indicated for patients with clinically and objectively confirmed CECS who fail to respond to conservative management. Contraindications include inadequate nonoperative treatment or unaddressed medical comorbidities. Proper diagnosis requires exclusion of vascular or neurologic causes that can mimic CECS symptoms.Technique Description:A 2-incision, 4-compartment fasciotomy is performed with the patient supine and a thigh tourniquet applied. Two lateral incisions—1 distal (7 cm, 11 cm from the lateral malleolus) and 1 proximal (3 cm, at the fibular head)—allow decompression of the anterior and lateral compartments under direct visualization. The superficial peroneal nerve is identified and protected throughout. Medially, a 7-cm incision is made 2 cm posterior to the tibial border to decompress the superficial and deep posterior compartments. Wound complications occur in 15% to 25% of cases but are mitigated by meticulous dissection and neurovascular protection.Discussion/Conclusion:Four-compartment fasciotomy remains the most reliable treatment for CECS refractory to conservative care. Success depends on comprehensive compartment release and precise identification of neurovascular structures to minimize complications. Background

Chronic exertional compartment syndrome (CECS) is a condition characterized by increased pressure within the muscle compartments of the lower leg during physical activity, which limits blood flow and causes pain, tightness, numbness, and paresthesia in the distal extremity. A trial of conservative treatment consisting of rest from the exacerbating activity, activity modification, and physical therapy focused on gait retraining is considered the first-line treatment. Surgical fasciotomy may be indicated in cases recalcitrant to conservative management.

The primary concern for this procedure is the identification and protection of the neurovascular structures found in the lower leg. In a 2-incision-based approach, the superficial peroneal nerve (SPN), which may have a variable location, should be identified and protected within the lateral incision. The common peroneal nerve typically travels posteriorly to anteriorly around the fibular neck before bifurcating into the SPN and deep peroneal nerve within the lateral compartment of the leg. The SPN then travels between the peroneus longus and brevis muscles before emerging deep-to-superficial through the crural fascia at a mean of 11.3 cm proximal to the tip of the lateral malleolus. 10 For a medially-based incision, care is taken superficially to protect the saphenous nerve and vein. Proximally, the saphenous nerve and vein are typically found deep to the sartorial fascia adjacent to the semitendinosus and gracilis tendons at their insertion on the tibia before emerging from beneath the fascia lata and coursing subcutaneously along the lower leg posteromedially to the tibia. 5 The posterior tibial neurovascular bundle must also be protected during release of the deep posterior compartment. Upon exposure of the deep posterior compartment of the leg, the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle is in proximity, typically between the tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus on the posterior surface of the tibia. 6

Contraindications

Diagnosis of CECS requires a careful history and physical examination and objective compartment testing in order to confirm the diagnosis and rule out other confounding neurologic or vascular causes of symptoms. Other relevant contraindications for surgery include an inadequate attempt at treatment with conservative modalities or uncontrolled medical comorbidities. 2

Technique Description

Patient Positioning

With this technique, the patient is positioned supine on a regular table with a thigh tourniquet.

Two, laterally-based incisions are marked out, centered between the fibula and crest of the tibia. The distal incision is 7 cm in length and is centered at a point 11 cm from the tip of the lateral malleolus. A 3-cm proximal, counter-incision is marked in line with the distal incision, beginning proximally at the level of the fibular head.

The lateral incision is performed first. Dissection is carried down until the SPN is identified, emerging through the fascia. The SPN is mobilized extrafascially as it travels distally. The intermuscular septum is then identified between the anterior and lateral compartments of the lower leg. Two 1-cm transverse fascial incisions are then made across the fascia of the anterior and lateral compartments in preparation for their fasciotomy.

To establish the prefascial and subfascial planes, long Metzenbaum scissors are advanced, with the tips closed and directed superficially, to the level of the desired fasciotomy. The scissors are then opened and slowly withdrawn. This is repeated proximally and distally for both the anterior and lateral compartments.

Next, with a retractor protecting the SPN posteriorly, fasciotomy of the anterior compartment is performed with the long Metzenbaum scissors pointed away from the intermuscular septum, extending distally, until reaching the extensor retinaculum. This is then carried out proximally to the level of the tibial tubercle. The proximal fasciotomy should extend only as far as can be performed under direct visualization. If necessary, the proximal counter-incision is made to allow for the fasciotomy to be safely performed under direct visualization.

Next, with the SPN carefully retracted anteriorly, a lateral compartment fasciotomy is performed in a similar fashion. This is performed distally to the level of the peroneal retinaculum and proximally to the level of the fibular neck with the tips of the scissors directed anteriorly. Again, the proximal counter-incision is utilized to allow the fascia to be released under direct visualization. Care is taken to avoid injury to the common peroneal nerve as it traverses from posterior to anterior at the level of the fibular neck.

At this point, attention is turned to the medial side of the leg. The tourniquet is deflated to facilitate identification of the saphenous vein and posterior tibial neurovascular bundle.

A single, 7-cm medially-based incision is made centered proximal to distal on the leg, 2 cm posterior to the posteromedial tibial border. Dissection is carried out to identify the fascia overlying the superficial posterior compartment. The saphenous vein and nerve are identified just posterior to the medial border of the tibia and carefully mobilized to allow their retraction anteriorly.

A 1-cm transverse incision is made over the superficial posterior compartment fascia. Again, long Metzenbaum scissors are used to establish the prefascial and subfascial planes proximally and distally. The fascia is then released proximally and distally, from the level of the tibial tubercle to the medial malleolus, respectively.

Attention is then turned to the deep posterior compartment release. The release is performed using a small key periosteal elevator to detach the soleal bridge (attachment of the soleus muscle fibers along the posteromedial aspect of the tibia) along the posteromedial tibia. 7 A finger is swept proximally to complete the release, ensuring appropriate decompression of the deep posterior compartment.

Once all compartments are decompressed, the wounds are copiously irrigated with saline. Hemostasis is confirmed, and subcutaneous tissues and skin are closed in a standard, layered fashion. and a soft, sterile dressing is applied.

Complications

Potential complications are listed here, but include hematoma, wound-healing complications, infection, deep vein thrombosis, neurovascular injury, and recurrent symptoms.

Pearls for successful outcomes in the anterior and lateral compartments include (1) proceeding with blunt subcutaneous dissection until the SPN is identified to avoid injury; (2) identifying and protecting the 1 or 2 branches of the SPN that may emerge from the crural fascia either anteriorly or posteriorly to the intermuscular septum at approximately 11.3 cm from the tip of the lateral malleolus; (3) pointing the Metzenbaum scissor tips superficially and away from the intermuscular septum during fasciotomy to avoid muscular or neurovascular injury; (4) performing a proximal counter incision to allow direct visualization to ensure full release of the anterolateral compartments; and (5) If the scissor tips fall off the fascia during release, performing a counter incision is preferable to safely ensure complete fasciotomy under direct visualization rather than continue blindly.

For the posterior compartments, (1) deflating the tourniquet before the medial incision improves visualization and facilitates protection of the saphenous vein and nerve; (2) identifying the tibial neurovascular bundle before release, usually located lateral to the flexor digitorum longus (FDL). The deep posterior compartment fascia is released medial to the FDL; and (3) detaching the soleus bridge proximally bluntly or with a periosteal key elevator to ensure complete release.

Pitfalls to avoid with this procedure include (1) delaying or missing a diagnosis due to a low index of suspicion with chronic exercise-related symptoms. Compartment testing is key if suggestive symptoms are present; (2) opening scissor tips too wide during fasciotomy risks injury to neurovascular structures; (3) missing a saphenous vein or posterior tibial artery injury may lead to hematoma formation in the posterior compartments; and (4) incompletely releasing the proximal deep posterior compartment is a common cause for recurrent symptoms.

Rehabilitation

Physical therapy (PT) focuses on active and passive range of motion of the ankle and on quadriceps and hip strengthening. During postoperative weeks 2 to 6, the patient is allowed unrestricted motion and may begin gait retraining and the use of a stationary bike. During weeks 6 to 8, PT focuses on progressing to symptom-free continuous aerobic activity, along with closed-chain lower-extremity exercises, gait drills, and plyometrics. Jogging begins at 8 weeks postoperatively, with further progression toward a return to sports activities. By 12 weeks, the patient may return to sports once able to perform without exertional pain or postactivity swelling.

Results and Discussion

Success rates for CECS are generally variable; however, 2 recent systematic reviews have found that surgical fasciotomy generally provides improved outcomes in appropriately indicated patients compared with conservative management alone.3,12

Turcotte et al 11 reported outcomes in a series of 25 patients (n = 21, 84%, received 4-compartment fasciotomies; n = 4, 16%, received 2-compartment fasciotomies) who underwent fasciotomy for exercise-induced compartment syndrome. 11 Of the included patients, 16 (64%) underwent bilateral fasciotomies. The authors reported that 24 (96%) of patients were able to return to function within the study period (mean, 3.2 years postoperatively). However, 6 (24%) patients underwent revision surgery, and 6 (24%) patients experienced postoperative wound complications.

Waterman et al 13 also used a military database to report outcomes after surgical fasciotomy for exertional compartment syndrome in 611 patients and 754 procedures. 13 44.7% of patients experienced symptom recurrence, and 27.7% were unable to return to full activity. Surgical complications occurred in 15.7% of patients, and 5.9% underwent revision surgery.

Despite encouraging outcomes with 4-compartment fasciotomy, single- or dual-compartment fasciotomies, although less comprehensive, may also provide clinically effective treatment when symptoms are restricted to specific compartments. 8 The available evidence suggests tailoring the extent of fasciotomy to documented compartment involvement rather than routinely performing 4-compartment release, while recognizing that incomplete release of symptomatic compartments increases recurrence risk.1,4

Collectively, outcomes highlight that although fasciotomy can provide functional improvement for appropriately indicated patients, meticulous surgical technique and a detailed understanding of the surgical anatomy are required to optimize postoperative outcomes, as complications—particularly symptom recurrence and hematoma—are not uncommonly reported in the literature.
