Associations Between Blood Heavy Metal Concentrations and Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy in the Japan Environment and Children's Study
Authors: Nobuhisa Morimoto, Hitomi Okubo, Shoji F Nakayama, The Japan Environment and Children's Study Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies reported inconsistent associations between blood heavy metal concentrations and the risk of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), potentially due to small sample sizes and variations in exposure levels, timing of blood sampling, and confounding factors. We investigated this association in Japanese women, a population with relatively low metal exposure, to provide a unique perspective in a low‐exposure setting.MethodsWe included 88 670 pregnant women, with a mean age of 31.2±5.1 years, enrolled in the Japan Environment and Children's Study. We examined the associations between concentrations of heavy metals (lead, cadmium, and mercury) in blood collected during the second or third trimester and HDP, using multiple logistic regression under a Bayesian framework, adjusting for potential effects of confounders including parity, body mass index, smoking, and diet quality.ResultsThe per‐woman incidence of HDP was 2703 (3.1%). The median blood concentrations of lead, cadmium, and mercury were 5.86 (interquartile range, 4.72–7.35), 0.664 (interquartile range, 0.496–0.906), and 3.62 (interquartile range, 2.53–5.17) ng/g, respectively. After adjusting for potential confounders, blood lead, cadmium, and mercury concentrations were positively associated with the odds of HDP. The median odds ratios for HDP in the highest vs lowest quartiles of blood metal concentrations were 1.58 (95% credible interval, 1.39–1.77) for lead, 1.30 (95% credible interval, 1.15–1.45) for cadmium, and 1.15 (95% credible interval, 1.03–1.28) for mercury.ConclusionsHigher blood lead, cadmium, and mercury concentrations were associated with HDP in Japanese pregnant women. Our findings suggest that low‐dose exposure to these heavy metals is an underrecognized but important risk factor for HDP, highlighting the need to develop measures to reduce heavy metal exposure.Keywords: heavy metal, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, lead, mercury, cadmium, pregnancySubject Categories: Epidemiology, Primary Prevention

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) are a major cause of maternal morbidity and death, accounting for 27 800 maternal deaths annually worldwide. 1 The categories of HDP include gestational hypertension, preeclampsia/eclampsia, chronic hypertension, and preeclampsia/eclampsia variants superimposed on chronic hypertension. 2 Numerous studies have shown the association between a history of HDP and an increased risk of future cardiovascular disease (CVD). 3 , 4 , 5 One large cohort study in the United States indicated an approximately 2‐fold increase in the risk of CVD for women with a history of HDP after a median follow‐up of >35 years. 3 In another large cohort study in the United Kingdom, a history of HDP was associated with an increased risk of coronary artery disease, heart failure, aortic stenosis, and mitral regurgitation. 5 Clinical guidelines for CVD prevention include a history of HDP as an important sex‐specific prognostic factor, 6 , 7 suggesting that identifying modifiable risk factors for HDP may help develop measures to reduce the CVD risk in women. 8

Major risk factors for HDP include a prior history of HDP, obesity, nulliparity, chronic hypertension, and other preexisting diseases. 8 Heavy metal exposure has also been linked to an increased risk of HDP. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Experimental studies indicated that some heavy metals, including lead, cadmium, and mercury, increased oxidative stress and decreased nitric oxide availability, leading to increased blood pressure. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Oxidative stress may induce cellular senescence, 17 which could inhibit angiogenesis and play a role in the pathogenesis of HDP. 18 Despite the suggestive evidence of adverse effects of heavy metals on HDP risk, to our knowledge, no major clinical guidelines have documented heavy metal exposure as a risk factor for HDP, 2 , 19 potentially due to a lack of consistent epidemiological findings. Some case–control and cohort studies reported the associations of higher blood levels of lead, cadmium, arsenic, and mercury with HDP, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 while other studies did not report such associations. 20 , 21 One recent prospective cohort study on 1560 Canadian pregnant women showed associations of elevated blood arsenic and lead concentrations with HDP, while blood cadmium, mercury, and manganese concentrations were not associated with HDP. 12 The inconsistent findings of these previous studies may be partly attributed to small sample sizes and differences in heavy metal exposure levels, the timing of blood collection during pregnancy, and potential confounders included in their analyses. 9 , 12

Studies on the associations between blood heavy metal levels and HDP have been scarce in Japan. Because exposure levels to certain heavy metals differed between Japan and other countries, 9 , 12 , 22 the associations between blood heavy metal levels and HDP in Japan may differ from those in previous studies. For example, blood lead levels in Japanese pregnant women were much lower than those of studies included in a meta‐analysis on the blood lead–HDP association 9 and those in Sweden and India, 23 , 24 but comparable to those in the United States and Canada. 12 , 25 In contrast, blood cadmium and mercury levels in Japan were higher than those in the United States, Canada, and Sweden. 12 , 23 , 26

Using data from a large prospective cohort of pregnant Japanese women, we aimed to investigate the associations between blood heavy metal concentrations and HDP, considering a wide range of potential confounders.

METHODS

Data are unsuitable for public deposition due to ethical restrictions and the legal framework of Japan. It is prohibited by the Act on the Protection of Personal Information (Act No. 57 of May 30, 2003, amendment on September 9, 2015) to publicly deposit the data containing personal information. Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects enforced by the Japan Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare also restricts the open sharing of the epidemiologic data. All inquiries about access to data should be sent to jecs-en@nies.go.jp. The person responsible for handling enquiries sent to this e‐mail address is Dr Shoji F. Nakayama, JECS Programme Office, National Institute for Environmental Studies.

Study Population

The JECS (Japan Environment and Children's Study) is an ongoing, nationally representative, government‐funded birth cohort study in Japan. 27 , 28 JECS recruited >100 000 pregnant mothers in 15 regional centers across Japan between January 2011 and March 2014. The JECS protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ministry of Environment's Institutional Review Board on Epidemiological Studies (No. 100910001) and the ethics committee of all participating institutions. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The data sets released in October 2019 (jecs‐ta‐20 190 930) and April 2021 (jecs‐qa‐20 210 401) were used for this study. Of the 103 057 pregnancies registered in the JECS, 95812 women had data on blood heavy metal concentrations. Of the 95 812 women, duplicate data from women with multiple registrations and those missing data on HDP were excluded, resulting in 90 394 women. For women with multiple registrations, data for the earliest registration were used. Of the 90 394 women, those with a history of hypertension or kidney disease (chronic glomerulonephritis or nephrotic syndrome) or missing data on these variables were excluded, resulting in a final sample size of 88 670 (Figure 1).

Outcome of Interest

The outcome of interest was HDP. In this study, HDP was defined as a physician diagnosis of HDP based on a questionnaire completed by obstetricians at the time of delivery or other medical staff through medical record transcripts. The questionnaire had questions regarding the presence of an HDP (yes or no) and the timing of HDP onset (expressed in weeks of gestation). Data on specific types of HDP, including gestational hypertension and preeclampsia, were not available. The definition of HDP mostly followed the definition outlined in the Japan Society for the Study of Hypertension in Pregnancy guideline in 2013: hypertension (blood pressure ≥140/90 mm Hg) with or without proteinuria (≥300 mg/24 h), appearing after 20 weeks of gestation but resolving up to 12 weeks postpartum. 29

Exposure of Interest

The main exposure of interest was blood concentrations of lead, cadmium, and mercury. Blood metal levels were determined using nonfasting blood samples collected during the second (14–27 weeks of gestation) or third trimester (≥28 weeks of gestation). Details of the blood collection methods and blood metal level measurements have been described previously. 22 Briefly, whole blood samples were collected into 3‐mL tubes containing sodium EDTA, sent to a central laboratory within 48 hours, and stored at −80 °C until chemical analysis. Blood concentrations of lead, cadmium, and mercury were measured by inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry on an Agilent 7700 device (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). The detection limits for these metals were obtained using the Currie's method as described previously. 22 The detection limits for lead, cadmium, and mercury were 0.129, 0.0234, and 0.0490 ng/g, respectively. The detection rates for the 3 elements were 100% in the JECS participants. The chemical analysis was performed in 3 different laboratories. Measurements in these laboratories demonstrated good agreement, with differences <10% relative SD.

Covariates

Information on participants' age at delivery, prepregnancy height and body weight, and parity was transcribed from medical records, maternal consent forms, and prenatal care records. For 9 participants missing data on age at delivery, age during the first trimester were used. Prepregnancy body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing body weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. Participants were categorized into 3 groups based on prepregnancy BMI using the World Health Organization Asian BMI classification as follows: underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), and overweight (≥23 kg/m2). 30 Parity was classified as nulliparity or multiparity. Data on demographics, lifestyle habits, and health histories were obtained by 2 self‐administered questionnaires completed during the first trimester (12–16 weeks of gestation) or the second or third trimester (22–28 weeks of gestation). Participants' educational attainment was classified into ≤12 or ≥13 years. Smoking habits in the second trimester were classified into never, past, or current smokers. Drinking habits in the second trimester were classified as never, past, or current drinker. Participants' physical activity levels before pregnancy were classified as low, moderate, or high, using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire short form, 31 , 32 , 33 which was embedded in the first‐trimester questionnaire. Information about dietary supplement use over the previous month was obtained from a questionnaire completed in the second trimester.

Information on habitual dietary intake over 1 year before the first trimester was obtained from a validated food frequency questionnaire. 34 , 35 The food frequency questionnaire asks participants about the consumption frequency and portion sizes of commonly eaten food items in Japan, dietary behaviors, and usual cooking methods. Daily intakes of energy, macronutrients, and micronutrients were estimated using an ad hoc computer algorithm based on the Standard Tables of Food Composition in Japan 2010. 36 Participants' diet quality was assessed by calculating the Balanced Diet Score (BDS) based on the Japanese Food Guide Spinning Top. 37 , 38 Details of the calculation of the BDS have been described previously and presented in Table S1. 38 Briefly, the BDS consists of the following 7 components: (1) grain dishes; (2) vegetable dishes; (3) fish and meat dishes; (4) milk; (5) fruits; (6) snacks, confectionery, and beverages; and (7) sodium from seasoning. A score was assigned to each component with a range of 0 to 10 based on the amount of intake relative to the recommended number of servings, which was determined on the basis of participants' age and physical activity levels. The amount of intake was expressed as per 2100 kcal for women with a moderate or high physical activity level and per 1800 kcal for those with a low physical activity level. Food and nutrient intakes were adjusted by total energy intake, using the density method. 39 The component scores were summed to calculate the total BDS score, with a range of 0 to 70. Higher scores indicated higher diet quality. In this study, women with implausible energy intake (<500 kcal/d or >4000 kcal/d) or missing data on energy intake, and those with invalid data on physical activity levels were considered missing data on the total BDS.

Statistical Analysis

Participants' characteristics were summarized by means±SDs for symmetrically distributed continuous variables, medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for asymmetrically distributed continuous variables, and numbers and percentages for categorical variables, respectively. Distributions of continuous variables were determined by visual inspection of histograms.

The correlation between blood concentrations of lead, cadmium, and mercury was evaluated by Spearman's rank correlation coefficients (r).

To examine the associations between blood metal concentrations and HDP, we performed multiple logistic regression analyses under a Bayesian framework, adjusted for potential confounders. We created 2 regression models, 1 adjusted for age at delivery and another adjusted for the following potential confounders based on previous literature and our assumed causal model using a directed acyclic graph 8 , 40 : age at delivery (continuous), prepregnancy BMI (continuous), educational attainment (≤12 years or ≥13 years), smoking habit (never, past, or current smoker), parity (nulliparous or multiparous), supplement use (yes or no), and the BDS (tertile groups) (Figure 2). We natural log‐transformed BMI and blood concentrations of lead, cadmium, and mercury and included these continuous variables in the regression model because their distributions were skewed. To evaluate the dose–response relationship, we also assessed the concentrations of metals in blood as quartiles (categorical variables). We performed these Bayesian logistic regression analyses using the “rstanarm” package (version 2.32.1) and the “bayestestR” package (version 0.13.2) in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). 41 Given the inconsistent evidence on the relationship between blood heavy metals and HDP, we used the default prior in the “rstanarm” package, which was intended to be weakly informative, to allow stable computation and provide moderate regularization. 42 , 43 We used the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method to estimate posterior distributions of odds ratios (ORs) with 95% credible intervals (95% CrIs) using highest‐density interval, probability of direction (PD), and percentage in region of practical equivalence (ROPE). We ran a total of 4 Markov Chain Monte Carlo chains, each chain with 2000 iterations and 1000 burn‐in (ie, 4000 posterior samples in total). We imputed covariates with missing data by multiple imputation by chained equations with random forest, using the “miceRanger” package (version 1.5.0) in R, assuming that data were missing at random. Because 10 796 (12.2%) eligible participants were missing at least 1 covariate, 15 imputed data sets were created. 47 We included the outcome variable (HDP), exposure variables (blood heavy metal concentrations), demographics, socioeconomic status, laboratory values, and lifestyle‐related variables in the imputation model. We used the majority vote method to generate the imputed data sets used for regression analyses. 48

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. First, we performed the main analyses in participants without any missing covariates (complete case analysis). Second, we repeated the main analyses after excluding participants with a history of HDP. Third, because a small proportion of cases in this study involved new‐onset hypertension that occurred after conception but before 20 weeks of gestation, we further repeated the analyses after excluding participants with HDP onset before 20 weeks of gestation, as well as those with missing data on the timing of HDP onset. Fourth, we repeated the analyses after including estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) during the first trimester in the regression model in a subset of participants with data on serum creatinine levels. The eGFR was calculated using a formula for Japanese individuals. 49 Finally, we performed the main analyses in participants with blood heavy metal levels measured during the second and third trimesters separately.

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) and R version 4.4.0 for Windows. 50

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics

This study included 88 670 women (mean age, 31.2±5.1 years) (Figure 1, Table 1). The per‐woman incidence of HDP was 3.1% (2703 women) (Table 1). Data on the timing of HDP onset were available for 2427 women (89.8% of the HDP cases), with 2307 women (85.4% of the HDP cases) having the onset at or after 20 weeks of gestation (Figure 1). As for BMI categories, 14 165 (16.0%) women were classified as underweight, 55 050 (62.1%) as normal weight, and 18 471 (20.8%) as overweight. Nearly 40% of women had an educational attainment of ≥13 years. A majority of women were multiparous. Approximately 60% of women never smoked cigarettes, while 4.5% of women were current smokers. The median total energy intake was 1690 (IQR, 1366–2109) kcal/d. The median BDS was 47.7 (IQR, 42.0–52.7). Participants excluded from the analysis due to missing data on blood heavy metal concentrations were not systematically different from those included in the analysis, except that a greater proportion of the excluded participants had missing data on most variables as compared with the included participants and that blood triglyceride levels were higher in excluded participants than included participants (Table S2). Participants excluded from the analysis due to missing data on HDP history and having a history of hypertension or kidney diseases (n=2269) tend to have higher serum triglyceride and hemoglobin A1c levels, higher BMI, and higher proportions of being overweight; be current smokers; and have dyslipidemia, gestational diabetes, anemia, and a history of HDP than those included in the study (n=88 670). Excluded women tend to have lower serum total cholesterol and albumin levels, education level, and physical activity level than included women.

Association Between Blood Metal Concentrations and HDP

Multiple logistic regression analyses demonstrated a positive association between blood lead concentrations and odds of HDP (age‐adjusted OR, 1.79 [95% CrI, 1.60–1.95]; PD, 100%; % in ROPE, 0%; fully adjusted OR, 1.57 [95% CrI, 1.40–1.73]; PD, 100%; % in ROPE, 0%) (Table 3). Analyses of the quartile groups of lead concentrations showed linearly increasing odds of HDP as lead concentrations increased (highest quartile versus lowest quartile: fully adjusted OR, 1.58 [95% CrI, 1.39–1.77]; PD, 100%; % in ROPE, 0%). Analyses for blood cadmium also showed a positive association between blood cadmium levels and odds of HDP (age‐adjusted OR, 1.28 [95% CrI, 1.17–1.39]; PD, 100%; % in ROPE, 0%; fully adjusted OR, 1.27 [95% CrI, 1.16–1.38]; PD, 100%; % in ROPE, 0%) (Table 3). The highest‐quartile group of blood cadmium had higher odds of HDP than the lowest quartile group (fully adjusted OR, 1.30 [95% CrI, 1.15–1.45]; PD, 100%; % in ROPE, 0%) (Table 3). Analyses for blood mercury also showed higher odds of HDP as blood mercury concentrations increased (age‐adjusted OR, 1.12 [95% CrI, 1.03–1.20]; PD, 99.8%; % in ROPE, 1.4%; fully adjusted OR, 1.09 [95% CrI, 1.02–1.17]; PD, 98.9%; % in ROPE, 5.5%) (Table 3); however, the association was not significant (% in ROPE >2.5%). The highest‐ and second‐highest‐quartile groups of blood mercury had higher odds of HDP than the lowest‐quartile group (fully adjusted OR, 1.17 [95% CrI, 1.05–1.31]; PD, 99.9%; % in ROPE, 0.7%; fully adjusted OR=1.15, 95% CrI=1.03–1.28, PD=99.7%, % in ROPE, 1.4%, respectively). The potential scale reduction factor for all regression analyses was <1.01, suggesting chain convergence.

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses excluding participants with missing covariate data, a history of HDP, or HDP onset before 20 weeks of gestation, along with those missing data on the timing of HDP onset, yielded results consistent with the main analyses (Tables S4 through S6). The results of sensitivity analyses that included eGFR were similar to those of the main analyses (Table S7). Sensitivity analyses found a positive association between blood mercury concentrations and HDP in women with second‐trimester blood samples, while blood mercury concentrations were not associated with HDP in those with third‐trimester blood samples (Tables S8 and S9).

DISCUSSION

Summary of Findings

In this large cohort of >80 000 pregnant women in Japan, we found associations between higher blood concentrations of lead, cadmium, and mercury and higher odds of HDP after accounting for potential confounders. Higher blood lead and cadmium levels were associated with HDP in a concentration‐dependent manner. These findings were largely unchanged after excluding women with a prior history of HDP. Our findings support that greater exposure to these heavy metals is an important risk factor for HDP.

Comparison With Previous Studies

Heavy Metal Exposure Levels in Pregnant Women in Japan

Blood lead concentrations in this cohort were generally low, as only 0.03% of women exceeded 5 μg/dL, the threshold above which necessitates further workup, including the search for lead exposure sources, as defined by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. 22 , 51 The median blood lead level in this cohort (0.63 μg/dL) 22 was lower than those in Sweden and India, 23 , 24 but comparable to those of pregnant women in Canada and the United States. 12 , 26 As for cadmium, the median blood concentration in this cohort (0.70 μg/L) 22 was higher than those in the United States, Canada, and Sweden, with geometric mean or median levels of 0.2 to 0.5 μg/L, 12 , 22 , 23 , 26 potentially due to higher rice consumption in Japan. 52 Regarding mercury, no women in this study had blood mercury concentrations >44 μg/L, the level equivalent to the tolerable methylmercury intake as determined by the Japan Food Safety Commission. 53 However, based on the German Human Biomonitoring Commission values, ≈30% of women had blood mercury levels between 5 and 15 μg/L, a range that cannot exclude potential adverse health effects. 22 , 54 The median blood mercury level in this cohort (3.83 μg/L) 22 was generally much higher than those in the United States, Canada, and Sweden with geometric mean or median levels of 0.4 to 1.2 μg/L, 12 , 23 , 26 but similar to those from China, potentially due to greater fish consumption in Asian countries. 22 , 55 , 56

Lead and HDP

Our analysis demonstrated a concentration‐dependent positive association between blood lead concentrations and HDP. Our results align with previous studies demonstrating the association between higher blood lead levels and HDP. 9 , 12 A meta‐analysis of 12 studies between 1940 and 2015 reported higher blood lead concentrations in women with HDP than those without HDP. 9 While the direct comparison of the meta‐analysis and our study is difficult due to the much higher median blood lead concentrations (1.32–60.20 μg/dL) as compared with our cohort (0.63 μg/dL), 22 a more recent study in Canada showed a similar median blood lead level to our study and an increased risk of HDP with increasing blood lead levels during the third trimester (risk ratio, 1.54 [95% CI, 1.06–2.22]), 12 consistent with our findings. With a large body of evidence on the association between blood lead levels and hypertension, 57 our findings suggest that higher blood lead concentration increases the risk of HDP.

Cadmium and HDP

We found an association between higher blood cadmium concentrations and HDP. However, previous studies on cadmium reported inconsistent results. 12 , 55 , 58 One nested case–control study in the United States showed a positive association between placental cadmium levels and preeclampsia (OR, 1.50 [95% CI, 1.10–2.20]). 58 In contrast, another nested case–control study in China found a lower cadmium level in blood collected before delivery in women with preeclampsia than controls. 55 One prospective cohort study on 1560 pregnant women in Canada showed a lack of associations of the first‐ and third‐trimester blood cadmium levels with gestational hypertension and preeclampsia. 12 The discrepancies between the Canadian study and our study may be partly attributed to lower blood cadmium concentrations in the Canadian study, which could have attenuated the association with HDP.

Mercury and HDP

Women in the higher‐quartile groups of blood mercury had increased odds of HDP than those in the lowest quartile. Our findings agree with some previous studies on this topic. 55 , 59 One nested case–control study in China found higher odds of preeclampsia in the highest tertile of blood mercury level as compared with the lowest tertile (OR, 1.60 [95% CI, 1.08–2.38]). 55 One birth cohort study in the United States showed a trend of increasing preeclampsia risk with higher mercury levels in red blood cells (risk ratio, 1.15 [95% CI, 0.98–1.36]). 59 One prospective cohort study in Egypt found a higher risk of HDP in dental staff with higher urinary mercury levels due to dental amalgams as compared with dental employees with lower urinary mercury levels. 11 In contrast, a prospective cohort study in Canada demonstrated a lack of association between the first‐ and third‐trimester blood mercury levels and HDP. 12 One reason for the different findings may be the timing of blood mercury measurements. Our sensitivity analyses showed a concentration‐dependent positive association between the second‐trimester blood mercury levels and HDP, while such an association was not observed for the third‐trimester blood mercury levels. Another potential reason may be the lower blood mercury concentrations in the Canadian study than in our cohort. The Chinese study, which showed a positive association between blood mercury levels and HDP, had a closer median blood mercury concentration to our study (median level 1.5 μg/L). 55 Given the inconsistent findings across different studies, further studies are warranted to elucidate the role of mercury exposure in HDP development.

Mechanism of the Association Between Blood Heavy Metal Concentrations and HDP

While the exact pathogenesis of HDP has been unclear, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired angiogenesis are considered to play important roles in the development of HDP. 60 One plausible mechanism that links heavy metal exposure and HDP is heavy metal–induced endothelial dysfunction. Previous studies showed that lead, cadmium, and mercury induced defective migration of endothelial cells and reduced nitric oxide availability, the hallmarks of endothelial dysfunction. 13 , 14 , 16 Another mechanism may relate to oxidative stress–induced cellular senescence, characterized by cell‐cycle arrest and inflammatory response. 15 , 17 Previous studies showed that lead, cadmium, and mercury could induce oxidative stress and DNA damage, 15 which can activate senescence signaling. 17 One study on mesenchymal stem cells derived from the subcutaneous adipose tissue of women with and without preeclampsia demonstrated the upregulated inflammatory response and senescence signaling in preeclampsia, which inhibited angiogenesis. 18 Altered angiogenesis could also lead to endothelial dysfunction. 60 Further mechanistic studies are warranted to elucidate how low‐dose exposure to heavy metals contributes to the development of HDP.

Implications

Our results have important implications for clinical practice and research. First, our findings suggest that low‐dose exposure to lead, cadmium, and mercury is an important risk factor for HDP that has been overlooked in clinical practice. Although only 0.03% of pregnant women in this study exceeded a blood lead concentration of 5 μg/dL, the cutoff that calls for action as determined by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 51 blood lead concentrations were linearly associated with HDP in a concentration‐dependent manner. As routine measurements of blood metal levels in pregnant women are not logistically feasible, our results underscore the need to identify major sources of heavy metal exposure and develop public health interventions to decrease the exposure. One previous study using this cohort found associations of smoking during pregnancy with blood cadmium, seafood intake with blood mercury, and nonalcoholic beverages with blood lead. 22 However, these associations were weak, suggesting the need for further studies to identify sources of metal exposure and guide future public health policy to decrease heavy metal exposure at the population level. As a decreasing trend of exposure to lead and cadmium partly explained the declining trend of hypertension and CVD death in the United States, 61 such efforts may be important in decreasing the incidence of HDP and mitigating the CVD burden.

Second, our findings suggest the possibility that lead and cadmium exposure may be one of the underlying mechanisms that explain the previously reported association between a history of HDP and a higher CVD risk. 3 , 4 , 5 This possibility is supported by previous evidence on the associations between greater exposure to lead and cadmium and higher CVD risk. 62 , 63 Further studies are warranted to investigate the role of exposure to these heavy metals in the development of HDP and CVD.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is the largest study on the associations between blood heavy metal levels and HDP. Most previous studies on this topic enrolled <2000 participants, 9 , 12 , 55 , 58 , 59 and they could be underpowered to detect associations between heavy metal exposure and HDP. Our large sample size likely improved the precision of our estimates. Second, the probabilities of direction for all 3 metals were >99%, which emphasizes the certainty of the effect existence. In addition, the percentage in ROPE, which reflects the proportion of the posterior distribution that falls in the range considered to be practically null, was <2.5%, providing strong evidence for the existence of the association between blood heavy metal levels and HDP. Third, the diagnosis of HDP was based on a questionnaire completed by obstetricians or medical record transcription of physician diagnosis of HDP, which likely reduced misclassification errors. 64 Previous studies indicated a low sensitivity of registry or diagnostic code–based diagnosis of HDP, partly due to the failure to identify mild cases of HDP. 64 Fourth, our cohort was assumed to be a representative sample of Japanese pregnant women, 28 suggesting that our results are broadly generalizable to pregnant women in Japan. Finally, the persistent associations between blood metal levels and HDP in our sensitivity analyses suggest the robustness of our findings.

We also acknowledge some limitations of this study. First, our findings may not be generalizable to non‐Japanese populations with higher heavy metal exposure levels, different socioeconomic and genetic backgrounds, dietary habits, and HDP incidence. For example, the per‐woman incidence of HDP in the United States was around 15%, which is much higher than that of Japan. 3 However, blood concentrations of some metals were comparable to those of other countries, 12 , 26 , 55 suggesting the relevance of our findings to pregnant women in those countries. Second, the timing of blood heavy metal measurements (the second or third trimester) and HDP onset could have overlapped. Thus, the temporality of the association was not completely ensured, raising the possibility of reverse causation. HDP could result in renal dysfunction, which may impair urinary heavy metal excretion and elevate blood heavy metal concentrations. 65 However, studies examining whether reduced renal function decreases urinary heavy metal excretion have been scarce. One previous study found a positive association between creatinine‐based eGFR and urinary cadmium level; however, cystatin C–based eGFR, a more accurate marker of renal function, was not associated with urinary cadmium level, suggesting that the association between creatinine‐based eGFR and urinary cadmium level was not due to renal function, but a biomarker‐specific effect. 66 Further longitudinal studies, preferably with prepregnancy blood heavy metal concentrations, are necessary to elucidate the temporal relationship between heavy metal exposure and HDP. Third, because the questionnaire for HDP diagnosis was completed at the time of delivery, the presence of hypertension and proteinuria could not be followed through 12 weeks after gestation. Fourth, while the definition of HDP in this study was similar to the definitions of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia in the International Society for the Study of Hypertension in Pregnancy guideline, 2 for approximately 5% of cases, the HDP onset was before 20 weeks of gestation, which corresponds to chronic hypertension in the International Society for the Study of Hypertension in Pregnancy guideline. However, our sensitivity analysis that excluded these women yielded similar results to those of the main analysis, suggesting the robustness of our findings. Finally, the observational nature of our study precluded us from drawing causal inferences on the relationship between blood metal concentrations and HDP. We could not exclude the effect of residual and unmeasured confounders, including genetic variability and iron status. Certain genetic variants associated with HDP, such as TRPC6, could play a role in renal glomerular function, 67 , 68 which may affect blood heavy metal levels. Iron deficiency could facilitate the intestinal absorption of lead and cadmium, 69 and iron deficiency could also increase oxidative stress, which may increase HDP risk. 70 We strove to account for nutritional status by including a diet quality measure and supplement use in our analyses. We also included other important potential confounders in our regression models, including BMI, smoking habits, and parity, which were not taken into consideration in some previous studies on this topic. 9 , 55 We expect that the direction of the associations would not materially change in the presence of unmeasured confounders.

CONCLUSIONS

In this large cohort of >80 000 pregnant women in Japan, we found positive associations of higher blood concentrations of lead, cadmium, and mercury with HDP. Our findings suggest that low‐dose exposure to these heavy metals is a pivotal risk factor for HDP, highlighting the need for measures to reduce heavy metal exposure to optimize cardiovascular health.
