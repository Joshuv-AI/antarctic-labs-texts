Direct cost of headache treatment in Benin, a West African country, in 2023
Authors: Mendinatou Agbetou, Toussaint Avolonto, Corine Houehanou, Oyéné Kossi, Thierry Adoukonou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeadache is a public health problem in Africa and is a significant cause of neurological consultations, imposing a heavy burden. Data on its actual economic burden are scarce in Africa. This study aims to evaluate the direct cost of headache management in 2023 and to identify factors associated with the high direct cost of headache management.MethodsThis cohort study with descriptive and analytical aims was conducted from June 15 to October 15, 2023, in the neurology unit of the teaching hospital of Borgou in Parakou, on 91 subjects with headaches, including migraine, tension type headache, trigeminal neuralgia, cluster headache, paroxysmal hemicrania and secondary headache. Any patient who had experienced headaches in the past 12 months, who was aged at least 18 years and who provided informed consent was included. The direct cost of headache management was defined as the sum of costs by consumption level category (consultation, diagnostic tests, treatments, transport, hospitalization costs, other nonmedical costs) and the monetary value of the main companion. This cost was expressed in Benin’s local currency (West African francs XOF), with a corresponding amount in euros. The direct cost of headache care was considered high when the mean monthly direct cost was more than 10% of the guaranteed minimum wage in Benin. Consumption level data were collected through an individual interview, supplemented by a review of medical records, notebooks, and receipts for headache management. Data entry was carried out via the KoboCollect application. Data analysis was performed using EpiInfo 7.2 software.ResultsA total of 91 participants were included, with 51 women (56.04%). The mean age was 38.91 ± 14.52 years. The monthly direct cost estimated one month after inclusion was high, calculated at XOF1,599,400 (€2,438.26), with an average of XOF17,575.82 ± 10,290.68 (€26.79 ± 15.69), representing 33.80% of the minimum wage in Benin. The global annual direct cost of headaches was XOF15,537,875 (€23,687.23) with a higher cost for tension type headaches at XOF6,077,530 (€9,265.09), whereas that of migraine was calculated at XOF4,603,970 (€7,018.67). The factors associated with high costs of headache management were a history of anxiety (p = 0.02), the presence of comorbidities (p = 0.005), the frequency of attacks (p = 0.048), and consumption related to psychiatric consultation (p = 0.02).ConclusionThe direct cost of managing headaches is high, with multiple associated factors. Strategies aimed at reducing this cost are urgently needed to optimize management and influence national public health policy for headache patients in Benin.Keywords: Direct cost, Migraine, Tension type headache, Secondary headache, Benin, West Africa

Introduction

Headache is one of the most common neurological symptoms and is a frequent reason for emergency calls and consultations [1]. Headaches are the third leading cause of disability worldwide [2], and over 50% of the adult population reports having experienced headaches in the past year [3]. The economic impact is very significant, and in addition to the economic costs to society, headaches are an enormous burden for sufferers. In America, this burden is estimated to cost at least US$100 million per million inhabitants every year [4], and $146 to $211 million are spent annually on headache imaging [5]. In Europe, the total cost of headaches is estimated at an average of €285 per person and is attributable mainly to indirect costs [6]. In another study, headaches cost €173 billion every year, and tension-type headache (TTH) in particular costs €21 billion a year, behind the cost of migraine estimated at €27 billion [7]. The direct cost of migraine-related healthcare has been the most extensively studied, estimated at $1 billion per year in the United States [8], and the total direct cost attributable to migraine treatment amounts to €242 million in France [9]. In an Italian center, the average annual expenditure per patient was €1482, mainly due to medications, which represented 86.8% of the global cost, and the annual direct cost of chronic migraine was 4.8-fold greater than that of episodic migraine [10]. In a context where there is no universal health insurance system and all healthcare costs are borne by patients, the economic burden of headache is steadily increasing in Africa, even though it has not yet been measured. In Ethiopia, all headache types are associated with impairments in quality of life, and across the population aged 18–65 years, disability from headache is 1.4%, with 1.6% of workdays lost, half of which are from migraine [11]. In Benin, the prevalence of all headache varies from 63.49% to 72.9% [12, 13], with a substantial burden in terms of lost health, as reported in other countries [14–16]. This study aimed to investigate the direct cost of headache management for northern Benin patients in 2023.

Methods

Study design

This was a prospective cohort study involving all patients living in Parakou suffering from headaches who were followed in the neurology unit of Borgou Departmental University Hospital (CHUD Borgou). This hospital is an urban university hospital classified as a second-line hospital in Benin’s healthcare system and located in the town of Parakou, approximately 450 km from the economic capital Cotonou, in the Republic of Benin.

Ethical approval

The protocol was approved by the Local Ethics Committee for Biomedical Research of the University of Parakou (CLERB-UP) under number 189/CLERB-UP/P/SP/SA.

All participants were informed of the nature and purpose of the study and provided written consent before enrollment. The anonymity and confidentiality of the information collected were respected in accordance with data protection laws. Necessary authorizations from administrative authorities were obtained, and the study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki [17].

Sampling and recruitment

The study was conducted from June 15 to October 15, 2023. Systematic and exhaustive recruitment of all patients who had consulted the neurology unit of the CHUD Borgou in Parakou for headaches and who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to take part in the study was carried out. These patients were identified from the department’s consultation records and then invited to participate.

The inclusion criteria were to be at least 18 years old and to have suffered from headaches at least in the last 12 months. Patients with no records of headache management costs were excluded. Research costs were also excluded, as they were not considered to be related to headaches. Costs related to informal care or indirect costs related to lost production due to absenteeism, presenteeism, or early retirement due to headaches were excluded.

Data were collected via face-to-face interviews with patients using a questionnaire pre-tested on five individuals not included in this study. Patients were seen twice, on day 0 (D0) at the time of the inclusion visit and one month (M1) after inclusion. During the first interview at D0, they were asked about expenses related to headaches over the previous 12 months prior to their inclusion, which constituted the retrospective part. During the second interview at M1, data on expenses from the time of inclusion until M1 were collected, which constituted the prospective part.

Diagnosis

Primary headache (migraine, TTH, trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias, other primary headache disorders), secondary headache, and other headaches were screened and diagnosed based on the ICHD-3 criteria [18].

Variables

The direct cost of headache management was expressed in West African francs (XOF), Benin’s local currency, with a corresponding amount in euros. In October 2023, €1 = XOF655.96. This direct cost was the sum of the mean costs by category of consumption level (consultation, diagnostic tests, treatments, transport, costs during hospitalization, other nonmedical costs) and the monetary value linked to the main companion. The main companion is the person who brings the patient to the hospital for treatment (this may be a parent, neighbor, relative, etc.). The costs calculated in this case are those incurred by the companion’s travel. A bottom-up approach was used [19].

The mean monthly direct cost of headache was calculated after the sum of the mean costs from inclusion at D0 to M1. The direct cost was considered high if the mean monthly direct cost was more than 10% of the guaranteed minimum wage of the Benin Republic, which is XOF52000 (i.e., 10% of €79.27). We considered a threshold of 10% to define high cost based on the low purchasing power of the population and the low minimum wage, as a loss of 10% of purchasing power in this context is significant and could have a real impact on patient follow-up care as well as on patients’ daily lives.

The independent variables were sociodemographic, economic, medical history (anxiety, depression, epilepsy, asthma, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and others), clinical characteristics of headache, and treatment.

Statistical analysis

Once the data had been processed, they were entered into the KoboCollect application. Data analysis was carried out via EpiInfo 7.2 software. Tables and graphs were created via Excel 2013 and Word 2013 for the report. Categorical variables are presented as headcounts and percentages with confidence intervals. Quantitative variables are presented as the means, standard deviations, medians, and quartiles as appropriate. Means were compared via Student’s t test, and proportions were compared via Fisher’s exact test (for categorical variables with small counts) and the chi-square test. The annual direct cost was calculated by averaging the costs based on the last 12 months prior to the survey and on information from D0 to M1 (the monthly direct cost was annualized by multiplying by twelve). Factors associated with the high direct cost of headache management were identified via a top-down multivariate logistic regression. Firstly, we have done univariate logistic regression to select independent variables for the initial model of multivariate analysis (using a p-value of 0.2 threshold). We obtained the final model by a backward stepwise procedure (using a p-value threshold of 0.05). Crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) were calculated.

Discussion

The direct cost of headaches is often underestimated according to data in the literature [20]. However, the handicap associated with headaches is so great that even the least affected people, to improve their quality of life, are prepared to pay for effective treatment if available [21]. In Ethiopia, the willingness to pay for headaches per month ranges from USD 1.57 for migraine to USD 1.32 for tension headache [11]. The average monthly direct cost of headache at CHUD Borgou in 2023 was high, more than 33% or 1/3 of the minimum wage in the Benin Republic. This high cost represents a real economic burden not only for patients but also for society. The cost of headaches at CHUD Borgou, an urban university hospital classified as a second-line hospital in Benin’s healthcare system, may be lower than that of the primary-level national referral hospital, or much higher compared to treatment costs in tertiary healthcare facilities in Benin. This is due to the decreasing technical facilities, quality of human resources and availability of financial resources from the first to the third level. These high costs could also be related to the method used to recruit participants, as they were recruited in a university hospital setting, which is a referral center in northern Benin. This led to the exclusion of less serious patients who are treated in other peripheral centers or who have self-medicated. Very few data in the African literature address the direct cost of headache in general, thus preventing comparisons with other regions of the continent. In this study, diagnostic tests were the first level of consumption, whereas in Malawi, higher costs of transport were associated with a higher probability of missing visits in the HIV-positive population with headaches, especially in rural areas [22]. Furthermore, the fact that TTH had the highest direct cost in our study is related to the higher frequency of these headaches in our context. In contrast, in Europe, the direct cost is greater for migraines than for TTH. The average annual cost of migraine was €1,222 with a 7% direct cost, i.e., €85.54 and €303 with an 8% direct cost, or €24.24 in TTH [23]. The majority of studies in Europe have dealt with migraine. In France, the average expenditure per patient suffering from headaches is approximately €220 per year [24], and the author reported annual direct costs of €128 per individual suffering from migraine [25]. In Iran, people suffering from headaches linked to overuse of medication spend an average of $104.4 on medical services and $132 on nonmedical services [26]. The cost of headaches is lower in Europe than in our study, and in most of these countries, this cost is covered by national health insurance. In Italy, the cost of chronic to episodic migraine is reduced by €533 per month, linked to an 80% reduction in indirect costs, particularly lost productive time [27]. In the USA, Messali et al., in a direct and indirect cost study of migraine, reported that the average annual cost of chronic migraine ($8,243) was 3 times greater than the cost of episodic migraine ($2,649) [28]. The most common methods of consumption were diagnostic tests (48.17%), medical treatment (32.63%), and consultations (12.41%). These results have been reported by others, who noted over 40% of paraclinical investigations [5]. Diagnostic uncertainty in primary headaches seen as a first-line treatment by general practitioners in our context leads to a series of often unnecessary diagnostic tests, justifying this increase in direct costs. However, in some studies, this high cost is due mainly to hospitalization (44% of cases) [24] or to medications [10]. According to the multivariate analysis, the factors associated with the high cost of headache management were a past history of anxiety, multiple comorbidities, the frequency of attacks and psychiatric consultation. Elston Lafata et al., in a study of medical care utilization and costs associated with migraine, reported that the presence of psychiatric symptoms, prophylactic medication use and chronic comorbidities were associated with healthcare utilization and costs [29]. In fact, addressing these associated factors improves headaches but requires additional costs. In this study we excluded indirect costs to avoid bias in the calculations. This limitation impacts the scope of the study as we cannot draw conclusions on the overall burden of headaches in our setting. Additionally, the small sample size may limit the detection of significant associations, especially for less common types of headaches, thus reducing the generalizability of the results. The absence of universal health insurance in Benin results in limited access to quality healthcare, specialist resources, paraclinical assessments, and drug treatments for most patients. This leads to inappropriate self-medication and delays in seeking medical advice, contributing to high morbidity from headaches and the need for referral to secondary or primary care, which requires more financial resources for treatment.

The high cost of treating headaches in our environment has significant public health implications and necessitates strong actions such as training healthcare professionals, implementing headache education programs for patients and professionals, integrating headache care into primary healthcare through the country’s non-communicable disease control program, and advocating for the availability of specific and subsidized medications.

Conclusion

The direct cost of headache management at CHUD Borgou in 2023 is high. The factors associated with this high cost are modifiable and linked to comorbidities, the frequency of attacks and psychiatric consultation. However, although limited, this study provides an idea of what headache patients spend in Benin. The impact of our study primarily lies in public health, calling for advocacy for standardized care and its coverage by health insurance in Benin. A better understanding of the direct costs of headache may help improve decision-making in the development of national public health policies on headache management and ensure adequate access to healthcare and treatment.

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first in sub-Saharan Africa and fills an information gap concerning the economic burden of headache in this region. The information gathered was based on self-reports, medical records, health diaries and patient receipts related to headache management. In the retrospective part, the information bias linked to the recall of healthcare expenditure over a period longer than the previous three months was minimized by estimating the overall cost, on an annual basis using recent information collected prospectively over one month. To limit memory bias beyond self-reporting, we collected the receipts that were still available. As data archiving in our healthcare system in Benin is not centralized, it was unrealistic to visit all healthcare facilities (health centers, pharmacies, etc.) to verify these receipts and data. The information on the receipt was considered authoritative. Difficulties in attributing headache-specific costs to patients with other health problems or comorbidities may have minimized the estimated direct costs. Similarly, the low power of the study limits the generalizability of the results.
