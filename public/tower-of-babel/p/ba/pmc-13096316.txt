Optimization of ultra-low volume spray for multi-drone based on wind sensitivity
Authors: Dachuan Zheng, Bing Wang, Yuzhe Lin, Weijie Shi, Peishun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Driven by the critical demand for efficient and compliant aerial operations in urban public health—particularly for vector-borne disease control—this study proposes a wind-sensitive trajectory optimization framework for multi-drone ultra-low volume (ULV) spraying. By integrating a Wind-Induced Stretched Deposition Footprint (WISDF) model with pesticide efficacy decay, we construct a unified objective that maximizes coverage and deposition uniformity while minimizing overspray risk and energy consumption. To tackle the high-dimensional constrained optimization, we develop an enhanced Collaborative Grey Wolf Optimization (C-GWO+) algorithm incorporating Opposition-Based Learning initialization, a cooperative sub-swarm mechanism, and a stagnation reset strategy to strengthen global exploration and mitigate premature convergence. Comprehensive experiments in complex wind fields show that C-GWO+ achieves superior solution quality, outperforming state-of-the-art meta-heuristics such as PSO and SSA by 5.7% and 16.3% in comprehensive fitness, respectively. From an engineering perspective, compared to the Lawnmower baseline, C-GWO+ reduces the operational energy proxy by approximately 43% while ensuring superior deposition consistency. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests against standard GWO confirm statistically significant improvements (p < 0.001) in coverage rate, uniformity, and safety compliance (overspray control), providing a robust and low-carbon solution for precise urban epidemic prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42125-7.

Introduction

The prevention and control of urban vector-borne diseases is transitioning from traditional “manual backpack spraying/vehicle-mounted spraying” to a routine operational model characterized by “unmanned and refined” practices. Manual methods present safety risks such as low efficiency, uneven coverage, and prolonged exposure of personnel to chemical environments. In contrast, multi-drone collaborative spraying is expected to reduce personnel exposure and chemical contact risks while improving coverage consistency, operational traceability, and scheduling flexibility. Industry practices are advancing rapidly: the position paper of the American Association for Mosquito Control1 indicates that approximately 56% of its member institutions are already using drones, with 32% planning deployment, covering tasks such as space-retention spraying and monitoring. At the public institution level, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) outlines operational objectives and population safety considerations in its Integrated Mosquito Management (IMM) and aerial spraying guidelines2,3. The World Health Organization (WHO) further emphasizes “localization, traceability, quality control, and safety” in its 2024–2025 updated guidelines on spraying and malaria control4,5, providing a clear framework for the compliance boundaries and reproducibility of drone spraying.

The distribution of pesticide residue deposition is influenced by both flight parameters (altitude, speed, spray volume and nozzle configuration) and environmental factors (wind direction, wind speed, weather conditions, terrain features, etc.), directly affecting coverage compliance within the target area and overspray risks beyond buffer zones. To analyze the impact of planning strategies on residue deposition within limited computational resources and data constraints, this study employs a simplified model combining wind-aligned anisotropic Gaussian kernels with pesticide efficacy time decay. This model characterizes deposition patterns and their temporal decay processes while quantitatively measuring buffer zone overspray, providing computable proxy targets for subsequent optimization.

In terms of technical approaches, multiple methods have been proposed for multi-drone coverage planning, zone load balancing, and online re-planning6. These include reducing trajectory conflicts and balancing loads through grid or area decomposition7, rapid re-planning based on burst constraints during execution8, and wind-sensitive planning strategies that incorporate wind field into the cost function in other task scenarios9. However, in the context of public health spraying—a high-compliance, high-safety requirement application—practical methods that can explicitly model wind-induced deposition patterns and temporal attenuation, while driving multi-drone collaborative planning with a unified four-dimensional scoring system of “coverage—uniformity—buffer overspray—energy consumption,” remain limited. A key reason is that spatially varying wind stretches the deposition footprint and fragments the objective into multiple locally optimal “spray corridors”, making the feasible search landscape highly non-convex and multi-modal.

To address the aforementioned requirements, this paper proposes a wind-sensitive multi-drone ULV spray joint optimization method for urban epidemic prevention scenarios. The operational effectiveness is characterized by “WISDF + time decay” integrating coverage, uniformity, buffer zone overspray, and energy consumption into a unified objective function. Hard constraints are applied to shield prohibited spray zones, while soft penalties are imposed on buffer zone overspray beyond 30 m. At the algorithmic level, Cooperative Grey Wolf Optimization (C-GWO⁺) is employed to jointly solve multi-drone trajectories and spray switches, with comparative analysis conducted against Lawnmower Baseline and traditional GWO. In a typical 180 × 120 m operation scenario with 5 m resolution, the threshold θ is set at the 50th percentile of snake-shaped deposition in the target area. Experimental results demonstrate that C-GWO⁺ stably improves coverage and deposition uniformity while significantly reducing buffer zone overspray and energy consumption proxies. Related work

The research framework revolves around the task spectrum of “multi-drone coverage—spray deposition—compliance constraints,” with existing studies primarily categorized into three main lines: First, evaluating spray deposition/drift physics to establish metrics for coverage, uniformity, and overspray. Second, regional decomposition and coverage planning for multi-drone systems. Third, explicitly incorporating environmental factors (particularly wind fields) into cost functions or constraints, combined with heuristic/evolutionary algorithms for large-scale implementation. Recent field and semi-physical studies on spray deposition and drift evaluation have demonstrated that under identical working heights, different nozzle configurations exhibit significant variations in effective spray width and downwind deposition. Comparative experiments between agricultural and lawn scenarios further reveal that changes in working height markedly alter drift patterns and uniformity. These findings provide direct evidence for adopting the “coverage rate + uniformity + overspray rate” metric system in public health contexts10,11. Meanwhile, mechanistic studies incorporating wind speed, temperature, humidity, and downwash airflow into experiments or simulations offer lightweight approximation and calibration approaches for planning-level applications12.

In multi-drone coverage and area decomposition, the classical DARP (Divide Areas Algorithm) reduces conflicts and improves parallel efficiency by decomposing global coverage into non-overlapping subdomains13. Building on this, Ni et al. integrated DARP with deep reinforcement learning to achieve preprocessing and load balancing for complex maps7. Parallel to DARP, Voronoi-based area partitioning breaks down multi-drone coverage into smaller units, providing geometric guarantees for convergence and safety8,14,15. These methods establish a solid foundation for our algorithmic strategy of “spatial grouping/cooperation”. Consistent with this approach, this paper employs a meta-heuristic (C-GWO+) for multi-drone coordination at the solution layer, reducing dimension explosion and conflict probability caused by multi-drone coupling through rotational intra-group updates.

The implementation of online re-planning and burst constraint response has progressed from proof-of-concept to system-level realization6. In multi-drone coverage systems, in-flight re-planning has been implemented, dynamically updating flight paths and resource allocation based on real-time information. Subsequent work has begun to incorporate operational constraints such as communication and energy consumption, ensuring the feasibility and efficiency of re-planning in practical applications. These system implementations provide a benchmark for our engineering roadmap to extend the model to online re-planning and burst constraint response in the future.

In wind farm planning paradigms, bridge inspection and general wind farm navigation studies have demonstrated that incorporating wind speed/direction directly into cost functions or safety constraints can significantly improve feasibility and energy efficiency. Notably, geometric shortest paths may prove impractical under strong wind conditions9,16. Building on this approach, we elevate “WISDF + efficacy time decay” as the primary objective function. Unlike conventional methods focusing solely on shortest path or energy minimization, this study incorporates “wind-induced stretching of effective deposition areas’ compliance area and uniformity” into the main objective, while explicitly penalizing overspray mass beyond buffer zones. This dual objective directly guides the planning solution to balance compliance and drug efficacy.

Heuristic/evolutionary computing and GWO family methods have seen increasing application in UAV path/coverage optimization. Recently, emerging bio-inspired meta-heuristics such as the Snow Geese Algorithm (SGA)17, Sparrow Search Algorithm (SSA)18, and Gannet Optimization Algorithm (GOA)19 have also demonstrated significant potential in solving complex engineering optimization problems. In 2024, research improved GWO for UAV path planning by refining trajectory evaluation functions, convergence factors, and position updates, demonstrating convergence/performance advantages20. In 2025, QGWO emerged through Q-learning integration to enhance convergence speed and path quality21, while hybrid algorithms combining GWO with Chaos Game Optimization were developed to improve diversity and escape local optima22. These advancements collectively demonstrate that engineering modifications centered on GWO are both feasible and effective. In this study, we adopt Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO) as the solution framework, introducing two enhancements:

(i)OBL initialization to improve early exploration quality;(ii)cooperative rotation updates to mitigate multi-agent dimensionality and coupling, supplemented by stagnation reset to enhance local escape capability.

Finally, at the level of public health and compliance frameworks, the CDC’s Integrated Mosquito Management (IMM) and aerial spraying guidelines, along with the WHO’s 2024–2025 operational manual and malaria guidelines, provide actionable standards and boundaries for “operational time windows, personnel safety, record traceability, and quality control”2–5. This article has achieved: a hard ban zone + a soft penalty for overspray beyond the 30 m buffer zone, which effectively meets public health requirements.

Results

To evaluate the performance of the proposed framework, we conducted comparative simulations under complex wind fields. Before detailing the experimental outcomes, it is briefly noted that our method (C-GWO+) integrates an opposition-based learning initialization and a cooperative sub-swarm mechanism to optimize the trajectory and spray actions. The detailed mathematical modeling and algorithmic implementation are provided in the Methods section at the end of this paper. In the following section, we analyze the performance of C-GWO+ against the baseline methods in terms of coverage, uniformity, and compliance.

Conclusion 2 (Comparison with Engineering Baseline): Compared to the deterministic Lawnmower baseline, the proposed C-GWO+ demonstrates a fundamental advantage in operational efficiency and deposition quality. As shown in Table 2, although the Lawnmower strategy achieves a higher raw coverage rate (0.4126), it does so at the cost of extremely poor uniformity (0.3866 vs. 0.818) and prohibitive energy consumption (17,976.73 vs. 10,204.43). This indicates that the Lawnmower method relies on “brute-force” path redundancy, resulting in uneven dosing and unsustainable flight durations. In contrast, C-GWO+ achieves high-quality uniform deposition with substantially lower energy cost than Lawnmower, while maintaining energy comparable to GWO.

Although this overspray rate is higher than the conservative Lawnmower baseline, it represents a deliberate trade-off to maximize Coverage Effectiveness. The Lawnmower pattern strictly avoids boundaries to prevent drift, which inevitably results in significant ‘under-spray’ gaps at the edges. In public health applications, missed areas (under-spray) pose a much higher risk than slight buffer incursions, as untreated pockets can become breeding grounds. Thus, our algorithm prioritizes eliminating these coverage gaps over absolute drift minimization.

Discussion

In the simulation scenario of urban public health spray, we directly incorporated “WISDF + drug efficacy time decay” into the target, and solved it with the four-dimensional indicators of “coverage attainment—uniformity—overspray—energy consumption.”

Statistical results from 30 independent runs (Table 2) confirm that the proposed C-GWO+ achieved significant and robust gains compared to traditional GWO. Specifically, the mean coverage increased from 0.337 to 0.370 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p<0.001$$\end{document}), and uniformity improved from 0.777 to 0.818 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p<0.001$$\end{document}), validating that the collaborative grouping mechanism effectively prevents local optima. The reduction in overspray rate was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Although strict boundary penalties kept both values low, C-GWO+ maintained a lower mean value (0.005 → 0.003). Meanwhile, the energy consumption proxy further declined (10351.71 → 10204.43).

Compared with the deterministic engineering baseline Lawnmower, C-GWO+ completed the operation with significantly lower energy consumption (path length reduced by approx. 43% relative to the dense serpentine path). However, the overspray was slightly higher than the conservative Lawnmower baseline (0.003 vs. 0.00277), reflecting a deliberate trade-off between “maximizing area above the threshold” and “buffer zone conservatism.”

The reported coverage of 37% reflects the fraction of the target zone where time-averaged deposition exceeds the efficacy threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} under a single short-duration mission (10–15 min) with 3 UAVs and 6 waypoints per aircraft. This metric should be interpreted in the context of operational deployment rather than as a standalone measure of system adequacy. In practice, urban vector control campaigns operate through repeated sorties: after a single pass, the C-GWO+ plan identifies the highest-priority spray corridors aligned with wind geometry, and subsequent missions—either immediate re-flights or scheduled rotations—progressively extend coverage to remaining areas. The framework’s rapid convergence (~ 15 s per run) makes such multi-pass planning computationally feasible. Furthermore, the 37% figure is primarily constrained by the current waypoint budget; preliminary experiments with 10 waypoints per UAV showed that coverage increases to approximately 51%, indicating that coverage scales effectively with planning resolution. The key contribution of this work is not absolute coverage maximization in a single pass, but rather the quality of the covered area—achieving 0.818 uniformity with minimal overspray—which is critical for avoiding both under-dosed breeding grounds and regulatory violations.

While simplifying assumptions (e.g., quasi-static wind fields) were adopted for computational efficiency and real-time planning, the proposed framework is designed to be extensible to dynamic scenarios. The current assumption of spatial and temporal wind field stability is reasonable for short-duration missions (10–15 min) in stable atmospheric conditions (e.g., early morning or evening operations) and small operational areas (~ 180 × 120 m). For scenarios with higher wind variability, the C-GWO+ algorithm’s rapid convergence (~ 15 s per run) enables potential integration into Model Predictive Control (MPC) frameworks for periodic re-planning. Future work will explore dynamic CFD model integration and online learning mechanisms to adapt to time-varying wind fields.

Detailed considerations on wind field assumptions

In densely built urban environments, wind fields are highly turbulent due to building-induced flow separation, recirculation zones, and urban canyon channeling effects. However, for operational planning at the trajectory level (as opposed to droplet-level CFD analysis), the WISDF model provides a pragmatic abstraction that captures the dominant wind-induced deposition bias while remaining computationally tractable. At the planning stage, Gaussian plume-based deposition approximations have been widely adopted as computationally tractable surrogates for trajectory-level coverage estimation. While full CFD validation is beyond the scope of this study, the WISDF model’s core assumption—that wind stretches the deposition footprint along an elliptical Gaussian profile—is consistent with the field-measured deposition patterns reported by Byers et al.10 and Koo et al.11, who observed systematic downwind elongation and cross-wind narrowing of spray swaths under constant wind conditions. The model’s parameterization (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}^{0}=1.5m$$\end{document}, wind-stretching coefficient 0.2) is calibrated to these empirical observations rather than derived from first principles.

The three proposed extension strategies offer a pathway to dynamic scenarios with different implementation timelines. Segmented re-planning divides missions into 3–5 min windows, with trajectory re-optimization at each boundary using updated anemometer data—leveraging C-GWO+’s 15-s convergence, this approach incurs only ~ 45 s total overhead for a typical 15-min mission. Model Predictive Control frameworks can further enhance this by integrating short-term wind forecasting (5–10 min predictions from CFD-based nowcasting or data-driven models) for proactive adaptation. For long-term development, online learning mechanisms could adapt WISDF parameters in real-time based on deposition feedback from ground-based detection cards or UV fluorescence imaging sensors, enabling self-calibration and robustness to model mismatch.

Limitation and future validation

A principal limitation of the current study is that all experiments were conducted under a spatially uniform, temporally static wind field. Although the quasi-static assumption is defensible for short-duration missions under stable atmospheric conditions, it has not been empirically validated against dynamic scenarios where wind direction shifts mid-mission. Specifically, we have not simulated cases such as wind rotation (e.g., 30° shift every 3–5 min) or spatially heterogeneous urban canyon flows. The proposed MPC-based re-planning strategy and the algorithm’s 15-s convergence time suggest that periodic trajectory updates are computationally feasible, but this claim remains untested. Future work will prioritize: (i) a dynamic wind scenario benchmark where wind direction rotates at fixed intervals, quantifying coverage degradation relative to the static baseline; (ii) integration with mesoscale wind nowcasting models for proactive re-planning; and (iii) wind tunnel validation of WISDF predictions under controlled turbulent conditions. Until such validation is completed, the framework should be deployed under the operational wind stability criteria specified by CDC aerial spraying guidelines2,3(wind speed < 5 m/s, direction variability < ± 20°).

Regarding the energy model, while wind-induced drag forces are not explicitly modeled in the current energy proxy, the multi-objective fitness function implicitly discourages prolonged upwind flight: upwind trajectories yield poorer deposition coverage (due to reduced effective spray width) and are therefore penalized through the coverage and uniformity objectives. This implicit mechanism naturally favors downwind-aligned paths that coincide with lower energy consumption. Future refinements will incorporate explicit wind-relative velocity terms (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E=\sum_{i}lengt{h}_{i}\times(1+\gamma\cdot\mathrm{cos}({\theta}_{wind,i}))$$\end{document}), where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\theta}_{wind}$$\end{document}, i is the angle between path segment i and wind direction) for enhanced physical accuracy. Additionally, while current WISDF parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({\sigma}_{x},{\sigma}_{y})$$\end{document} and decay constant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document}) are calibrated from literature data10,11,26,27, planned validation efforts include wind tunnel experiments to verify WISDF predictions under controlled conditions, small-scale field trials (50 × 50 m test areas) with ground sampling to assess coverage accuracy, and operational deployment partnerships with local vector control agencies.

Wind-induced stretched deposition footprint (WISDF) and efficacy time decay

We model the instantaneous spray deposition using a Wind-Induced Stretched Deposition Footprint (WISDF), i.e., an elliptical Gaussian kernel whose covariance matrix is stretched along the wind direction.

The operational efficacy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{C}\mathrm{(}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{,}t)$$\end{document}is characterized by deposition density per unit area. A two-dimensional kernel function, stretched along the wind direction, is employed to approximate instantaneous deposition, with consideration given to the exponential decay of drug efficacy over time. While Lagrangian particle tracking offers higher fidelity, it is computationally prohibitive for iterative multi-drone optimization. Therefore, we model the wind effect as a geometric stretching of the covariance matrix along the wind vector.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\int}_{{t}_{0}}^{t}{r}_{i}\left(\tau\right)K(x-{p}_{i}(\tau);w(\tau),{h}_{i}(\tau\left)\right){e}^{-\frac{t-\tau}{{\tau}_{1/2}}}d\tau$$\end{document}

The spatial distribution is modeled using an elliptic Gaussian kernel:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K({\Delta}x;w,h)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\left|{\Sigma}\right|}}\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2}{\Delta}{x}^{T}{{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\Delta}x\right)$$\end{document}

The covariance matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Sigma}$$\end{document}is aligned with the wind direction and defined as a diagonal matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Sigma}=diag({\sigma}_{\perp}^{2},{\sigma}_{\parallel}^{2})$$\end{document}. The diffusion coefficients are empirically modeled as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{\parallel}={\sigma}_{0}+{\alpha}_{w}\left|\right|w\left|\right|+{\alpha}_{h}h,\hspace{1em}{\sigma}_{\perp}={\sigma}_{0}+{\beta}_{h}h$$\end{document}

These coefficients are set according to actual field conditions. If finer granularity for parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{0},{\alpha}_{w},{\alpha}_{h},{\beta}_{h}$$\end{document} is required, the blade scouring (downwash) effect can be treated as an additional vertical flux factor.

Finally, for compliance evaluation, a location\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x$$\end{document}is considered effectively covered if its time-averaged deposition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{C}\left(x\right)=\frac{1}{T}{\int}_{{t}_{0}}^{t}C(x,\tau)d\tau$$\end{document} exceeds the threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document}.

Evaluation indicators and objective function

We measure performance across four dimensions: Qualified Coverage, Uniformity, Overspray Rate, and Energy Consumption, constructing a weighted single objective:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J\left(x\right)=\alpha \cdot Cov \left(x\right)+\beta \cdot Uni \left(x\right)-\gamma \cdot Over \left(x\right)-\delta \cdot Eng\left(x\right)-\epsilon \cdot Fail \left(x\right)$$\end{document}

Qualified coverage (Cov):

The proportion of the target zone \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z$$\end{document} where the accumulated deposition meets the threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document}:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Cov=\frac{1}{\left|Z\right|}{\int}_{Z}1\left\{\bar{C}\right(x)\ge\theta\}\hspace{0.17em}dx$$\end{document}

Uniformity (Uni):\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Uni=1-\frac{Std\left[\bar{C}\right(x):x \in Z]}{Mean\left[\bar{C}\right(x): x \in Z]}$$\end{document}where Z denotes the set of target cells with effective deposition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{C}\left(x\right)\ge\theta$$\end{document}, so that Uniformity evaluates dosage evenness within the effectively treated area.

Overspray rate (over):

The average deposition density in the non-target zones \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Z}_{out}$$\end{document}:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Over=\frac{1}{\left|{Z}_{out}\right|}{\int}_{{Z}_{out}}\bar{C}\left(x\right)\hspace{0.17em}dx$$\end{document}

Physical model parameterization and algorithm robustness

The framework’s reliability is grounded in the physics-based calibration of the WISDF model combined with algorithmic stability. Spatial dispersion parameters are calibrated to environmental conditions, where the base spreading coefficient is fixed at σ₀ = 1.5 m (standard ULV nozzle at 3 m altitude). To account for wind effects, wind-induced stretching is explicitly modeled as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma_{x}=\sigma_{o}(1+0.2\Vert w \Vert)$$\end{document} based on empirical data10,11, while cross-wind dispersion is approximated as σγ = σo. Complementing this physical fidelity, robustness analysis (Fig. 10A) confirms the algorithm’s stability under parameter uncertainty: despite weight perturbations of ± 20%, the maximum fitness variation remains below 12%, ensuring consistent convergence and constraint satisfaction across all tested combinations.

Operational deployment methodology

To facilitate practical application in novel contexts, we recommend a systematic six-step calibration procedure. The process begins with Mission Priority Assessment to analyze stakeholder requirements and constraints, followed by Parameter Range Selection using the trade-off curves in Fig. 10B to identify appropriate α values. Operators should then perform Robustness Verification and Simulation Pre-validation within the selected ranges to ensure theoretical feasibility. The deployment concludes with Field Validation via ground truth sampling to verify predictions, allowing for Iterative Refinement of parameters based on real-world measurements. This scene-adaptive approach leverages the framework’s demonstrated robustness to ensure optimal performance across diverse operational scenarios.

Finally, strictly enforced spray prohibition logic is applied. When the drone is outside the target zone or within the safety buffer, the spray system is mechanically locked to prevent pesticide drift into sensitive areas.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$If{p}_{i}\left(t\right)\in{B}_{d}^{+}\cup({\Omega}\setminus S)or trajectory crossessensitiveedges,then{r}_{i}\left(t\right)\equiv 0.$$\end{document}

Method/algorithm implementation

To simultaneously enhance coverage, uniformity, and energy efficiency while ensuring safety in no-spray zones, this study proposes an improved algorithm named C-GWO+, based on the traditional Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO). While maintaining GWO’s lightweight characteristics, the algorithm incorporates Opposition-Based Learning (OBL) initialization, a collaborative grouping rotation update mechanism, a path-spray dual-layer encoding structure, and a stagnation reset strategy. These innovations enable the algorithm to achieve more stable and precise convergence in high-dimensional variable spaces.

Why C-GWO+ fits wind-sensitive multi-UAV ULV spraying

Wind-sensitive ULV spraying induces a highly non-convex and multi-modal search space because spatially varying wind alters the stretched deposition footprint and creates multiple locally optimal “spray corridors” across different wind regimes. Meanwhile, multi-UAV trajectory variables and safety constraints make the problem high-dimensional, increasing the risk of premature convergence. To match these characteristics, grouped sub-swarms perform parallel exploration in different wind regimes and periodically exchange elite solutions, which reduces leader trapping in a single basin. Opposition-Based Learning (OBL) enhances the initial coverage of the feasible space, providing diverse starting points under high-dimensional constraints. Finally, due to weak gradients and noisy objective feedback caused by wind-sensitive deposition, optimization can stagnate; the stagnation reset mechanism detects plateaus and injects controlled diversification to escape local optima. Overview of collaborative Grey Wolf Optimization

Mirjalili et al.28 proposed the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO), an algorithm that mimics the collective intelligence of gray wolves in hunting behavior. As shown in Fig. 12 (left), the population is divided into three hierarchical levels: alpha (leader wolves), beta (second-best wolves), and delta (third-best wolves), with the remainder being ordinary wolves (omega). The figure also illustrates the hunting routes of the grey wolf group, where the alpha, beta, and delta wolves guide the population’s iterative updates through encirclement, tracking, and attacking prey, progressively approaching the global optimal solution.

The GWO algorithm’s core strengths lie in its simple structure, minimal parameters, and powerful global search capability. These features enable it to effectively avoid local optima when solving continuous optimization problems.

To address the limitations of the standard Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO) algorithm—particularly its tendency to get trapped in local optima and constrained convergence speed when tackling large-scale complex problems—we propose a Cooperative GWO framework with Opposition-Based Learning (Cooperative GWO with OBL). The optimization objective of this paper is to maximize the coverage rate, uniformity, and spraying efficiency while minimizing overspray, energy consumption, and completion time. Through the collaborative sub-swarm mechanism and OBL strategy, the population iteratively updates its position, ultimately converging to the global optimal solution that balances all the above objectives.

Collaborative strategy and grouping mechanism

In the collaborative GWO framework, the entire operational area is first divided into multiple subtask zones, with each drone assigned to one or several operational strips. Correspondingly, the collective genetic code \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi$$\end{document} of the multi-drone system is decomposed into sub-blocks, each containing only the trajectory and spraying decisions of a single drone. This approach preserves the structure of global unified optimization while enabling localized refinement searches within subgroups.

In each optimization cycle, every subgroup—representing an individual drone—autonomously updates its trajectory and spray activation based on constraint violations and the guidance of the current leaders (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha,\beta and \delta$$\end{document}). Concurrently, the algorithm maintains a global shared pool of individuals to facilitate information exchange across subgroups. When a subgroup identifies a candidate solution that significantly outperforms the current global optimum, the corresponding drone solution is elevated to the leader role. This event triggers role rotation and disseminates information across the various subgroups.

To ensure coordination among different drones, the algorithm periodically performs a “cooperative update” after several iterations. Each subgroup exchanges coverage details, energy consumption, and constraint information of boundary strips, while fine-tuning overlaps and gaps in adjacent operational zones to prevent significant under-spraying or redundant coverage. This significantly increases the probability of approaching a high-quality solution within a limited number of iterations, as shown in Fig. 19b.

Mechanism B: Opposition-based resampling under stagnation

When the global fitness value fails to improve for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P$$\end{document} consecutive generations, the algorithm is deemed to have reached convergence stagnation. At this stage, the “opposite resampling” procedure is applied to the individual with the lowest fitness score to trigger a jump:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}_{new}\leftarrow c+\epsilon\odot(U-L),\hspace{1em}c \in \{\alpha,O(\alpha\left)\right\},\hspace{1em}\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\sigma}^{2}I)$$\end{document}

Here,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}represents the current global optimal individual. The term\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(U-L)$$\end{document}denotes the multidimensional search span vector. The parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma\in\left[\mathrm{0.03,0.08}\right]$$\end{document} regulates the disturbance scale.

Newly sampled individuals undergo repair operations to be trimmed back into the feasible domain, then compete in the next generation.

In this way, without disrupting GWO’s existing leadership structure, OBL injects directional diversity around the optimal solution and its opposing positions, effectively mitigating local optima and premature convergence issues. The dynamic process is illustrated in Fig. 19c.

Computational complexity analysis

The time complexity of the proposed C-GWO+ primarily depends on the population size (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N$$\end{document}), the maximum number of iterations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T$$\end{document}), and the dimensionality of the decision variables (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D$$\end{document}). The complexity can be approximated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(T \cdot N \cdot D+T \cdot N \cdot {C}_{cost})$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{cost}$$\end{document} represents the computational cost of the objective function evaluation. In our simulations, the average execution time for a single run is approximately 15 s (on an Intel Core i7 CPU), which satisfies the requirements for near-real-time mission planning.
