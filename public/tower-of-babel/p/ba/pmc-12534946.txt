A nomogram for predicting ICU mortality of sepsis associated encephalopathy: a retrospective cohort study based on MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD
Authors: Xuemei Hu, Jianbao Wang, Shaowei Wang, Tianfeng Hua, Min Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE) is a fatal complication of sepsis, with a high mortality rate worldwide. This study aimed to reduce mortality and improve the quality of life of patients with SAEs by developing a practical nomogram to predict the risk factors associated with ICU mortality.MethodThe MIMIC database was used as the training set to build the model, and the eICU-CRD served as the validation set for external verification. LASSO regression analysis was conducted to identify predictive variables and develop the nomogram model. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to assess the model’s discriminative ability. Model calibration was assessed using calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit tests. Clinical decision curves were plotted to assess the model’s net benefit and evaluate its clinical applicability.ResultsA total of 5,242 patients from the MIMIC database and 3,103 from the eICU-CRD were included in the study. LASSO regression, identified eight predictive variables for inclusion in the final model. The nomogram was evaluated against standard ICU scoring systems, including SAPS II, SOFA and GOS scores, with AUROC values of 0.832, 0.769, 0.607, and 0.575, respectively, in the training set. Conversely, the validation set demonstrated AUROC values of 0.825, 0.715, 0.714, and 0.587. P-values from the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test for both the training and validation sets were 0.129 and 0.583, respectively, indicating a good fit quality. DCA revealed that the nomogram consistently provides greater net benefits compared to SAPS II, SOFA, and GCS scores.ConclusionDeveloping mortality prediction models for SAE patients in the ICU can facilitate early intervention strategies and potentially reduce mortality rates in this high-risk population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03222-1.Keywords: Sepsis associated encephalopathy, In-ICU modality, Nomogram, MIMIC-IV database, eICU-CRD

Background

Sepsis is a common critical illness characterized by life-threatening multiorgan dysfunction primarily caused by infection. It is associated with high morbidity and mortality, and is a leading cause of death in intensive care unit (ICU) patients [1]. Sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE) is one of the most common complications in sepsis [2]. SAE is characterized by acute cerebral dysfunction resulting from systemic infection without direct central nervous system infection [3, 4]. The clinical manifestations include a spectrum of neurological disturbances, ranging from mild alterations in consciousness and attentional deficits to severe presentations such as hallucinations, lethargy, and even deep coma [5, 6]. The pathogenesis of SAE involves a complex interplay of inflammatory mechanisms, including endothelial and microglial activation, including blood-brain barrier disruption, cerebral hypoxia, neurotransmitter dysregulation, glial cell activation, and subsequent neuronal and axonal degeneration [7]. Epidemiological studies indicate that SAE affect 30%-70% of hospitalized patients with sepsis or systemic inflammatory response syndrome, leading to significant clinical consequences such as extended durations of mechanical ventilation, prolonged ICU and hospital stays, and elevated rates of both ICU and in-hospital mortality [8, 9].

In clinical practice, sepsis-associated delirium is often used as an alternative method for diagnosing SAE [10]. Because SAE diagnosis is an exclusionary diagnosis, SAE diagnosis cannot be recognized early in clinical practice, thus delaying the diagnosis and treatment of patients, resulting in high mortality. In the ICU, many scores can be used to indicate the severity of the disease and it has been shown that the relevant scores can predict the mortality of the patient, similar to the APACHE II, and these scores are relatively well established for clinical use [11]. Nomograms are visual statistical tools that integrate data to develop continuous scoring systems that accurately reflect the individual risk probabilities accurately [12]. Therefore, it is crucial to establish an accurate dynamic nomogram with important implications for both individuals and society through prognostic and clinical management decisions.

This study aimed to identify the critical risk factors for patients with SAEs through a comprehensive retrospective analysis of large-scale clinical databases, with the ultimate goal of establishing a predictive model.

Materials and methods

Data source

This study employed a retrospective cohort design using data extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV database (version 3.0). The dataset encompasses patient records from 2020 to 2022, supplementing existing records from version 2.2, access MIMIC-IV v3.0 (physionet.org). The MIMIC database is a comprehensive, publicly accessible repository comprising de-identified clinical data from more than 40,000 intensive care unit admissions at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts [13]. Furthermore, the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD, version 2.0) serves as a multi-center telemedicine database and is frequently utilized as an independent external validation cohort for critical care research. It contains a multi-center database of over 200,000 ICU admissions in the United States. The first author, Xuemei Hu, completed the “data or specimen research” training and was granted permission to use public data (ID: 13677449). Because this study was conducted using an anonymous public database and a review board protocol, ethical consent was not required.

Population selection criteria

The inclusion criteria consisted of the following: (1) Meet the diagnostic criteria for sepsis 3.0 [1]. (2) Patients admitted to the ICU for the first time and have been hospitalized for at least 24 h. (3) Age ≥ 18 years. (4) Patients with sepsis who had a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of < 15 or had manifestations of delirium were identified as having SAE [3, 14].

The exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) failure to meet the diagnostic criteria for sepsis 3.0 and SAE. (2) ICU stay time less than 24 h. (3) age < 18 years. (4) dementia. (5) Primary brain injury, such as traumatic brain injury, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, or subarachnoid hemorrhage. (6) Epilepsy. (7) Hypertensive encephalopathy. (8) Metabolic or toxic encephalopathy. (9) Meningitis, encephalitis, and tumor. (10) Hepatic encephalopathy. (11) Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. (12) Mental and behavioral disorders caused by alcohol, drugs or psychoactive substances or alcohol and drug abuse. (13) Severe electrolyte and metabolic imbalances, including hyponatremia (< 120 mmol/L), hyperglycemia (> 180 mg/dL), hypoglycemia (< 54 mg/dL), and partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2) ≥ 80 mmHg. (14) No GCS score were obtained (15) Diagnosis of another encephalopathy.

Data extraction and management

The study extracted relevant variables from the MIMIC-IV database and eICU-CRD: (1) patient general information; (2) past disease history; (3) severity scores, including Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS); (4) vital signs and biochemical indicators of patients admitted to ICU within 24 h; and (5) treatment after ICU admission. (6) Outcome: death after ICU admission.

Variables with missing data rates exceeding 30% were excluded. R version 4.2.2 was used for data cleaning and imputation of missing values for the variables included in the analysis. R’s “randomForest” package was used to handle missing values and multiple imputation method was performed to generate five datasets for Lasso regression analysis. Specific details are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

This study was analyzed and plotted using the R version 4.2.2, with the software packages detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Categorical variables were analyzed for group differences using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability method. Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as means ± standard deviation and analyzed for differences in distribution between the two groups using the t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as medians (percentiles) and compared using the rank-sum test. Intergroup comparison results are expressed as p-values.

Internal and external validations were based on the MIMIC IV and eICU-CRD, respectively. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) were constructed to further evaluate the applicability of the model.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 5242 and 3103 SAE patients were screened in the MIMIC IV database and eICU-CRD, respectively. (Fig. 1). Supplementary Table 1 provide the specific classifications, demographic characteristics, and baseline clinical data of SAE patients.

Correlation heat map

Correlation matrix and correlation tests were conducted for all variables in 5242 patients, and a correlation heat map was generated (Supplementary Fig. 2). Significant collinearity between the variables, indicating that variables could be directly included in the logistic regression analysis.

LASSO regression selection of predictor variables

According to the correlation heat map, we can conclude that there is collinearity between the variables, which cannot be directly analyzed; therefore, we performed lasso regression to screen the most suitable variables for establishing the prognosis model of patients. Lasso regression was employed for predictor variable selection in the machine learning model, with the model performance evaluated through cross-validated mean square error analysis (Fig. 2A) and the variable selection process was visualized using coefficient trajectory plots (Fig. 2B). We also evaluate the goodness-of-fit of the regression model, which results in an R2 of 0.292 and an adjusted R2 of 0.291. To ensure that the variables selected from the Lasso regression conform to the optimal model, we compute the values of Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion, thus validating the statistical model selection and evaluation, as detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

Lasso regression screening of prognostic model predictor variables: A) the selection process of the most appropriate λ in the lasso model; B) lasso coefficient curves

Production of nomograms

The variables selected by lasso regression model included eight predictors, blood urea nitrogen (bun), activated partial thromboplastin time (aptt), arterial oxygen saturation (spo2), heart rate, charlson comorbidity index (CCI), lactate, vasopressor, and mechanical ventilation were eight variables. According to the “Points” in the nomogram, the individual scores of each variable of a specific individual can be obtained, and the sum of the individual scores can obtain the “Total Points” in the nomogram, and the corresponding “ICU mortality rate” is the ICU mortality probability (Fig. 3). To further assess the potential multicollinearity among the independent variables in our model, we employed the variance inflation facto as a diagnostic measure; detailed results are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Construction of nomograms

Multivariable logistic analysis

Multifactorial logistic regression analysis was performed for the eight potential predictors (Fig. 4). The results showed that bun, aptt, spo2, heart rate, CCI, lactate, vasopressor use, and mechanical ventilation were independent prognostic factors in patients with SAE.

Discrimination, calibration and evaluation

To evaluate the discriminative ability of the model, receiver operating curves (ROC) were constructed for both the training and validation cohorts. The area under the curve of the training cohort was 0.832 (95% CI: 0.814–0.850), while the area under the curve of the validation cohort was 0.825 (95% CI: 0.801–0.850). These results significantly outperformed conventional scoring systems, including the SAPS II score (training cohort AUC: 0.769, 95% CI:0.748–0.789); validation cohort AUC: 0.715, 95% CI: 0.684–0.746), SOFA score (training cohort AUC: 0.607, 95% CI: 0.581–0.632; validation cohort AUC: 0.714, 95% CI: 0.684–0.744) and GCS score (training cohort AUC: 0.575, 95% CI: 0.552–0.599; validation cohort AUC: 0.587, 95% CI: 0.533–0.620). The results are presented in Fig. 5. From Fig. 5, we can see that the AUC values of the training set and validator of the prediction model are higher than those of other indicators, indicating that the performance of the prediction model we established is better.

Discussion

Sepsis is a prevalent and life-threatening condition in clinical settings and often results in multiorgan dysfunction throughout the body. Extensive research has demonstrated that sepsis survivors frequently experience long-term cognitive deficits and diminished health-related quality of life, particularly affecting physical functioning, general health, vitality, and social functioning [15, 16]. Even in patients without pre-existing central nervous system disorders, the systemic inflammatory response can trigger acute diffuse brain dysfunction, leading to SAE, which is associated with poor clinical outcomes and increased mortality rates [17]. Therefore, it is essential to assess the mortality of patients admitted to the ICU with SAEs. However, there is growing evidence from in both animal models and human studies that surviving animals show long-term changes in post-SAE behavior, learning, and memory, whereas survivors have long-term cognitive impairments related to SAE [18].

Studies have shown that increased exposure of the brain to uremic toxins, results in brain metabolic dysfunction, vascular self-regulation disorders, and blood-brain barrier damage [19]; for example, excessive urea nitrogen may enter brain tissue through the blood-brain barrier, affecting the metabolism and function of nerve cells. These poisons are thought to inhibit brain oxygen consumption and the active products of certain respiratory enzymes [20]. In addition, the accumulation of toxic metabolites can lead to neurotoxic effects and injuries [21], resulting in increased cerebral vascular permeability, and facilitating the diffusion of toxins into the brain. This process may also trigger brain dysfunction through the blood-brain barrier, induce nerve cell apoptosis and inhibit the protein kinase pathway activated by astrocytes, thereby aggravating brain function injury and affecting the prognosis of SAE patients [22, 23]. Therefore, it is important to detect buns and other products in the clinical setting.

Endothelial dysfunction occurs in the early stages of sepsis, and exposure of subcutaneous components in blood vessels causes activation of the coagulation process and the formation of a large number of microthrombes [8], which leads to further consumption of platelets, pro-coagulant substances, anticoagulant substances, microcirculation disorders, and eventually tissue ischemia and hypoxia [24, 25]. This is consistent with our study. As the APTT value prolongs, leading to a gradual rise in patient mortality rates. The occurrence of SAE further impacts the consumption of coagulation factors, resulting in sepsis-related coagulation abnormalities.

Cerebral tissue hypoperfusion and ischemic hypoxic injury leading to nervous system dysfunction are one of the main causes of encephalopathy. Hypoxia leads to an increased production of reactive oxygen species and active nitrogen in the cerebral microvascular endothelium, leading to oxidative stress [26], which aggravates the inflammatory response and cell apoptosis in vivo. Li et al. concluded that when SPO2 ≥ 93%, the incidence of SAE may be reduced [27]. Low SpO₂ is often associated with tissue hypoxia, which will activate inflammatory cells, release a large number of inflammatory mediators, aggravate the inflammatory response in the body, and aggravate the loss of brain tissue. When SpO₂ decreases and blood oxygen content decrease, brain tissue will have energy metabolism disorders due to hypoxia, and ATP production is insufficient, resulting in dysfunction of the nerve membrane ion pump, intracellular oedema, and abnormal nerve function, which affect the prognosis of patients with SAE [28]. Therefore, monitoring Spo2 in patients with SAE is essential to mitigate mortality risk. Additionally, when heart rate is excessively elevated, the diastolic period is markedly shortened. Diastolic period is an important period for heart filling and coronary artery blood supply. Too short diastolic period will lead to insufficient heart filling and reduced stroke output, the blood supply from the heart to the brain, resulting in cerebral hypoperfusion. Cerebral hypoperfusion results in the deprivation of oxygen in the brain. Cerebral hypoperfusion and hypoxia activate a cascade of molecular and cellular damage, leading to the rupture of the blood-brain barrier and apoptosis of nerve cells [29]. Lactic acid is a well-established prognostic indicator in sepsis patients [30] and lactate levels are routinely used to assess tissue hypoperfusion and hypoxia. During hypoxia, lactic acid accumulates, suggesting that patients with SAE have microcirculation dysfunction, potentially exacerbating tissue ischemia and hypoxia and perpetuating a vicious cycle [31]. This finding aligns with the results of our study. With the increase of lactic acid, the score in the model is also high, and the ICU mortality is also gradually increasing. Therefore, it is necessary to detect the level of lactic acid clinically, and timely identification and removal of lactic acid can reduce the mortality of patients.

Mechanical ventilation has two advantages. First, most clinical studies use ventilators when a patient is in a critical situation of respiratory failure, respiratory arrest, or low oxygen saturation. Ventilator-associated pneumonia is a pulmonary infection the occurs during mechanical ventilation. In addition, sepsis can worsen a person’s condition to worsen [32, 33]. Second, overventilation or underventilation may occur when mechanical ventilation is used, which may cause neuronal excitability and prolonged seizure duration, leading to decreased brain tissue and changes in cerebral perfusion flow [34, 35].

Vasopressors restore and maintain sufficient cerebral perfusion pressure by increasing the mean arterial pressure to ensure cerebral perfusion flow [36]. Phenylephrine and norepinephrine both cause arterial constrictions [37]. An increase in mean arterial pressure may be followed by reflex bradycardia and a decrease in cardiac output, resulting in an increase in cerebral perfusion flow; however, both result in a decrease in cerebral tissue oxygen saturation [37–39]. In addition, at low doses, epinephrine can increase cardiac output and decrease vascular resistance, thereby raising blood pressure; however, at high doses, cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance increase, causing arrhythmia and impaired circulatory function [40]. Improper use of epinephrine can result in arrhythmia and disturbances of internal circulation, which can increase oxygen consumption and lactic acid production [41]. Different drugs have different effects and different doses bring different effects. First of all, there may be a significant difference between the number of survival groups and the number of deaths. Second, this paper failed to analyze the effects of different drugs in depth, which is also something we need to explore further in the future.

Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) is a simple and rapid scoring system for evaluating the severity of basic diseases in critically ill patients. It quantifies the severity of basic diseases by assigning scores to different weights according to the disease severity [42]. The sensitivity of the CCI has been demonstrated in clinical practice, and a gradual increase in the CCI has been associated with increased mortality [43]. The CCI is used to evaluate the prognosis of critically ill patients; the higher the CCI, the worse the prognosis [44]. The finding of this study indicated that an increase in the CCI, was associated with higher mortality, aligning with the clinical use of CCI as a prognostic indicator. Therefore, CCI is a simple and practical comorbidification evaluation tool, which has important application value in clinical practice and medical research, but it also needs to be used in combination with the specific situation of patients to make comprehensive judgments.

The SAPS II, SOFA, and GCS scores are widely implemented in clinical practice as standardized tools for assessing patient prognosis [45, 46]. Consistent with previous studies, these scores effectively quantified disease severity and demonstrated predictive value of mortality [47, 48]. The GCS, in particular, is frequently used to asses consciousness in encephalopathy, which is of great significance in judging the severity of the disease and predicting prognosis. Therefore, these three variables were not included in the model construction. In this study, the SOFA, SAPSII, and GCS scores were used to compare their performances with those of the predictive models. The nomogram demonstrated superior predictive accuracy, as indicated by the DCA curve. This comparison further demonstrates that the efficiency of the constructed model is better.

The primary endpoint of this study was ICU mortality in patients with SAEs. Utilizing lasso regression analysis, we identified and screened independent risk factors, subsequently constructing a robust risk prediction model based on these determinants. The nomogram serves as a valuable clinical tool that enable healthcare professionals to assess individual prognoses and facilitates clinical decision-making. Previous research utilising a prospective cohort study design to develop an in-hospital mortality prediction model demonstrated that predictive modelling surpasses the reliance on clinical judgment alone, highlighting the clinical utility of such computational approaches. Our results confirmed the robustness of the prediction models. In future, it will be necessary to confirm their advantages in a clinical environment [49]. This predictive model allows clinicians to anticipate patients at risk of poor prognosis, enabling timely implementation of targeted interventions to mitigate mortality risk. The application of this model not only enhances treatment outcomes but also contributes to optimized resource allocation, reduced healthcare expenditures, and improved quality of life for patients. Furthermore, it provides decision-makers with a scientific foundation for developing personalized treatment strategies, ultimately leading to better survival outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

The development of robust predictive models empowers clinicians to proactively identify potential health complications at earlier stages, enabling timely interventions that significantly enhance treatment efficacy and optimize patient quality of life. However, several methodological considerations must be considered. Although, the current investigation employed a multi-center retrospective design, the generalizability of its findings necessitates validation through rigorously designed prospective cohort studies with comprehensive clinical data collection. Although our study emphasized the predictive ability of ML models, it did not incorporate clinical judgment, which will require future studies of mine to discuss the superiority of predictive models compared to clinical judgment [50]. Secondly, the high number of missing values for the predictors included in this study did not adequately address the methodology used to deal with the missing data, which is one of the limitations of this study [51]. Although the predictive model was developed using a substantial cohort of 5,242patients with SAEs, future research should focus on external validation across larger, more diverse populations to ensure model stability, improve predictive performance, and enhance clinical applicability. Finally, despite using multimodal criteria, some exclusion conditions may have been missed owing to incomplete laboratory data, and encephalitis diagnosis relies on ICD codes, which may lack sensitivity in retrospective data.

Conclusion

The nomogram created based on the above factors assessed the risk of death in patients, and may be implemented in future clinical work; however, clinical data are needed to evaluate the clinical value of the model.
