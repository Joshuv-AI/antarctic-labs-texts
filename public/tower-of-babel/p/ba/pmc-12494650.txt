Localizing retrograde K-wire insertion points in distal phalanges using the nail plate: a clinical controlled study
Authors: Xianting Zhou, Chenxi Zhang, Renhai Feng, Zhiying Yan, Xin Wang, Xuekai Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDistal phalangeal fractures are the most common hand injuries, and K-wire fixation is the most frequently used surgical treatment. This study aims to utilize the nail plate shadow on preoperative X-ray images to locate the insertion point for retrograde(from the distal to the proximal direction of the distal phalanx) K-wire fixation of distal phalanges, thereby improving the accuracy of K-wire placement.MethodsA case-control study was conducted on 60 patients with distal phalangeal fractures from November 2020 to May 2024. Patients in Group A underwent retrograde longitudinal implantation of a single K-wire based on the surgeon’s experience. In Group B, preoperative standard anteroposterior and lateral X-rays were used to outline the nail plate shadow, the central axis between the middle and distal phalanges, and measure the distance between the central axis and the nail plate shadow. Intraoperatively, the measured values were used to mark the skin insertion point, through which a single K-wire was inserted retrogradely. The distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint range of motion (ROM), DASH score, VAS score, operation time, intraoperative times of fluoroscopy, and postoperative complications were compared between the two groups.ResultsAll cases achieved healing without clinical deformity, and all fractures showed radiological union. In Group A, 2 cases developed superficial infections; in Group B, 1 case had a superficial infection. All 3 infected cases healed after debridement, dressing changes, and oral antibiotic therapy. There were no significant differences in demographic characteristics between the two groups. No statistically significant differences were observed in DASH scores or DIP ROM (P > 0.05). However, significant differences were found in VAS scores, operation time, and times of fluoroscopy (P < 0.05), with Group B performing better than Group A.ConclusionCompared with empirically placed retrograde K-wires, using the nail plate shadow on preoperative X-rays to locate the insertion point for distal phalangeal retrograde K-wire fixation improves the accuracy of K-wire placement, avoids repeated insertion, shortens operation time, and reduces radiation exposure for both surgeons and patients.Keywords: Distal phalangeal fracture, K-wire fixation, Nail plate

Introduction

Distal phalangeal fractures are among the most common fractures requiring surgery in hand trauma [1–4]. Surgical treatment of phalangeal fractures demands meticulous techniques to avoid unnecessary soft tissue damage. Minimizing soft tissue dissection during surgery re duces scarring and adhesions, facilitating early functional exercise and recovery [5]. Common surgical methods include K-wire fixation [6–8], plate-screw fixation [9–12], and screw fixation [13, 14]. K-wire fixation is preferred [2, 4] due to the drawbacks of plate-screw and screw fixation, such as extensive soft tissue dissection, scarring, etc [5]. When the soft tissue condition is favorable, especially with an intact nail plate, single K-wire fixation suffices because the nail plate, being harder than general soft tissue, provides additional stability against rotation and bending [7, 8, 15]. However, K-wire fixation may lead to complications like instability and displacement often attributed to suboptimal placement [6, 8, 16]. This study proposes using the nail plate shadow on preoperative X-rays to locate the K-wire insertion point, aiming to enhance placement accuracy, shorten operation time, and reduce radiation exposure.

Materials and methods

General information

From November 2020 to May 2024, 60 patients with distal phalangeal fractures meeting the inclusion criteria (Table 1) were enrolled in this case-control study. Patients were randomly divided into Groups A and B, with 30 cases each. Demographic data, injury mechanisms, fracture locations, treatment methods, operation time, intraoperative times of fluoroscopy, postoperative complications, and hand function recovery were recorded. Each group was operated on by a senior hand surgeon.

Surgical procedures

Both groups underwent surgery under local anesthesia with the patient in the supine position. Closed fractures were reduced manually, while open fractures were debrided before reduction. Fracture reduction was confirmed using C-arm fluoroscopy.

Group A: A 1.0 mm K-wire was inserted retrogradely(from the distal to the proximal direction of the distal phalanx)based on the surgeon’s experience for fracture fixation.

Group B: Preoperative standard anteroposterior and lateral X-rays were used to outline the nail plate shadow, the central axis between the middle and distal phalanges, and the center of the middle phalangeal head (DIP joint rotation center) [17, 18]。When the fracture ends show obvious angulation, rotation, or other deformities on anteroposterior or lateral X-rays, it is necessary to ensure that the distal fracture segment appears in standard anteroposterior and lateral views on the X-rays. The central axis should be drawn based on the distal fracture segment. The intersection of the central axis with the distal end of the distal phalanx was defined as the ideal bone insertion point, and its projection on the distal skin as the skin insertion point. On anteroposterior views, the distance between the central axis and the radial edge of the nail plate shadow was measured; on lateral views, the distance between the central axis and the distal edge of the nail plate shadow was measured (Fig. 1). These measurements were used intraoperatively to mark the skin insertion point, through which a 1.0 mm K-wire was inserted retrogradely.

For all patients, the K-wire tip was positioned just below the subchondral bone of the distal phalanx. If preoperative X-rays showed thin proximal fragments or unstable fractures due to severe soft tissue damage, the K-wire was extended into the middle phalanx through the DIP joint for additional stability. Post-insertion, anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopy confirmed optimal placement. If unsatisfactory, reinsertion and rechecking were performed until the K-wire was aligned with or close to the central axis. The K-wire was left exposed for easy outpatient removal. Operation time and times of fluoroscopy during K-wire fixation were recorded.

Postoperative management and follow-up

Open fracture patients were hospitalized for 3 days of anti-infective treatment, while closed fracture patients were observed for 1 day. The K-wire was removed 4–6 weeks postoperatively based on radiological healing; for inadequate callus formation, removal was delayed to at least 8 weeks. Radiological healing was defined as continuous bridging callus formation [19]. Nonunion as no healing signs within 4 months [20, 21].

Patients with non-transarticular K-wire fixation wore a finger splint for 3–4 weeks, followed by a removable splint for another 3–6 weeks, with ROM exercises initiated after splint removal. For transarticular fixation, ROM exercises started after K-wire removal. Heavy labor and sports were permitted after complete healing.

Outcome measures

To assess the stability of fingertip function recovery, the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for pain was used with a comparison to the contralateral side. Additionally, the range of motion (ROM) of the distal interphalangeal joint was evaluated. Meanwhile, the DASH score was employed to assess the patients’ upper limb symptoms and their ability to perform daily activities [22]. The DASH questionnaire consists of two parts: Part A + B and Part C. Part A + B contains 30 questions, mainly focusing on the impact of hand conditions on daily living abilities; Part C includes 4 questions, primarily investigating the influence of hand conditions on work capacity. Each question is rated on a 5-point scale, and the DASH score ranges from 0 to 100, where 0 indicates fully normal upper limb function and 100 represents extremely limited upper limb function. A higher score indicates poorer upper limb function. Furthermore, the occurrence of complications such as infection and nonunion in patients was recorded.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were analyzed using descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, and continuous variables using the Mann-Whitney U test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS 28.0 (IBM, USA).

Results

All fractures healed without clinical deformity, with radiological union confirmed at the final follow-up. The average follow-up duration was 5 months (range: 3–12 months). Two cases in Group A and one case in Group B developed superficial infections, all in open fractures, which healed after debridement, dressing changes, and oral antibiotics.

No significant differences were found in demographic data, injury mechanisms, fracture locations, soft tissue involvement, or fixation methods between the two groups (Table 2). DASH scores (A + B and C) and DIP ROM showed no statistical differences (P > 0.05), while Group B had significantly lower VAS scores, shorter operation time, and fewer fluoroscopies than Group A (P < 0.05) (Table 3).

In Group B, anteroposterior X-rays showed a nail plate width of 10.8 ± 1.8 mm (range: 7–17.1 mm) and a distance of 5.4 ± 0.9 mm (range: 3.6–8.6 mm) between the central axis and the radial edge of the nail plate. Typical case

A 22-year-old male sustained a closed fracture of the left index finger distal phalanx base due to a machine crush injury. Preoperative X-rays showed a nail plate width of 10.1 mm on anteroposterior view, with a 5.1 mm distance between the central axis and the radial edge of the nail plate, and a 1.2 mm distance on lateral view. Intraoperatively, the skin insertion point was marked using these measurements, and a 1.0 mm K-wire was inserted through the DIP joint into the middle phalanx. Fluoroscopy confirmed optimal placement (Fig. 2). The finger was splinted, with an operation time of 15 min and 2 fluoroscopies.

Intraoperative measurement and marking of the skin entry point using a ruler (c, d). Intraoperative fluoroscopy demonstrating that the Kirschner wire fixation position is close to the midline (e, f)

At 4 weeks postoperatively, radiographs showed significant bridging callus, and the K-wire was removed. ROM exercises were initiated, with a removable splint worn for 4 weeks. At 3 months, fracture union was confirmed, with a DASH (A + B) score of 0, DASH (C) score of 0, active DIP extension of 5°, flexion of 75°, and VAS score of 0. Minor nail deformity was noted due to nail bed injury (Fig. 3). The patient resumed normal life and work with satisfactory functional recovery.

Discussion

Hand fractures are among the most common types of fractures. Phalanges are more susceptible to injury than metacarpals, as they receive less protection and thus are more vulnerable to various forces and injury mechanisms [2–5, 23]. Moreover, the distal phalanx is the most common site for finger fractures [7, 24].

Fractures of the distal segment of the distal phalanx are typically caused by severe crushing injuries and often present as comminuted fractures. Due to the dense fibrous connections in the surrounding area, most of these fractures remain stable and are amenable to conservative treatment [3, 5]. Shaft fractures are commonly categorized as transverse or longitudinal, resulting from direct crushing or axial loading [5]. Proximal metaphysis fractures are typically caused by an injury mechanism involving excessive flexion. [5, 25].

Due to the insertions of extensor and flexor tendons on the distal phalanx, deforming forces are generated. In fractures of the proximal and middle phalanges, the fracture ends develop angular deformities, which typically require surgical treatment [5, 23]. Surgical treatment is considered for cases involving intra-articular fractures, rotational displacement, angulation of fracture fragments exceeding 15°, shortening of fracture ends greater than 6 mm, or open fractures [26]. Failure of manual reduction also warrants surgical treatment [27].

However, among the commonly used surgical treatment methods, plate fixation is associated with several drawbacks, including soft tissue dissection, scarring, tendon adhesion, nail bed injury, and nail plate deformity [9, 11]. Screw fixation is associated with drawbacks including soft tissue dissection, scarring, and soft tissue irritation [5].

For most individuals, simple fixation with Kirschner wires alone is sufficient to meet the treatment needs for distal phalanx fractures [2, 7, 15]. Unstable distal phalanx fractures are typically treated by fixation of the distal phalanx itself or the distal interphalangeal joint [6, 8, 28]. Common distal digit injuries include varying degrees of distal phalanx fractures, along with nail bed lacerations or nail plate avulsions. The recommended treatment for these injuries involves fixing the fracture with Kirschner wires, and after meticulous repair of the nail bed, replanting the nail plate through various methods [29, 30]. Retaining the nail plate facilitates both intraoperative reduction of fracture fragments in the distal phalanx and postoperative fracture stability [15, 30]. However, Kirschner wires, as the preferred surgical treatment method are also associated with complications including infection, nail deformity, joint malalignment, failure of internal fixation, and residual pain [31]. This may be associated with repeated attempts at Kirschner wire fixation [11]. Meanwhile, due to repeated attempts at Kirschner wire fixation, surgical time is prolonged and radiation exposure is increased for both surgeons and patients.

To address the issue of attempted repeated Kirschner wire fixation, we preoperatively measure the distance between the nail plate shadow and the midline of the distal phalanx on X-ray images to locate the optimal entry point for retrograde Kirschner wires. This approach thereby improves the accuracy of Kirschner wire placement, avoids multiple repeated fixation attempts, and prevents the occurrence of related complications.

Our research findings indicate that patients in Group B demonstrated significant advantages over those in Group A in terms of VAS scores, operative time, and the times of intraoperative fluoroscopy. This can be attributed to the accurate preoperative localization of the retrograde Kirschner wire insertion point in the distal phalanx, which effectively reduced prolonged operative time, increased times of intraoperative fluoroscopy, and additional damage to bone and soft tissues caused by repeated Kirschner wire punctures.

However, no significant differences were observed in terms of DASH (A + B) scores, DASH (C) scores, active ROM for DIP joint extension, and active ROM for DIP joint flexion.This may be partially associated with the degree of soft tissue injury, as reduced finger mobility correlates with the severity of soft tissue damage [32]. Furthermore, patients’ compliance with the treatment plan and their degree of cooperation with rehabilitation therapy are equally crucial for achieving optimal prognostic outcomes [1, 33].

In preoperative anteroposterior X-ray images, we observed that the distance between the midline of the distal phalanx and the radial margin of the nail plate is approximately equal to half the width of the nail plate. Thus, during treatment, if the nail plate shows no obvious detachment or deformity, the midline of the nail plate can serve as a reference central axis for the anteroposterior direction of the distal phalanx. Since the nail plate does not extend further parallel into the subcutaneous tissue on either side beyond the nail groove [34, 35], the width of the nail plate observed on anteroposterior X-rays corresponds to its width visible to the naked eye, minimizing the risk of measurement error. Furthermore, due to its unique linear morphology, the nail groove is easily identifiable on the body surface and facilitates measurement marking with a ruler during surgery. Surgeons can, based on personal preference, measure the distance between the central axis of the distal phalanx and the radial or ulnar margin of the nail plate, using this as a reference distance for the anteroposterior direction of the distal phalanx.

This study has certain limitations. First, the mean follow-up duration was 5 months, and the incidence of long-term complications such as arthritis requires further follow-up. Another limitation is that due to variations among patients in terms of gender, body type, age, and digit characteristics, there are significant individual differences in nail plate morphology [34] and distal phalanx shape [36]. Additionally, the sample size of cases is small, which limits the reference value of the distance measurements between the nail plate shadow and the central axis of the distal phalanx obtained in this study. A strength of this study lies in the fact that no patients were lost to follow-up.

Conclusion

The integrity of the nail plate not only facilitates intraoperative fracture reduction and postoperative stability of the fracture site but also assists in preoperative localization of the entry point for retrograde Kirschner wire fixation in the distal phalanx. Accurate localization helps shorten surgical time, enhance the reliability of internal fixation, avoid additional damage to soft tissues, nail beds, and bone tissues caused by repeated needle insertion, and reduce radiation exposure for both surgeons and patients.
