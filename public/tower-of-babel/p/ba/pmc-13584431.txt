Mapping the intellectual landscape of malaria drug repurposing: a systematic analysis of the 51 most cited studies
Authors: Daniel Thakuma Tizhe, Israel Ogwuche Ogra, Jeremiah Zaphnathpaaneah Adaji, Samson Usman, Yonni Eshovo Apeji, Jacob Kwada Paghi Kwaga, Grace Sabo Nok Kia, Kodjovi Sossou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe escalating threat of Plasmodium falciparum resistance to artemisinin-based therapies necessitates accelerated drug development. Drug repurposing offers a promising pathway to bypass the extensive time and cost of de novo discovery.MethodsThis study conducted a systematic analysis of the top 51 most cited original research articles in malaria drug repurposing identified from the Scopus database. Data on methodologies, drug candidates, targets, and outcomes were extracted and synthesized.ResultsThe analysis reveals a field dominated by computational approaches, with molecular docking (AutoDock Vina) and virtual screening serving as primary filters for identifying candidates from FDA-approved libraries. These methods are increasingly integrated with advanced techniques like machine learning for synergy prediction and transcriptomics-guided discovery. The repurposing landscape has identified promising antimalarial activity in diverse drug classes, including anticancer agents (methotrexate, lapatinib), antivirals (lopinavir, ritonavir), and immunosuppressants (everolimus). Key validated targets include the proteasome, PfHT1 glucose transporter, and various parasite enzymes. Candidates such as alisporivir, bazedoxifene, and nitrofurantoin have demonstrated efficacy in in vitro and in vivo models, often showing synergistic potential when combined with standard antimalarials.ConclusionThe convergence of computational power and biological insight has generated a rich pipeline of repurposable candidates. A quantitative analysis revealed molecular docking as the most frequent (31.4% of studies) and successful strategy, though in vivo preclinical evaluation yielded the highest validation score. Introduction

Malaria remains a formidable global health challenge, with the World Health Organization estimating 247 million cases and 619,000 deaths in 2021 alone [1]. The disease, caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Plasmodium, disproportionately affects people in sub-Saharan Africa. The efficacy of current frontline treatments, particularly artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), is severely compromised by the emergence and spread of resistant Plasmodium falciparum strains [2]. This resistance crisis underscores the critical and urgent need for new therapeutic agents with novel mechanisms of action.

The traditional drug discovery pipeline is notoriously protracted and expensive and carries a high attrition rate. In this context, drug repurposing (also known as drug repositioning) has emerged as a highly attractive alternative strategy [3]. This approach investigates existing drugs, with established safety profiles and pharmacokinetic data, for new therapeutic indications. By leveraging existing clinical data, drug repurposing can significantly reduce the development timeline and cost, thus accelerating the delivery of new treatments to patients [4].

The past decade has witnessed a surge in research dedicated to repurposing drugs for malaria. These efforts employ a wide array of methodologies, from in silico computational screenings to in vitro and in vivo experimental validations. Key approaches such as network-based analyses, machine learning models, and signature-matching techniques have generated viable candidate drugs by identifying shared molecular pathways between drugs and diseases [5, 6]. This is exemplified by the rapid identification of baricitinib for COVID-19 treatment through computational signature matching with eventual clinical validation [7]. While these in silico approaches effectively generate high-probability hypotheses and reduce development timelines, their ultimate success remains dependent on subsequent experimental and clinical validation to bridge the translational gap [3]. A diverse range of drug classes, including antivirals, anticancer agents, and immunosuppressants, have been explored for their potential antimalarial activity. While numerous studies exist, an analysis of the most influential publications can reveal the foundational methodologies, consensus findings, and evolution of paradigms that have shaped the field. This study therefore systematically analyzes the top 51 most cited articles to map the intellectual landscape, synthesizes proven strategies, and highlights consensus candidates with the highest translational potential. We seek to map the current landscape, synthesize the predominant methodologies, highlight the most promising repurposed candidates and their targets, and discuss the future directions necessary to translate these findings into clinical solutions.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and data collection

A systematic literature search was conducted using the Scopus database to identify the most influential research articles in malaria drug repurposing. The search query was designed using relevant keywords and Boolean operators: TITLE-ABS-KEY [(“malaria” OR “*Plasmodium falciparum*”) AND (“drug repurposing” OR “drug repositioning” OR “virtual screening”)) AND (LIMIT-TO (LANGUAGE, “English”)]. The search was performed on 3rd July, 2025 with no lower limit on the publication year to capture all relevant publications. A pool of 466 documents was retrieved from the Scopus database and were manually screened to include only research articles. A record of 235 original research articles was sorted in descending order by citation count (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the systematic screening and selection process of studies included in the analysis

Selection criteria and data extraction

The initial search results were sorted in descending order by citation count. The top 51 most cited original research articles out of the 235 screened publications were selected for in-depth analysis. For each included article, the following data were extracted into a standardized table (Supplementary Table S1): authors and publication year, journal name, citation count, specific algorithm or experimental technique used, the type of study, the primary application, and the key outcomes or findings are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Data synthesis

The extracted data were synthesized using a narrative approach. Trends in methodologies, commonly targeted pathways, and frequently identified drug classes were identified and analyzed. The results are presented to provide a coherent overview of the field’s evolution, current state, and key successes. The complete dataset extracted from all eligible studies is provided as Supplementary Table S1 to enhance transparency and reproducibility.

Results

Publication trend of drug repurposing in malaria

The temporal annual distribution of original research articles (Fig. 2) reveals a clear and accelerating growth in malaria repurposing research. While seminal works appeared earlier [4, 8], a marked increase in annual output is evident from 2017 onward, with a peak between 2020 and 2023. This surge correlates with the broader adoption and refinement of key enabling techniques and tools such as molecular docking, machine learning, and increased availability of parasite multi-omics data. This timeline suggests the field is currently in a highly productive phase, driven by computational and data-centric innovations.

Dominance of computational screening methods

The analysis revealed that computational approaches are the cornerstone of modern malaria drug repurposing efforts. Molecular docking programs, particularly AutoDock Vina and its variants (QuickVina-W), were the most frequently employed tools for the initial screening of large drug libraries against specific P. falciparum targets [9–11]. These libraries often comprised FDA-approved drugs or compounds from databases like DrugBank. Beyond standard docking, the field is increasingly adopting more sophisticated computational strategies. These include machine learning models to predict compound synergy from large-scale screening data [12], transcriptomics-guided in silico repurposing to identify compounds active against multiple life stages [13], and deep learning models using convolutional neural networks to predict drug-target binding affinities without traditional docking [14]. These tools reflect a shift toward computational-first, data-integrated approaches-combining AI, multi-omics, structural biology, and systems pharmacology in a bid to accelerate the discovery of repurposed antimalarial drugs especially drug-resistant P. falciparum.

Promising repurposed drug candidates

Screening efforts have identified potent antimalarial activity in drugs from various therapeutic classes. Notable findings are presented in Table 3. A significant number of promising candidates are cancer drugs (Torin 2, Methotrexate, Epirubicin, AZD0156 and Salirasib). Their cytotoxic mechanisms can be redirected against the rapidly dividing parasite. Antivirals show high potential. HIV and Hepatitis C drugs (Lopinavir, Ritonavir, and Alisporivir) have proven to be highly effective in preclinical studies, often with well-defined novel mechanisms in the parasite. Many candidates (Nitrofurantoin, Alisporivir, and Zinc Pyrithione) are not proposed as monotherapies but as partners for existing antimalarials like artemisinin to overcome resistance. The most valuable candidates, like AZD0156 and Alisporivir show activity against multiple life stages (asexual blood stages and gametocytes), which can treat disease and block transmission.

The role of combination therapy

Combination therapy is not just an option but a cornerstone strategy in modern malarial control, especially when repurposing drugs. Its role extends far beyond simply improving efficacy to directly addressing the existential threat of drug resistance. Several studies highlighted the potential of using repurposed drugs in combination with existing antimalarials. Some studies now actively screen for synergistic partnerships rather than just standalone drug. For example, Kalantarmotamedi et al. [12] used a machine learning algorithm specifically to predict synergistic compound combinations with high precision. Similarly, Matthews et al. [34] optimized the Chou-Talalay method to validate the synergy between emetine and atovaquone. Combination therapy can rescue repurposed drugs that would be unsuitable as monotherapies. A drug like zinc pyrithione or emetine, for example might be too toxic at the dose required at work alone, but in synergy, a safe dose becomes effective. As noted by Lotharius et al. [19], some drugs fail due to poor bioavailability. A synergistic partner can compensate for this weakness. The entire modern antimalarial arsenal is built on the ACT model, a fast-acting artemisinin derivative paired with a longer-acting partner drug. Repurposing strategies are now finding new partners for artemisinin (Nitrofurantoin) or creating entirely new non-ACT combinations (Emetine & Atovaquone) to prepare a potential post-ART future. Molecular docking and dynamics is the most frequently employed strategy, constituting nearly one-third of all studies (31.4%). Its high frequency and strong average success score (1.56) validate it as the cornerstone initial filter for candidate identification. While less frequent, in vivo preclinical evaluation strategies achieved a perfect average success score of 2.00, as they represent the crucial step of validating efficacy in a live animal model. Similarly, medicinal chemistry and lead optimization campaigns scored highly (1.80), indicating that optimizing a repurposed hit significantly enhances its potential. Machine Learning/AI models represent a smaller but highly successful cohort (Score 1.75). This includes synergy prediction and transcriptomics-guided discovery, suggesting these advanced methods are efficient at generating high-quality, validated leads. The high scores of experimental strategies (in vivo, in vitro, and medicinal chemistry) highlight that computational predictions, while essential for scale, must be followed by laboratory validation to prove true effectiveness.

Discussion

This systematic review consolidates the substantial progress made in repurposing drugs for the treatment of malaria. A steady growth in annual output is observed from 2014 onward, with a pronounced peak between 2020 and 2023, highlighting interest and the accelerated increase of computational and integrated repositioning strategies during the period under study. The field has matured from simple screenings to a highly integrated discipline that leverages computational power, structural biology, and multi-omics data. Among the 51 articles analyzed, in silico approaches form the methodological backbone, employed as the integral strategy in approximately 55% of the top-cited original research articles (28 out of 51). Within this computational landscape, molecular docking emerged as the dominant initial tool, serving as the primary screening method in nearly one-third (31.4%, 16/51) of all the analyzed studies. The consistent success of molecular docking in identifying true positives from vast chemical libraries validates its utility as a primary filter particularly when followed by in vitro validation- a technique utilized by the majority of computational hits to validates antiplasmodial activity and selectivity [9, 10]. The trend is evolving beyond the conventional docking towards more integrated strategies. The emergence of machine learning for synergy prediction [12], transcriptomics-guided approach for multi-stage activity [13], and deep learning for binding affinity prediction [14] reveals a shift towards data-driven, AI-enhanced techniques. These tools (summarized in Table 1) reflect a move from single-target screening to systems-level approaches that can predict polypharmacology and complex host-parasite-drug interactions.

The identification of active compounds from such pharmacologically diverse classes strongly supports a polypharmacology approach, where a single drug can interact with multiple parasitic targets [39]. Targets such as the apicoplast, the proteasome, and parasite-specific enzymes like plasmepsins and PI4K offer novel mechanisms of action crucial for overcoming existing resistance. This target diversity reflects the pharmacological potential of the repurposed candidates identified in Table 3. Notably, drugs from oncology (Torin 2, AZD0156), virology (Lopinavir, Alisporivir), and even dermatology (Zinc Pyrithione) have shown potent antimalarial activity. This strongly supports a polypharmacology paradigm, where a single drug's interaction with multiple parasitic targets raises the genetic barrier that the parasite must overcome [39]. The validation of candidates such as alisporivir, which acts synergistically with artemisinin, demonstrates the potential of repurposing not only to provide new monotherapies but also to rejuvenate the efficacy of current frontline treatments, critical for both treatment and transmission blocking [24].

Perhaps one of the most significant themes emerging from this analysis is the central role of combination therapy in the repurposing strategy. As outlined in Table 4, repurposed drugs are increasingly viewed not as standalone monotherapies but as synergistic partners for existing antimalarials. This approach directly addresses the primary challenges of overcoming extant resistance (Nitrofurantoin + Artemisinin), delaying the emergence of new resistance (Emetine + Atovaquone), and enhancing the therapeutic index of potentially toxic compounds. The validation of synergistic pairs like Alisporivir with Dihydroartemisinin [24] demonstrates repurposing potential to rejuvenate the efficacy of current frontline therapies and build the next generation of combination regimens.

Despite the rich pipeline of candidates, a critical challenge remains the translational gap between promising in vitro activity and clinical utility. Our analysis (Table 5) highlights this: while computational strategies are dominant, the strategies with the highest success scores are those involving in vivo preclinical evaluation (2.00) and medicinal chemistry optimization (1.80). This underscores a vital sequence: computational hits must be rigorously validated in biologically relevant models. As noted by Lotharius et al. [19], candidates must possess suitable pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and an impeccable safety profile for vulnerable populations. Therefore, the future trajectory of successful repurposing depends on coupling powerful in silico predictions with robust in vivo validation and sophisticated PK/PD modeling, as exemplified by the PBPK work for ivermectin [18].

Despite encouraging preclinical findings, most repurposed drug candidates have not obtained regulatory approval for malaria treatment. A major limitation is the disconnect between in vitro potency and clinical feasibility. Several candidates originally developed for oncology or immunological indications possess dose-limiting toxicities or narrow therapeutic indices that are unsuitable for large-scale use in malaria-endemic populations, particularly children and pregnant women. Moreover, achieving effective antiplasmodial plasma concentrations without systemic toxicity remains challenging, and poor bioavailability has been highlighted as a barrier to clinical translation [19]. As emphasized in broader drug repurposing frameworks, experimental validation and optimization are essential to bridge this translational gap [3].

Economic and regulatory factors further constrain development. Malaria predominantly affects low-income regions, limiting commercial incentives for pharmaceutical investment in large-scale clinical trials. Many repurposed drugs are off-patent, reducing financial motivation for reformulation, dose optimization, and regulatory submission. Additionally, stringent requirements for safety, pharmacokinetics, drug-drug interaction assessment (especially with ACTs), and resistance profiling increase development costs and delay progression to market [3, 19]. These realities explain why promising mechanistic discoveries have not yet translated into licensed therapies.

Nevertheless, repurposed drugs retain strong applicability within strategic frameworks, particularly in combination therapy. Synergistic partnerships such as those predicted through machine learning models or validated experimentally can reduce required doses, mitigate toxicity, and delay resistance emergence [12, 34]. Furthermore, medicinal chemistry optimization and pharmacokinetic modeling have demonstrated the potential to refine repurposed hits into more viable candidates [18, 40]. Thus, while most candidates are not yet market-ready, their integration into optimized combination regimens and transmission-blocking strategies represents a realistic pathway toward clinical implementation in the era of emerging resistance. Future directions

To build upon the current successes in malaria drug repurposing, future research should focus on several strategic areas. First, there is a critical need to integrate multi-omics data more comprehensively to identify novel, essential, and parasite-specific targets, thereby moving beyond targets with human homologs. Second, the development of more sophisticated AI and machine learning models that integrate chemical, biological, and clinical data will improve the prediction of in vivo efficacy, toxicity, and potential resistance mechanisms [12, 13]. A pressing priority is the focus on transmission-blocking agents; further screening for compounds active against gametocytes and early sporogonic stages, like brusatol [41], should be prioritized to support malaria eradication goals. For the most promising repurposed hits, dedicated lead optimization campaigns are crucial. As demonstrated with astemizole and nitazoxanide [32, 40], medicinal chemistry efforts can enhance potency, improve pharmacokinetics, and reduce off-target effects. Finally, the most promising candidates with robust preclinical packages, such as alisporivir and bazedoxifene, warrant accelerated clinical translation in well-designed trials to confirm their safety and efficacy in human populations and ultimately deliver the next generation of antimalarial treatments.

Limitations

This systematic analysis has several inherent limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First and most significantly, the study's foundation on the “top 51 most cited” articles from a single database (Scopus) introduces substantial selection bias. This approach inherently favors older, well-established publications that have had more time to accumulate citations, creating an average lag of approximately 5–8 years between publication and analysis. Consequently, recent methodological innovations and emerging candidates from 2023 to 2025 are systematically underrepresented, potentially missing important advances in the field. Furthermore, exclusive reliance on Scopus excludes relevant studies indexed only in PubMed, Web of Science, or regional databases, limiting the comprehensiveness of the analysis.

Second, the citation-based selection creates a “success bias,” disproportionately representing positive findings and promising candidates while underreporting negative or null results. The true denominator of screened compounds versus validated candidates remains unknown, and failed repurposing attempts critical for understanding attrition rates and avoiding unproductive approaches are absent from this analysis. This one-sided reporting may create an artificially optimistic view of repurposing success rates and limit practical utility for researchers seeking to avoid unsuccessful strategies.

Third, this narrative synthesis does not include systematic critical appraisal of study quality, experimental rigor, or risk of bias within included articles. There is no differentiation between methodologically robust studies and those with potential design flaws, and the author-weighted evidence score presented in Table 5 is a descriptive metric developed specifically for this review. This scoring system has no statistical validation, lacks inter-rater reliability testing, and should not be interpreted as predicting clinical success. It simply reflects the authors' subjective weighting of evidence types for descriptive purposes within this review.

Fourth, the analysis inadequately addresses why, despite decades of research and hundreds of preclinical candidates, no repurposed drug has achieved regulatory approval for malaria. Critical translational barriers, including intellectual property constraints, manufacturing costs suitable for endemic region distribution, formulation challenges, combination compatibility with artemisinin-based therapies, and the limited commercial incentives for investment in malaria therapeutics are mentioned but not systematically analyzed. These factors represent the fundamental challenges that have prevented preclinical promise from translating into clinical reality.

Finally, this review cannot account for the critical distinction between in vitro potency and clinical feasibility. Many candidates originally developed for oncology or chronic diseases possess dose-limiting toxicities or narrow therapeutic indices unsuitable for acute malaria treatment in vulnerable populations, particularly children and pregnant women. Achieving effective antiplasmodial plasma concentrations without systemic toxicity remains a substantial hurdle that computational predictions and in vitro assays cannot adequately address. These limitations underscore that the preclinical candidates identified herein represent starting points for further investigation rather than solutions ready for clinical implementation.

Conclusion

This systematic analysis demonstrates that drug repurposing represents a productive strategic approach within the antimalarial drug discovery arsenal. The convergence of computational power, biological insight, and innovative screening assays has generated a substantial pool of preclinical candidates requiring further investigation. While computational predictions provide a valuable launchpad for hypothesis generation, their translations into clinical solutions require rigorous experimental validation, medical chemistry optimization, and systematic evaluation in appropriate animal models followed by human trials. The field has generated numerous preclinical candidates, including alisporivir, bazedoxifene, and AZD0156, that warrant further optimization and validation before clinical translation can be considered. Their progression toward regulatory approval remains uncertain and will depend on overcoming significant hurdles. Despite encouraging preclinical findings, the reality remains that no repurposed drug has yet achieved regulatory approval for malaria treatment. The continued and strategic application of drug repurposing, particularly when integrated with rigorous experimental validation, novel combination therapy optimization, and transmission blocking objectives, holds promise for expanding the antimalarial treatment options. However, this promise will only be realized through sustained commitment to the full development pathway, from computational prediction through clinical evaluation, with transparent reporting of both successes and failures to guide future efforts.
