Contraceptive use, adherence, and perceptions among women aged 18–45 years in Czechia: a 2019 cross-sectional quota-based online survey
Authors: Vladimír Dvořák jr, Vladimír Dvořák, Ľudovít Paulis, Radovan Pilka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnintended pregnancies remain common globally, underscoring the need to understand real-world contraceptive behaviors and their determinants to optimize counseling and public health planning. In Czechia, robust evidence on contraceptive method mix and perceptions has been limited.MethodsWe analyzed a cross-sectional online survey conducted in September 2019, among women aged 18–45 years (n = 1501) irrespective of current contraception usage. The survey used quota sampling for age, education, household income/size, and region; no weighting was applied. We analyzed prior and current contraceptive use, emergency contraception, drivers and barriers to method choice, pill adherence, and information sources. Age-group differences (18–24, 25–34, 35–45 years) were tested with Cochran-Armitage test for trend (categorical) and ANOVA (continuous).ResultsThis survey provides a detailed age-stratified picture of contraceptive experience, current use, and perceptions among women aged 18–45 years in Czechia. One-third reported no current method (33%, 95% CI [31%, 35%]) and the current method mix concentrated on short-acting methods (oral contraception [OC] 38% [36%, 41%] and barrier methods 14% [12%, 16%]). Intrauterine device (IUD) use was comparatively low overall (hormonal 4.7% [3.6%, 5.7%]; non-hormonal 3.5% [2.6%, 4.5%]) but increased with age (hormonal 2.5%→2.9%→7.4%, p < 0.001; non-hormonal 0.9%→3.6%→4.8%, p < 0.01), while barrier use declined (22%→14%→10%, p < 0.001). Among current OC users, 37% reported forgetting at least one pill in the preceding 3 months. Younger women had the highest missed-pill rates among OC users, greater concern after a missed pill, and more frequent recent emergency contraception use. The major source of information were various internet-based sources combined, while gynecologists were the main single source of information (73%, [70%, 75%]), followed by internet search engines specifically (44%, [42%, 47%]). While the relevance of gynecologist increased with age, the relevance of internet search engines declined.ConclusionsContraceptive use was dominated by short-acting methods, with low but age-increasing IUD uptake. Younger women more frequently reported at least one missed OC pill, greater concern after missed pills, and more frequent recent emergency contraception use. Interpretation should consider mainly the use of non-probability quota sampling, the absence of weighting, and reliance on self-reported questionnaire data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12978-026-02390-1.Keywords: Age factors, Contraception behavior, Contraception oral, Contraception postcoital, Counseling, Intrauterine devices, Patient preference

Introduction

Almost 50% (121 million) of pregnancies worldwide are unintended, with 61% of them ending in abortion [1]. These numbers underscore the need for effective contraceptive methods to mitigate potential medical and psychological consequences for both mother and child [2–4]. The rate of unintended pregnancies has declined worldwide, in correlation with increased use of contraception. Estimates are that in 2017, the use of modern contraceptives prevented 308 million unintended pregnancies [5]. From the wide range of contraceptive modalities available, options in Czechia include prescription methods: female barrier contraception (BC), oral contraception (OC), hormonal vaginal rings, hormonal depot injections, hormonal transdermal patches, and hormonal and non-hormonal intrauterine devices (IUD) (both inserted in an outpatient setting); as well as non-prescription options, such as male BC, emergency contraception (EC) from pharmacies, fertility awareness-based methods or coitus interruptus. Contraceptive implants were not available in Czechia at the time of the survey.

Patterns of contraceptive use are shaped by a complex interplay of determinants extending beyond method availability or reimbursement policy. The perceived contraceptive method benefits, drawbacks, and even myths and misconceptions are influenced by cultural norms, social media, educational attainment, socioeconomic status, health literacy, and trust in healthcare systems. The landscape of these factors is highly region and even country dependent [6].

While data on prescription-based contraceptives in Czechia are available [7], data from these administrative gynecological reports do not capture non-prescription methods. They also do not provide information on reasons for method choice or non-use, or on where women obtain contraceptive information. Perceptions and attitudes toward contraceptive use in real-world settings are more appropriately captured through population surveys, which can complement routine administrative data and support reproductive health policy decision-making [8, 9]. At present, country-specific, age-stratified survey evidence on contraceptive use, adherence and perceptions in Czechia remains limited.

Another relevant question is the extent to which women are protected against unintended pregnancy. The analysis of the country-specific contraceptive method mix and the assessment of how well contraceptive needs are met in relation to unintended pregnancy are closely related but distinct questions. The population of women using contraception does not fully overlap with the population of women currently trying to avoid pregnancy. Some women use contraceptive methods for non-contraceptive indications or for precautionary reasons, whereas some women who may be at risk of unintended pregnancy do not use contraception. Moreover, current pregnancy-risk status may be fluid over time and difficult to classify reliably in a cross-sectional survey. In the present study, we aimed to analyze contraceptive use and perceptions in the general population, providing a descriptive analysis of contraceptive patterns. On the other hand, this focus does not allow a direct estimate of unmet need for contraception or the proportion of women currently protected against unintended pregnancy.

In summary, our primary objective was to describe the contraceptive method mix among women aged 18–45 years in Czechia, including non-prescription methods. Our secondary objectives were to examine reasons for method choice, non-use, and discontinuation where appropriate and to assess age-related differences in these variables.

Methods

Participant selection and enrolment

The data come from the online quota survey of women (n = 1501) aged 18–45 in 2019 in Czechia, irrespective of whether they were currently at risk of unintended pregnancy. Respondents were recruited from an online panel with more than 50 000 members in Czechia. Recruitment was conducted using pre-specified quotas designed to approximate the distribution of the Czech female population aged 18–45 years according to age, region, education, size of place of residence, and net monthly income, without considering parity. The survey was conducted in September 2019. Data collection was sponsored by BAYER s.r.o., and performed by Institut Public de Sondage d’Opinion Secteur (IPSOS). The present study reports an analysis of this publicly available, anonymized dataset (dataset and questionnaire available at [10]).

Data collection

The survey was administered online as a computer-assisted self-interviewing (CASI) questionnaire in Czech language with an average completion time of approximately 10 min. The questionnaire was validated through a pilot study on a sample of 50 respondents. For validation, the respondent first completed the questionnaire independently, and then the interviewer checked with them the clarity of the questions and the logic of their answers.

The participants had to answer all the questions without missing data. Questions on contraceptive discontinuation and non-initiation were displayed conditionally according to prior and current experience with the respective method. Analyses of OC discontinuation were restricted to former OC users, defined as women reporting prior OC experience but no current OC use. Analyses of non-initiation were restricted to women without prior experience of the respective method. Accordingly, questions on the reasons for not using any contraception were displayed conditionally to respondents currently not using any contraception.

Recruitment took place in two rounds. In the first round, 1,800 women were approached and asked to complete the questionnaire. Non-responders received two reminders for filling the questionnaire. If they did not respond after reminders, they were replaced by another respondent, with the same socio-demographical characteristics. Overall, 2,056 panelists were approached and 1,501 completed the questionnaire in full, corresponding to a completion rate of 73% among approached panelists. Participation in the survey was voluntary, and respondents received a standard incentive for questionnaire completion, paid by bank transfer. Informed consent was obtained electronically at the beginning of the questionnaire, before any survey questions were displayed or any data were collected. Consent documentation is retained with the online panel provider (IPSOS).

The present paper reports secondary post-hoc analysis of the publicly available anonymized data. The Ethics Committee of the Centre of Experimental Medicine, Slovak Academy of Sciences, determined that this study did not require formal ethical review (reference EC/CEM/2026/2, issued March 2, 2026). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. No medical intervention was performed and no adverse events or other situations subject to pharmacovigilance reporting were recorded during the course of the survey.

Statistical analysis

Participants had to answer all the questions before submitting the data. The data are presented as answered without any treatment or imputation of missing data. No post-stratification weighting procedures were applied due to used quotas and their compliance with general population characteristics of Czechia. All analyses were performed with SPSS software v20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Participant characteristics were summarized using standard descriptive statistics. For categorical variables, numbers, percentages within the respective group, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated using the Wald normal approximation method are shown. For method-specific discontinuation and non-initiation analyses, denominators were defined according to questionnaire routing and prior/current experience with the respective contraceptive method. For continuous variables mean and standard deviation (SD) are shown. For comparisons across ordered age groups (18–24, 25–34, and 35–45 years), categorical variables were additionally evaluated using the Cochran–Armitage test for trend, whereas continuous variables were compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA). P value < 0.05 was considered significant. Because of the exploratory nature of the study and the broad set of outcomes, no formal correction for multiple testing was applied; all p-values should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Results

Participants

Data from 1501 participants were included in the analysis. 38% (570/1501) of participants had children under 5 years. 35% (525/1501) were planning children in the next 5 years. Age groups for further analyses were pre-specified as 18–24 years (n = 322/1501, 21%), 25–34 years (n = 559/1501, 37%), and 35–45 years (n = 620/1501, 41%). This age stratification was defined a priori to reflect distinct reproductive and socio-economic (such as completion of education, enter to the labor market or employment rate) stages relevant for decision-making contexts rather than equal chronological intervals. Contraception experience

Out of the 89% (1339/1501) participants, who had experience with contraception usage, 35% (472/1339) had experience with two forms of contraception, 34% (460/1339) had experience with one form only and 30% (407/1339) had experience with three or more forms of contraception. 77% (1161/1501) of women had experience with OC, with lower experience in young women (p < 0.001). 49% (740/1501) of women had experience with barrier contraception with higher experience in young women (p = 0.02) and 22% (337/1501) with EC, without age differences in experience. Experience with hormonal IUD (8.8%, 132/1501) and non-hormonal IUD (6.2%, 93/1501) showed a significant trend (p < 0.001) toward higher usage in older age groups. Current use of contraception

Currently used contraceptive methods included OC (38% of women, 574/1501), BC (14%, 211/1501), hormonal and non-hormonal IUDs (4.7%, 70/1501 and 3.5%, 53/1501 users respectively). Current non-use of contraception was reported by 33% ( 495/1501) of women without significant age-related difference. EC was rarely (1.1% of users, 16/1501) used as the main contraception method. While the use of barrier methods declined with age (p < 0.001), the use of both IUD methods was significantly higher in higher age groups (p < 0.001 and p = 0.003, respectively) (Table 4).

Contraception choice

The choice for OC was reported to be based mainly on its efficacy (38% of users, 216/574) and the ease of administration (36%, 204/574). Barrier contraception was chosen due to the desire for hormone-free protection (53%, 112/211) or the inability to use OC due to health-related reasons (23%, 49/211). Hormonal IUDs were preferred due to convenience (57%, 40/70), the menstrual cycle control (34%, 24/70), the ease of use (30%, 21/70), and their effectiveness (29%, 20/70). Non-hormonal IUDs were favored by those seeking hormone-free contraception (36%, 19/53) and considering cost implications (Table 7).

Discussion

This survey provides the first detailed, age-stratified picture of contraceptive experience, current use, and perceptions among women aged 18–45 years in Czechia, regardless of contraceptive need. Three findings merit emphasis. First, one-third of women reported no current contraceptive use (33%, 95% CI: 30.6–35.4), while users concentrated on short-acting methods: oral contraception (38%, 95% CI: 35.8–40.8) and barrier methods (14%, 95% CI: 12.4–15.9). Second, IUD use was comparatively low overall but increased with age. Third, younger women had the highest missed-pill rates, greater concern after a missed pill and more frequent recent emergency contraception use.

Our findings indicate that only 11% of (mainly younger) women have no experience with any contraceptive method. The proportion of women reporting no current contraception (33%) is substantial and is comparable to survey estimates from the United States [11] and slightly higher than the 24% of non-users in United Kingdom survey [12]. The 54% of non-users recently reported from National Health Data System in France [13] highlights the difficulty in comparing survey data and prescription data, which do not report non-prescription-based methods, such as condoms. Moreover, the observed prevalence of non-use (33%) was not synonymous with unmet contraceptive need. Among current non-users, 46.7% were classified as not at risk of unintended pregnancy because they reported trying to conceive, no sex with men, infertility or sterilization of the respondent or her partner. Thus, the non-user category is clinically heterogeneous and should be interpreted descriptively within the general-population contraceptive method mix.

Within the method mix, OC remains the leading method (38%), followed by barrier contraception (14%), while hormonal and non-hormonal IUD methods account for 5% and 4%, respectively. In routine reporting to the Institute of Health Information and Statistics of the Czech Republic (ÚZIS), approximately 30% of women of reproductive age use OC, 6% use hormonal IUD, and 2.5% use non-hormonal IUD [7]. Given our narrower age window (18–45 years) relative to ÚZIS (15–49 years), OC estimates are concordant, while there is some modest difference in the hormonal/non-hormonal IUD split. Although this difference could be only statistical, we hypothesize, that it may reflect misclassification by some users and the split reported by the gynecologists to ÚZIS might more likely reflect the real ratio between hormonal and non-hormonal IUD in Czechia. In addition to IUDs, other less frequently adherence-independent used methods are available in Czechia and captured by the survey including injectable contraception, transdermal patches and vaginal rings. Their use however, is low, with no significant age-related trend.

Contextualizing these findings historically and internationally requires caution. Earlier UN estimates from 2008 [14] positioned Czechia out of the Eastern Europe subgroup, among European countries with high OC use (≈ 34%). On the other hand, with relatively low IUD use (≈ 4%), Czechia ranks lower than the Western Europe countries (10%) as well as lower than Eastern Europe (9%) [14]. In our survey the current IUD usage in Czechia reached almost 9%, which is the previously reported average in Eastern Europe. Congruently, administrative data indicate a gradual rise in hormonal IUD accompanied by a decline in OC between 2013 and 2019 [7]. A similar shift toward greater IUD use alongside declining OC use was documented for 1999–2017, in the United States [15]. However, the question remains whether the trend in Czechia led to similar usage rates as in western-European countries. The multicountry TANCO survey of women seeking contraceptive counseling reported IUD use of about 9% in Czechia - the lowest among participating countries (Spain, Portugal, France, Switzerland, Ireland, Sweden, Finland, Slovenia, Poland) - highlighting persistent headroom for uptake [16], but cross-country comparisons are sensitive to sampling frames, denominators, and case definitions and should be interpreted cautiously.

Nevertheless, the contraceptive mix captured in our study represents a snapshot of the current usage. In fact, contraceptive choice is a dynamic rather than fixed behavior, as women may switch methods over time in response to changing preferences, reproductive plans, tolerability, relationship status, and life circumstances.

Considering choice rationale for individual contraceptive methods, OC was most often selected for efficacy and ease of use, whereas method specific causes for discontinuation was driven primarily by a desire to avoid “artificial hormones” (51%) and by adherence challenges (32%). In parallel, barrier contraception was frequently chosen to achieve hormone-free protection (53%) or for medical contraindications to OC (23%). These patterns align with international evidence showing broad interest in lower-hormone options and simplified regimens in European surveys [13, 16, 17]. Our survey confirms the concern for adverse effects of long hormonal therapy among the younger generation leading to a decline from traditional OC methods. It remains to be seen, whether these perceptions will persist within younger cohorts such as Generation Z or decrease with longer personal experience.

For hormonal IUD users in our study, convenience, menstrual benefits, and perceived effectiveness were prominent reasons for choice. Other European data indicate that “low-dose hormone exposure” ranks among the top drivers for hormonal IUD selection [18]. Pharmacokinetic data indicate substantially lower systemic levonorgestrel exposure in LNG-IUS compared to oral levonorgestrel-containing contraceptives [19]. However, low-dose hormone exposure was not among the most frequently selected reasons for choosing IUS in our Czech survey. This discrepancy may reflect differences in questionnaire design, country-specific counseling practices, user preferences, difference in “acceptable” external hormone exposure or awareness of differences in systemic hormonal exposure between contraceptive methods.

Barriers to IUDs centered on foreign-body concerns, fear of insertion pain, and the need for insertion by a gynecologist—findings that echo prior literature citing fear of insertion pain or foreign object concern as IUD uptake barriers [17, 20, 21]. Recent data show, that almost half of the women report intense pain at IUD placement, with higher pain perception in nulliparous women and hormonal IUD placement [18]. This underscores the importance of discussing expected insertion-related discomfort and available pain-management options as part of contraceptive counseling.

Cost has been cited elsewhere as a barrier to IUD uptake [22, 23]. In Czechia, contraception is generally not reimbursed, including IUDs. Higher one-time IUD cost, in particular for the more expensive hormonal IUD, might be considered by the users when choosing contraception method. In our cohort, cost was not reported as major barrier for hormonal or non-hormonal IUD use, but it was one of the main drivers for the non-hormonal IUD choice. Adherence patterns further contextualize method choice: Among current OC users, over one-third reported having forgotten at least one pill within the preceding three months. Although missing a single pill rarely compromises contraceptive efficacy substantially, this finding may reflect perceived adherence burden and concern associated with user-dependent contraception. In this context, counseling should include not only support with contraceptive method choice and instructions on correct OC use, but also reassurance and clear guidance on how to manage missed pills according to clinical recommendations and the product leaflet, so that unnecessary anxiety or inappropriate use of EC can be avoided when the objective risk is low.

Age-related gradients were evident: with increasing age, use of hormonal/non-hormonal IUD rose while reliance on barrier contraception declined. The broader European PULSE survey similarly observed higher long-acting method use in older cohorts [24]. In contrast to lower uptake of adherence independent IUD usage in young women (aged 18–24 years), we observed greater anxiety about missed pills and more frequent recent EC use. The data on the relatively widespread usage on EC in Czechia are of a particular note, as they do not occur in administrative reports from gynecologists’ offices. Similar to our finding, US studies also report more frequent use of user-dependent contraception or no contraception in young women [11]. These data, together with higher fecundity at younger ages [25] and higher rates of unintended pregnancy reported in this group [26–29], lead us to hypothesize that adherence-dependent methods may be less well aligned with contraceptive behaviors in younger women. This interpretation is supported by the observed convergence of higher rates of missed pills, greater concern following missed doses, and more frequent recent emergency contraception use in this age group; however, causal inference cannot be made in the absence of adjusted analyses accounting for potential confounders.

Implications for practice and policy should be interpreted cautiously in light of the cross-sectional design and descriptive nature of the study. Major guidelines endorse long-acting reversible contraception as safe and appropriate for adolescents and nulliparous women [30–32]. A puzzling observation is that despite reduced contraception uptake in Czechia between 2006 and 2020, no trend for increased number of pregnancies nor spontaneous nor artificial abortions can be observed [33]. This ecological observation opens the questions on potential changes in timing of childbearing, fertility intentions, and sexual behavior in younger generation.

Our data indicate that gynecologists were the most frequently cited individual source of contraceptive information, particularly among women over 25 years of age. This likely reflects the organization of contraceptive care in Czechia, where women can access gynecologists without referral and where gynecologists often serve as the primary healthcare contact for reproductive health and contraceptive counseling. At the same time, internet-based sources collectively represented a major information channel, especially among younger women, suggesting that contraceptive communication in this age group may require a different mix of professional and digital channels. Although gynecologists were the most frequently cited individual source of contraceptive information in this survey, contraceptive counseling and education naturally extends beyond gynecologists to other qualified healthcare professionals, including primary care physicians, nurses, and midwives, who may be more accessible in routine care settings. Notably, a substantial proportion of women reported using the internet as a source of information regardless of age, underscoring both its reach and the potential risk of exposure to inaccurate or non–evidence-based content.

In conclusion, these findings are consistent with current recommendations for evidence-based, patient-centered contraceptive counseling that aligns method characteristics with individual preferences, health profiles, and lifestyle factors. This study provides Czechia-specific insight into the range of preferences, perceived barriers, practical concerns, and possible misconceptions that women may bring into contraceptive decision-making. Clear and balanced communication regarding the benefits, risks, and practical considerations of all available contraceptive methods using an age-tailored mix of digital and personal channels may help healthcare providers deliver personalized guidance and support informed, sustainable contraceptive choices across reproductive life stages.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first publication describing contraceptive usage patterns and perceptions in Czechia beyond the administratively reported data [7]. This study provides large unique dataset that fills this important information gap. The advantage of survey as compared to medical database data is its ability to capture both prescription and non-prescription contraceptive methods together with reasons for choice or discontinuation. The age-stratified analysis provides novel insights into intergenerational differences in contraceptive use and perceptions.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the survey used a non-probability, internet-based panel with quota sampling, which may introduce selection bias, particularly related to internet access and willingness to participate in online research. Second, the online questionnaire required completion of all displayed questions before submission. Although participation was voluntary and respondents could discontinue the survey, this design may have discouraged some respondents from completing the questionnaire or may have influenced responses to sensitive items. Therefore, selection bias and response bias related to mandatory item completion cannot be excluded. Third, although quotas were applied to approximate population characteristics, no post-stratification weighting was performed, and residual differences between the sample and the underlying population cannot be excluded. Fourth, all measures relied on self-reported data, which are subject to recall and social desirability bias, particularly for events such as emergency contraception use in the preceding 12 months. Fifth, coitus interruptus was not actively offered as a predefined response option in the questionnaire and could only be captured under open-text ‘other’ responses. It is therefore likely underreported in the present analysis, particularly as an episodic or secondary contraceptive practice. Sixth, the category of women currently reporting no contraceptive use contains a large proportion of women not at risk of unintended pregnancy (trying to conceive, not sexually active etc.) but the dataset does not capture the current pregnancy-risk status among contraceptive users. Therefore, current non-use of contraception should not be interpreted as equivalent to unmet need for contraception. Seventh, this is a secondary post-hoc analysis of data collected without an a priori defined statistical plan or hierarchical testing, it uses p-values for single comparisons that are not adjusted for multiplicity and present a risk of Type I error when making conclusions. Eighth, the dataset lacked detailed clinical and behavioral variables, in particular parity (but also sexual activity patterns or medical contraindications), limiting adjustment for potential confounders and precluding causal inference. Finally, the data reflect the situation in 2019 and may not capture subsequent changes in contraceptive availability, counseling practices, or broader social context, including the COVID-19 pandemic or the fluidity of patient motivations and choices in general.

Despite accompanying limitations of all post-hoc analyses, this database provides useful, so far unpublished data and insights into contraceptive use in Czechia that may be used for informed decision making, counseling and as a baseline reference for future studies.
