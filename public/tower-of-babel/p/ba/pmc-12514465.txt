SpiceSpectrum: Class-balanced dataset of commercially valuable spice cultivars
Authors: Md. Noushad Jahan Ramim, Samira Islam, Muhtasin Towkir, Md Mubtasim Fuad, Noor Mairukh Khan Arnob
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This data paper introduces a comprehensive visual dataset of 11 globally significant spices, comprising 11,000 high-quality images curated to support research and applications in agriculture, trade, and culinary arts. The dataset features the following spices: Black Pepper, Cardamom, Cinnamon, Cloves, Coriander, Cumin, Ginger, Nutmeg, Paprika, Saffron, and Turmeric, selected for their economic importance, medicinal value, and widespread culinary use across global cuisines. Images were collected from Bangladesh, capturing diverse lighting conditions, backgrounds, natural textures, packaging styles, and multiple angles to reflect real-world scenarios. Stored in JPG format at a standardized 512 × 512 pixel resolution, the dataset is organized into dedicated folders with 1000 images per spice, ensuring consistency and ease of use. This resource is designed to facilitate machine learning and computer vision applications, such as spice recognition, classification, and quality assessment, as well as AI-driven tools for agricultural analysis and trade monitoring. By providing a robust and diverse visual dataset, this collection aims to advance research and to assist innovation in the global spice industry.

Background

While substantial research has been conducted on crops [1], vegetables [2], and fruits [3], the spice domain remains significantly underrepresented in publicly available datasets, especially those based on global market demand and trade relevance. Spices are not only vital culinary ingredients but also play an essential role in health, cosmetics, and international trade.

To address this gap, we introduce a market-oriented image dataset featuring 11 of the most economically significant spices worldwide: Black Pepper, Cardamom, Cinnamon, Cloves, Coriander, Cumin, Ginger, Nutmeg, Paprika, Saffron, and Turmeric. These were selected based on global demand, market pricing, medicinal value, and widespread use across various cuisines—from Indian and Middle Eastern to Latin American and Southeast Asian. Many of these spices, such as saffron, turmeric, and ginger, are extensively studied for their medicinal and pharmacological properties, commonly used in Sments, teas, and traditional healing [4]. Economically, they are key commodities in global trade, with major producers including India, Iran, Indonesia, and Spain. However, most existing spice datasets are limited in scope—often focusing solely on Indian varieties, lacking diversity in image backgrounds, and failing to reflect real-world market conditions such as lighting variations, packaging differences, or spice quality. To statistically prove the superior image diversity of our entire dataset, we have employed the following metrics:

Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS): LPIPS evaluates the perceptual similarity between two images [6]. Unlike traditional metrics like Mean Squared Error (MSE), which compare pixel values directly, LPIPS uses the internal activations of a deep neural network (VGG in this case) to mimic human perception of similarity. A high LPIPS score between two images means they are perceptually different. We calculated the average LPIPS score across many randomly sampled pairs of images within a dataset. A higher average LPIPS score indicates greater intra-class diversity. This means the images within that category exhibit a wider variety of appearances, such as different lighting, angles, backgrounds, and arrangements. It is observed from Table 2 that our dataset achieves better LPIPS score than the Indian Spice Images dataset on all classes of spices. Feature Volume (Trace of Covariance): Trace of the covariance matrix is a plausible estimate for image diversity measurement in current literature [7]. we first passed each image through a pre-trained neural network (ResNet-50) to extract a high-dimensional feature vector that represents its content. The “Feature Volume” is calculated as the trace of the covariance matrix of these feature vectors. The trace of the covariance matrix sums the variances along each feature dimension. A higher trace value signifies that the features are more spread out in the feature space. This implies the dataset contains a richer and more varied set of visual patterns (textures, shapes, colors, etc.). It complements LPIPS by measuring the overall “volume” occupied by the dataset's features. It is clear from Table 2 that the Feature Volume scores for SpiceSpectrum are dramatically larger (e.g., 320.11 vs. 73.26 for Coriander), indicating a much wider spread of visual features. This suggests SpiceSpectrum contains spices photographed under more varied conditions.

Blind/Referenceless Image Spatial Quality Evaluator (BRISQUE): BRISQUE is a “no-reference” image quality assessment (IQA) model [8]. This means it can predict the perceptual quality of an image without needing a “perfect” or “pristine” version for comparison. It works by analyzing the image's pixel statistics and comparing them to the statistics of natural, high-quality images. It outputs a score that correlates with human judgments of quality. A lower BRISQUE score indicates higher image quality, meaning fewer undesirable artifacts like blur, noise, compression artifacts, or unnatural distortions. A higher score suggests the image is of lower quality. The BRISQUE scores for SpiceSpectrum are consistently lower than for Indian Spice Image Dataset (e.g., 24.08 vs. a very high 56.50 for Cinnamon) in Table 2. This implies that images in Indian Spice Image Dataset are more likely to suffer from blur, noise, or other distortions, while images in SpiceSpectrum are perceptually cleaner and more “natural”.

To overcome these limitations, our dataset was collected from various environments in Bangladesh, an active participant in South Asia’s spice trade. The images capture real-world scenarios, including diverse lighting, natural textures, packaging styles, and multiple angles, as shown in Fig. 1. This makes the dataset highly suitable for training machine learning models and developing AI-powered tools for agricultural analysis, trade monitoring, and culinary research.

Data Description

This dataset comprises a comprehensive collection of 11,000 high-quality images representing 11 globally significant spices, curated to reflect the diversity of the global spice market. Each spice category: Black Pepper, Cardamom, Cinnamon, Cloves, Coriander, Cumin, Ginger, Nutmeg, Paprika, Saffron, and Turmeric are organized into their own dedicated folder, with 1000 images per category. The dataset is class-balanced. As shown in Fig. 2, the images were captured under various lighting conditions and from multiple angles to ensure consistency and diversity, making the dataset well-suited for a variety of computer vision and AI-based applications.Fig. 2Folder organization of our SpiceSpectrum dataset.Fig 2:

Folder organization of our SpiceSpectrum dataset.

All images are stored in JPG format, a widely adopted standard that balances image quality with storage efficiency [9]. The resolution of each image is standardized at 512 × 512 pixels, ensuring clear and detailed visuals suitable for machine learning, visual recognition, and classification tasks. Within each category folder, images have been sequentially renamed to maintain an organized and systematic structure. Data collection procedure

The collection procedure was designed to ensure diversity in image representation while maintaining consistency in quality and format. Five data annotators were assigned to collect the images from local markets in Bangladesh, capturing real-world scenarios such as varying lighting conditions, natural textures, orientations, packaging styles, backgrounds, and multiple angles. We took photos of multiple spices of the same variety to increase the diversity of our dataset. Each image was taken under diverse settings where possible, with some images captured in natural daylight and others in indoor lighting to reflect real-world market and kitchen conditions. The data collection process was structured into five distinct tasks (Task 1 to Task 5) for a single category of spice, as shown in Fig. 6. Each task required the collection of 200 photos per spice, totalling 1000 images per spice across the 11 spices, resulting in a dataset of 11,000 images. The workflow involved parallel data collection by the five data collectors (Data collector 1 to Data collector 5), with each data collector independently collecting 200 photos per spice. The processed images were then uploaded and stored in a centralized Google Drive folder for seamless access and management, as depicted in Fig. 6.Fig. 6Data collection process we followed to collect 1000 images of Cumin. The images were then processed using a Python (version 3.9) script with the Pillow library to resize them to a uniform resolution of 512 × 512 pixels, renaming them sequentially within each spice category folder (e.g., black_pepper_1.jpg to BlackPepper_1000.jpg), and convert all images to JPG format, ensuring consistency and efficient storage for machine learning applications. This procedure ensured that all annotated images were uniformly archived and easily accessible for future use. The entire methodology for developing the dataset is outlined in Fig. 7.Fig. 7Complete dataset creation methodology, including data collection and post-processing procedure.Fig 7:

Sampling: A stratified sampling strategy was employed to curate a balanced dataset of 11,000 images across 11 spice categories. Images were systematically captured by five annotators from local markets under diverse environmental conditions, including varied lighting, angles, and backgrounds to ensure authentic representation of real-world variability. The parallelized collection approach, with equal contributions per annotator and category, minimized bias and maximized intra-class diversity, thereby enhancing the dataset’s robustness for downstream computer vision tasks.

Annotation Standards: Since images were directly uploaded by annotators into predefined category-specific Google Drive folders, manual annotation was unnecessary, ensuring inherent label accuracy. Clear guidelines were provided to annotators to maintain consistency during image collection. Regular cross-validation among annotators further minimized discrepancies, and only confidently identified images were retained. Metadata on capture conditions was preserved to support transparency and facilitate future dataset validation or extension.

Preprocessing Code and Metadata Availability: All images were uniformly resized, converted, and renamed using a Python script with the Pillow library. Accompanying metadata, including camera specifications and capture conditions, are embedded in the image files to support reproducibility. The full preprocessing code and metadata are openly accessible at https://github.com/noushad999/SpiceSpectrum

Limitations

All of the images contained in the SpiceSpectrum dataset were collected from Bangladesh, potentially limiting the dataset’s representativeness to other regions of the world. Hence, we tried our best to capture images in diverse environments to increase representativeness as much as possible. Even though we collected images of 11 spice varieties, some commercially valuable spices such as Vanilla, Star Anise, Fenugreek, Mustard Seeds, Allspice, etc. are not included in our dataset, indicating future scope of further enhancing our dataset.
