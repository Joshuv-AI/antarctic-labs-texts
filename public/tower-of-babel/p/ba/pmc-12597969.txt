Predicting the Pharmacokinetics of T‐Cell Engagers as a Function of Target‐Mediated Drug Disposition
Authors: Mark Penney, Alberto Ippolito, Elisa Fevola, Scott Rata, Liam Brown, Pablo Morentin Gutierrez, Rhys D O Jones
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTT‐Cell Engagers (TCEs) are biological multi‐specific molecules designed to co‐engage targets on T cells and on a target cell for destruction. Molecular formats are typically derived from IgG monoclonal antibodies or similar fusion proteins with FcRn‐mediated recycling, such as albumin binders. Despite this similarity, TCEs are often observed to have much attenuated half‐lives of a few days, compared to the weeks expected of IgG monoclonal antibodies; furthermore, the usual ranking of half‐lives in humans being greater than those in the non‐human primate (NHP) is not preserved and we demonstrate that the usual methods of PK scaling by allometry are poor predictors of human PK. The half‐life of biological molecules may be reduced due to clearance from target‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD). In this paper we identify how the TMDD of the TCE from interactions with both the T‐cell (via CD3) and its tumor‐associated antigen on the target cell determines the PK across species and present a method for the prediction of TCE PK in both the NHP and human patients. This method was tested against a selection of published TCEs and 16 of 18 TCEs' half‐lives were predicted within 2‐fold, compared to only 10 from 17 by allometry alone, and with a mean absolute error of 1.48 versus 2.22, respectively.Keywords: pharmacokinetic modeling, pharmacokinetic prediction, pharmacokinetics, target‐mediated drug disposition, T‐cell engagers

Introduction

T‐Cell Engagers (TCEs) represent a rapidly expanding class of therapeutic proteins, primarily in oncology indications with nine now approved for a range of indications, initially in liquid and now solid tumors, with over a 100 more in development.

The principle of these multi‐specific proteins is that they create a synthetic immune synapse by binding to one or more tumor‐associated antigens (TAAs) on the tumor cell and an activating receptor on a T cell (most commonly CD3). The resulting association leads to T cell activation and subsequent tumor cell killing by the proximal immune cells. The first TCE to gain approval was blinatumomab, a CD3 and CD19 bi‐specific T‐cell engager (BiTE) molecule [1], for the indication of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). This molecule is highly potent and effective at clearing target cells in vitro at picomolar concentrations. However, its relatively small size with a molecular weight of about 50 kDa means that blinatumomab is subject to rapid renal clearance, thus requiring dosing via continuous infusion to maintain therapeutic levels of exposure. To access more convenient routes of administration later molecules have sought to reduce the clearance, either by adding an Fc arm to the molecule (e.g., AMG701 (pavurutamab) [2]), adopting a molecular format similar to an IgG (e.g., mosunetuzemab [3]), or by the addition of a further binding arm that binds serum albumin (HPN328 [4]). Each serves to increase the size of the molecule beyond that subject to significant renal clearance and then confers a further decrease in clearance by allowing the molecule to undergo FcRn‐mediated recycling [5].

Such revised molecular formats, however, do not necessarily display the same low clearance and long terminal half‐lives typical of IgG antibody therapeutics, Fc‐fusion proteins or albumin‐binding nanobodies [6, 7, 8]. Table 1 lists the published half‐lives in patients of 29 clinical stage TCEs and while some, such as mosunetuzemab, have half‐lives of over 2 weeks [3], many constructs' half‐lives are less than 10 days. The half‐life of an IgG biologic in humans can normally be estimated well from that in the cynomolgus monkey using allometric scaling [6, 28], with a similar relationship seen for the hFcRn (Tg32) mouse [29]. However, an inspection of Table 1 suggests this correlation and also the rank ordering are not conserved for TCEs, with many occurrences of shorter half‐lives in humans versus the monkey.

In this paper we present a model for TCE PK that allows for the prediction of the human half‐life that is superior to allometric scaling. The model consists of three components. First, we have an “intrinsic” clearance, or that not impacted by Target Mediated Drug Disposition (TMDD), which is still scalable allometrically, and may only be measured at a high dose (if tolerable) where target saturation is assured, or more readily from pre‐clinical species where the binding is not cross‐reactive (e.g., hFcRn Tg32 mouse). The second component is the impact of TMDD via the binding to the activating receptor on T cells (here CD3) and its internalization. The third is the complementary binding to the TAA(s) and internalization, which is determined for each of the receptors that the TCE binds to and built into the PK model to derive a fuller projection for the PK of the TCE in patients. We apply this model of TCE PK to a series of case studies of TAAs and the TCEs that target them, demonstrating that the relationship of half‐life between the observed pre‐clinical and clinical PK can be explained by this combination of the intrinsic PK and the TMDD. Additionally, accounting for the CD3 and TAA‐mediated TMDD permits accurate prediction of the PK of the TCE in the cynomolgus monkey and then in human, and superior to that of the application of allometric scaling alone.

Prediction of the Human Half‐Life Allowing for TMDD: Model Equations

The model consists of the set of Equations (1–6). Unless otherwise specified, the PK of TCEs is assumed to be described by a two‐compartment model with central and peripheral compartments of volumes VC and VP, respectively. The transport between these compartments is characterized by the volumetric rate Q. Intrinsic and TMDD mediated clearances are assumed to occur in the central compartment. The intrinsic clearance CL is set according to the assigned terminal half‐life as ln2VC+VPhalflife [30]. The free drug DFree can bind to either CD3 or TAA; a single association rate kon is assumed and the appropriate binding determined by the respective dissociation rates of konKDCD3 and konKDTAA. Bound drug DCD3 or DTAA are lost at the rates of kintCD3 or kintTAA, respectively. Although the therapeutic mechanism of action is that the drug binds simultaneously to the CD3 and the TAA, that is, forming a trimer, we have not built this into the model. Different trimer models exist in literature [31, 32, 33, 34] but we found that an implementation of the method in [33] led to a negligble difference to the predicted half‐life in the scenarios assessed in this paper; given the additional uncertainty due to the different methods and the complications to the code, trimer formation was omitted.

CD3‐Mediated TMDD

Representative values for CD3 expression on T cells of 50,000/T cell and T cell count in blood of 1500/μL are applied [33]. CD3 internalization rate has been measured by [35] at 1.4%/minute, equating to a half‐life of about 36 min. The published in vitro measured KDCD3 value (typically measured by SPR) for the respective TCE is assumed to be the in vivo binding affinity: a fast kon rate of 106/M/s is assumed and the dissociation rate calculated as the product of the association rate and dissociation constant KD.

With all but one of the TCEs considered here binding to the CD3, sample simulations were generated showing the effect of CD3‐mediated clearance in the absence of TAA binding. A generic set of PK parameters was assumed for the TCE of V c = 40 mL/kg, V p = 60 mL/kg, CL set to produce a half‐life of 15 days and Q = 10 × CL. These were simulated for a single IV bolus dose of 0.01 mg/kg for a range of affinities of binding to CD3 from 1000 nM to 1 nM, in half‐log steps. A corresponding simulation was run for a fixed affinity of 10 nM for doses of 0.01–10 mg/kg.

TAA‐Mediated TMDD

The extent of TAA‐mediated clearance of the TCE will be determined by the overall TAA burden accessible to the TCE and its turnover, and as such is specific to that TAA. For the TAA of BCMA, the TMDD model developed by Cai et al. is adopted [36]. For CD20, the PK‐B‐cell model described by Yu et al. for ofatumumab was adopted [37]. For all other TAAs, clinical PK demonstrating TMDD PK from TAA‐targeting antibodies was found in the literature, and the data digitized using WebPlotDigitizer [29, 38, 39]. This data was fitted to a two‐compartment PK model with a TMDD component and the published KDTAA value for the antibody assumed, to derive an apparent TAA concentration and degradation rate that were applied to our model (In practice, our workflow actually used our model described above but with the CD3 binding turned off so that the derived parameters were already inserted in the correct units in the model for later simulation). The estimated values are assumed to apply unscaled to the TCE in both the cynomolgus monkey and human patients. No adjustment is made for the valency of the source or target molecules. No such data could be found for DLL3 or GPRC5D and so these predictions are made for CD3‐binding only.

TCE Selection

The TCEs listed in Table 1 were selected where pre‐clinical (NHP) and/or clinical PK, and the respective binding affinities (dissociation constants, KD) to CD3 and the TAA, have been reported.

Predicting the Half‐Life of TCEs in the Cynomolgus Monkey

The set of Equations (1–6) is simulated with the generic CD3‐mediated clearance and the TAA‐mediated clearance, assuming that the parameters derived for the CD3‐ and TAA‐mediated clearances apply without scaling to the cynomolgus monkey. Generic PK parameters are assumed of V c = 40 mL/kg, V p = 60 mL/kg, CL set to produce a terminal half‐life of 9 days and Q = 10 × CL for all TCEs except Pf‐07062119, which was scaled from mouse PK data according to Avery et al. [29, 39]. Our dataset includes some smaller molecular weight (~50 kDa) TCEs such as HPN328 and SAR444200, which may be expected to undergo significant renal clearance and therefore have a shorter intrinsic half‐life, but aim to avoid this via albumin binding. We verified separately that the albumin binding affinity was sufficient to achieve a serum albumin‐like half‐life using a model with an additional TMDD component explicitly modeling the albumin binding and furthermore that this larger model replicated the results of the simpler model assuming the generic half‐life.

SAR444200 binds TCRαβ rather than CD3: we assumed in the simulations that this internalized like CD3, although this has no consequence on the predicted PK for this particular molecule as the TAA‐mediated clearance dominates.

Simulations are run for the reported doses, and dosing intervals and duration where also reported; if the study details are not given then the terminal half‐life following a single dose is calculated. The half‐life is calculated within the simulation as ln(2)/(d(D free)/dt/D free): we have not output time‐matched samples and then derived the half‐life from an NCA of the discrete dataset. Where the reported data is given for a range of doses, the median of the half‐lives obtained from the simulation of those doses is used for the comparator.

Prediction accuracy is assessed from a plot of the observed versus predicted half‐life. The number of predictions within two‐fold of the observed value, the mean absolute fold error, and the bias (mean fold error) are calculated.

Predicting the Half‐Life of TCEs in Human Patients

The set of Equations (1–6) is simulated with the generic CD3‐mediated clearance and the TAA‐mediated clearance. Generic PK parameters were assumed initially of V c = 40 mL/kg, V p = 60 mL/kg, CL set to produce a terminal half‐life of 15 days and Q = 10 × CL. However, the linear PK parameters were updated based on available PK from the cyno as follows: the local sensitivity of the apparent half‐life of the cyno prediction on the assumed 9 day half‐life was assessed. If it is sensitive then we judge that the cyno linear PK half‐life is identifiable from the data: the model refitted with half‐life as the only free parameter and this is then scaled to human using allometry with an exponent of 0.15. The insensitivity of the apparent half‐life on the assumed generic value indicates that the target‐mediated clearance is dominant; the linear PK is not being observed and so unidentifiable, and the generic values retained. An arbitrary value of a 10% increase in the apparent half‐life for an increase in half‐life from 9 to 15 days was used as the sensitivity threshold (in practice, we found a clear separation between those TCEs dominated by target‐mediated clearances and those that were not).

Simulations are run for each of the reported doses and scenarios, such as dose level, single or repeat doses and where half‐lives are reported following the initial dose versus that at steady‐state. Where an observed half‐life has been reported for a dose range rather than an individual dose, we use the median of the half‐lives from our simulations of that range of doses for the calculation of the prediction accuracy and plots.

In addition, some anti‐BCMA TCEs report an increase in half‐life after repeat dosing. For these cases and where the extension has been ascribed by the authors to be due to the depletion of the target cells, we have treated these as a separate prediction scenario: further simulations are performed where the binding to the TAA is turned off, modeling the removal of the TAA‐mediated clearance as may be expected if those cells have been depleted.

Prediction accuracy is assessed from a plot of the observed versus predicted half‐life. The number of predictions within twofold of the observed value, the mean absolute fold error, and the bias (mean fold error) are calculated.

Generic Results due to CD3 Binding

All but one of the TCEs considered in the case studies utilizes CD3 as the T cell binding receptor. Figure 2 shows the PK profiles associated with our default human PK, CD3 expression and turnover parameters, but no TAA binding, for a range of binding affinities to CD3 for a low dose of 0.01 mg/kg, shown in Figure 2a, and a binding affinity to CD3 of 10 nM for a range of doses, as shown in Figure 2b. For a low CD3 binding affinity of 1000 nM where the CD3 binding is weak, the TCE displays the half‐life of its intrinsic PK of 15 days. As the binding affinity increases, the observed half‐life drops. With an affinity of 10 nM—a representative CD3 binding affinity of “first generation” TCEs (see Supporting Information)—the half‐life is reduced to 4.4 days. Increasing the dose leads to the expected saturation of CD3 and a return to the intrinsic half‐life; however, this requires a dose that is generally large for a TCE therapy, with even 1 mg/kg showing a reduced half‐life with these parameters. Note also that at doses below 0.1 mg/kg the PK is essentially linear, consistent with the fourth phase of TMDD PK according to Peletier and Gabrielsson [40]. Thus, TCEs with a CD3 affinity in the region of 10 nM or higher may be expected to observe half‐lives of a few days in vivo, even though the intrinsic PK anticipated from their IgG‐like or albumin‐binding structure may be weeks.

Discussion

We have presented a model of TCE PK in which the elimination half‐life of the TCE is determined by the summation of the clearances due to the intrinsic non‐specific clearance and the clearances due to its target bindings. This method is superior to allometric scaling alone, which has a strong bias to overestimate human half‐lives and cannot account for human half‐lives shorter than those in the monkey. Our inclusion of the target‐mediated clearances explains much of this difference in half‐lives observed for TCEs, both across TAAs and within those TCEs targeting the same TAA. Generally, the lack of correlation observed between the half‐lives in cynomolgus monkey versus the human is due to the variation in affinity (cross‐reactivity) between the two, meaning that the major route of clearance for a given TCE in one species (intrinsic, CD3‐mediated or TAA‐mediated) may be the same or different from that in the other, and thus show no apparent trend when looking across a range of TCEs. Taking a specific case, the example of the two anti‐CD19 TCEs of AZD0486 (surovatamig) and CLN‐978, the different affinities for CD3 (no binding in cyno versus 16 nM [41, 42]) determine their respective IgG‐like versus TMDD‐driven short half‐lives. Similarly, our model explains the difference seen in the longer half‐lives of talquetamab anticipated from the cynomolgus monkey PK and observed for the RP2Ds (about 10 days) versus the short half‐lives observed at the much lower initial human doses (< 6 days) as a function of the saturation of the CD3 binding [43]. These examples illustrate that consideration of the target‐mediated elimination is necessary for an accurate estimation of the TCE half‐life and that the application of allometry alone for scaling is scientifically incorrect. The method explains the retention of linearity PK at these low doses relative to their binding affinities as the observation of the fourth phase of TMDD identified by Peletier and Garbrielsson [40]. At its most basic level, this model provides a means to identify which TCEs are likely to show the long half‐lives expected of their IgG or albumin‐binding formats, or those with short half‐lives caused by their target binding.

Furthermore, the model provides an accurate quantitative prediction of the PK half‐life in first the cynomolgus monkey, which is estimated without prior in vivo data, and secondly human, which then outperforms the standard method of scaling from the cyno PK using allometry alone: each measure of prediction accuracy is improved and, with the exception of ERY974, each individual prediction is closer to the true value.

As presented here, the model contains some simplifications that could be improved further: the predictions for the cynomolgus monkey and then some for the human rely on an assumed single generic value for the intrinsic half‐life and while this works quite well here (perhaps as the range of half‐lives, even for TCEs, is rather narrow), it might be improved upon if either were to be estimated from PK in the hFcRn‐knock‐in Tg32 mouse [29]. In such cases, a full PK profile prediction could then be performed by applying conventional allometric scaling to the volumes and any inter‐compartmental clearance [6]. We omit entirely the formation of the “trimer” of simultaneously binding the CD3 and the TAA, which is the mechanism of action of TCEs. This is a convenient simplification of the model, avoiding the need for additional equations and parameters and a choice of which of the different trimer models available is the most accurate, as we found that implementing the Jiang model [33] and assuming that the trimer was subject to both routes of TMDD caused only negligible changes in the predicted half‐life for the TCEs in this paper.

We have assumed generic values of CD3 expression and T‐cell count, and that the internalization in human resting T cells observed by Liu et al. [35] applies universally to all CD3‐binding TCEs: species‐ and strain‐specific values and their variation over time and with dosing, along with explicit measurement of internalization for the TCE of interest may improve this. A potentially weak assumption is made, along with, we accept, a risk of circular reasoning, that the clearance due to the TAA can be derived from the TMDD‐affected PK observed for standard antibodies: this assumes that the TMDD is determined more by the intrinsic turnover of the receptor rather than a feature of the binding molecules (epitope, affinity or valency). That the model predicts well serves as a validation of this, with the notable exception of ERY974, which we discuss below.

Moreover, levels of T‐cells (so CD3) and the target cells (TAA) may change over the course of dosing: it is beyond the scope of this model to predict these changes but we would expect it to track and predict the associated changes in half‐life, if the time course was known. In this regard, an increase in half‐life for anti‐BCMA TCEs has been reported and ascribed to the depletion of the BCMA‐expressing cells, reducing the BCMA‐mediated clearance [44]. Where this claim has been made, we attempted to account for this by switching off the TAA‐mediated turnover (equivalent to no TAA expression) and this results in improved predictions for the half‐life at steady‐state dosing.

Relying on precedent data on TAA‐mediated TMDD leaves a gap in the model when such data is not available and here our predictions for the anti‐DLL3 and GPRC5D TCEs only consider additional clearance due to CD3 binding. Novel TAAs could be addressed by building such an analysis into a cynomolgus monkey PKPD or toxicology study but the potential benefit must be weighed against any additional animal usage.

Of the poorly predicted TCEs we consider that ERY974 is the only true “miss” for the workflow. ERY974 has been observed to have a half‐life of about 3 days in the cynomolgus monkey, showing linear PK across a dose range of 0.1–10 μg/kg [45]. Its CD3 affinity of 155 nM is too low to be expected to cause such a short half‐life (see Figure 2a,b). However, the GPC3 binding of 1.5 nM is predicted to lead to a much shorter half‐life of 21 h and is the cause of our under‐prediction. The TAA‐mediated clearance here is determined from Zhu et al. [46] from a study of GC33, the parent of the anti‐GPC3 binding arm in ERY974. In humans, where the CD3 binding is of slightly lower affinity at 207 nM, the GPC3 affinity of 1.5 nM again leads to a prediction of a 21 h half‐life; a range of 25.2–121 h following the first dose and 47.6–96.0 h following the third dose [47] was observed, which suggests such a significant clearance mechanism in some patients is plausible. In support of this, the same strong TAA‐mediated clearance is the cause of our accurate prediction of the anti‐GPC3 TCE SAR444200 in humans, observed to have a 26 h half‐life [48]. The failure to predict the ERY974 PK half‐life, at least in the cynomolgus monkey, would appear to be a specific feature of this TCE that our model does not account for.

Regarding the other predictions more than two‐fold off the observed value, in cynomolgus monkey we under‐predict AMG160 (PSMA) at 2.9 days versus 6 days observed [49], although we predict well for the anti‐PSMA HPN424 at 3.1 days versus 3.5 days observed [50]. The anti‐MUC17 TCE AMG199 is under‐predicted at its 0.1 mg/kg dose at 1.7 days versus 3.9 days observed, although we predict 2.3 days versus 3.4 days for the 1 mg/kg dose, suggesting some variability in PK and perhaps an over‐estimation for the cynomolgus monkey of the TAA burden when extrapolated from human patients [51]. Tarlatamab shows linear PK and a long (12 day) half‐life in the cynomolgus monkey [52] whereas we would predict a CD3‐driven shorter half‐life of 4 days; however, the corresponding prediction for human is good at 6.5 days versus 5.7 days observed [53]. In humans, we under‐predict the first dose half‐life of anti‐CD20 mosunetuzumab at 3 days versus 6.5 days observed [54], similarly with 10.3 days predicted versus 16.1 days for steady‐state dosing [55] and correspondingly under‐predict at 4 days versus 7.6 days observed for the other anti‐CD20 considered, glofitamab [55]: we may speculate that the CD20‐mediated clearance extrapolated from ofatatumumab over‐predicts the TAA burden in these target heavily‐pretreated populations.

In summary, we propose a model for TCE PK in which the elimination half‐life is explained as the combination of the intrinsic clearance of the molecular format and its target‐mediated clearances. While TMDD is commonplace for biological molecules and often cited as the likely cause for rapid clearance, we have quantified these phenomena and developed an accurate method for the estimation of the half‐life of the TCE in humans as well as the cynomolgus monkey with no requirement for in vivo data. We hope that the method may be applied by others to TCEs in development, with improvements and refinements and, in particular, any apparent confounders, published to further the collective knowledge on these exciting and novel therapeutic modalities.
