Photocatalytic, Scalable Anti-Markovnikov Hydrocarboxylation of Unactivated Alkenes via Amine-Mediated CO2 •– Generation from Formate
Authors: Pintu Ghosh, Bikiran Pal, Debabrata Maiti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The use of formate salt as a C1 synthon offers a practical and accessible carboxylating reagent relative to the gaseous alternatives. However, photochemical transient CO2 •– generation from formate and its utilization for carboxylation reactions is mostly restricted to the activated alkenes. Herein, we report a general, photocatalytic platform for the selective anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylation of unactivated and activated alkenes using formate salt. The scope of the protocol is demonstrated through the selective hydrocarboxylation of a range of alkenes, independent of the chain length. The control experiments and preliminary mechanistic studies indicate synergy between the tertiary aminium radical cation-mediated hydrogen atom abstraction (HAA) and efficient hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) from the silyl hydride underpin the controlled hydrocarboxylation process. Mechanistic studies also indicate that the radical-polar crossover of the carboxylated alkyl radical can be avoided by the judicious choice of the HAT reagent, providing access to unactivated alkenes in the hydrocarboxylation reaction utilizing formate. This strategy using light energy is mild, robust, and effective for the carboxylation of various alkenes under ambient conditions. Our method enables a modular route for the controlled, steady-state release of CO2 •– using formate, providing a unique pathway for the carboxylation of organic molecules. The application of this protocol has been demonstrated through the large-scale synthesis, synthetic manipulation, and synthesis of isotopically labeled aliphatic carboxylic acids from unactivated and activated alkenes.

Introduction

The carboxylation of hydrocarbons, such as alkenes, is highly desirable in synthetic chemistry owing to the resulting enhancement of water solubility, bioactivity, utility in the polymer synthesis, and value as a versatile functional handle. − Among various approaches, the use of CO2 as a carboxylating agent is the most commonly employed technique. − Recently, the photocatalytic approach has been explored elegantly by Yu and others. − Despite its utility, the activation of CO2 typically requires harsh conditions and/or high pressure due to its high kinetic and thermodynamic stability as well as its inherently low reactivity. , Moreover, the direct single-electron activation of CO2 to generate carbon dioxide radical anion (CO2 •–) demands highly reducing conditions (E 1/2 (CO2/CO2 •–) = – 2.2 V vs SCE). − In this context, formate serves as an excellent alternative for the generation of CO2 •– due to the low C–H bond dissociation energy (BDE = 86 kcal/mol) and accessible oxidation potential (E ox = 1.25 V vs SCE). In addition, the use of formate avoids gas handling issues, favors monocarboxylation over multicarboxylation pathways that sometimes occur under bulk CO2 atmosphere. Although the reactivity of formate as a two electron reductant has been extensively explored in the traditional metal-based catalysis, its application to generate CO2 •– and further valorization are limited. , Trialkylamines serve as excellent alternatives for the CO2 •– generation from formate via the formation of trialkylaminium radical cation (R′3N•+). The comparatively lower reactivity of the trialkylaminium radical cations (R′3N•+) aids in higher selectivity in the carboxylation reactions. Li and co-workers elegantly demonstrated the trialkylamine-mediated generation of CO2 •– from formate, which can then participate in single-electron reductions of aryl and alkyl halides, with CO2 serving as the ultimate source of carboxylic acids. , Subsequently, this strategy was applied by others to achieve dicarboxylation of activated alkenes or the hydrocarboxylation of activated imines. In all these cases, CO2 was used together with formate to enable the efficient incorporation of the carboxylic acid group. , These CO2/formate strategies, operating through a radical-polar crossover (RPC) mechanism, enabled either selective Markovnikov carboxylation or dicarboxylation of activated alkenes. In this context, the thiyl radical-mediated CO2 •– generation has been exploited to accomplish radical chain reduction of various aryl (pseudo)­halides. , Recently, this thiol-catalyzed CO2 •– generation for the carboxylation of alkenes and alkene-type substrates has been demonstrated by several research groups. − However, this approach has predominantly enabled the carboxylation of activated alkenes, whereas the only reported literature of hydrocarboxylation of C4 alkenes (i.e., alkenes located two carbons away from a functional group) was not applicable to activated alkenes and longer chain alkenes. Hence, the development of a general platform for the anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylation of both activated and unactivated alkenes using formate is highly desirable. Recognizing these advances, we envisioned that the direct use of abundant, bench-stable formate as the sole carboxylating agent without reliance on gaseous CO2 remains an exciting opportunity for the selective carboxylation reaction. Moreover, the broad application of this approach to feedstock unactivated alkenes has remained elusive due to the subdued reactivity of these alkenes.

The efforts to employ this trialkylaminium radical cation-mediated CO2 •– generation strategy from formate salts for the anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylation of unactivated alkenes have been so far unsuccessful. This shortcoming may be attributed to the challenging radical-polar crossover (RPC) of the carboxylated alkyl radicals derived from unactivated alkenes, or to the inefficiency of available HAT reagents (Figure c). We questioned whether it would be possible to devise a strategy wherein we can avoid this RPC challenge by leveraging trialkylamines radical cation-mediated CO2 •– generation from formate. We hypothesized that following the addition of CO2 •– to the terminal position of the unactivated alkene, the resulting carboxylated alkyl radical would quickly undergo selective HAT with an efficient HAT reagent, thereby circumventing RPC step. Our group has recently developed a synergistic triple catalysis model for the formation of CO2 •– from CO2 and couple with its consumption in anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylation of alkenes. Building on this advance, we sought to determine whether bench stable, solid formate salts could be used directly instead of gaseous CO2 for the general anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylation of both unactivated and activated alkenes in a scalable manner. We sought to harness the synergy of the trialkylamines radical cation-mediated hydrogen atom abstraction (HAA) and an appropriate HAT reagent to achieve controlled anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylation of alkenes using formate. This synergistic platform for CO2 •– generation from abundant formate salts offers an effective alternative for desired carboxylation reactions and can potentially be used for scalable hydrocarboxylation of unactivated and activated olefins.

Results and Discussion

Formate salts are stable, cheap, and abundant C1 precursors for carboxylation reactions. However, a carbon-centered radical must be generated to activate them for carboxylation. The previous methods based on trialkylamine- or thiol-mediated activation were successful for the CO2 •– generation; however, the long-chain unactivated alkene hydrocarboxylation remained underexplored due to the inherent poor reactivity and instability of the CO2 •–. We leveraged this opportunity for the anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylation of unactivated alkenes which are widely available and industrially relevant substrate class. While the generation of the CO2 •– anion from formate was achieved using (Ir­[dF­(CF3)­ppy]2(dtbpy))­PF6 as the photocatalyst and DABCO as HAA, the subsequent HAT to the carboxylated alkyl radical remained a significant challenge. A wide range of hydrogen atom donors were evaluated under the standard reaction conditions, among those only a few silyl hydrides produced the desired product. The superiority of silyl hydrides as HAT reagents is well-known for their favorable thermodynamic properties and the stability of the silicon-centered radicals under mild conditions.

Reaction Optimization

We attempted the hydrocarboxylation reaction of 12 using KHCO2 (2 equiv) as the carboxylic acid source, (Ir­[dF­(CF3)­ppy]2(dtbpy))­PF6 (1 mol %) as the photocatalyst, DABCO (1 equiv) as the HAA reagent, tris­(trimethylsilyl)­silane (TTMSS) (2 equiv), Cs2CO3 (3 equiv) as the base in the DMSO solvent, and irradiating the solution at 35 °C using 456 nm blue LEDs for 24 h. The product formed in 55% yield along with the formation of unwanted reduced alkane product (15%). In order to enhance the yield of the product and improve selectivity, we attempted to modify the reaction parameters. At first, we altered the amount of HAT reagent TTMSS, however, either reducing or increasing the amount had a deleterious effect on the reaction outcome. Upon further analysis of the reaction conditions, we noted that DABCO plays a critical role in HAA from formate; hence, we next varied the amount of this HAA reagent. To our delight, we observed that lowering it to 50 mol % significantly improved the reactivity providing only the desired anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylated product in 76% yield. This is attributed to the requirement of DABCO in the catalytic amount, as shown in the plausible mechanism of the reaction (Figure a). We employed various optimization conditions to test the impact on the desired product yield (see the Supporting Information). Through detailed optimization, we found that potassium formate was the optimal salt along with the (Ir­[dF­(CF3)­ppy]2(dtbpy))­PF6 photocatalyst and DABCO as the suitable HAA reagent, commercially available TTMSS to be the superior HAT reagent and Cs2CO3 as the suitable base in the DMSO solvent (see Supporting Information for the detailed optimization conditions).

Mechanistic Hypothesis

Our mechanistic proposal for this anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylation reaction is shown in Figure . The photocatalyst (Ir­[dF­(CF3)­ppy]2(dtbpy))­PF6, 1 would undergo photoexcitation under 456 nm blue light to generate 2. The excited-state photocatalyst could then be subjected to reductive quenching in the presence of trialkylamine 4 which would eventually generate the reduced Ir­(II) complex 3 and oxidized trialkylamines radical cation 5. The intermediate 5 thus formed via the single-electron transfer (SET) event would abstract the hydrogen (BDE of N–H+ ∼100 kcal/mol) of the C–H bond (BDE = 86 kcal/mol) of formate 7 to engender the metastable CO2 radical anion (8) and trialkylamines cation 6. Then, Cs2CO3 would abstract the acidic proton from 6 to regenerate the trialkylamine 4. The CO2 •– would readily add to the terminal site of the unactivated alkene 9 to induce the formation of the carboxylated alkyl radical 10. The radical thus generated would undergo hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) with a suitable reagent to produce anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylated product 11. The photocatalytic cycle would be completed by the single-electron oxidation of the reduced photocatalyst 3.

Mechanistic Studies

We performed several mechanistic studies of this formate-mediated anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylation through a stepwise experimental approach aimed at elucidating the prevailing reaction pathway. Luminescence quenching studies revealed that both DABCO and formate are capable of quenching the excited state of (Ir­[dF­(CF3)­ppy]2(dtbpy))­PF6, indicating that either species might initiate electron-transfer processes leading to radical formation (Figure a). The decrease in emission intensity was significantly higher in the case of DABCO compared to KHCO2 at identical quencher concentrations as observed in the luminescence quenching experiment (Figure a), suggesting that DABCO can interact directly with the excited state of the Ir photocatalyst. This was further supported by cyclic voltammetry (CV) experiments (Figure g), which showed that DABCO possesses a lower oxidation potential (E ox = 0.88 V vs Ag/AgCl) compared to formate (E ox = 1.21 V vs Ag/AgCl), making it more thermodynamically favorable to undergo oxidation and quench the Ir­(III)* reductively. To probe the radical nature of the reaction, we conducted the standard reaction of 12 in the presence of 3 equiv of 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO) (Figure b). This reaction did not produce the desired hydrocarboxylated product, which indicated the formation of radical intermediates in the reaction. To test the feasibility of solvent being the hydrogen atom source or the possibility of the radical-polar crossover of the carboxylated alkyl radical and subsequent protonation of the carbanion from DMSO, the reaction of vinylcyclohexane was performed under the standard reaction conditions using DMSO-d 6 as the solvent (Figure c). No deuterium incorporation was observed in the desired hydrocarboxylated product 13.

Mechanistic studies. (a) Luminescence quenching study. (b) Reaction in the presence of radical scavengers. (c) Reaction using DMSO-d 6. (d) Reaction using sodium formate-d. (e) Feasibility of RPC. (f) ReactIR study. (g) CV study.

Then, we performed the standard reaction using sodium formate-d as the carboxylating agent (Figure d). The reaction yielded 45% hydrocarboxylated product without any deuterium incorporation, suggesting that formate is unlikely to serve as the hydrogen atom source. The feasibility of the RPC was tested using 20 equiv. CD3OD under the reaction conditions (Figure e). We did not observe any deuterium incorporation in the product, indicating the nonexistence of the RPC. To provide evidence for the generation of CO2 •– in the reaction, we performed in situ React IR FT-IR experiment under the standard reaction conditions (Figure f). We observed the appearance of the characteristic signal for CO2 •– at 1605 cm–1. , Further monitoring the React IR over 30 min showed an initial rise in CO2 •– concentration, which eventually plateaued (see Supporting Information), indicating the rate of formation of CO2 •– was balanced by the rate of consumption during the carboxylation event.

Substrate Scopes

After finding the suitable experimental conditions, we examined the scope of this synergistic CO2 •– generation and controlled HAT protocol for the anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylation of unactivated alkenes. We evaluated various terminal unactivated alkenes irrespective of their chain length. Interestingly, in all the cases, the desired products were formed with excellent yields and selectivities. The unactivated alkenes containing cyclic six or five-membered ring substituents were hydrocarboxylated with excellent yields (13 and 14, 83% and 78%, respectively). The compatibility of the internal alkene was tested using a molecule containing both internal and terminal double bond, where selective hydrocarboxylation occurred at the terminal double bond to produce 15 with 82% yield. This highlighted the effectiveness of the protocol in the selective hydrocarboxylation of terminal alkenes within a mixture containing both terminal and internal alkenes. Along with the monosubstituted alkenes, the disubstituted terminal unactivated alkenes provided the corresponding hydrocarboxylated products (16-17) in very good yields (75, 78% yields, respectively). Among these, the hydrocarboxylated product 17 served as a key structural motif in GAT1 inhibitor compounds. The previous protocol, which adhered the direct reduction pathway of CO2 to CO2 •–, converted the unactivated alkenes containing 1,n-HAT sites into the dicarboxylated products, which lacked control over the anti-Markovnikov selectivity. In our method, the substrates containing possible hydrogen atoms available for 1,5-HAT or 1,6-HAT were selectively monocarboxylated at the terminal sites (18-20) avoiding the possible HAT events and corresponding dicarboxylation.

The long-chain fatty acids serve as the key building blocks for lipids, biomaterials, and surfactants and are widely used in the production of soaps, detergents, lubricants, and biodiesel. The synthesis of these fatty acids using simple, abundant, less-expensive starting materials such as hydrocarbons is highly desirable. The simple long-chain hydrocarbons containing terminal double bonds were anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylated to the corresponding long-chain fatty acids 21-29 with very good yields (61–72%) irrespective of the chain length. The light alkenes were hydrocarboxylated to 21 and 22 with 72% and 68% yields. The product pelargonic acid (24) has broad applications in antifungal, herbicide, plasticizer industries. The product 27 is also known as palmitic acid, which has widespread applications in both industry and academia, particularly in the preparation of soaps, cosmetics, and surfactants. The amine or phenol tethered unactivated alkenes were also efficiently anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylated (30-32). The substrate containing N-Boc was converted into product 33 in 64% yield, demonstrating the functional group tolerance of our protocol. The 1-adamantanol tethered allylic alkene underwent anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylation (34), effectively overriding RPC and subsequent dicarboxylation events. The menthyl ether derivative was well tolerated in product 35, highlighting the compatibility of the method with sterically hindered chiral tags. Unactivated alkenes containing reactive functional groups such as ketone, ester afforded the products 36-38 in very good yields.

To demonstrate the generality of this protocol, we evaluated the reactivity of activated alkenes such as styrenyl derivatives under the standard reaction conditions. The reactions proceeded with excellent yields and high selectivity toward the anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylated products (Figure ). The electron-withdrawing as well as electron-donating functional groups on the styrenes were well sustained under the reaction conditions (39-43, 46). Substrates containing halide functional groups such as fluoro and chloro were well tolerated in our protocol (40 and 41). The DrugBank-listed small molecule NSC-76043 (46) was obtained in 84% yield using a simple, commercially available alkene in combination with formate salt. Disubstituted styrenes were also successfully hydrocarboxylated (44, 45, and 47-49) without compromising the yields. The GPR40 agonist, an antidiabetic agent 49, was also prepared using our reaction conditions in 80% yield. Activated alkene substrates bearing sensitive benzothiophene (50) and other reactive functional groups such as ketone (51) were well tolerated under the reaction conditions. The complex substrate containing multiple functional groups such as amide and ester afforded the hydrocarboxylated product 52 in good yield. Although this protocol shows broad functional group tolerance, substrates containing NO2, CO2H, NH2, alkyl halides, and aldehydes were not compatible under the standard conditions, likely because of competing radical or redox processes.

Synthetic Application

Aliphatic carboxylic acids serve as versatile functional handles. Demonstrating the feasibility of this method for large-scale synthesis is crucial for attracting the attention of both academic and industrial communities. We carried out decagram-scale syntheses of four representative molecules (15, 17, 18, and 49) to highlight the scalability of the protocol (Figure a). The scaled-up anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylated products were obtained in yields comparable to those from small-scale reactions, underscoring the practicality of the method for larger-scale production. Notably, retention of the internal double bond in product 15 demonstrates the stability of this functionality under the reaction conditions. Isotope Labeling Utilizing C-13-Labeled Formate

Isotope labeling is at the center of biological evaluation of drugs, agrochemicals, and nuclear imaging. This technique facilitates a comprehensive assessment of both risks and benefits before large-scale synthesis and utilization of these materials. In this context, the use of isotope-labeled CO2 •– generation technology from cheap, solid, and easily available isotopic source sodium formate could provide a versatile technique for the synthesis of important isotope-labeled carboxylated molecules. Although isotope-labeled CO2 •– holds significant promise, its utilization for the carboxylation reaction has only recently been achieved. ,,, We aimed to apply our methodology to the synthesis of 13C-labeled carboxylic acids from both activated and unactivated olefins, utilizing 13C-formate as the carbon source for the first time. To our delight, under a modified reaction condition, employing 1 equiv NaH CO2, we obtained the carboxylated products [ 13 C]­14, [ 13 C]­15, [ 13 C]­19 from the corresponding unactivated alkenes and [ 13 C]­45 from the activated alkene with good radiochemical yields (Figure ), avoiding excessive use of radio-labeled reaction components. The synthesis of carboxylic acid [ 13 C]­15, a fragrance ingredient, highlights the strength of our protocol for the radio-labeling studies of the bioactive molecules.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have developed a photocatalytic synergistic hydrogen atom abstraction and hydrogen atom transfer strategy for the selective anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylation of both unactivated and activated alkenes using operationally simple, abundant, and bench-stable formate salts. This method generates transient CO2 •– from formate at room temperature and maintains a steady-state concentration that enables selective hydrocarboxylation while preventing formation of unwanted byproducts. The high selectivity toward anti-Markovnikov hydrocarboxylation was evident in substrates bearing accessible 1,n-HAT sites, which typically favor dicarboxylation. Furthermore, this strategy exhibits excellent scalability, as demonstrated through syntheses performed on the decagram scale. This technique is also applicable to the synthesis of isotopically labeled aliphatic carboxylic acids from unactivated alkenes utilizing 13 C-labeled formate for the first time. Overall, this visible-light-driven methodology enables formate-mediated generation of CO2 •– and its controlled utilization in combination with silanes, providing a general platform for selective hydrocarboxylation.

Experimental Section

General Procedure for the Anti-Markovnikov Hydrocarboxylation Reaction Utilizing Formate

To an oven-dried Schlenk tube (25 mL) equipped with a magnetic stir bar was added the [Ir-2] (1 mol %), the tube was moved into the glovebox where the alkene (0.2 mmol, 1 equiv), KHCO2 (2 equiv), Cs2CO3 (3 equiv), and DABCO (HAA) reagent (0.5 equiv) were added. Tris­(trimethylsilyl)­silane (TTMSS) (2 equiv) (if liquid) was added under the nitrogen atmosphere followed by anhydrous DMSO added under the nitrogen atmosphere, and the tube was sealed using the Teflon screw-cap. The reaction was stirred and irradiated with a 440–456 nm Kessil PR-160L Blue LED lamp (3 cm away, with a cooling fan to keep the reaction temperature at 35–40 °C and keeping the reaction region located in the center of LEDs lamp) for 24 h. The reaction mixture was diluted with 8 mL of EA and quenched by 2 mL of HCl (2 N, dil.). After adding 10 mL of H2O, the mixture was extracted by EA six times and the combined organic phases were concentrated with the aid of a rotary evaporator. The resulting crude material was then purified by flash column chromatography. Yields of the products are reported as the isolated yields.
