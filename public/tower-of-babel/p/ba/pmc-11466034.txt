Comparison of productive performance of Marandi, White Leghorn, and Marandi-White Leghorn crossbred chickens
Authors: Somayeh Pourhamidi, Ali Esmailizadeh, Mohamad Salarmoini, Masood Asadi Fozi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecently, efforts like crossbreeding, strain crossing, and inbred hybridization have been performed to improve productive performance in native chickens. This experiment was carried out to evaluate the effects of crossbreeding on the improvement of the productivity of a native chicken. The White Leghorn exotic chicken (WL) as dam line and the Marandi indigenous chicken (MA) as sire line were used to generate the crossbreds (MA × WL). Body weight and egg production traits were measured in the three groups.ResultsThere are significant performance differences between WL and MA, with WL exhibiting superior body weight and egg production compared to MA (P < 0.05). Crossbreeding between WL and MA significantly improved most traits, with MA × WL outperforming MA. Negative heterosis was observed for body weight showing similarities between MA and MA × WL. Notably, MA × WL showed a closer resemblance to WL than MA in hen-day egg production at 40 and 44 weeks (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe results derived from this study show that the economic performance of native chickens can be enhanced through crossbreeding with exotic chickens. Using the crossbred chickens in rural areas can be more efficient than the native chickens. In addition, the resilience of the native chickens to the local environment can be transmitted genetically to the crossbred which should be investigated in further studies.

Background

Commercial chicken production dominates globally, with Iran reflecting this trend. Native breeds contribute less than 15% economically, emphasizing the prevalence of pure and commercial breeds. Indigenous chickens, raised in rural areas worldwide, fulfill protein needs and support household income. Different native breeds serve various purposes, such as egg or meat production. For example, Egyptian Fayoumi and Desi chickens are solely for egg production, while Asil chickens are bred for meat. These breeds offer organic eggs, flavourful meat, disease resistance, and adaptability to harsh conditions [1–3]. Nowadays, there is more demand for the products.

Previous research has shown that indigenous fowl survived better than exotic chickens under village production conditions [4, 5]. The low performance of native chicken egg production is due to various factors such as low genetic potential, seasonal effects, low nutrition quality, and broodiness. Modified exotic fowls, on the other hand, produce more eggs and meat than local hens, but they have less resistance than native chickens against hard and unfavourable environmental conditions [6].

Local fowls as national genetic resources can play an important role in traditional poultry breeding programs. Nonetheless, the productivity performance of native birds compared to exotic chickens is low. Improvement of village poultry production could increase the nutritional status and income of villagers. Crossbreeding local chickens with elite exotic strains can elevate their genetic potential to be resistant to harsh environmental conditions, and produce a middle level of egg and meat production [7, 8]. Iranian native chickens exhibit lower growth rates and egg production compared to modified exotic breeds, but they possess superior attributes when compared to unimproved domestic chickens in other regions [9, 10]. Crossing native fowls with elite exotic breeds, can yield crossbreds with superior growth and egg production traits [4, 11]. Numerous studies, have shown that crossing improved foreign chickens with native breeds results in offspring that outperform pure foreign chickens in terms of body weight, egg production, survivability, hatchability, fertility, and egg quality traits [12–14].

However, the past efforts to boost the native chickens egg production through crossbreeding have not fully satisfied the villagers. The present study utilized Marandi (MA) indigenous cocks and White Leghorn (WL) exotic hens to produce crossbreds. MA is a native Iranian breed from East Azerbaijan province. It is renowned for its laying capabilities and distinct appearance [10]. The WL is globally well-known as the premier egg-laying breed. WL has been recognized for its exceptional egg production, efficient feed conversion ratio, large egg size, high reproductive capacity, and early sexual maturity [15].

This experiment was carried out to evaluate the effects of crossbreeding between MA and WL on the production and reproduction traits of Marandi-White Leghorn crossbreds. The findings from this research can provide valuable insights for enhancing native poultry breeding programs.

Methods

This study was carried out in the poultry station of the Department of Animal Science, Shahid Bahonar University of Kerman. This site is located in Kerman which is in the Southeast of Iran with a dry and warm climate, maximum temperature of 41.5 °C, minimum temperature of -15.1 °C, annual rainfall of 148 mm, and humidity lower than 25% during summer. To ensure a contamination-free environment, thorough disinfection was carried out on the cages, equipment, and surrounding areas where two populations of White Leghorn (WL) and Marandi (MA) chickens at different ages were kept. 24 WL females and 4 MA males were randomly selected from the two populations at 24 weeks of age and used for the crossbreeding to produce MA × WL offspring. The parents were divided into four groups, each consisting of one MA rooster and six WL hens. At the same time, 24 MA females and 4 MA males and 24 WL females and 4 WL males at 24 weeks of age were randomly selected from the two populations of MA and WL. Similarly, each cage consisted of one male and six females. These birds were housed in standardized cages with dimensions of (100 × 120 × 150 cm), furnished with wood shavings, nipple drinkers, and feeders. Feed and water were provided ad libitum to maintain optimal conditions for the birds. Since the nutritional requirements of the native birds were unknown, the parent birds were fed based on the nutritional guidelines for WL hens. They were provided with a laying ration containing specific percentages of essential nutrients. In addition, temperature, humidity and light were those recommended for WL at different ages [16].

One month after mating, fertile eggs of WL, MA and MA × WL were collected from the parents’ nests for 7 days and were kept in a storage room with temperature of 10 to 15 °C and relative humidity of 70 to 80% until hatching, and then placed in the incubator machine. The number of eggs that were collected for incubation in a single hatch was 220, 175, and 132 for WL, MA × WL, and MA, respectively. Female chicks after hatching (WL = 86, MA × WL = 65, and MA = 50) were vaccinated for Newcastle disease, Infectious Bursal Disease (IBD), and Marek’s disease and then transferred to the breeding pen. In addition, the vaccination against Newcastle disease was repeated at 7 days, 21 days, and 8 weeks of age and thereafter every 3 months. They were raised up to the age of 17 weeks in the rearing houses. From each group, 44 chicks were randomly selected and divided into 22 experimental units, including 2 laying hens each, to be determined as replicates. The birds moved into laying pens designed for two chickens with dimensions of (40 × 35 × 35.5 cm). Figure 1 shows pictures of the three genetic groups.

Studied traits

Body weight

The body weight (BW) of MA, WL, and MA × WL chickens were measured at 18, 34, and 52 weeks of age using a digital scale.

Egg production

The egg production traits including hen-day egg production (HDEP), egg weight (EW), and egg mass (EM) were measured at different ages. The HDEP was calculated according to the following formula and recorded for the three groups of MA, WL, and MA × WL chickens at 32, 36, 40, 44, 48, and 52 weeks of age.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{HDEP}=\frac{\text { Total of eggs produced on a particular period }}{\text { Total of alive chickens on a particular period }} \times 100$$\end{document}

EW and EM were measured at 40 and 52 weeks of age using a digital scale with an accuracy of 0.01 gr. EM was measured using the following formula:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{EM}(\mathrm{gr} / \text { hen day })=\operatorname{HDEP}(\%) \times \mathrm{EW}(\mathrm{gr})$$\end{document}

Data analysis

The data were analysed using the following statistical model:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{y}_{\mathrm{ij}}=\mu+\mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{i}}+\mathrm{e}_{\mathrm{ij}}$$\end{document}

where yij is the value of each observation, µ is the mean, ai is the ith breed, and eij is the error. The means were compared using the Tukey test. R (version 3.0.1) was used for the analysis. The heterosis for the studied traits was calculated using the following equation:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\operatorname{Heterosis}(\%)=\left[\frac{\mathrm{F} 1-\left(\frac{\mathrm{P} 1+\mathrm{P} 2}{2}\right)}{\frac{\mathrm{P} 1+\mathrm{P} 2}{2}}\right]$$\end{document}

where F1 is the average performance of crosses, P1 and P2 are an average performance of parents [17].

Results

Body weights (BW) of MA, WL, and MA × WL chickens at 18, 34, and 52 weeks of age were (1060 gr, 1334 gr, and 1026 gr), (1472 gr, 1519 gr and, 1461 gr), and (1557 gr, 1696 gr and, 1577 gr), respectively (Table 2). The highest BW was observed in WL at 52 weeks of age (1696 gr), followed by MA × WL (1577 gr) and MA (1557 gr). Heterosis for BW at 18, 34, and 52 weeks of age was − 14.28%, -2.31%, and − 3.04%, respectively.

Discussion

The present study shows that the BW in WL was significantly higher than in MA and MA ×\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\text{W}$$\end{document}L at 18, and 52 weeks of age with no significant differences between MA and MA ×\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\text{W}$$\end{document}L. WL is bigger than MA genetically that is due to their breed differences and the crossbreds are more similar to MA than WL. No significant difference was observed between the three groups at 34 weeks of age. Laying was started at different age of the three genetic groups. WL started laying earlier than the other two groups. In addition, MA began laying later than MA ×\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\text{W}$$\end{document}L. Then the level of egg production was not the same in the three investigated groups. This can cause changes in the body weight trends of WL, MA, and MA ×\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\text{W}$$\end{document}L at 34 weeks of age compared to the other ages.

In our study, the body weight (BW) of WL aligned with previous research [18–20]. Meanwhile, MA weighed 1060 gr, 1472 gr, and 1557 gr at 18, 34, and 52 weeks of age. Previous studies, reported average BW of 1332.14 gr, 692.05 gr, 1284.57 gr, and 840.32 gr for the native chickens of Azerbaijan, Yazd, Isfahan, and Fars at 12 weeks of age, respectively. The results indicate lower BW in MA compared to the other Iranian breeds [21–23].

Negative heterosis was obtained when BW of MA and WL (as parents) and the crossbreds were compared. In the past studies, the heterosis of BW in chickens ranged from − 22 to 30.07% that is depending on the genetics of the parents mated. Williams et al., [24] reported negative heterosis (-22% to -4%) for BW at six weeks of age and at the start of laying in the hybrids of high and low live weights of White Plymouth Rock lines. Sutherland et al., [25] found out negative heterosis for growth rate in the first and second generations of Red Junglefowl and White Plymouth Rock crossbred. Mai et al., [26] reported negative heterosis for BW at two to eight weeks of age in reciprocal crosses of Cornish (meat-type chicken) and Rhode Island White (egg-type chicken). In contrast, Okada [27] recorded a significant increase in BW of crossbreds of White Plymouth Rock and native Japanese compared to their pure parents. Positive heterosis (21.6%) was obtained for BW at 10 weeks of age using crossbreeding of native Nigerian chickens and commercial broilers [28]. Saleh et al., [29] also reported a range of 0.4–7.7% for the heterosis of BW in local chickens and modern hen crossbreds. Safalaoh [30] showed that the crossbreds of the Desi native hen and the Australorp cock had a higher BW than the native Desi hen. Lalev et al., [31] reported positive heterosis (3.76–22.33%) for BW in the offspring of White Plymouth Rock chicken lines. A range of -14.11–30.07% was reported by Tyasi et al., [32] for the heterosis of BW in a diallel cross of three native hen genotypes at different ages, in South Africa.

Egg production is considered the most important trait in laying hens, which is affected by several factors such as genotype, production system, and age at sexual maturity [33, 34]. Heterosis effects on egg production traits are influenced by epistasis and dominance, as noted by Fairfull et al. [17]. Crossbreds of MA × WL showed statistically significant superiority in egg production traits (HDEP, EW and EM) compared to the local MA (P < 0.05).

In this study, the HDEP at the different ages of WL was in the range of 50.35–88.14% reported by other authors [20, 35–37]. WL exhibited the highest HDEP of 87.36–94.71% in the laying period, (32 to 52 weeks of age) followed by MA × WL (76.39–78.96%) and MA (30.27–58.03%) (P < 0.05). Leghorn is widely used as a good layer in many countries around the world. Therefore, WL is genetically superior in egg production compared to MA. In the present study, the WL started laying earlier than MA. MA × WL outperformed MA in egg production, with HDEP closer to WL, indicating positive heterosis, aligning with findings by Khawaja et al., [38] on Fayoumi-Rhode Island Red hybrids and previous studies on improved egg production through crossbreeding [39, 40]. The results show a range of (8.62–22.24%) for heterosis of the HDEP at 32 to 52 weeks of age, contrasting with negative heterosis of the HDEP in native breed-commercial chicken hybrids [41–43].

The effect of crossbreeding on the EM was investigated in the current study. The highest and lowest EM were found in WL and MA, respectively. Low EM in MA chickens may be attributed to delayed laying onset that caused a reduction in egg production. The EM in the crossbreds was closer to WL than to MA. The results indicate that the EM has been improved in the crossbreds where positive heterosis of 18.07–8.51% for EM aligns with previous studies [44, 45], contrasting with negative heterosis observed in local-commercial strain crossbreeding reported by Soliman et al., [43].

The average egg weight of WL, MA, and MA × WL was measured at 40 and 52 weeks of age (Table 3). The heaviest eggs were observed in WL chickens, followed by MA × WL and MA. The lower egg weight of MA correlates with their lower body weight as compared to WL. Significant positive genetic correlation between body weight and egg weight of birds reported by [46–48]. The weight of egg was increased with the chicken’s age in the three groups. Previous studies likewise reported that showed increased egg weights with increased age of hens [49, 50].

The results derived from the crossbreeding in this study showed positive heterosis for the egg weight at the studied ages demonstrating the superiority of MA × WL over MA. The results of this study showed that the EW of MA can be improved using crossbreeding with WL, while the EW in MA is higher than other Iranian native chickens [22, 23, 51]. Adamu et al., [52] reported heavier eggs in Rhode Island Red × White Leghorn crossbreds at 52 weeks versus the pure breeds. Khawaja et al., [38], Hristakieva et al., [53] and Soliman et al., [43] also showed heavier egg weight in crosses of local and commercial strains compared to local chickens. In contrast, Ghanem et al., [54] and Abou El-Ghar et al., [55] reported negative heterosis.

Conclusion

Crossbreeding between native MA fowl with WL exotic chickens was associated with a positive heterotic effect on hen-day egg production, egg mass, and egg weight. The study suggests that utilizing crossbred native chickens which can be resilient with higher genetic potential, can enhance the rural economy by producing organic, flavourful meat and eggs. However, further research should be performed to explore fertility, hatchability, age of sexual maturity, mortality, egg quality, carcass traits, disease resistance, and other productivity traits of the crossbred chicken under farm conditions compared to White leghorn and local chicken.
