Exercise induced irregular wide QRS tachycardia with right bundle branch block morphology and extreme QRS axis deviation
Authors: Eliyahu Kalfon, Tsafrir Or, Bernard Belhassen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 29-year-old active woman was referred for evaluation following the development of an irregular wide complex tachycardia with right bundle branch morphology and extreme QRS axis during exercise stress testing. Two main alternative diagnoses are discussed: a) atrial fibrillation with either aberrancy or pre-excitation; b) left ventricular tachycardia originating from the Purkinje system including left posterior fascicle versus posteromedial papillary muscle.

Case presentation

A 29-year-old healthy and active single woman who regularly runs recreationally 5-10 km presented for an exercise stress test in preparation for marathon distance endurance training. She has no history of palpitations nor exhibited syncope in the past and has no family history of cardiac disease or sudden death. Her baseline electrocardiogram (ECG) showed normal sinus rhythm (66 bpm) with normal PR interval (0.16sec), incomplete right bundle branch block (RBBB) and normal QTc interval (Fig. 1). After 6 minutes of a Balke 4 protocol stress test, the patient developed multiple bouts of hemodynamically stable irregular wide complex tachycardia (Fig. 2). The tachycardia resolved within 3 minutes following cessation of exercise. No obvious structural heart disease was found by transthoracic echocardiogram and magnetic resonance imaging of the heart. An electrophysiologic study failed to produce any arrhythmia during atrial or ventricular pacing protocols before and after infusion of isoproterenol (1mcg/min). There was no evidence of an accessory pathway. She underwent 2 additional exercise stress tests 2 days apart within 6 days of the initial stress test with no induction of any significant arrhythmia.

Discussion

The patient developed multiple bouts of hemodynamically stable irregular wide complex tachycardia of RBBB morphology with QRS duration varying between 120 and 160 ms, extreme QRS axis (−150°) and rates varying between 180 and 300 bpm. A fundamental question to attempt to answer is whether this particular arrhythmia originates above or within the ventricles. Which supraventricular tachycardias may elicit such a ventricular response? Atrial fibrillation with variable atrioventricular nodal conduction and rate related RBBB is a possibility. Prolonged endurance training is a known trigger of atrial fibrillation during intense exercise in athletes such as this patient. Although P waves are not clearly seen during the tachycardia, upright P waves at a rate of 150 bpm are seen in leads II, III, AVF during breaks in the tachycardia with normal PR intervals and followed by narrow QRS complexes prior to the wide complex tachycardia resumption, ruling out atrial fibrillation. In addition, although the right bundle branch is known to be more susceptible to aberrant conduction, the pattern of RBBB during tachycardia in our patient with rsR’ in V1 and V6 R/S ratio <1 does not support a supraventricular rhythm with aberrant conduction [1]. Another possibility includes atrial fibrillation with competitive antegrade conduction down an accessory pathway causing pre-excitation associated with rapid and irregular ventricular rates. The absence of pre-excitation in the baseline ECG does not completely rule out such a possibility. However, as noted before, the apparently sinus upright P waves at a rate of 150 bpm are incompatible with a diagnosis of atrial fibrillation. Thus, we are left with the only possible diagnosis of ventricular tachycardia (VT).

Polymorphic VT is characterized by beat-to-beat chaotic changes in QRS morphology, axis and rate driven by rapidly altering reentrant circuits. Pleomorphic VT presents as more than one distinct but otherwise stable alternating QRS morphologies driven either by alternating but defined reentrant circuits, or a single circuit with more than one exit site. We believe that in our patient the beat-to-beat changes in QRS morphology appear to depend more on rate variations rather than varying ventricular activating sequences as the overall RBBB pattern and extreme axis are preserved throughout the VT episodes. Therefore, we suggest that the VT in our patient fulfills the criteria for monomorphic VT.

Although monomorphic and not polymorphic VT was induced during exercise, the possibility of an exceptional diagnosis of catecholaminergic VT linked to RYR2 or CASQ2 gene mutations, should not be excluded [2]. Genetic testing should be considered and was recommended to the patient.

VT originating in the Purkinje network such a left posterior fascicular (LPF) or Belhassen-type VT typically produces a regular monomorphic RBBB (rsR’) pattern very similar in morphology to the one presented here. However, almost all cases exhibit a regular rate [3]. In addition, when the tachycardia is irregular, this is frequently due to cycle length alternans, especially after administration of intravenous verapamil, which was not the case in our patient [3].

Left papillary muscle (particularly posteromedial) VT can also present as an RBBB pattern similar to the one observed in our patient. The QRS is typically broader than LPF-VT (>140 ms). Doppalapudi and coworkers were the first to report this distinct clinical syndrome [4]. In all cases, VT was not inducible with programmed ventricular or atrial stimulation; however, VT or premature ventricular complexes were induced in all patients with intravenous epinephrine or isoproterenol. This observation, combined with the fact that sustained VT could not be terminated by overdrive pacing and with the absence of criteria for transient entrainment, suggests a nonreentrant mechanism for this arrhythmia: either abnormal automaticity or triggered activity.

While we have no doubt about the site of origin of VT in our patient, we cannot further precisely determine its mechanism. The only important point we can make is that the VT was induced only once on exercise out of the 3 times the stress test was performed within a one-week period. The patient was discharged on a trial of flecainide 50mg twice daily pending additional follow up and monitoring. She stopped the medication shortly thereafter due to feeling of shortness of breath. She otherwise feels well and has since returned to running 5-8 km without symptoms, having been duly advised of the possible risks for a recurrent tachyarrhythmia associated with endurance or high intensity training.

Resuming unrestricted exercise in this case is in line with the recommendations outlined by Hsu and coworkers who reviewed management options in athletes with monomorphic ventricular arrhythmias [5]. In the absence of any structural or electrical heart disease or high-risk ECG features (such as high premature ventricular contraction (PVC) burden, no greater than PVC couplets on maximal exercise testing) then competitive sport participation can be reinitiated.

Editorial commentary (Evan Saenger, Brian Olshansky)

Our compliments to the authors on their succinct overview of a complicated ECG and case study of a young patient with exercise-induced, self-limited, wide-complex tachycardia. The authors were prudent to raise the possibility of pre-excited atrial fibrillation given the age of the patient and the irregular, wide-complex nature of the rhythm.

However, this is quickly ruled out upon close inspection when we find a tachycardia with an extreme axis, capture beats, AV dissociation and precordial (albeit atypical) right bundle branch block pattern. These findings bolster the agreed diagnosis of ventricular tachycardia. We argue that the tachycardia shown on the ECG recording is irregular and polymorphic rather than monomorphic with subtle variations in QRS duration and morphology.

Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) becomes the leading consideration; this is supported by the patient's structurally normal heart and lack of inducibility, findings classic for this diagnosis. The subtle variation in QRS morphology during ventricular tachycardia, as well as the irregularity of the rhythm, can be explained by triggered activity from delayed afterdepolarizations, with propagation of the VT originating at slightly different velocities and locations within the left ventricle.

The most common form of CPVT involves an autosomal dominant missense mutation of the ryanodine receptor (CPVT1). A recessive form mapped to 1p13-21, a missense mutation of CASQ2, is cause for CPVT2. This form has been noted in individuals with a Bedouin background. We wonder if that could be the case here.

Further clarification of the EP study findings could prove insight regarding an accessory pathway, for example, that the authors mention as possible. It is noteworthy that dosing of isoproterenol during the EP study was low.

Discussion of flecainide as the choice to treat this patient instead of nadolol (or another beta-blocker) would be worthwhile. It will be elucidating to know this patient's genetic testing to support her diagnosis, and we are curious how management may change should she result positive for a mutation implicated in CPVT. We are not as sanguine as the authors are about clearing the patient to run a marathon with the information thus presented.

The lack of reproducibility of the tachycardia based on three treadmill tests does not necessarily reassure us that the tachycardia induced on one is benign or unlikely to occur during competitive or extreme athletics. Alternatively, we wonder about the value of, or need for, treadmill testing for otherwise healthy, asymptomatic athletes about to embark upon preparation for a marathon run.
