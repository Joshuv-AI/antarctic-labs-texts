Loss of daily phase relations identified by multiomic analyses in acute pancreatitis
Authors: Heather Waddell, Xiaozhong Zheng, Lucile Neyton, Tyler J Stevenson, Natalia Bochkina, Damian J Mole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute pancreatitis (AP) is sudden inflammation of the pancreas which can cause organ failure and death, and with no targeted therapies in the clinic, there is an imperative to identify new potential avenues for therapeutics discovery. This exploratory study investigates the potential role of circadian rhythms in AP pathogenesis. We integrated and analysed multiomics data, including transcriptomics, metabolomics, and clinical laboratory measurements, synthesised from peripheral whole blood samples obtained from AP patients. Our exploratory findings identified a potential association between circadian rhythm dysregulation and disease severity. Specifically, we observed that the rhythmic expression of circadian genes across a 24-h period, such as the circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (CLOCK) and the brain and muscle ARNT-Like 1 (BMAL1) gene, and a specific subset of metabolites may be altered in AP. These exploratory results provide a foundation for future investigations into the potential therapeutic implications of targeting circadian rhythms in AP. Additional studies are required to validate these findings and to elucidate the specific mechanisms involved.Keywords: Acute pancreatitis, Circadian rhythms, Harmonic regression, Inflammatory markers, Metabolomics, Multi-omics, Time series

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is acute inflammation of the pancreas [1] that affects 56 per 100,000 individuals per year, and is one of the most common causes for hospital admission with gastrointestinal pain [2]. Approximately 50% of AP episodes are attributable to gallstones, followed by alcohol misuse (25%), with the remainder caused by other factors [2,3] including trauma, iatrogenic pancreatic instrumentation, prescription medications, viral infections and, in rare cases, scorpion venom [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Four fifths of AP episodes are categorised as mild, characterised by a self-limiting disease course, tissue injury confined to the pancreas gland, and no systemic or significant local pancreatic complications [8,9]. However, in some individuals, AP can be severe, which can give rise to extra pancreatic organ failure, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) [10,11] and significantly increased risk of mortality, between 30 and 50% [9,12,13].

AP onset begins with localised pancreatic inflammation and injury which in turn activates an inflammatory cascade of mediators and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) (Fig. 1) [6,9,10,14]. Given the diverse aetiology and individualised response to AP onset [15], exploring the role of the immune system and its connection to the circadian rhythms (CRs) through the circadian clock genes is a rational step towards understanding, and thus potentially mitigating the high mortality rates observed in severe AP [16], and may discover opportunities for new targeted therapies [6,12]. Our aim in this study was to investigate the reciprocal relationship between AP and CRs. Specifically, we aimed to explore the potential impact of AP on CRs reflected in peripheral whole blood multiomics and clinical laboratory data, and conversely, to examine how the CR phase might influence AP outcomes.

The CRs are defined as internal self-sustained oscillators which perpetuate 24-hr changes in behaviour and physiology in all organisms [17]. These observed changes are in response to variation in environmental conditions, for example temperature and light availability induced by the earth's rotation [18]. As part of the evolutionary process all organisms developed the ability to entrain with the external environment through the CRs in order to maximise the likelihood of survival [19]. The CRs are entrained via external cues, known as zeitgebers, which include light, feeding habits, temperature, and social interaction [[19], [20], [21]]. In addition to this, the CRs are governed by the central circadian pacemaker located in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) which is critical in i) integrating zeitgeber signals ii) disseminating temporal information to other regions of the brain and, iii) regulating peripheral circadian clocks which are present within organs and tissues through humoral signals and autonomic nervous system feedback [[22], [23], [24]].

Over the past decade dysregulation of CRs have been implicated in the development and progression of many diseases including certain types of cancer [25,26], obesity [27,28], diabetes [29,30], and mental health disorders [31,32]. In recent years, focus has shifted to the impact of CR on inflammation and inflammatory mediators, such as cytokines and danger signals released as part of the inflammatory response [33,34]. Previous studies have used lipopolysaccharide endotoxin (LPS) to model critical illness and investigate the impact of peripheral inflammation on the master clock in rats [[35], [36], [37], [38]]. Direct administration to the SCN and peripheral injection both alter expression of key circadian genes located within the SCN and peripheral tissues [35,37]. From this, it can be inferred that diseases that cause peripheral inflammation, for example sepsis and AP-MODS, have the potential to impact on healthy CRs. To date, it has not been established whether AP, which elicits a strong inflammatory response, will result in the same disruption to the CRs. Furthermore, it is also not known what the effect of the phase of the CR at the time of onset of disease has on AP outcomes [39].

It is plausible to propose that AP impacts on CRs via effector immune cell populations [16,40,41]. In both human and murine studies, numbers of immune cells like neutrophils, monocytes, and leukocytes have been reported to rhythmically vary under the control of CRs, and in a diurnal pattern [16,40,[42], [43], [44]]. This was substantiated experimentally in mice by Halberg et al., who reported that mice exposed to LPS in the active vs rest phase had a statistically significant difference in observed mortality [45,46]. This finding was replicated by Keller et al., who showed a large difference in circulating macrophage numbers depending on the time of day when mice were injected with LPS [47].

In this paper we used previously processed data from the Inflammation, Metabolism and Organ Failure in AP (IMOFAP) study [48] to assess the potential impact on overall gene expression and on core circadian clock gene expression in peripheral whole blood samples, in the context of mild, moderate, and severe AP, classified according to the revised Atlanta criteria [8,9,33,49,50], and partially compared that in single timepoint metabolomics, clinical laboratory and outcome data from an independent cohort of AP from the Kynurenine Pathway in AP Validation (KAPVAL) study. However, it should be strongly emphasised that this was a partial comparison to identify key biological themes between the two AP cohorts given that KAPVAL contained single time points per patient and not repeated measures. Our goal was to discover novel insight into AP and its potential impact on time-of-day dependent disease features, using integrated transcriptomic, clinical and metabolomics data. We specifically asked: i) are CRs detectable in multiomics analysis of peripheral blood samples; ii) does AP severity potentially influence the rhythmicity of the circadian clock genes of interest and rhythmic metabolites; and iii) does the phase of the CR at time of onset of AP potentially impact on inflammatory markers involved in AP pathophysiology.

Results

Overall rhythmicity of transcriptomics dataset

First, we identified which genes were rhythmically expressed in keeping with a CR pattern. Transcriptomics data was filtered (n = 19,649 genes, N = 49 AP patients) prior to harmonic regression. Genes were retained if they contained greater than three different gene values across zeitgeber time (ZT) (N = 19,206, N = 49 patients with multiple samples). All genes that did not meet this assumption were filtered out they would produce arrhythmic gene expression. Genes were determined to be rhythmically expressed if they were below the significance threshold, p-value <0.01. This threshold was set to minimise the likelihood of false positives within the data and given the datasets size. All transcriptomics data are presented as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM).

After fitting a harmonic regression loop (using the package ‘HarmonicRegression’) 315 genes were determined to be rhythmically cycling, under the appropriate p-value threshold (p < 0.01). The top ten genes, that exhibit a statistically significant circadian pattern across time, are plotted below, see Fig. 2. These genes included: Abhydrolase Domain-Containing Protein 3 (ABHD3), ADP Ribosylation Factor Like GTPase 17B (ARL17B), ASXL Transcriptional Regulator 2 (ASXL2), Cortistatin (CORT), Exocyst Complex Component 8 (EXOC8), HECT And RLD Domain Containing E3 Ubiquitin Protein Ligase 4 (HERC4), NLR Family CARD Domain Containing 5 (NLRC5), Protein Prenyltransferase Alpha Subunit Repeat Containing 1 (PTAR1), SET Domain Containing 5 (SETD5), and SMG1 Nonsense Mediated MRNA Decay Associated PI3K Related Kinase (SMG1). All genes, and their HGNC symbols, were identified using the R package biomaRt (version 2.54.0).

Overall rhythmicity of circadian genes of interest in peripheral blood samples

Next, we tested the specific hypothesis that known CR genes were rhythmically expressed in our data. The circadian genes of interest were analysed for rhythmic expression, using CircWave, reported statistically significant gene expression for the circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (CLOCK) gene (F: 5.07, r2 = 0.14, p = 0.0091) computed on the whole AP cohort. All other genes of interest did not report rhythmic expression, with all p values above the significance threshold (p < 0.05) shown in Fig. 3. For the majority of the circadian genes, apart from MAGE family member L2 (MAGEL2), the genes shown a general pattern but not significant enough to show rhythmic expression detectable by CircWave.

The ‘red lines’ in the plots represent the fitted values for each gene. These indicate the overall trends in the data. Gene names are presented as HGNC symbols and are FPKM.

Circadian genes of interest stratified by AP severity

To explore if peripheral blood sample circadian genes of interest were potentially influenced by AP severity, the data were split into groups according to AP severity as defined by the Revised Atlanta Criteria and analysed using CircWave and ‘HarmonicRegression’. Patients with mild AP showed rhythmic expression of two circadian genes CLOCK (F: 4.59, r2 = 0.23 p = 0.0182) and Brain and Muscle ARNT-Like 1(BMAL1) (F: 4.94, r2 = 0.25, p = 0.0140), which is congruent with our hypothesis that this group with lower levels of inflammation will not experience the same level of CR dysregulation as those in the moderate and severe AP cohorts (Table 1 and Fig. 4). All 14 circadian genes of interest were then stratified by AP severity shown in Fig. 5 andFig. 6. Consistent with these findings, the circadian genes CLOCK and BMAL1, which were rhythmically expressed in patients with mild AP (p < 0.05), exhibited a loss of rhythmicity in those with moderate of severe disease (moderate and severe, CLOCK (p-value = 0.1040, p-value = 0.9880) and BMAL1 (p-value = 0.9848, p-value = 0.4623)). This suggests that daily rhythmicity of these specific circadian genes may be disrupted in patients with critical illness. Notably, the other circadian genes examined remained arrhythmic in the moderate and severe AP cohorts.

Overall rhythmicity in white blood cells

Next, we measured whether total white blood cell and differential white blood cell counts exhibited a CR pattern across a 24-h period. Similar to the circadian genes of interest, the data were formatted into ZT and imported into CircWave for exploratory analysis. No significant rhythmic expression was detected for the neutrophils (F: 0.85, r2 = 0.01, p = 0.4820), monocytes (F: 0.82, r2 = 0.01, p = 0.4411), or lymphocytes (F: 2.29, r2 = 0.02, p = 0.1043) shown in Fig. 7 Panel A. To evaluate whether there was any effect attributable to AP severity, the data were split into AP severity groups: mild, moderate, and severe. This aspect of AP severity was explored as a subgroup and is solely exploratory analyses. When imported into CircWave for the respective white blood cell counts, the mild, moderate, and severe AP groups did not show any significant rhythmic expression for neutrophils (F: 0.48, r2 = 0.01, p = 0.6174), monocytes (F: 0.70, r2 = 0.02, p = 0.5016), or lymphocytes (F: 2.10, r2 = 0.05, p = 0.1293) shown in Fig. 7 Panels B, C, and D) and in Table 2.

Time of day and inflammatory markers of acute pancreatitis

Time of day (T0 or also known as recruitment) was explored in relation to four inflammatory markers of AP; these being C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin 6 (IL-6), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α)). These markers are indicative of disease severity and are known to vary across the day due partly to the CRs. Linear mixed effects models were generated for each of the inflammatory markers in relation to time of day and AP severity (N = 57). Time of day was not found to be statistically associated with any of the four inflammatory markers of interest, reflected in the respective p-values (threshold of p > 0.05). Following this, AP severity was then included in the model, the results of these exploratory analyses can be found in Table 3.

Overall rhythmicity of metabolomics datasets

Next, we investigated the circadian rhythmicity of the peripheral blood metabolome in the context of AP. Using harmonic regression applied to both the IMOFAP and KAPVAL metabolomics datasets and setting a false discovery rate of p-value <0.05, 42 out of 683 metabolite species within the IMOFAP metabolomics dataset (N = 38, 248 metabolomes from N = 56 individuals at multiple timepoints) were identified to be rhythmically cycling. In the larger KAPVAL metabolomics dataset (N = 269,390 metabolomes, 1 per individual), 143 out of 869 metabolite species were rhythmically cycling to a statistically significant degree. The top 10 rhythmically expressed metabolites for each dataset are plotted in Fig. 8 (Panel A: IMOFAP, Panel B: KAPVAL). All metabolite names, for both datasets, have been abbreviated due to length constraints. It should also be carefully noted that as our analyses are solely exploratory and use small sample sizes for all omics datasets (IMOFAP and KAPVAL), raw p-values were used in the computations. All p-values are presented in their raw form and therefore, should be interpreted cautiously.

Top ten rhythmic metabolites for the IMOFAP (Panel A) and KAPVAL (Panel B) cohorts. The red lines represent the fitted values, for each metabolite, showing the overall trends in the data. Given that the KAPVAL cohort (Panel B) did not contained repeated measures, each data point indicates a single patient. Metabolite names have been abbreviated, the full metabolite description has been supplied in Supplementary Table 2 (S2).

Metabolomics stratified by AP severity

Both IMOFAP and KAPVAL metabolome datasets were then stratified by severity of AP. For the IMOFAP dataset, the Revised Atlanta Criteria could be used but for the KAPVAL dataset, the surrogate variable intensive care unit (ICU) vs non-ICU had to be used due to missingness of metadata required for the Atlanta classification. In the IMOFAP dataset, patients with mild and moderate AP retained rhythmicity for 22 and 24 metabolite species respectively, whereas in the severe AP subgroup only 1 metabolite exhibited rhythmicity (tyrosine, p-value = 0.0290). In the KAPVAL dataset, those with less severe AP (non-ICU) showed rhythmicity for 41 metabolites, compared to the metabolomes of the ICU subgroup where only 3 metabolites were rhythmic (these are in Supplementary Table 4 (S4)). It is key that these results, the potential association between AP severity and metabolite rhythmic expression, be interpreted as exploratory and do not imply causality.

Super and sub metabolomic pathways

Metabolites within the metabolome are not standalone molecular species and by definition belong to metabolic pathways that are standardly grouped as super- and sub-metabolomic pathways. An example of a super-metabolomic pathways is aromatic amino acid metabolism, with the sub-pathway being tryptophan metabolism. Within the IMOFAP data, the most prevalent super-pathway group identified in the rhythmic group of metabolites (N = 42) was lipid metabolism (N = 21), comprising approximately 50% of the metabolite super-pathway groups (see Fig. 9, Panel A). Following stratification into sub-pathways, the most prevalent type of metabolite reported was monoacylglycerol (N = 14), shown in Fig. 9 (Panel B). The KAPVAL metabolomics data presented a similar trend with lipids (N = 67) comprising the majority (46.9%) of super-pathways, shown in Fig. 9 (Panel C). The majority of the rhythmic KAPVAL metabolites sub-pathways were the phosphatidylcholine (N = 9) and sphingolipid metabolism (N = 9) pathways, as shown in Fig. 9 (Panel D). These results should be interpreted cautiously given that the IMOFAP and KAPVAL datasets cannot be directly compared, only partially, given the difference in study design of repeated measures and single time point, respectively.

Discussion

The role of systemic inflammation in CR disruption has emerged as a potential focal point for circadian based research [31,33,34,51,52], building on the long-standing relationship between CRs and the pathogenesis of disease [25,26,32,53]. In this paper we used harmonic regression to explore the potential rhythmicity of a whole peripheral blood transcriptomics, metabolomics, and clinical laboratory data, in the context of AP, and discovered that i) the rhythmic expression of circadian genes present in mild patients becomes diminished in severe AP, ii) these observed changes in severe AP were not attributable to circadian changes in total but may be the result of peripheral circadian disruption potentially impacting on gene expression and iii) metabolite species, specifically lipids, exhibit clear rhythmic expression but this is dampened in severe AP. Other experimental models of systemic inflammation, mostly using LPS administration, have demonstrated this previously via temporary suppression of core circadian [35,37]. We hypothesised that a similar phenomenon may occur in AP. In accordance with this, we observed diminished circadian gene expression in previously rhythmically expressed genes. This was possibly due to AP severity observed in peripheral whole blood transcriptomics for the IMOFAP cohort, specifically in the gene CLOCK. Furthermore, we failed to observe clear evidence of the typical antiphase between the positive and negative components of the feedback loop that we would expect to view. Therefore, this might suggest that AP could be impacting on the timed phasing of circadian gene expression [54].

Clinical AP covers a spectrum of severity of inflammation that is measurable using canonical inflammatory markers, for example cytokines and CRP, or by clinical features, for example extrapancreatic organ dysfunction or requirement for critical care admission. This property of AP allowed us to explore the effect of varying degrees of systemic inflammation on circadian gene and metabolome disruption. To consider AP severity as a potential factor which might influence overall rhythmicity within our circadian genes of interest, we therefore tested the AP datasets grouped into mild, moderate, and severe AP. Interestingly, both CLOCK and BMAL1 exhibited rhythmic gene expression within the mild AP group, but this rhythmic expression was lost in the moderate and severe AP groups. While this was congruent with our hypotheses that more severe AP results in more severe disruption of the CRs, we are mindful that the lack rhythmic gene expression within these two more severe AP groups could be the result of having a relatively small sample size, which would limit the statistical power and generalisability of results. Furthermore, the exploratory analyses of severity subgroups, specifically the severe AP group, may have incurred limitations and influenced the findings. These limitations must be considered when interpreting the results. However, these exploratory results do preliminarily indicate that AP severity potentially influences circadian gene and metabolite expression [[55], [56], [57]].

Previous studies have reported that white blood cells, specifically neutrophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes, are known to oscillate according to the CRs, over the course of the day [33,43,44,58]. These cell populations were explored to identify if they were rhythmically expressed, as previously reported by others [21,59,60]. In contrast to previous findings, our white blood cell data was not found to follow a circadian pattern in any of the observed cell populations when analysed as an entire cohort. Even when we stratified by AP severity to account for the possibility of a masking effect caused by peripheral inflammation incurred by the onset of AP, we still did not observe rhythmically expressed white blood cell populations of interest. Given that the gene expression in peripheral whole blood (which is almost completely derived from the white cell population) is rhythmic, and the cell counts themselves are not, at least in AP, suggests that gene rhythmicity is potentially regulated at the expression level rather than at the whole cell level. The lack of statistically significant cyclical variation in white cell counts is unexplained, however, especially given reports by others of diurnal rhythms in white blood cell populations [61,62].

Currently, there is a lack of consensus regarding diurnal variation in inflammatory markers, CRP, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α driven by the CRs [63,64]. Previous research has focused on the expression of these inflammatory markers in response to CRs, but to date, no research has investigated time of day and these markers in the context of AP. Time of day was not found to be associated with the inflammatory markers of interest, but this could be due to the broadness of the time categories used which could lack the sensitivity to effectively capture a time-of-day effect. In addition, other models of critical illness (for example sepsis) have shown that increased expression of these markers, such as TNF-α, can hinder the expression of core circadian genes. This relationship is speculated to be bi-directional, and therefore, potentially suggests that these inflammatory markers could be suppressed by AP-induced alterations in circadian gene expression. Further exploratory analyses, including AP severity, reported that clinical severity was associated with CRP, TNF-α, IL-6 but not IL-10. This is in accordance with current literature which endorses CRP, TNF-α, IL-6 as prognostic markers of AP severity [65,66].

The exploration of the AP metabolomics data in IMOFAP and KAPVAL, produced novel exploratory findings. Both datasets identified a range of metabolites that were rhythmically expressed across time (24-h) using harmonic regression (IMOFAP = 42 metabolites and KAPVAL = 143 metabolites). However, when AP severity was factored into the exploratory analyses, the number of rhythmic metabolites was depleted significantly in the mild AP group in the IMOFAP data (N = 22 metabolites) and in the KAPVAL non-ICU group (N = 41 metabolites). This significant reduction in the overall number of rhythmic metabolites in relation to increased clinical severity in both datasets may indicate that severe AP may cause metabolites to become deranged and arrhythmic. Following this, the exploration of the super- and sub-pathways identified that most of the rhythmic metabolites in both datasets were lipids. This is in agreement with the current literature which validates the cyclical nature of lipids which are, in part, mediated by the CRs [[67], [68], [69]]. However, these results should be interpreted with caution given that both the IMOFAP and KAPVAL data were comprised of a substantial proportion of lipids, at 38.4% and 45.6% respectively. Furthermore, these datasets cannot be directly compared, only contrasted for similar trends and therefore should be interpreted as such.

It should be emphasised that the IMOFAP and KAPVAL datasets were not integrated due to differences in study design. IMOFAP included repeated measures per patient, whereas KAPVAL contained only a single timepoint per individual. Analyses were conducted in parallel, and any observed similarities in rhythmic metabolites across the two cohorts are conceptual and exploratory rather than a formal multi-cohort validation. Zeitgeber time transformation was applied to each dataset separately to enable approximate circadian alignment, but repeated-measures effects could not be assessed in KAPVAL. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating patterns, rather than definitive multi-cohort findings.

This study has several strengths. Firstly, the omics data obtained from the IMOFAP cohort provided us with a unique opportunity to extensively investigate a focussed area within the field of critical illness. This is a largely understudied area due to multiple barriers including i) omics associated costs, ii) difficulty in recruiting AP patients due to sudden onset of disease and iii) obtaining multiple samples, per patient, over the course of the disease. Therefore, a second strength of this study is the collection of multiple blood samples per patient for the whole IMOFAP cohort. This allowed us to assess the IMOFAP cohorts overall CR, through select circadian genes, which is novel finding within this critically ill population and has to date not been investigated. Thirdly, this study provides new insights into the potential biological mechanisms which underpin AP and how they may interlink with the CRs. Furthermore, this could potentiate new treatment options which currently rely mostly on a supportive approach to manage symptoms instead of addressing the underlying cause [70,71]. Lastly, research within this area is becoming increasingly vital given the current strain on the health services to manage the rising incidence rates of AP, and therefore, emphasises the importance of this study [71].

This study is not without limitations which must also be considered. The first limitation within this study is the lack of recorded wake-up times for both AP cohorts, which may vary between patients depending on AP severity, treatment approach and wards patients were kept on. Secondly, as all AP patients require hospitalisation, they will be exposed to irregular lighting, altered feeding times, noise levels, experience disturbed sleep, sedative medication and in severe cases, mechanical ventilation. These environmental and clinical factors are known to strongly influence CRs [[72], [73], [74], [75]] and may partially overshadow disease-specific effects. Consequently, some of the observed disruptions in circadian gene expression and metabolite rhythmicity could reflect the hospital environment rather than AP itself, limiting the ability to draw causal inferences. Future studies that monitor or control for these factors will be important to clarify the relative contribution of disease versus environment.

The lack of a healthy control population is also a limitation, as it prevents benchmarking the observed circadian patterns and disruptions against baseline rhythms in healthy individuals. Consequently, findings should be interpreted as exploratory, highlighting potential patterns within the AP population rather than definitive disease-specific alterations. Future studies including healthy control are needed to quantify the magnitude of circadian disruption occurring within the AP population. Age must also be considered as various studies have reported that the CRs become dampened as we get older which may be the case in our IMOFAP cohort which had a mean cohort age of 60.32 years [[76], [77], [78], [79]]. Our study was exploratory and based on the size of the sample; therefore, all omics analyses used raw p-values, as formal correction for multiple testing would have risked being underpowered and potentially obscured hypothesis-generating signals. This should be addressed in future research. Furthermore, future studies would benefit greatly from a larger sample size, with a repeated measures design for all patients, equally balanced sample timepoints and possess an equal number of patients per severity group. Finally, the exploratory findings from this study should be validated within another AP cohort that uses the same data acquisition and sampling methods.

Additionally, the variation in number of observations per patient across sampling timepoints within the transcriptomics data represents a clear limitation. Future studies could explore the possibility of multiple imputation to address the missing data, specifically per patient, however caution should be exercised as imputation does not always reflect the natural biological variation present in omics data. Other limitations that must be considered include several statistical limitations. The relatively small sample size, particularly in severe AP patients, may potentially limit the statistical power. Statistical methods such as CircWave and ‘Harmonic Regression’ must also be scrutinised given that these methods do not explicitly account for a repeated measures data structure. All of these limitations should be considered when interpreting these exploratory results.

In conclusion, this study reports that numerous key circadian genes found within the peripheral blood in AP patients exhibit rhythmic patterns, including the flagship circadian gene, CLOCK. However, this rhythmic expression may be lost in individuals who develop more severe forms of AP. Our analysis did not find a significant association between the cycling of total and differential white blood cell counts and time of day. Additionally, the peripheral blood metabolome, particularly lipid species, exhibited circadian patterns in two separate AP cohorts. While AP severity may influence these patterns, further research is needed to confirm this association. Studies such as this are critical for exploring the potential for new interventions to target critical illness in AP and reduce the high mortality rates associated with severe AP, and open avenues for further research which links these two fields.

Methods

IMOFAP cohort (inflammation, Metabolism and Organ Failure in AP)

Samples and clinical measurements obtained from the IMOFAP cohort were selected for investigation from our data repositories. To summarise the IMOFAP dataset, data were generated from patients recruited to the IMOFAP study with suspected AP (N = 79). Patients were initially enrolled in the study if they met the following criterion: sudden onset abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting and a serum amylase of >100 international units per litre (iU/L). The Atlanta criteria was then used to confirm patients had AP by a clinician, from this 57 patients presented with true AP and were used within the subsequent analyses [8]. Data were collected, where possible, in the form of peripheral blood samples, taken at different time points in the disease course. Peripheral blood samples were obtained at recruitment (timepoint 0 or T0) and then at 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72 and 168 h after the initial sample was taken. Blood was sampled into gel clot-activator tubes for serum and into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes for plasma. Serum and plasma samples were stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. 2.5 ml of peripheral venous blood was also collected into the PAXgene blood RNA tube (BD Biosciences) following the manufacturer's instructions and stored at −80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction and sequencing [48].

The generation of the clinical samples has been discussed in full within the Skouras et al., publication [15,48]. Briefly, clinical samples were comprised of the above peripheral venous blood samples and recorded physiological measurements. For the purpose of this manuscript, CRP, IL-6, IL-10 and TNF-α will be focused on as inflammatory biomarkers given their role in inflammation. CRP is measured in milligrams per litre (mg/L), IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α in picograms per millilitre (pg/mL). Neutrophil (x109 per litre), monocyte (x109 per litre) and lymphocyte (x109 per litre) count were also recorded and will be explored given their potential implication in AP pathophysiology. Some demographics and several key clinical variables for the IMOFAP data have been summarised in Table 4. The table includes the binary variables which do not change across time (N = 57 patients) and then the repeated measures variables (continuous) which includes N = 456 observations in total. The table includes percentages for certain continuous variables and the interquartile range (1st and 3rd quartiles) for others depending on the variable type. The other outstanding laboratory variables have not been included due to the length constraints of this manuscript.

Transcriptomics data

Peripheral venous blood samples (2.5 ml) were drawn at each timepoint with the extraction, storing process and RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) data generation described in full previously by Neyton et al. [15]. Initial raw counts data contained 58,051 genes; a protein coding filter was then applied which reduced the total number of genes to include 19,649 genes in the downstream analysis. Following this, the package NOISeq (ARSyNseq function, version 2.42.0) from the Bioconductor database was them employed to account for the batch effect that was present due to a difference in library preparation methods, polyA selection and rRNA depletion, used to generate the data [15]. Data were then transformed into FPKM to normalise the data prior to analysis. The data were then plotted as principal component analysis (PCA) to observe the difference between raw and the batch corrected data which is available as Supplementary Fig. S1. All plots used within the main manuscript text are spaghetti plots (besides Fig. 8B), used to visualise the repeated measures design for some of the IMOFAP patients. However, it should be carefully noted that the repeated measures design was not employed within analyses.

Metabolomics data

Metabolomics data were generated for both the IMOFAP and KAPVAL cohorts as serum extracts from peripheral blood samples. The IMOFAP metabolomics data was collected at sample time points 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72 and at 7 days (N = 56), whereas KAPVAL contained only one sample time point, 0 (N = 312). In short, serum samples were processed and quantified by Metabolon Inc, using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS). Following this, Metabolon's proprietary software carried out detection and quantification of compounds. In the case of missing data, the minimum value was imputed for that specific metabolite as is common practice. The raw data was then passed on where we transformed the data to log base2 (x+1) and subsequently converted to z-scores for comparison. A subsequent 683 metabolites were retained in the IMOFAP data and 869 metabolites within the KAPVAL dataset. All corresponding super- and sub-pathways were identified by Metabolon as part of the pathway analysis process and were not conducted by us.

Zeitgeber time transformation

Data were originally supplied with sample timepoints but also contained the “real” time of day that samples were taken for the initial sample. The hour information pertaining to each real time of day sample was extracted and converted to a 24-h time scale. Data points which were recorded as sample time point 24 had an additional 24 h added to each point to obtain the real time of day value and distinguish these from sample time point 0. Data were then filtered to keep only values between 0 and 24 h. Therefore, excluding sample timepoint 48 and hour values over 24 (N = 65). The data were then transformed into ZT prior to CircWave analyses. ZT was calculated by setting all values of 0 to 24 (midnight) and by subtracting a period of 7 h from all time values. This step was conducted to ensure that all patients had a “start” time of 7 a.m., and therefore, start at ZT0. An additional 24 h was then added to all negative time points to ensure that time values which had become negative, were adjusted, and set to the opposite end of the ZT day. This time transformation has been visualised and can be found in Supplementary Fig. 2 and S2. For both IMOFAP and KAPVAL datasets, the real time of day was converted to ZT separately for each cohort to align with circadian phases. Given that KAPVAL contained only a single timepoint per patient, repeated-measures effects could not be evaluated, and analyses were conducted independently for each dataset.

Statistics

Statistical analyses of all datasets were carried out within R studio (version 4.2.3 ‘Shortstop Beagle’) and using CircWave software (version 1.4) [80]. CircWave software developed by Hut et al. (available at http://www.euclock.org/and https://www.clocktool.org/index.php), was used to explore the rhythmic expression of genes, metabolites and white blood cell populations, one at a time. Briefly, CircWave software works by fitting a sinusoidal curve, or more than one, to the data to estimate overall rhythmicity. CircWave also provides the following parameters which are useful in assessing amplitude, periodicity, and centre of gravity (COG): data mean, standard deviation (SD), COG (which represents peak time), analysis of variance (ANOVA), F-stat, p-value and r2.

The package ‘HarmonicRegression’ (version 1.0), using the same principles as CircWave, was used to calculate the appropriate p-value, amplitude, and acrophase of each component of interest for all datasets [81].It is important to note that ‘HarmonicRegression’, like CircWave, does not account for the repeated measures structure of the data. However, given the size of the sample and variation in number of observations per patient, these methods were selected to support exploratory, descriptive analyses rather than confirmatory inference. These statistical methods were solely exploratory and aimed to uncover if certain aspects of the data such as genes, metabolites and white bloods cells, were expressing circadian variation. The amplitude (maximum peak value from mesor line, also known as the midline estimating statistic of rhythm) and acrophase (time at which the amplitude is achieved) were converted from radians to 24-h time (and presented to two decimal places). This calculation was made using the R package ‘NISTunits’ (version 1.0.1).

A linear mixed effects model was derived for each of the inflammatory markers of interest and time of disease onset to observe if an association was present, using the packages lme4 and lmerTest [82,83]. Briefly, time categories were as follows creating four categories for our linear mixed effects models: “12am-6am”, “6am – 12pm”, “12pm-6pm” and “6pm-12am”. All linear mixed effects models used a random intercept and an interaction effect was computed for AP severity and the specific time categories. All of these inflammatory markers were log-scaled in the form of log base (x+1) due to their distribution.
