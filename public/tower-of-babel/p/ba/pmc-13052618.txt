Specialised heart valve clinics result in greater adherence to guideline-directed care: a comparative study
Authors: Natalie Montarello, Curtis Page, Sophie Bennett, Vitaliy Androshchuk, Sahrai Saeed, Karine Grigoryan, Camelia Demetrescu, Can Zhou, Ronak Rajani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInternational guidelines emphasise the role of heart valve centres (HVC) and specialist heart valve clinics (SHVC) to deliver optimal care to patients with heart valve disease (HVD). We sought to determine whether we could reaffirm the importance of SHVCs in our centre.MethodsA total of 3731 consecutive non-SHVC (N-SHVC) patients were reviewed over 18 months. Patients (134) with a primary diagnosis of HVD were compared with 173 consecutive SHVC attendees based on international guidelines. Concordance was defined as adherence to recommended frequency of follow-up, investigations, timing of surgery and valve care advice, and discordance as any deviation from this.ResultsOf 134 N-SHVC patients (4.5%) with HVD, 89 (66.4%) had native valve disease and 45 (33.6%) prior heart valve surgery compared with 123 (71.1%) and 50 (28.9%) of SHVC patients, respectively, in the SHVC cohort. In symptomatic patients, the relationship to HVD was not documented in 31.5% of N-SHVC patients versus 5.7% of SHVC patients (p<0.001). Exercise testing was used infrequently in N-SHVCs compared with SHVCs (3.4% vs 32%, p<0.001) and surgical referral was lower (3.4% vs 26.8%, p<0.001). No N-SHVC patients with prior valve surgery received endocarditis prevention advice compared with 100% of SHVC patients. Overall, 34 (38.2%) of N-SHVC patients with native valve disease and 34 (84.4%) with prior cardiac surgery had discordant management compared with 4 (3.3%) and 2 (4%) of SHVC patients respectively.ConclusionAlmost 5% of patients with valve disease are still seen in an N-SHVC setting despite the availability of an SHVC within the same institute and receive suboptimal care.Keywords: Heart Valve Diseases; Echocardiography; Outcome Assessment, Health Care

Introduction

Heart valve disease (HVD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality and is projected to increase in the coming decades. It is currently estimated that in the USA, unexpected significant HVD may be observed in up to 2.5% of the population with an increasing prevalence to 13.2% in those aged >75 years.1 With data suggesting that up to 50% of patients are in NYHA (New York Heart Association) Class III or IV at the time of surgery,2 there remains a perpetual need to improve the detection of disease and to optimise timely treatment before symptoms have ensued. Current guidelines now place emphasis on specialist heart valve centres (HVC) which are defined as centres of excellence in the delivery and treatment of HVD.3 4 Each HVC should have access to specialists in HVD, advanced cardiac imaging, along with surgical and transcatheter treatment options that are determined by appropriate heart team discussions. Despite an emphasis on valve treatment by providers to improve clinical outcomes, many patients with HVD remain asymptomatic and may either go undetected in the community or receive surveillance with their local cardiologist or in a non-specialist heart valve clinic (N-SHVC). Whereas cardiologists may have experience with a large spectrum of cardiac conditions, a heart valve specialist may be considered as one who has (1) a record of training within a heart valve centre, (2) valve-related programmed activity (eg, clinics, inpatient care, Heart Team meetings pertaining to valve disease and research) and (3) continuing medical education in valve disease by attendance at specialist meetings of professional societies.5 The heart valve specialist, in turn, may work within a specialised HVC organisational structure that is comprised of a physician looking after complex or severe valve disease, a specialised valve physiologist/clinical scientist providing clinical surveillance and assessments of patients with moderate/severe disease and a specialised valve nurse who provides long-term care to post-surgical and transcatheter treatment patients.6 This system is ultimately designed to ensure that patients receive guideline-indicated care to ensure not only timely referral for treatment but to provide ongoing care post-treatment to mitigate the risk of complications. Despite the growing interest in SHVC working within HVC, there remains a dearth of data demonstrating that they result in the improved delivery of care compared with more traditional pathways of care being coordinated within N-SHVC. The principal aim of the current study was to therefore evaluate management and treatment outcomes between patients being seen within SHVC and N-SHVC settings.

Methods

Background

The Guy’s Valve Clinic at Guy’s and St Thomas’ NHS Foundation Trust was founded 20 years ago with the principal aim to optimise the outcome of patients with HVD. Its model and design have been replicated across other HVCs across the UK and Europe. It is currently accepted as a standardised validated model of SHVC. Aside from structural valve clinics and cardiac surgical clinics, the core service is comprised of five physicians with a specialist interest in HVD and cardiac imaging, one heart valve specialist nurse and nine highly specialised physiologists/clinical scientists. Together, the service delivers a total of 3400 patient encounters per annum (1240 physiologists/scientists, 1520 physicians and 640 nurse-based appointments).

Study cohort

Inclusion

A total of 3731 clinical attendances were reviewed over a period of 18 months from two N-SHVCs at Guy’s and St Thomas’ Hospitals, London (figure 1). The first was a coronary disease outpatient clinic and the second a heart failure/cardiomyopathy clinic. All clinical letters were reviewed and verified by two independent investigators. After excluding 755 duplicates, 2976 patients remained. Among these, 134 patients with predominant HVD (89 with native valve disease and 45 with prosthetic valve or repair) as a main reason for the clinical attendances were identified.

Study design. HVD, heart valve disease; NHS, National Health Service; N-SHVC, non-specialist heart valve clinic; SHVC, specialist heart valve clinic.

Exclusion

Patients were excluded if there was a valid reason to remain in these clinics (eg, untreated coronary disease, heart failure or cardiomyopathy) with valve disease being considered in these instances to be a bystander to the primary medical condition.

Control cohort

A control group of 173 consecutive patients (123 with native valve disease and 50 with prosthetic valve or repair) was recruited from specialist HVCs over a 4-month time within the recruitment window. Patients with native valve disease were taken from an equivalent valve specialist physician clinic, and post-surgical patients from a valve specialist nurse clinic.

Data collection

The follow-up data and the frequencies of valvular interventions (percutaneous and surgery) were collected by reviewing the electronic patient records. Standard two-dimensional and Doppler transthoracic echocardiograms were obtained by using commercially available ultrasound systems. The severity of HVD was graded according to the joint European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging and American Society of Cardiology guidelines.1 2

Statistical analyses

SPSS V.24.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA) was used for data management and statistical analyses. Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD and categorical variables as proportions in percentage. Comparison of patients at N-SHVC and SHVC was done with Student’s t-test and χ2 test as appropriate. A p value <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

There were 123 patients with a mean age of 67.8±14.6 years, who attended the SHVC and 89 patients with a mean age of 69.1±16.4 years who attended the N-SHVC (table 1). The percentage of men was higher within each clinic, but there was no difference in gender between SHVC and N-SHVC. In both groups, the aortic valve was the most affected valve, followed by the mitral and tricuspid valves. Within the N-SHVC, patients more often had an aortic regurgitation, while in SHVC patients more often had aortic stenosis. Although there was no statisical difference in the symptomatic status of patients who attended SHVC versus N-SHVC, symptoms were more often ascribed to valve disease in SHVC. Exertional dyspnoea was a documented symptom in 24 (20%) of patients attending the SHVC and 35 (39%) the N-SHVC. In 25 (28%) patients attending the N-SHVC symptoms were not specified, while this figure was 0 in the SHVC . Exercise testing to evaluate symptoms was performed in 40 (33%) patients at SHVC versus only 3 (3%) attending the N-SHVC (table 2). In those patients who had a guideline indication for an exercise stress test, this was not performed in 18 (31%) patients attending the SHVC. This increased to 15 (83%) of those patients attending the N-SHVC. Patients who attended SHVC were more likely to be referred for surgery and transcatheter procedures, while patients in the N-SHVC tended to be followed up conservatively. In patients with native HVD, 119 (97%) patients seen within the SHVC received guideline adherent management. This compared with only 55 (62%) patients seen within the N-SHVC (table 3). The principal reasons for this were an inappropriate frequency of investigations, unnecessary earlier follow-up, lack of stress testing and inappropriate discharge from the clinics. The frequencies of patients with prior aortic or mitral valve replacement or repair did not differ between SHVC and N-SHVC, while tricuspid valve replacement or repair was followed up only within the SHVC setting (table 4). There was a trend towards more frequent follow-up than clinically indicated postsurgical patients who attended N-SHVC compared with who attended the SHVC (9.4±4.1 vs 11.9±2.5 months, p<0.001) (table 5). In the subgroup of postoperative patients with higher risk of endocarditis (50 valve replacement or repair in SHVC and 45 in N-SHVC), 2 (4%) patients at SHVC and 38 (84%) at N-SHVC had a discordant management plan to that indicated to be best practice in the guidelines for HVD. Advice on dental care and antibiotic prophylaxis was not given to any patients (0%) seen within the N-SHVC, while this figure was 100% for those patients seen in the SHVC. Similarly, inappropriate frequency of investigation and lack of advice on anticoagulation were more common in the N-SHVC but not observed in the SHVC. Both genders were equally represented in terms of discordant management in the N-SHVC (45 (62.5%)) in men versus 27 ((37.5%) in women, p=0.662). Patients with prosthetic valves or previous repair were more likely to receive discordant management compared with patients with native valve pathology (42 vs 18%, p<0.001). In a univariate logistic regression analysis, higher age (OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.04, p=0.069), male gender (OR 1.16, 95% CI 0.68 to 1.96, p=0.593) or the presence of symptoms (OR 1.14, 95% CI 0.67 to 1.94, p=0.641) were not associated with discordant management.

Discussion

There are several important observations from the current study. First, despite an emphasis on specialist care for patients with HVD, up to 5% of patients continue to be seen in non-specialist valve clinics. Second, our results show that valve care co-ordinated in this setting has only a modest adherence to international guidelines recommended care at 62% versus 97% to those seen in a specialist setting. Finally, in those patients who have undergone valve treatment, aftercare advice and guidance are universally poor when delivered by non-valve specialists. With the recognition that valve disease is a leading cause of death in Europe and other western countries, there have been substantial efforts to improve the clinical outcomes for patients with HVD. These have focused on not only new transcatheter and surgical treatments, but also cardiac imaging and organisational systems to early detect and monitor asymptomatic HVD. The fundamental goal is to provide surveillance to patients until the onset of symptoms to ensure the timely delivery of treatment before the onset of irreversible cardiac damage ensues. Following treatment, there is also a requirement to provide lifelong management to patients with prosthetic heart valves and to those who have undergone valve repair. Current guidelines emphasise the importance of HVC to deliver optimal quality of care to patients with HVD.3 4 This is being achieved by centres who not only have a high procedural volume but also in conjunction with specialists who have a common interest in HVD and have undergone appropriate training.5 Within the HVC, SHVCs are a critical requirement. These serve a means to organise standardised and guideline-directed care to patients. Although a framework exists for their design, these may vary in their design and complexity from an isolated physician to a multiple physician service with specialist heart valve nurses and physiologists.6,8 Despite this, the fundamental requirement is the same. Those working within this structure should be individuals who are valve specialists. This is being validated by those taking part routinely in clinical duties pertaining to HVD, having received appropriate specialist training and taking part in relevant continuing medical education and research. Outside of an SHVC, at least one-third of elderly patients may not be considered for valve intervention despite clear indications. Furthermore, following surgery data suggests that only 56% of centres offer routine surveillance, although guidelines would recommend an annual assessment.9 There are only a few studies that indicate that SHVCs provide improved care. Chambers et al showed that the implementation of patient-centred care within SHVC improved adherence to best practice guidelines from 41% to 92% and reduced the need for unnecessary echocardiograms.10 Furthermore, Lancellotti et al showed that for patients with asymptomatic aortic stenosis, patients followed up in SHVC had a low rate of death which supports this model of care as being safe and effective.11 There nevertheless remains a dearth of data directly comparing guideline-based management for patients with HVD within and outside an SHVC setting.

In the current study, we observed that despite having evolved SHVC available in our institute, 5% of patients remained in N-SHVC. Whereas 97% of patients within an SHVC setting received guideline-based care, this was only 62% in the N-SHVC. Furthermore, despite 62% of patients reporting symptoms in the N-SHVC, it was not specified in 45% of patients whether their symptoms were attributable to valve disease or not. The concordance with guidelines was far lower in the N-SHVC with the main culprits being a lack of referral to treatment when appropriate thresholds had been reached, inappropriate follow-up durations, increased investigation use, inappropriate discharge from hospital services and a lack of exercise testing to reveal potentially masked symptoms. When postsurgical patients were reviewed, we found that whereas 100% of SHVC patients received appropriate advice about dental hygiene and antibiotic prophylaxis against endocarditis, no patients in the N-SHVC received similar advice. Although the percentage of patients with HVD being seen in N-SHVC was relatively small, this is likely to reflect the long-term establishment of referral pathways to SHVC at our institute. Concerningly, despite this, we observed significant differences in guideline-directed care administered to presurgical and postsurgical patients. In centres where SHVCs are yet to evolve, it is perceivable that this effect may be magnified. The differences we observed are likely explained by a poorer awareness of international guidelines,3 4 newer treatment options and the indications for these. Furthermore, it is likely that those physicians with valve disease competencies are better able to determine symptoms related to valve disease and to unmask concealed symptoms than those without.12

Overall, our data provides compelling evidence to endorse SHVC to administer care to patients with HVD. It also provides important insights as to where differences in management may occur, not only in preoperative evaluation but also postoperative care. It is anticipated that these differences may result not only in differences in patients outcomes but also increased costs to healthcare systems by virtue of more frequent follow-up and unnecessary investigations.10

Limitations

There are several limitations to our study. We recognise that this is a single centre retrospective study with their being a lack of long-term follow-up. Nevertheless, the objective was not overall clinical outcomes, but more so guideline-indicated care and the index clinical appointment and how this differed between specialist and non-specialist clinics. It would have been useful to compare our findings with other centres who do and do not have SHVC. This was not possible, however, owing to the heterogeneity of SHVC design across the UK and the lack of a unified health records system. We are also unable to account for verbal discussions and advice that were administered to patients in the N-SHVC that were not documented in writing in clinical notes and recognise that this may have been a confounder to the data collection.

Conclusions

HVD care when administered by SHVCs results in a greater adherence to clinical guidelines for patients with native valve disease and in postsurgical patients. Furthermore, our data signals a more efficient clinical pathway with fewer unwarranted investigations and unnecessary follow-up appointments, and better utilisation of functional testing to unmask concealed symptoms.
