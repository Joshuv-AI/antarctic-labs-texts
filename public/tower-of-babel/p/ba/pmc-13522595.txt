Stepwise effects of formulation additives and process parameters on ethanol injection-prepared nanoliposomes for cancer delivery: a preformulation study
Authors: Ahmed E Gouda, Omar S Sakr, Maha Nasr, Omaima A Sammour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ethanol injection method has emerged as a simple and rapid technique for the preparation of nanoliposomes. Since the size and polydispersity of nanoliposomes are crucial properties which directly affect their therapeutic effectiveness, the focus of this paper was to study the influence of different additives such as cholesterol, poloxamer 188, PEG 400, and cationic lipids, as well as process parameters on the particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) of nanoliposomes intended for cancer treatment. The optimum conditions were using phospholipid concentration 20 mg/mL in ethanol, injection of the ethanolic phase along the direction of aqueous phase, use of hand-press injection of the ethanolic solution, and the use of buffer as aqueous phase. The nanoliposomes had an average size of 91.12 ± 5.50 nm with a PDI of 0.15 ± 0.01. Their properties were influenced by cholesterol content and additives such as poloxamer 188, PEG 400, and cationic lipids. PEG 400 reduced particle size to 79.70 ± 1.80 nm, while cholesterol and cationic lipids increased it to 126.29 ± 8.39 nm and 130.34 ± 8.77 nm, respectively. The optimized formulation co-loaded with apigenin and gallic acid showed high entrapment efficiency (84.74 ± 2.33% and 60.06 ± 3.94%, respectively). It also demonstrated significantly higher cytotoxicity than free drugs, with a much lower IC50 (2.99 ± 0.2 µM vs. 25.97 ± 0.9 µM), highlighting the importance of formulation parameters. This suggests that preformulation studies are crucial to delineate the best process parameters and optimum additives and their concentrations.Keywords: Nanoliposomes, Ethanol injection, PEG 400, Poloxamer 188, Cationic lipid, Particle sizeSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Several drug delivery systems have been developed for the treatment of cancer such as polymer-based nanoparticles, dendrimers, nanospheres, magnetic nanoparticles, and lipid-based nanoparticles such as liposomes1–8, with the latter accounting for the majority of the published reports owing to their safety, biocompatibility, and ability to encapsulate both hydrophobic and hydrophilic moieties8–18. A liposome is a vesicular delivery system composed of self-assembled amphiphilic molecules formed of one or several concentric bilayers19,20. They are known for their sustainment of drug release at a predetermined rate, minimization of undesirable side effects of drugs, biocompatibility, localization of drugs at the site of action, and improvement of the solubility of drugs. For cancer-related applications, drug-loaded liposomes are often designed with particle sizes below 400 nm, as this size range is generally considered favorable for passive accumulation in tumor tissues via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect21,22, although this behavior requires biological validation.

Generally, vesicular systems preparation methods can be classified into three categories: mechanical methods, detergent removal methods, and solvent dispersion methods (Fig. 1). The mechanical method comprises three consecutive steps: preparation of a lipid film, hydration of the lipid film, and size reduction of the produced vesicular system. For the preparation of the lipid film, the lipid is dissolved in chloroform or a mixture of chloroform and methanol followed by evaporation of the organic solvent23. After complete drying of the lipid film, the hydration step is carried out by adding an aqueous phase heated to the phase transition temperature of the lipid, with continuous swirling of the flask, followed by size reduction using sonication or extrusion23–25. The detergent removal method involves the use of a detergent at its critical micelle concentration to solubilize the lipid, hence acting as the organic solvent, followed by its removal to initiate lipid aggregation and formation of the bilayer structure26–28. On the other hand, the solvent dispersion method is based on the presence of a solvent and an antisolvent which are miscible together, in which the lipid and lipophilic drugs are dissolved in the organic solvent such as ethanol, then dispersed into an antisolvent (aqueous phase)27,29. The ethanol injection method is one of the solvent dispersion techniques that has gained widespread use for the preparation of nanoliposomes due to its ability to produce homogeneous particles with reproducible sizes, along with its simplicity, safety (as it does not involve harmful chemicals), and the small size of the resulting nanoliposomes30–32. One of the merits of the ethanol injection method is that it offers the energy required for the formation of small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs) in only one step through bending of small bilayer phospholipid fragments (BPF)33, as shown in Fig. 2.

Comparison between different methods used to prepare liposomes, highlighting that the ethanol injection method involves the fewest steps and enables the direct formation of SUVs.

Schematic illustration of the mechanism of liposome formation by the ethanol injection method. Upon injection of the ethanolic lipid solution into a stirred aqueous phase, the solution is dispersed into fine droplets containing a small number of phospholipid molecules. As ethanol diffuses into the surrounding aqueous phase, phospholipid bilayer fragments are formed and subsequently fuse to produce small unilamellar vesicles SUVs.

The integral component in the composition of liposomes is phospholipids, which are amphiphilic molecules having an affinity for both organic and aqueous phases. Nanoliposomes may consist of single or multiple lipid bilayers, in which the hydrophobic domains of the lipid are oriented inward, away from the external water phase, while the hydrophilic head groups are oriented outward toward the surrounding aqueous environment. Phospholipids, particularly phosphatidylcholine (PC), are biocompatible molecules that undergo self-assembly in aqueous media to form of vesicles with an aqueous core, in which the polar head groups are directed outward to the aqueous phase while the non-polar tails are directed inward34. The polar head group may possess a neutral, positive, or negative charge29,34,35. In addition to phospholipids, several functional additives have been reported to be incorporated into the liposomal structure to enhance its physicochemical and functional properties. Among these additives, cholesterol has been shown to alter the arrangement and mechanical strength of the formed bilayer, thereby stabilizing the liposomes both physically and in vivo. This stabilization prolongs their circulation time and is anticipated to enhance their anticancer efficacy36–38.

Another additive reported to be incorporated in liposomes is polyethylene glycol (PEG 400), a polyether compound composed of repetitive ethylene glycol units. PEG is well known for its stealth properties, offering steric stabilization to nanoliposomes, decreasing their immunogenicity, enhancing drug targeting, minimizing uptake by the mononuclear phagocytic system, and improving the uptake of the encapsulated drug by cancer cells34,39–42. Poloxamer 188 is another additive, which is a nonionic polymer which can be incorporated within liposomes to modulate the release of encapsulated drugs and increase their entrapment, as it forms a viscous aqueous core43. It also prevents nanoliposome aggregation through steric hindrance, thereby improving nanoparticle stability44. Poloxamer 188 can also overcome multidrug resistance by inhibiting drug efflux transporters, such as P-glycoprotein, while enhancing the cellular uptake and cytotoxicity of drugs in cancer cells45–50. In addition, cationic lipids such as dioctadecyl dimethylammonium bromide (DODAB) when incorporated in liposomes, have been reported to enhance the delivery of entrapped cytotoxic drugs to tumor tissues compared to neutral vesicles. This effect is attributed to electrostatic interactions with negatively charged components on tumor endothelial cells and cancer cell membranes51. The combination of PEG with cationic lipids has been shown to further enhance the cytotoxicity and localization of the liposome-entrapped drugs in tumor tissues52,53.

Although ethanol injection is widely used, the isolated and combined effects of common additives and process variables on nanoliposomal size uniformity remain insufficiently delineated. Therefore, this study aims to address this gap by systematically evaluating both process parameters and formulation additives within a unified framework, enabling rational optimization of nanoliposome design and delineating their influence on particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) for improved cancer treatment. The effect of incorporating some of the most common functional additives such as cholesterol, cationic lipid, poloxamer 188, and PEG 400 into liposome formulations was investigated for their influence on the particle size and PDI of the nanoliposomes, with selected concentrations chosen based on previously published studies. Furthermore, apigenin and gallic acid were selected as model anticancer compounds to be co-loaded into the optimized nanoliposomal system for preliminary evaluation of its therapeutic potential. It is noteworthy that the present study adopts a stepwise preformulation optimization strategy, rather than a formal factorial or response surface design, as an initial screening approach.

The effect of solvent to antisolvent percent

As shown in Table 1, changing the percent of solvent (ethanol) to antisolvent (PBS) from 7.5% to 25% resulted in a non-significant change in the particle size of nanoliposomes (P > 0.05), while it started to increase significantly from 25% till 200% (P < 0.05), with an accompanying increase in the PDI reaching 1 in some formulations.

Based on the aforementioned, and owing to the non-significant particle size between S7.5, S15, and S25 (prepared using a solvent to antisolvent ratio 7.5%, 15%, and 25% respectively), the solvent to antisolvent 25% ratio was selected for further experiments since it yielded nanoliposomes with the least PDI value (0.05) compared to the other two formulations.

Effect of injection direction

Injecting the solvent either along with or opposite to the antisolvent flow resulted in no significant difference in the particle size of nanoliposomes (P > 0.05), with values of 89.55 ± 7.3 and 95.54 ± 8.42 respectively. Therefore, for subsequent experiments, the solvent was injected along the direction of the antisolvent flow.

Effect of injection speed

Injecting ethanol by hand press yielded significantly smaller particle size than using dropwise injection of solvent (88.92 ± 7.06 nm and 112 ± 3.79 nm respectively, P < 0.05). Therefore, the nanoliposomes were prepared using hand-press rather than dropwise injection in further experiments.

Effect of the antisolvent type

Upon preparing nanoliposomes using deionized water or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) pH 7.4, the particle size of liposomes was not significantly affected (102.15 ± 22.1 and 82.81 ± 2.6 respectively, P > 0.05). Therefore, PBS (pH 7.4) was selected as the antisolvent of choice due to its ability to mimic physiological conditions.

Comparing the formulations prepared with cholesterol (C0.5 to C8) with the formulation prepared at similar conditions without cholesterol at all (S25) (Table 1), it was obvious that the particle size increased significantly (P < 0.05). On the other hand, the increase in cholesterol concentration led to a significant increase in PDI values (P < 0.05). Only formulation C-0.5 showed PDI below 0.2 which represents a unimodal particle size distribution for the nanoliposomes formulation. Therefore, cholesterol at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL was included in nanoliposomes preparation in further experiments, and the rest of the formulations were excluded from the optimization consideration.

Characterization of the drug-loaded nanoliposomal formulation

Upon loading apigenin and gallic acid within the nanoliposomal formulation CT, its particle size increased to 146.36 ± 7.87 nm with PDI 0.14 ± 0.08 and zeta potential + 10.30 ± 4.30. Upon storage for three months, the formulation displayed insignificant changes in particle size (161 ± 14.3 nm), PDI (0.12 ± 0.07) and zeta potential (+ 11.50 ± 3.22 mV) (P > 0.05). The entrapment efficiency of apigenin and gallic acid in the prepared nanoliposomes were 84.74 ± 2.33% and 60.06 ± 3.94%, respectively.

Regarding cytotoxicity assessment in A549 cancer cell line, the IC50 value of the drug-combination was 25.97 ± 0.9 µM, which was decreased to 2.99 ± 0.2 µM for the drug-loaded nanoliposomal formulation.

Discussion

Mechanistically, vesicle size in the ethanol injection method is governed by the balance between solvent diffusion-driven lipid self-assembly and bilayer-modifying effects of formulation additives. Factors that slow ethanol exchange or expand bilayer packing tend to promote the formation of larger vesicles, whereas factors that enhance dispersion or perturb lipid packing may favor smaller vesicles.

The mean particle size of nanoliposomes has a great impact on their fate, in which the smaller the nanoliposomes the better their uptake by the cells and the longer their circulation time27,55. It was reported that nanoliposomes with particle size less than 500 nm are preferred for uptake by the cancerous cells, relying on the EPR effect52,56,57. Therefore, several parameters were tested: namely the phospholipid concentration, injection direction and speed (dropwise or hand press), solvent to the antisolvent percent, and type of antisolvent (aqueous phase), to study their effect on the particle size and polydispersity of nanoliposomes. However, it is important to emphasize that while particle size is an important determinant of nanoliposomal behavior, it alone does not define therapeutic performance, which is governed by additional factors such as drug encapsulation, release characteristics, and cellular interactions, as partially demonstrated by the functional evaluations included in this study.

The range of phospholipid concentration was chosen to cover low to relatively high lipid concentrations commonly reported in ethanol injection-based liposome preparation, allowing evaluation of its effect on particle size and dispersity while maintaining manageable viscosity and injectability. The range for solvent-to-antisolvent ratio was chosen to systematically assess the influence of ethanol fraction on liposome formation. Lower ratios were selected based on commonly reported conditions, while higher ratios were included to probe the upper boundary at which particle size and homogeneity begin to deteriorate, thereby defining a robust operating window. Cholesterol concentration range was selected based on literature reports demonstrating the role of cholesterol in modulating bilayer rigidity and stability, while allowing assessment of both minimal and relatively high incorporation levels. The selected concentrations for the additives PEG 400 and poloxamer 188 were based on previously published studies reporting effective steric stabilization and modulation of liposomal properties at these levels. Finally, the cationic lipid DODAB ratio was selected based on preliminary screening experiments aimed at identifying a concentration that provides sufficient surface charge without compromising colloidal stability. While these preliminary data are not included to maintain the focus of the current manuscript, the selected ratio falls within commonly reported ranges for cationic liposome formulations.

The increase in the particle size of nanoliposomes with increased phospholipid concentration could be attributed to the elevation of the internal viscosity of the lipid solvent phase with increasing phospholipid concentration, which in turn slows the diffusion rate of the ethanol to the external aqueous phase, hence providing a chance for more lipid to precipitate and form bigger liposomes58,59.

The increase in particle size and PDI of nanoliposomes with changing the percent of solvent (ethanol) to antisolvent (PBS) is consistent with previous reports60,61. This increase in particle size could be attributed to differences in the solubility of phospholipids in water and ethanol, as well as their affinity for ethanol. This high affinity may lead to the retention of residual ethanol among the phospholipid head groups, thereby possibly explaining the larger liposome size at higher ethanol percentages30,62.

Injection direction has been reported to affect the particle size of the produced nanoparticles, with injection along the antisolvent flow direction yielding smaller particles63. In our case, injecting the solvent either along or opposite the antisolvent flow resulted in no significant difference in the particle size of the nanoliposomes, suggesting that this factor is not crucial for nanoliposome optimization.

It was reported that increasing the injection speed of the solvent causes a decrease in the particle size of the vesicles, owing to the increased sheering force between ethanol and the aqueous phase at the injecting point. This in turn enhances the dispersion of ethanolic lipid molecules in water, leading to the subsequent precipitation of lipids at the boundaries of small bubbles and the formation of vesicles with smaller particle size64–66. This concurred with our results, in which injecting ethanol by hand press yielded a significantly smaller particle size compared to dropwise solvent injection.

The osmolarity of the aqueous phase was also reported to influence the particle size of liposomes27,58,67,68. However, upon preparing nanoliposomes using deionized water or PBS (pH 7.4), the particle size of liposomes was not significantly affected.

Regarding the effect of different formulation additives on the properties of nanoliposomes, several additives have been reported to be incorporated into liposomal vesicles, to improve their physicochemical properties, or enhance their therapeutic activity for the intended indication. The effects of four additives; namely cholesterol, poloxamer 188, PEG 400, and the positive charge inducer cationic lipid DODAB on the properties of nanoliposomes was investigated in this study.

Introducing cholesterol to liposomes has been reported to fill the spaces within the phospholipid bilayer, improve liposomal stability, reduce membrane flexibility, and confer controlled release properties38,69,70. Increasing the cholesterol concentration from 0.5 to 8 mg/mL did not significantly affect the particle size of nanoliposomes, which came in accordance with other authors65,71,72. Comparing the formulations prepared with cholesterol with the formulation prepared at similar conditions without cholesterol at all, the particle size increased significantly, which could be attributed to the incorporation of cholesterol within the phospholipid bilayer, thereby increasing the distance between phospholipid molecules, with consequent increase in particle size70,73. On the other hand, increasing the cholesterol concentration led to a significant increase in PDI values, suggesting greater heterogeneity in the nanoliposomes population, which came in accordance with other authors74,75. The PDI quantifies the breadth of particle size distribution within a sample, scaled from 0.0 (perfectly monodisperse) to 1.0 (highly heterogeneous). While a PDI of ≤ 0.2 is the standard benchmark for polymer-based nanoparticles, lipidic systems like nanoliposomes typically permit a slightly broader threshold, in which a PDI of 0.3 or less is recognized as a sign of a sufficiently homogeneous vesicular population for drug delivery76,77.

The incorporation of PEG 400 into liposomes is particularly beneficial for cancer treatment, since it increases circulation time, decreases phagocytosis, and increases accumulation in tumor tissues34,78. Regarding poloxamer 188, it was reported to enhance the cytotoxic activity of drugs in cancer treatment, as well as to increase the viscosity of the aqueous part of the liposomes, hence inrceasing the entrapment of hydrophilic drugs43,46. In the current study, PEG 400 was used at a concentration of 5% v/v in ethanol79, while poloxamer 188 was used at a concentration of 5% w/w of the phospholipids80, based on previously reported findings. In the present study, the incorporation of poloxamer 188 did not exhibit a significant change in particle size of liposomes, while on the contrary, the inclusion of PEG 400 either alone or in combination with poloxamer 188 significantly decreased the particle size of liposomes. This might be attributed to the perturbation effect of PEG 400 on the phospholipid packing within the bilayer, due to its extensive interaction with and immobilization of water molecules, thereby possibly enhancing the bilayer fluidity and reducing the size of liposomes81.

Inclusion of the cationic lipid DODAB in liposomes confers benefits in cancer treatment since the cationic liposomes are expected to adhere better to the negatively charged molecules on the surface of tumor tissue51,82–84. In our study, the incorporation of DODAB significantly increased the particle size of the liposomal formulations, which could be attributed to the inclusion of positive charge molecules within the bilayer; creating repulsion within the phospholipid bilayer, with a consequent increase in the particle size84. While such surface charge characteristics may influence interactions with negatively charged cell membranes, these effects should be interpreted cautiously, as zeta potential was evaluated for a limited subset of formulations in the current study. Despite the increase in the particle size induced by DODAB, these formulations still exhibited a favorable size for cancer treatment, since particles less than 500 nm were reported to be effective85,86. It is worth noting that the proposed optimized nanoliposomal system was successfully used to co-load two model nutraceutical anticancer drugs, apigenin and gallic acid, for cancer treatment. Apigenin was highly entrapped in nanoliposomes owing to its lipophilicity, delineated by its log P value of 3.0287, which allows its subtle incorporation within the lipidic bilayers of the nanoliposomes. On the other hand, gallic acid which has a lower log P value of 0.788, and higher aqueous solubility than apigenin was entrapped at a lower extent, mostly in the aqueous core of the nanoliposomes. The formulation retained its stability after storage for three months, which might be attributed to presence of cholesterol as a membrane stabilizing agent, and the charged nature of the formulation which hindered its aggregation via electrostatic repulsion9. The demonstrated anticancer activity of apigenin and gallic acid coincided with their reported apoptosis induction in A549 cancer cells89,90. The superior anticancer activity of the nanoliposomal formulation compared to free drugs in A549 cells is likely due to improved cellular uptake via endocytosis, while the incorporation of DODAB further enhances this effect through electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged cell membrane, promoting more efficient internalization and increased cytotoxicity91–93.

While the ethanol injection method for nanoliposomes preparation was successfully optimized in this work, the investigation was restricted to a specific set of additives (cholesterol, PEG 400, poloxamer 188, and DODAB), and the results may not be applicable to other functional additives. Despite the comprehensive evaluation of process parameters and selected formulation additives, this study has certain limitations. While encapsulation efficiency, preliminary stability, and cytotoxicity assessments were included to support the functional relevance of the optimized formulation, other important characterization aspects such as detailed drug release behavior, advanced morphological analysis (e.g., TEM or cryo-TEM), and residual ethanol quantification were not investigated within the scope of this work. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted within the context of a preformulation study, and further in-depth investigations are required to fully establish the performance and translational potential of the developed nanoliposomal system. Moreover, while the obtained particle size range is often considered favorable for passive targeting, it should be emphasized that size alone does not ensure tumor accumulation, and validation through in vitro and in vivo studies is required to confirm any targeting or therapeutic advantage. Furthermore, while the ethanol injection method offers simplicity and suitability for preformulation screening, its scalability and reproducibility may be limited compared to advanced techniques such as microfluidics, which provide tighter control over mixing and particle formation.

Preliminary optimization of nanoliposomes preparation using the ethanol injection method

Many factors influence the characteristics of nanoliposomes. In order to optimize the properties of the produced nanoliposomes, several testing parameters must be evaluated for their effect on the particle size and polydispersity. These parameters include the phospholipid concentration of ethanolic lipid solution, injection direction and speed (dropwise or hand press), solvent to the antisolvent ratio, and type of antisolvent (aqueous phase). The ethanolic lipid solution was injected into an aqueous phase using a 3 mL disposable syringe fitted with a 21-gauge needle. For dropwise injection, the ethanolic lipid solution was added over approximately 45–75 s until complete introduction into the aqueous phase. The aqueous phase was maintained under continuous stirring at around 700 rpm at ambient temperature, followed by evaporation of ethanol using a rotary evaporator under vacuum until complete removal (IKA RV 10 basic, Germany).

Four different phospholipid concentrations were prepared by dissolving phospholipid in 1.25 mL of ethanol, followed by hand press injection of ethanol into 5 mL of stirred PBS (pH 7.4) along the flow direction, to achieve solvent to the antisolvent percent of 25%. Preliminary functional and stability evaluation of the drug-loaded optimized formulation CT

The plain nanoliposomal formulation CT was chosen for loading two model anticancer nutraceuticals; apigenin and gallic acid, for further testing the anticancer activity and storage stability of the prepared nanoliposomes. Formulation CT was prepared as previously described, with the inclusion of 0.3 mg apigenin and 12.5 mg gallic acid in the ethanolic phase. The entrapment efficiency was determined after centrifugation using a Nanosep tube (PALL laboratory Company, USA)11. The formulations were centrifuged for 30 min at a speed of 15,000 g at room temperature (NEYA 16 R, REMI laboratory instruments, India). The supernatant was collected from the lower compartment and diluted with ethanol, then the unentrapped drugs were quantified using HPLC as reported in our previously published paper94. The entrapment efficiency percentage of the drugs was calculated from the supernatant according to the following equation:

Entrapment Efficiency (%) = (total drug − unentrapped drug)/total drug × 100.

The drug-loaded formulation was also assessed for particle size, PDI, zeta potential, and all these criteria were re-measured after refrigeration storage at 2–8 °C for 3 months, to assess its storage stability11.

Furthermore, the cytotoxicity of the drug-loaded formulation and the drugs-combination was determined using SRB assay on A549 lung cancer cells from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Aliquots of 100 µl cell suspension (5 × 10³ cells) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated in complete media (DMEM + 10% FBS) for 24 h. Cells were then treated with 100 µl media containing the samples at different concentrations (0.03–300 µM). After 72 h of exposure, cells were fixed with 10% TCA and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by washing with distilled water. SRB solution (0.4% w/v) was added and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Plates were washed with 1% acetic acid, air-dried, and TRIS buffer (10 mM) was added to dissolve the bound dye. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability % was calculated by comparing the optical density of treated cells to control cells, and the results were used to determine IC50 values.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate. All data passed normality test, and was displayed as mean ± standard deviation. GraphPad Prism version 8.00, USA was used to perform the statistical analysis of data using student T-test (Sect. 2.1.3, 2.1.4, 2.1.5 and 2.2.3) or One-way analysis of variance test (ANOVA) followed by Tukey Kramer test (all other sections) to check the statistical significance of the difference. A P value ≤ 0.05 was used to determine the degree of significance.

Conclusions

In the current paper, we have delineated some key parameters influencing the particle size and polydispersity of nanoliposomes prepared using the ethanol injection method. Increasing phospholipid concentration as well as increasing the percent of solvent to antisolvent led to significant increase in the particle size and polydispersity of nanoliposomes. Moreover, the effect of some functional additives most commonly used to decorate nanoliposomes for cancer treatment has been studied as well. Both cholesterol and cationic lipid caused a significant increase in particle size of nanoliposomes, while PEG 400 decreased it and poloxamer 188 had no effect. The effect of incorporating the cationic lipid was evident from the shift in the zeta potential of the liposomes. The results delineate that preformulation studies should be conducted prior to drug loading in nanoparticles to identify the optimum conditions for their preparation.
