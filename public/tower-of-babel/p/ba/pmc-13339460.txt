The m.14484T>C MT‐ND6 Mutation Presenting with a Hereditary Spastic‐Paraparesis Phenotype
Authors: Gabriel Amorelli, Christelle Nilles, Gerald Pfeffer, Matthew Joel, Davide Martino
Published: 2026

Mutations in the MT‐ND6 gene are classically associated with Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON). This presents as a subacute, painless loss of central vision in one eye, followed by loss of vision in the second eye within weeks or months in young men. 1 Variable neurological, cardiac, and skeletal abnormalities have been described with LHON. Neurological manifestations include cerebellar ataxia 1 and, rarely, Parkinsonism. 2 Here, we report an unusual presentation of late‐onset spastic ataxia with cognitive impairment, but without visual loss associated with LHON.

A 74‐year‐old man, a former smoker (10 pack‐years) who quit in his twenties, social drinker, without any history of other recreational drug exposure and without a family history of neurological conditions, developed a subjective sensation of “brain fog” at age 64. During the following 3 years, he progressively developed dysarthria, gait abnormalities and balance impairment, resulting in frequent falls. Initial assessment at the Movement Disorders Clinic revealed a complex movement disorder consisting of prominent gait ataxia and dysarthria (Video 1—Segment A). Over time, he progressed with worsening dysarthria, spastic paraparesis, and hyperreflexia (Video 1—Segment B). Approximately 5 years after onset, he began to experience mild dysphagia for both liquids and solids. A comprehensive neuropsychological assessment subsequently showed evidence of frontal cognitive impairment.

Segment A of the video demonstrates an earlier phase of this patient's clinical evolution, dominated by cerebellar syndromic features encompassing scanned dysarthric speech, wide‐based gait with an irregular trajectory, disequilibrium and inability to walk in tandem, all consistent with cerebellar gait ataxia. Segment B demonstrates a later phase, in which spastic paraparesis has become the predominant feature, expressed, amongst other features, by hyper‐reflexia with slow return in neutral position and spastic features on spontaneous gait.

Brain, cervical, and thoracic spine MRIs and brain PET‐FDG did not reveal intracranial or intraspinal structural or metabolic findings. A paraneoplastic antibody panel, syphilis screening, vitamin E deficiency screening, HIV, HTLV, and anti‐GAD antibody screening, and electromyography with nerve conduction studies were all normal. CSF showed no inflammatory changes. He underwent genetic screening for autosomal dominant spinocerebellar ataxias, Friedreich ataxia and a targeted sequencing panel for hereditary spastic paraparesis, all of which returned normal. Clinical testing for FGF14 and RFC1 expansion was also negative. Subsequent targeted sequencing of the ataxia panel identified a pathogenic m.14484T>C variant in MT‐ND6 with 99% heteroplasmy in blood. To ensure no alternative or dual genetic diagnosis had been missed, we analyzed raw exome sequencing data from the genetic testing panel from Blueprint Genetics (Helsinki, Finland), which did not reveal an alternative genetic diagnosis (Supplementary Methods and Results).

The result of a mitochondrial mutation strongly associated with LHON prompted us to pursue a formal neuro‐ophthalmological assessment that revealed slow saccades, saccadic smooth pursuit, mild convergence insufficiency, and asymptomatic left hyperphoria. There were no abnormalities in visual acuity, and only scattered drusen were noted on fundoscopy, without optic disc edema. Optical coherence tomography showed normal macular volume, a non‐significant slight elevation of the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (OD, 111; OS, 107), and normal ganglion layer measurements. Findings that are not in keeping with optic neuropathy.

We report the case of a late‐onset spastic paraparesis (HSP) syndrome associated with gait ataxia and early falls, dysarthria, cognitive impairment, and dysphagia, associated with a pathogenic variant in MT‐ND6, commonly causative of LHON. Interestingly, the absence of optic neuropathy in our case is not so unusual for this variant, which has an incomplete penetrance for vision loss (18% in men). 3 Furthermore, environmental exposures and more precisely tobacco have been associated with increased clinical penetrance in LHON regardless of gender and alcohol consumption. 4 The patient had a remote history of smoking, which may represent another element favoring this presentation.

Genetic HSP syndromes are characterized by wide phenotypic variation. 5 There are currently more than 90 nuclear genes associated with HSP with autosomal dominant, recessive or X‐linked inheritance. Certain mtDNA variants, such as m.9176T>C in MT‐ATP6, represent a small minority of genetic causes of HSP. 6 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of a pathogenic variant of MT‐ND6 presenting with a late‐onset complex HSP syndrome. Although not definitive by itself, we were not able to identify an alternative diagnosis with analysis of exome data, nor with extensive workup for acquired causes of neurodegeneration or other secondary causes.

The combination of LHON with extra‐ocular manifestations has been described as “LHON‐plus syndrome.” Cases of LHON‐plus syndrome have included ataxia, movement disorders such as Parkinsonism, dystonia (cervical, generalized) and rarely pyramidal findings. In some of these cases, abnormal T2/FLAIR signal in the putamen, 1 the midbrain, the pons, 7 and leukoencephalopathy 8 were found on brain MR. Exceptionally, brain MRI can be normal, 2 like in our patient, and movement disorders can develop up to 37 years after the onset of the ophthalmological disease. 8 Interestingly, LHON‐plus syndromes were reported in children in association with pyramidal findings and movement disorders. 9

Our patient carries a m.14484T>C pathogenic variant in MT‐ND6, one of the most common causative variants for LHON. 3 LHON‐plus syndromes manifesting with movement disorders are also genetically heterogeneous. Earlier case reports documented a LHON‐plus syndrome with autosomal recessive inheritance associated with a variant of the nuclear DNAJC30 gene and manifesting with spastic paraparesis, dystonia, ataxia with or without visual abnormalities. 10 The DNAJC30 gene is crucial for the repair of the Complex I mitochondrial complex, which would potentially explain this phenotypic overlap between the different genetic mutations. 11 Importantly, in our case, we did not find any abnormalities in the DNAJC30 gene after careful exome data review.

In summary, our observation broadens the clinical spectrum of MT‐ND6 variants and should encourage physicians to screen for this condition in case of gait ataxia and spastic paraparesis with concerns about late‐onset HSP. Eventually, adding MT‐ND6 to HSP screening panels should be considered.
