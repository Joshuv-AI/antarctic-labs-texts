Resting-state neural oscillations of the motor system influence the resting motor threshold
Authors: Jingna Jin, Xin Wang, Xinyu Zhao, He Wang, Ying Li, Zhipeng Liu, Tao Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterindividual variability in resting motor threshold (RMT) reflects baseline differences in corticospinal tract excitability and brain sensitivity to transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS). However, the impact of baseline motor system neural oscillation on RMT remains unknown. We used a cross-sectional observational study. RMTs and resting-state electroencephalograms were collected in a single experimental session from 95 participants in our study. The characteristics of neural oscillations are reflected by power spectral density (PSD) and phase locking value (PLV) in different bands. We found significant weak-to-moderate negative correlations of RMT with delta-band PSD in bilateral central and frontal regions, as well as theta-band PLV in bilateral central regions. Together, these measures accounted for approximately 18.5% of the variance in RMT. Our findings enhance the understanding of how resting-state neural oscillations modulate the brain’s sensitivity to TMS and provide new insights for developing more effective TMS strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55832-y.Keywords: Resting motor threshold, Transcranial magnetic stimulation, Electroencephalography, Neural oscillations, Motor systemSubject terms: Motor cortex, Biomedical engineering, Neurophysiology

Introduction

The resting motor threshold (RMT) is a fundamental measurement in transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) studies and is defined as the lowest stimulus intensity capable of evoking MEPs with a peak-to-peak amplitude greater than 50 µV in at least 5 out of 10 consecutive TMS pulses, while the target muscle was completely relaxed1–3. The RMT reflects the transsynaptic activation of corticospinal neurons, as it can be modulated by changing the conductivity of presynaptic sodium or calcium channels4. The RMT is commonly used as an indicator of corticospinal tract (CST) excitability and as a basic dosing unit for TMS-based therapeutic interventions.

Interindividual variability in the RMT is well known5,6, reflecting the differences in CST excitability among individuals at baseline and the individual differences in the brain’s sensitivity to TMS. Owing to significant differences in brain anatomy among individuals, researchers first speculated that variations in brain structural characteristics might cause individual differences in the RMT. A stable positive correlation was found between the distance from the coil to the cerebral cortex and RMT7. List et al.8 found cortical thickness of the hand knob of the primary motor cortex was a significant positive predictor of RMT, independent of coil-to-cortex distance. Klöppel et al.9 reported a linear inverse relationship between the RMT and large bihemispheric clusters and fractional anisotropy in areas such as the primary motor area, premotor area, white matter under the posterior prefrontal cortex, internal capsular knee, cerebral infarction, and corpus callosum. Mirchandani et al.10 also reported an association between the anisotropy value and the RMT. Rosso et al.11 reported that the RMT is inversely correlated with the dominant hemisphere gray matter volume. In addition, studies have shown that RMT is also influenced by factors such as age12 and coil orientation13. RMT increases with age, and a smaller RMT is obtained when the coil current is placed perpendicular to the central sulcus.

In the resting state, brain functions and connections between brain regions have individual-specific “fingerprints” that capture a wide range of interindividual differences in social, cognitive, and behavioral tendencies and the brain response to external stimuli14. Therefore, the interindividual differences in RMT might be accounted for by individual-specific brain functional characteristics at baseline. Rosso et al.11 reported that the RMT was inversely correlated with the functional connectivity between the premotor area and M1. However, Engelhardt et al.15 replicated Rosso et al.’s research and obtained contradictory results. This discrepancy highlights the complexity of the relationship. The influence of brain functional characteristics on the interindividual variability in RMT remains understudied.

Neural oscillations play crucial roles in the realization of brain functions, information processing, and the physiological and pathological states of diseases16–18. Specifically, neural oscillations at different frequencies can affect the membrane potential of neurons, maintaining them in different excitatory states. Using the amplitude of the MEP as an indicator of CST excitability, numerous studies have demonstrated that the instantaneous power and phase of mu and beta rhythms in the sensorimotor cortex selectively influence CST excitability19–21. There are differences in the intensity of neural oscillations in various frequency bands and the collaborative level of neural oscillations between brain regions among individuals in the resting state22,23. These differences might explain the reasons for the interindividual variability in RMT from the perspective of neural oscillations. Cai et al.24 reported that resting-state oscillatory characteristics, including alpha-band power in the sensorimotor cortex and theta-band global network efficiency, were significantly correlated with RMT. It has been preliminarily suggested that the RMT is related not only to the neural activity of the motor area but also to the neural activity of other brain areas. However, very few studies have explored interindividual variability in the RMT from the perspective of neural oscillations. How neural oscillations in the resting state predict RMT and how RMT is related to coordinated oscillatory activities between the motor area and other brain regions remain understudied.

The RMT reflects CST excitability. The CST is crucial in the motor system, controlling the active movements of the extremities, especially the flexible movements of the hands. CST axons are derived from vertebral cells in the primary motor cortex and other brain areas associated with movement25. Diffusion tensor imaging revealed that the CST originates from the primary motor region (M1), primary somatosensory area (S1), supplementary motor area (SMA), and premotor region (PMA), contributing 37%, 32%, 25%, and 7% of CST axons in healthy individuals, respectively26,27, although, these proportions may vary somewhat across studies and tracing methodologies. Therefore, interindividual differences in the neural oscillations of these brain regions might determine interindividual differences in the RMT.

Our hypotheses are as follows: (1) Interindividual variability in the RMT is related to the neural oscillation characteristics of the motor system at the baseline level and (2) the RMT depends not only on the neural activity of the M1 but also on the oscillatory power and synchronization originating from the vicinity of the M1, such as the PMA, the SMA, and the S1. Recording with eyes open was chosen to avoid drowsiness and microsleep, reduce interference from occipital alpha rhythms, and ensure consistency with commonly used RMT measurement conditions. For these reasons, we used a resting-state electroencephalogram (EEG) with eyes open to collect neural oscillations at baseline. Calculating the power spectral density (PSD) of neural oscillations in different frequency bands within the motor system and the functional connections (FCs) between brain regions enabled a systematic exploration of the relationships between the RMT and endogenous neural oscillations at the baseline level. Our study presents a mechanism by which neural oscillations were associated with CST excitability and provides a basis for understanding the interactions between neural oscillations and TMS.

Multivariate regression

Based on the above results, delta-band PSD in LC, RC, LFC, RFC, as well as theta-band PLV between LC and RC, showed significant negative correlations with RMT. We therefore calculated the sum of delta-band PSD across these four regions (LC, RC, LFC, and RFC), referred to as summed delta-band PSD. A multiple linear regression model was constructed with summed delta-band PSD and interhemispheric theta-band PLV (LC–RC pair) as independent variables, and age and sex as covariates. The results are provided in Table 4. The overall regression model was statistically significant (F = 6.351, p < 0.001) with an adjusted R2 of 0.185, suggesting that the predictors together accounted for approximately 18.5% of the variance in RMT. Discussion

Our study explored the relationship between resting-state neural oscillations in the motor system and RMT. The results indicated that intrinsic neural oscillations at the baseline level were significantly associated with the RMT. More specifically, RMT showed significant weak-to-moderate negative correlations with delta-band PSD in bilateral central and frontal regions, as well as with theta-band PLV in bilateral central regions. Together, these two measures accounted for 18.5% of the variance in RMT. These findings suggest that intrinsic neural oscillations might underlie individual differences in brain responsiveness to TMS, and that enhancing such oscillations might be helpful for improving TMS efficacy.

RMT is related to resting-state neural oscillations in the low-frequency band

Our study found that higher resting-state delta-band PSD in bilateral central and frontal regions was associated with lower motor cortex RMT. This indicates higher baseline excitability of the CST. Thus, intrinsic delta-band characteristics of resting-state neural oscillations may modulate CST excitability. Inter-individual variability in delta-band oscillations might be an important endogenous source of significant RMT differences. Traditionally, delta-band oscillations have been linked to sleep, cortical inhibition, and pathological slow waves following brain injury. In such contexts, increased delta-band power is often interpreted as enhanced cortical inhibition28. This view seems contradictory to our finding that higher delta-band power accompanies increased cortical excitability. However, most traditional findings are based on studies of sleep or brain injury. Delta-band oscillations in the resting state may also have different functional significance. In the study about motor control, delta-band oscillation in the motor cortex has been identified as an important feature of skilled motor execution, contributing to the coordination and stabilization of motor programs29. In stroke rehabilitation, upregulated delta-band power in the motor cortex during the chronic phase was also thought to reflect attention engagement, cognitive control, and adaptive neuroplasticity, indicating more efficient synchronous integration within local neural networks30. Together, these findings suggest that the function of delta-band oscillations is highly context-dependent.

On the other hand, our study revealed that stronger theta-band functional connectivity in the bilateral central regions was associated with lower RMT. This suggests that individual differences in theta-band oscillations represent another endogenous source of corticospinal tract excitability variability. This result shares some similarities with the study conducted by Cai et al.24. While their research only analyzed the correlation between the global efficiency of the theta-band in the resting state and RMT, we used a more comprehensive approach. Recent evidence indicates that theta-band oscillations also play a critical role in the sensorimotor system. Cruikshank et al.31 found that theta-band oscillations in the human sensorimotor cortex are directly involved in sensorimotor integration and coordination. Popovych et al.32 reported significant delta-theta phase locking in the M1 region during motor learning tasks. Storti et al.33 further demonstrated that betweenness centrality of the motor cortex theta network increases significantly during motor execution. Supporting this view, a study of the post-effects induced by rTMS in the primary motor cortex demonstrated that rTMS could change the MEP amplitude and revealed the connection characteristics of the theta band. These findings imply that theta-band oscillations serve as an important rhythmic substrate for motor cortex activation and functional reorganization.

Although our findings do not yet allow us to elucidate the causal relationship between delta/theta oscillations and cortical excitability at the mechanistic level, we propose the following hypothesis: resting-state neural oscillations reflect an “integrative readiness state” of the sensorimotor system in the absence of explicit tasks. Enhanced resting-state delta-band power in the central-frontal regions and theta-band functional connectivity may indicate greater baseline synchrony and synaptic responsiveness of the motor cortex and prefrontal networks, manifesting as superior motor control and attentional levels34,35. Delta-band and theta-band oscillations are closely associated with attentional modulation. Increased power and connectivity in these frequency bands may reflect ongoing information integration and cross-regional coordination within the brain to support attentional focus and task execution. Previous studies have shown that CST excitability increases and RMT decreases during periods of focused attention, implying that RMT is modulated by the brain’s attentional networks36. We therefore speculate that individual differences in baseline attentional level may represent an important contributor to the substantial inter-individual variability in RMT. Therefore, the low-frequency neural oscillation characteristics identified in our study potentially elucidate the contributions of baseline sensorimotor integration and attention levels to individual differences in RMT. In the future, experiments with direct assessments of attention are needed for further in-depth and systematic research, thereby enhancing our understanding of the underlying neurophysiological mechanisms.

No correlation between the RMT and the resting-state neural oscillations of the alpha frequency band

Neural oscillation of the alpha frequency band was the main oscillation rhythm of the central region during the resting state with eyes opened. It has been reported that the PSD and/or instantaneous phase characteristics of the alpha frequency band in the central region are related to the MEP amplitude, which reflects CST excitability11,19. The higher the PSD is, the greater the MEP amplitude. The MEP amplitude was found to be greater when the TMS was performed at the trough of the alpha oscillation rhythm than when it was performed at the peak. These previous findings suggest that the neural oscillations of the alpha frequency band might be related to the RMT since the RMT is determined by the MEP amplitude. However, the results of our study do not support this conclusion. We found no correlation between the RMT and the neural oscillation of the alpha frequency band. We suspect that this is because we focused on the characteristics of neural oscillations over time in the resting state rather than on transient characteristics. Neural oscillations reflecting CST excitability across individuals might differ from that within individuals. Cai et al.24 reported an inverse correlation between the PSD of the alpha2 frequency band and RMT, which is inconsistent with our results. They studied EEG in the resting state with eyes closed, whereas we used the resting state with eyes opened. A large difference has been reported between the characteristics of the resting state with eyes closed and those of the resting state with eyes opened36,37, potentially explaining the inconsistent results. The response to TMS might depend on the brain state. A multicenter, large-sample study is needed to verify the reliability of our results.

Implications for future TMS applications

After controlling for age and sex, our study found that intrinsic neural oscillations accounted for 18.5% of the variance in RMT, indicating a weak-to-moderate modulatory effect on RMT. This effect size suggests that they may jointly determine individual differences in RMT along with other known influencing factors such as cortical thickness and cortex-to-scalp distance. Nevertheless, the results of partial correlation analysis and multiple linear regression analysis remained consistent in significance, demonstrating that resting-state EEG oscillatory characteristics have an independent contribution to RMT beyond age and sex. These findings might have important implications for future TMS application: by assessing an individual’s delta-band power and theta-band phase synchrony, it might be possible to predict their sensitivity to TMS, thereby identifying patients who are more suitable for treatment, and adjusting stimulation intensity or targeting to compensate for low responsiveness. Furthermore, enhancing such oscillations in the target brain regions through neurofeedback or other means before TMS stimulation may reduce individual RMT, thereby enabling stronger cortical excitatory effects with the same absolute TMS intensity, although this causal relationship remains to be experimentally verified.

Limitations

The RMT reflects the excitability of the CST, which originates from the M1, PMA, SMA, and S1 regions. Thus, we studied the relationship between RMT and intrinsic neural oscillations in these brain areas. However, several limitations remain. First, we investigated only the relationship between the RMT of dominant hemisphere and intrinsic neural oscillations. Further study is needed to determine whether the relationships between RMT and intrinsic neural oscillations in the nondominant hemisphere are similar. Second, we focused only on the relationship between resting EEG features and RMT without considering the structural features of the brain on MRI. Numerous studies have explored the relationships between brain structural features and the RMT. In particular, there was a significant correlation between the RMT and the distance between the scalp and the cortex. In the future, it is necessary to collect both MRI and EEG data to further investigate the combination of brain functional and structural indicators to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the causes of interindividual variability in the RMT. Third, our study employed an approximate electrode parcellation in sensor space, with an imbalance in the number of electrodes across regions and simplified representations, which may introduce noise and affect the sensitivity and reliability of regional comparisons. Finally, in our study, CSD transformation was applied before PLV analysis. Nevertheless, given the close spatial proximity of electrodes C1 and C2 corresponding to bilateral M1, the observed phase synchrony may still carry a risk of spurious connectivity. Future studies could adopt metrics more robust to volume conduction, such as the weighted phase lag index and the imaginary part of coherence, to further validate the present findings. Despite these limitations, by employing partial correlation analysis and multivariate regression, we were able to determine the effects of resting-state neural oscillations in the motor system on the RMT, and the results are reliable.

Conclusion

In conclusion, although neural oscillations account for only a weak-to-moderate proportion of RMT variance, our findings confirm that RMT is associated with intrinsic neural oscillations in a frequency- and region-specific manner. The limited effect size suggests that EEG features alone are insufficient to fully explain individual differences in RMT; structural factors (e.g., cortical thickness, scalp-to-cortex distance) should also be considered. Nonetheless, as a non-invasive and cost-effective tool, EEG might provide a useful supplement to existing methods for studying corticospinal excitability.

Materials and methods

Participants

A total of 100 right-handed participants (mean age 23.24 ± 1.80 years; 46 men) were included in this study. Handedness was assessed using the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory38. None of the participants took psychotropic drugs or had a history of central nervous system diseases or head trauma. Individuals with contraindications to TMS were excluded39. Participants were asked to avoid caffeinated beverages on the day before the experiment, were reminded to sleep by 10:30 PM, and reported having at least 8 h of sleep on the day of the experiment. Two participants were excluded because of excessive noise or drift in their EEG recording. An additional three participants were excluded because their RMT values exceeded the maximum stimulation output (MSO) of the TMS instrument. Consequently, EEG analysis was performed on the remaining 95 participants. All participants were informed of the purpose and content of the experiment, and they signed informed consent documentation before participating. The Ethics Committee of the Institute of Biomedical Engineering, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and Peking Union Medical College approved the study (L2024-D12-001).

RMT acquisition

TMS was generated through a figure-eight-shaped coil (70 mm standard coil, Magstim, Whitland, UK) connected to a Magnetic Rapid2 stimulator (The Magstim Company, Whitland, UK). For all TMS in our study, the stimulation was guided by a neuronavigation system (BrainSight, Rogue Research Inc., Montreal, Canada) to precisely define the neuroanatomical target from a T1-weighted MR image of one participant’s brain. In our study, individual TMS positioning was determined by the M1 hotspot eliciting maximal FDI MEPs, based on physiological responses rather than anatomical landmarks. The template MRI mainly served to maintain stable coil positioning across repeated stimulations. Therefore, using one participant’s MRI as the group template appears methodologically reasonable and acceptable. The coil was held tangentially to the skull with the handle pointing backward at an angle of 45° to the sagittal plane such that an anterior‒posterior current flow, followed by a posterior‒anterior current flow (AP‒PA), was induced in the underlying cortex. The coil was held over the hand area of the left motor cortex. The scalp position resulting in the most consistent and largest MEP in the first dorsal interosseous muscle (i.e., the motor “hotspot”) was determined and used throughout the session. During RMT measurement, participants were instructed to maintain complete muscle relaxation. Muscle relaxation was continuously monitored via visual inspection of the EMG trace, with resting EMG amplitude kept below 10 μV throughout the procedure. RMT was defined as the lowest stimulus intensity that evoked MEPs with a peak-to-peak amplitude greater than 50 µV in at least 5 out of 10 consecutive TMS pulses, while the target muscle was completely relaxed. The mean RMT of the 95 included participants was 63.31% ± 10.65% MSO.

EEG acquisition and preprocessing

EEG data were collected in a quiet electrophysiological laboratory before RMT assessment. The participants sat in a comfortable chair, and their EEG in the resting state with their eyes opened was recorded for 5 min. The participants were required to remain awake throughout the recording. EEG data were recorded via a 64-channel BrainAmp EEG system (Brain Products GmbH, Munich, Germany). A 64-channel EEG cap was positioned on each participant’s head. The reference electrode was at the FCz site, and the ground electrode was at the FPz site. The impedance for all electrodes was maintained below 10 kΩ. The EEG data were sampled at a frequency of 1,000 Hz and subsequently processed offline.

All channels were re-referenced to the common average. The data were segmented into consecutive 2-s epochs. The epochs containing large muscular and nonstereotyped artifacts were then carefully pruned from the signals. Continuous recordings were bandpass filtered between 0.5 and 100 Hz and notched to remove power-line interference (50 Hz) using the built-in FIR filter function (pop_eegfiltnew) in EEGLAB. The data were then processed via independent component analysis. We used the ADJUST toolbox in MATLAB combined with careful inspection of the temporal, spectral, and spatial characteristics of each independent component to identify and remove artifacts, including eye blinks, eye movements, and heart rhythms40,41. On average, 4.19 ± 1.65 ICA components were rejected per participant. Subsequently, PSD and FC analyses were conducted on these 2-s data segments. After artifact rejection, the mean number of clean epochs retained across all participants was 142.91 ± 11.44.

Partitioning of electrodes

To study the possible reasons for interindividual variability in RMT, we selected EEG electrodes related to motor function and divided them into seven groups: (1) left frontal-central (LFC): F1, FC1, FC3, FC5; (2) right frontal-central (RFC): F2, FC2, FC4, FC6; (3) midline frontal (MF): Fz; (4) left central (LC): C1; (5) right central (RC): C2; (6) left centroparietal (LCP): C3, CP1; (7) right centroparietal (RCP): C4, CP2, as illustrated in Fig. 4. This grouping was based on previous anatomical localization studies using the international 10–10 system42, which suggested that these electrode clusters may approximately correspond to distinct motor-related cortical regions. However, we acknowledge that such parcellation in sensor space is approximate and has inherent limitations.

Power spectral density and functional connectivity

The PSD was computed using the fast Fourier transform algorithm for each 2-s epoch and then averaged across epochs. We calculated the regional PSD for the seven regions across the 0.5–100 Hz range and extracted six frequency bands: delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–14 Hz), beta (14–30 Hz), gamma1 (30–45 Hz), and gamma2 (55–100 Hz).

Functional connectivity was evaluated using PLV. To mitigate the influence of volume conduction, current source density (CSD) transformation was applied to the data before PLV analysis using a MATLAB-based CSD toolbox. Bandpass filters were used to extract the delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma1, and gamma2 frequency bands. The PLV was applied to estimate the FC for seven regions, as shown in Fig. 1. The PLV can quantify neural signal interactions between brain regions, but it neglects the effect of the instantaneous amplitude. Statistical analysis

EEG data were processed offline using MATLAB (version R2023a) and the EEGLAB toolbox (version 2020.0). All statistical analyses were performed in SPSS (version 20).

First, partial correlation analyses were performed to examine the associations between total PSD (i.e., the summed PSD across the seven brain regions; Fig. 1) and RMT, with gender and age as covariates. This approach helped verify that the observed associations were independent of demographic effects. Given the non-normal distribution of total PSD, bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) was applied to obtain 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and two-tailed p-values. Since different frequency bands reflect distinct and independent neural oscillations, multiple-comparison correction was not applied across bands in PSD analyses to avoid over-correction and minimize false-negative outcomes. Second, partial correlation analyses were performed to examine the associations between regional PSD in each of the seven brain regions and RMT, using the same statistical approach. To account for multiple comparisons across regions, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied within each frequency band. Third, similar partial correlation analyses were performed to examine the associations between interregional PLV and RMT, with FDR correction applied within each frequency band.

Finally, EEG features that showed significant partial correlations with RMT were selected as candidate predictors. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to examine the predictive effects of these EEG measures on RMT. RMT was set as the dependent variable, the significant EEG features as independent variables, and age and sex as control variables. All variables were entered into the model simultaneously. The unstandardized regression coefficient (B), standardized coefficient (β), t-value, and p-value for each variable were extracted, along with the overall F-statistic and adjusted R2, to assess model fit and the independent predictive effect of each predictor. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at α = 0.05.
