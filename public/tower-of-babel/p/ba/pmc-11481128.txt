Effect of calcium channel blockers on influenza incidence: a population-based retrospective cohort study using administrative claims data in Japan
Authors: Takanori Imai, Hideki Hashimoto, Naoki Kanda, Yusuke Sasabuchi, Hiroki Matsui, Hideo Yasunaga, Shuji Hatakeyama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesLaboratory experiments have indicated that calcium channel blockers (CCBs) inhibit the entry and replication of influenza A virus in cells. However, no clinical studies have assessed the incidence of influenza among patients receiving CCBs. This study aimed to investigate the association between CCB use and the incidence of influenza among patients with hypertension using administrative claims data in Japan.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingAdministrative health insurance claims database of Kumamoto Prefecture, Japan.Participants360 515 patients with hypertension (10th edition of the International Classification of Diseases code I10) who were prescribed CCBs and 171 142 patients who were prescribed angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs)/angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) between 2012 and 2016.Primary outcomeWe compared the incidence of influenza between the CCB and ACEI/ARB groups using high-dimensional propensity-score (HD-PS) matching.ResultsA total of 166 814 HD-PS matched pairs were obtained. Before HD-PS matching, the CCB group had a significantly lower influenza incidence than the ACEI/ARB group in the overall analysis (2.4% vs 2.5%, p=0.007; risk ratio 0.95, 95% CI 0.92 to 0.99). However, no significant difference was observed between the two groups after HD-PS matching (2.4% vs 2.5%, p=0.067; risk ratio 0.96, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.00); only in 2012 did the CCB group have a significantly lower likelihood of influenza than the ACEI/ARB group.ConclusionsNo significant difference was observed in the influenza incidence between the CCB and ACEI/ARB groups. A direct comparative study between background-matched patients with and without CCBs is warranted to confirm the effect of CCBs on reducing the incidence of influenza.Keywords: infectious diseases, respiratory infections, hypertension

Introduction

Influenza is an acute respiratory infection that usually becomes epidemic yearly worldwide and is associated with increased mortality, particularly among older people.1 Although global influenza activity was extremely low after the emergence of COVID-19, a significant resurgence occurred worldwide in 2023.2 Influenza prevention remains an important public health issue.

Infection with influenza A virus is initiated by the binding of hemagglutinin, a viral glycoprotein, to sialic acid on respiratory epithelial cells. Calcium ion influx may have a potential role in mediating the entry and replication of influenza A virus in the cells.3 An in vitro study showed that influenza A virus entry is inhibited by calcium channel blockers (CCBs) in Madin-Darby canine kidney cells and that prophylactic administration of diltiazem to mice infected by influenza A virus PR8 (H1N1) strain resulted in a significant and dose-dependent reduction in the viral load of nasal lavage fluid.4

Since influenza vaccines do not offer complete protection, exploring alternative preventive measures is crucial. The potential association between CCB and reduced incidence of influenza could significantly influence the selection of antihypertensive agents, particularly for patients at high risk of severe influenza-related complications. However, no studies have assessed whether CCBs reduce the incidence of influenza in clinical settings. This study aimed to investigate the association between CCB use and the incidence of influenza among patients with hypertension using administrative claims data in Japan.

Patients and methods

Study design and data source

In Japan, all residents are enrolled in a universal health insurance system provided by either the social (employee) health insurance, National Health Insurance or Late Elders’ Health Insurance. We conducted a retrospective cohort study using an administrative health insurance claims database of Kumamoto Prefecture, which has a population of approximately 1.8 million and is located in southwestern Japan. This database includes information on enrollees residing in Kumamoto Prefecture and their dependents in the two health insurance systems: the National Health Insurance and Late Elders’ Health Insurance. The former covers individuals aged <75 years who are not covered by the employee health insurance system (eg, self-employed persons or pensioners), while the latter covers individuals aged ≥75 years. The number of enrollees and their dependents included in this database was estimated to be 797 613, 785 725, 772 958, 757 658 and 742 942 in 2012, 2013, 2014, 2015 and 2016, respectively. This represents approximately 42% of the prefecture’s residents. While Kumamoto Prefecture has a relatively high proportion of older residents compared with the national average, the overall population structure is largely consistent with that of Japan.

The database consists of three dimensions of outpatient and inpatient data: diagnosis, medication and medical procedures. It provides information about patient demographics (unique patient ID in the database, year and month of birth, and sex), diagnostic codes according to the 10th edition of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) with date of diagnosis, medical procedures (tests and treatment), technical and service fees, medication code according to Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification, date of procedures and prescriptions and prescription days. All data in this database were anonymised using unique identification numbers, with no linkage table maintained, ensuring that individual patients could not be identified. Additionally, access to the data was strictly controlled, and the data were available only within the secure environment of the Data Science Center at the Jichi Medical University. More detailed information on the database used in this study is described elsewhere.5

Study cohort

We defined the exposure period as 1 July to 30 September in each fiscal year between April 2012 and March 2016. We included all patients with a diagnostic code of hypertension (I10) who had prescriptions (for at least 12 weeks) of CCBs or angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs)/angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) in the exposure period. Individuals who took two or more antihypertensive medications (CCBs, ACEIs, ARBs, beta-blockers, thiazide diuretics and alpha-blockers) during the exposure period were excluded. CCBs were defined by ATC classifications C08 and C10BX03. ACEI/ARBs were defined by ATC classifications C09A, C09B, C09C and C09D.

Outcome definitions

The outcome was the incidence of influenza during the observation period each year. We defined 1 October to 31 March as the observation period for each fiscal year between April 2012 and March 2016. Patients could contribute to multiple exposure/observation periods, potentially leading to the inclusion of the same patient in different years. The accuracy of the diagnosis was not validated owing to the use of an administrative claims database. Therefore, to enhance diagnostic validity, influenza was defined as meeting all three of the following criteria: ICD-10 codes for influenza (J101, J110, J111 and J118), diagnostic tests and anti-influenza viral agents. Influenza diagnostic tests were defined as rapid antigen tests (Japanese electronic claims code, 160169450), antibody tests (Japanese electronic claims codes, 160042210 and 160042310) or nucleic acid tests (Japanese electronic claims code, 160198010) performed within 7 days from the day of influenza diagnosis. Anti-influenza viral agents were defined by ATC code J05AH (oseltamivir, zanamivir, laninamivir and peramivir) or N04BB (amantadine) prescribed within 7 days from the day of diagnosis.

Analysis

Information about confounding factors, such as influenza vaccine status, smoking history, pregnancy and obesity, was not available in the database. Therefore, we performed high-dimensional propensity-score (HD-PS) matching to adjust for confounding factors, including unmeasured confounders in baseline characteristics between the groups. HD-PS matching has the possibility of adjusting for unmeasured confounders between the two groups.6,8 We estimated HD-PSs with the following five steps according to Schneeweiss et al6: (1) identify different data dimensions, (2) select 200 of the most prevalent codes in each dimension as candidate covariates during the look-back period (180 days before exposure period), (3) assess the frequencies (code occurred ≥1 times, ≥median number of times and ≥75th percentile number of times) in each candidate covariate for all included patients, (4) calculate the multiplicative bias term (BiasM) and (5) select the top 500 covariates in descending order of the BiasM as potential covariates for PS-modelling. BiasM is given by the following equation: BiasM = [PC1 (RRCD − 1) + 1] / [PC0 (RRCD − 1) + 1] (PC1, proportion of covariates among exposed persons; PC0, proportion of covariates among unexposed persons; RRCD, unadjusted risk ratio for covariates and disease outcome). In this manner, we identified 600 candidate covariates (200 codes in three dimensions: diagnosis, medication and procedure), and then assessed the three frequencies of the 600 covariates (600×3=1800 covariates). After the BiasM was calculated for the 1800 candidate covariates, 500 potential covariates were selected in descending order of BiasM values.

We used PS models that adjusted for the following variables: age, sex, pulmonary disease (J41–J45, J47, J60–J70, J82 and J84), cardiovascular disease except isolated hypertension (I05–I09, I11, I13, I20–I28, I34–I37, I42, I44–I45 and I47–I50), chronic liver disease (B18–B19 and K70–K76), renal disease (I12, N00–N08, N12, N14, N18–N19 and N25), cerebrovascular disease (I60–I69), metabolic disease (E10–E14, E24–E27 and E70–E90), haematological disease (D55–D77 and D80–D89), neurological disease, including disorders of the brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerve and muscle (F00–F05, F71–F73, G10–G13, G20–G26, G30–G32, G35–G37, G40–G47, G50–G64, G70–G72, G80–G83, G90–G99, T060–T061, T093 and T913), malignancy (C00–C97), transplantations (Z94) and the 500 potential covariates detected by HD-PS matching. We conducted one-to-one and nearest-neighbour matching with 0.20 times the SD of the logit of the PS as the calliper width without replacement. The standardised mean difference (SMD) was calculated to balance the distribution of variables between the CCB and ACEI/ARB groups. An SMD >0.1 was defined as out of balance. We used the χ2 test to compare the annual incidence of influenza between the two groups.

As part of the sensitivity analyses, we estimated ORs for influenza incidence using inverse probability-weighted logistic regression models based on the propensity score (PS) constructed in the main analysis. Additionally, we conducted analyses using conventional PS matching, incorporating age, sex and comorbidities as covariates. The threshold for significance was p≤0.05. All analyses were performed using R V.3.5.3 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Patient and public involvement

The research question and outcome measures were developed without direct involvement from patients. Additionally, patients did not participate in the planning or implementing of the study design. There is no plan to disseminate the study findings to the patient community.

Results

We included 68 369 and 33 017 patients in 2012, 70 318 and 34 333 patients in 2013, 72 167 and 34 314 patients in 2014, 73 456 and 35 183 patients in 2015 and 76 205 and 34 295 patients in 2016 in the CCB and ACEI/ARB groups, respectively. Overall, 360 515 and 1 71 142 patients were included in the CCB and ACEI/ARB groups, respectively. Using HD-PS matching, we generated 32 060 pairs in 2012, 33 296 pairs in 2013, 33 325 pairs in 2014, 34 489 pairs in 2015 and 33 644 pairs in 2016. The inverse probability-weighted logistic regression model showed no significant difference in the odds of influenza incidence between the CCB and ACEI/ARB groups, except for the year 2012 (online supplemental table 1). These results are consistent with those of the main analysis using HD-PS matching. Furthermore, the analysis using conventional PS matching demonstrated that the CCB group exhibited a significantly lower risk of influenza compared with the ACEI/ARB group during both the overall study period (2012–2016) and specifically in 2012 (overall (2.4% vs 2.5%, p=0.05) and in 2012 (1.4% vs 1.6%, p=0.01)) (online supplemental table 2).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first observational study to examine the relationship between CCBs and the incidence of influenza. Although it varied by year, we found no significant difference in the overall incidence of influenza between the CCB and ACEI/ARB groups. The CCB group had a significantly lower influenza incidence than the ACEI/ARB group only in 2012. Potential factors affecting the differences between the years are as follows. First, the prevalent types and subtypes of influenza virus varied from year to year. In Japan, influenza A (H3N2) virus was predominant in 2012, 2014 and 2016, and influenza A (H1N1)pdm09 virus was predominant in 2013 and 2015. Concurrent epidemics of influenza B were observed in 2013, 2015 and 2016. In previous in vitro studies, the effects of CCBs were tested against only a subset of the H1N1 and H3N2 influenza A virus strains,3 4 9 and the efficacy of these agents against other strains remains unknown. The antiviral effects of CCBs have been shown to vary among different agents. Verapamil is less effective in inhibiting viral replication than amlodipine and diltiazem.4 Nisoldipine demonstrated efficacy against multiple influenza A virus strains, including A/PR8/34 (H1N1), FM-1/1/47 (H1N1), WSN/33 (H1N1), Aichi/2/68 (H3N2) and PR8/34 harbouring NA-H274Y mutation (oseltamivir-resistant strain), with the strongest inhibitory effect observed against the A/WSN/33 strain.9 In our study, because of the limitations in sample size, we were unable to perform stratified analyses based on the type of CCBs; therefore, further research from this perspective is necessary. The association between CCBs/ACEIs/ARBs and influenza B virus has been scientifically unproven to date.

Second, vaccine efficacy also varies yearly based on the antigenic concordance between the vaccine and epidemic strains, although the influenza vaccination rate and magnitude of influenza epidemic were similar between fiscal years during the study period according to the data from the Japanese National Institute of Infectious Diseases. A meta-analysis showed that the effectiveness of influenza vaccine was 55.4% against A (H1N1), 26.8% against A (H3N2), 47.2% against B (Yamagata) and 40.6% against B (Victoria). The effectiveness against A (H3N2) was significantly lower than that against A (H1N1) and B (Yamagata).10 These variations in vaccine effectiveness could potentially influence the year-to-year differences observed in this study, although our database does not include vaccine-related data. Third, random error may be the primary cause.

The National Health Insurance and Late Elders’ Health Insurance cover a relatively higher proportion of older individuals. However, since our study adjusted for the age and sex distribution of the entire Japanese population, this factor is unlikely to have a major impact on our findings. As the study relies on data from a single prefecture, the unique influenza outbreak patterns, vaccination policies or treatment practices in Kumamoto Prefecture could influence the results, if they significantly differ from other prefectures. However, no such differences are known.

In Japan, individuals who exhibit influenza-like symptoms often seek medical care. An online surveillance study conducted in Japan showed that approximately 80% of individuals with influenza-like symptoms visited healthcare facilities.11 Furthermore, an analysis of Japanese claims data showed that >90% of patients with influenza underwent influenza diagnostic tests and received antiviral medication.12 Therefore, in Japan, most patients with influenza are likely to seek medical care and undergo diagnostic testing and antiviral medication. Consequently, the influenza case definition used in this study is considered an effective approach for accurately identifying the majority of cases in Japan.

A retrospective cohort study analysing electronic healthcare records in the UK showed that the use of ARBs or ACEIs was associated with a lower incidence of influenza, depending on the duration of use. The comparison group was defined as those without ACEIs/ARBs therapy, and the administration of other antihypertensive drugs was not clearly addressed. The definition of influenza was based solely on ICD-10 codes.13 In our study, CCBs were compared with ACEIs/ARBs, and although the lack of statistically significant difference observed except in 2012, the CCB group tended to have a lower incidence of influenza than did the ACEI/ARB group. Therefore, the absence of a significant difference could be attributed to the study design, which compared CCBs with ACEIs/ARBs. Our findings suggest that a direct comparative study is warranted to confirm the effect of CCBs on reducing the incidence of influenza, ideally between background-matched cohorts who have and have not received CCBs or ACEIs/ARBs.

In the claims database used in this study, there were too few patients receiving single antihypertensive agents other than CCBs, ACEIs or ARBs to adjust for background factors and compare them. Furthermore, a previous study analysing the Japanese claims database showed that the accuracy of the diagnostic codes for hypertension was lower in patients not receiving antihypertensive medications than in those receiving them.14 Therefore, we did not include patients who had the diagnostic code of hypertension without prescribing antihypertensive agents as controls in this study. Further research using larger claims databases is required.

The C-statistic for predicting the prescription of CCBs was 0.622 (95% CI: 0.621 to 0.624), indicating that the calculated PS demonstrated acceptable discriminatory ability for treatment allocation. The finding from the sensitivity analyses using conventional PS matching suggests that, in addition to the significant difference observed in 2012 with HD-PS matching in the main analysis, a significant difference was also detected across the entire study period. PS matching is based on observed confounders, and the influence of unmeasured confounders cannot be fully eliminated when they are present. While HD-PS is designed to mitigate more effectively the effect of unmeasured confounders, as with conventional PS matching, some residual influence from unmeasured confounders may still persist. Conversely, in HD-PS analysis, there remains the possibility that bias is introduced due to over-adjustment of covariates, although such bias has generally been reported to be minimal.

This study had some limitations. First, vaccine information is not included in the claims database because vaccines are not covered by health insurance in Japan. The lack of information regarding vaccination for influenza as well as obesity may have influenced the results. Second, unmeasured confounding factors may not have been fully adjusted even after HD-PS matching. Third, we did not consider the duration of prescription (periods of use of the agents) or changing/discontinuing the prescription during the observation period. As a previous study showed that patients with long-term (>2.5 years) ACEI/ARB treatment had a significantly lower influenza incidence,13 duration of prescriptions may be associated with the incidence of influenza. Finally, we excluded approximately 40% of patients who were taking multiple antihypertensive agents—primarily a combination of CCBs and ACEIs/ARBs (84%)—to avoid including patients who were simultaneously taking the study drugs. While this exclusion may have introduced selection bias, its effect on the study was likely minimal, as other drug combinations were less common, and matching based on underlying conditions likely mitigated potential biases.

Conclusion

There was no significant difference in the incidence of influenza between 2012 and 2016 between patients receiving CCBs and ACEIs/ARBs after HD-PS matching. A direct comparison study between background-matched patients with and without CCBs is warranted to exclude the probable influence of ACEIs/ARBs on reducing the incidence of influenza.
