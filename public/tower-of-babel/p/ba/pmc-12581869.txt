The Risk of Rheumatic Disorders Among Patients With Rhinosinusitis: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Arshbir Aulakh, Pradumn Choudhary, Gurwinder Sidhu, Deanna Gigliotti, Jane Jun, Andrew Thamboo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEmerging evidence suggests a possible link between rhinosinusitis and systemic rheumatic diseases; however, no meta‐analysis has comprehensively examined this association to date. We aimed to investigate if patients with rhinosinusitis have a predisposition to unmasking rheumatic diseases compared to individuals without rhinosinusitis.MethodsA comprehensive search in MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science was conducted until February 2025 for studies characterizing rheumatic disease incidence, prevalence, and risk in cohorts of rhinosinusitis patients. The search was limited to records authored in English and involved combining both rhinitis and sinusitis Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms. Relative risk and prevalence data were pooled using random‐effects models.ResultsNine studies with 86,081 rhinosinusitis patients were included. Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) was significantly associated with increased risk of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) (odds ratio [OR]: 1.70; 95% CI: 1.44–2.00; p < 0.00001), systemic lupus erythematosus (OR: 1.61; 95% CI: 1.25–2.08; p = 0.0002), and ankylosing spondylitis (OR: 1.48; 95% CI: 1.26–1.72; p < 0.00001). Acute rhinosinusitis (ARS) showed weaker associations, notably with seronegative RA. Rheumatic disease prevalence in rhinosinusitis patients was highest for RA (10%, 95% CI: 8.2–13).ConclusionRhinosinusitis, particularly CRS, is associated with several rheumatic diseases. Mechanisms of mucosal immune dysregulation associated with rhinosinusitis may contribute to, or act in parallel with, the pathogenesis of systemic autoimmunity. Management of CRS (surgical or immunomodulatory) may lead to the sentinel presentation and subsequent identification of previously undiagnosed systemic autoimmune conditions. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for early autoimmune symptoms in rhinosinusitis patients.

Introduction

Rhinosinusitis is a heterogenous inflammatory disorder involving the nasal and paranasal mucosa that broadly encompasses acute rhinosinusitis (ARS) and chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS). ARS is typically a self‐limited infectious process, whereas CRS persists for more than 12 weeks. These conditions are responsible for significant patient morbidity, increased health care costs, and reduced societal productivity [1, 2, 3]. CRS affects an estimated 2%–16% of people worldwide and is further subdivided into CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) and CRS without nasal polyps (CRSsNP) based on endoscopic phenotype [4, 5]. These clinical phenotypes correspond to distinct immunologic endotypes: Type 2 and non‐Type 2 inflammation [6, 7]. Type 2 inflammation is characterized by an eosinophilic immune response driven by T‐helper 2 (Th2) cytokines, such as IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13, ultimately leading to an IgE antibody response that is predominantly observed in CRSwNP. Meanwhile, non‐Type 2 inflammation, predominantly observed in CRSsNP, is neutrophil‐driven and involves variable contribution of T‐helper 1 (Th1) cell‐mediated Type 1 inflammation and T‐helper 17 (Th17) cell‐mediated Type 3 inflammation.

Emerging evidence suggests a possible link in pathophysiological pathways between rhinosinusitis and rheumatic diseases [8]. In two separate case‐control studies, Kronzer et al. [9, 10, 11] reported that preceding ARS/CRS was associated with an increased risk of several rheumatic diseases, including antiphospholipid syndrome, Sjögren's syndrome (Sjs), and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [9, 11]. A cross‐sectional study by Chung et al. found a diagnosis of CRS was associated with increased odds of RA and ankylosing spondylitis (AS) but not systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [12]. However, other studies, such as Sandberg et al., reported no significant association between sinusitis and RA [13]. Lee et al. found no risk of developing SLE or AS in CRS patients [14]. These conflicting findings and a paucity of consensus in existing literature highlight the need for a comprehensive review of current evidence to characterize a possible relationship between rhinosinusitis and rheumatic disease.

To our knowledge, no prior meta‐analysis has comprehensively evaluated the risk of unmasking underlying rheumatic disease among patients with rhinosinusitis. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to evaluate whether rhinosinusitis is associated with a higher likelihood of identifying underlying rheumatic disease. Further analysis assessed the prevalence of rheumatic disorders in patients diagnosed with rhinosinusitis and evaluated whether the risk of rheumatic disorders differs between ARS and CRS.

Methods

Search Strategy and Study Selection

The present systematic review and meta‐analysis were conducted in accordance with the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta‐analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [15]. The protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42025639059).

A comprehensive search was conducted across four electronic databases: MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. The search was limited to studies published in English from database inception to February 2025. A medical librarian from the University of British Columbia assisted, and a detailed search strategy was developed using Boolean operators (“AND,” “OR”), Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), and keywords related to rhinitis and sinusitis. Reference lists of relevant articles were also screened. The complete search strategy for each database is provided in Appendix A.

Eligibility Criteria

Two reviewers (A.A. and G.S.) screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts of each potential study independently, according to our inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies were considered appropriate for inclusion if they adhered to the following PICOS criteria. Participants (P): Adults or children diagnosed with rhinosinusitis, either CRS or ARS, based on clinical, diagnostic, or imaging criteria. Intervention/Exposure (I): Patients with rhinosinusitis who are diagnosed with new‐onset rheumatic disease, including, but not limited to, RA, SLE, and AS. Comparator/Control (C): Individuals without rhinosinusitis drawn from the general population or matched controls, based on demographics or clinical characteristics. Outcome (O): Risk of unmasking underlying rheumatic disorders and prevalence of rheumatic disorders in patients diagnosed with rhinosinusitis. Study design (S): Observational studies, such as cohort, case‐control, and cross‐sectional. Randomized clinical trials with data relevant to the research objective were also considered eligible for inclusion.

Studies that did not adhere to the above criteria and those designed as case reports, editorials, conference abstracts, systematic reviews, and meta‐analyses were excluded. We also excluded studies reporting the risk of developing rhinosinusitis among patients with rheumatic disorders.

Data Extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized form, capturing study characteristics (authors, year, design, setting), participant demographics (age, sex, smoking status, race), comorbidities, diagnostic criteria for rhinosinusitis, and outcome measures (effect estimates, prevalence rates). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the relative risk (odds ratio [OR], hazard ratio [HR], or risk ratio [RR]) for the association between rhinosinusitis and incident rheumatic disorders. The secondary outcome was the pooled prevalence of rheumatic disorders among patients diagnosed with rhinosinusitis.

Quality Assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Newcastle‐Ottawa scale (NOS) for cohort studies and case‐control studies and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for cross‐sectional studies. Using the NOS, studies were assigned scores based on three domains: selection, comparability, and outcome. Thereafter, each study was graded as having poor, fair, or high methodological quality according to the total NOS scores (NOS scores between 0–3, 4–6, and 7–9, respectively).

Data Synthesis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan version 5.4) and STATA 18 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA). Data regarding the prevalence of rheumatic disorders were pooled in a one‐arm meta‐analysis, and the overall effect size was computed using the untransformed (raw) proportion. On the other hand, the association between rhinosinusitis and the risk of rheumatic disorders was estimated using the generic inverse variance, with the overall effect being measured as OR or HR. The OR and HR data were retrieved only from the multivariable logistic regression models, which were adjusted for several confounding factors. A random‐effects model was used to pool all the effect estimates, as it accounts for heterogeneity and provides conservative estimates. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I 2 statistics, of which index values greater than 50% indicated significant heterogeneity [16, 17].

In addition, we categorized rhinosinusitis as ARS and CRS to enable subgroup analyses and elucidate potential variations in the prevalence and incidence of specific rheumatic disorders (e.g., RA, SLE, and AS). Other subgroup analyses included smoking status (smokers vs. non‐smokers) and RA serostatus (seropositive RA vs. seronegative RA). When significant heterogeneity (I 2 > 50%) was noted, sensitivity analysis, which involved removing studies one by one from the pooled results, was conducted to ascertain the sources of heterogeneity.

Results

Search Results

The online search for potential studies yielded 5156 records. One additional study was identified via manual screening, reaching a total of 5157 records screened. A total of 94 of these records were excluded after being deemed duplicates. The titles and abstracts of the remaining records were thoroughly scrutinized, of which 5051 records were identified to be irrelevant to our study objective or designed as case reports, case series, and review articles and therefore were excluded. Finally, 12 full‐text articles were subjected to the eligibility criteria, of which 9 met the inclusion criteria. The other three studies were excluded, as they reported the risk of rhinosinusitis in patients with rheumatic disorders (Figure 1).

PRISMA chart for study selection.

Characteristics of Included Studies

A total of 9 studies involving 86,081 patients with rhinosinusitis were included, but only 8 were used the meta‐analysis [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 18, 19, 20]. The study by Kronzer et al. [10] was not used in the meta‐analysis to avoid double‐study bias because it was a sub‐study of the main study by Kronzer et al. [9]. Five of the nine included studies were case‐control studies, three were cohort studies, and one was a cross‐sectional study. These studies were published between 2014 and 2024 and were conducted in various countries (United States [n = 3], Korea [n = 2], China [n = 2], Taiwan [n = 1], and Sweden [n = 1]). Of the 86,081 patients, 78,637 had CRS, 6675 had ARS, and 769 were unspecified (Table 1).

Rhinosinusitis and Prevalence of Rheumatic Disorders

To evaluate the prevalence rate of rheumatic disorders in patients with rhinosinusitis, a one‐arm subgroup analysis was conducted. The analysis revealed that RA was the most common rheumatoid disorder, with a pooled prevalence rate of 10% (95% CI: 8.2–13). Additionally, we found that the prevalence of SLE, AS, Sjs, and antiphospholipid syndrome was 0.16%, 0.88%, 0.38%, and 0.1%, respectively (Figure 2).

Prevalence rates of rheumatic disorders in patients with rhinosinusitis.

ARS and Risk of Rheumatic Disorders

A subgroup analysis according to specific rheumatic disorders revealed that ARS was associated with a significant increase in RA (OR: 1.33; 95% CI: 1.01–1.75; p = 0.04) (Figure 3). However, there was no association between ARS and AS (OR: 2.12; 95% CI: 0.50–9.00; p = 0.31) or Sjs (OR: 2.43; 95% CI: 1.0–5.90; p = 0.05) (Figure 3). Furthermore, subgroup analysis according to serostatus showed that ARS significantly increased the risk of seronegative RA (OR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.12–2.47; p = 0.01) but not seropositive RA (OR: 1.33; 95% CI: 0.73–2.40; p = 0.35) (Figure 4).

Association between acute rhinosinusitis and rheumatic disorders.

Association between acute rhinosinusitis and rheumatoid arthritis, stratified according to serostatus.

CRS and Risk of Rheumatic Disorders

Using OR as the point estimate, we found that CRS was significantly associated with an increased risk of RA (OR: 1.70; 95% CI: 1.44–2.00; p < 0.00001), SLE (OR: 1.61; 95% CI: 1.25–2.08; p = 0.0002), AS (OR: 1.48; 95% CI: 1.26–1.72; p < 0.00001), and antiphospholipid syndrome (OR: 6.00; 95% CI: 1.50–24.00; p = 0.01) but not Sjs (OR: 2.46; 95% CI: 0.90–6.70; p = 0.08) (Figure 5). Similarly, when using HR as the point estimate, we observed that CRS was significantly associated with increased risk of RA (HR: 1.40; 95% CI: 1.28–1.54; p < 0.00001) and Sjs (HR: 1.70; 95% CI: 1.26–2.29; p = 0.0005) but not SLE (HR: 1.15; 95% CI: 0.61; p = 0.66) nor AS (HR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.78–1.40; p = 0.77) (Figure 6).

Association between chronic rhinosinusitis and rheumatic disorders using OR as the point estimate. SLE, systemic lupus erythematosus.

Association between chronic rhinosinusitis and rheumatic disorders using HR as the point estimate. SLE, systemic lupus erythematosus.

We found no significant association between CRS and the incidence of seronegative RA (OR: 2.03; 95% CI: 0.83–4.95; p = 0.12) or seropositive RA (OR: 1.39; 95% CI: 0.95–2.04; p = 0.09) after conducting a subgroup analysis (Figure 7). In contrast, our meta‐analysis revealed that CRS was significantly associated with RA risk in non‐smokers (OR: 1.67; 95% CI: 1.22–2.29; p = 0.001) but not in smokers (OR: 1.10; 95% CI: 0.80–1.51; p = 0.56) (Figure 8).

Phenotype of CRS and Risk of Rheumatic Diseases

Due to inconsistent reporting, the association between the phenotypes of CRS (CRSsNP and CRSwNP) and the risk of rheumatic diseases was narratively synthesized. According to Lee et al., both CRSsNP and CRSwNP patients had a significantly higher risk of developing RA compared to non‐CRS patients. However, the risk was slightly higher in patients with CRSsNP than in patients with CRSwNP (adjusted HR: 1.42 and 1.37, respectively) [20]. Lee et al. [14] also found that patients with CRSsNP had a significantly higher risk of developing Sjogren's syndrome (adjusted HR: 1.78), whereas CRSwNP patients showed no association with Sjogren's syndrome (adjusted HR: 0.37). Furthermore, the authors found no significant association between the two phenotypes and the risk of SLE or AS. Another case‐control study that adjusted for gender, age, monthly income, urbanization, and comorbidities found that patients with CRSsNP had a significant risk of being diagnosed with SLE (adjusted OR 1.83, 95% CI 1.36–2.47) and RA (adjusted OR 1.51, 95% CI 1.22–1.88); whereas those with CRSwNP had a higher likelihood of being diagnosed with AS (adjusted OR 1.46, 95% CI 1.16–1.83) and RA (adjusted OR 2.03, 95% CI 1.40–2.94) [18].

Sensitivity Analyses

Significant heterogeneity was noted across all subgroup analyses assessing the prevalence of different rheumatic disorders (i.e., RA, SLE, and AS). Therefore, sensitivity analyses were conducted to investigate the sources of heterogeneity in these subgroups. By excluding the results of Kronzer et al. [10], Lee et al. [20], Chung et al. [12], Sandberg et al. [13], and Shih et al. [18] from RA analyses, the heterogeneity reduced from 99.68% to 32.07%, implying that these studies accounted for the substantial heterogeneity. These studies varied in design, study population, sample size, adjustment of confounders, and diagnostic criteria for rhinosinusitis, which may explain the heterogeneity. Similarly, after excluding the results of Kronzer et al. [11] and Lee et al. [20] from SLE analyses, we noticed that the heterogeneity reduced from 91.54% to 0.02%, indicating that these studies were significant sources of heterogeneity. However, the sensitivity analysis in the AS analyses did not seem to affect heterogeneity, meaning that the heterogeneity in these analyses was likely caused by other factors that could not be captured by sensitivity analysis.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis found that rhinosinusitis is significantly associated with increased risk of several rheumatic diseases, most notably RA, SLE, and AS. The association was strongest for CRS, particularly in relation to RA, and remained robust across multiple effect measures (Figures 4 and 5). Specifically, RA had the highest pooled prevalence among patients with rhinosinusitis (Figure 2). Subgroup analyses showed stronger associations with seronegative RA than seropositive disease for ARS but not CRS (Figures 3 and 6). Risk was especially elevated among non‐smokers with CRS, with no significant association seen in smokers (Figure 7). This pattern mirrors previous findings suggesting that seronegative RA is more common among non‐smokers [21], highlighting possible immunologic or environmental modifiers. These results support the hypothesis that mucosal inflammation may contribute to systemic immune dysregulation in predisposed individuals.

Although prior studies have suggested links between sinus disease and autoimmunity, this is the first meta‐analysis to quantify these associations across multiple rheumatic conditions. Our findings support the notion that shared inflammatory pathways between the airways and systemic immune responses may underlie this relationship between sinus disease and autoimmunity. The observed association with RA and Sjs suggests a possible role for mucosal immune activation in triggering systemic autoimmunity. In contrast to earlier studies [13, 14] that found no such link, our analysis incorporates more recent and diverse data, emphasizing the importance of rhinosinusitis phenotype, serostatus, and population differences. Notably, our synthesis suggests CRSsNP may carry a stronger autoimmune risk than CRSwNP, likely reflecting distinct inflammatory endotypes (Th1/neutrophilic vs. Th2/eosinophilic) [22].

It is well established that CRSwNP is characterized by Th2 cytokines, high eosinophilic infiltration, edematous stromal tissue, frequent epithelial damage, and a thickened basement membrane, whereas CRSsNP demonstrates high levels of Th1 cytokines and less eosinophilic infiltration [23, 24]. The underlying Th1/Th17 pathways and pro‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor alpha [TNF‐α], IL‐17) driving rheumatic diseases such as RA and AS may explain why CRSsNP was associated with stronger autoimmune risk than CRSwNP.

The pathophysiological link between rhinosinusitis and rheumatic diseases is not fully understood, but mounting evidence supports a role for chronic mucosal immune dysregulation—particularly in CRS, and more so in CRSsNP [9, 11, 19]. Inflammation in the sinonasal tract may promote systemic autoimmunity by disrupting mucosal tolerance and facilitating antigen exposure [25]. Several studies suggest that RA may originate at mucosal sites such as the lung and airway epithelium [26], gingiva [27], and gut [28]. Pro‐inflammatory cytokines involved in RA pathogenesis—TNF‐α, IL‐1, and IL‐6—are also active in CRS, contributing to its inflammatory cascade [19, 22, 29]. CRS may act as a mucosal trigger for systemic autoimmunity by compromising epithelial barriers and allowing antigenic stimulation that leads to autoantibody formation [9, 19]. This concept is reinforced by Mendelian randomization studies linking chronic airway inflammation with autoimmune disease [19] and by elevated cytokine levels and aberrant mucosal immune responses observed in CRS patients [9].

ARS also demonstrated a measurable, though somewhat weaker, association with rheumatic disease risk. Studies suggest that timing and frequency of acute sinus infections may be the key. For example, upper respiratory tract infections occurring 5–10 years prior to RA diagnosis have been linked to increased future RA risk, particularly in patients who later develop seronegative RA [10, 30]. This supports a model in which ARS episodes trigger transient immune dysregulation and barrier disruption, contributing to the early initiation of systemic autoimmunity. The immune system's response to recurrent infections may foster chronic inflammation and tolerance breakdown.

Our findings showed a stronger association between CRS and RA among non‐smokers, with no significant association observed in smokers (Figure 7). This aligns with prior evidence suggesting that seronegative RA, which lacks the anti‐citrullinated protein antibody (ACPA)‐smoking link seen in seropositive RA, is more common in non‐smokers [21]. Although smoking is a known risk factor for seropositive RA, other environmental exposures such as air pollution, occupational dust, and periodontal disease have been implicated in seronegative RA, particularly among non‐smokers [31, 32]. Future studies should explore how modifiable factors like airborne irritants, oral health, and diet interact with sinonasal inflammation to influence autoimmune risk.

Our review identified a strong link between CRS and increased risk of rheumatic diseases, particularly RA, likely reflecting shared immune dysregulation. Biologic therapies for CRS targeting IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13 effectively reduce Type 2 inflammation but may shift immune responses toward Th1 or Th17 pathways—both implicated in RA and AS—potentially increasing rheumatic risk [33]. This immune rebalancing may help explain the elevated risk of autoimmune disease among CRS patients and highlights the importance of cautious use of biologics in individuals with autoimmune predisposition.

In the context of RA, the difference between seropositive and seronegative is clinically crucial. Seropositive RA is generally marked by the presence of rheumatoid factor and/or ACPAs and is considered more aggressive, with increased risk for joint erosions and other complications. Seronegative RA, on the other hand, lacks the autoantibodies present in seropositive RA and might have a more heterogeneous clinical presentation [34]. In the current study, we found a slight but non‐significant (p = 0.09) association between CRS and seropositive RA, which suggests a potential underlying immunopathologic link. In addition, we observed a significant association between ARS and seronegative RA (p = 0.01), which might be attributed to the overlapping inflammatory pathways independent of autoantibody production.

Despite the absence of treatment data in the included studies, the potential impact of immunosuppressive medications, particularly biologics, merits attention as a contributing factor to the observed associations. Several clinical studies have reported an increased prevalence of sinusitis in RA patients treated with TNF‐α inhibitors. For instance, a study of RA patients treated with infliximab reported a sinusitis incidence of 17% compared to 6% in the placebo arm [35]. This increased incidence of sinusitis after treatment with TNF‐α inhibitors is not clearly understood but has been linked to impaired local immune defense and altered mucosal inflammatory responses [36, 37]. Moreover, research indicates that autoimmune vasculitides, including granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) and eosinophilic GPA (EGPA), may lead to sinonasal disorders [38, 39]. Our broad, medical librarian‐assisted search strategy and expanded screening efforts suggest this reflects a gap in the current literature rather than a methodological limitation. Therefore, subsequent research should aim to encompass a wider array of autoimmune disorders to clarify the potential correlation with rhinosinusitis.

Our study findings have shown that ARS is associated with increased risk of RA, whereas CRS increases the risk of unmasking multiple rheumatic diseases (RA, SLE, and AS). Therefore, rhinologists should remain vigilant when treating patients with CRS/ARS presenting with early signs of systemic autoimmune disease, such as joint pain and swelling, fatigue, recurring fever, and skin problems. In addition, the current study revealed that ARS increases the risk of seronegative RA but not seropositive RA. Therefore, clinicians should holistically monitor and investigate patients with ARS, even if their blood work is normal. Contrary to ARS, CRS did not show significant association with either seropositive or seronegative RA. A high interstudy heterogeneity was observed, with data from one of the pooled studies suggesting that CRS might be associated with significant risk of seronegative RA but not seropositive RA [9]. As such, further research including prospective data collection, robust phenotyping of CRS and RA subtypes, and biomarker analysis to clarify mechanistic pathways is needed to understand whether CRS also increases the risk of seronegative RA.

We categorized the outcomes according to the different types of rhinosinusitis (CRS and ARS) and carried out subgroup analysis based on different rheumatic diseases. This categorization offers insights that may not have been identified in broader analyses and improves clinical applicability. By understanding the association between ARS or CRS with different rheumatic diseases (e.g., RA, SLE, and AS), clinicians might contemplate a more comprehensive differential diagnosis in rhinosinusitis patients exhibiting visceral symptoms, such as arthralgia, tiredness, or rashes, thus facilitating early referral to rheumatology.

Nonetheless, the present meta‐analysis has some limitations. First, only eight studies were included in the meta‐analysis. This small number of studies might have limited the statistical power of our analyses. Second, significant heterogeneity was noted in some of the statistical analyses. This heterogeneity might be attributed to variations in study design, sample sizes, and diagnosis criteria for rhinosinusitis. However, we used a random‐effects model in all pooled results to minimize the impact of heterogeneity on the overall effect size and provide conservative estimates. Third, we intentionally omitted records published in languages other than English, which may have introduced selection bias. Fourth, we did not conduct a meta‐analysis on the relationship between sinusitis and the severity of rheumatic diseases because these data were not available in the evaluated studies. Finally, due to inconsistent reporting, the association between CRS phenotype and rheumatic diseases was made via narrative synthesis. This limited us from making convincing conclusions regarding these associations. Therefore, further research is needed to support these findings. Finally, although immunosuppressive therapies such as disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) and biologics may influence the course of rhinosinusitis, our review focused on the development of rheumatic diseases following a diagnosis of rhinosinusitis [40]. None of the included studies reported treatment regimens, and this remains an important limitation. Future research should explore the reverse temporality and account for immunosuppressive therapy as a potential confounder.

Conclusion

Our systematic review and meta‐analysis findings suggest that rhinosinusitis, particularly CRS, may serve as an early clinical marker and possible mechanistic contributor to systemic rheumatic disease. Chronic mucosal inflammation, dysbiosis, and immune dysregulation in CRS offer a plausible pathway linking sinonasal disease with systemic autoimmunity. Although the associations with ARS were weaker, they suggest that acute inflammation primarily driven by infectious agents may influence early immunologic priming. A stronger association in non‐smokers further supports distinct immunologic pathways underlying seronegative RA. Clinicians should monitor for autoimmune manifestations in patients with CRS, and further studies are needed to clarify underlying mechanisms and guide preventive strategies.
