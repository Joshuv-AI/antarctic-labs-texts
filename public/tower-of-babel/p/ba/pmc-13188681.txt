Reprogramming the gut–liver immune axis: probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles as precision nanotherapeutics in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Moslem Javidan, Shahdokht Abbasiniya, Hadi Esmaeili GouvarchinghalehGhaleh, Ruhollah Dorostkar, Reza Golmohammadi, Majid MirzaeiNodooshan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide and exhibits limited responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), largely due to profound tumor-associated immunosuppression and the complexity of gut–liver immune crosstalk. Probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs) have recently emerged as biologically active mediators capable of delivering immunomodulatory cargos and influencing host immune pathways. This mini-narrative review critically evaluates the evolving role of PEVs in liver disease and their emerging relevance to HCC.ObjectiveThis review examines the molecular composition, immunomodulatory mechanisms, and translational potential of PEVs in the context of HCC, with an emphasis on delineating direct evidence from pre-malignant and supportive disease models.MethodsWe synthesized evidence from recent preclinical and translational studies (2020–2026) addressing PEV biogenesis, cargo–target interactions, immune modulation within hepatic microenvironments, and emerging delivery and manufacturing strategies.ResultsAccumulating preclinical evidence demonstrates that PEVs can modulate innate and adaptive immune pathways most robustly in inflammatory and pre-malignant liver disease models by promoting dendritic cell maturation, enhancing cytotoxic T-cell responses, and attenuating immunosuppressive myeloid and regulatory T-cell programs. Advances in vesicle purification, nanoformulation, and targeting strategies have improved PEV stability and biodistribution, supporting their evaluation as complementary modalities alongside existing therapies. However, direct validation of these effects in established HCC models remains limited, warranting cautious interpretation.ConclusionPEVs represent a versatile and biocompatible platform with emerging relevance to immune modulation in liver disease and HCC. While current evidence supports their immunoregulatory potential primarily in pre-malignant settings, successful clinical translation will require rigorous liver-relevant validation, standardized manufacturing and dosing frameworks, and carefully designed early-phase clinical studies.Future perspectivesKey priorities include elucidating in vivo biodistribution, refining cargo–target specificity, establishing potency assays, and integrating PEV-based strategies into rational combination regimens to define their therapeutic boundaries in HCC.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), including over 80% of primary liver malignancies, continues to be a significant contributor to cancer-related death globally, with a 5-year survival rate around 20% despite progress in surgical, locoregional, and systemic treatments [1]. Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy, most commonly anti-PD-1/PD-L1 or anti-CTLA-4 antibodies, has demonstrated modest efficacy in HCC, with objective response rates rarely exceeding 15–20%, largely due to the profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) and high intratumoral heterogeneity [2, 3]. Overcoming these obstacles requires novel immunomodulatory strategies capable of reshaping immune signaling within the liver microenvironment. The gut–liver axis, linked through the portal venous system, plays a central role in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) pathogenesis, whereby dysbiosis-driven translocation of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and lipoteichoic acid (LTA), activates pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) and Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), on Kupffer cells, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, and hepatocytes. This triggers NF-κB and MAPK signaling cascades, fueling hepatic inflammation, fibrosis, and ultimately hepatocarcinogenesis [4, 5]. Probiotics such as Bifidobacterium longum, Lactobacillus casei, and Lactobacillus rhamnosus have shown efficacy in preclinical diethylnitrosamine (DEN)-induced HCC models, restoring gut barrier integrity, reshaping microbial communities, enhancing natural killer (NK) cell infiltration, and reducing tumor burden [6]. However, the clinical application of live probiotics is constrained by safety concerns in immunocompromised hosts, strain-dependent variability, and the challenge of achieving reproducible therapeutic effects [7]. Probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs), nanosized (30–150 nm) bilayered membrane particles released during bacterial growth, represent a promising cell-free alternative that may mitigate some safety and reproducibility concerns associated with live probiotics [8]. However, the majority of mechanistic and functional data currently derive from inflammatory, metabolic, or fibrotic liver disease models rather than from established hepatocellular carcinoma models, necessitating careful distinction between direct and inferential evidence.

PEVs carry proteins, small RNAs, lipids, and metabolites that can modulate immune pathways locally in the gut and systemically in distant organs, including the liver [9, 10]. Early studies suggest that PEVs can modulate macrophage polarization, attenuate regulatory T-cell programs, and enhance cytotoxic T-cell responses, predominantly demonstrated in inflammatory and pre-malignant liver disease contexts[10, 11]. Notably, Lactobacillus johnsonii-derived EVs have been reported to attenuate tumor-associated inflammation and modulate macrophage phenotypes in orthotopic HCC models (Fig. 1) [10].

Nevertheless, critical knowledge gaps remain, including limited understanding of liver‑specific biodistribution and pharmacokinetics of PEVs in vivo; insufficient mechanistic mapping of cargo–target interactions within hepatic immune subsets; lack of standardized PEV isolation and dosing strategies for reproducible outcomes; and scarcity of robust preclinical HCC models and clinical trial data.

This mini-narrative review systematically examines the molecular payloads, immunomodulatory mechanisms, and transport capacity of PEVs in liver disease and HCC, with particular emphasis on delineating evidentiary boundaries and translational relevance.

Probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles: characteristics and cargo

PEVs are nanoscale, bilayered structures secreted by Gram-positive and Gram-negative probiotic species, facilitating interkingdom communication through the transfer of diverse molecular cargos [12, 13]. In the context of HCC, these vesicles offer a cell-free modality for delivering immunomodulatory and metabolic signals directly to the hepatic tumor microenvironment.

Composition of EVs: proteins, RNAs, and metabolites

The proteomic content of PEVs varies with strain identity, culture conditions, and isolation method. Lactobacillus salivarius SNK-6-derived vesicles contain > 320 proteins, including anti-inflammatory enzymes (e.g., PrtP) and metabolic regulators (e.g., elongation factor Tu) with potential to modulate pro-tumorigenic inflammation in HCC [13]. Similarly, E. coli Nissle 1917-derived vesicles display size-dependent protein heterogeneity, influencing macrophage cytokine responses [14]. Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG-derived extracellular vesicles (LDEVs) significantly reduce the growth of hepatocellular carcinoma cells by triggering death by altering Bcl-2 family proteins [15]. Collectively, it is highlighted the therapeutic potential of harnessing probiotic EV proteins to attenuate chronic inflammatory circuits and restore anti-tumor immunity in the liver, supporting novel therapeutic strategies against HCC. Although less extensively studied than proteins, PEVs also carry small RNAs (sRNAs) and microRNA-like fragments capable of modulating host gene expression. For example, PEVs generated from Lactobacillus reuteri DSM 17,938 are rich in sRNAs that respond to stress [16]. However, PEVs derived from Lactobacillus plantarum have sRNAs that control host genes related to immunity, which may have an impact on signaling pathways linked to HCC [17]. Metabolomic analyses have identified bioactive molecules in PEVs such as lactic acid, bile salt hydrolases, and antioxidants like glutathione, which contribute to host metabolic homeostasis. Lactiplantibacillus plantarum BGAN8-derived PEVs are enriched in glycolytic enzymes and peptide transporters, reflecting strain-specific metabolic functionality [18]. The dual function of oxidative stress in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is essential to the development of the tumor as well as the susceptibility to treatment. Excess ROS causes metabolic rewiring, inflammation, and DNA damage; however, HCC cells combat this by upregulating antioxidant systems, including the Nrf2-glutathione axis, which allows them to survive oxidative stress. The microbiome-derived extracellular vesicle cargo with redox-active proteins can rebalance the redox state, reduce pro-tumorigenic oxidative injury, and simultaneously sensitize tumor cells to apoptosis and immune attack. Strategies that mitigate pathological oxidative stress while tipping the balance toward immunogenic ROS signaling represent a promising avenue to restore anti-tumor immunity, improve response to immunotherapies, and limit therapeutic resistance in HCC [19].

Strain-specific cargo differences

The composition of PEVs is highly strain-dependent. Bifidobacterium longum-derived vesicles are enriched in peptidoglycan hydrolases and carbohydrate-active enzymes, whereas Lactobacillus rhamnosus-derived vesicles contain elevated levels of immunomodulatory proteins and stress-response chaperones [20, 21]. In HCC models, such proteins may enhance cytotoxic T-cell responses, thereby supporting therapeutic potential [15]. Bacterial phenotype also influences PEV cargo. For instance, L. plantarum biofilm-derived vesicles are enriched in quorum-sensing and intercellular communication proteins compared to vesicles from planktonic cultures [22]. These differences may modulate PEV–host cell interactions in the hepatic immune niche, potentially altering HCC progression [15].

Biochemical and biophysical profiling

High-resolution characterisation of PEVs has been made possible by advanced analytical methods such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM), LC-MS/MS, and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Larger vesicles include more membrane-associated proteins and enzymes, while smaller vesicles, which are usually spherical and range in size from 50 to 200 nm, are concentrated in nucleic acids [23]. Detailed biochemical profiling has revealed the complexity of PEV protein cargo. Notably, the selective uptake of PEVs by hepatocytes in HCC cell lines underscores their potential to modulate the tumor microenvironment directly [13, 15].

Nevertheless, critical knowledge gaps remain, including limited mapping of how specific PEV cargo components influence oncogenic versus immunomodulatory pathways in HCC; incomplete understanding of biogenesis differences between Gram-positive and Gram-negative probiotic EVs and their impact on cargo loading; and lack of standardized comparative datasets linking strain identity, cargo profile, and functional outcomes in hepatic contexts.

Bridging these gaps will be essential for designing strain-specific PEV formulations with optimal therapeutic potential against HCC.

Mechanisms of action and signaling pathways in immune modulation and anti-tumor activity

PEVs have emerged as multifunctional immunotherapeutic agents capable of modulating key cellular and molecular components of the tumor microenvironment in HCC [28]. Their mechanisms of action encompass a range of key processes, including engagement of pattern recognition receptors, inhibition of tumor-promoting signaling cascades, reprogramming of immunosuppressive cells, induction of immunogenic cell death (ICD), enhancement of antigen presentation, and T-cell activation. Collectively, these approaches work together to restore antitumor immunity and prevent cancer progression [29].

Pattern recognition and TLR signaling

PEVs from Lactiplantibacillus plantarum are recognized by TLR2 heterodimers (TLR1/2 or TLR2/6), leading to MyD88-dependent NF-κB activation and downstream cytokine induction [24]. In innate immune cell responses, these vesicles elevate IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IFN-γ, and IL-12, creating an environment conducive to proinflammatory activation and promoting Th1 bias, as indirectly inferred from IFN-γ/IL-12 roles. Identification of an EV-associated lipopeptide (Lp19180) as a TLR2 ligand further underscores the centrality of TLR2 signaling in mediating these effects. In HCC, such TLR2-driven innate rewiring may enhance antigen presentation and facilitate effector T-cell infiltration, helping counteract the immunosuppressive TME [24].

Inhibition of TGF-β1/Smad and NF-κB Pathways

PEVs can disrupt oncogenic signaling relevant to HCC progression. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) from Bifidobacterium longum demonstrate considerable protective effects against HCC by diminishing fibrosis, preventing apoptosis, and modifying oxidative stress. The main mechanism of this antitumor activity is attributed to the suppression of the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway. This process is associated with reduced collagen deposition, restoration of the balance of programmed cell death [30]. Similarly, L. plantarum-derived EVs inhibited NF-κB nuclear translocation in intestinal epithelial cells, reducing IL-8 and TNF-α secretion, cytokines implicated in chronic hepatic inflammation and hepatocarcinogenesis [24]. These effects may help reprogram the hepatic TME toward an anti-tumor state [3].

Modulation of macrophages and tregs

PEVs can reprogram immunosuppressive cell populations. EVs from L. plantarum and L. rhamnosus promoted M1-like macrophage polarization (↑iNOS, ↑IL-12; ↓Arg1, ↓IL-10) [31, 24]. B. adolescentis-derived EVs reduced Foxp3 and IL-10 expression in regulatory T cells (Tregs) in colitis models, suggesting potential in reversing TME immunosuppression in HCC. In hepatic tumors, such shifts could enhance antigen presentation and CTL activity [32].

Induction of apoptosis and immunogenic cell death

Extracellular vesicles derived from Lactobacillus reuteri exhibit significant anticancer activities by activating intrinsic apoptotic pathways; this phenomenon has been well documented by increasing the expression of Bax, caspase-3/9, as well as PARP cleavage. Evidence from in vitro and in vivo studies also confirms that these vesicles are able to induce apoptosis, inhibit angiogenesis and metastasis, and ultimately accelerate tumor regression. The clinical significance becomes more prominent when EVs are loaded with indocyanine green and used in combination with near-infrared photothermal therapy, as under these conditions, not only is cytotoxicity enhanced, but also immunogenic cell death markers such as calreticulin deposition on the cell surface and HMGB1 release are induced. Subsequently, antitumor immune responses are enhanced. Overall, L. reuteri-derived EVs (REVs), as an emerging probiotic-derived nanoplatform with the ability to simultaneously induce apoptosis and immunogenic cell death, provide valuable prospects for the development of multi-targeted cancer therapies [33]. This dual apoptosis ICD effect could synergize with immunotherapies in HCC.

Enhancement of antigen presentation and T cell activation

PEVs can enhance antigen presentation by increasing the expression of MHC-II, CD86, and CD80 molecules on the surface of dendritic cells, thereby priming CD4+ and CD8+ T cells for an effective immune response. In tumor models, probiotic EVs have been reported to increase infiltration and effector function of IFN-γ–producing CD8⁺ T cells and to reduce immunosuppressive regulatory T cells, supporting a mechanistic basis for synergy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in combination anticancer strategies [12, 34]. Such immune ‘heating’ effects may be particularly relevant for immunologically ‘cold’ HCC phenotypes.

Beyond initial priming, adaptive immune reprogramming in HCC critically depends on the differentiation trajectory of tumor‑infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells, ranging from effector to exhausted and memory states, which shapes durable responses to checkpoint blockade. Chronic antigen exposure and immunosuppressive cues in the HCC microenvironment promote T-cell exhaustion programs characterized by sustained inhibitory receptor expression and transcriptional/epigenetic remodeling, limiting cytotoxicity and contributing to ICI resistance (2, 35]. Accordingly, future PEV studies should quantify whether vesicle‑driven antigen presentation translates into (i) expansion of stem‑like/progenitor exhausted CD8⁺ T cells (TCF1/TCF7⁺), (ii) reinvigoration and clonal expansion under PD-1 blockade, and (iii) establishment of long-lived memory (e.g., TCM/TEM or liver‑resident TRM) that supports tumor control and rechallenge protection. Notably, gram‑negative bacterial outer membrane vesicles have been shown to activate cancer antigen‑specific stem‑like CD8⁺ T cells and synergize with anti-PD-1 therapy in preclinical cancer models, providing a mechanistic blueprint to be tested with probiotic EVs in HCC [36].

Remaining knowledge gaps include limited resolution of how PEV exposure reshapes CD8⁺ T‑cell exhaustion and memory trajectories within hepatic tumors; absence of longitudinal analyses to assess durability and rechallenge protection; insufficient in vivo biodistribution and uptake mapping in orthotopic/autocthonous HCC models; and a lack of mechanistic links between defined PEV cargo and specific antigen‑presentation checkpoints or TCR clonal expansion in the liver.

Current evidence in HCC and other liver pathologies

To contextualize translation, current evidence can be framed across the HCC disease continuum into: (i) preventive/prophylactic use in pre‑malignant liver disease (e.g., steatohepatitis, cholestatic injury, fibrosis/cirrhosis) to dampen inflammatory–fibrotic drivers of hepatocarcinogenesis; (ii) adjunctive use alongside standard HCC therapies (immune checkpoint inhibitors, tyrosine‑kinase inhibitors, and/or locoregional therapies) to enhance antitumor immunity and overcome resistance; and (iii) therapeutic use as a direct antitumor vesicle intervention (native or engineered) in established HCC models. Overall, the strongest data currently support preventive and adjunctive immunomodulation, whereas bona fide anti‑HCC therapeutic evidence remains emerging (4, 11, 28, 37, 38].

Prophylactic and anti-inflammatory roles in chronic liver diseases

Probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs) have been predominantly investigated in inflammatory and barrier-disruption settings, where they modulate NF-κB–associated cytokine programs and macrophage activation in epithelial and immune cells [39, 40]. In liver-focused studies, probiotic EVs have been reported to attenuate hepatic injury and fibrosis and to regulate gut–liver axis–related signals, including bile acid metabolism, suggesting potential preventive roles during pre-malignant stages of chronic liver disease [41, 42]. However, whether these anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects translate into a reduced incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma in at-risk populations remains unresolved and requires direct evaluation in carcinogenesis models and longitudinal studies [4].

Immunoregulatory and anti-fibrotic properties

Liver fibrosis and cirrhosis represent major risk factors for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and are closely associated with chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation. In experimental models, probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs) have been shown to attenuate fibrotic pathology while concurrently shaping hepatic immune responses. For instance, Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG–derived EVs improved cholestatic liver injury in mice through modulation of the gut microbiota and bile acid metabolism, accompanied by reduced fibrotic remodeling [42]. Similarly, EVs derived from Lactobacillus plantarum have been reported to ameliorate liver injury and fibrosis in murine models, partly through immunoregulatory effects within the hepatic microenvironment [41]. Collectively, these findings support a potential preventive positioning of PEVs in pre-malignant fibrotic liver disease; however, direct mechanistic links to downstream HCC risk, as well as optimal dosing and treatment windows in carcinogenesis settings, remain to be established [43].

Emerging evidence in HCC models

Although still limited, direct hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)–focused studies are beginning to emerge. In preclinical settings, Bifidobacterium longum–derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) have been reported to mitigate disease progression in a diethylnitrosamine (DEN) – driven mouse model, primarily through suppression of pro-fibrotic signaling and modulation of the tumor-associated microenvironment [28]. In parallel, EVs derived from Lactobacillus rhamnosus strains have shown inhibitory effects on HCC cell proliferation and survival in vitro, supporting a direct tumor-modulatory potential [15]. Beyond naturally derived probiotic EVs, engineered bacterial extracellular vesicles and outer membrane vesicles have been explored as immunomodulatory nanoplatforms capable of reshaping post-ablation immunosuppressive niches and activating antitumor immunity in experimental HCC models [37]. Collectively, these early studies support feasibility while underscoring the need for standardized, strain-defined, and liver-relevant models to rigorously quantify antitumor efficacy.

Mini-clinical perspective

Although clinical trials specifically evaluating probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs) in HCC patients are not yet available, emerging human data provide indirect translational support. Recent cohorts of patients with unresectable HCC receiving anti-PD-1–based combination therapies have demonstrated distinct gut microbiome profiles between responders and non-responders, with specific bacterial taxa correlating with improved overall and progression-free survival [44, 45]. Broader multi-cancer studies further support gut microbiota features as modulators and biomarkers of immune checkpoint inhibitor responsiveness, reinforcing the concept that microbiota-derived effectors, including vesicular components may influence therapeutic outcomes [46]. Importantly, recent methodological advances have enabled the reliable isolation and proteomic characterization of bacterial extracellular vesicles from human plasma, establishing the technical feasibility of studying circulating bacterial vesicles in clinical settings [47]. Together, these observations provide a rationale for carefully designed phase 0/I studies to evaluate the safety, biodistribution, and immunological impact of PEVs in HCC patients.

Translational outlook and limitations

While probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs) show promise in mitigating fibrosis, inflammation, and early tumorigenesis, their clinical translation to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) faces several challenges. These include pronounced tumor heterogeneity and the complex architecture of the HCC tumor microenvironment, variability in EV yield and cargo composition across bacterial strains and manufacturing conditions, and the current scarcity of robust orthotopic or autochthonous in vivo HCC models as well as prospective clinical trials. Moreover, a substantial fraction of the mechanistic evidence summarized in this review is derived from non-HCC disease models, such as colitis, lung injury, or colorectal cancer, or from engineered and non-probiotic bacterial vesicles. As a result, these systems may not fully recapitulate the tolerogenic immune wiring of the liver or the exhaustion-prone T-cell states that are characteristic of HCC [2, 48]. Collectively, these limitations underscore the need for direct validation of PEV-mediated mechanisms and adaptive immune endpoints in liver-relevant experimental settings.

Despite these challenges, preclinical evidence from models of liver injury, fibrosis, and early disease states supports continued investigation of probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles as potential preventive or adjunctive strategies in HCC-relevant contexts. Progress toward translation will require standardized vesicle isolation and dosing protocols, systematic cargo target mapping, and rigorous validation in liver-relevant and orthotopic HCC models. Future studies should prioritize reproducible manufacturing frameworks and longitudinal immunomonitoring approaches to more clearly define the therapeutic boundaries of PEV-based interventions.

Therapeutic perspectives and future research

Purification and dosage standardization of probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs)

Clinical translation of PEVs hinges on achieving high purity and reproducible dosing [54]. Size-based isolation methods alone [e.g., ultracentrifugation, size exclusion chromatography (SEC), or tangential flow filtration (TFF)] often co-isolate proteinaceous or bacterial contaminants that confound bioactivity readouts. In a direct comparison, TFF in conjunction with high-performance anion exchange chromatography (HPAEC) markedly enhanced BV purity relative to size-based approaches alone. It decreased flagellin contamination and produced more uniform anti-inflammatory bioactivity among strains [55]. In vitro findings show that the producer strain, culture duration (24 h versus 48 h), and isolation method play a decisive role in the yield and composition of extracellular vesicle (EV) cargo. For example, the use of ultracentrifugation without changing particle size produced about 1.5 times higher yields than conventional isolation methods. Marker profiling (e.g., OmpA for Gram-negative; Lipoteichoic acid (LTA) for Gram-positive) and absence of canonical eukaryotic EV markers (e.g., TSG-101) are essential for QC. Current best practices converge on multistep, orthogonal workflows UC, UF, SEC/CL-6B, TFF, and density gradients. These should be supplemented with rigorous particle sizing nanoparticle-tracking analysis (NTA), morphology checks (TEM/cry-TEM), and dye-only controls [56]. Dose reporting is inconsistent, with measurements varying from particle counts to protein mass to CFU-normalized yield. It is advisable to employ dual-metric reporting (particles with protein) and functional potency assays (such as macrophage cytokine modulation) to ensure cross-study comparability and readiness for scale-up in HCC applications [55]. Standardizing purification pipelines and dose metrics will be pivotal to ensuring reproducible PEV quality for preclinical-to-clinical transition in HCC.

Nanoformulation and targeted delivery of PEVs

Translating PEVs into clinical immunotherapy requires nanoformulations that maintain vesicle integrity, enhance stability, and achieve liver-targeted delivery. Advances in nanotechnology have enabled functionalized BVs with improved pharmacokinetics and biodistribution [48]. Strategies include ligand conjugation (e.g., galactose derivatives for ASGPR-mediated hepatocyte targeting) and PEGylation to prolong circulation and reduce clearance [57]. In proof-of-concept work, tumor-targeting peptide-engineered BVs enhanced uptake by tumor-associated macrophages and hepatocytes in orthotopic HCC models. This increased the therapeutic index and reduced off-target activation [58]. Ligand-functionalized PEVs from Lactobacillus species preferentially targeted hepatic stellate cells in fibrotic livers [12]. Hybrid systems, liposome–EV composites or polymer-coated EVs, can co-deliver synergistic agents (e.g., checkpoint inhibitors, siRNAs). This enables spatiotemporal control over TME remodeling [59, 60]. Engineering targeted, stable PEV nanoformulations opens new avenues for microenvironment-specific modulation and combination regimens in HCC.

Combination/adjunctive therapy with ICIs or virotherapy

HCC often resists ICIs (e.g., anti-PD-1/PD-L1, anti-CTLA-4) due to an immunosuppressive TME and low immune infiltration. PEVs can act as adjuncts by altering immune composition, modulating checkpoint ligand expression, and boosting CTL function [61]. Gram-negative OMVs have enhanced PD-1 blockade efficacy in murine cancer models by promoting DC maturation and CD8⁺infiltration [36]. Besides immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs) can exhibit considerable synergy with virotherapy. Oncolytic viruses (OVs) function by provoking immunogenic cell death (ICD) and liberating tumor antigens; however, their effectiveness is frequently constrained by innate antiviral responses and inadequate activation of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Experimental evidence has shown that pretreatment with bacterial EVs enhances the efficacy of OVs by activating type I interferon and increasing the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines. This process not only leads to the conversion of “cold” tumors to “hot” ones but also improves viral oncolysis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) models [62]. Mechanistically, PEVs downregulate Tregs and MDSCs while upregulating MHC-I/II, aligning with immune prerequisites for ICI and OV success [62, 63]. Challenges remain, including sequencing (simultaneous vs. sequential), dose/formulation standardization, and safety in immunocompromised patients [64]. Rationally designed trials combining PEVs with ICIs or OVs could overcome resistance and broaden therapeutic windows for advanced HCC.

Manufacturability, quality control, and regulatory considerations

From a translational perspective, probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs) reside at the interface of microbiome-derived postbiotics and nanomedicine. Key manufacturability challenges include strain selection and genetic stability, control of culture conditions that influence vesicle yield and cargo composition, and the development of scalable purification workflows such as tangential flow filtration and chromatography capable of removing soluble proteins, nucleic acids, and other contaminants while preserving vesicle integrity [53, 65]. In parallel, defining robust release criteria, including size distribution, particle-to-protein ratios, sterility, and biologically relevant potency assays, as well as demonstrating batch-to-batch comparability, remains a major barrier to clinical translation. Although emerging purification strategies, including anion-exchange–based workflows, provide proof-of-principle examples of scalable bacterial vesicle isolation, cross-study standardization and comparability are still lacking [55, 65]. Recent reviews summarizing preclinical and early translational applications of bacterial extracellular vesicles in health and oncology further underscore the need for harmonized quality attributes across different vesicle platforms [34, 63, 64].

Regulatory and safety considerations for PEVs are likely to draw on lessons learned from licensed outer membrane vesicle (OMV)–based vaccine platforms, where yield optimization, detoxification or reactogenicity control, and stringent quality attributes were essential for regulatory approval [66]. Compared with live probiotics or engineered live biotherapeutic products, PEVs are non-replicating and may theoretically reduce risks associated with uncontrolled colonization or translocation in immunocompromised hosts; however, vesicles can still carry potent immunostimulatory components, including pathogen-associated molecular patterns and bacterial DNA, necessitating rigorous safety evaluation [63, 64, 66]. Positioning PEVs relative to alternative microbiome-derived modalities such as live biotherapeutics, postbiotic metabolites, or fecal microbiota transplantation therefore requires careful balancing of efficacy, manufacturability, and safety, alongside evidence-based biomarker strategies for patient stratification [7, 67]. Beyond vesicles, probiotic-derived soluble mediators have also been shown to modulate adaptive immune responses, and comparative studies remain limited but are needed to delineate contexts in which EV-based formulations may offer distinct advantages over other postbiotic approaches [43].

Conclusion

Probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs) are increasingly recognized as biologically active mediators of gut–liver immune crosstalk with emerging relevance to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Accumulating preclinical evidence indicates that PEVs can modulate innate and adaptive immune pathways, reshape inflammatory and fibrotic liver microenvironments, and influence host immune sensing mechanisms implicated in hepatocarcinogenesis. Notably, the most robust mechanistic support for these effects currently derives from inflammatory and pre-malignant liver disease models rather than established HCC tumor systems, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation and clear delineation of evidentiary boundaries.

Despite these limitations, the intrinsic properties of PEVs, including their biocompatibility, cargo diversity, and capacity to engage host immune pathways, position them as a compelling cell-free modality for future immunomodulatory strategies in liver disease. Advances in vesicle purification, nanoformulation, and combination approaches further suggest that PEV-based interventions complement existing therapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors or antiviral regimens, provided that liver-specific efficacy and safety are rigorously established. However, successful clinical translation will depend on resolving key challenges related to in vivo biodistribution, cargo–target specificity, dose standardization, and manufacturing reproducibility, as well as on the development of liver-relevant and orthotopic HCC models.

Taken together, PEVs represent a promising yet still early-stage platform whose therapeutic potential in HCC will require systematic validation through well-controlled preclinical studies and carefully designed early-phase clinical trials. Addressing these translational gaps will be essential to determine whether PEV-based strategies can meaningfully contribute to future, immune-inclusive, precision-oriented approaches to HCC management.
