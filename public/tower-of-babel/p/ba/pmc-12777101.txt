Introduction

“Every tooth in a child's mouth is more valuable than a diamond.”

Dental caries contributes almost 10 times more to the global burden of mouth-related illnesses than periodontal disease, along with oral ailments combined. An adverse psychological environment lowers intelligence quotient (IQ), hinders intellectual growth, and negatively impacts behavioral and mental abilities.1 Early-life cognitive impairment may contribute to a lack of knowledge regarding diet awareness, oral hygiene practices, and maintaining oral health, all of which have a direct bearing on tooth decay. A high IQ could be more useful than a low IQ for managing a child's level of cooperation during dental treatment.2

The occurrence of tooth decay is influenced by lifestyle choices and dietary habits, which are closely correlated with socioeconomic level. Moreover, studies on nutritional disparities between the sexes have shown that boys like to eat sweets and drink soft beverages more frequently than girls.3 Concerns and suffering from caries may cause problems with eating, chewing, smiling, and speaking since teeth that are missing, discolored, or fractured are among the symptoms.4

The unique qualities of intelligence that each child possesses are measured by their intelligence quotient, or IQ. “A person's capacity to adjust and effectively deal with issues in their environment” is how Wechsler describes intelligence. A person's relative intellect, stated as a score on a standardized intelligence test is what IQ is defined as, on the other hand.” It is believed that children's comprehension of information, instructions, and causes and effects is significantly influenced by intelligence.

Additionally, it might affect their capacity to express emotions or pain and to act appropriately in a dental setting. It takes a lot longer for kids with low IQs to comprehend and accept the oral care therapy scenario. There have been reports of lower caries rates in orphanages, which are linked to a mix of living circumstances, including being institutionalized; a sheltered life at the facility, free from emotional stress, and regular and appropriate nutrition maintenance.5 Rud and Kisling claim that children's acceptance of dental treatment is significantly influenced by their chronological and mental ages.6 Each child has a unique blend of psychological qualities, one of which is their IQ, which is quantifiable and referred to as their IQ. Children with varying intelligence quotients are not all alike, and this difference in IQ might make it difficult for them to comprehend the value of dental health and to adhere to preventive measures.7 It is reasonable to assume that children's comprehension of information, instructions, and causes and effects will be greatly influenced by intelligence. It might also affect their capacity to express emotions or discomfort and to act appropriately in the oral care setting.

Children who refuse dental treatment have been linked to attention issues. It takes considerably longer for children with low IQs to accept the dental treatment scenario. Children's dental anxiety was found to be more closely correlated with intelligence.8,9 Thus, using Raven's colored progressive matrices as an IQ test, this study sought to find out whether children with dental caries had higher intellectual capacity.

Methodology

Estimation of Intelligence Quotient Level

The IQ status estimate was verified using the utilization of Raven's colored progressive matrices, 1998 edition, in every child between the ages of 7 and 11 years. It is widely used to evaluate the proper operation of the brain and is one of the most approved tests for assessing cognitive abilities. In all, there are 36 issues, categorized as A, Ab, and B. The questions were organized in a geometric pattern with one component missing; this is a kind of nonverbal evaluation. The students were given an examination with questions and an answer sheet that had been specifically made for this purpose. In addition, out of six possible choices, they had to choose the cell that was missing. In accordance with the instructions in the test booklet, they were also given 30 minutes to finish the exam.

Clinical Examination

Oral caries records were kept using the decayed, missing, and filled teeth (DMFT)/dmfs index. The DMF index was first presented by Henry Klein, Carrole. Using a mouth mirror and a WHO Probe, Palmer and Knutson's 1938 index examines every permanent tooth. In 1944, Gruebbel introduced the “def index” as a substitute for the DMF index in assessing dental caries in the primary dentition.

Standardization of the Investigator

The correct scoring and interpretation of the IQ test were carried out by a clinical psychologist. The subjects' dental caries condition was documented by the dentist.

Statistical Analysis

Results were analyzed with the use of IBM Chicago's SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) 21.0 version. We used the Chi-square test to compare the category variables. The one-way ANOVA was used to compare continuous variables across groups. Statistical significance was determined by a p-value less than 0.05.

Discussion

The “relative intelligence of an individual expressed as a score on a standardized test of intelligence” is what IQ is defined as.9 The following are examples of intelligence testing scales: the Sternberg Triarchic Abilities Test, the Cognitive Assessment System, the Differential Ability Scales, the Stanford-Binet IQ, the Kaufman Tests, the Turing test, the Wechsler Intelligence Scale, and Raven's colored progressive matrices. This intelligence quotient test was first created for use in educational settings to investigate the hereditary and environmental factors that influence cognitive capacity. To accommodate members with different skill levels, the matrices are available in three different versions: standard progressive matrices, colored progressive matrices, and advanced progressive matrices. Raven's colored progressive matrices are used in this study; they are intended for use with children aged 7 to 11, generally developing adults, and those with physical or cognitive impairments. The results of the current study provide insight into the cognitive abilities and attitudes of kids who have dental cavities. This will assist the clinician in prioritizing the children who require counselling, which will change the children's IQ and help with child management by gaining an understanding of their personality level. To determine the correlation between intelligence and dental caries, researchers conducted a cross-sectional comparative study on 150 orphans ranging in age from 7 to 11 years old (93 boys and 57 girls). A mean age of 8.83 ± 1.74 years was recorded for the participants in this research. A total of 122 children—78 (64%) boys and 44 (36%) girls—had dental caries in the current study. Most of them fell into the category of average intelligence (IQ grade III). In the present study, the majority of children, or 68 out of 150, had an IQ of grade III, or 45.3% (intellectually average), while the fewest, or 3 out of 150, had an IQ of grade I, or 2% (intellectually superior). Children with greater DMFT scores had lower IQ scores. Similar findings were noted in studies by Dhanu et al.,2 Navit et al.,8 and Virk et al.,10 that is, dental caries increased as IQ grade (grade V, intellectually challenged) increased. However, there is a potential relationship between dental caries and IQ. In the current study, grade V had the highest level of caries, and the level of caries decreased in subsequent grades, such as grade IV, grade III, and grade II. Grade I (intellectually superior) had the lowest level of caries. Since this study focused on orphans, we saw that their health was not very good. This could be attributed to environmental deprivation, a lack of sensory stimulation, and cognitive deficits that arise in orphanages. It is important to consider several limitations when interpreting the results of this study. Firstly, since the reduced sample only included a small number of Indore city's orphanages, it is possible that the findings cannot be applied to a broader population. Secondly, the nonequivalent gender distribution employed in this study prevents us from establishing a relationship between various psychological factors and oral disorders. Therefore, more studies with larger samples and other psychological dimensions on oral health status are needed to provide a clearer picture of the correlation between IQ and dental caries. Using this inquiry, we can learn about the thoughts and actions of kids who have dental caries.

Conclusion

Research has shown that dental caries is more common in orphans with lower IQs. Researchers found a statistically significant correlation between IQ and dental decay. Oral health programs for kids should include IQ testing into their procedures to empower them to take better care of their teeth and foster better communication with their doctors.
