Coping with the challenges of caregiving: A qualitative exploration of the experiences of family caregivers of children with rare diseases in South Africa
Authors: Jodie van Niekerk, Marianne Gush, Shahida Moosa, Chrisma Pretorius
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRare diseases are a group of chronic conditions with low individual prevalence. These conditions predominantly affect children and severely impact their lives. Oftentimes, children with rare diseases receive care from family caregivers, who experience several challenges in their roles and regularly struggle to cope with caregiving demands, with consequences for their physical and psychological well‐being. Therefore, enhanced understanding of caregivers' unique challenges and coping is required. This phenomenologically situated, exploratory qualitative study explored the caregiving experiences of 10 South African parents (aged 22–54) whose children have rare diseases and receive care at a public hospital in Cape Town, South Africa. These parents were recruited using a convenience strategy and interviewed thereafter. Interview transcripts were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis to develop themes representing patterns of shared meaning across participants' narratives. Seven themes were developed, representing diverse dimensions of caregiving. First, uncertainty was a challenge that some addressed by seeking knowledge. Second, experiences in healthcare settings reflected how healthcare providers' knowledge and communication shaped caregivers' sense of support or marginalization. Third, caregivers described the all‐encompassing nature of meeting children's holistic needs. Fourth, acceptance, frequently aided by normalization, was challenging yet central to coping. Fifth, caregivers' coping was shaped by structural inequities and contextual constraints. Sixth, religious beliefs, positivity, and love for their children ascribed meaning to caregiving, enabling perseverance amid adversity. Finally, caregiving was experienced as both depleting and inevitable; participants described significant physical and psychological tolls yet expressed a sense of duty that compelled them to continue. When interpreted alongside international literature, these themes suggest that while caregivers in South Africa negotiate similar meaning patterns to those reported elsewhere, their experiences are further shaped by context‐specific limitations in support and healthcare infrastructure. It is vital that family caregivers receive improved support and consideration in South Africa.Keywords: challenges, coping, experiences, family caregivers, parents, rare diseases, South Africa

What is known about this topic

Existing international literature documents that family caregivers of children with rare diseases experience substantial psychosocial, physical, and emotional burden, including uncertainty, social isolation, stigma, and reduced quality of life. Studies from high‐income countries have highlighted the importance of healthcare provider communication, social support, and meaning‐making processes in shaping caregivers' coping and adaptation. In South Africa, qualitative research has provided insights into caregiving in relation to a few specific rare conditions, such as epidermolysis bullosa, albinism, and Bardet–Biedl syndrome. However, research from low‐ and middle‐income countries remains limited overall, and there is a paucity of work examining caregiver experiences across rare diseases more broadly, particularly within the context of constrained healthcare systems, structural inequities, and limited formal support.

What this paper adds to the topic

This phenomenologically informed qualitative study provides one of the first in‐depth accounts of the experiences of South African family caregivers of children with rare diseases within a public healthcare context. Extending previous South African research focused on specific diagnoses, this study offers a more holistic view of how caregivers navigate uncertainty, care demands, and coping across diverse rare disease conditions. The findings demonstrate how caregiving is shaped not only by illness‐related challenges common across settings but also by context‐specific factors, including healthcare system strain, limited access to resources, and social misunderstanding. By integrating caregivers' narratives with the Family Systems Illness model, the study highlights acceptance, meaning‐making, and perseverance as central coping processes, while underscoring the urgent need for improved psychosocial support and rare disease‐specific services in South Africa.

BACKGROUND

Rare diseases (RDs) are a group of over 7000 chronic conditions with extremely low prevalence (Malherbe, 2023). RDs are widely defined as conditions that affect fewer than one in 2000 people in a population (Orphanet, 2012). These conditions are often debilitating, frequently diminishing physical and cognitive functioning and shortening life expectancy (Baynam et al., 2020; McMullan et al., 2022), with severe impacts on patients and their families (Malherbe, 2023).

Due to the onset of RD symptoms usually occurring before adulthood, parents often assume the role of family caregivers for their children (Atkins & Padgett, 2024; Wu et al., 2024). While caregivers provide care to children with different RDs, their experiences share numerous commonalities (Baumbusch et al., 2019; Boettcher et al., 2021). Caregivers of children with RDs face some similar challenges to those caring for children with more common chronic conditions; however, the rarity of RDs presents further challenges for parents (Atkins & Padgett, 2024; Kolemen et al., 2021; Rice et al., 2020).

In addition to daily care provision, caregivers' roles may involve advocating for (Currie & Szabo, 2019) and coordinating their children's healthcare (Simpson et al., 2021). Moreover, due to many healthcare providers' (HCPs') lack of knowledge about RDs, caregivers often need to research their children's RDs (Pasquini et al., 2021), and educate HCPs (Pignolo et al., 2022). Therefore, family caregivers play a multifaceted role (Hassall, Smith, Rust, & Wittkowski, 2022), which is critical in the comprehensive management of RDs (Baumbusch et al., 2019; Boettcher et al., 2021; Kolemen et al., 2021).

Unfortunately, caregivers are regularly overwhelmed by caregiving demands (Schiller et al., 2024), have various unmet needs (Pelentsov et al., 2015), and frequently have insufficient support (Anderson et al., 2013; McMullan et al., 2022). Consequently, caregivers experience numerous psychosocial challenges, physical impacts, and regularly report low quality of life (QoL) (Anderson et al., 2013; Atkins & Padgett, 2024).

Managing RDs in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), such as South Africa, is uniquely challenging (Baynam et al., 2020; De Vries, 2017). Technological advances accelerating RD diagnosis are often not accessible in LMICs (Chediak et al., 2022), and many approved treatments are not yet registered for use in South Africa (Conradie et al., 2021). The South African healthcare system is furthermore under severe strain, with the doctor–patient ratio having decreased from 0.79 per 1000 in 2019 to 0.31 in 2022, and a critical lack of nurses leaving over 5000 nursing positions unfilled—the nurse‐to‐patient ratio now standing at 1–2300 (Hoare & Matisonn, 2024). In addition, RDs have received limited policy attention, likely due to competing public health priorities, such as nutrition and communicable disease prevention (Conradie et al., 2021; De Vries, 2017).

To support South African caregivers, it is essential to identify their challenges and factors aiding their coping so that interventions can be tailored to address their unique needs and augment supportive factors understood to enhance coping (Hassall, Smith, Rust, & Wittkowski, 2022; Pelentsov et al., 2016). Against the backdrop of rapid advancements in research about the management of RDs (Austin et al., 2018) and several developments in RD diagnosis (Baynam et al., 2020; Chediak et al., 2022), research about caregivers (Boettcher et al., 2020), and especially South African caregivers, remains insufficient. A call has been made to raise awareness about RDs and to take action to benefit those impacted in Africa (Baynam et al., 2020) and South Africa (Jacobson, 2022; Malherbe, 2023). Therefore, this study qualitatively explored the experiences of South African family caregivers of children with RDs from an experiential‐phenomenological perspective, with the aim of understanding how caregivers cope with RD‐related challenges.

METHODS

Recruitment and data collection

We followed an exploratory design involving in‐depth semi‐structured interviews with 10 parents of children with RDs receiving care at the Genetics Department at Tygerberg Hospital, a large public tertiary hospital in the Western Cape, South Africa.

Participants were recruited from Tygerberg Hospital, using a convenience recruitment approach. Although 41 prospective participants were approached, only 10 enrolled in the study. Several parents cited difficulties arranging care for their children during interview times, with three participants rescheduling and five ultimately canceling their appointments. The first author inferred, based on this context, that the remaining nonparticipation was likely influenced by the significant demands on caregivers' time associated with extensive daily caregiving responsibilities.

Recruitment efforts involved a research nurse, who was working at the hospital, distributing informational flyers and more detailed study information sheets about the study to the prospective participants in person. These recruitment materials informed prospective participants about the study, what their participation would entail, and instructed parents on how to contact the first author should they wish to take part in the research project. The inclusion criteria required caregivers to be at least 18 years old, to be a biological or legal family member of a child with an RD, and to reside in the same location as the child. Once caregivers contacted the first author, a meeting was arranged for the process of informed consent, completion of the biographical information form, and interviews. These meetings and the subsequent interviews took place either in a private room at Tygerberg Hospital or telephonically. Seven interviews were conducted at Tygerberg Hospital, and three took place telephonically. All interviews were conducted by the first author.

The semi‐structured interviews, consisting of 12 open‐ended questions, were piloted in two initial interviews conducted by the first author to ensure rich data elicitation and to identify any questions that may have been embedded in problematic assumptions. Questions served to enquire about the participants' caregiving experiences, particularly how they had coped with any challenges encountered while caring for their children. After the two pilot interviews, question three was changed from “When did your journey as a caregiver begin? Please tell me about the circumstances leading to you being here today” to “Please tell me about how your journey as a caregiver began.” This change was deemed necessary after one participant interpreted the question as slightly accusatory—as though we were assuming she played a part in her child's condition.

All participants consented to their interviews being recorded digitally for the purpose of transcription and data analysis.

Data analysis

The interview data were coded and analyzed, guided by Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2021). This study was situated within an interpretivist and phenomenological paradigm, which assumes that reality is subjectively experienced and socially constructed. The analysis followed an experiential orientation to RTA, focusing on participants' lived experiences and meanings. The researcher's role in knowledge construction was recognized throughout, consistent with Braun and Clarke's (Braun & Clarke, 2024) conceptualization of RTA as a reflexive and flexible method. First, the recordings of interviews were transcribed (Braun & Clarke, 2006). The first author manually transcribed all interview recordings in their original language to facilitate familiarization and reflexive engagement with the data (Braun & Clarke, 2006). During this stage, the first author began engaging deeply with the transcripts, making reflexive notes, and attending to potential patterns of meaning and shared experiences across the dataset (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Second, building on this early engagement, the first author generated initial codes (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Since the nature of this study was exploratory, the goal of this stage was to generate rich, inclusive codes. These codes were developed, expanded, and refined through an iterative and reflexive process (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Third, these codes were examined to determine how they could be interpreted and developed into candidate themes and subthemes representing patterned meanings across the dataset (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Fourth, these provisional themes were reviewed in relation to the data extracts informing them to assess whether they appropriately captured the meanings being interpreted (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Themes were additionally scrutinized by referring to the original uncoded dataset to evaluate whether they provided a coherent and meaningful interpretation of participants' accounts (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Fifth, the themes were finalized by ascribing to each with a specific name, scope, and analytic narrative (Braun & Clarke, 2006). At this stage, the first author additionally considered how themes and subthemes could be ordered to craft a cohesive narrative that would resonate with the way in which the participants told their own stories (Braun & Clarke, 2006). The sixth step outlined by Braun and Clarke (Braun & Clarke, 2006) is to produce the report. Naturally, this step involved writing about the themes developed through this analysis.

Reflexivity, transparency, and methodological integrity

In line with Braun and Clarke's (Braun & Clarke, 2024) RTA framework, the quality of this study was ensured through methodological coherence, transparency, and reflexive engagement, rather than through traditional criteria, such as “trustworthiness” or “rigor.”

Throughout data collection and analysis, the first author (J.v.N.) maintained reflexive awareness of her assumptions, positionality, and influence on interpretive decisions. Specifically, she was aware of how her childhood experience of having a brother with autism spectrum disorder, and witnessing the resulting financial, emotional, and physical hardship endured by her parents, fostered in her a desire to build meaningful connections with caregivers during the research process. She hoped that the interviews would provide participants with a space to share their experiences and unburden themselves. The stories shared with the first author resonated deeply with her and influenced her emotional engagement throughout the study, shaping her approach to both data collection and analysis. Additionally, aspects of the first author's identity may also have influenced participants' responses. Being a young, privileged White woman, she may have affected the willingness of some caregivers to share certain experiences, particularly those they believed she might not fully understand. Reflexive journaling and ongoing supervisory discussions (with C.P.) supported a process of critical self‐scrutiny, helping to make visible how meanings were constructed and interpreted. Transparency was promoted by offering a detailed account of analytic processes, and coherence and consistency were prioritized by ensuring the study's philosophical stance, data collection, and analytic method were aligned throughout.

Consistent with RTA, analytic responsibility rested primarily with the first author (J.v.N.), who designed the interview schedule, conducted participant recruitment, facilitated interviews, and undertook coding and theme development. Emerging codes and themes were discussed iteratively with C.P., contributing to later stages of theme refinement and interpretation. S.M., as clinical geneticist, provided clinical insight and contextual expertise relevant to the healthcare setting throughout the study. M.G. reviewed and revised successive versions of the manuscript to ensure methodological consistency, clarity, and responsiveness to reviewer feedback. All authors reviewed and approved the final manuscript.

Ultimately, the value of this study lies in its rich, situated, and interpretive account of caregivers' experiences. Following Braun and Clarke's RTA reporting guidelines, the focus was on thoughtful, reflexive, and contextually grounded practice as the basis for high‐quality qualitative research (Braun & Clarke, 2006).

Ethical clearance

Prior to the commencement of this study, ethical clearance was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee (HREC reference number S22/06/114) at Stellenbosch University, as well as permission from the South African Department of Health and Tygerberg Hospital.

FINDINGS

Five participants spoke Afrikaans, three spoke isiXhosa, and two spoke English as their first language. Three interviews were conducted in Afrikaans, and seven were conducted in English. One of the interviews conducted in English was with a participant who speaks both isiXhosa and English but chose to answer all questions in English. However, she asked the study translator to clarify some questions in isiXhosa before responding. For the sake of promoting accessibility of these findings to international audiences, all quotes presented in the findings section below have been translated by the first author into English.

Six participants lived with a spouse or partner, and four were single. Participants' education ranged from grade 10 to tertiary‐level certification. Six caregivers were unemployed, two worked part‐time, and two worked full‐time. A table reflecting participant demographics can be found in Table 1.

Interviews spanned between 45 and 60 min (median of 46 min), and although the participant group was small, the collected data provided sufficient richness and depth (information power*) to support a meaningful interpretation of these parents' experiences within this specific context.

As stated, the parents in this study were diverse in terms of demographic factors and cared for children with different RDs. Still, several shared patterns of meaning were identified through reflexive analysis. These patterns were developed into seven themes, which are reported below.

Theme 1: Uncertainty and knowledge

During most interviews, parents described their experiences as being characterized by uncertainty. However, gaining knowledge about their children's RDs provided a means of coping with some challenges associated with uncertainty.

For many, uncertainty began before diagnosis. As seven of 10 participants received an RD diagnosis over a year after birth, many parents suspected an unnamed problem due to their children's unexplained symptoms while seeking a diagnosis. Although receiving an RD diagnosis for their child was devastating for most parents, it also facilitated caregivers seeking relevant information, which both improved their understanding of the RD and the related approach to caregiving. In this way, a diagnosis addressed aspects of caregivers' uncertainty and related fear, as illustrated by the following interview excerpt:

It had been very difficult not to get the right information, because you don't know yourself, and the doctors don't know, so that makes it very uncertain—even more uncertain and afraid for a person. (P4)

It was a very big relief. Yeah, still half unsure because (you) still don't know what… You hear this terrible name and it sounds like something terrible you've never heard of, but yeah once they explained what it was, it was quite reassuring, because from that one can work with something, you have something to work with, because it is an illness. (P4)

Caregivers further stressed the significance of understanding their child's RD. Initially, some felt hopeless due to misunderstandings about the implications of the diagnosis:

So that time I didn't understand. I said I don't think this baby is going to walk. (P7)

Although gaining knowledge about the RD could either inspire hope or incite fear depending on the related prognostic information, a diagnosis facilitated independent research, helping caregivers cope with uncertainty about their children's futures. Learning about the RD allowed parents to better prepare for their caregiving roles and ready themselves to explain their child's condition to others:

It helped a lot to understand more and yes, try to understand what you have to prepare for and what you have to do as a caregiver. (P4)

I like to go and Google and to search everything and I know things like even if there's questions that somebody's going to ask me. I want to know and I want to search. (P9)

Moreover, although independent research facilitated learning, most participants relied on HCPs to acquire information:

I went to research this disease. And I talked to (the doctor) about the disease. I basically asked her every day, and then she explained to me, because I wanted certainty. (P1)

It helped me a lot, because I really had to go on nothing. I got my information that I wanted from the doctors. (P10)

Initially, most participants felt overwhelmed, apprehensive, and ill‐equipped. Although this intensity subsided as parents gained knowledge and experience, uncertainties remained. For many, uncertainty arose from the lack of a clear prognosis or developmental expectations:

This is also just part of the challenges because you wonder if he will manage. Because with other babies you know when they will be able to walk, or how they will talk. There is a standard for most babies at that age, then they do it, it's almost like a surprise, you don't know when it's going to happen. (P4)

In line with this, some caregivers feared that their child would die unexpectedly, and this intensified the anxiety they experienced when their child was sick:

We can expect anything for her at any day, so we just, we feel like, she's borrowed to us. It's only temporary. (P9)

In addition, caregivers of older children experienced uncertainty about their children's adult lives, which substantially contributed to their stress:

Yes it's a big fear, I already have the fear because (P2's son) is already 18, he can't work in large groups at a workplace … Their future is very important to us, and at this stage we are not sure which way it will go. (P2)

Theme 2: Acceptance

As informal family caregivers, the parents in this study became caregivers out of necessity, rather than because they actively sought to care for a child with an RD. Most participants were devastated by their child's diagnosis, and many struggled to accept it at first. However, parents saw no other choice than to do so:

Then I said, 'I can't do anything', so I just have to accept that (P1's son) is like that (P1). I didn't feel good you know but in the end of the day I have to accept it (P8)

Is this a punishment for me or what? But I have to take care of the child, because it's mine (P7)

Nonetheless, a few participants explained that acceptance was necessary to provide care from a place of love rather than duty:

It is not always easy for us to accept, and if you can't accept that, it's hard to come to terms with your situation, with what you have to do with the depth of your ability as a caregiver. So, yes, only then can you really do what you have to do with love, and not just as a duty. (P4)

Faith and religious beliefs often aided acceptance:

God gave me (my son) like this, and I'm going to accept him. The way he don't walk or don't talk, I told myself, because I did ask God to give me a child. So I accept him and then I love him. (P3)

You just have to accept that this is what the Lord wanted and this is what He gave you. (P4)

Likewise, the process of normalizing the RD promoted acceptance. This often occurred through caregivers becoming aware of other children who had the same RD as their child. Sometimes this exposed parents to more severe cases of the RD. This exposure, despite sometimes being distressing, made them feel fortunate and grateful for their situations:

It was a bit scary because there were much worse cases, but on the other hand, we were very grateful because his case is not that serious, so it actually goes both ways. (P4)

And when I started watching videos, I saw pictures of children, I stopped asking God why He gave me a baby like that. I stopped judging myself, I stopped feeling sorry for myself and I just started appreciating because everything that happened could have been worse. I could have lost him when I gave birth and that God gave him to me… (P6)

Furthermore, learning of worse cases and realizing others experienced similar difficulties alleviated shame and inspired hope:

When I was on that group, when I saw there were more parents, why should I feel bad? Because out there, there are more parents who have it, their children who have it. (P1)

They show me other kids that are like (her son), also got that, yes. So it's where I get hope. (P7)

Finally, many caregivers ultimately accepted their children simply due to their love for them:

I will take her the way she is, I don't care about this stuff. I love her the way she is. (P5)

So, it doesn't matter if you have normal children or not, it's different children. They are your children, and you just have to love them because they came from you. (P6)

Theme 3: Caregiver connections with HCPs

HCPs either contributed to caregivers' challenges or supported their coping. This depended on the HCPs' knowledge about RDs, empathy, and manner of communication with caregivers.

Although many HCPs were instrumental in providing valuable information to caregivers about their children's RDs, some HCPs lacked adequate RD‐related knowledge. These HCPs sometimes contributed to challenges by dismissing early signs and symptoms of children's RDs, leading to a need for caregivers to advocate for their child's healthcare:

And then the doctor told me “no, it can't be, he's still small” and all that stuff, but it's like a mother's heart telling you there's something wrong. And I was very upset afterwards because I was right and the doctor didn't look into it. (P2)

This mother also received inaccurate information about her sons, which caused her unnecessary emotional pain:

Yes, (with) quite a few doctors there were negative things that were said and that were basically diagnosed over them but they did not have all the facts. It affected us a lot and at one point broke us. (P2)

Another consequence of HCPs' lack of knowledge about RDs was that parents oftentimes had to explain their child's condition to doctors. This was especially frustrating since most participants had to see multiple HCPs while coordinating their children's formal healthcare.

A lot of doctors don't even know what we're talking about, so I have to tell the doctor now that this is what he's doing. That was also a bit bad for me. (P4)

They ask me a lot of questions and then I'm getting tired sometimes, I'm getting angry. Why they ask me? They don't check on the system. Sometimes I am getting cross. Why they ask me? Why don't they speak together as doctors? (P7)

Furthermore, caregivers' relationships with HCPs shaped their experiences in significant ways. Effective communication by HCPs was characterized by the use of simple and accessible language, which helped caregivers understand medical information:

(Doctor's name) came on our path and she was the one who was explaining it to us in more simple terms, you know, so we could understand what's going on with her development and all those things… (P9)

However, unsympathetic communication or a lack of communication led to negative experiences, dissatisfaction, and emotional distress:

The doctor told us in front of my children that their lifespan is only up to thirty. I was very upset and I walked out and my oldest son, who is now 18, he came and sat next to me. He was still small but it showed me that it affected him. (P2)

They didn't want to tell us, like, okay, we are worried about this or something, but we could see the worry. (P9)

In addition, caregivers were encouraged and reassured by HCPs whom they perceived to be compassionate or considerate. If parents believed that HCPs genuinely cared about them and their child, healthcare interactions were supportive of caregivers' coping:

Especially the therapists who, it feels like they want to give back a lot, so it helped me very, very much. (P4)

(Doctor's name), she's very kind. You see the way she speaks to me. She speaks politely and calms my nerves. Even though I'm going home, I'm going peacefully. (P5)

They said don't stress mama he's going to be fine, he is gonna grow. (P7)

Both positive interactions with HCPs and signs of progress, which were credited to HCPs' involvement in children's care, motivated caregivers to persevere in fulfilling health‐related care demands.

Theme 4: Experiences of providing holistic care

Caregivers reported diverse health‐related care demands based on their child's specific condition and related symptoms. Commonalities included managing healthcare, administering medication, keeping medical records, and taking their children to numerous medical appointments with multiple HCPs. This sometimes required caregivers to spend a significant amount of time traveling to these appointments:

We live outside the town, so I have about 3 to 4 appointments a month at the clinic itself, the audio therapist, the occupational therapist, the speech therapist, the physiotherapist we go to see in Malmesbury, it's quite a long drive, and then we have to go see the paediatrician there too, and then next month we have appointments again in Paarl and Malmesbury, and on Mondays we are at Tygerberg. (P4)

In addition, caregivers found monitoring their children's symptoms to be stressful, especially since they often struggled to distinguish which symptoms warranted emergency action:

Well, now that's the whole challenge, because we are not sure if it's something that happens to every normal child or if it's something that comes from, it's like part of the syndrome, or something. So sometimes that is making us confused. (P9)

Moreover, prolonged stays in hospital during periods of increased symptom severity were particularly challenging for parents. Caregivers experienced physical exhaustion, emotional distress, and frustration due to the need to fulfill additional demands in these uncomfortable settings:

I think it was almost a month that she was in hospital. (P9)

I was feeling very tired, especially my back, because those couches are very hard. Yes, during the day I have to sit the whole day in a chair and okay, I had to change my child, the nappy, and change the way he is sleeping, so, I was feeling very tired. (P7)

Another consequence of prolonged hospital stays, along with the extent of their other caregiving demands, was that many caregivers were required to cease working:

I was working so, I gave up my job to look after him because I was in hospital four times in a month and I only got two days for that out of the time. So, it became a problem and then I decided my child is more important than my work, which I'm financially in need of work, but that is how I took it at that time. (P10)

Loss of income, along with considerable expenses related to their children's care, intensified financial stress:

So financially yes, it hit us very hard. (P2)

It makes quite a big difference in one's life because you already have a lot to stress about, so finances do make a difference. (P4)

Moreover, children also required considerable support from their caregivers for basic daily functioning:

Then I wash him, I take the chair and sit like that and stand and I wash him and after that I must take him out and it's difficult but because of I love him. (P3)

I must do everything for him, like clean him in the toilet so he can go to the toilet by himself. I would have to wash him. He don't try to wash himself. I have to do all that. (P10)

Some symptoms, such as a child not being able to walk, led caregivers to experience physical strain, like back pain. Additionally, sleep disturbances compounded caregiving challenges, exacerbating fatigue:

And then I think now, I can't sleep, because I hear his chest rumble, and his nose is closed, and he complains of his stomach hurting, but I feel deep down I'm tired. (P1)

It's like I'm getting tired. I'm getting tired by the end of the day but I must be there to support her no matter what she can't do or she can do. (P8)

Moreover, caregivers who had nonverbal children reported communication difficulties, which introduced both practical challenges and emotional barriers in daily care provision. Despite these difficulties, caregivers stressed the importance of showing love and understanding to their children, emphasizing an additional need for participants to provide emotional care:

It takes a lot of time at a time, because now you have to try to guess what it is until you know what it is, otherwise sometimes they become unhappy because you can't notice what he wants now. (P4)

Just love them and care for them, listen to them if they can, so (P10's son), he can't speak, but I listen, try to understand what he is trying to say, because if you don't take note of them, they will get aggressive. (P10)

However, caregivers faced all caregiving demands necessitated by children's conditions, in addition to the normal demands of parenting. This included entertaining and building relationships with their children. Despite finding it exhausting to fulfill these roles simultaneously, caregivers recognized the importance of this for their children:

I can't tell her tonight no I can't play with her again, because tomorrow she'll say “but Dad doesn't have time for me”. (P8)

For some children, these emotional needs were intensified by experiences of being visibly different or misunderstood by others. Caregivers described having to protect their children from stigma and social judgment, particularly in public spaces:

If I go somewhere and people look at him as a disabled child, I will always tell them, no, he is my child, he is, treat him as my child, not as a disabled child. (P10)

These moments of social protection and advocacy were not only part of intimate caregiving, but were shaped by the broader social environments in which families lived—an issue taken up in the following theme.

Theme 5: Caregiving in context

In keeping with an RTA approach, the experiences captured in this theme are necessarily interwoven rather than conceptually discrete. Caregivers did not describe stigma, misunderstanding, and support as separate domains, but as intertwined features of their social worlds. For analytic and narrative clarity, Theme 5 is presented through two closely related subthemes: stigma and social meaning, and help and hindrance within caregivers' support systems.

Stigma and social meaning:

Stigma and discrimination, such as strangers staring at their children in public or asking them intrusive questions, were a central part of many caregivers' challenges, leading many to feel defensive:

I would say it's a challenge for us and with the people that's around us. There are people who look at your child strange. (P9)

You know your child is sick so if someone has something to say about it, you immediately take defence about the child. (P2)

One mother experienced severe discrimination, which led to psychological distress and feelings of social isolation:

Others are now making fun of looking after my child. They even take pictures and post it on the internet, on social media, without sending it to me. It's my friends, it's my family. (P5)

I was feeling so sad I wish sometimes I could kill myself. I don't know what I should do because they make fun of my child as if I asked God to give me a child like this. I really suffered. (P5)

Help or hindrance:

An additional contextual factor impacting caregiving experiences involved caregivers' friends and family, who could either amplify challenges or aid caregivers' coping, depending on their understanding and respect. Some intensified parents' challenges by being unsupportive or showing disregard for children's needs:

… I say no I can't leave my child at home, she needs me. Now they start to make fun of me because I can't go with them. (P5)

Some parents further explained that where their friends and families contributed to their difficulties, it was because they did not understand their child's RD:

Because it is a rare disease, people do not understand it. (P4)

So yes, we have experiences, it's difficult, it's actually very difficult, because many people don't understand it. They don't understand why we took them out of school, why we had to. But they don't understand the disease so that's why they don't understand why you do what you do. (P2)

Conversely, considerate friends and family provided practical assistance, emotional support, and empathy, bolstering caregivers' coping abilities in meaningful ways:

I go to open days, I'm looking for a job. One of my close friend's mom offers to take care of him for like a few hours for me. (P6)

But I feel in my case it's nice to talk to someone about it, because it's not always that you can keep it to yourself and keep it inside, because like I told you, it's draining. (P9)

Similarly, caregivers' home environments significantly shaped their experiences. While challenges facilitated closeness between some families, care demands also led to exhaustion and conflict, straining household dynamics:

I have to say, the more difficult it goes for us, together, the easier it is for us to live together and to love each other more. The challenges bring us closer together rather than further apart. (P2)

At first it was really challenging, but we were facing a lot of things and we were feeling like this stressfulness in the house. (P9)

Romantic partners faced unique challenges, with some couples separating as a result. For a few mothers, lack of support from a child's father brought about both emotional and practical difficulties:

He started cheating on me. He started saying that there's a reason (her son) is like this and he can't face life like this and he never wanted a life like this. (P6)

It was very emotional for me because I had to stand alone. … I had to go through all that by myself. (P10)

Despite increased tension between couples, spouses were often credited by caregivers as their most fundamental source of support:

I appreciate my husband that he sees what I see with me and supports me in it, because there are few people who support you. (P2)

I feel so bad sometimes but his father is always next to me. That's the thing that makes me get through. He has supported me a lot through all this. (P7)

Therefore, the context of care was meaningful as it determined which forms of support were available to help parents cope. Some caregivers lacked support since facilities, such as schools, were unsuitable or because friends and family members were insufficiently equipped to care for their children:

For us it's a worry when she is away from us. Because now we should think about when she's away from us and all people don't treat your children the way you would you know. (P9)

Although privacy concerns and fear of pity could deter support‐seeking behaviors, many caregivers would have liked to receive more support than they did. A pertinent example was that, while no participants belonged to RD communities, one mother specifically mentioned that she would be grateful for this type of support:

I've noticed with the moms I've dealt with, not everyone likes to talk about what their demands are, or what their problems are, and the doctors and the therapists can't really support you like that always, so I almost want to say if you could have like a support group of moms who are facing the same problems… who can also share their experiences with you, so you can feel, okay, we have a similar problem. (P4)

Across participants' accounts, caregiving unfolded within social environments that could both challenge and sustain them. Caregivers described being stared at, questioned, or judged in public spaces while also navigating varying levels of understanding and support from friends, family members, and partners. These experiences were not described as separate, but as overlapping parts of everyday life, shaping how caregivers managed practical demands, emotional strain, and relationships over time.

Theme 6: Motivation through meaning

Caregivers addressed emotional distress through various independent activities; however, the most significant approaches to coping entailed parents finding meaning in their experiences, which motivated them to persevere in the face of challenges.

In this regard, religion and faith‐based approaches were highly significant. Caregivers found religious activities, such as reading the Bible, to be comforting, and many participants emphasized that prayer played an important role in how they coped:

I think if you stay on your knees then it helps a lot. (P4)

When I pray I speak to God and I just feel okay, and everything is like okay. (P6)

Moreover, trust in a higher power provided hope in the face of uncertainty and fear:

I was worried they were going to tell me he is not going to make it, but I trust God with my child. (P7)

Faith gave meaning and purpose to caregiving challenges. A few framed their challenges as being part of God's plan for their lives:

The fact that I believe in God helps, as I say, God gave me a big heart so I was raised in a tough way. And I was raised in tough way for a reason. This is my reason. God has made me strong, to be able to deal with what I am sitting with now, what has been dealt to me. I don't mean what has been delt to me, they are my gifts. (P2)

Sometimes when I pray, I said, yo, maybe God planned this. Yes, he planned this. (P3)

Oftentimes in connection with parents' religious beliefs, many participants emphasized maintaining a positive attitude despite caregiving challenges. Through ignoring negativity and actively focusing on positive aspects of their experiences, caregivers were better able to maintain a positive outlook. Participants identified several aspects of their experiences that were meaningful or valuable to them. For example, some enjoyed learning about their child's condition and valued the fact that they could use their knowledge to the benefit of others:

I can learn a lot about this kind of thing, at the end of the day I can advise other young persons about how it is to raise a child. (P8)

Others noted their own personal growth as meaningful:

I wasn't like how I was now, like now that I have him. I was always outside and wild. I didn't really care about doing home chores and stuff like that, so now I have him, so everything changed, like, I want to go back to school, I want to study now. He changed everything in a good way for me. (P6)

Furthermore, some parents highlighted that they were proud of themselves for overcoming past challenges:

I'm a strong woman and I can cope. I can cope under these stresses. That really I can say I'm very strong because it was not easy. (P7)

Finally, seeing their child's progress inspired hope, encouraged caregivers and improved their confidence in their caregiving abilities:

Since he's crawling I have that hope that he will walk one day. (P3)

Every bit of progress that he shows or that happens inspires you to just keep going. Yes, it means quite a lot to a person. I think that probably inspires me the most. (P4)

I have brought them to where they are and that is valuable to me. (P2)

Ultimately, love for their children was the main motivation driving parents' caregiving efforts. Participants valued caregiving as an expression of love:

First you must have the love, because if you don't love your child, you can't do anything. You must love your child and then you can do everything. (P3)

And you have to do it out of love in the first place … if you have to do it as a duty, then it won't work, because it takes a lot extra from one. So, yes, it helps a lot if you love your child very much. (P4)

Theme 7: The need to carry on regardless of challenges

Most participants were overwhelmed by the demands on their time, energy, and emotional resilience. In addition to their caregiving roles, participants needed to attend to responsibilities such as working, studying, or home maintenance. Balancing multiple demands was challenging for most:

If I work and I come home, then (P1's son) is clingy and says Mummy Mummy, and then I hear his chest rumble, and then I'm so tired, but then I have to sit with him. (P1)

Yes, there's been a lot of challenges, like school, I have had to study at night and then be with him during the day when I come home from school and cook food. (P6)

I have to balance, because I have to make time for everyone. I can't just lie on the bed. (P8)

Being overburdened in this way led to physical impacts such as exhaustion and frequent illness:

It feels like my body is really, I wouldn't say getting weak, but you struggle to feel standard, I almost want to say, to feel normal. (P4)

Sometimes you get tired, like me, I get very sick. (P9)

In addition, caregivers reported several psychological impacts:

It affects me a lot. I get anxiety attacks… So sometimes it's a bit difficult. Sometimes it's hard for me to cope, with everything… (P2)

Especially with the physical pain that one experiences, it is quite difficult along with the emotional pain of depression. (P4)

I want people to know that it's hard, because it is. It's not easy. Sometimes, some days, like me, I break down and cry. I tell myself it's normal because if I don't do it I'll just lose it. (P6)

Despite caregivers' coping strategies, their extreme burden remained. Therefore, some caregivers emphasized that they were not coping adequately:

I can't say I'm getting rid of the stress, I'm working on it, I'm just trying to be strong that's all. I'm afraid that I will break, I can feel I'm close to breaking, but I'm still just trying to be strong every day. (P2)

Often we feel like we can't cope. (P9)

Regardless of the extent of participants' difficulties, caregivers had to carry on as they saw no other option than to do so:

If (P1's son) is sick, then there is a time when I want to… I give up, I'm tired. But then I realise, I have to help him. Because this is my child. He is in my care. (P1)

I can't tell them ‘like no, I can't do this’ so I must find a way. (P8)

DISCUSSION AND THEORETICAL INTEGRATION

Although this study's data were analyzed inductively, the findings can be meaningfully interpreted in relation to established models of family and illness (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Rolland's Family Systems Illness (FSI) model (Rolland & Williams, 2005) provides a particularly relevant biopsychosocial framework for understanding chronic conditions. The model conceptualizes illness experience across three interrelated dimensions: psychosocial types of health conditions (including onset, course, outcome, disability, and uncertainty), major developmental phases of the illness journey (including initial crisis, chronic phase, and terminal phase), and key family system variables (including belief systems, interweaving development, multigenerational legacies, goodness of fit with health providers, and communication styles). What follows is a discussion of the findings, which includes how each key finding relates to the FSI model.

This study's findings highlight key aspects of caregiving that shaped participants' experiences of caring for a child with an RD. These aspects of caregiving were meaningful since they either posed challenges or facilitated parents' coping. Consistent with the study's interpretivist and phenomenological stance, these findings reflect the participants' subjective meanings and lived experiences, as interpreted through a reflexive analytic process.

The extent of HCPs' knowledge about RDs was an influential factor in shaping caregiving experiences. Consistent with findings from other countries (Baumbusch et al., 2019; Cardão et al., 2021), South African HCPs oftentimes lacked RD‐related knowledge, thus problematizing healthcare access for participants. Similar to Currie and Szabo (2019) and Simpson et al. (2021) research, this lack of HCP knowledge forced caregivers to advocate for their children's healthcare.

Conversely, some participants in this study credited HCPs as being instrumental in finding information about their children's conditions. Prior studies have often emphasized that many HCPs are not knowledgeable about RDs (Anderson et al., 2013; Bogart et al., 2022; Somanadhan & Larkin, 2016), creating a need for caregivers to do independent research (Ekins & Perlstein, 2018; Gómez‐Zúñiga et al., 2019; Pasquini et al., 2021) and sometimes to educate HCPs themselves (Pignolo et al., 2022). However, in this study, knowledgeable HCPs enhanced caregivers' experiences by educating them about their children's RDs. In connection with this, the way that HCPs communicated with parents often established whether they contributed to challenges in healthcare settings, mirroring previous findings (Baumbusch et al., 2019; Fitzgerald et al., 2021; Gómez‐Zúñiga et al., 2019). These findings are supported by the FSI model, which states that the “goodness of fit” between the family and the healthcare system is an important factor within the components of family functioning dimension, which can facilitate either effective or dysfunctional coping (Rolland & Williams, 2005, p. 64). Another significant element of caregiving involved acceptance. Acceptance has been described as a challenging yet important aspect of caregiving (Anderson et al., 2013; Castro et al., 2020; Somanadhan et al., 2022). In this study, participants struggled to accept their children's conditions; however, as in Kerr et al. (2023) research, acceptance supported caregivers' coping. Within the FSI model, acceptance is described as a core task, especially within the initial crisis phase of illness, involving the need to grieve the loss of the pre‐illness identity (Rolland, 2025; Rolland & Walsh, 2006).

The unique context in which participants provided care to their children was also pivotal. It is understood that the context in which caregiving takes place substantially shapes caregiving experiences (Atkins & Padgett, 2024). This study found that parents' social environments were highly impactful because they either introduced challenges, such as stigma and lack of understanding, or provided sources of support that enhanced caregivers' ability to cope with the challenges they faced.

Aspects of social support, or lack thereof, also fit within the components of the family functioning dimension of the FSI model, with connectedness with kin and community networks cited as key processes in family resilience (Rolland & Walsh, 2006). Importantly, in the context of RDs, the psychosocial typology of the illness (e.g., dysmorphic features or the condition's extreme rarity) may contribute to the development of stigma and limited understanding from others.

In addition, certain aspects of caregiving were noteworthy due to how challenging they were for parents. As per previous research (Bell et al., 2019; Chateau et al., 2023; Hassall, Smith, Rust, & Wittkowski, 2022; Somanadhan & Larkin, 2016; Withers et al., 2021), uncertainty was a major challenge reported by participants. Within the FSI model, uncertainty is listed explicitly as one of the five elements that define the psychosocial types of health conditions (Rolland, 2025). Uncertainty, according to the FSI model, refers to the predictability of the illness and uncertainty regarding recurrence in non‐symptomatic phases, with families who are able to put long‐term uncertainty into perspective being considered best prepared to avoid exhaustion and dysfunction (Rolland, 2025).

Another challenge faced by participants was the requirement to meet children's diverse needs, which included healthcare, social, and emotional needs. Participants found it especially difficult to be both a parent and caregiver to their children simultaneously. It is well‐documented that many caregivers are overburdened by a multitude of caregiving demands (Bose et al., 2019; Schiller et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2020). Gómez‐Zúñiga et al.'s (2021) research emphasized that parenting or caregiving in isolation is far easier than fulfilling these roles simultaneously. Many participants stories reflected this, as providing holistic care was a key challenge. This finding fits well into the FSI model's components of family functioning dimension and relates to the interweaving development of family members as they not only navigate illness but also the individual and family life course tasks and transitions (Rolland, 2025). Rolland specifically notes the intersection of parenting and caregiving and the need for significant role flexibility to maintain healthy family organization (Rolland & Walsh, 2006; Rolland & Williams, 2005).

Participants reported several factors that supported their coping. For some, gaining knowledge facilitated coping. As uncertainty is a key aspect of caregiving challenges (Bell et al., 2019; Hassall, Smith, Rust, & Wittkowski, 2022), gaining knowledge can be immensely helpful (Dellve et al., 2006; Gundersen, 2011; Krabbenborg et al., 2016).

Religious beliefs also supported some parents' coping by instilling meaning in caregiving and the difficulties they had to endure. As in Hassall, Smith, Rust, Jones, and Wittkowski's (2022) and Purcell et al.'s (2015) studies, some parents found meaning in being chosen to care for their child. Religious caregivers often find perceiving their challenges as having a greater purpose to be beneficial (Purcell et al., 2015), and religious beliefs have been shown to offer comfort to caregivers (Hassall, Smith, Rust, Jones, & Wittkowski, 2022; Peay et al., 2018).

Similarly, positivity, and specifically the active identification of meaningful elements in caregiving, helped caregivers cope with some of the challenges they experienced. Framing caregiving as a positive and meaningful endeavor has been seen to promote caregivers' well‐being (Dellve et al., 2006; Gómez‐Zúñiga et al., 2021; Hassall, Smith, Rust, & Wittkowski, 2022). Thus, this was a helpful coping strategy for participants in this study. Parents' love for their children was also reported to be an essential source of meaning, thereby both motivating parents and supporting their coping. All of the preceding means of coping relate to the components of family functioning dimension in the FSI model. Most notably, these facilitators of coping describe family communication and family belief systems, which support family adaptation and resilience (Rolland, 2025).

In the seventh theme, the physical and psychological impacts reported by caregivers were discussed. Caregiving appeared to have impacted most participants both physically and psychologically. Not surprisingly, the emotional and psychological difficulties commonly faced by caregivers (Bose et al., 2019; Khair & Pelentsov, 2019) negatively impact their QoL (Boettcher et al., 2021; Cardinali et al., 2019; Mauritz et al., 2021). Caregivers of children with RDs have also been observed to have compromised physical health (Berrocoso et al., 2020; Boettcher et al., 2021; Cardinali et al., 2019). The final theme derived from the findings of this study highlights that the caregivers in this study struggle immensely. Despite their challenges and the severe impacts thereof, these parents are required to persevere in their caregiving roles. This perseverance is related to the chronic “long haul” phase described by Rolland (Rolland, 2025, p. 66) and may be marked by cumulative exhaustion and burnout.

It is imperative that South African family caregivers receive more support. A concerning finding of this study was that no participants belonged to RD communities or support groups, despite previous studies, including the FSI model, highlighting their value (Baumbusch et al., 2019; Bose et al., 2019; Cardão et al., 2021; Castro et al., 2020; McMullan et al., 2022; Rolland, 2006). Notably, one mother requested assistance in forming such a group for parents of children with RDs. Consequently, future research should investigate ways to establish these supportive communities in South Africa.

In addition to these broader structural needs, the findings also have relevance for clinical encounters with families affected by RDs. To support translation of the study's themes into practice, a Table S1 has been included that aligns caregivers' reported experiences with possible areas of clinical attentiveness in genetic counseling. This table is not intended as practice guidelines, but as a reflective tool grounded in participants' accounts.

Study limitations

While this study is one of the first exploring the experiences of family caregivers of children with RDs in the South African context, one key limitation should be acknowledged.

Ideally, participants would have been offered the opportunity to offer reflection (member reflection) beyond the semi‐structured interviews; however, this was not possible under the circumstances. The extent of caregiving demands and participants' time constraints posed logistical barriers to engaging further outside the interview appointments. Future research might consider exploring alternative qualitative methodologies that are less time‐intensive or more participatory in nature, or making childcare available to participants, to make ongoing engagement more feasible for caregivers with substantial daily responsibilities.

CONCLUSION

The findings of this research indicate that South African family caregivers of children with RDs at Tygerberg Hospital face many of the same challenges as caregivers in other countries and also often cope in comparable ways. These findings also illustrate that these parents are both overburdened and overwhelmed by the vast challenges they face and that this has severe consequences for some parents' physical and psychological health. Despite finding a few strategies to mitigate some practical and emotional challenges, caregivers' approaches to coping are insufficient given the extensive burden associated with their roles and their powerlessness to make significant changes at an institutional or policy level. There is a dire need for greater awareness and consideration of caregiving challenges in South African society and in public healthcare. It is hoped that this phenomenological and exploratory study will encourage further exploration into caregivers' experiences and drive meaningful developments in the interest of this vulnerable yet profoundly important group within the larger RD community.
