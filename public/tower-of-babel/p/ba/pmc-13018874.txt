Multidimensional analysis of depression and diabetic kidney disease using GBD, NHANES, and MR
Authors: Shuhui Jing, Lujun Xu, Yuwei Wang, Keyu Zhang, Kaixin Yao, Jie Niu, Juan Pan, Yuan Ge, Xi Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDepression and diabetic kidney disease (DKD) are leading global health burdens that frequently co-occur. Using Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2013–March 2020, and Mendelian randomization (MR), we investigated epidemiological patterns and genetic underpinnings of this relationship. GBD data indicate a rising DKD burden and high depression rates, especially in women and younger adults. NHANES demonstrated a strong, graded cross-sectional association between depressive symptom severity and prevalent DKD, with moderately severe-to-severe symptoms conferring the highest odds of disease. MR analyses, however, yielded predominantly null findings. Although a nominal association between depressed affect and DKD was observed in European ancestry populations, this estimate attenuated following sensitivity analyses and did not provide robust evidence for a direct causal relationship. Integrating mental health screening into diabetes care may mitigate the combined burden of these conditions.Subject areas: health sciences, medicine, medical specialty, psychiatry, endocrinology, nephrology, public health

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is one of the most prevalent and severe microvascular complications of diabetes mellitus, affecting approximately 40% of patients with diabetes.1 Characterized primarily by albuminuria and a progressive decline in the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR),2,3,4 DKD has become the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), accounting for more than half of global ESKD cases.5,6,7 According to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, DKD-related mortality has been steadily increasing, with 528,430 deaths reported between 1999 and 2020, particularly among older adults.8 These alarming trends highlight the urgent need for identifying modifiable risk factors for DKD, beyond traditional metabolic parameters.

Depression is a common mental disorder characterized by persistent low mood, anhedonia, and cognitive impairment.9 The World Health Organization reports an 18% global increase in depression prevalence over the past decade, with over 350 million individuals affected worldwide. By 2030, depression is projected to become the leading contributor to the global disease burden.10,11 Recent studies suggest a potential link between depression and the progression of chronic diseases, including diabetes and kidney dysfunction.12 Nationwide cohort studies demonstrate, for example, that systemic illnesses such as intestinal infections may increase psychiatric disorder incidence,13 while sensory or environmental deprivation (e.g., reduced blue-light exposure) can elevate depression risk,14 underscoring the bidirectional relationship between chronic disease and mental health. Previous studies have demonstrated that patients with comorbid depressive symptoms, particularly those with diabetes aged ≥65 years, show a higher prevalence of DKD or ESKD.15 However, these findings are largely based on cross-sectional or observational studies, which are prone to confounding and reverse causality. Whether depression causally contributes to the development of DKD remains unclear.

We employed a triangulated analytical framework to characterize the depression-DKD relationship across three complementary levels of inference, while explicitly delineating the inferential scope and limitations of each approach (Figure 1). At the population level, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study data quantified epidemiological trends and disease burden; however, these estimates are subject to ecological fallacy, whereby aggregate-level associations may not reflect individual-level risk. At the individual level, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) enabled dose-response modeling within a nationally representative cohort, though its cross-sectional design precludes definitive conclusions regarding temporal sequence or causality. At the genetic level, Mendelian randomization (MR) applied instrumental variable analysis to strengthen causal inference by reducing residual confounding (Table S1)16; nevertheless, MR estimates depend on three core instrumental variable assumptions—validity, independence, and exclusion restriction—and may be underpowered to detect modest causal effects. By integrating these complementary components, our approach triangulates observational and genetic evidence within their appropriate inferential boundaries, providing a methodologically rigorous framework for interpreting the depression-DKD relationship.

The findings of this study will provide important insights into: (1) improving the understanding of the comorbid relationship between depression and DKD, and identifying key risk factors for their progression; (2) guiding global public health policies on mental health and CKD prevention, especially in vulnerable populations; (3) informing the development of targeted interventions to reduce the burden of depression and DKD, particularly in older adults; and (4) promoting further research on the causal relationship between depression and DKD, using advanced methods such as MR analysis.

Relationship between depressive symptoms and DKD

Multivariable logistic regression indicated that diabetic patients with depressive symptoms had significantly higher odds of prevalent DKD than those without depressive symptoms. In the unadjusted model, depressive symptoms were associated with more than a 2-fold increase in the odds of DKD (odds ratio [OR]: 2.17; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.76–2.68, p < 0.001). After full adjustment for all covariates (adjust II), the association remained and was slightly strengthened (OR: 2.19; 95% CI: 1.49–3.22, p < 0.001). A dose-response pattern was observed: compared with minimal symptoms, moderately severe-to-severe depressive symptoms were associated with the highest odds of prevalent DKD (unadjusted OR: 3.10; 95% CI: 2.12–4.52, p < 0.001), whereas mild depressive symptoms were associated with modestly but significantly higher odds (unadjusted OR: 1.78; 95% CI: 1.45–2.18, p < 0.001). These gradients persisted after multivariable adjustment (Table 3). Sensitivity analyses using component-specific outcomes showed that the association between depressive symptoms and DKD was primarily driven by the albuminuric phenotype. Results for the albuminuria-defined DKD were consistent with those observed in primary clinical and severity-based analyses. In contrast, no statistically significant association was detected for reduced eGFR-defined DKD (p > 0.05) (Tables S2 and S3).

Subgroup analysis

We examined whether age, sex, race/ethnicity, and other key covariates modified the cross-sectional association between depressive symptoms and prevalent DKD (Figure 7). In these models, the depressive symptom score was treated as a continuous variable, and all interaction tests and stratum-specific associations were performed using the fully adjusted model (adjust II). A statistically significant interaction was detected for sex (p interaction < 0.05); the effect of depressive symptoms on DKD was stronger in men (adjusted OR: 1.09; 95% CI: 1.06–1.11 per unit increase in the depressive symptom score) than in women. Educational attainment and hypertension also emerged as potential effect modifiers. No evidence of effect modification was found for age group, race/ethnicity, poverty-income ratio (PIR), body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol use, hyperlipidemia, or hyperuricemia (all p interaction > 0.05). Furthermore, a complementary subgroup analysis treating depressive symptoms as a categorical variable based on depressive symptom severity was conducted, which yielded results highly consistent with the continuous model (Figure S1).

MR analysis

We conducted a series of forward MR analyses to assess potential causal effect of depression on DKD. These analyses leveraged three depression phenotypes across two ancestral populations. Instrumental single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were selected at phenotype-specific significance thresholds (depressed affect: p < 5 × 10−8; major depressive disorder [MDD]: p < 5 × 10−6; East Asian depression: p < 1 × 10−5). To ensure instrument independence, we applied linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping (r2 < 0.001; window = 10,000 kb). All instruments exhibited sufficient strength (F statistics >10). We performed rigorous quality control and data harmonization protocols to ensure the validity of MR analyses. Using PhenoScanner v2, we excluded SNPs showing genome-wide significant associations (p < 5 × 10−8) with potential confounders (BMI, hypertension, and other metabolic traits) (Tables S4, S9, and S22). During data harmonization, we used the MR_harmonise package to calculate correct_causal_direction and steiger_pval, confirming that the genetic instruments were consistent with the expected causal direction (Tables S5, S10, S14, S18, S23, and S27). Heterogeneity was assessed using the MR_heterogeneity package with Q_pval; non-significant values (p > 0.05) indicated instrument consistency across datasets (Tables S6, S11, S15, S19, S24, and S28). Pleiotropy was evaluated via the MR_pleiotropy_test package calculating MR-Egger intercept test; non-significant p values (p > 0.05) suggested the absence of directional pleiotropy (Tables S7, S12, S16, S20, S25, and S29). Instrument strength was quantified using SNP R2 and F statistics (Tables S8, S13, S17, S21, S26, and S30). F statistic >10 indicated strong instruments, minimizing weak instrument bias and ensuring adequate statistical power. These comprehensive quality control measures supported the robustness of our MR results, with no significant heterogeneity or pleiotropy detected.

Depressed affect and DKD in Europeans

Genetically predicted depressed affect (ebi-a-GCST006475) was used as the exposure, and DKD (ebi-a-GCST90018832) as the outcome. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) analysis indicated a nominally significant association between genetically predicted depressed affect and DKD (OR: 2.452; 95% CI: 1.061–5.671; p = 0.036), while the weighted median method also yielded a statistically significant estimate (OR: 3.709; 95% CI: 1.146–12.003; p = 0.029). To limit horizontal pleiotropy, we repeated the analysis after excluding SNPs associated with established DKD risk factors. After excluding SNPs strongly associated with traditional metabolic risk factors, the IVW estimate attenuated to borderline significance (OR: 2.383; 95% CI: 0.976–5.816; p = 0.056) (Table S4; Table 4), suggesting diminished statistical support for a robust causal effect. Although the weighted median method remained statistically significant (p = 0.031), with consistent effect direction, the overall evidence warrants cautious interpretation. Visual diagnostic analyses confirmed the robustness of our findings. Scatterplots illustrate the relationship between SNP effects on depressive phenotypes (depressed affect, MDD, and depression) and DKD. Effect size plots demonstrate estimates for each SNP across different analysis methods (IVW and MR-Egger), reflecting methodological consistency. Funnel plots show no apparent asymmetry, indicating minimal heterogeneity. Leave-one-out analyses validated result stability by demonstrating that no single SNP disproportionately influenced causal estimates (Figure 8). Collectively, these diagnostics indicated no significant bias or weak instrument effects, supporting the reliability of our MR estimates.

MDD and DKD in Europeans

To assess whether the observed signal generalized to clinically defined depression, we used MDD (ebi-a-GCST90086058) as the exposure. Instrumental SNPs were selected at a p value < 5 × 10−6, and one variant (rs78260322) was excluded on the basis of associations with potential confounders recorded in PhenoScanner (Table S9). After LD-clumping and harmonization, the remaining instruments were carried forward to MR analyses. Across all methods, including IVW (OR: 1.108; 95% CI: 0.917–1.338; p = 0.288), MR-Egger, weighted-median and mode-based estimators, no evidence of a causal effect on DKD was detected (Table 4). Sensitivity analyses showed no indication of heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy. Thus, the modest causal signal detected for depressive affect does not extend to the categorical diagnosis of MDD, underscoring the phenotypic distinction between continuous mood symptoms and clinical disorder.

Depression and DKD in East Asians

Exploratory two-sample MR was performed using East Asian genome-wide association Study (GWAS) summary statistics for depression (ebi-a-GCST90018613) and DKD (ebi-a-GCST90018612). All estimators yielded null results (IVW, OR: 1.134; 95% CI: 0.811–1.588; p = 0.462), and sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of these findings (Table 4). The absence of a detectable effect in this population highlights possible cross-ancestry heterogeneity and the urgent need for larger, well-powered GWASs in non-European ancestries.

Reverse MR analyses

Reverse MR analyses, with DKD as the exposure and depression-related phenotypes as outcomes, produced null effects across all method, with no evidence of heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy (Tables S18–S30). Full results are provided in the supplemental information (Figures S2–S4).

MR mediation analyses

MR mediation analyses suggested that BMI may partially mediate the association between depressive affect and DKD, as adjustment for BMI attenuated this relationship (Figure S5). For glycemic traits and inflammatory markers, the required assumptions for genetic mediation were not satisfied; therefore, these results are presented as exploratory analyses in the supplemental information (Figure S5).

Causal analysis using summary effect estimates

Results from the causal analysis using summary effect (CAUSE) framework (Figures 9A–9C) corroborated the primary MR findings. In all three configurations, both the causal and sharing models produced effect estimates tightly distributed around the null, with no suggestion of either forward or reverse causation between depression and DKD. The expected log pointwise predictive density values were virtually identical between models, indicating equivalent fit and providing no statistical basis for favoring a causal interpretation over a polygenic-sharing mechanism. Collectively, these data indicate that the observed genetic covariance between depression-related phenotypes and DKD is best explained by the shared polygenic architecture, rather than directional causality, and further argue against meaningful, unmeasured confounding or pleiotropy-driven biases.

Discussion

This study harmonized data from the GBD study, NHANES, and multilevel MR to examine the depression-DKD relationship from three complementary angles: population-level trends, observational associations, and genetic causality.

GBD 1990–2023 showed a parallel, sustained rise in age-standardized rates of MDD, CKD due to T1DM, and CKD due to T2DM in the United States (all EAPC > 0). These synchronized upward trajectories may reflect shared macro-level environmental or biological influences and warrant further investigation into a potential pathophysiologic link.

In NHANES, depressive symptoms were positively and monotonically associated with prevalent DKD in cross-sectional analyses, even after adjustment for sociodemographics, lifestyle factors, glycemic control, and comorbidities (p trend <0.001). The observation that the depression-DKD association was specific to the albuminuric rather than the filtration-based phenotype suggests that depressive symptoms may be more closely linked to early glomerular or tubular dysfunction than to advanced filtration impairment. Although the underlying mechanisms remain speculative, this pattern aligns with the hypothesis that behavioral factors and systemic inflammation may confer renal vulnerability prior to measurable renal decline.

In addition, we identified a significant sex interaction in the depressive symptom-DKD association, with stronger effects observed in men (p < 0.05). The observed sex interaction—wherein depressive symptoms exhibited a stronger association with prevalent DKD in men—likely reflects the convergence of biological and sociobehavioral mechanisms. Biologically, men tend to demonstrate greater visceral adiposity and insulin resistance at comparable BMI levels, potentially amplifying stress-related metabolic dysregulation.18,19 Sociobehaviorally, lower rates of help-seeking and delayed depression treatment among men may prolong exposure to chronic physiological stress.20 Additionally, sex-specific differences in depression phenotypic expression—including greater externalizing symptoms and poorer glycemic treatment adherence in men—may further exacerbate glycemic instability and renal vulnerability. The interplay of these factors may collectively contribute to the heightened DKD risk observed in men with depressive symptoms. Finally, the robust dose-response pattern observed across the depressive symptom severity suggests that shared behavioral, neuroendocrine, or metabolic pathways may underpin the observed association. Observational data, however, cannot exclude residual confounding or reverse causation. This sets the stage for further exploration of causality through MR analyses. Two-sample MR was, therefore, performed with three depression phenotypes: (1) continuous depressed-affect score, (2) lifetime MDD diagnosis, and (3) an East Asian depression GWAS. MR analysis of depressed affect and DKD in the European population indicated that after excluding SNPs associated with metabolic risk factors, the IVW estimate attenuated to borderline significance (OR: 2.383: p = 0.056), while the weighted median method remained significant (p = 0.031). Results across MR methods were directionally consistent. However, although the genetic instruments demonstrated sufficient strength (F statistics >10), statistical power was limited, and the overall evidence remained weak, failing to support a direct causal relationship. In contrast, analyses using MDD as the exposure yielded no significant causal effect. These divergent results likely reflect differences in phenotypic heterogeneity and instrument strength. Continuous depressive symptom traits (e.g., depressed affect) capture variation across the full symptom spectrum and yield more powerful genetic instruments. In contrast, clinically diagnosed MDD represents a coarse binary phenotype with substantial heterogeneity and potential misclassification, which may attenuate GWAS signals and weaken instrument strength. Similarly, exploratory MR analyses in East Asian populations did not detect significant causal effects, possibly due to limited sample size and the relatively low heritability of DKD in this population, compromising statistical power.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated a strong association between depression and DKD across diverse populations. In a cohort of 847 Indian patients with T2DM, the prevalence of depression was significantly higher among those with DKD than in those without DKD.21 Similarly, other studies have reported that individuals with depression are more likely to exhibit diabetic complications such as retinopathy and dyslipidemia.22 Among older adults with diabetes, severely reduced kidney function (eGFR ≤29 mL/min/1.73 m2) has been associated with a markedly higher prevalence of depression.23 Systematic evidence further indicates that depression is associated with a higher burden of diabetic complications and mortality,24,25,26,27 with particularly strong associations observed for macrovascular complications.28 Patients with depression also tend to have a longer duration of diabetes and a greater burden of comorbid complications.29,30 Notably, a recent systematic review suggested that depression may be more strongly associated with the subsequent development of diabetic complications than with the reverse temporal sequence, underscoring the potentially pivotal role of depressive symptoms in the progression of diabetes-related complications.31 Collectively, these findings, along with our own, support a robust and consistent link between depression and DKD.

Mechanistically, depression may influence kidney health through multiple interrelated biological and behavioral pathways. Prior research has linked depression to chronic low-grade inflammation, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation, heightened sympathetic nervous system activity, and impaired central serotonergic signaling.32,33,34,35 These alterations contribute to metabolic disturbances—including hypertension, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and visceral adiposity36,37 —all of which are established risk factors for DKD. Depression is also associated with poor treatment adherence and self-care behaviors, leading to suboptimal glycemic control and unhealthy lifestyle patterns,38,39,40,41,42 thereby accelerating DKD progression (Figure 10). Furthermore, different classes of antidepressants exert heterogeneous metabolic effects; for instance, fluoxetine has been shown to improve glycemic and lipid profiles, whereas sertraline may destabilize glycemic control in individuals with diabetes.43,44,45 These observations underscore the complex interplay between psychological treatment, metabolic regulation, and pathways relevant to DKD.

Overall, this study integrates population-level trends, observational associations, and genetic causal inference to delineate a complex relationship between depression and DKD. Depression may act as a systemic correlate of DKD through behavioral, metabolic, neuroendocrine, and inflammatory pathways. However, MR analyses provided largely null evidence across depression phenotypes and ancestral populations, with a suggestive association observed only between depressed affect and DKD in Europeans. MR mediation further revealed heterogeneity across candidate biological intermediates. Adjustment for BMI attenuated the depression-DKD association, suggesting a potential partial mediating role. The strong observational associations from NHANES, contrasted with the largely null MR findings, suggest that the depression-DKD relationship may be partially attributable to residual confounding from shared risk factors rather than robust genetic causality. Overall, the triangulated evidence suggests that the depression-DKD relationship is more likely driven by behavioral and environmental mechanisms, rather than strong genetic causation. Depression may influence DKD through behavioral pathways—including treatment adherence, lifestyle factors, and inflammatory processes—not captured by MR, which indexes only lifelong genetically influenced liability and fails to reflect environmentally triggered or context-dependent depressive states arising from chronic disease. This limitation is particularly relevant for “reactive depression due to chronic disease burden,” a clinically important subtype that may explain the discrepancy between observational and MR results. Modifiable factors such as physical activity are associated with depression outcomes and may subsequently influence DKD progression.46 Methodological considerations may also contribute to this inconsistency: residual pleiotropy cannot be entirely excluded despite PhenoScanner filtering; limited statistical power in DKD GWAS may hinder detection of modest effects; and heterogeneity in DKD phenotype definitions across studies may compromise comparability.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, a significant proportion of NHANES participants were excluded due to missing PHQ-9 or DKD data (Figure 11), which may introduce selection bias, although baseline characteristics remained balanced in the final analytic sample. Second, both the observational and GWAS datasets were predominantly composed of individuals of European ancestry, limiting the generalizability of our findings to other populations. We included East Asian MR analysis as an exploratory complement; although underpowered to detect only large effects, these analyses provide preliminary information on cross-ancestry effect directionality. Third, neither NHANES nor the currently available DKD GWAS datasets distinguish between T1DM and T2DM, precluding subtype-specific analyses. Future DKD GWAS should be stratified by diabetes subtype and encompass more ancestrally diverse populations to enhance genetic resolution and generalizability. Furthermore, integrated multiomics approaches—such as transcriptomic or metabolomic MR—may help elucidate biological pathways beyond traditional risk factors like BMI. Finally, recent studies highlight the importance of addressing biases in observational research, particularly immortal time bias, which can distort association interpretation.47 Future research should prioritize identification and adjustment for such biases to ensure accurate characterization of the depression-DKD relationship.

From a translational perspective, pilot implementation studies are warranted to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and clinical effectiveness of integrating routine depression screening into diabetes care settings. While brief instruments such as the PHQ-9 can be readily embedded within existing clinical workflows, further implementation research is needed to determine whether such strategies yield sustained improvements in the metabolic and renal outcomes.

Concurrently, greater attention to psychological distress among patients with DKD, particularly those with advanced disease, supports the implementation of a bidirectional screening strategy. Antidepressant selection should be individualized, as these agents exhibit heterogeneous metabolic effects; tailored prescribing and follow-up management may optimize therapeutic benefits while minimizing metabolic risk. Collectively, these recommendations advocate for translation of these findings into practice by promoting the integration of diabetes management and behavioral health services, aiming to reduce the combined burden of depression and DKD.

Experimental model and study participant details

GBD study population

National disease burden estimates for the United States from 1990 to 2023 were obtained from the GBD 2023 database. These data were used to assess the burden of depressive disorders and DKD. We stratified estimates by age group (5-year intervals), year (1990–2023), and sex to enable trend analyses across time periods.

NHANES study participants

Individual-level data were obtained from the NHANES cycles spanning 2013 to March 2020. After excluding participants with missing PHQ-9 score (n = 16,851), undetermined diabetes or depression status (n = 11,106), no diabetes history (n = 4,953), and missing ACR or eGFR data (n = 460), 2,337 participants were included in the final analysis (Figure 11). NHANES employs a multistage stratified sampling design that ensures national representativeness. The study population comprised non-institutionalized U.S. adults. Demographic information, including sex, age, and self-reported race/ethnicity, was collected via standardized questionnaires.

GWAS data for MR

Three depression-related phenotypes—depressed affect, MDD, and depression—were used as exposures, with DKD as the outcome. These datasets included populations of European and East Asian ancestry. GWAS summary statistics for DKD were obtained from FinnGen and additional publicly available cohorts. Study details, including data sources and sample sizes, are provided in Table S1.

Method details

Institutional approvals and ethics

This study utilized publicly available, de-identified data. No additional institutional review board approval was required. NHANES protocols were approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board (Protocols #2011–17 and #2018–01), with written informed consent obtained from all participants. GBD data are aggregated and publicly available; GWAS summary statistics were derived from studies with prior ethical approval. All analyses adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013).

Exposure and outcome assessment

In the GBD database, the diseases considered were depressive disorders, as well as CKD due to T1DM and T2DM. In NHANES, diabetes was defined by self-reported physician diagnosis, fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, or glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%.50 DKD diagnosis was based on ACR ≥30 mg/g or eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2,51 calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI, race-independent) equation.52 Depressive symptoms were assessed using the PHQ-9. We employed two complementary classification approaches: (1) a binary definition (scores ≥10 indicated clinically significant depressive symptoms) and (2) an ordinal categorization to evaluate the dose-response relationship, with scores stratified into minimal (0–4, reference), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), and moderately severe to severe (≥15).17

In the MR analyses, three depression phenotypes—depressed affect, MDD, and depression—were selected as exposures, with DKD as the outcome. Depressed affect represents a continuous affective trait derived from self-reported mood measures. MDD denotes a clinical diagnosis meeting DSM-5 or ICD criteria. Depression serves as a general term encompassing all symptom severity levels. These definitions reflect heterogeneous diagnostic approaches across cohorts. Phenotype-specific characteristics were derived from respective GWAS summary statistics.53,54,55

NHANES covariates and imputation

Demographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, and clinical variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, PIR, BMI, smoking status, alcohol intake, physical activity, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. Biochemical markers comprised serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, total cholesterol, triglycerides, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, urinary protein, and urinary creatinine. We performed complete case analysis for key variables with <5% missing data, which were confirmed as missing completely at random (MCAR) by Little’s test. Missing covariates were imputed using median values for continuous skewed variables and mode values for categorical variables.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.1), augmented with the EmpowerStats and BioWinford platforms to ensure computational reproducibility. For global burden analysis, we utilized GBD 1990–2023 data to calculate age-standardized rates of incidence, mortality, and DALYs; temporal patterns were assessed via age-period-cohort models and exponential smoothing for 2023–2033 forecasting. For observational analyses in NHANES, multivariable logistic regression was employed to evaluate the association between depressive symptoms (categorized by PHQ-9 scores) and DKD status. All NHANES analyses accounted for the complex multistage probability sampling design by incorporating survey weights, strata, and primary sampling units, with variance estimation via Taylor series linearization.

In the MR framework, causal effects were estimated primarily using the IVW method, supplemented by MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted-mode estimators. Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR-PRESSO global test and MR-Egger intercept test. Sensitivity analyses included the Steiger directionality test and Cochran’s Q statistic for heterogeneity. Additionally, MR–based mediation analyses were performed to explore potential mediation by BMI, glycemic traits, and inflammatory markers. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05. For all figures presenting statistical significance, the specific tests, exact p-values, and sample sizes (N) are defined in the respective legends. Effect estimates (ORs and 95% CIs) are provided in the figures or supplemental materials. Analyses were conducted in R using the TwoSampleMR,48 MR-PRESSO,49 and survey packages in R. Verification of GBD collaboration is provided in Data S1.
