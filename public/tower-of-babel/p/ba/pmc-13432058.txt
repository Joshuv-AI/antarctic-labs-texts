Influence of kinematic motion and instrumentation strategy on apical debris extrusion during root canal preparation: An in vitro study
Authors: Amira Alghazaly, Jumanah Aljohani, Khadijah Mohabat, Rafah Ghous
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Endodontic treatment aims to eliminate infection while maintaining the health of the tooth and surrounding tissues. Apical extrusion of debris during instrumentation is unavoidable and may lead to inflammation, postoperative pain, or delayed healing. The extent and direction of extrusion are influenced by the instrumentation system and motion pattern.Aims:This study aimed to evaluate the effect of kinematic motion (continuous rotary vs. reciprocating) on apical debris extrusion during mechanical root canal preparation.Materials and Methods:Thirty freshly extracted human single-rooted teeth with fully formed apices were standardized in length and mounted in preweighed Eppendorf tubes to collect extruded debris. The samples were randomly assigned into two groups (n = 15 each) and instrumented using either ProTaper Gold (PTG; continuous rotation) or ZenFlex ONE (reciprocating files) according to manufacturer instructions. A single operator performed all instrumentation. Irrigation was carried out using 2.5% sodium hypochlorite. The volume of irrigant was kept consistent for all samples to ensure standardization. After instrumentation, the extruded debris was incubated until complete drying. The debris was then weighed using a high-precision microbalance.Statistical Analysis Used:Mann–Whitney U test was used for statistical analysis; the significance level was set at P < 0.05.Results:PTG produced significantly more apically extruded debris (1.49 ± 0.69 g) than ZenFlex ONE (0.85 ± 0.47 g), with P = 0.009.Conclusions:This study demonstrates that the ZenFlex ONE reciprocating system significantly reduces apical debris extrusion compared to the PTG rotary system. The reciprocating motion appears to favor coronal evacuation of debris, thereby limiting its apical displacement. This may reduce postoperative pain and inflammatory complications, particularly in teeth with necrotic pulps or periapical lesions.

INTRODUCTION

Preservation of apical integrity is a fundamental concern during the mechanical preparation phase of root canal treatment. However, extrusion of dentinal debris is unavoidable during instrumentation. Debris may be transported along the canal and extruded beyond the apical foramen, potentially resulting in postoperative pain and periapical inflammation. Extruded material typically consists of dentin particles, necrotic pulp tissue, irrigant remnants, and microbial biofilm, all of which may induce local inflammatory reactions and compromise treatment outcomes.[12]

Several factors are controlling the amount of debris extrusion, including instrument design, cross-sectional geometry, taper, and particularly the kinematic motion employed during canal treatment.[34]

Continuous rotary systems employ uninterrupted 360° rotation. In converse in reciprocation systems operate with alternating rotational movements, which can affect both the quantity and direction of debris extrusion.[2456]

Optimizing treatment outcomes requires recognizing that the extent of debris extrusion largely depends on the duration of the procedure, which is influenced by the number of instruments used – such as single-file versus multiple-file systems.[6789101112] Thus, evaluating how different instrumentation techniques, file systems, and kinematic motions affect debris extrusion can guide clinicians in selecting approaches that minimize undesirable postoperative complication.

ProTaper Gold (PTG) files were used in the current study as it produces less apical debris extrusion compared to earlier widely used ProTaper Universal systems.[1314] This may be attributed to the advanced heat-treatment metallurgy that enhances safety and efficiency in root canal treatment, as flexibility was improved by 24%, which makes this file system conform better to the natural anatomy of the canal, reducing the potential for ledging, transportation, or straightening of the canal path. Combined with their proprietary heat-treated “gold wire” alloy, allows them to better navigate challenging, curved canals.

The other used system in the current study was ZenFlex ONE reciprocating file system; it is designed to deliver high cutting efficiency, enhanced flexibility, and minimal apical transportation. Its features support a more conservative approach to endodontic shaping by helping to limit the amount of debris extruded apically, which is a common concern with reciprocating systems.[15]

Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of kinematic motion and different metallurgical features on the extruded debris under standardized in vitro conditions. It was hypothesized that no significant difference would be observed in the amount of extruded debris between the two kinematic motions across the different systems used.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Thirty freshly extracted human permanent single-rooted teeth with mature apices and intact root structures were collected, disinfected, and stored in saline until use to prevent dehydration; teeth with multiple canals, open apices, resorption, fractures, calcification, or previous endodontic treatment were excluded. Teeth were collected anonymously after patients signed the informed consents. Sample size was determined using G*Power software (G*Power (Version 3.1.9.7), Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, North Rhine-Westphalia,German) assuming a large effect size (0.8), power of 80%, and α = 0.05. The minimum required sample was 15 specimens per group; therefore, 20 samples were used in each group to increase reliability. Eppendorf glass (Royal Falcon Glass Bottle with Cork, 22 mm × 95 mm, clear; Royal Falcon) was preweighed before embedding the teeth. Teeth were placed directly inside acrylic balls using a self-curing acrylic resin prepared by mixing VITA opener liquid with Vertex powder (Vertex Dental and VITA Zahnfabrik) to stabilize them. Weights were recorded at multiple stages: before inserting the teeth, following tooth and acrylic insertion, after tooth preparation, then after cleaning and shaping, and finally after drying in the incubator (Zenithlab IB-9025A Laboratory Incubator; Zenithlab). Tooth preparation was done using a round carbide bur, SIZE #2 with head diameter 1.00 mm. Root canal preparation was performed using PTG rotary files (Dentsply Sirona) with a SybronEndo motor (SybronEndo) set at 350 rpm, and 2 N.cm torque, and 8:1 reduction ratio, and ZenFlex ONE reciprocating files (Kerr Corporation). Irrigation was performed with 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl; Clorox®, Clorox). Extruded debris was dried and weighed using a digital microbalance (Generic) with a capacity of 3000 g and 0.1 g accuracy. This methodology follows previously established protocols for measuring apical debris extrusion.[21617] To minimize apical extrusion during instrumentation, several strategies were implemented. A crown-down instrumentation technique was employed to reduce apical debris accumulation. Frequent and copious irrigation was performed throughout the procedure to facilitate debris removal. Additionally, appropriate file sizes were carefully selected to maintain apical patency while preventing unnecessary extrusion. Conventional coronal access cavities were prepared using a high-speed bur under water cooling, and working length was determined by introducing a #10 K-file into each canal until the file tip became visible at the apical foramen, and the working length was established by subtracting 1 mm from this measurement. The use of a glide path was implemented in this study for all the used files, as it has been shown to reduce procedural errors and preserve the canal pathway.[18]

Each tooth was mounted through a rubber stopper into a preweighed Eppendorf tube. The stopper–tooth assembly was then tightly fitted into the neck of the tube and stabilized around the cementoenamel junction region to prevent lateral leakage while allowing only apical extrusion into the tube, with a 27-gauge needle inserted to equalize internal and external pressure. Each tube was coded, and its initial weight was recorded with a precision microbalance. The teeth were then randomly allocated into two groups (n = 15 each). Group A was instrumented with PTG in continuous rotation following the manufacturer’s sequence and Group B with ZenFlex ONE in reciprocating motion per manufacturer protocol. All canals were instrumented by a single experienced operator using gentle in-and-out pecking motions without forcing the instruments to working length, and canal patency was verified with a #10 K-file between instruments. Canals were irrigated using 10 mL 2.5% NaOCl after each file: one syringe delivered the irrigant coronally while the second syringe gently flushed the apical third to avoid pressure build-up.[1920] After final irrigation, debris adhering to the root apex was flushed into the Eppendorf tube using 1 mL distilled water. Tubes were incubated at 70°C for 5 days to evaporate moisture from the collected samples without causing thermal degradation or weight alteration of the solid debris. This temperature is considered sufficiently high to dry the samples efficiently while remaining low enough to avoid burning, decomposition, or loss of organic and inorganic components, thereby ensuring accurate measurements.[217] Dry heat block incubator was routinely calibrated and used according to manufacturer guidelines.

The tubes were then allowed to cool to room temperature. The dry weight of the extruded debris was calculated by subtracting the pre-instrumentation weight from the post-instrumentation weight using a precision microbalance. To reduce measurement bias, the investigator performing the weighing procedures was blinded to group allocation. The sample size (n = 15 per group) was chosen to be comparable with previous in vitro studies evaluating apically extruded debris using similar methodology and nickel–titanium (NiTi) systems. Ethical approval was obtained from Taibah University, collage of Dentistry, Research Ethics committee, Approval Number 281024, Date: October 24, 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The confidence interval was set to 95%, and the margin of error accepted was set to 5%. Hence, the P value was considered statistically significant as follows: the significance level was set at P ≤ 0.05.

DISCUSSION

Apical debris extrusion was observed regardless of the kinematic approach used, which is consistent with previous studies.[212223]

However, the results of the present study demonstrated that the reciprocating file system produced significantly less apical debris extrusion compared to the rotary system.[242526]

This suggests that the kinematics of reciprocating motion may be more effective in limiting the extrusion of debris beyond the apical foramen in agreement with earlier reports.[2728]

These findings may be further explained by the mechanics of reciprocating instrumentation, which operates through alternating clockwise and counterclockwise movements. This motion reduces torsional stress and the risk of instrument separation.[272930] In addition, the controlled-memory NiTi alloy enhances flexibility, allowing better adaptation to curved canals with minimal deviation.[30] Moreover, by preventing continuous apical advancement of the cutting blades, reciprocating motion may limit the apical transportation of debris, thereby reducing the amount of apically extruded material.

In contrast, other studies[23132] have reported superior performance of continuous rotary systems over reciprocating systems in terms of debris extrusion. These discrepancies may be attributed to variations in instrument motion dynamics. Additionally, differences in file design, taper, cross-sectional geometry, cutting efficiency, irrigation protocols, and the number of instruments used may further influence these outcomes and explain inconsistencies among studies.[33]

Aiming to investigate the stated hypothesis, two different NiTi systems were used: PTG and ZenFlex ONE. Each system possesses distinct design and metallurgical characteristics that may influence debris extrusion behavior. PTG, a continuously rotating system, is characterized by a progressive taper and a convex triangular cross-section, which enhance its cutting efficiency. Furthermore, the absence of radial lands increases its dentin-cutting ability, resulting in greater dentin removal and canal enlargement. This may explain the findings of the present study, where a significantly higher amount of apically extruded debris was observed with PTG compared to the reciprocating ZenFlex ONE system, in agreement with previous studies.[3435]

Continuous rotation is used in our work as multi-file systems (PTG) enlarge the canal sequentially, with each instrument generating dentin debris. Repeated file insertion increases debris production and promotes apical extrusion, as successive instruments may compact debris toward the foramen, especially with inadequate irrigation or maintained patency. In contrast, single-file systems ZenFlex ONE reciprocating file system require fewer passes, resulting in less dentin removal and reduced apical debris transportation. Consequently, multi-instrument techniques are generally associated with greater apical debris extrusion than single-file systems.[30]

The greater variable taper design used in PTG may result in the removal of a larger volume of dentin both coronally and apically compared with the constant 0.04 taper of the ZenFlex ONE reciprocating file system, which was found in agreement with a previous study.[36] This increased dentin removal may generate a greater amount of debris within the canal space. Furthermore, the reciprocating system evaluated in this study features a smaller maximum flute diameter, which may help minimize canal transportation and preserve the original root canal anatomy. Consequently, improved preservation of periapical tissues and reduced procedural errors may be anticipated.[13738]

NaOCl was used in this study to better simulate clinical conditions, as it is considered the most effective irrigating solution. It was delivered using two syringes targeting both the coronal and apical portions of the canal to improve flushing action, enhance disinfection, and reduce the microbial load in extruded debris.[39]

Although NaOCl does not completely prevent apical extrusion, its strong tissue-dissolving and antibacterial properties help minimize the risk of periapical infection when small amounts of debris are extruded. This contributes to improved patient comfort and supports periapical healing.

Several strategies were adopted in the current work to keep the extrusion minimal following what was reported in the previous findings: the use of crown-down instrumentation techniques, frequent and copious irrigation, and the selection of appropriate file sizes to maintain apical patency.[40]

Despite the advancements in NiTi instrumentation systems, complete prevention of apical debris extrusion remains impossible. Therefore, clinicians should consider the selection of instrumentation techniques that minimize debris extrusion and reduce the risk of postoperative complications. Further studies are recommended to evaluate the clinical implications of debris extrusion and its relation to postoperative pain.

CONCLUSIONS

Within the limitations of our study, ZenFlex ONE reciprocating file systems produced significantly less apically extruded debris than PTG as a continuous rotary system. This outcome may be attributed to the reciprocating motion’s alternating cutting and releasing action, which promotes coronal transportation of debris and reduces its compaction toward the apex. In contrast, continuous rotary motion tends to act like a screw conveyor, pushing debris apically. Therefore, reciprocating systems may be a more suitable choice in clinical situations where minimizing apical extrusion – and consequently reducing the risk of postoperative pain and inflammation – is of particular importance, such as in teeth with periapical lesions or necrotic pulps.
