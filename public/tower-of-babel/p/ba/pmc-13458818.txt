Associations of paraspinal muscle quantity and quality with patient-reported outcomes in patients aged ≥85 years undergoing lumbar spine surgery: an exploratory retrospective study
Authors: Shutaro Yamada, Sadaaki Kanayama, Tsuyoshi Kono, Shota Takenaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPopulation aging has driven an increase in lumbar spine surgery among the super-elderly. The associations of preoperative paraspinal muscle quantity and quality with patient-reported outcomes in patients aged ≥85 years undergoing lumbar spine surgery remain unclear. We therefore examined these associations at baseline and 1 year after surgery.MethodsThis is a retrospective observational study. Forty-three patients aged ≥85 years undergoing surgery for degenerative lumbar disorders were studied. Preoperative L3 muscle quantity [paraspinal muscle index (PMI)] and quality [computed tomography (CT) attenuation (Hounsfield units, HU); magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based Goutallier grading] were assessed. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were collected preoperatively and at 1 year. Associations at baseline, 1 year, and change from baseline (Δ) were examined using Spearman correlations. Multivariable linear and baseline-adjusted Firth logistic regression assessed the preoperative Japanese Orthopaedic Association Back Pain Evaluation Questionnaire (JOABPEQ) low back pain score and treatment effectiveness, respectively. An exploratory receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis evaluated HU to identify low preoperative JOABPEQ low back pain scores (≤29).ResultsPMI showed no statistically detectable association with any PROM in this sample. In separate exploratory multivariable models, HU and Goutallier grade showed nominal associations with the preoperative JOABPEQ low back pain score, whereas no statistically detectable association was observed for PMI; no muscle measure showed a statistically detectable association with treatment effectiveness after baseline adjustment. HU correlated positively with preoperative JOABPEQ low back pain (ρ=0.379, P=0.02), whereas Goutallier grade correlated inversely with the same PROM (ρ=−0.374, P=0.02) and with preoperative 8-Item Short Form Health Survey physical component summary (SF-8 PCS) (ρ=−0.307, P=0.048). In exploratory ROC analysis, HU showed fair discrimination for low JOABPEQ low back pain scores [area under the curve (AUC) =0.740, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.586–0.893]; the data-derived cutoff of 33.7 HU requires external validation and is not ready for clinical application.ConclusionsIn this small, single-center exploratory study, HU and Goutallier grade showed nominal associations with baseline pain-related disability, whereas PMI showed no statistically detectable association. No muscle measure showed a statistically detectable association with treatment effectiveness after baseline adjustment. Given limited power, no multiplicity adjustment, and potential selection bias, these findings require validation and should not guide preoperative decision-making.Keywords: Super-elderly, paraspinal muscle, myosteatosis, patient-reported outcomes, lumbar spine surgery

Highlight box

Key findings

• In separate exploratory multivariable models, computed tomography (CT) attenuation and Goutallier grade showed nominal associations with the preoperative Japanese Orthopaedic Association Back Pain Evaluation Questionnaire (JOABPEQ) low back pain score, whereas no statistically detectable association was observed for paraspinal muscle index (PMI).

• No muscle measure showed a statistically detectable association with JOABPEQ low back pain treatment effectiveness in the parsimonious baseline-adjusted models; fully adjusted sensitivity analyses yielded the same overall conclusion.

• Exploratory receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.740 and a data-derived cutoff of 33.7 Hounsfield units (HU); external validation is required before clinical application.

What is known and what is new?

• Sarcopenia has been associated with adverse surgical outcomes; however, its relationship with patient-reported outcomes remains unclear, particularly in the super-elderly population.

• This exploratory study found nominal associations between muscle-quality measures and the preoperative JOABPEQ low back pain score, whereas no statistically detectable association was observed for PMI or for any muscle measure with treatment effectiveness after baseline adjustment.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These hypothesis-generating findings require external validation and do not currently support changes in surgical selection or individual operative decision-making.

Introduction

Rapid population aging has increased lumbar spine surgery in super-elderly patients. In Japan, the peak age group shifted from 40–59 years in 1994 to the 70s in 2011 and 2021, with lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) remaining the most common indication (1). As candidacy expands to include patients aged ≥85 years, clinicians must balance perioperative risk with realistic expectations for postoperative function and health-related quality of life (2,3).

Sarcopenia—progressive loss of muscle mass, strength, and function—reflects reduced physiological reserve and is common in very old adults (4,5). In spine surgery, sarcopenia has been associated with increased complications and mortality (6); however, evidence for patient-reported outcomes is mixed (7,8). Recent studies have reported associations between lumbar paraspinal muscle quality and disability in community-dwelling older adults, as well as procedure-dependent associations with postoperative patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) after lumbar decompression (9,10). Most studies include broad age ranges (e.g., ≥65 years), and data specific to patients aged ≥85 years remain limited.

Muscle status can be characterized by quantity and quality. We used the paraspinal muscle index (PMI) as a computed tomography (CT)-based measure of muscle quantity (mass) (11), and CT attenuation [Hounsfield units (HU)] (12) together with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based Goutallier classification of fatty infiltration as complementary measures of muscle quality (13). In patients aged ≥85 years, the associations of these imaging-derived muscle measures with PROMs before and after lumbar spine surgery remain unclear.

Therefore, we conducted an exploratory study to examine the associations of preoperative PMI, HU, and Goutallier grade with PROMs at baseline and 1 year. We additionally explored associations with change scores and baseline-adjusted Japanese Orthopaedic Association Back Pain Evaluation Questionnaire (JOABPEQ) low back pain treatment effectiveness. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0112/rc).

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the institutional review board of Japan Community Healthcare Organization Osaka Hospital (approval No. 2025-021). Informed consent was obtained using an opt-out approach (information posted on the institutional website).

Study design and patients

This single-center, retrospective observational study used a surgical case registry at Japan Community Healthcare Organization Osaka Hospital. We screened patients aged ≥85 years who underwent surgery for degenerative lumbar spine disorders between January 2018 and December 2024. Eligible diagnoses included LSS, degenerative spondylolisthesis (DS), lumbar foraminal stenosis (LFS), lumbar disc herniation (LDH), and isthmic spondylolisthesis (IS). Standard lumbar procedures were included; major deformity-related procedures (vertebral osteotomy, deformity correction, and vertebral body replacement) were excluded. The number of levels and the use of endoscopic techniques were unrestricted.

Of 67 consecutive patients, 24 were excluded [infection, n=1; osteoporotic vertebral fracture or diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH), n=9; major complications, n=5; prior multiple lumbar operations involving the index and/or adjacent levels, n=6; loss to follow-up within 1 year, n=2; reoperation within 1 year, n=1], leaving 43 eligible patients. Surgery-related variables (procedure type, operative time, blood loss, and number of fused levels) and baseline comorbidity burden [updated Charlson Comorbidity Index (uCCI)] were extracted from the medical records (14).

CT- and MRI-based paraspinal muscle quantity and quality

Although the L3 skeletal muscle area correlates strongly with whole-body skeletal muscle mass (15), routine preoperative lumbar CT often has a limited scanning range, making comprehensive assessment that includes abdominal wall musculature difficult. Therefore, we used PMI as an index of regional lumbar muscle quantity. PMI comprised the bilateral psoas major, quadratus lumborum, multifidus, and erector spinae muscles, whereas HU and Goutallier grade were assessed in the bilateral multifidus and erector spinae muscles (11). Preoperative CT scans were obtained using a standardized protocol (slice thickness, 0.5 mm; tube voltage, 120 kV).

On a single axial CT slice at the mid-L3 vertebral body level, the cross-sectional areas of the psoas major, quadratus lumborum, multifidus, and erector spinae were manually traced bilaterally. The summed area was defined as the L3 paraspinal muscle area (L3 PMA). PMI was calculated as follows:

The summed regional lumbar muscle area was normalized by height squared to obtain the PMI. Measurements were performed on preoperative CT images acquired for routine clinical purposes. Muscle quality was assessed using preoperative CT attenuation (HU) as a surrogate of myosteatosis, with lower HU values reflecting greater intramuscular lipid content (16). Mean HU was obtained by averaging the subfascial attenuation of the bilateral multifidus and erector spinae at mid-L3. On axial T1-weighted MRI at mid-L3, fatty infiltration of the lumbar paraspinal muscles was graded using the Goutallier classification (13).

Two blinded observers independently measured PMI and HU on CT images (Synapse 5; FUJIFILM, Tokyo, Japan) and graded Goutallier classification on MRI. Measurements were repeated after a 2-week washout to assess intraobserver reliability. For the association analyses, the first measurement obtained by observer 1 was used for PMI, HU, and Goutallier grade.

PROMs

PROMs were evaluated preoperatively and at 1 year postoperatively using validated Japanese versions of the JOABPEQ (17), the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) (18), the Zurich Claudication Questionnaire (ZCQ) (19), and the SF-8 Health Survey (20). Pain intensity was assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS; 0–10, 0= no pain, 10= worst imaginable) for low back pain and buttock/leg pain and numbness. The 1-year postoperative time point was chosen because 12-month patient-reported outcomes closely reflect 24-month outcomes (21), whereas longer follow-up in super-elderly patients may be confounded by age-related conditions unrelated to lumbar disease.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), except for Firth penalized logistic regression, which was performed using R 4.5.2. All P values were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered nominally statistically significant. Inter- and intraobserver reliability for continuous CT-based measures (PMI and HU) was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) (two-way random-effects model, absolute agreement, single-measurement ICC), and reliability for the ordinal Goutallier classification using weighted kappa with quadratic weights; point estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. ICCs and weighted kappa values were interpreted using conventional benchmarks.

Given the number of outcomes and muscle measures examined, all association analyses were considered exploratory. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied; therefore, the reported P values are nominal and were interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Associations between paraspinal muscle measures (PMI, HU, and Goutallier grade) and PROMs were examined for preoperative scores, 1-year postoperative scores, and change from baseline (Δ=1-year postoperative minus preoperative) using Spearman rank correlations (ρ). The strength of correlations was interpreted according to Cohen’s guidelines: |ρ| <0.30= small, 0.30–0.49= moderate, and ≥0.50= large (22). To assess adjusted associations with the preoperative JOABPEQ low back pain score, three separate exploratory multiple linear regression models were fitted. Each model included one muscle measure (PMI, HU, or Goutallier grade) together with age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and uCCI. Diagnosis and surgical procedure were not included because of the limited sample size and the risk of overfitting. Unstandardized regression coefficients (B) with 95% CIs were reported.

Because the 1-year JOABPEQ low back pain score showed a marked ceiling effect, an additional baseline-adjusted responder analysis was performed. Treatment effectiveness was defined according to the JOABPEQ criterion as an increase of ≥20 points from the preoperative score or a postoperative score of ≥90 points when the preoperative score was <90 points (17). Patients whose preoperative and postoperative scores were both ≥90 were excluded from the responder analysis. For the parsimonious analysis, each muscle measure was entered separately into a Firth penalized logistic regression model together with the preoperative JOABPEQ low back pain score. As a sensitivity analysis, fully adjusted models additionally included age, sex, BMI, and uCCI. Odds ratios (ORs) with profile penalized-likelihood 95% CIs were reported. Given the small outcome groups and the number of model parameters, both analyses were considered exploratory, and the fully adjusted models were interpreted as sensitivity analyses because of the risk of overfitting.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed as a post hoc exploratory analysis to evaluate the ability of HU to identify patients with low preoperative JOABPEQ low back pain scores. The low-score threshold of ≤29 points was selected from the observed score distribution and was not prespecified. Low JOABPEQ low back pain score was designated as the positive state, with lower HU values indicating a positive test. The area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% CI were calculated, and the Youden index was used to identify a sample-specific cutoff.

For change scores (Δ=1-year postoperative minus preoperative), a positive value indicates improvement for scales in which higher scores represent better status (e.g., JOABPEQ and SF-8), whereas a negative value indicates improvement for scales in which higher scores represent worse status (e.g., VAS, ODI, and ZCQ).

No a priori sample-size calculation was performed because the sample size was fixed by the number of eligible patients during the study period. With a typical available-case sample of approximately 40 patients, the minimum detectable correlation was approximately |ρ|=0.43 at 80% power and a two-sided α of 0.05; smaller associations may therefore have gone undetected.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

Forty-three patients aged ≥85 years were included (21 men; median age, 86 years; Table 1). Diagnoses consisted of LSS (n=19), DS (n=15), LFS (n=5), LDH (n=3), and IS (n=1). Surgical procedures included decompression alone (n=23) and fusion surgery with or without decompression (n=20). PROM completion rates ranged from 90.7% to 97.7% (39/43–42/43) preoperatively, from 93.0% to 100% (40/43–43/43) at 1 year, and from 88.4% to 95.3% (38/43–41/43) for change scores. Intra- and interobserver reliability

Interobserver reliability was high across both measurements. For the first measurement, the ICCs were 0.742 (95% CI, 0.376–0.906) for PMI and 0.842 (95% CI, 0.140–0.959) for HU, with a weighted kappa of 0.834 (95% CI, 0.658–0.960) for the Goutallier grade. For the second measurement, the ICCs were 0.937 (95% CI, 0.823–0.978) for PMI and 0.899 (95% CI, 0.466–0.972) for HU, with a weighted kappa of 0.785 (95% CI, 0.588–0.921) for the Goutallier grade. Intraobserver reliability was similarly high: for PMI, ICCs were 0.914 (95% CI: 0.768–0.970) for rater 1 and 0.751 (95% CI: 0.419–0.908) for rater 2; for HU, ICCs were 0.971 (95% CI: 0.917–0.990) for rater 1 and 0.955 (95% CI: 0.873–0.984) for rater 2; and for Goutallier grade, weighted kappa values were 0.777 (95% CI: 0.593–0.910) for rater 1 and 0.881 (95% CI: 0.731–0.974) for rater 2.

Discussion

In this exploratory retrospective cohort of 43 patients aged ≥85 years undergoing surgery for degenerative lumbar spine disorders, we examined preoperative CT- and MRI-derived lumbar muscle measures in relation to PROMs. Three findings deserve emphasis. First, no statistically detectable association was observed between PMI and PROMs in this sample. Second, in separate multivariable linear regression models adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and uCCI, lower HU and higher Goutallier grade showed nominal associations with worse preoperative JOABPEQ low back pain scores. Third, the parsimonious baseline-adjusted Firth models did not identify a statistically detectable association between any muscle measure and JOABPEQ low back pain treatment effectiveness; fully adjusted sensitivity analyses yielded the same overall conclusion for the muscle-measure coefficients. These findings do not establish the absence of an association for PMI or the superiority of muscle quality over muscle quantity.

Prior spine-surgery literature links sarcopenia to higher postoperative complications and mortality (6). However, evidence regarding patient-reported outcomes remains mixed, with some studies reporting worse outcomes (7,23) and others showing minimal impact after decompression or fusion (8,24). In this cohort, no statistically detectable association was observed between PMI and PROMs. Given the fixed sample size and the minimum detectable correlation of approximately |ρ|=0.43, smaller associations may have gone undetected. Restricted variation in regional muscle quantity among very old adults may also have contributed, but this explanation remains speculative. Accordingly, the PMI findings should not be interpreted as evidence that regional lumbar muscle quantity is unrelated to PROMs.

Previous studies in patients with LSS have reported associations between several features of lumbar muscle morphology, including muscle size and composition, and functional status or pain, although the relevant muscle compartment and outcome have varied (25,26). In the present cohort, lower HU values and higher Goutallier grades showed nominal associations with worse preoperative JOABPEQ low back pain scores in separate multivariable models adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and uCCI. However, because PMI and the muscle-quality measures were derived from nonidentical muscle compartments, these findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating that muscle quality is superior to muscle quantity. Biologically, reduced HU values and fatty infiltration may reflect myosteatosis and reduced contractile tissue (16), which may accompany deconditioning and greater vulnerability to pain-related activity restriction (27,28). In the post hoc exploratory ROC analysis, HU yielded an AUC of 0.740 for identifying low preoperative JOABPEQ low back pain scores. The 33.7-HU cutoff was data-derived from this small sample and requires external validation before clinical application. Muscle-quality measures showed nominal associations with the JOABPEQ low back pain score, whereas no statistically detectable association was observed with VAS low back pain. This discrepancy may reflect differences in measurement constructs: JOABPEQ captures pain-related functional impairment, whereas VAS reflects pain intensity alone. This pattern may suggest that these imaging measures relate to pain-related functional limitation rather than pain intensity alone, but this interpretation remains hypothesis-generating.

Interpretation of change scores (Δ) requires caution because they are influenced by baseline severity and may be affected by ceiling or floor effects, mathematical coupling, and regression to the mean (29,30). Accordingly, the unadjusted change-score correlations in this study were regarded as exploratory and were not used to infer prognostic effects. In the parsimonious Firth models, which included one muscle measure and the preoperative JOABPEQ low back pain score, no muscle measure showed a statistically detectable association with treatment effectiveness. Fully adjusted sensitivity analyses that additionally included age, sex, BMI, and uCCI yielded the same overall conclusion for the muscle-measure coefficients. However, the small responder sample and wide CIs preclude concluding that these muscle measures have no prognostic association.

From a clinical standpoint, opportunistic assessment of muscle quality on routine preoperative imaging may help contextualize baseline pain-related disability in patients aged ≥85 years (28,31). However, these exploratory data do not support using HU, Goutallier grade, PMI, or the ROC-derived HU cutoff to select surgical candidates, estimate individual treatment benefit, or guide operative decision-making. Previous studies have reported acceptable outcomes after lumbar surgery in very old adults (32,33), but the present study cannot determine whether muscle quality modifies surgical benefit or whether poor muscle quality should be considered a contraindication, particularly because patients with major complications and early reoperations were excluded.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study with a small, fixed sample. With a typical analyzable sample of approximately 40 patients, correlations smaller than approximately |ρ|=0.43 may have gone undetected; therefore, non-significant findings, particularly those for PMI, should not be interpreted as evidence of no association. Second, multiple muscle measures, PROM domains, and time points were examined without formal adjustment for multiple comparisons. The reported P values are therefore nominal, and both type I and type II errors remain possible. Third, the multivariable linear models used a limited covariate set, and diagnosis and surgical procedure were not included to avoid overfitting. The parsimonious Firth responder models were intentionally restricted to the baseline score and one muscle measure, whereas the fully adjusted models were presented as sensitivity analyses. Firth penalization reduces small-sample bias but does not eliminate overfitting or residual confounding. In addition, because the responder analysis dichotomized the outcome, it estimates a different effect from a continuous baseline-adjusted model and may have reduced information. Fourth, PMI and the muscle-quality measures were derived from nonidentical muscle compartments; therefore, differences in their associations may partly reflect anatomical measurement differences rather than muscle quantity versus quality alone. CT attenuation may vary with acquisition parameters (34), and the ordinal nature of Goutallier grading limits measurement resolution. Fifth, objective physical performance, muscle strength, nutritional status, and broader frailty measures were unavailable, and missing PROM data were analyzed using an available-case approach. Finally, excluding patients with major perioperative complications and early reoperations selected a cohort with a relatively favorable postoperative course and may have led to overestimation of postoperative improvement. Consequently, the results may not generalize to all patients aged ≥85 years undergoing lumbar spine surgery and should not guide individual operative decisions.

Conclusions

In this exploratory cohort of patients aged ≥85 years undergoing lumbar spine surgery, lower HU and higher Goutallier grade showed nominal associations with worse preoperative JOABPEQ low back pain scores in separate adjusted models, whereas PMI showed no statistically detectable association with this score. No muscle measure showed a statistically detectable association with JOABPEQ low back pain treatment effectiveness after adjustment for the baseline score, and fully adjusted sensitivity analyses yielded the same overall conclusion for the muscle measures. These findings do not establish the superiority of muscle quality over muscle quantity. Given the small sample size, multiple testing without formal adjustment, residual confounding, and selection bias, the findings are hypothesis-generating, require external validation, and should not be used to guide surgical selection or individual operative decision-making.
