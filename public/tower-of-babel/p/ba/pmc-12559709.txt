Angle-resolved scatterometry combined with deep learning assisted in-situ monitoring of nanowire doping in molecular beam epitaxy process
Authors: Dekun Yang, Yunfei Song, Wei Liang, Wenze Zhang, Wei Shen, Chong Shen, Kang Liang, Sheng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The sub-atomic precision of molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) allows for highly flexible elemental doping in nanowires (NWs). Optimizing doping quality for specific elements requires a comprehensive understanding of the relationship between process parameters and doping concentrations. This necessitates in-situ monitoring of the doping process to define the corresponding process window. However, the reflection high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED) technique, commonly used during MBE growth, has limited sensitivity to atomic arrangement changes caused by doping and is primarily capable of monitoring the structural quality of the sample. To address this limitation, we propose a nanowire doping concentration measurement method based on angle-resolved scatterometry (ARS). This method captures scattering information across the full angular range of NWs, allowing for high-resolution measurement of doping concentration. Using GaN NWs and AlN films doped with Si as a case study, we measured the Si concentration at different doping temperatures. The results demonstrate that the proposed method achieves a doping concentration resolution of 0.01% and 0.06% within the investigated temperature range. Furthermore, we employed deep learning to establish the relationship between angle-resolved reflectivity and nominal doping concentration. The predictive results indicate that the measurement error is maintained below 0.027%. We also validated the robustness of the method across multiple measurement wavelengths and explored the feasibility of using reduced angle reflectance for neural network training. This work paves the way for in-situ monitoring of nanowire doping processes through ARS, significantly enhancing doping control precision in MBE growth.

Introduction

Molecular Beam Epitaxy (MBE) has emerged as a pivotal technology in materials science, offering unparalleled precision in the composition, structure, and properties of thin film growth1–4. This level of control has revolutionized material synthesis and design, enabling the creation of custom-tailored materials for a broad spectrum of applications, including quantum mechanics5, photonics, energy conversion, and catalysis6–10. Despite the promising potential of emerging thin-film growth technologies11, MBE remains irreplaceable. This is primarily due to its capability for sub-atomic level manipulation, enabling the production of large-area thin-film materials characterized by minimal defects and superior performance12–14. This precise control also enables in-depth exploration of structure-property relationships, a key factor in the advancement of application-oriented materials15–18. However, the quality of MBE growth is highly sensitive to various interacting parameters, where even minor deviations can introduce growth defects19,20. Achieving an optimal balance among these parameters necessitates the use of advanced characterization techniques.

Characterization methods in MBE can be categorized into ex-situ and in-situ techniques. The selection of the appropriate technique is crucial for optimizing growth parameters and enhancing material quality21–23. Traditional ex-situ methods often alter the material’s surface during measurement, leaving imprints that can degrade surface quality and obscure the true nature of the material. It is also known that the transfer and handling of the samples during class 100 clean room may easily induced surface contaiminations24. Moreover, ex-situ techniques fail to account for the dynamic and often unstable nature of the growth environment, potentially leading to inaccurate reflections of the material’s inherent structure and properties25–27. In contrast, in-situ characterization techniques preserve the growth environment, allowing real-time monitoring of dynamic changes in material structure, morphology, and reaction kinetics28. Consequently, in-situ techniques have become increasingly preferred for their ability to provide more accurate and immediate feedback during the MBE process29.

In-situ characterization techniques for MBE are further categorized based on their purpose: microscopic morphology analysis, surface property analysis, phase analysis, and compositional analysis. Among these, compositional analysis plays a critical role in understanding the composition and content of materials, thereby offering practical guidance for process control during growth30. However, commonly used compositional analysis methods, such as scanning tunneling microscopy/spectroscopy (STM/STS) involves time-intensive data acquisition31–33, while techniques like X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) require complex light source equipment, making them less suitable for routine, real-time analysis31–36. Although ellipsometry has shown potential as a compositional analysis tool37,38, its complexity in resolving multiple parameters hinders its applicability in real-time measurements39. These challenges underscore the need for more efficient and accurate compositional analysis techniques in MBE.

To address these limitations, this paper introduces an in-situ compositional analysis technique for MBE based on angle-resolved scatterometry (ARS). This technique employs a straightforward ARS setup to capture reflection data across the entire angular range of a sample in a single measurement. By leveraging deep learning tools, we streamline the inversion process of characterization parameters. Specifically, an artificial neural network (ANN) is trained using angle-resolved spectral correlated with the doping temperature of Si-doped GaN nanowires (NWs) and AlN films. The trained ANN demonstrates the ability to accurately identify multiple GaN NWs and AlN films within a defined doping temperature, achieving an exceptional concentration resolution of 0.01% and 0.06% for different RGB measurement wavelengths. Furthermore, the ANN predicts that the measurement error for Si doping concentration using ARS technology is within an impressive 0.027%. Remarkably, precise Si doping concentration measurements can be achieved using only a few angle-resolved reflectance values. This technique, characterized by its simplicity, computational efficiency, and high resolution, holds significant promise for advancing multifunctional in-situ characterization in the field of MBE.

Results

MBE device configuration and ARS measurement principle

The typical structure of an MBE growth chamber is depicted in Fig. 1a. In this setup, Ga, N, and Si atoms are released from their respective effusion cells and introduced into the growth chamber as molecular beams through a controlled heating process. The substrate, positioned centrally within the chamber, serves as the site for GaN NWs growth and is maintained at a precise temperature using a thermal management system. To monitor the quality of film growth in real-time, a reflection high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED) system is employed. The RHEED gun emits an electron beam that reflects off the substrate surface, producing a diffraction pattern displayed on the RHEED screen. In this study, Si-doped GaN NWs were synthesized using a custom-developed MBE system at Wuhan University (Fig. S1). This system is composed of three main subunits: the growth chamber, the annular manipulator, and the STM unit. The growth chamber provides the necessary high-vacuum environment, while the annular manipulator ensures accurate positioning and control of mechanical components. The STM unit facilitates in-situ characterization, enabling the assessment of growth quality in real time.

The Si doping concentration in GaN NWs is primarily controlled by adjusting the temperature of the Si effusion cell. Notably, the optical properties of the NWs exhibit slight variations at different doping temperatures, the wavelength sensitivity remains constant across the illumination range. As shown in Fig. 1b, variations in the doping temperature lead to changes in the peak intensity of the wavelength-resolved reflectance, but without causing any peak shift. This observation is further validated by photoluminescence (PL) measurements (Fig. S2), which confirm the wavelength insensitivity. These results suggest that traditional wavelength-resolved measurement techniques, such as ellipsometry, exhibit low sensitivity to Si doping concentration in GaN NWs. In contrast, ARS offers a viable alternative, owing to its heightened sensitivity to nanostructures39,40.

The employed ARS system is depicted in Fig. 1c, where the doping concentration is determined by capturing back focal plane (BFP) images at varying Si doping temperatures. The measurement principle of ARS is further elaborated in Fig. S3. Information corresponding to the angles θ and φ for samples with different doping concentrations is projected onto the BFP plane of a high-magnification, high-numerical aperture (NA) objective lens, as shown in Fig. 1d. For specific applications, the measured BFP data must be converted into angle-resolved reflectance data (Fig. 1e) to align with simulation or data reduction requirements. The conversion methodology based on standard samples is detailed in Supplementary Note 1.

Machine-learning based technology to retrieve doping parameters

Light scattering spectra have emerged as a promising tool for distinguishing nanostructures41. These spectra are intrinsically linked to the material’s nanostructure and optical properties, such as doping concentration. In this study, we propose a machine learning approach to retrieve the doping temperature (doping concentration) parameters of NWs from BFP information. A BFP image containing angle-resolved spectra is obtained using the ARS setup depicted in Fig. 2a. These spectra, corresponding to NWs grown at different doping temperatures (Figs. 2b, c), are used to train an ANN (Fig. 2d). To assess the accuracy of this approach, the trained ANN is tested with spectra not included in the training dataset. As illustrated in Fig. 2d, the ANN comprises a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (ConvNet) followed by a fully connected network, a configuration well-suited for pattern recognition tasks. The measured BFP images (angle-resolved spectra) are fed into the input layer, which consists of parallel channels. In the “softmax” output layer, each neuron corresponds to a specific doping temperature. Detailed information on the network architecture, training parameters, and data preprocessing is provided in Methods. Given that deep learning models require a substantial amount of data, 50 samples were grown and measured for each doping temperature. From these measurements, 1000 training spectra for each doping temperature were generated using the method outlined in Supplementary Note 341.

To further analyze the dataset and assess the distinguishability between different doping temperatures of the NWs, we employed t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) for dimensionality reduction. In the t-SNE plot, well-separated scatter points represent distinctly identifiable entities in the dataset, while closely adjacent or overlapping points indicate very similar data. Optimization of doping parameter retrieving

The use of 1/4 BFP data successfully demonstrated error-free retrieval of doping parameters. To further explore the balance between data volume and retrieval accuracy, we trained the network using only a single line of reflectance data (360 pixels) corresponding to the incident angle (x-axis). The variation in reflectance with incident angle for different doping temperatures is shown in Fig. 4a, demonstrating clear distinguishability among the datasets. The generated training spectra are provided in Fig. S4, while the evaluation of the trained ANN is presented in Fig. 4c. Remarkably, the network achieved 100% validation accuracy after just 9 training epochs. Consistently, the t-SNE images also displayed excellent feature separation (Fig. 4d). To assess the efficiency of this approach, we conducted a statistical analysis of the training time required to achieve 100% validation accuracy. The ARS system is integrated with a monochromator, enabling multi-wavelength measurements. To assess the sensitivity of different RGB wavelengths to Si doping concentration, additional measurements were conducted at 644 nm (red light) and 480 nm (blue light) wavelength. The corresponding BFP images are shown in Figs. S5 and S6, with the extracted angle-resolved reflectance data presented in Fig. S7. Figures S8 and S9 contain the 1000 angle spectra used for training the ANN. The ANN framework is trained for 30 epochs to evaluate the retrieval accuracy of doping temperatures under red and blue light conditions.

Figures S10 and S11 illustrate the convergence process for both measurement wavelengths. For red light, the retrieval accuracy stabilized at 100% after 14 epochs. In contrast, for blue light, the recovery accuracy remained below 100% even after 30 epochs. Despite requiring significantly more training epochs for red and blue light compared to green light, the t-SNE plots still demonstrated clear feature separability. However, in practice, determining the most sensitive spectral response for a specific set of NWs is not straightforward. For a given sample with known design dimensions, directly selecting the optimal ARS measurement wavelength is challenging.

To address this, we attempted to develop an equivalent simulation model for Si-doped GaN NWs. In this model, the doping concentration is approximated as a Si thin film coating on the GaN NW (Fig. S12). Based on this model, green light measurements exhibited the highest sensitivity, corroborating the results obtained from the RGB wavelength measurements. While this study focused on optimizing the recovery of doping temperatures, the use of an equivalent model presents a potential direction for optimizing measurement wavelength selection. However, the model did not achieve a perfect match with the angle-resolved reflectance values. This discrepancy likely arises due to the complex nanowire structure, which contains numerous unknown parameters, such as duty cycle, height, and width, along with inherent differences in refractive indices between the Si film and Si-doped GaN. In future work, global sensitivity operators42, which we previously proposed, could be employed to optimize the measurement wavelengths, although this lies beyond the scope of the current study.

Our primary objective is to assess the capability of the deep learning-assisted ARS technique in measuring doping concentration. To this end, it is essential to characterize the Si doping concentration at each temperature, with the EDS characterization results shown in Fig. S13. The statistical results of Si doping concentrations are shown in Fig. 5c. Within the temperature range of 1080–1120 °C (Error range ± 0.1 °C), the doping concentration increases rapidly with temperature. However, beyond 1120 °C, the Si doping reaches saturation, and further temperature increases lead to a decrease in doping concentration. The smallest change in doping concentration occurs between 1160 °C and 1180 °C, with a difference of just 0.01%, indicating that the ARS technique based on deep learning can achieve a Si doping concentration resolution of 0.01%.

We conducted a further statistical analysis of the ANN model’s prediction errors, defined as the average difference between the 300 predicted values and the actual values. As shown in Fig. 5d, the maximum prediction temperature error is 1.1 °C, corresponding to a maximum prediction concentration error of 0.027%. These results demonstrate the robust capability of the ARS technique for accurately measuring doping parameters in MBE.

To explore the extreme measurement capabilities of this technique, we tested its performance using fewer angle-resolved reflectance data points. We proposed two data selection strategies, as shown in Fig. 5e: discrete selection, where data points are chosen at fixed intervals, and continuous selection, which symmetrically expands from the data center. As shown in Fig. 5f, the ANN’s test accuracy initially remains stable as the number of reflectance data points decreases, but drops sharply below a certain threshold (30 and 25 data points, respectively). This critical behavior aligns with the theoretical expectation that insufficient sampling density compromises the ANN’s ability to capture nonlinear reflectance patterns, thereby degrading generalization performance. Both strategies maintain high accuracy (>90%) when the number of data points is 30 or more. However, when the data points fall below 20, the measurement accuracy deteriorates significantly (accuracy < 60%).

To evaluate the universality of the deep learning-assisted ARS technique for different material systems, we further conducted measurements on Si-doped AlN thin films. Similar to GaN nanowires, the doped substrates were Si-based. Compared to nanowires, thin films exhibit both distinct material systems and structural variations, thereby enhancing the demonstration of the technique’s universality. To verify the high sensitivity of this measurement technique, we selected four doping temperatures ranging from 1116 °C to 1119 °C, with 30 samples prepared at each temperature interval.

Conclusions

In summary, we have proposed a deep learning-assisted ARS technique with significant potential for in-situ characterization in MBE. Experiments on Si-doped GaN NWs and AlN films demonstrate that deep learning can accurately recover doping temperature and concentration from ARS measurements with negligible error. We further optimized the measurement process, showing that selecting single-line angle-resolved data instead of the entire BFP data improves both model training efficiency and measurement accuracy. Additionally, we developed an approximate model of the NWs to verify the high sensitivity of green light to Si doping concentration, offering a pathway for measurement optimization. Our evaluation demonstrates that the proposed technique achieves Si-doping concentration resolutions of 0.01% for nanowires and 0.06% for thin films, with maximum prediction temperature errors of 1.1 °C and 0.189 °C, respectively.

This work paves the way for rapid in-situ characterization in MBE using a straightforward optical characterization approach combined with deep learning. The easy-to-integrate setup allows this method to be extended beyond doping concentration to other parameter measurements, enabling real-time optimization during the MBE process.

Methods

Preparation of Si-doped GaN NW samples

The analyzed samples were fabricated using plasma-assisted molecular beam epitaxy (PA-MBE) on 4 inch Si (111) wafers. Prior to loading into the vacuum chamber, the Si (111) wafers underwent a rigorous cleaning procedure. This process involved sequential rinsing with acetone, methanol, and deionized (DI) water to remove organic contaminants, followed by treatment with a diluted HF solution to effectively eliminate the native oxide layer from the wafer surface. This thorough pretreatment is crucial for enhancing GaN adhesion and promoting uniform nanowire growth. After cleaning, the substrates were placed in the loading chamber of the PA-MBE system, where the pressure was reduced to below 10-6 Pa, ensuring a pristine vacuum environment. The wafers were then transferred to the preparation chamber and heated to 950 °C for 20 minutes. This step is critical for desorbing residual surface oxygen and achieving a clear 7 × 7 reconstruction, as confirmed by reflection RHEED.

In the growth chamber, the substrate temperature was uniformly maintained at 735 °C. The temperatures of the Ga and Si sources were meticulously controlled, with the Ga cell temperature set at 1010 °C and the doping temperature varied between 1100 °C, 1120 °C, 1140 °C, 1160 °C, and 1180 °C to study the effects of Si doping on GaN NWs. The nitrogen (N2) flow rate was precisely regulated at 1.5 SCCM, and the plasma power was maintained at 400 W, ensuring a stable supply of active nitrogen species required for the formation of GaN NWs. The growth process was conducted under nitrogen-rich conditions, a known strategy for promoting vertical nanowire growth.

To ensure reproducibility and accuracy in nanowire growth, the Si surface temperature was monitored using RHEED by measuring the Ga desorption transients from the 7 × 7 Si (111) reconstructed surface after Ga flux exposure. This real-time monitoring technique ensured optimal and consistent growth conditions across different samples, thereby enhancing the reliability of the experimental results.

Nanowire characterization

The morphology of Si-doped GaN NWs was characterized using a TESCAN MIRA field emission scanning electron microscope. The high-resolution transmission electron microscopy images were obtained by using the JEOL JEM-F200 instrument. Photoluminescence (PL) measurements were performed at room temperature using a 266 nm excitation pulsed laser, with the PL signal collected by an ultraviolet objective and analyzed using an Ocean Optics spectrometer.

Diffraction efficiency simulation

In the process of seeking an approximate model to verify the sensitivity of RGB wavelengths, we used the DiffractMod module from Synopsys’ RSoft simulation suite. This module employs the rigorous coupled-wave analysis (RCWA) to calculate angle-resolved reflectance43. To quickly achieve results for varying Si film thicknesses, the Scan function was utilized, scanning thicknesses in the range of 1 nm to 10 nm with a step size of 1 nm. Since RCWA solves the problem based on periodic boundary conditions, the model was set to 2D to accelerate the computation. The incident plane wave was set to TM polarization to match the polarization used in the experiment. The index resolution in the x and z directions was set to the default values of 0.00175 and 0.06, respectively. The harmonics in the x direction were set to 5.

Network architecture

The ARS measurement network based on angle-resolved scattering spectra is a one-dimensional convolutional network (ConvNet) followed by a fully connected section. The convolutional neural network, which uses the “leaky ReLU” activation function, consists of three layers. The first layer, with 64 filters per channel, uses a kernel size of 7. The second and third layers each have 32 filters, with kernel sizes of 5 and 3, respectively. Each 1D convolutional layer is followed by a max-pooling layer with a kernel size of 2, as well as batch normalization. The fully connected network comprises two layers with 64 and 32 neurons, respectively, using the “tanh” activation function. For the ConvNet portion, we applied L2 regularization, and for the fully connected part, we used dropout regularization with an 85% keep probability. We evaluated the learning quality using categorical cross-entropy as the loss function. The ANN architecture was trained for 30 epochs (based on the actual training quality), with a learning rate of 0.0001.
