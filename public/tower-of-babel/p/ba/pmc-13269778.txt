Multi-bearing collaborative optimization of the lubrication system for an opposed-piston two-stroke diesel engine
Authors: Fukang Ma, Yuxiang Chen, Chiyu Wang, Lingfei Kong, Zhengyang Liu, Kai Zhang, Ruifan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDriven by the demands for high power density and low energy consumption, optimizing the lubrication system of opposed-piston two-stroke (OP2S) diesel engines is crucial for enhancing efficiency and reliability. This paper proposes a lubrication system design method based on multi-bearing, multi-objective collaborative optimization. By combining the AMESim simulation platform and the HEEDS optimization algorithm, the minimum oil film thickness and oil consumption are optimized under three typical operating conditions (800, 1800, and 2800 r/min). The results show that the optimized circulating flow rate is reduced by 6% under all operating conditions. The opti-mized scheme effectively improves the efficiency of the lubrication system under various operating conditions, with significant optimization effects at high speeds. This optimization method solves the single-objective optimization and over-lubrication problems existing in traditional design methods, providing guidance for practical applications.Keywords: Opposed two-stroke diesel engine, Lubrication system, Lubrication supply strategy, Multi-bearing collaborative optimization, Multi-objective optimizationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Since the invention of the internal combustion engine, it has experienced rapid development throughout the 20th century owing to its advantages of high thermal efficiency, smooth operation, compact structure, excellent starting performance, and wide power output range, and it has been widely applied in marine, aviation, and vehicular power systems1,2. In recent years, performance requirements for diesel engines have continued to increase, among which improving engine power density has become a key development objective.Opposed-piston two-stroke diesel engines (OP2S) have attracted renewed attention due to their advantages of high power density, high efficiency, and good dynamic balance. In OP2S diesel engines, the main components are arranged in a horizontally opposed configuration, and a complete working cycle is accomplished within a single crankshaft revolution. This configuration results in lower energy losses, higher power density, a compact structure, and favorable balance characteristics, making OP2S diesel engines suitable for application scenarios with stringent requirements for high power density and high reliability3–5.However, while OP2S diesel engines benefit from high peak firing pressure, high rotational speed, and lightweight design, the alternating loads acting on the bearings are significantly increased. Under high-intensity cyclic loading conditions, bearings are prone to overheating and wear, which in turn severely degrades lubrication performance and reduces the overall reliability of the engine. Therefore, it is essential to form a stable and effective oil film on the bearing surfaces within a very short time to ensure adequate lubrication, thereby guaranteeing the safe operation of the engine.

At the initial stage of diesel engine lubrication system design, in order to avoid insufficient lubrication, reduce the probability of failure, and extend service life, designers usually adopt experience-based design approaches and reserve large safety margins to ensure an adequate oil supply to all friction pairs. However, traditional empirical design methods often estimate the oil pump flow rate roughly based on engine power6, which commonly results in excessive oil supply and unnecessary energy consumption, and can no longer meet the requirements of modern refined and optimized design.Against the background of global energy shortages and increasingly stringent environmental regulations, diesel engine development has placed strong emphasis on energy-saving and emission-reduction technologies. Reducing lubricating oil consumption has become one of the important directions for advancing diesel engine optimization design, yet related research in this field remains insufficient domestically. Statistics show that when a diesel engine operates at half of its rated load, a 10% reduction in friction losses can lead to an approximately 4% decrease in fuel consumption rate7. Bearings are among the most critical friction pairs in diesel engines, with more than 30% of total friction losses originating from the bearing system8. Poor bearing lubrication performance can adversely affect the operation of the entire engine.Therefore, guiding the optimization design of lubrication systems through computational analysis of bearing lubrication characteristics can effectively reduce bearing friction losses and decrease oil pump power consumption, thereby achieving significant benefits in terms of energy conservation and fuel economy9.

In the field of lubrication system research, lubricating oil supply strategies also constitute an important topic. Current studies on the lubrication and oil supply systems of journal bearings mainly focus on lubrication characteristic analysis and simulation-based verification. Zhao Dongxu et al.10 considered the elastic deformation of the bearing bush induced by oil film pressure and investigated the elastohydrodynamic lubrication characteristics of journal bearings under oil-starved conditions. Their simulation results showed that the oil supply rate has a significant influence on bearing lubrication performance.Lü Tengchao et al.11 established a simulation model based on the governing theoretical equations and validated its accuracy through experiments, providing a theoretical basis for the oil circuit design of lubrication systems in large wind turbine gearboxes. Zhao Yifan12 optimized the flow rate calculation formula for oil nozzles using numerical simulation and verified its feasibility and accuracy in lubrication flow design. Qin Zhi13 employed the AMESim software to investigate the effects of parameters such as rotational speed, bearing clearance, and temperature on the flow field characteristics of lubrication systems.With respect to journal bearing lubrication system design, Wang Ao et al.14 proposed a software-based modeling approach using Flowmaster. Through flow rate calculations as well as steady-state and transient simulation analyses, they verified that the designed lubrication system met performance requirements. Fu Yanhong et al.15 combined theoretical calculations, AMESim simulations, and experimental validation to carry out the forward design of a lubrication system for a power-shift tractor, providing a new solution for the design of journal bearing lubrication systems.

With respect to the optimization of lubricating oil supply strategies, researchers both domestically and internationally have explored a variety of methods and tools. Xuan Hui et al.16 improved the internal oil pressure distribution of a lubrication system by modifying the original oil passage structure. Cui Jiansong et al.17 optimized the lubrication system of a bearing housing in an offshore oilfield water injection pump by reconstructing the oil pipelines and enlarging the pipe diameters. Qiu Yu18 aimed at reducing flow resistance in the lubrication network and applied a genetic algorithm to optimize the lubrication piping design of an inline four-cylinder gasoline engine.Ge Yuzhu et al.19 accurately calculated the power losses of a forced oil-jet lubrication system, proposed principles for lubricating oil flow rate calculation and distribution, and optimized a gearbox lubrication system through a combination of simulation and experimental validation. Dong Qingwei et al.20 investigated the effects of geometric parameters such as oil inlet diameter and angle on the oil film temperature of journal bearings, optimized the bearing structural parameters accordingly, and finally verified the results experimentally. Wang Jiezhen et al.21 established an operational optimization model with the total energy consumption cost of a crude oil pipeline system as the objective function, achieving optimized energy regulation of the pipeline system.Li Zhuoxue et al.22 developed a lubrication piping model for the journal bearings of a planetary gear train in a wind turbine gearbox based on theoretical calculations, and optimized the pipe diameters through simulation analysis. Lintao Wang et al.23 employed computational fluid dynamics software to analyze the influence of the number of rectangular hydrodynamic grooves on the performance of planetary gear journal bearings, constructed a BP neural network–based optimization equation, and optimized the bearing structural parameters.

Beyond conventional crank-slider diesel engines, the importance of multi-component coupling and loss management becomes even more pronounced in advanced high-efficiency energy conversion systems, such as free-piston Stirling engines and thermoacoustic Stirling engines. In these architectures, the absence of a conventional crank mechanism leads to strong dynamic coupling among oscillating components, and the operating stability, start-up behavior, and overall efficiency can be highly sensitive to dissipation sources and mechanical losses. For instance, higher-order modeling and experimental studies on free-piston Stirling engines have shown that coupled thermodynamic–dynamic interactions and loss mechanisms play a critical role in performance prediction and system behavior24.Similarly, recent work on thermoacoustic Stirling engines has emphasized that stability and design feasibility are governed by coupled acoustic–thermal processes, where loss control and parameter interactions dictate whether stable oscillations can be sustained25.These findings highlight the universality of coupling-driven loss-control challenges across advanced engine concepts; similarly, in OP2S lubrication networks, oil distribution, inter-bearing leakage, and lubrication safety margins are strongly coupled, which motivates the collaborative, system-level, and multi-objective optimization strategy adopted in this work rather than isolated single-component tuning.

Despite the increasing research on modeling and optimization of diesel engine lubrication systems and sliding bearings in recent years, several key gaps remain to be filled. First, existing research largely focuses on single-objective optimization of individual bearings or single oil circuit structures (such as reducing friction or local pressure drop), failing to characterize the coupling and mutual constraints between main bearings, connecting rod big-end bearings, connecting rod small-end bushings, and cooling nozzles within the same oil supply network. Especially in multi-bearing systems, oil distribution and inter-bearing leakage flow are strongly correlated, and inherent conflicts often exist between different objectives, making it difficult for traditional “component-by-component, index-by-index” optimization strategies to provide systematic solutions. Second, while some work has made progress in oil circuit structure optimization, research on integrating circulating oil volume (and its pumping energy consumption implications) and lubrication reliability into a unified multi-objective framework, under the premise of “meeting oil film safety thresholds and branch oil supply/cooling constraints,” remains relatively insufficient. Third, many optimization conclusions are based on single-speed or single-oil-temperature conditions, while the actual engine bearing geometry is fixed once manufactured. Feasibility screening and robustness verification across speeds and temperatures are necessary to ensure lubrication safety and oil supply rationality under all operating conditions.

Meanwhile, emerging advancements in machine learning applications in industry have provided new toolchains for reliability assessment and decision-making in complex mechanical systems. On the one hand, high-resolution time-frequency analysis and state recognition for non-stationary signals of rotating machinery are constantly developing, which can enhance feature extraction and reliability-oriented diagnostic capabilities26. On the other hand, multi-sensor fusion and deep network structures (including multi-branch feature fusion and Transformer-like models) have shown outstanding performance in robust diagnosis of key components such as bearings, which helps to improve the recognition capability under complex working conditions and noise backgrounds27,28. These trends suggest that future research can further explore combining physical mechanism models with data-driven methods: on the one hand, constructing fast surrogate models to reduce optimization costs, and on the other hand, realizing robust assessment and online safety margin monitoring across working conditions, providing more efficient support for engineering decisions of complex lubrication systems.Note that machine learning is not adopted in the present workflow; it is discussed here to position future extensions such as surrogate-assisted optimization and online safety-margin monitoring that can complement physics-based modeling.

This work is novel in that it moves lubrication optimization from isolated component-level tuning to a system-level, multi-bearing, multi-speed, constraint-satisfying decision framework, where oil distribution and inter-bearing leakage coupling are explicitly modeled and optimized. The main innovations and contributions are as follows: (i) A system-level multi-bearing collaborative optimization framework is proposed, placing the main bearing, connecting rod big-end bearing, and connecting rod small-end bushing in the same oil supply network for unified modeling and solution, explicitly characterizing the coupling relationship between oil supply distribution and leakage between bearings, thus overcoming the limitations of previous “single-bearing” fragmented optimization; (ii) Mechanism-related geometric variables that can simultaneously connect “local lubrication safety” and “system circulating oil demand” are selected to synchronously optimize the clearance and width of all bearings, expanding the traditional design space while maintaining manufacturability and interpretability; (iii) Based on the AMESim system model and combined with the adaptive multi-objective SHERPA algorithm, explicit trade-offs are made under multi-speed operating conditions and hard constraints, outputting an optimization scheme that meets the feasibility of all operating conditions, achieving coordinated optimization between lubrication, circulating oil demand, and cooling requirements. In summary, the novelty of this paper lies not only in the use of a mature toolchain, but also in embedding the physical coupling of multiple bearings into a unified optimization problem, using key geometric parameters to connect local tribological safety and system cyclic requirements, and achieving constraint satisfaction and multi-objective trade-offs across operating conditions, thus clearly distinguishing it from existing lubrication system optimization research.

Basic structure and working principle of lubrication system

The lubrication system of the OP2S diesel engine mainly consists of components such as an oil pump, pressure relief valve, oil filter, and cooling nozzles (as shown in Fig. 2). The oil pump draws oil from the oil pan, pressurizes it, and then delivers the lubricating oil to the filter before it enters the main oil passage. A portion of the oil travels along the oil passage to provide pressure lubrication to critical components such as the main bearings, connecting rod bearings, and connecting rod small end bushings; the remaining portion is sprayed through the cooling nozzles to cool the piston.

Engine lubrication system model verification.

Before conducting performance analysis, the accuracy of the established model needs to be verified. Model verification relies on data obtained from engine bench tests. The main oil passage pressure can be measured through tests, but due to the difficulty in sensor placement, the complexity of the measurement environment, and the high cost of experiments, parameters such as main bearing pressure, minimum oil film thickness, and peak oil film pressure cannot be directly measured.

This study uses data on the oil pump outlet pressure and main oil passage pressure to verify the accuracy of the model. As shown in Figure 5, the simulated oil pump outlet pressure agrees well with the experimental value, with a maximum relative error of 4.2%; the maximum relative error between the simulated and experimental main oil passage pressure values is 3.6%. Since neither error exceeds 5%, the established model has high accuracy and can be used for subsequent lubrication system analysis.

Multi-objective optimization method and algorithm selection

By combining the physical modeling capabilities of AMESim software with the intelligent search and optimization capabilities of HEEDS software, a design scheme that satisfies constraints and has excellent performance can be automatically searched in the design space while ensuring the accuracy of system simulation.

The complete HEEDS optimization process requires defining input variables, experimental design methods, optimization algorithms, and output variables. The objective function and constraints are set within the HEEDS software, and its built-in algorithms are used to search and verify the effectiveness of the simulation model and design parameters. (1) Selection of optimization factors.

Given that the main factors affecting the minimum oil film thickness and leakage flow rate of the bearing in the OP2S diesel engine lubrication system are bearing clearance and bearing width, this study selects these two parameters as design variables for multi-objective optimization and establishes a multi-objective optimization model for the lubrication system based on them.

The specific design is as follows:

Input Variables: Given the high sensitivity of bearing minimum oil film thickness and leakage flow rate to bearing clearance and width, this design sets six parameters—the clearance and width of the main bearing, connecting rod big end bearing, and connecting rod small end bushing—as optimization input variables.

Output Variables: The optimized output indicators include the cooling nozzle flow rate, and the minimum oil film thickness and leakage flow rate for each of the three types of bearings mentioned above.

Constraints: Two main constraints are set: First, the minimum oil film thickness of all bearings must not be lower than the safety threshold; second, the bearing leakage flow rate and cooling nozzle flow rate must meet the cooling requirements of the engine piston and bearings.

(3) Selection of optimization algorithm.

HEEDS software uses a hybrid adaptive search algorithm called “SHERPA” by default. The main features of this algorithm are: it can find a relatively optimal solution on the first search, without repeatedly adjusting parameters to find the optimum; and it optimizes directly based on the actual model evaluation, rather than relying on an approximate response surface model. Therefore, SHERPA can perform global and local searches simultaneously, exploring complex design spaces more efficiently and finding high-quality solutions for various optimization problems. The SHERPA algorithm can also adapt to different problems without requiring users to specify adjustment parameters. This high efficiency can save several days or even weeks of CPU computation time in general engineering optimization. It is worth mentioning that SHERPA employs two unique adaptive strategies: a full-objective weighted summation method and a multi-objective trade-off method.

The multi-objective trade-off method is suitable for optimization problems involving multiple conflicting objectives. This method is essentially the same as the traditional weighted summation strategy, but its advantage lies in its ability to handle each objective separately, providing a series of alternative Pareto optimal solutions. In these solutions, each scheme can be considered optimal for a certain objective in some aspect, and the final optimal combination is selected by comparing the merits of the schemes. SHERPA operates according to the Pareto dominance principle: if a design is superior to all other designs in at least one objective, it is said to “dominate” the other designs. During the algorithm’s operation, feasible designs not currently dominated by any other scheme are listed as the first-level Pareto front; then, undominated designs are selected from the remaining schemes as the second level, and this process is iterated to stratify all schemes. The set of designs not dominated by any other design is called the Pareto set, and the set of undominated designs in the entire set constitutes the Pareto front. As the optimization iterates, the found Pareto set continuously approaches the theoretically optimal Pareto front, and newly emerging designs occupy the Pareto front position and replace inferior designs. Ultimately, the algorithm outputs a set of non-dominant solutions, which constitute the Pareto optimal solution set. By reviewing these solutions, the one that best meets the design requirements can be determined.

Given that the optimization model in this paper simultaneously incorporates multiple operating conditions (RS = 800/1800/2800 r/min), multiple objective trade-offs (lubrication safety—circulating oil volume—cooling requirements), and strong constraints (oil film thickness threshold, branch flow lower limit), this paper adopts the adaptive multi-objective search algorithm SHERPA from the HEEDS platform. Compared to single local search methods, SHERPA can achieve global exploration and local refinement with a limited number of evaluations and output feasible Pareto solutions, making it suitable for system-level optimization problems with multiple constraints and coupled operating conditions. Optimization results and performance under multiple operating conditions.

Given that bearing clearance and width are fixed once manufactured, this study does not simply average the “single-condition optimal” designs obtained at 800, 1800, and 2800 r/min. Instead, the three optimized candidates are treated as alternative fixed-geometry solutions and are re-simulated under all three speed conditions. A feasibility-first screening is then applied using hard constraints: the minimum oil film thickness of the main bearing, big-end bearing, and small-end bushing must remain above the safety threshold at all operating conditions, and the critical branch leakage/cooling flow requirements must also be satisfied. The re-evaluation indicates that Scheme 1 fails to meet the safety threshold for both minimum oil film thickness and leakage flow in the small-end bushing at 1800 r/min; at 2800 r/min, it further shows that the minimum oil film thickness of all bearings drops below the threshold and the leakage flow of both the big-end bearing and the small-end bushing also violates the requirement, thus lacking full-speed feasibility. In contrast, Scheme 2 violates the leakage-flow constraint of the big-end bearing at 800 r/min and still fails the leakage-flow requirement of the small-end bushing at 2800 r/min, indicating insufficient robustness for cross-condition operation. Based on the above all-condition hard-constraint screening, only Scheme 3 satisfies both the minimum oil film thickness and leakage-flow thresholds at 800, 1800, and 2800 r/min, and is therefore selected as the final compromise fixed-geometry design for practical full-speed operation. This scheme is subsequently used for the overall performance evaluation with the objective of reducing the system circulating oil flow rate.

While keeping the optimized bearing geometry unchanged, a single-parameter sweep was performed on the pump displacement. The feasibility criterion was “the minimum oil film thickness of each bearing meets the safety threshold, and the leakage flow/oil supply demand of the key branch meets the threshold,” to determine the minimum feasible pump displacement at different speeds. The results are shown in Table 7: at 800 r/min, 1800 r/min, and 2800 r/min are 6.3, 6.5, and 7.0 cc/rev, respectively. Further observation revealed that as the pump displacement continued to decrease, the first failure triggered was the “insufficient leakage flow” constraint of the main bearing, indicating that the main bearing branch constitutes the first bottleneck in oil supply distribution. It is noteworthy that as the speed increases, the minimum pump displacement required to meet the threshold shows an upward trend (6.3→7.0 cc/rev), which means that high-speed operating conditions are more sensitive to oil supply capacity and flow margin. Therefore, this paper selects as the fixed displacement scheme across operating conditions in the engineering recommendation, so as to ensure that the safety threshold is met and the necessary margin is retained in the full speed range, while realizing the system efficiency benefits brought about by the reduction of circulating oil volume.

As shown in Fig. 11, further sensitivity analysis under the 2800 r/min operating condition reveals that the minimum oil film thickness of the main bearing is significantly more sensitive to radial clearance than to bearing width; while the large and small end bearings of the connecting rod show the opposite trend. The physical mechanism is as follows: Because the main bearing has a larger shaft diameter and bears the main load, oil film formation is more dependent on wedge geometry; small changes in clearance directly alter the oil film geometry parameters and quickly reflect in the oil film thickness. Due to the smaller diameter of the connecting rod branch, differences in the oil inlet/outlet and end leakage ratios, and different heat dissipation conditions, width changes play a more significant role in altering the bearing area, end leakage ratio, and axial flow path length, thus having a stronger coupled impact on local lubrication safety. Therefore, in engineering design, a priority control strategy for the bearings should be adopted: prioritizing clearance tolerance control for the main bearing, while focusing more on the control and optimization of width, end leakage, and cooling ratio for the connecting rod bearing.

High-speed operation poses more stringent challenges to the bearing lubrication of the OP2S engine, mainly in two aspects: “temperature rise-viscosity decrease coupling” and “risk of local oil film rupture”. As the rotational speed increases, the surface velocity of the bearing increases, leading to a rise in shear rate. The shear friction power of the oil film can be expressed as , resulting in an increase in lubricating oil temperature. The temperature rise further causes a decrease in viscosity , weakening the dynamic pressure bearing capacity and compressing the minimum oil film thickness, forming an unfavorable coupling of “temperature rise-viscosity decrease-increased leakage-further thinning of the oil film”. Under this background, the oil film is more likely to approach the safety threshold at the cyclic trough and exhibits a risk trend of local rupture. To more directly evaluate the ability of the optimization scheme to cope with the high-speed temperature rise challenge, as shown in Fig. 12, this paper uses two sets of conditions, 70℃ (calibrated temperature) and 90℃ (unfavorable temperature after temperature rise), to compare the changes in oil film thickness over time for the main bearing, connecting rod big end bearing, and connecting rod small end bushing. The results show that the oil film thickness of the three types of bearings decreases overall at 90℃, with the minimum oil film thickness being a key indicator for evaluating high-speed safety. Compared with before optimization, the optimized values for each bearing at 90℃ are 1.25 μm, 1.44 μm, and 1.49 μm, respectively, all within safe limits and higher than before optimization. Furthermore, the optimized hmin shows a smaller decrease in minimum oil film thickness when the temperature rises from 70℃ to 90℃, indicating that under the unfavorable coupling of temperature rise and viscosity decrease, the optimized scheme has a stronger resistance to disturbances related to oil film thinning, thereby reducing the risk of insufficient oil film safety margin under high-speed operating conditions.

Conclusions and outlook

Conclusions

(1) The multi-bearing multi-objective optimization method proposed in this paper significantly improves the efficiency of the lubrication system. By simultaneously optimizing the geometric parameters of the main bearing, the connecting rod large end bearing, and the connecting rod small end bushing, a comprehensive improvement in lubrication efficiency is achieved while ensuring lubrication safety. By rationally allocating the oil flow rate, the supply of lubricating oil is optimized, enabling the lubrication system to minimize energy loss while maintaining lubrication safety, thereby improving the overall performance and reliability of the diesel engine.

(2) Through verification under different operating speeds, the optimized scheme exhibits excellent performance under all operating conditions, especially at high speeds, effectively reducing the risk of oil film rupture and fuel consumption. This indicates that the optimized design has good adaptability and can cope with the complex operating conditions of diesel engines under different operating conditions, thereby improving the reliability and stability of the lubrication system in practical applications.

(3) The multi-bearing, multi-objective optimization framework proposed in this study has strong universality and scalability. Although this study focuses on the lubrication system of the OP2S diesel engine, the method is not only applicable to the OP2S diesel engine, but can also be widely applied to the lubrication system optimization of other engines, especially for engines that require multi-bearing collaborative design. With the continuous development of engine design, the scalability of the optimization framework provides a foundation for the further promotion of multi-objective collaborative optimization methods in the future.

(4) Because bearing geometry is fixed once manufactured, the per-speed “theoretical optima” at RS = 800/1800/2800 r/min are not directly implementable. We therefore select a single fixed-geometry compromise design via. Only Scheme 3 remains feasible across the full speed range; therefore, the manufacturing scheme adopts the most demanding conditions (RS = 2800 r/min): MW = 16 mm, MC = 0.050 mm; BW = 22 mm, BC = 0.022 mm; SW = 20 mm, SC = 0.015 mm, with pump displacement for full-speed feasibility.

Limitations and future work

Although this paper proposes a multi-bearing collaborative optimization framework and verifies its feasibility under various operating conditions, applying this method to more complex real-world engines still faces several challenges and limitations. First, the lubrication interactions between multiple bearings are not only reflected in flow coupling such as “oil supply distribution-leakage backflow,” but may also generate stronger inter-bearing couplings through mechanisms such as spatial non-uniformity of the oil temperature and viscosity fields, dynamic pressure drop in branches, and local cavitation changes. When the actual system has a significant thermal gradient or unsteady load, simplified isothermal or quasi-steady-state assumptions may underestimate local risks. Second, collaborative optimization inherently involves potential conflicts: for example, reducing clearance to decrease the system’s circulating oil volume may weaken the oil film safety margin of some bearings under high-temperature, low-viscosity conditions; increasing the width helps reduce specific pressure and increase oil film thickness, but may also increase the shear heating area and change the oil temperature, thereby amplifying leakage demand. Third, uncertainties introduced by manufacturing and lifespan factors exist at the engineering implementation level. For example, bearing wear leads to increased clearance over time, oil deterioration causes viscosity drift, and deviations in pump/valve characteristics and pipeline resistance can all cause the “nominal optimal solution” to deviate from the design point during long-term operation.Notably, in the present study, thermo-hydrodynamic effects are represented through the viscosity-temperature relationship; transient thermal expansion of bearing components and the associated time-varying clearance are not explicitly modeled and remain a subject for future high-fidelity coupled thermo-mechanical lubrication analysis.

To improve the adaptability of this method in practical applications, this paper suggests further expansion in three aspects: First, introduce reliability constraints, incorporating uncertainties such as oil temperature, viscosity, load, pump efficiency, and valve characteristics into the optimization as perturbations or probabilities, ensuring that the solution still satisfies oil film thickness and supply constraints under worst-case conditions or with a given confidence level; Second, enhance the model’s characterization of interactions, for example, by adding energy equations for key nodes or using a higher-fidelity local bearing thermo-fluid coupling model for secondary verification under high-risk conditions; Third, combine sensor data (pressure, temperature, flow rate) for online calibration and health monitoring, constructing a “mechanism model + data-driven” proxy model to reduce computational costs and dynamically update constraint margins throughout the lifespan, thereby extending co-optimization from a one-time design to a sustainable engineering decision-making tool.
