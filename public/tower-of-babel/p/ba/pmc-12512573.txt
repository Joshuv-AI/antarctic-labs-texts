HMGB1 regulates autophagy via TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway in myocardial injury induced by coronary microembolization
Authors: Tingsheng Yang, Xiaodan Wu, Xiantao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary microembolization (CME) is a common and refractory complication of coronary atherosclerotic plaque spontaneous rupture or reperfusion treatment in patients with coronary heart disease, which is closely related to the patient’s near-term and long-term prognosis. The specific molecular mechanism of CME leading to myocardial injury is not yet fully understood. This study aims to elucidate the regulatory function of high‐mobility group protein box 1 (HMGB1) within the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, and explore the role of autophagy mediated by this signaling pathway in CME-induced myocardial injury.MethodsA rat model of CME was established by left ventricle injection of microspheres. HMGB1 inhibitor (glycyrrhizin) or autophagy inhibitor (3-methyladenine) was pretreated before CME modeling. Following the animal modeling of CME, we examined alterations in the expression and localization of proteins associated with signaling pathways. Additionally, we monitored the autophagic activity within the myocardium and assessed cardiac function, histological changes in myocardium, and biomarkers indicative of myocardial injury present in serum samples.ResultsIn the CME model group, there was a significant upregulation of HMGB1 expression and its migration into the cytoplasm. Concurrently, TLR4 and NF-κB expression levels were elevated, while autophagic activity was diminished. In contrast, when compared to the model group, the glycyrrhizin acid pretreatment group exhibited notable improvements in cardiac dysfunction (LVEF increased by 18.50 ± 1.13%) and myocardial injury (microinfarct size reduced by 19.94 ± 1.07%, P < 0.001). However, this cardioprotective effect was nullified upon pretreatment with the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA.ConclusionHMGB1 regulates autophagy through TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway in CME rat model of myocardial injury induced by coronary microembolization.Graphical Abstract Keywords: HMGB1, Coronary microembolization, Myocardial injury, Autophagy, TLR4/NF-κB

Introduction

Coronary microembolization (CME) is triggered by the spontaneous rupture of coronary atherosclerotic plaques or the infiltration of plaque debris, microemboli, and neutrophil–platelet aggregates into the coronary microcirculation during reperfusion therapy for patients with CAD. This phenomenon can lead to coronary microvascular dysfunction, myocardial microinfarction, systolic dysfunction, arrhythmia, and potentially sudden cardiac death. As research continues to deepen, substantial advancements have been achieved in our comprehension of CME. However, there is currently no empirical evidence from clinical practice that can effectively enhance patient prognosis [1].

High-mobility group protein box 1 (HMGB1) is a DNA binding protein that plays a pivotal role in the regulation of transcription within the nucleus. Beyond its nuclear function, HMGB1 functions as an extracellular signaling molecule in a variety of processes, including inflammation, cell differentiation, cell migration, and tumor metastasis [2]. Research has demonstrated that HMGB1 interacts with its receptor, TLR4. This interaction subsequently activates the downstream NF-κB pathway, leading to the transcription of cytokine and chemokine genes [3].

Autophagy is an evolutionarily conserved mechanism for the degradation of cytoplasmic components. The initiation of autophagy can be significantly promoted by the binding of Beclin-1 to phosphatidylinositol 3-hydroxy kinase, and the activation of autophagy is positively correlated with the ratio of LC 3 II/LC 3 I [4, 5]. A growing body of evidence substantiates the pivotal role of cardiomyocyte autophagy in the etiology of a broad spectrum of cardiovascular diseases [6], including myocardial infarction [7], myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury [8], atherosclerosis [9] and cardiomyopathy [10]. Cardiomyocyte autophagy may also play an important role in CME. In our prior research, we discovered a significant downregulation in autophagic activity of cardiomyocyte within the rat CME model [11]. Therefore, interventions to restore autophagic activity of cardiomyocyte may have a cardioprotective effect.

This research endeavored to elucidate the mechanism and function of HMGB1-mediated cardiomyocyte autophagy via TLR4/NF-κB in relation to myocardial injury and cardiac dysfunction, which are induced by coronary microembolization. This will be the first evidence demonstrating that HMGB1-regulated autophagy is one of the key mechanisms underlying myocardial injury induced by coronary microembolization, providing new insights into the challenging clinical issue of ischemia–reperfusion injury.

Materials and methods

Animal model and grouping

Healthy male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats, aged 8 weeks and weighing between 250 and 300 g, were sourced from the Experimental Animal Center of Guangxi Medical University. The establishment of the CME model referred to previously reported [12]. In summary, the rats were anesthetized using an intraperitoneal injection of 40 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital, then an endotracheal tube was inserted via oral intubation and subsequently connected to an animal ventilator. After shaving the chest skin of the rat, routine disinfection and installation of sterile surgical cloth, the sternum was cut open until the heart was exposed, and the ascending aorta was separated. Four thousand polyethylene microspheres (Polybond Microspheres, diameter 45 μm; Polycycles Inc., USA) were injected into the left ventricle, while the ascending aorta was clamped with a suitable animal hemostatic clip for 10 s. The chest was closed after the rat’s heart rate returned to normal, and the endotracheal tube was removed when the rat recovered spontaneous respiration. All animal experiments were meticulously conducted in accordance with the guidelines established by the Animal Research Ethics Committee at Guangxi Medical University. These procedures adhered strictly to the standards prescribed by the National Institutes of Health for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Twenty-four rats were randomly divided into sham, CME, CME + glycyrrhizin (CME + Gly) and CME + glycyrrhizin + 3-methyladenine (CME + Gly + 3-MA). The CME model groups were established as described above. The sham group was injected with an equal volume of saline into the left ventricle. Glycyrrhizin (10 mg/kg, MedChemExpress, USA) was injected via the tail vein, and 3-methyladenine (15 mg/kg, MedChemExpress, USA) was injected intraperitoneally 30 min before modeling. Our previous study showed that cardiac function reached a minimum level at 12 h after CME [13]. Consequently, echocardiography was conducted 12 h post-CME modeling. Subsequently, the rats were euthanized to ensure efficient collection of blood and cardiac samples.

Echocardiography

Rats were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (30–40 mg/kg). Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDd) and left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESd) were measured by a dedicated animal ultrasound device (Estate, Myasis, Italy). All measurements were performed by experienced operators. All values were the mean of three cardiac cycles.

Tissue sampling and treatment

After echocardiography, all rats were anesthetized using an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital at an overdose of 60 mg/kg. Blood was collected from the abdominal aorta, and subsequently, serum was isolated and preserved at − 80 °C for ELISA assay analysis. The heart tissue was excised, rinsed with pre-chilled saline, sectioned along the longitudinal axis of the left ventricle, and subsequently divided into apical and mid-heart segments. These were then stored at − 80 °C for further analysis using Western blot and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). The basal part was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin wax, and serially sectioned into 4 μm slices for histological observation.

Myocardial injury measurement

The collected blood was permitted to stratify at room temperature for an approximate duration of one hour, subsequently centrifuged at a speed of 3000 rpm for a period of 10 min. The resultant supernatant was then collected. The concentration of cTnI in the supernatant was quantified utilizing a commercial ELISA kit (Fankew, China), adhering strictly to the manufacturer’s protocol. Each experimental group comprised six rats.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining was used to evaluate the expression of HMGB1 and LC3B in myocardium. Briefly, sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated sequentially in 100%, 95%, 80%, and 75% ethanol. The samples were then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton-X (Solarbio, China) and blocked with 5% BSA (Solarbio, China). Subsequently, the samples were incubated with either a monoclonal rabbit anti-human HMGB1 antibody (1:250; Abcam, USA) or an LC3B primary antibody (1:200; Abcam, USA) at 4 °C overnight. The slides underwent staining with a Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000, Abcam, USA) in the dark for 1 h. DAPI (Solarbio, China) was used to stain nuclei. The slides were observed using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining

The pathological changes of myocardial tissue were assessed through HE staining. Paraffin-embedded myocardial tissue was serially sectioned at 4 μm. The deparaffinized and hydrated tissues were then stained with hematoxylin, subsequently counterstained with alcohol–eosin. All sections were analyzed using an optical microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Pathological scores were scored according to a previously published method [14], 0 points: no myocardial injury; 1 point: lesion area < 25%; 2 points: lesion area 25–50%; 3 points: lesion area 50–75%; 4 points: diffuse inflammatory cell infiltration, lesion area > 75%.

Heidenhain’s iron hematoxylin staining

Microinfarction regions were stained utilizing Heidenhain’s iron hematoxylin Stain Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China), adhering strictly to the manufacturer's guidelines. Heidenhain’s iron hematoxylin staining is an important method for detecting early myocardial ischemia, and ischemic myocardium appears dark gray while normal myocytes are stained red [15]. Five vision fields (× 200 magnification) of each section were randomly selected under an Olympus optical microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The ratio of the microinfarct area, as measured by Image J software, to the entire assessed area was used to calculate the percentage of microinfarct area.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), total RNA was extracted from the samples and then quantified using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA). After that, RNA was reverse transcribed according to the procedure of HyperScript RT kit (EnzyArtisan Shanghai, China). qRT-PCR was carried out using a SYBR Green I kit on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR instrument (Applied Biosystems, USA)). The expression of related genes was detected by the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal reference. The gene-specific primers were as follows: HMGB1 primers: forward: 5′-CCA GTT GTC CAA GCC ATT TGC TTT T-3′, reverse: 5′-GCG CGG AAA ATT ACA AGA ACG CT-3′; TLR4 primers: forward: 5′-TAC AGT TCG TCA TGC TTT CTC-3′, reverse: 5′-ATT AGG AAG TAC CTC TAT GCA G-3′; NF-κB primers: forward: 5′-CTG TGT GAC AAA GTG CAG AAA GA-3′; reverse: 5′-GAT CGT CTG TGT CTG GCA AGT A-3′; GAPDH primers: forward: 5′-GAA GGT CGG TGT GAA CGG AT-3′, reverse: 5′-CCC ATT TGA TGT TAG CGG GAT-3′.

Western blotting

Myocardial tissue samples were lysed using RIPA buffer (Solarbio, China) containing protease inhibitors to extract total proteins. Subsequently, the protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (Solarbio, China). Proteins were resolved on 10–12% SDS polyacrylamide gels and then electroblotted onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Following a 1-h blocking process with 5% skim milk at room temperature, the membranes underwent an overnight incubation at 4 °C. This was done using primary antibodies that were specific to several proteins: HMGB1 (Abcam, USA), TLR4 (Proteintech, China), NF-κB (Proteintech, China), Beclin-1 (Proteintech, China), LC3B (Abcam, USA), and GAPDH (Abcam, USA). The membranes were incubated for 1 h with a secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase. Western blotting was executed using advanced chemiluminescence reagents (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA) and the gray values of protein bands were meticulously evaluated utilizing Image J software.

Statistical analysis

All results were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 software and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Multigroup comparisons were conducted utilizing one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), which was followed by Tukey’s post hoc test multiple comparisons. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Expression of HMGB1 in rats

The expression of HMGB1 significantly escalates following myocardial injury. This heightened expression facilitates its ingress into the cytoplasm, thereby enhancing its functionality. We detected the levels of HMGB1 by real-time PCR and western blotting. The findings indicated a significant upregulation of HMGB1 expression in the CME model (Fig. 1A and B). Furthermore, we observed that HMGB1 was predominantly localized in the nucleus within the sham group. After CME, HMGB1 was translocated from the nucleus to the cytoplasm (Fig. 1C). HMGB1 inhibitor glycyrrhizin could significantly inhibit the expression and translocation of HMGB1.

Expression of TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway in rats

To further explore the function of HMGB1 on downstream pathways, we conducted an assessment of TLR4 and NF-κB expression levels. The results demonstrated that CME upregulated the expression levels of HMGB1, TLR4, and NF-κB when compared to the Sham group. Furthermore, inhibiting the expression of HMGB1 also suppressed the expression of TLR4 and NF-κB (Fig. 2). PCR and western blotting proved the same results.

The HMGB1/TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway inhibits autophagy after CME in rats

In the sham group, a high expression of LC3BⅡ/LC3BⅠ and Beclin-1 was observed (Fig. 3A), and a higher number of fluorescent LC3BⅡ positive spots (autophagy activity) was observed under microscopy (Fig. 3B), which indicated structural activity with autophagy in normal rat myocardium. CME downregulated the autophagy marker LC3BⅡ/LC3BⅠ (Sham: 1.48 ± 0.20 vs. CME: 0.81 ± 0.13 vs. CME + Gly: 1.32 ± 0.29 vs. CME + Gly + 3-MA: 0.81 ± 0.178, P < 0.05) and Beclin-1 (Sham: 1.15 ± 0.12 vs. CME: 0.75 ± 0.07 vs. CME + Gly: 1.3 ± 0.10 vs. CME + Gly + 3-MA: 0.75 ± 0.06 P < 0.05). Compared to the CME group, inhibition of the HMGB1/TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway could restore autophagic activity in myocardium. The autophagy inhibitor, 3-MA, could significantly decrease autophagic activity in myocardium.

The HMGB1/TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway mediates cardiac dysfunction after CME via suppression of autophagy

The echocardiographic results are shown in Fig. 4 and Table 1. In comparison to the Sham group, there was a significant reduction in both LVEF and LVFS within the CME group, while an increase was observed in both LVEDd and LVESd. Notably, preconditioning with glycyrrhizin to inhibit HMGB1 mitigated the cardiac dysfunction caused by CME. This attenuation was evident in the CME + Gly group, as indicated by the increase in LVEF (CME: 55.67 ± 2.25% vs. CME + Gly: 74.17 ± 1.17%) and LVFS, along with a decrease in LVEDd and LVESd. However, this cardioprotective effect was reversed by the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA.

The HMGB1/TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway mediates myocardial injury after CME by inhibiting autophagy

Histopathological evaluation of myocardium was performed using HE staining, Heidenhain’s iron hematoxylin staining. The HE staining results revealed that in the CME group, the nuclei of cardiomyocytes within the microinfarct area were absent or dissolved, while the cytoplasm appeared dark red with surrounding myocardial edema. Obvious inflammatory cell infiltration and microspheres were observed in small arteries (Fig. 5A). We also observed multiple microinfarcts in CME heart tissues as shown by Heidenhain Feulgen staining (Fig. 5B). Meanwhile, serum cTnI levels were elevated in the CME group compared to the Sham group (Fig. 5E). These results revealed significantly abnormal myocardial morphology and myocardial injury after CME. Importantly, myocardial degeneration, edema, and inflammatory cell infiltration were alleviated upon inhibition of HMGB1 by pretreatment with glycyrrhizin as shown by HE staining. Notably, pretreatment with glycyrrhizin significantly reduced microinfarct size (CME: 29.78 ± 2.40% vs. CME + Gly: 9.83 ± 2.22%) as well as serum cTnI level compared to the CME group. However, these beneficial effects were abolished by treatment with autophagy inhibitor 3-MA. Taken together, these results provide strong evidence supporting that impaired autophagy plays an important role in CME-induced myocardial injury.

Discussion

In this experiment, our observations revealed that CME triggered an upregulation in the expression of HMGB1, along with its downstream TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway; impaired cardiomyocyte autophagy and induced myocardial injury and cardiac dysfunction. Inhibition of HMGB1 attenuated myocardial injury and improved cardiac function after CME by restoring autophagic activity. Autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine reversed these effects. Collectively, these results indicate that HMGB1 may contribute to CME-induced myocardial injury and cardiac dysfunction by modulating cardiomyocyte autophagy via the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway.

It is well-established that HMGB1 serves as a critical mediator in inflammatory processes; nevertheless, its specific role in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases remains incompletely elucidated [16]. Prior research has demonstrated that the expression levels of HMGB1 were notably elevated in both in vivo rat CME models and in vitro OGD models. Under physiological conditions, HMGB1 predominantly resides within the nucleus. However, in response to cellular stimulation or pathological states, there is an upregulation of HMGB1 expression and its subsequent release from the nucleus [17, 18]. TLR4 serves as one of the receptors for HMGB1. Upon binding of extracellular HMGB1 to TLR4, a downstream signal is initiated, resulting in the expression of cytokines and chemokines [19, 20]. NF-κB is a downstream factor of TLR4; activation of NF-κB depends on the TLR4-mediated pathway [21, 22]. Research has demonstrated that an elevation in HMGB1-induced TLR4 expression level is intricately linked to the secretion of TNF-α and NF-κB in myocardial IRI [23]. The findings of our research align with a preceding study in which the authors utilized siRNA treatment to suppress HMBG1 [24]. Glycyrrhizin is a naturally occurring bioactive compound that has been employed therapeutically for centuries in the treatment of various diseases and has demonstrated a well-established safety profile through extensive clinical use [25]. In contrast, the major challenges associated with most siRNA-based therapies remain unresolved, thereby limiting their broader therapeutic applicability [26]. Therefore, our study elucidates the therapeutic potential of glycyrrhizin in treating CME through its targeted modulation of HMGB1.

Despite the significant advancements and applications in cardiac catheterization, periprocedural myocardial injury remains a prevalent consequence following coronary revascularization [27]. This is attributed, in part, to the occurrence of CME [28]. CME may result in progressive cardiac contractile dysfunction, microinfarcts in the myocardium, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and a diminished coronary blood flow reserve [29, 30]. The pathogenesis of CME is intricate, and its underlying mechanism has yet to be fully elucidated. Autophagy is an evolutionarily conserved catabolic pathway that targets various cellular components, from protein aggregates to entire organelles, for degradation. This process aids in promoting cell survival under diverse stress conditions [31]. A multitude of studies have unequivocally established the pivotal role of autophagy in the pathophysiology of an array of cardiovascular diseases. Gatica et al. revealed that autophagy was a key event during myocardial I/R injury, and activation of autophagy protects cardiomyocytes from I/R injury [32]. Recent research findings suggest that the concurrent administration of PT1, a specific AMPK activator, and 3HOI-BA-01, a potent MTOR inhibitor, can induce autophagy and decrease cardiomyocyte death caused by simulated I/R. Additionally, the in vivo administration of either PT1 or 3HOI-BA-01 in a murine I/R injury model has been shown to stimulate autophagy and reduce infarct size [33]. Previous studies showed that autophagy was associated with CME-induced myocardial injury, and the activation of autophagy exerted a cardioprotective effect [34]. The present study found that CME could cause the inhibition of myocardial autophagy, manifested as a substantial reduction in the protein expression levels of Beclin-1, LC3BⅡ/LC3BⅠ, and LC3BⅡ-positive spots subsequent to CME. Pretreatment with glycyrrhizin can significantly restore cardiomyocyte autophagy and alleviate CME-induced myocardial injury. This is supported by the observation that the use of an autophagy inhibitor, 3-methyladenine, exacerbated myocardial injury.

Finally, there are some limitations of this study. First, the drawback of this method is the inertness of these microspheres, which would lead to physical blockage of coronary microcirculation but not thrombogenic, pro-inflammatory, or vasoconstrictive potential. Second, whether HMGB1 mediates autophagy through other pathways remains to be explored. Third, the limitations in physiological, pathological, and immunological differences of the rodent model restrict the direct extrapolation of research findings to humans. Further research is needed to address these limitations.

Conclusions

In summary, HMGB1 inhibits cardiomyocyte autophagy through the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby mediating myocardial injury caused by CME. This study uncovers the mechanistic underpinnings of HMGB1-induced myocardial injury triggered by CME, establishing a novel theoretical foundation for the prevention and management of CME in patients with coronary heart disease.
