Self selected music during warm up improves anaerobic performance in female handball players across time of day
Authors: Houda Bougrine, Imed Gandouzi, Ismail Dergaa, Julien Maitre, Abdulwahed Ahmed Alaizari, Osama Aljuhani, Thierry Paillard, Nizar Souissi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined the effects of self-selected motivational music during warm-up on time-of-day (TOD) variations in short-term maximal performance among adolescent female handball players. Eighteen athletes (16.16 ± 0.38 years) completed eight sessions in randomized order across four TOD points (08:00 h, 11:00 h, 15:00 h, 18:00 h) and two warm-up conditions: with music (Yes-MUS) and without music (No-MUS). At each session, oral temperature (OT), countermovement jump (CMJ), medicine ball throw (MBT), 20-meter sprint (20 m-ST), and Illinois Agility Test (IAT) were assessed. Results showed that oral temperature increased significantly from 35.63 ± 0.72 °C at 08:00 h to 36.78 ± 0.12 °C at 18:00 h (p < 0.001), mirroring improvements in all physical performance tests across the day. Notably, music enhanced performance at all TODs and significantly reduced the amplitude of diurnal variation in CMJ (5.7% vs. 2.3%), MBT (13% vs. 6.6%), 20 m-ST (5.9% vs. 3.3%), and IAT (7.1% vs. 4.7%). The largest gains were observed during morning sessions. These findings suggest that self-selected motivational music during warm-up can act as a practical ergogenic strategy to counteract time-of-day performance decrements, especially in female team-sport athletes. Introduction

Circadian rhythms, endogenous biological oscillations regulated by the central biological clock, regulate numerous physiological processes1. These rhythms are modulated by external, psychobiological, internal, and chronobiological factors2–4, influencing body temperature5,6, and hormonal regulation (e.g., cortisol, melatonin)7. Such fluctuations extend to skeletal muscle, altering molecular clock mechanisms8. These variations have been shown to influence maximal strength output9–11 and the ability to perform repeated sprints12, thereby contributing to time-of-day-dependent variations in neuromuscular performance13.

Indeed, athletic performance whether endurance or strength-based fluctuate with the time of day (TOD)14–20. Maximal short-duration efforts, heavily reliant on anaerobic metabolism, demonstrate pronounced performance differences between morning and evening21–25, with peak performance typically occurring in the late afternoon or early evening (16:00–20:00)26–28 compared to morning performance (06:00–10:00)17,26–28. These fluctuations are primarily driven by peripheral (muscular) rather than neural adaptations29. While factors such as active warm-up, fasting, controlled environmental conditions, and consistent morning training can mitigate these diurnal variations17, research on female athletes, particularly in team-sport contexts, remains comparatively scarce. Nonetheless, a few recent studies have begun to address this gap. For instance, Martín-López et al. (2022)30 demonstrated time-of-day effects on neuromuscular performance in semi-professional female volleyball players, showing superior outcomes in the afternoon. Similarly, Mhenni et al. (2017)31 reported enhanced handball-related short-term maximal performances in the evening compared to the morning among female handball players. These findings confirm the presence of circadian variation in female athletes, yet highlight the need for more targeted studies across sports and performance domains. Consequently, optimizing training and competition schedules according to time-of-day (TOD) may be particularly relevant for female athletes.

In parallel, music has emerged as a potential modulator of athletic performance. Listening to music during exercise has been shown to reduce perceived exertion, improve motivation and arousal, and support better motor coordination32. Music acts as a cognitive distractor33,34, helping to reduce effort perception, while also stimulating neural activity35,36, improving running economy and overall physical output37,38. Its ergogenic effects span endurance exercise39, sprint performance40, and resistance training41–43. Music has also been reported to influence mental states, helping athletes to energize, relax, or increase confidence and self-esteem39,41,43.

Importantly, music may also attenuate time-of-day-related performance declines, particularly during suboptimal morning periods44–46. While some studies report enhanced power output during anaerobic tasks when music is used during warm-up44,47, others report inconsistent results35,48. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in exercise type, individual music preferences, or psychological factors such as self-confidence and arousal17,49. For instance, results on anaerobic threshold performance show, for example, that there is no increase in the threshold, although performance is improved in women50, whereas its impact on supramaximal efforts remains contested48,51. The impact of rhythm and tempo on physical performance remains an area of uncertainty, with mixed findings in the literature52. Thus, while music clearly interacts with multiple physiological and psychological domains, the precise mechanisms remain insufficiently understood, justifying continued investigation50,53.

The exclusive focus on female athletes is intentional: hormonal fluctuations across the menstrual cycle influence neuromuscular performance and can also modulate auditory processing and the ergogenic impact of music. Prior work shows sex-dependent responses to self-selected or preferred music during exercise, including in female collegiate athletes, and evidence that auditory processing and audio-motor integration vary across menstrual phases50–55. Moreover, previous research has shown that female athletes may respond differently to auditory stimuli compared to males, with variations in arousal regulation, affective responses, and motivation50,56,57. Despite these sex-specific factors, most chronobiological and music-performance studies have been conducted predominantly in male or mixed samples, leaving a critical gap in evidence for adolescent female populations.

The present study addresses this gap by investigating how motivational music may interact with time-of-day–dependent performance in female team-sport athletes. Based on the arousal hypothesis and chronobiological models of performance, we hypothesize that listening to self-selected motivational music during warm-up will enhance physical performance at all times of day, and particularly attenuate morning performance decrements when arousal and neuromuscular readiness are typically reduced.

Methods

Participants

The protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and discipline-specific guidelines for human chronobiology and sports science research and was approved by the local ethics committee (ISSEPK-0033/2024)58,59, written parental/guardian consent and athlete assent were obtained for minors. A priori power analysis in G*Power60, following Beck’s recommendations61, with α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and an anticipated effect size of 0.30 (based on prior work62 and author consensus) indicated a minimum of 16 participants. Of 37 athletes screened, 26 met inclusion criteria; two withdrew and six were excluded for menstrual-cycle reasons, yielding a final sample of 18 female handball players (age 16.16 ± 0.38 y; height 1.67 ± 0.09 m; body mass 57.68 ± 13.1 kg; BMI 20.28 ± 3.2 kg/m2) from the same regional team, each with > 3 years of training (4.66 ± 0.48 y) and ~ 3.77 ± 0.42 sessions per week. Inclusion criteria were: females aged 16–18 years; ≥3 years of organized handball; ≥2 sessions/week; injury-free ≥ 4 months; regular menstrual cycles in the prior 6 months; and no hormonal contraceptive use in the prior 6 months. Exclusion criteria were: medical conditions/medications that could affect performance; stimulant/narcotic/psychotropic drugs; dietary supplements or restrictive diets likely to alter hormonal balance within 3 months; PSQI > 5; alcohol/tobacco use; extreme chronotypes; and ear/hearing disorders. Chronotype was assessed with the Horne–Östberg questionnaire; only intermediate types (scores 42–58; sample 49.5 ± 4.56) were included63. Sleep quality in the preceding month was normal (PSQI 2.08 ± 0.83; ~7.22 ± 0.54 h/night) using the validated Arabic PSQI64. Menstrual cycles were tracked with the My Calendar® app65; to limit hormonal confounding, only sessions in the follicular and luteal phases were analyzed, in line with recommendations on cycle-related variability in neuromuscular and metabolic performance18,62,65, and athletes with irregular cycles or incompatible timing were excluded66–69.

Experimental procedure

Each athlete completed eight sessions (2 conditions × 4 times of day) in randomized order with 48–96 h between sessions. Testing occurred at 08:00, 11:00, 15:00 and 18:00, selected to bracket typical circadian troughs/peaks for short-term performance and oral temperature, with intermediate points providing evenly spaced intervals [63–65]. Participants avoided strenuous exercise and caffeine for ≥ 24 h, maintained regular sleep (≥ 7 h), replicated diet/hydration across sessions, and logged compliance; no illness/injury was reported. Upon arrival, resting oral temperature was measured sublingually with a calibrated digital thermometer (± 0.05 °C). After a standardized 10-min warm-up (light jog, dynamic stretches, sprint drills, progressive runs), athletes rested seated for 2 min, then performed: countermovement jump (CMJ), 2-kg medicine ball throw (MBT), 20-m sprint (0–5 m and 0–20 m splits via timing gates), and Illinois Agility Test (IAT). Three trials were completed for CMJ/MBT/20-m sprint (best retained; standard inter-trial recovery), and two for IAT (best retained). Familiarization preceded testing; test–retest reliability was excellent (ICC2,1: CMJ 0.91; MBT 0.89; 20 m-ST 0.93; IAT 0.87). Sessions were conducted indoors (≈ 22 °C; ≈47% RH). Music condition

In Yes-MUS, athletes listened to their single, self-selected motivational track throughout the warm-up via over-ear headphones at a standardized 80 dB (tempo ≥ 120 bpm; mean 136.5 ± 12.78 bpm; motivational quality verified with BMRI-2) 53,70,71 (Online Appendix 1). The same headphones were worn without audio in No-MUS to equate tactile/occlusive inputs. Tracks were looped as needed to cover the full warm-up. No genre/tempo subgroup analyses were planned.

The 2 kg medicine ball throw test (MBT)

Medicine ball throw is the most widely known and indirect test used to evaluate the power of the upper limbs in team sports72,73. The players throw the medicine ball as vigorously, far and straight forward as they can, while keeping their back flush against the wall, and their elbows in towards their sides during the push maneuver. Three maximal throws for distance were performed using a measuring tape and measurements were recorded in meters from the wall to where the medicine ball landed74. The best throw was retained for our analysis. With a recovery period of ten seconds between three repetitions the best performance was retained for our statistical analysis.

20-m sprint performance (20 m-ST)

The 20-m sprint test was administered as a test of acceleration and sprint ability. Linear speed was measured by a 20 m sprint with timing gates (Witty, Microgate®, Bolzano, Italy) setup at 0, 5 and 20 m to measure the 0–5 m and 0–20 m intervals. Timing gates were placed at the approximate hip height for all players as previously recommended75. Players were instructed to initiate the sprint when ready and cover the set distance as fast as possible. The subjects completed three trials of sprint, with a minimum of 3-min rest between each trial. The best performance from each of the 3 trials was used for analysis.

Illinois agility test (IAT)

The Illinois Agility Test incorporates acceleration, deceleration, change of direction, and sprinting. The change of direction Illinois Agility Test was reported to have high reliability and validity for team sports76,77. The length of the course is 10 m and the width is 5 m where four cones are used to mark the start, finish, and the two turning points. Four more cones are placed down the center an equal distance apart (spaced 3.3 m apart). The duration of their performance was quantified using timing gates (Witty, Microgate®, Bolzano, Italy) positioned at the beginning and end points, and the superior outcome from the two trials was documented. Players were given instructions to maximize their running speed while following the designated course in the specified direction to reach the endpoint.

Statistical analysis

Data are reported as mean ± SD. Normality was checked with Shapiro–Wilk. A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA tested Music (Yes-MUS vs. No-MUS) × Time-of-day (08:00, 11:00, 15:00, 18:00), with Tukey’s HSD post hoc where appropriate. Partial eta-squared (ηp2) quantified ANOVA effects (≈ 0.01 small, 0.06 moderate, 0.14 large)78; pairwise effects used Cohen’s d (≤ 0.20 trivial; 0.20–0.60 small; 0.60–1.20 moderate; 1.20–2.0 large; 2.0–4.0 very large; >4.0 extremely large)79. Significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. To contextualize individual responses, the Smallest Worthwhile Change (SWC) was defined as 0.2 × between-subject SD at baseline (08:00, No-MUS)79; thresholds were 0.388 cm (CMJ), 0.176 m (MBT), 0.062 s (20 m-ST), and 0.100 s (IAT). Analyses were performed in STATISTICA (StatSoft, France).

Results

Oral temperature

Two-way Anova revealed a moderate effect with statistical significance for TOD [F (3,51) = 35.2, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.67], However, there were no significant effects observed for MUS [F (1,17) = 1.7, p = 0.2, ηp2 = 0.09] or for the interaction between MUS and TOD [F (3,51) = 0.2, p = 0.91, ηp2 = 0.009]. Bonferroni test indicated that core temperature was considerably higher at 18:00 h compared to 8:00 h across No-MUS and Yes-MUS (both p < 0.001). The morning-to-afternoon differences in core temperature showed amplitudes of 3.2% under both testing conditions (No-MUS and Yes-MUS) (Table 1).

CMJ test

A statistically significant main effect of MUS was observed on CMJ height (F(1,17) = 70.58, p < 0.001), with a large effect size (η2p = 0.80). A small significant effect of TOD [F (3,51) = 13.85, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.44] on CMJ height. No significant effects were observed on the interaction of MUS and TOD [F (3,51) = 0.92, p = 0.43, ηp2 = 0.05].

Post hoc analysis revealed an improvement on CMJ performance from 08:00 h to 18:00 h (p < 0.001, 5.7%) under No-MUS condition. The morning-afternoon performance differences were blunted under Yes-MUS condition (p < 0.001, 2.3%) (Table 1). Regarding Music effects, CMJ performance increased significantly during 08:00 h (p < 0.001, 4.2%), 11:00 h (p < 0.01, 3.1%), 15:00 h. (p < 0.01, 3%) and 18:00 h. (p < 0.05, 2.4%) compared to No-MUS condition (Table 1).

The 2 kg medicine ball throw test

Statistical analysis of MBT performance indicated a moderate significant effect for MUS [F (1,17) = 36.31, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.68], along with a small significant effect of TOD [F (3,51) = 19.97, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.54]. Likewise, no significant effects were revealed for the interaction of MUS and TOD [F (3,51) = 1.77, p = 0.16, ηp2 = 0.09].

MBT performance was observed to improve in the afternoon compared to the morning (p < 0.001, 13%) under No-MUS condition. However, this daily diurnal variation was blunted under Yes-MUS condition (p < 0.001, 6.6%). Regarding Music effects, a significant improvement in MBT performance were observed during 08:00 h (p < 0.001, 8.9%), 11:00 h (p < 0.01, 6.4%), 15:00 h. (p < 0.05, 5.9%) compared to No-MUS condition (Table 1).

20-m Sprint test (20 m-ST)

The results of the two-way ANOVA showed a moderate significant main effect of MUS [F (1,17) = 69.50, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.80] on 20 m-ST. A small significant main effect of TOD [F (3,51) = 9.01, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.34], as well as the interaction of MUS and TOD [F (3,51) = 4.56, p < 0.01, ηp2 = 0.21] were revealed. According to the post hoc test, 20 m-ST were greater in the afternoon compared to the morning for conditions (p < 0.001, − 5.9%) during No-MUS condition. Further investigations showed that these daily morning to afternoon variation were diminished during Yes-MUS condition (p < 0.001, − 3.3%), as presented in Table 1. Regarding the impact of Music, 20 m-ST time significantly decreased during 08:00 h (p < 0.001, − 4.8%), 11:00 h (p < 0.001, − 3.3%), 15:00 h. (p < 0.001, − 3.7%), and 18:00 h (p < 0.05, − 1.9%) compared to No-MUS condition (Table 1).

Illinois Agility test (IAT)

A moderate significant effect on IAT were observed for both MUS [F (1,17) = 62.63, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.78] and TOD [F (3,51) = 82.47, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.82] on agility performance. Additionally, a small significant effect on interaction between MUS and TOD [F (3,51) = 15.04, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.46] was recorded. Post hoc analysis revealed that IAT performance were lower in the morning compared to the afternoon (p < 0.001, − 7.1%) under No-MUS condition. Interestingly, these morning-afternoon differences were blunted under Yes-MUS condition (p < 0.001, − 4.7%) (Table 1). Compared to No-MUS condition, IAT time significantly decreased during 08:00 h (p < 0.001, − 3.9%), 11:00 h (p < 0.001, − 3.6%), 15:00 h. (p < 0.001, − 3.2%), and 18:00 h (p < 0.01, − 1.3%) (Table 1).

Discussion

This study examined whether self-selected motivational music during warm-up modulates time-of-day (TOD) effects on short-term, high-intensity performance in adolescent female handball players. Three clear findings emerged: (i) CMJ, MBT, 20 m sprint, and agility improved across the day and peaked at 18:00 h; (ii) music enhanced performance at all TODs, most notably in the morning; and (iii) the diurnal amplitude was attenuated under the music condition. Beyond statistical significance, predefined SWC thresholds (e.g., 0.062 s for the 20 m sprint) showed a larger proportion of athletes achieved meaningful gains with music across all TODs, underscoring practical relevance.

Our TOD pattern aligns with previous reports in female team-sport athletes and the meta-analytic window for peak anaerobic output in the late afternoon30,31,80,81. Extending this literature, we show that warm-up music provides additional benefits specifically when performance is typically suboptimal (08:00–11:00 h), consistent with prior work—largely in males—on music’s ergogenicity during preparatory phases42,44,56,82. The reduction of morning-to-afternoon gaps, particularly for sprint and agility, indicates that motivational music can partially offset circadian-related decrements in explosive tasks among female adolescents.

Mechanistically, superior afternoon performance has been linked to higher core temperature, enhanced enzymatic activity, muscle compliance, and nerve-conduction velocity83–85. In our cohort, best results coincided with highest oral temperature at 18:00 h, supporting a temperature-facilitation account. Morning attenuation in the No-MUS condition is compatible with residual sleep inertia and lower arousal in early hours, commonly reported in youth populations80,86,87. Music likely acts through psychological and central pathways heightened arousal, motivation, attentional focus to elevate psychomotor readiness when the body is less primed, which explains the larger morning benefits44,56,88. Notably, incremental improvements were still observed in the afternoon, suggesting music functions both as a compensatory aid at troughs and as a mild booster near peaks.

From an applied standpoint, self-selected tracks during warm-up offer a low-cost, non-invasive strategy to stabilize daily performance profiles when scheduling cannot align with peak circadian windows. Individual-level interpretation via SWC complements group effects and helps practitioners identify athletes most responsive to the intervention.

Several limitations warrant caution. Findings pertain to adolescent female handball players and isolated performance tests rather than match play; ecological validity during competitions remains to be established. We did not collect baseline physiological capacities (e.g., VO₂max or 1-RM), which limits between-study comparability. Finally, the absence of placebo/sham and blinding means expectancy effects cannot be ruled out in a music intervention52,53. Future work should incorporate neutral or low-arousal music controls, blinded procedures where feasible, objective arousal indices (e.g., HRV, cortisol, EEG), and game-relevant outcomes.

Conclusion

Short-term, high-intensity performance in adolescent female handball players peaks in the late afternoon, but self-selected motivational music during warm-up enhances performance at all times of day and meaningfully narrows morning deficits. Implemented pragmatically, personalized music can help mitigate TOD-related variability when optimal scheduling is impractical. Further research should clarify underlying neurophysiology and generalizability across sports, populations, and performance domains.
