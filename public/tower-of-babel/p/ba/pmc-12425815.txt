A Case for the Use of Combined Hormonal Therapy for Aggressive Angiomyxoma: Complete Response to Goserelin and Letrozole Following Incomplete Surgical Resection
Authors: George Mallat, Annabelle Farnsworth, Florian Honeyball, Michael Solomon, Peter Grimison
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report on the use of combined hormonal therapy with a gonadotropin releasing hormone antagonist and aromatase inhibitor in pelvic aggressive angiomyxoma, with a complete response.Keywords: aggressive angiomyxoma, aromatase inhibitor, gonadotropin releasing hormone agonist, hormonal therapy

Introduction

Aggressive angiomyxoma (AA) is a rare mesenchymal neoplasm of females, usually involving the pelvis, vulvovaginal region, perineum, and urinary bladder [1]. Classified as a ‘tumour of uncertain differentiation’, AA is typically locally aggressive, but metastases are rare [2]. Due to its slow growing but infiltrative nature, there is often extensive local invasion by the time of diagnosis, and a high rate of local recurrence even after complete excision which can occur up to 15 years following initial treatment [1]. Histopathology demonstrates spindled stromal cells, which generally lack mitotic activity, a myxoid extracellular matrix, and thick‐walled vessels of various caliber [1]. Immunohistochemistry is positive for desmin, estrogen receptors and progesterone receptors in 97% of AA [3]. Surgery remains the first line treatment of AA and can be curative [1]. Curative intent treatment requires wide local excision with negative resection margins, which can be challenging to achieve because of the infiltrative nature of the tumor and the absence of a defined capsule, and commonly requires extensive and mutilating surgery (e.g., requiring pelvic exenteration) [4]. The role of hormone therapy as an adjunct or alternative to surgery is not established.

Case History, Examination and Differential Diagnosis

We present the case of a 51‐year‐old, pre‐menopausal female with pelvic AA who was treated with hormonal therapy after incomplete surgical excision, with exceptional response. Her medical history included previous tubal ligation and varicose vein surgery; she was a non‐smoker. She presented with symptoms of vaginal prolapse with occasional per‐vaginal discharge, urinary incontinence, constipation, pain on defaecation, and then a palpable golf‐ball sized mass of the vagina. A pelvic ultrasound 03/2023 (Figure 1) demonstrated a tubular structure in the right adnexa extending to the region of the vagina, and a hypoechoic collection in the pelvis, measuring 158 × 94 × 61 mm (Figure 1).

Pelvic ultrasound showing continuous hypoechoic adnexal structure with low‐level internal echoes.

Upon examination, she was thought to have a simple vaginal cyst and so underwent transvaginal resection on 17/04/2023, by an outside gynecological surgeon. The mass was more extensive than initially thought and so only part of the tumor was resected, leaving a portion in situ. Histopathology unexpectedly showed an enormous (320 × 100 × 40 mm, 555 g) aggressive angiomyxoma, with positive margins (Figure 2). Immunoperoxidase stains showed the cells to be strongly positive for desmin and estrogen and progesterone receptors (Figure 3), as well as CD34. They were negative for SMA and SM100, myogenin, and myo D1. The mitotic figure count was < 1/10 hpf.

Histopathology with H&E staining demonstrating small oval and spindling cells in abundant loose myxomatous stroma, with focal areas of increased cellularity. There is cytological atypia, and mitotic activity is absent.

Immunoperoxidase stain for estrogen (left) and progesterone (right) receptors demonstrating 100% strong nuclear‐positive staining.

Post‐operative MRI pelvis 16/05/23 revealed significant (120 × 111 × 59 mm) residual pelvic disease with direct involvement of the right posterior lateral lower third of the vaginal wall, external anal sphincter, intersphincteric plane, right mesorectum, and right ischio‐anal fossa with displacement of the uterus, vagina, bladder, and rectum, and possible involvement of the rectum, without metastatic disease (Figure 4). FDG PET CT on 14/06/2023 demonstrated mild metabolism in the pelvic mass with no evidence of distant disease.

Post‐operative MRI 05/2023 (axial and sagittal images) demonstrating a large residual, T2 hyperintense mass in the right pelvic sidewall. The enhanced retrovesical structure seen in the axial image is due to the use of vaginal gel.

The pelvic sarcoma multi‐disciplinary team recommended pelvic exenteration surgery as the only curative option, which the patient declined because of the extent of surgery and the requirement for a colostomy and ileal conduit. In July 2023, she was commenced on hormonal therapy with a gonadotrophin releasing hormone agonist (GnRH, goserelin 3.6 mg monthly) in combination with an aromatase inhibitor (letrozole 2.5 mg daily), with the initial aim to reduce the rate of growth and size of the tumor, and delay the time to surgery or reduce the extent of surgery required. Toxicities were of vasomotor symptoms and myalgias.

Conclusion and Results

A repeat MRI performed 10/10/23 demonstrated near complete resolution of the transpatial right pelvic side wall mass with only minimal residual linear soft tissue thickening/scarring along the right pelvic floor musculature at the site of the prior mass (Figure 5). The consensus of the multi‐disciplinary team was to continue with goserelin and letrozole with close monitoring and avoid surgery while there is a maintained excellent response to hormonal therapy. Close monitoring involves 3 monthly follow‐ups with assessment of symptoms and treatment toxicities and serial MRI for assessment of disease progress. Currently, the patient is 24 months post commencement of hormonal therapy with sustained complete response, which we define as no radiological evidence of residual or recurrent angiomyxoma. Repeat MRI 30/07/2025 demonstrates residual minor soft tissue scarring in the right pelvic sidewall, with no evidence of disease recurrence.

Repeat MRI after 3 months of hormonal treatment demonstrating tumor response, with only a small area of residual soft tissue thickening. The area marked in the figure on the right represents a simple right para‐ovarian/paratubal cyst.

Discussion

AA is derived from hormone‐sensitive stromal tissue in the lower genital tract and therefore typically affects women of reproductive age [2]. In view of the estrogen receptor status of most AA tumors, hormonal therapy has been used in several case reports. However, responses vary, and it appears unlikely that this treatment is curative in premenopausal women, as discontinuation of hormonal treatment usually leads to disease recurrence or progression [2]. Cases were identified through a Medline search of the PubMed database using the search terms “aggressive angiomyxoma” and “hormonal therapy” and restricted to female patients, publications available online, and published in English. In these case reports, we defined partial response as regression of angiomyxoma with radiological evidence of residual tumor; stable disease was defined as tumor that had not changed in size on repeat imaging.

As shown in the table, hormonal therapy has been used in three main scenarios: as neoadjuvant treatment in patients with locally advanced AA requiring morbid surgical procedures; as adjuvant treatment in patients with positive tumor margins; and in cases of tumor recurrence. In this sample, the average age of women who received hormonal therapy was 39 years. GnRH agonists were the most common hormonal therapy and appear to be effective in most cases, with most patients achieving complete or partial responses. SERMs and AIs also appear effective, particularly when used in combination with a GnRH agonist. The duration of treatment and follow‐up imaging varied significantly between the case reports. Aside from menopausal symptoms, no other adverse effects were reported in the above case studies, although follow‐up was short‐term.

To our knowledge, there are no cases in the literature reporting on the use of a GnRH agonist combined with an aromatase inhibitor. We elected to treat with combination hormonal therapy to achieve upfront synergistic estrogen suppression, due to the extent of the tumor in our patient. GnRH agonists achieve ovarian function suppression by suppressing the release of follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) from the pituitary gland, through downregulation of GnRH receptors [19]. However, peripheral estrogen production is unaffected. Aromatase inhibitors (AIs) suppress plasma estrogen levels by inhibition of the enzyme aromatase, which is responsible for the peripheral conversion of androgens to estrogens [20]. The combination of GnRH agonists with AIs causes greater estrogen suppression [21]. In hormone‐sensitive breast cancer, the addition of ovarian function suppression to tamoxifen or an AI appears to improve disease‐free survival, freedom from distant recurrence, and overall survival [21]. The most substantial survival benefits in breast cancer have been observed when ovarian function suppression was used in combination with an AI [21].

Combination hormonal therapy has also been used successfully in endometrial cancer. One pilot study explored fertility‐preserving treatment for obese women with endometrial cancer using a combination of a GnRH agonist and AI [22]. In this study, 6 patients with grade 1 endometrial cancer (EC) were treated with intramuscular triptorelin 3.75 mg every 4 weeks and letrozole 2.5 mg daily. Each of the patients achieved a complete response within the first 3–6 months, defined as regression of EC to hyperplasia or atypia based on transvaginal ultrasound and endometrial sampling. This was sustained out to 4 years of follow‐up, and 3 out of 6 patients were able to conceive during this time period. A retrospective cohort study investigated the efficacy of a combination of GnRH agonist with either a levonorgestrel‐releasing intrauterine device or AI in women with EC or atypical endometrial hyperplasia [23]. In this retrospective review of 179 patients who were unsuitable for high‐dose oral progestin, 95% of patients treated with GnRHa and letrozole achieved complete remission, and 42% of those who wished to conceive became pregnant. While further research is needed to evaluate longer‐term efficacy and fertility outcomes, these studies provide evidence that combination hormonal therapy has favorable oncological and reproductive outcomes in EC, a hormone‐sensitive tumor.

Although having a distinct origin and markedly lower malignant potential, AA is a hormone‐sensitive tumor. We would argue that a combination of a GnRH agonist and AI leads to a greater overall reduction in oestradiol levels and may lead to a more pronounced tumor response compared with GnRH agonist alone, as demonstrated in our patient, with minimal additional toxicity.

AA is a rare tumor and the optimal approach to treatment has not been established. Whilst surgical resection remains the treatment of choice, our case supports the use of combination hormonal therapy as an effective option when complete resection confers unacceptable morbidity. Further research is needed to determine timing (neoadjuvant vs. adjuvant) and duration of treatment, selection of hormone therapy, appropriate clinical endpoints (e.g., oestradiol levels) and to identify the disease and patient factors that can help predict the magnitude and durability of response. Identifying a biomarker for efficacy of treatment may also be a focus of future research.
