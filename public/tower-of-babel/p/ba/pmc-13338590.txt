Effect of pseudo-periosteum on hard and soft tissue regeneration following lateral bone augmentation using a titanium mesh: an experimental in vivo study
Authors: Seung-Hyun Park, Kyeong-Won Paeng, So-Ra Yoon, Goran I Benic, Daniel S Thoma, Ui-Won Jung, Ronald E Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aims of this study were 1) to evaluate the 16-week bone augmentation outcomes using a titanium mesh (TM), including an 8-week transmucosal healing period following TM removal, and 2) to investigate the impact of pseudo-periosteum formation on regenerative outcomes.MethodsAtrophic alveolar ridge defects were induced in 6 canine mandibles, and 5 peri-implant defects were created in each hemi-mandible. Bone augmentation was attempted using the following randomly allocated modalities: 1) Control: no treatment; 2) TM group: blood clot covered by TM; 3) TM + bone substitute (BS) group: synthetic BS covered by TM; 4) TM + collagen membrane (CM) group: blood clot covered by TM and CM; and 5) TM + BS + CM group: BS covered by TM and CM. TMs were removed after 8 weeks, followed by abutment connection and an additional 8-week healing period. After sacrifice, histomorphometric analyses of hard and soft tissues were conducted.ResultsRegardless of early TM exposure (5/30), all implants achieved successful osseointegration and demonstrated comparable bone regeneration outcomes. No significant differences in horizontal or vertical bone gain were observed among the groups, whereas superiority of the TM + BS group over the control group showed a higher augmented bone percentage (P=0.021). The thickness of the pseudo-periosteum was not associated with hard or soft tissue regeneration outcomes.ConclusionsTM removal after 8 weeks did not significantly affect the histologic outcomes of lateral bone augmentation performed with simultaneous implant placement. The presence of a pseudo-periosteum did not adversely influence either hard or soft tissue regenerative outcomes.Keywords: Alveolar ridge augmentation, Bone regeneration, Histology, Periosteum, Titanium

INTRODUCTION

Over the past decades, guided bone regeneration (GBR) has been established as a reliable technique for the horizontal augmentation of deficient alveolar ridges, aiming to restore the ridge to an ideal morphology [1]. Among the various techniques available, the use of titanium mesh (TM) has emerged as a viable option in GBR due to its ability to provide stable space maintenance [2]. The rigid structure of TM enables mechanical protection of the blood clot, thereby facilitating bone regeneration through the migration and proliferation of osteoprogenitor cells. However, the porous structure of TM has been identified as one of the major drawbacks of this approach. While the presence of pores allows proper vascular supply to the underlying graft materials, thereby supporting bone regeneration [3], these pores do not ensure complete cell occlusiveness, which is considered one of the prerequisites of GBR, and may be clinically associated with a higher incidence of wound dehiscence [4,5,6]. To overcome these drawbacks, various strategies have been investigated, primarily involving the incorporation of additional biomaterials.

The addition of particulate bone substitute materials or resorbable membranes in conjunction with TM has been extensively investigated [7,8,9]. An in vivo study conducted on rabbit parietal bone demonstrated that the combination of a non-crosslinked collagen membrane (CM) and particulate bone materials yielded the most favorable outcomes in terms of newly formed bone [7]. In contrast, a subsequent study in rats reported that the use of a crosslinked CM did not provide significant benefits for bone regeneration [8]. Collectively, these findings underscore the need to identify the key factors required to achieve optimal bone regeneration when using TMs.

The presence of a highly dense connective tissue layer on top of the TM at the time of re-entry after bone augmentation has been reported in multiple studies and is commonly referred to as “pseudo-periosteum” [2,10,11]. Histologic findings from experimental in vivo studies indicate that this specific layer consists of circumferential, densely arranged fibers interwoven through the pores of the TM. This dense layer does not appear to provoke inflammatory reactions and exhibits minimal angiogenesis [12].

Although the existence of pseudo-periosteum has been recognized for some time, its association with bone regeneration has not yet been clearly elucidated. The first study to describe pseudo-periosteum suggested that its presence may help prevent graft resorption and postoperative infection [13]. In contrast to previous findings demonstrating that the periosteum prevents fibrous tissue ingrowth [14], a preclinical study reported that pseudo-periosteum induces fibroblast migration, which may, in turn, impede bone regeneration [15]. The difficulty of performing multiple re-entries in clinical settings further limits investigations into the potential role of pseudo-periosteum.

Our research group previously reported the formation of pseudo-periosteum in an experimental in vivo study examining the outcomes of lateral bone augmentation with TMs following submerged healing [16]. The study evaluated radiographic and histomorphometric outcomes after TM-assisted ridge augmentation during a 16-week submerged healing period using various combinations of synthetic bone graft materials and crosslinked CMs, demonstrating comparable outcomes among the different modalities. Whether these observed tendencies would be maintained during a transmucosal healing period following earlier TM removal represents an additional question of interest addressed in the present experiment.

The present study aims 1) to assess the extent of bone regeneration following GBR with TMs and simultaneous dental implant placement after an initial 8-week healing period, followed by abutment connection, TM removal, and an additional 8-week healing period, and 2) to conduct further analyses of pseudo-periosteum formation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was approved by the Bioethics Committee regarding animal selection, management, and surgical procedures at the Animal Care and Use Committee of Yonsei Medical Center, Seoul, Republic of Korea (approval No. 2016–0165). The study adhered to the Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments guidelines [17].

This investigation aimed to evaluate 16-week bone augmentation outcomes using TMs, consisting of an initial 8-week submerged healing period followed by TM removal and abutment connection, and an additional 8-week transmucosal healing period. All surgical procedures have been reported previously in detail [16].

Experimental and technical design

Animals

Six mongrel dogs (age: 1 year; weight: 35–40 kg) with a healthy periodontium were used. Each dog was housed individually in an indoor run (W 2,000 mm × D 1,200 mm × H 1,500 mm) under controlled environmental conditions, including an ambient temperature of 22±2°C, relative humidity of 50±10%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The dogs were fed a standard pellet diet (Purina Canine Lab Diet, Cargill, Dangjin, Korea) throughout the study and had ad libitum access to water, which was filtered through a reverse osmosis system with ultraviolet sterilization.

Materials

Prefabricated TMs (CTi-mem B9 type, Neobiotech, Seoul, Korea) were used, with 4 meshes applied per animal. A biphasic calcium phosphate bone substitute (Osteon III, GENOSS, Suwon, Korea) and a crosslinked CM (Collagen Membrane-P, GENOSS) were used in selected test groups. The control group received no additional biomaterials. These biomaterials were applied with or without TMs to evaluate their individual and combined effects in the context of GBR.

Surgical procedures

An atrophic alveolar ridge was induced in 6 canine mandibles, with 5 peri-implant defects created in a single hemi-mandible (Figure 1). The following treatment modalities were randomly allocated using web-based software (sealedenvelope.com):

All surgical procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon (U.W.J.). General anesthesia was administered via inhalation of isoflurane (Forane, Choongwae Pharmaceutical, Seoul, Korea), followed by intravenous injection of xylazine (Rompun, Bayer Korea, Seoul, Korea) in combination with zolazepam and tiletamine (Zoletil, Virbac, Carros, France).

Mandibular premolars and first molars were extracted, and the buccal plate was removed to create narrow ridge defects measuring 5 mm in the apico-coronal dimension.

After 8 weeks of healing, a mucoperiosteal flap was reflected on the selected hemi-mandible of each animal to expose the buccal bone, and 5 dental implants (Ø 4.3×8 mm, Implantium, Dentium, Seoul, Korea) were placed at equal intervals along the ridge (Figure 2A and B).

Serial clinical photographs of the procedure. (A-D) Implant placement with guided bone regeneration in an experimental narrow ridge at 8 weeks after extraction; (E) stitch removal after 2 weeks; (F-I) titanium mesh removal and abutment connection at 8 weeks after implant placement.

Defects measuring 3 mm in apico-coronal height were created at the mesial, distal, and buccal aspects of each implant site. Except for the control group, all other groups were treated with prefabricated TMs (Figure 2C). Depending on group allocation, the biphasic calcium phosphate bone substitute was placed beneath the TM, the CM was applied over the TM, or both materials were used together. Each TM (12×10 mm) was manually shaped to fit the buccal contour and secured to the implant shoulder with a cover cap, with its bendable wings stabilizing the graft materials. Finally, the flaps were sutured using single interrupted sutures to allow submucosal healing at all implant sites (Figure 2D).

TM removal

At 8 weeks after bone augmentation, TMs and cover caps were removed from all implants, except those in the control group, and medium-sized titanium healing abutments (5.5 mm in diameter and 5.0 mm in height) were subsequently connected (Figure 2F-I).

Sacrifice

At 16 weeks after bone augmentation and implant placement, all animals were euthanized by injection of an overdose of potassium chloride-40 (Daihan, Seoul, Korea) following the induction of full anesthesia. To obtain samples for radiographic and histologic analyses, the implants and surrounding circumferential soft tissues were carefully examined and dissected.

Histologic preparation

Bone blocks containing each implant site were dissected and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin. The blocks were dehydrated using graded ethanol solutions and embedded in resin for ground sectioning. Specimens were sectioned bucco-lingually, and the central sections were selected, reduced to a final thickness of approximately 20 μm, and stained with Goldner’s trichrome stain. Digital histologic images were captured using optical microscopy (BX50, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Histomorphometric analysis

Histomorphometric measurements were performed in a blinded manner by an experienced examiner (K.W.P.) using a computer-based image analysis system (Photoshop, Adobe, San Jose, CA, USA). All measurements were conducted according to a previously published protocol [18], with the implant fixture platform serving as the reference point.

Correlation analysis by pseudo-periosteum thickness

To evaluate the relationship between pseudo-periosteum thickness and histomorphometric outcomes, correlation analyses were performed between (a) P–B (vertical distance from the implant platform to the first BIC, mm) and the mean pseudo-periosteum thickness, and (b) the soft tissue vertical dimension and the mean pseudo-periosteum thickness, for each treatment group.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated using statistical software (G*Power version 3.1.9.7, Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on findings from a previous preclinical study evaluating the effectiveness of supplementary bone graft materials and membranes in vertical augmentation with TMs [7], the combined use of biomaterials resulted in a significant increase in the percentage of newly formed bone. Considering a mean difference of 8.6% and a standard deviation (SD) of 6.5%, the effect size was set at 0.65. Assuming an effect size of 0.65, an α error of 0.05, and a statistical power of 70%, the total required sample size was calculated to be 30 (5 samples per animal), accounting for the 5 experimental groups and the anticipated attrition ratio.

All data were presented as mean ± SD and median (first and third quartiles) values. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The Friedman test was used to detect differences among the treatment modalities. When statistically significant differences were identified (P<0.05), post hoc analyses were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with the adjusted criterion for statistical significance set at P<0.005. To minimize inter-individual variability, a linear mixed-effects model was applied, with individual animals included as a random effect. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to assess the association between pseudo-periosteum thickness and histomorphometric parameters.

RESULTS

Clinical and histologic findings

Early mesh exposure occurred in 5 of the 30 implants: 1 case in the TM group, 1 case in the TM + BS group, 2 cases in the TM + CM group, and 1 case in the TM + BS + CM group. All exposures were detected on the day of stitch removal. The exposed TMs were located predominantly in the anterior regions, with 2 of the 5 cases occurring in the first premolar area and 3 in the second premolar area. None of the exposed sites exhibited inflammatory signs or adverse symptoms until the study endpoint at 16 weeks, and all specimens were included in the histomorphometric analysis.

Regardless of early wound dehiscence, all histologic sections (n=30) demonstrated successful osseointegration of all implants. Complete resolution of the peri-implant dehiscence defect, achieved through NB formation within the augmented area, was observed in 2 specimens in the TM + BS group and in 1 specimen in the TM + BS + CM group. The remaining specimens in the test groups showed partial defect recovery through newly formed bone.

Compared with the control group, the use of TMs was associated with a reduced incidence of buccal dehiscence (Figure 5A-E). The pseudo-periosteum observed alongside TMs in the previous study [16] remained present after the transmucosal healing period following mesh removal (Figure 5F). However, the previously reported grid-like pattern of pseudo-periosteum, caused by collagen fibers interposing through the mesh pores, was no longer observed. No inflammatory cell infiltration was detected adjacent to the pseudo-periosteum, although a limited number of small-diameter blood vessels were present.

Correlation analysis based on the pseudo-periosteum thickness

No significant correlation was identified between mean pseudo-periosteum thickness and fixture dehiscence, expressed as P–B (vertical distance from the implant platform to the first BIC) (P>0.05) (Supplementary Figure 1). DISCUSSION

The present data are part of a series of experimental in vivo studies reporting on hard tissue regeneration and the formation of pseudo-periosteum during lateral bone augmentation using TMs [16]. The objectives of this study were to investigate 1) peri-implant hard and soft tissue dimensions following TM removal and 2) the correlation between the presence of pseudo-periosteum and hard and soft tissue regeneration.

To date, a clear consensus regarding the influence of pseudo-periosteum formation on bone regeneration or complication rates has not been established [20]. In a randomized controlled trial (RCT) using polytetrafluoroethylene membranes, the addition of CM was reported to prevent soft-tissue ingrowth, resulting in a decrease in pseudo-periosteum thickness [21]; however, this finding did not translate into improved bone formation. Similarly, a recent RCT employing TMs demonstrated that the presence of pseudo-periosteum was not associated with the amount of newly formed bone, although it was accompanied by an increase in dense connective tissue and residual graft material [22]. Moreover, a previous case report noted that intentional exposure of the pseudo-periosteum layer after mesh removal contributed to the formation of new adherent gingiva and subsequent vestibular depth gain, thereby suggesting a potential biological role of pseudo-periosteum in soft-tissue healing [23].

When comparing hard tissue regeneration outcomes among the treatment groups, the addition of biomaterials (BS or CM) did not result in significantly greater NB formation (Tables 1 and 2). This trend is consistent with the findings of our previous study, in which histomorphometric analysis was performed following submerged healing [16]. Nevertheless, the combination of TM and BS was the only modality that demonstrated a statistically significant increase in augmented bone percentage compared with the control group (Supplementary Table 1). This finding suggests that the addition of BS may exert a beneficial effect on osteoconductivity, even within the completely occlusive environment created by a non-resorbable barrier.

The present study identified a dense connective tissue layer persisting after TM removal, consistent with findings from a previous human autopsy report [24]. Distinguishing the outer and inner layers before mesh removal was not possible due to histologic homogeneity, indicating integration of the outer and inner pseudo-periosteum layers after mesh removal. Overall, the percentage of NB formation ranged from 8.9% to 26.4% across treatment modalities following transmucosal healing, which is comparable to the previously reported range of 0.4% to 32.1% under submerged healing conditions [16]. This observation further supports prior findings indicating that regenerative potential is independent of the implant healing mode (submerged vs. transmucosal healing) [25].

Most sites healed uneventfully without early mesh exposure (5/30); however, complete resolution of the peri-implant dehiscence defect was not achieved in the majority of cases (27/30). This observation is consistent with previous clinical studies suggesting that achieving complete resolution through lateral bone augmentation is challenging in non-contained defects, regardless of the treatment modality used [26]. At sites with early TM exposure, only incomplete defect resolution was observed, often accompanied by dispersion of graft materials into surrounding areas. These findings are consistent with an earlier study indicating a negative correlation between the extent of TM exposure and the amount of regenerated bone [27]. Collectively, the formation of a pseudo-periosteum layer around the TM does not appear to function effectively as a barrier following early exposure.

The addition of CM in lateral augmentation using TM did not demonstrate a significant synergistic effect on bone regeneration, consistent with previous studies (Tables 1 and 2) [8,9]. Since its introduction in GBR [28], CMs have been widely utilized for various bone augmentation indications. Their combined use with TM has been suggested to 1) reduce the incidence of wound dehiscence [29] and, 2) enhance NB formation [7]. In particular, crosslinked CMs have attracted attention due to their superior physical properties and lower resorption rates [30,31], and have demonstrated favorable outcomes in horizontal bone augmentation even without additional fixation devices [32]. In contrast, a recent series of studies indicated that the addition of crosslinked CM did not result in significant differences in postoperative complications or bone regenerative outcomes [22,33,34], corroborating the findings of the present study (Tables 1 and 2). Notably, unlike the crosslinked CM used in the present study, previous reports demonstrating improved bone regeneration with membrane addition employed non-crosslinked native CMs [7,21]. Accordingly, further investigation is warranted to determine whether the crosslinking properties of the overlying CM influence healing responses and regenerative outcomes.

Pseudo-periosteum is commonly observed in bone augmentation procedures using non-resorbable membranes, and a classification system based on thickness has been proposed [35]. In a prospective cohort study of 30 patients, clinical assessment using a periodontal probe demonstrated that more than half of cases exhibited pseudo-periosteum thicknesses of less than 1 mm [33]. In the present study, histomorphometric analysis revealed mean pseudo-periosteum thicknesses ranging from 0.59 to 0.77 mm, consistent with the previous report (Figure 7). These values were also comparable to those observed under submerged healing conditions in a previous study [16], which ranged from 0.6 to 1.0 mm. Although not directly compared within identical sites, these findings suggest that pseudo-periosteum thickness may decrease after TM removal.

The correlation analysis demonstrated comparable vertical bone gain irrespective of pseudo-periosteum thickness (Supplementary Figure 1A). This finding corroborates results from a previous study reporting no substantial differences in NB formation according to pseudo-periosteum thickness (type I: 34.88%, type II: 34.19%, type III: 36.05%) [22]. Discrepancies in the absolute amounts of NB formation between the 2 studies may be attributed to differences in specimen location. In the clinical study by Cucchi et al. [33], specimens were obtained as core biopsies at the dental implant site, whereas in the present study, histomorphometric analysis was performed on the peripheral (buccal) side of the implants.

It is well established that the vertical dimension of healthy peri-implant mucosa consists of approximately 1.9 mm of epithelial attachment and 1.7 mm of connective tissue attachment [19]. Peri-implant dehiscence defects accelerate marginal bone loss under plaque-accumulated conditions [36], while thin peri-implant mucosa is particularly associated with increased susceptibility to progressive bone loss over time [37,38]. In the present study, comparable soft tissue dimensions were observed across treatment groups, with no notable correlation with bone regeneration outcomes. While the formation of pseudo-periosteum was evident throughout the test groups, it did not result in an increased thickness of connective tissue attachment above the mesh. Although pseudo-periosteum has been proposed to contribute to graft stabilization and establishment of a favorable peri-implant mucosal structure [12], further investigation is required to clarify whether its overall impact is beneficial or detrimental.

The present study has several limitations: 1) due to ethical considerations, the number of animals approved for experimental use was limited; 2) the short mandibular arch length of the mongrel dogs resulted in augmentation sites being positioned relatively close to each other, which may have influenced the healing process; 3) the absence of radiographic measurements for qualitative analysis limited comprehensive interpretation of the findings; and 4) anatomical differences in ridge morphology between anterior and posterior regions may have contributed to variations in complication rates and bone regenerative capacity.

In conclusion, TM removal after 8 weeks in lateral bone augmentation may not substantially affect bone regeneration in implant dehiscence defects, within the limits of this preclinical model. Quantitative and qualitative hard and soft tissue dimensional outcomes were comparable regardless of pseudo-periosteum thickness.
