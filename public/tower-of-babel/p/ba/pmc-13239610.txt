Impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on obesity: a prospective cohort study of the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA)
Authors: Mohammad Nazmus Sakib, Lauren E Griffith, Urun Erbas Oz, Nicole E Basta, Hertzel C Gerstein, Jason D Morgenstern, Susan Kirkland, Lehana Thabane, Brendan T Smith, Cynthia Balion, Jacqueline M McMillan, Parminder Raina, Laura N Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo examine the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on body mass index (BMI) and obesity status among Canadian residents and explore how this association varied by sociodemographic and health status.DesignProspective cohort study.SettingCanada.Participants41 302 adults, aged 45–85 at baseline, participating in the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging.Primary and secondary outcomesBMI and BMI-defined obesity were measured at baseline, follow-up 1 and follow-up 2 (FUP2), with 33% of FUP2 data (n=13 444) gathered after 16 March 2020, when COVID-19 restrictions began. Correction factors were applied for self-reported BMI and weighted generalised estimating equations assessed BMI changes before and during the pandemic.ResultsWe found a significant interaction between follow-up time and timing of FUP2 data collection (before or during the pandemic). Participants measured during the pandemic had an excess BMI increase of 0.21 kg/m² (95% CI 0.15 to 0.28) and 1.06 times higher odds of obesity (95% CI 1.03 to 1.09) compared with prepandemic trends. Increases were more pronounced among females, middle-aged adults and those without diabetes.ConclusionsThe COVID-19 pandemic was associated with a modest increase in BMI and obesity among Canadian adults. Ongoing research is needed to assess long-term trends.Keywords: COVID-19, Obesity, PUBLIC HEALTH, Aging

Introduction

Obesity is a major public health concern in Canada and globally.1 In Canada, obesity rates have risen steadily over the past two decades. According to the 2022 Canadian Community Health Survey, nearly one in three Canadians aged 18 and older (30%) had obesity in 2022, an increase from just over one in five (21%) in 2003.2 3 The COVID-19 pandemic and associated public health restrictions had a profound impact on health and health behaviours. Numerous potential mechanisms for an increase in obesity during the pandemic have been proposed, including lifestyle changes, particularly reduced physical activity and increased food consumption,4 increased stress and mental health concerns,5 and reduced access to healthcare.6

Systematic reviews examining the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on body weight and body mass index (BMI) have reported an increase during the pandemic.4 7 8 For instance, a meta-analysis found that BMI significantly increased during the first year of the pandemic compared with the pre-pandemic period, with a pooled mean difference in BMI of 0.38 kg/m².7 Although most studies reported an increase in body weight and BMI, this trend was heterogeneous across population subgroups, with mixed results by sex9,12 and age, with some studies suggesting that older adults were susceptible to significant weight loss and malnutrition.13 14 Differences have also been observed by prepandemic weight status15,17 and among people with diabetes.18 19 The objective of this study was to examine the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on BMI and obesity status among Canadian residents and explore how this association varied by sociodemographic and health status, using data from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA).

Methods

Study design and data source

A prospective cohort study was conducted using data from the CLSA. The CLSA is a long-term national prospective cohort study that enrolled 51 338 Canadian men and women who were between 45 and 85 years old at the time of recruitment.20 21 The participants were recruited from all Canadian provinces at baseline between 2010 and 2015. The CLSA collects follow-up data every 3 years and plans to continue this for 20 years or until participants die or are lost to follow-up. In addition to baseline data, the CLSA completed follow-up 1 (FUP1) and follow-up 2 (FUP2) data collection from 2015 to 2018 and 2018 to 2021, respectively. Notably, COVID-19 pandemic restrictions were implemented in Canada midway through the FUP2 data collection, and 16 170 participants provided FUP2 data after the onset of the travel ban on 16 March 2020, creating a natural experiment with some participants having FUP2 data collection before implementation of COVID restrictions (Spring 2015 to 15 March 2020) and some after (16 March 2020 to Sept 2021).

The CLSA consists of two cohorts: Tracking and Comprehensive. The Tracking cohort included 21 241 participants at baseline who were interviewed over the phone and were randomly sampled (within age/sex strata) across 10 Canadian provinces. The Comprehensive cohort included 30 097 participants who were interviewed at home and visited a nearby data collection centre for physical assessments and to provide biological samples. The Comprehensive cohort participants were sampled from addresses within a 25–50 km radius of the 11 data collection sites in seven provinces. The details of the sampling strategy and recruitment process for the CLSA have been published elsewhere.21

Participants

The CLSA included community-living adults aged 45–85 years at the time of recruitment. At baseline, people were excluded if they were living in institutions (including long-term care institutions) or First Nations reserves, full-time members of the Canadian Armed Forces, unable to respond in English or French or with cognitive impairment were excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patients and the public were not involved in any stage of this study.

Dependent variables

BMI. The primary dependent variable for this study is BMI, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres. For participants in the CLSA Comprehensive cohort, height and weight were measured in person by trained staff using standard protocols at data collection sites. However, during the FUP2 data collection, a subset of the CLSA participants (n=16 170) reported their height and weight via telephone on or after 16 March 2020, due to pandemic restrictions which prevented in-person study assessments. In contrast, height and weight for the Tracking cohort were self-reported and collected by phone at all time points. Self-reported BMI is subject to social desirability bias;22 23 to address this we applied a correction factor to the self-reported BMI values.24 25 Obesity was defined as a binary variable following the WHO cut-off of 30 kg/m2 or higher.26

Independent variables

Time variable. The time variable in the analysis represents the data collection waves of the CLSA. The current analysis incorporates data from three waves: baseline, FUP1 and FUP2. Each participant contributed data for these three waves, except for those with missing observations due to death or loss to follow-up. This variable was coded as 0 for the early phase (indicating baseline and FUP1) and 1 for FUP2, with the early phase serving as the reference category in the analysis. For a sensitivity analysis, we also used a time variable with three levels. For this purpose, the time variable was coded as 0 for baseline, 1 for FUP1 and 2 for FUP2, with baseline serving as the reference category in the analysis.

FUP2 data collection timing. Following the implementation of COVID-19 restrictions in Canada, a subset of CLSA participants provided data during the FUP2 wave. Consequently, the CLSA cohort can be divided into two groups based on when the FUP2 data was provided. This variable was coded as 0 ‘pre-pandemic’ for participants who provided FUP2 data before the pandemic restrictions (n=35 168), and 1 ‘during pandemic’, for participants who provided FUP2 data following the pandemic restrictions (n=16 170).

Covariates

The analysis adjusted for sociodemographic variables, including age, sex, ethnicity, income, education, and residence. Participants’ ages in years were categorised into four groups: 45–54, 55–64, 65–74 and 75 years and older. Missing age data at follow-ups were imputed using the median year difference between data collection waves and included as a time-varying covariate. Sex, ethnicity, income and education were based on self-report and coded as categorical variables. Residence was classified as urban or rural, based on postal codes.27 Variables that change over time, such as income and residence, were taken from the FUP2 data, while the other variables were taken from the baseline data.

Variables for subgroup analyses

We conducted subgroup analyses based on age group, sex, CLSA cohort (ie, Tracking vs Comprehensive), baseline BMI categories and baseline diabetes status. BMI was categorised according to the WHO’s standard thresholds.26 Diabetes status was determined from a self-report survey question in which participants were asked whether a doctor had ever informed them that they have diabetes, borderline diabetes or high blood sugar.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software, V.9.4. For the descriptive analysis of baseline characteristics, we calculated the mean (SD) for continuous variables and frequency (percentage (%)) for categorical variables. To examine changes in BMI over time (ie, continuous BMI outcomes), we used weighted generalised estimating equations (WGEE) models with an identity link through the PROC GEE procedure. Similarly, to analyse changes in the prevalence of obesity over time (ie, dichotomous obesity outcomes), we employed WGEE modelling with a logit link, also using the PROC GEE procedure. The weighted GEE approach28 is appropriate for analysing longitudinal data that includes missing observations and employs inverse probability-weighting to adjust for dropouts.29,31 This approach results in less biased estimates than traditional GEE; however, this procedure can only handle monotonous missingness, therefore individuals who provided intermittent BMI data (eg, at baseline and FUP2 but not at FUP1) were excluded from the analysis. Additionally, 293 participants with missing data on any covariates at baseline and 3624 who were reported deceased before FUP2 were also removed from the analyses. After applying these exclusions, we included 41 302 individuals in our longitudinal analysis, with n=27 858 in the prepandemic group and n=13 444 in the ‘during pandemic’ group (figure 1, table 1). Within the ‘during pandemic’ group, n=7254 individuals were from the Comprehensive cohort, and n=6190 were from the Tracking cohort.

We analysed both continuous BMI and binary obesity outcomes using three models. Model 1, the unadjusted model, included the time variable, FUP2 data collection timing and their interaction term. Model 2 adjusted for age and sex, while Model 3 included those same covariates plus further adjustments for ethnicity, household income, education level and urban/rural residence. The primary effect estimate of interest was the interaction term between the time variable and FUP2 data collection timing, which indicates the additional or excess change in BMI (or odds of obesity) during the pandemic, compared with the usual prepandemic trend from before 16 March 2020.

To examine how pandemic effects varied across different sociodemographic characteristics and health status, we conducted subgroup analyses by age group, sex, CLSA cohort type, baseline BMI, and diabetes status. Each subgroup analysis was performed using the fully adjusted model. Finally, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness and reliability of our findings. These analyses included using a three-level time variable and a different date for the onset of pandemic restrictions (eg, 16 September 2020). When using 16 September 2020 as the onset date for pandemic restrictions, we excluded participants who provided data between 16 March 2020 and 15 September 2020 (n=5385), resulting in a sample size of 35 917 for this analysis. A different date of pandemic onset was used in the sensitivity analysis, considering that weight gain and BMI changes require time; therefore, a time point 6 months apart would produce a stronger effect.

Results

Descriptive analysis

The baseline characteristics of the analytical sample (n=41 302) are summarised in table 1. The sample comprised 51.66% females, and 61% of participants were younger than 65 years, with 95.94% identifying as white. At baseline, 41.29% of participants were overweight, 30.88% had obesity and 16.31% reported having diabetes or high blood sugar. There were no significant differences in baseline BMI categories or obesity status between participants who completed FU2 before and after the pandemic (table 1).

Temporal changes in BMI and obesity status

For continuous BMI, there was an excess increase of 0.21 kg/m2 (95% CI 0.15 to 0.28) during the pandemic compared with the increasing prepandemic rate, after adjustment for age, sex, ethnicity, income, education and residence (table 2). The usual increase in BMI at FUP2 compared with the prepandemic period for the model with the interaction term present was 0.08 kg/m2 (95% CI 0.04 to 0.12). The results were similar in both the adjusted and unadjusted models. Similar findings emerged when a dichotomous obesity variable was used as the outcome variable (table 3). There was an excess increase in the odds of having obesity of 1.05 times (95% CI 1.03 to 1.07) during the pandemic compared with the usual prepandemic rate of increase, after adjusting for sociodemographic variables (table 3). The usual increase in the odds of having obesity at FUP2 compared with the prepandemic period, for the model with the interaction term included, was 1.06 times (95% CI 1.03 to 1.09).

Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analysis using continuous BMI outcomes suggests that females (β=0.30, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.40) experienced comparatively higher additional changes in BMI during the pandemic compared with males (β=0.17, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.25) (online supplemental table 1). However, the difference in the additional increase between males and females was not statistically significant (p=0.264) (online supplemental table 1). Regarding age groups, only middle-aged individuals showed a statistically significant additional increase in BMI during the pandemic (45–54 years: β=0.28, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.40; 55–64 years: β=0.33, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.45). For the CLSA cohort, the Comprehensive cohort primarily showed a statistically significant increase in BMI (β=0.42, 95% CI 0.33 to 0.51). Regarding health status, individuals with normal weight (β=0.42, 95% CI 0.31 to 0.52), overweight (β=0.20, 95% CI 0.13 to 0.28) and obesity (β=0.15, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.28) and people without diabetes (β=0.22, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.29) exhibited a statistically significant additional increase in BMI during the pandemic (online supplemental table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted the same analysis using a three-level time variable (baseline, FUP1 and FUP2) and observed similar results (figure 2). For example, the interaction effects between the three-level time variable and the FUP2 data collection timing indicated that the ‘during pandemic’ group showed an additional increase of 0.22 kg/m2 (95% CI 0.15 to 0.29) in BMI during FUP2; however, their additional changes at FUP1 were not significant (0.01, 95% CI −0.05 to 0.07) (online supplemental table 3). Similarly, for the dichotomous obesity outcome, the ‘during pandemic’ group had 1.07 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.11) times excess odds of having obesity during the pandemic, with no evidence of a significant difference at FUP1 (OR=1.01, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.04) (online supplemental table 4). Subgroup analysis suggests the increase in BMI is greater for younger adults, participants in the comprehensive cohort, participants who had a BMI in the normal or overweight range prepandemic, and those without diabetes or high blood sugar (online supplemental table 5). Subgroup results revealed similar patterns when obesity was considered as a dichotomous outcome measure (online supplemental table 6). Our sensitivity analysis using a date for the onset of pandemic restrictions 6 months later than the primary analysis (16 September 2020), allowing for a longer lag or accrual period, showed a stronger pandemic effect, with a slightly higher additional increase in BMI (β=0.24, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.33) and odds of obesity (OR=1.07, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.11) (online supplemental tables 7 and 8).

Discussion

The results of our study suggest that the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with an increase in both BMI and the prevalence of obesity, greater than the usual annual increase in the prepandemic period. Consistent with our hypotheses, we observed that the ‘during pandemic’ group, who provided FUP2 data after the onset of pandemic restrictions, exhibited an additional increase in BMI due to the pandemic. However, the change in BMI and obesity status was not uniform across subgroups. The findings indicate that females, middle-aged individuals, those within the Comprehensive cohort of the CLSA, individuals in all BMI categories except underweight and individuals without diabetes exhibited a higher increase in BMI even after accounting for all other factors. Overall, our findings highlight that the COVID-19 pandemic adversely impacted adiposity status, with the effect being disproportionately pronounced in certain subgroups within the population.

Our findings align with the existing literature on the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on obesity. Numerous studies have explored this issue using international samples and reported similar outcomes. For instance, a meta-analysis8 examined the effect of the COVID-19 lockdown from March to May 2020 on body weight and BMI and reported significant increases in both measures post-lockdown in adolescents and adults. Similarly, a large-scale meta-analysis7 found that the first year of the pandemic led to small but clinically significant increases in weight gain and BMI in both children and adults.

The literature presents mixed findings on which demographic groups experienced the most significant increase in adiposity. Consistent with our findings, several studies indicate that younger individuals and females gained relatively more weight and BMI compared with older adults and males.11 32 This finding for younger adults is important as widening health inequalities in early adulthood may have long-lasting adverse health consequences. Our study suggests that CLSA participants in all BMI categories, except those who were underweight, tended to show a greater increase in BMI during the pandemic. This is somewhat consistent with previous studies that found individuals who were overweight before the pandemic were more likely to gain additional weight.16 Additionally, while our study showed that people without diabetes experienced a greater increase in BMI, several studies involving patients with T2DM revealed that people with T2DM experienced a deterioration in weight status, leading to impaired glycaemic control.18 33 However, a significant proportion (19%) of individuals with overweight or obesity and T2DM took advantage of the lockdown to improve their health behaviours, which could also be true for our CLSA sample.19

The strengths of this study include the use of large-scale, population-based datasets from a representative sample of Canadian residents to explore the association. For the statistical analysis, we used WGEE methods, which included individuals with missing data in a monotonic pattern in the analysis, thereby increasing the statistical power of our analysis. However, this study also had several limitations. First, there was a change in the BMI data collection method for the Comprehensive cohort, from measured to self-reported height and weight during the pandemic. Since BMI calculated from self-reported data is subject to social desirability bias, this transition introduced heterogeneity in the BMI data for the Comprehensive cohort. Although we applied a correction factor to adjust self-reported BMI, as done in previous CLSA analyses,34 the correction factor was older and validated in a different sample and residual measurement error in self-reported BMI likely persisted and may have introduced bias into the estimated associations. Further, although it has been recommended to use ethnicity or race specific BMI cut points, especially for South Asians,35 this was not done since the vast majority of CLSA participants (95.94%) were white.

Another limitation is that our subgroup analyses revealed that the increases in BMI and obesity were primarily among the Comprehensive cohort and not the Tracking cohort. It is unknown whether this is due to differences in sample characteristics (Comprehensive cohort participants were primarily living in urban areas) or due to measurement error, since the Comprehensive cohort had measured anthropometrics collected at the data collection site for all visits, except during the pandemic, compared with the Tracking cohort which was based entirely on self-report telephone questionnaires. It is possible that despite adjustment for measurement error through correction equations, greater measurement error in the Tracking cohort, where BMI was consistently self-reported, may have biased estimates toward the null and obscured true changes over time. It is also possible that participants in the Comprehensive cohort, who were more likely to reside in urban areas, may have experienced stricter or greater changes in pandemic-related disruptions and may have actually experienced larger true increases in BMI. Furthermore, the WGEE method does not allow for the use of survey weights in the analysis, which further affects the generalisability of the findings. Finally, data were not available beyond September 2021, and we were unable to explore if the observed trend of increased BMI was sustained postpandemic. Future studies with longer follow-up periods are needed.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our analysis suggests that the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in an additional increase in BMI that exceeded the usual prepandemic increase. Our findings indicate that females, middle-aged individuals, those with normal BMI, overweight and obesity, and those without diabetes experienced a greater increase in BMI during the pandemic. Future research should explore this trend using additional postpandemic data and investigate specific public health restrictions or other factors that may be associated with the increase, while policymakers should be aware of and address the potential rise in obesity-related chronic diseases.
