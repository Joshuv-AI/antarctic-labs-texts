Influence of bioactive varnish on the shear bond strength of orthodontic brackets
Authors: C M F Da Silva, M V N Oliveira, M A B Da Silva, N B Dos Santos, J M D De Freitas, J M S Oliveira, F L B Amaral
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate the effect of bioactive varnish application on the shear bond strength (SBS) of orthodontic brackets bonded to demineralized enamel. Bovine incisors were selected and randomly assigned to four groups based on enamel treatment: Group 1 – sound enamel, no treatment (positive control); Group 2 – enamel subjected to a pH-cycling demineralization protocol (negative control); Group 3 – demineralized enamel treated with bioactive varnish (PRG Barrier Coat®, Shofu) and stored in distilled water for 24 h; and Group 4 – demineralized enamel treated with the same varnish and stored in distilled water for 28 days. For SBS and Adhesive Remnant Index (ARI) tests (n = 15 per group), orthodontic brackets (Roth Max® slot 22, Morelli) were bonded to the enamel using the Transbond XT® adhesive system (3 M Solventum). Samples underwent thermocycling (500 cycles between 5 °C and 55 °C), followed by SBS testing using a universal testing machine (0.5 mm/min) and ARI evaluation using stereomicroscopy. Enamel surfaces from each group (n = 3 per group) were also examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). SBS data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test. ARI scores were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test, with significance set at 5%. The positive control group showed significantly higher SBS values compared to Groups 3 and 4 (p < 0.001). The negative control group showed intermediate values, with no significant differences from the other groups. A significant association was found between groups and ARI scores: in the positive control group, 53.3% of samples had ARI = 0 and 33.3% had ARI = 3; in the negative control group, 93.3% had ARI = 0. In Groups 3 and 4, 100% of the samples showed ARI = 0. SEM analysis revealed an intact surface in the positive control group, a porous surface in the negative control, and a varnish-covered surface in Groups 3 and 4, with Group 3 exhibiting a more homogeneous coating than Group 4. Conclusion: The application of bioactive varnish to demineralized enamel significantly reduced bracket bond strength and resulted in predominantly adhesive failures at the enamel interface. Clinically, this suggests that bonding orthodontic brackets directly to enamel treated with this bioactive varnish may compromise bond integrity, regardless of the waiting period after application.Keywords: Orthodontics, PRG Barrier Coat®, Shear strength, Scanning electron microscopy

Introduction

Dental caries is a multifactorial disease driven by biofilm and induced by sugar consumption. The interplay between pathological and protective factors results in cyclic demineralization and remineralization of hard dental tissues (Pitts et al. 2017). Although its prevalence has decreased with the advent of fluoride-based therapies, dental caries remains the most widespread oral disease, affecting approximately 2.4 billion individuals worldwide (Pitts et al. 2017; Taher et al. 2012).

Histologically, the early stage of carious lesions has been characterized by four distinct zones. The superficial zone, influenced by saliva, retains more minerals than the inner layers. Beneath it lies the body of the lesion, where mineral loss and porosity are most pronounced. This is followed by the dark zone, a less porous region with signs of remineralization, and the translucent zone, located deepest, which exhibits mild mineral loss and increased porosity (Silverstone et al. 1981).

During orthodontic treatment, such incipient lesions are observed in approximately 36% of patients (Chapman et al. 2010), primarily because fixed appliances bonded to the teeth significantly impair oral hygiene. Orthodontic brackets facilitate biofilm accumulation, which results in esthetically undesirable white spot lesions and compromises bracket bond strength (Mohanty et al. 2014; Daneshkazemi et al. 2021; Kobayashi et al. 2021; Nabawy et al. 2021; Nalbantgil et al. 2013).

Non-invasive therapies aimed at remineralizing white spot lesions have gained attention (Klaisiri et al. 2022). Among them are bioactive materials incorporating GIOMER technology, such as the PRG Barrier Coat® varnish (Shofu). These materials contain surface pre-reacted glass ionomer (S-PRG) particles, which offer biological effects similar to those of glass ionomer cements—including fluoride release and recharge, combined with the superior mechanical properties of nanohybrid composites (Mendonça et al. 2022).

When fluoride interacts with dental enamel, it forms calcium fluoride (CaF₂) and fluorapatite, both of which reduce demineralization and reinforce the enamel against acid attacks, since they are less soluble in acidic environments than hydroxyapatite (Okuyama et al. 2023). In addition to fluoride, S-PRG-containing materials release other ions (Na⁺, BO₃3⁻, Al3⁺, SiO₃2⁻, and Sr2⁺), which contribute to inhibiting demineralization, mitigating white spot lesions, providing buffering capacity, and suppressing bacterial growth (Imazato et al. 2023; Zandi Karimi et al. 2021; Kobayashi et al. 2021).

However, because orthodontic brackets may be bonded to enamel surfaces previously treated with bioactive agents, it is important to determine whether such treatments interfere with bond strength. Gulec and Goymen (2019) reported that the application of resin infiltrants or CPP-ACP-based gels adversely affected the immediate bond strength of orthodontic brackets to enamel. The literature lacks studies evaluating the effect of S-PRG-containing varnish on bracket adhesion—particularly after aging simulations, such as thermocycling. This study addresses this gap by providing a clinically relevant assessment of bracket bonding following bioactive varnish application, supporting evidence-based clinical decisions.

Shear bond strength (SBS) testing is commonly used to measure bond strength, while the enamel surface after bracket removal is assessed using the Adhesive Remnant Index (ARI) (Ekizer et al. 2012). Macroscopic and microscopic methods are available to examine white spot lesions, among which scanning electron microscopy (SEM) allows for detailed surface analysis of dental enamel (Mohanty et al. 2014).

In light of these findings, studies are warranted to evaluate the effects of bioactive varnish application on demineralized enamel and its influence on orthodontic bracket SBS. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate whether applying bioactive varnish to demineralized enamel affects the SBS of orthodontic brackets. The null hypothesis (H₀) tested was that no statistically significant differences in SBS or ARI would be observed following the application of bioactive varnish to demineralized enamel, regardless of whether brackets were bonded after 24 h or after 28 days.

Selection of bovine teeth and sample preparation

The sample consisted of 72 bovine incisors with intact enamel, free from caries, cracks, or prior exposure to chemical agents (e.g., hydrogen peroxide, alcohol, formalin). After cleaning with a No. 15 scalpel blade (Lamedid, Osasco, SP, Brazil) to remove periodontal tissues, the teeth were stored in 0.1% thymol solution (Alphatec, Macaé, RJ, Brazil) at 4 °C to prevent enamel demineralization and microbial growth.

Dental roots were removed, and the crowns intended for SBS and ARI testing were embedded in self-curing acrylic resin (Jet®, Clássico, São Paulo, SP, Brazil) inside PVC tubes (32 × 10 mm; Tigre, Joinville, SC, Brazil), exposing the buccal surface. Positioning was standardized using modeling wax (7 NewWax®, Technew, Rio de Janeiro, RJ, Brazil), which was then removed to expose an enamel window measuring 84 mm2 (Romano et al. 2004).

Demineralization protocol

Each sample was individually immersed in 10 mL of demineralizing solution for 22 h (Table 2), then rinsed and immersed in 10 mL of remineralizing solution for 2 h, completing one 24-h pH cycling period (Gulec and Goymen 2019). The pH of each solution was measured using a digital pH meter (mPA210, MS Tecnopon, Piracicaba, SP, Brazil). The cycle was repeated daily for 14 days at 37 °C, with both solutions replaced every 24 h.

Following cycling, demineralization was confirmed using a DIAGNOdent® laser fluorescence device (655 nm, Kavo, Joinville, SC, Brazil). The calibration of the DIAGNOdent must be performed before each use to ensure reliable readings. First, the appropriate tip is connected to the device and placed on the white area of the ceramic calibration block, adjusting the display to zero. Next, the tip is placed on the dark reference area of the same block, checking whether the displayed value corresponds to the expected range (usually between 90 and 100). After confirming that the device shows zero on the white area and high values on the dark area, the calibration is complete and the equipment is ready for clinical use. Measurements were taken at three points on the enamel surface designated for bracket bonding: center, mesial, and distal (1 mm apart) using tip A. The average reading was 5.8 (range: 5 to 8), consistent with early caries formation, as defined by Tam and McComb (2001).

After application, Groups 1, 2, and 3 were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h. Group 4 remained stored for 28 days, with daily water replacement.

Preparation of specimens for SBS testing and ARI evaluation

The 60 specimens designated for SBS testing and ARI evaluation were numbered from 1 to 60 using a round bur in a high-speed handpiece (D-X, Ribeirão Preto, SP, Brazil; FAVA, São Paulo, SP, Brazil). An external operator randomly allocated specimens into the experimental groups to reduce selection bias. Group assignments were kept confidential until data collection was completed.

The buccal surfaces of the teeth were subjected to prophylaxis using fluoride-free extra-fine pumice paste (S.S. White, Rio de Janeiro, RJ, Brazil) and distilled water, applied with a rubber cup (Microdont, Socorro, SP, Brazil) mounted in a low-speed contra-angle handpiece for 10 s. Each rubber cup was used for up to five cleanings. The teeth were then rinsed with distilled water for 10 s and dried with oil-free compressed air (Dental Air, Limeira, SP, Brazil) for an additional 10 s (Nabawy et al. 2021).

Bracket bonding protocol

The bonding procedure was performed by a single blind operator, following the initial randomization in the group separation stage. The enamel surfaces were etched with 37% phosphoric acid (Dental Gel® 37%, Dentsply, Petrópolis, RJ, Brazil) for 15 s (Silva et al. 2021). After thorough rinsing for 10 s and drying with oil-free air for another 10 s, Transbond XT® adhesive system (3 M/Solventum, St. Paul, MN, USA) was applied using a microbrush (KG Sorensen, Cotia, SP, Brazil) and light-cured for 20 s with the Valo Grand® curing unit (Ultradent, Utah, USA) at a power of 1000 mW/cm2 (Holzmeier et al. 2008).

Transbond XT® orthodontic resin (3 M/Solventum, St. Paul, MN, USA) was applied to the base of Roth Max® metal brackets, slot 22 (Morelli, Sorocaba, SP, Brazil). The brackets were positioned in the center of the etched area using bracket tweezers (Morelli, Sorocaba, SP, Brazil) and pressed against the tooth surface with a Gilmore Needle applying a standardized force of 451 g. Excess resin was removed with an explorer No. 5 (Golgran, São Caetano, SP, Brazil) (Nalbantgil et al. 2013).

Light curing was performed using the Valo Grand® unit for 40 s—10 s per bracket face—at 1000 mW/cm2. After bonding, the specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h to simulate oral rehydration conditions.

Thermocycling protocol

After 24 h at 37 °C, all specimens were subjected to thermocycling to simulate temperature variations in the oral cavity. A total of 500 cycles were performed in distilled water between 5 °C and 55 °C, with a dwell time of 30 s and transfer time of 10 s between baths (Daneshkazemi et al. 2021).

SBS test

Following thermocycling, SBS testing was performed using a universal testing machine (EMIC® DL 2000, São José dos Pinhais, PR, Brazil). A chisel-shaped active tip was positioned at the tooth–bracket interface, and shear force was applied at a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min with a 50 Kgf load cell.

SEM analysis

Three samples from each group were analyzed using SEM (Vega3®, Tescan, Brno, Czech Republic). Specimens were sputter-coated with platinum for 3 min and gold for 5 min at a current of 10 mA. SEM analysis was conducted at 20 kV, with magnifications ranging from 536 × to 1570 ×. Fields of view varied from 88.2 µm to 310 µm, and images were acquired using a 50 µm scale (Kaga et al. 2020). All SEM images were captured by a single trained operator.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive and exploratory analyses were conducted. Since the data did not meet parametric assumptions, non-parametric tests were used. The Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to compare SBS values among groups, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. The association between groups and ARI scores was assessed using Fisher’s exact test. Statistical analysis was performed using R software (R Core Team, 2025, Vienna, Austria), with the significance level set at 5%.

Experimental procedures and places of research

The laboratory procedures for the preparation of demineralization and remineralization solutions, tooth selection and sample preparation, execution of the demineralization protocol, application of the bioactive varnish, bracket bonding, and thermocycling were carried out at the Dental Materials Laboratory of the School of Dentistry, Federal University of Alagoas (UFAL), Maceió, Alagoas, Brazil. The shear bond strength testing and the assessment of the adhesive remnant index were performed at the Dental Materials Laboratory of São Leopoldo Mandic School, Campinas, São Paulo, Brazil. Finally, scanning electron microscopy analyses were conducted at the laboratory of the Federal Institute of Alagoas (IFAL), Maceió, Alagoas, Brazil.

Discussion

For orthodontic treatment to be considered effective, brackets bonded to enamel must exhibit sufficient bond strength to remain through the course of the therapy. Many patients present with white spot lesions prior to initiating orthodontic treatment and may be treated with bioactive varnish to manage these lesions. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate whether this type of material interferes with bracket adhesion.

Based on the findings of this study, the null hypothesis was rejected. A statistically significant reduction in SBS was observed in Groups 3 and 4, in which bioactive varnish was applied, and brackets were bonded after 24 h or 28 days, respectively, compared to the sound enamel group (Group 1 – positive control). These findings suggest that the application of bioactive varnish, although proposed as a therapeutic measure to promote the remineralization of demineralized enamel, may compromise the effectiveness of orthodontic bonding, possibly due to the formation of a surface layer that interferes with the penetration of the adhesive system or modifies the chemical characteristics of the dental substrate. Moreover, SBS values in the varnish-treated groups were lower than the threshold proposed by Reynolds (1975) for clinically acceptable bonding, which ranges from approximately 60 Kgf/cm2 (5.88 MPa) to 80 Kgf/cm2 (7.94 MPa), regardless of storage time in distilled water. The intervals of 24 h and 28 days were selected to evaluate the immediate effect and long-term stability, respectively, of the bioactive varnish on bond strength. The 24-h period simulated immediate post-application clinical conditions, while the 28-day period represented an extended duration to analyze potential varnish degradation in an aqueous environment. Distilled water was used to avoid interference from external ions, and daily replacement ensured a neutral pH.

This reduction in bond strength may be attributed to the obstruction of enamel microporosities by the bioactive varnish, which likely impaired the effectiveness of 37% phosphoric acid etching prior to bracket bonding—findings consistent with the literature (Gulec and Goymen 2019). Indeed, the application of PRG Barrier Coat® has been shown to inhibit demineralization in the treated area (Alsayed et al. 2016), supporting the notion that varnish-treated enamel surfaces were more resistant to acid etching, thus compromising micromechanical retention.

Yamamoto et al. (2021) found that bioactive varnish inhibited biofilm formation and bacterial adhesion to dentin when compared to untreated surfaces and those treated with non-S-PRG-based materials. The tested varnish also demonstrated high resistance to acidic challenges without surface loss, consistent with findings by Ma et al. (2012), who reported that the material sealed the dentin surface hermetically, with no observable interface gaps.

In contrast, Ekizer et al. (2012) reported higher SBS values in groups treated with bioactive materials compared to controls. This discrepancy may be explained by methodological differences, particularly in their demineralization protocol and the materials used in their study—such as resin infiltrants, CPP-ACP, and 1.23% acidulated phosphate fluoride—which may have permitted more effective acid etching and microroughness formation. A similar pattern was observed in Groups 1 and 2 of the present study (Es-Souni et al. 2002).

Similarly, Daneshkazemi et al. (2021) reported conflicting findings, with experimental groups treated with infiltrant, CPP-ACP, and 5% fluoride varnish exhibiting significantly higher SBS values than the demineralized negative control group. In contrast, the present study found no statistically significant difference between the negative control and varnish-treated groups. A possible explanation lies in the difference in demineralization protocols. The method proposed by Gulec and Goymen (2019) was adopted in this study because it more closely replicates the dynamics of oral demineralization and remineralization by using separate solutions and a prolonged demineralization period (22 h), which is sufficient to overcome the aprismatic enamel layer. Although the protocol was standardized, variations in the extent of demineralization across specimens—ranging from 5 to 8—may have introduced variability, possibly influencing the absence of statistical differences between the negative control and varnish-treated groups.

The present findings also diverge from those of Silva et al. (2021), who reported SBS values between 11 and 12 MPa. Their methodology involved enamel surface polishing and did not include pH cycling or thermocycling. In contrast, this study preserved the superficial enamel layer, where early caries lesions typically form, as demonstrated by confocal laser scanning microscopy (Sousa et al. 2017). This decision may have contributed to the lower SBS values observed, since thermocycling is known to reduce the effectiveness of resin-based materials (Klaisiri et al. 2020).

Kobayashi et al. (2021) reported higher SBS values (~ 7 MPa) in groups treated with PRG Barrier Coat®; however, their methodology did not include pH cycling or thermocycling. In the present study, groups without thermocycling were excluded to replicate intraoral conditions better and ensure greater clinical relevance. Future research should investigate the isolated effect of thermocycling on bracket adhesion to bioactive materials, since this variable was not independently assessed in current studies.

Arita et al. (2017) also reported higher SBS values (8–12 MPa) following the application of bioactive varnish, which contrasts with the findings of this study. However, a key methodological difference lies in their use of dentin, rather than enamel, as the bonding substrate. Although studies such as Jäggi et al. (2024) typically demonstrate higher bond strength to human enamel compared to dentin, the present study revealed an inverse pattern, with lower adhesion to demineralized enamel. This divergence may be attributed to the demineralization protocol employed in our study, which critically altered the enamel structure and compromised the effectiveness of acid etching. Nabawy et al. (2021) found no significant difference in SBS between the positive control group (16.70 MPa) and groups treated with Er,Cr:YSGG laser (17.01 MPa) or laser combined with CPP-ACP (20.61 MPa), despite numerically higher values. These results may be influenced by their use of artificial saliva storage, which can modify enamel structure, and by the effects of laser conditioning. The methodology of the present study employed distilled water for sample storage as it is a chemically pure product, free of ions and organic compounds. This approach allowed for standardized and controlled evaluation of the bioactive varnish's stability while preventing mineral interference with the demineralized enamel structure.

Elshenawy et al. (2025) also reported slightly higher SBS values in groups treated with silver diamine fluoride and PRG Barrier Coat®, although the differences were not statistically significant. In contrast to our study, their protocol included phosphoric acid etching prior to varnish application, which deviates from the manufacturer’s instructions. This deviation may explain the differing outcomes, as acid etching improves micro-roughness and adhesion.

In the present study, the negative control group—subjected to demineralization without varnish application—showed no significant difference in SBS compared to the varnish-treated groups. This may indicate that mineral loss was insufficient to create a clear distinction from sound enamel; however, it was still enough to reduce SBS. These findings suggest that varnish, rather than demineralization, is the primary factor reducing adhesion. Although bioactive varnish offers desirable remineralization benefits for enamel, its use prior to bracket bonding may compromise adhesion. Additional studies are warranted to confirm this outcome.

Considering that remineralization therapies should maintain acceptable adhesive properties, the use of erbium lasers appears promising. In a recent meta-analysis, Jiang et al. (2022) reported that SBS following Er:YAG or Er,Cr:YSGG laser conditioning was comparable to conventional acid etching. Cheng et al. (2023) demonstrated that Er,Cr:YSGG laser combined with CPP-ACP provided superior remineralization compared to CPP-ACP alone, improving enamel microhardness and reducing lesion depth. These findings suggest that subablative erbium lasers may represent a safe and effective alternative for enamel remineralization in orthodontic patients, while preserving bond strength.

Regarding ARI outcomes, the positive control group exhibited ARI = 3 in 33.3% and ARI = 0 in 53.3% of specimens. The presence of ARI = 0 in this group may be attributed to the preservation of the aprismatic enamel layer, since the enamel surfaces were neither polished nor abraded. In contrast, ARI was 0 in nearly all specimens in the other groups, particularly in those treated with bioactive varnish, which interfered with the enamel–adhesive interface and reduced bonding performance.

Gulec and Goymen (2019) reported similar findings, showing a drop in ARI scores from 2 to 1 in experimental groups compared to controls. In the present study, ARI scores dropped to 0 in varnish-treated groups—consistent with Kobayashi et al. (2021), who found ARI = 0 in 84.2% of experimental specimens. These outcomes support the hypothesis that the weakened enamel–adhesive interface contributes to reduced bond strength (Munchow et al. 2015; Shukla et al. 2014).

By contrast, Nabawy et al. (2021) observed an increase in ARI scores (from 1 to 2) in experimental groups, possibly due to storage in artificial saliva. Ekizer et al. (2012) also reported higher ARI scores in experimental groups, differing from our results. These differences may stem from variations in demineralization protocols and surface preparation techniques.

SEM analysis in this study revealed clear differences in enamel surface morphology. Group 1 (sound enamel) showed no microporosities, in contrast to Group 2 (demineralized enamel), which exhibited surface porosities. Groups 3 and 4, both treated with bioactive varnish, displayed visible surface coverage. Group 4 (28-day storage) presented a more homogeneous varnish layer, likely due to varnish dilution during prolonged water exposure. However, this did not significantly affect SBS relative to the values observed in Group 3 (24-h storage).

These findings align with those of Kaga et al. (2020), who reported that PRG Barrier Coat®-treated enamel resembled sound enamel in appearance, with minimum porosities, while lactic acid-treated enamel showed porosities. Similar observations were made by Es-Souni et al. (2002) and Alsayed et al. (2016), who confirmed that the varnish effectively covered the enamel and blocked acid etching, thus reducing micromechanical retention.

In the present study, no control group consisting of sound enamel treated with varnish was included. This decision was based on the fact that, from a clinical standpoint, the application of bioactive varnishes is not indicated for sound, intact enamel surfaces. Varnishes are primarily recommended as a preventive or therapeutic measure in situations of initial demineralization or increased caries risk, where their bioactive components can exert a remineralizing effect. Applying varnish to completely sound enamel would not reflect a realistic clinical protocol and therefore was not considered a meaningful comparison group in the design of this study.

This in vitro study presents inherent limitations that must be acknowledged when extrapolating the results to clinical practice. The oral environment is highly complex and dynamic, being influenced by masticatory forces, salivary flow, pH fluctuations, and enzymatic activity—factors that were not fully reproduced in the present experimental design. To address these limitations, future research should: (1) explore strategies to counteract the reduction in bond strength, such as extending acid etching time or applying alternative enamel conditioning methods (e.g., erbium lasers) before bonding to varnish-treated surfaces; (2) assess the wear resistance and long-term stability of the varnish coating under clinically simulated abrasive conditions; (3) investigate the relationship between wear resistance, thickness, and material composition through quantitative analysis using scanning electron microscopy; (4) incorporate thermocycling as an experimental variable to better understand its impact on outcomes; and (5) perform well-designed clinical trials to confirm these in vitro findings and determine the balance between the preventive benefits of the varnish and its influence on orthodontic bonding in patients.

Conclusion

The application of bioactive varnish affected both the bond strength of brackets to demineralized enamel and ARI outcomes, regardless of whether the brackets were bonded after 24 h or 28 days. Varnish-treated groups exhibited lower bond strength and higher frequency of ARI = 0 compared to the positive control group, although no significant differences were found in comparison to the negative control group.
