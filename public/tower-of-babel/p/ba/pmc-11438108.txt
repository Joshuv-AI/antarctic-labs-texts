M1 macrophage-derived exosomal microRNA-29c-3p suppresses aggressiveness of melanoma cells via ENPP2
Authors: Byoungha An, Cheol-Hee Shin, Jae Won Kwon, Na Ly Tran, A Hui Kim, Hyeyeon Jeong, Sang-Heon Kim, Kwideok Park, Seung Ja Oh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the tumor microenvironment, macrophages play crucial roles resulting in tumor suppression and progression, depending on M1 and M2 macrophages, respectively. In particular, macrophage-derived exosomes modulate the gene expression of cancer cells by delivering miRNAs which downregulate specific genes. The communication between macrophages and cancer cells is especially important in immunogenic tumors such as melanoma, where the cancer pogression is significantly influenced by the surrounding immune cells. In this study, we identified that M1 macrophages secrete exosomal miR-29c-3p in the co-culture system with melanoma cells. Simultaneously, ENPP2, the target of miR-29c-3p, decreased in the melanoma cells which are co-cultured with M1 macrophages. Additionally, we observed that the reduction of ENPP2 alleviates melanoma cell migration and invasion, due to the changes of cholesterol metabolism and ECM remodeling. Based on these findings, we demonstrated that M1 macrophages suppress aggressiveness of melanoma cells via exosomal miR-29c-3p-mediated knock-down of ENPP2 in cancer cells. Introduction

Melanoma is an aggressive form of skin cancer characterized by high metastatic potential and resistance to therapy. Because of these, melanoma is responsible for the majority of skin cancer-related deaths, despite accounting for only about about 1% of all skin cancer [1]. Early-stage melanoma is usually curable through surgical resection, but advanced melanoma has remains highly lethal despite the advent of targeted therapies and immunotherapies with a 5-year survival rate of just 22.5% (Melanoma Research Alliance, 2021) [2]. As a result, extensive efforts have been made to develop more effective therapeutic strategies for advanced melanoma. In this context, the tumor microenvironment (TME) has gained increasing attention, as melanoma is an immunogenic tumor whose interactions with resident immune cells within TME influence cancer progression and respond to therapeutic interventions [3, 4].

The TME play a crucial role in cancer progression as a complex and dynamic milieu surrounding tumor. The TME consists of multiple cell types, vascular networks, and secreted molecules; in the TME, immune cells, stromal cells and heterogeneous cancer cells secrete ECM components and various bioactive molecules through which they interact with each other. Among these cells, tumor-associated macrophages (TMAs) are the most abundant tumor-infiltrating immune cells, comprising up to 50% of solid tumor [5, 6]. Generally, activated macrophages are classified into two groups; M1 and M2 phenotypes, each with distinct functions. M1 macrophages exhibit pro-inflammatory and anti-tumor effects, while M2 macrophages are associated with anti-inflammatory and pro-tumor activity. TAMs are typically identified as M2-like due to a high M2/M1 ratio in TME. Consequently, researchers have sought to convert the polarity from M2 like to anti-tumoral M1 like phenotype [7]. However, some studies have reported the ramifications of TMAs are observed with different aspects. For instance, Yao et al. demonstrated that M2 macrophages inhibit the glioma aggressiveness by blocking the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, which contrast with the commonly recognized function of M2 macrophages inducing the tumor metastasis [8]. Similarly, Sharen et al. reported that M1-like TAMs activates NF-κB signaling pathway to promote proliferation and anti-apoptotic properties in liver cancer cells, which conflicts with the typical anti-tumor effect of M1 macrophages [9]. Therefore, further research is necessary to clarify which properties contribute to the anti-tumoral or pro-turmoral effects of the M1 and M2 macrophages within TME.

Exosomes are small, cell-derived membrane vesicles that mediate intracellular communication in the TME. As mediators, exosomes carry biomolecules such as microRNAs (miRNAs), long noncoding RNAs, mRNAs, DNA, protein, and lipids, which regulate recipient cells by delivering these contents upon uptake [10]. Among these biomolecules, exosomal miRNAs have garnered significant attention for their ability to control the expression of target genes in recipient cells, including cancer cells. Recent research has shown that TAM-derived exosomal miRNAs can modulate cancer in either a pro-tumoral or anti-tumoral manner, depending on the type of macrophages and the specific miRNAs involved. The intracellular transfer of exosomes from macrophages to cancer cells has been widely observed in the previous studies. One such studies demonstrated that M1 macrophage-derived exosomal miR-16-5p exerts anti-cancer effect on gastric cancer by targeting PD-L1, leading to subsequent activation of the T cell immune response [11]. Another study reported that M2 macrophages promote the progression of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma by delivering exosomal miR-501-3p, which target TGFBR3 to activate TGF-β signaling pathway [12]. However, the role of M1 macrophage-derived exosomal miRNAs in regulating melanoma aggressiveness has not yet been fully elucidated.

Ectonucleotide Pyrophosphatase/Phosphodiesterase 2 (ENPP2), also known as autotaxin, is a secreted enzyme that converts lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) into lysophosphatidic acid (LPA). LPA, a bioactive molecule, mediates various biological processes, including survival, proliferation, migration, angiogenesis and inflammation [13]. Unsurprisingly, aberrant regulation of ENPP2, followed by abnormal LPA production, has been linked to cancers. Overexpression of ENPP2 contributes to tumor progression in multiple myeloma [14], metastasis and chemoresistance in breast cancer [15], and cell motility in glioblastoma [16]. As a result, regulatory miRNAs affecting ENPP2/LPA axis have been investigated as potential targets for cancer therapy. For instance, MiR-101-3p have been shown to exhibit tumor-suppressing activity in glioblastoma cells by directly targeting ENPP2 to reduce its expression [17]. In the melanoma, however, the dysfunctional regulations of ENPP2 is not yet fully understood. Thus, identifying the mechanisms that regulate ENPP2 expression in melanoma, particularly through regulatory miRNAs such as exosomal miRNAs, is of great importance.

Based on the above findings, we hypothesized that M1 macrophage-derived exosomal miRNA contributes to the anti-cancer effect of M1 macrophages on melanoma cancer cell lines, which is distinguished from the functions of M2 macrophages. To test this, we identified the key exosomal miRNA predominantly secreted from M1 macrophages, but not M2 macrophages, in a co-culture system with melanoma cells. The target gene of the key exosomal miRNA, miR-29c-3p, was then validated in melanoma cells. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we further investigated how knock-down of target gene, ENPP2, led to decreased migration and invasion of melanoma cell by modulating both intracellular and extracellular process. Collectively, we demonstrated that M1 macrophages inhibit the melanoma aggressiveness via exosomal miR-29c-3p-mediated down-regulation of ENPP2.

Results

Identification of monocyte-derived M1 and M2 macrophages

The macrophages with different phenotypes were obtained as shown in Fig. 1A. First, human THP-1 monocytes were incubated with PMA to differentiate into M0 macrophages. M0 macrophages were then polarized into M1 macrophages by stimulation with LPS and INF-γ, or into M2 macrophages with IL-4 and IL-13. Morphologically, M0 macrophages exhibited small and round shapes with an attached culture property. In contrast, M1 and M2 macrophages displayed flat, ameboid and branched characteristics, consistent with previous reports (Fig. 1B). To further verify the successful induction of macrophages, we assessed the expression level of specific biomarkers for each macrophage state using RT-qPCR. The results demonstrated that CD197 and TNF-α (M1 macrophage phenotype marker) were significantly elevated in M1 macrophages, whereas CCL22 and CD206 (M2 macrophage phenotype marker) were up-regulated in M2 macrophages compared with M0 macrophages (Fig. 1C). Taken together, these findings confirm that macrophages were successfully differentiated from monocytes and subsequently polarized into M1 and M2 phenotypes.

The miR-29c-3p was validated as a key M1 macrophage-derived exosomal miRNA in the co-culture system with melanoma cells

After the M1 macrophages were confirmed to have antitumor effects on melanoma cells, as previously reported (Fig. S1), we sought to identify the M1 macrophage-derived exosomal miRNAs which may affect melanoma cells. M1 macrophages were co-cultured with A375SM melanoma cells using transwell inserts, and the exosomes were harvested to analyze the expression level of individual exosomal miRNAs. As controls and comparators, exosomes were also collected from M2 macrophage and A375SM co-culture media, as well as from A375SM monoculture media (Fig. 2A). The purified exosomes were examined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) to measure their size, with the main population observed in the 100 to 200 nm range (Fig. 2B). Additionally, the exosomal marker CD63 was identified via western blot. Compared to cell lysate, the CD63 was enriched in the exosome lysate (Fig. 2C). Through the small RNA sequencing, differentially expressed miRNAs (DE-miRNAs) were identified among the exosomal miRNAs. According to the results, 86 miRNAs were differentially expressed in M1 macrophage-derived exosomes relative to the exosomes from A375SM monoculture.

We selected the top 30 DE-miRNAs based on the | log2[FC] | value and further compared their expression profiles between M1 and M2 macrophage-isolated exosomes (relative to the A375SM-derived exosomes). This comparison is demonstrated in the heatmap, which indicates that significantly higher amount of miR-29c-3p was secreted in M1 macrophage-derived exosomes than in M2 macrophage-derived exosomes (Fig. 2D). Additionally, miR-29c-3p exhibited the highest fold change in the volume plot (Fig. 2F). We further verified the expression level of miR-29c-3p in M0, M1 and M2 macrophages using miRNA RT-qPCR in a separate in vitro system. The data demonstrated that miR-29c-3p was significantly over-expressed in M1 macrophages compared to M0 and M2 macrophages (Fig. 2F). Thus, we identified miR-29c-3p as the key exosomal miRNA which is released by M1 macrophages that may influence melanoma cells.

ENPP2 was predicted as a target gene of M1 macrophage-derived exosomal miR-29c-3p in melanoma cells

Next, to figure out the target gene of exosomal miR-29c-3p in melanoma cells, we performed a microarray analysis on A375SM cells co-cultured with or without M1 macrophages. Compared to the A375SM monoculture, 165 genes were differentially expressed (|log2FC|≥ 1) in response to co-culture with M1 macrophages. We further narrowed down the potential miR-29 targets, using a three-step approach. First, we selected genes whose expression changes were in opposite direction when compared to the M2 macrophage co-cultured group, in order to pick the specifically affected genes by M1 macrophages, but not by M2 macrophages. In detail, 139 genes were up- (or down-) regulated by M1 macrophages but down- (or up-) regulated by M2 macrophages. Second, miR-29 binding sites on the mRNA of these genes were examined by web base target prediction tools (Targetscan ver.8.0: https://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/ and miRDB: https://mirdb.org/index.html) and only 6 genes had miR-29 binding site [18, 19]. Finally, we selected down-regulated genes as miRNAs typically reduce the expression of target genes. Through this process, three candidates were identified: TMEM183A, DAB1 and ENPP2 (Fig. 3A). Among the three genes, ENPP2 was the most significantly down-regulated by miR-29c and we chose to focus on ENPP2 for further study (Fig. 3D, Fig. S2).

To verify that ENPP2 is a target of miR-29c-3p in melanoma, we conducted dual luciferase reporter assays. ENPP2 3’-UTR wild-type (WT) and seed sequence mutant-type (MT) vectors were constructed (Fig. 3B), then co-transfected with miR-29c-3p mimics or miR-NC into A375SM cells. As shown in Fig. 3C, co-transfection of miR-29c mimics with ENPP2 3’-UTR WT vector reduced relative luciferase activity, whereas the luciferase activity of the ENPP2 3’-UTR MT vector was unaffected by miR-29c mimics or miR-NC. Additionally, we performed RT-qPCR, western blot and ELISA assay after transfection with miR-29c-3p into A375SM cells. The results showed that both mRNA and protein expression levels of ENPP2 were down-regulated (Fig. 3D, E), and ENPP2 secretion into the medium also declined following miR-29c-3p treatment (Fig. 3F). These data suggest that miR-29c-3p directly targets ENPP2 and inhibits its expression. Furthermore, the results imply that ENPP2 may be a key factor by which M1 macrophages control A375SM cells, considering that miR-29c-3p is a major exosomal miRNA released by M1 macrophages after co-culture with A375SM cells.

Knock-down of ENPP2 inhibited migration and invasion of melanoma cells

The foregoing results demonstrated that M1 macrophage-derived exosomal miR-29c-3p may affect A375SM melanoma cells by decreasing ENPP2 expression. At the same time, M1 macrophages are well known for exerting antitumor effects on various cancers, including melanoma [20]. Therefore, we tested whether ENPP2, the identified target of miR-29c-3p, plays a role in melanoma cell aggressiveness, such as migration and invasion. To this end, siENPP2 was transfected into A375SM cells to knock-down ENPP2, and the knock-down efficiency was confirmed via RT-qPCR, western blot and ELISA. The results showed that both mRNA and protein expression levels of ENPP2, as well as secreted ENPP2, were decreased by siENPP2 (Fig. 4A, B, C). Scratch wound healing assay and transwell assay were then employed to evaluate the effect of ENPP2 on the migration and invasion of A375SM cells. As depicted in Fig. 4D, the wound closure rate of siENPP2-transfected A375SM cells was slower than that of si-NC transfected cells, which means that ENPP2 can promote the migration of A375SM cells. In the transwell assay, significantly fewer cells migrated and invaded when siENPP2 was transfected into A375SM cells, compared to the si-NC transfected group (Fig. 4E). These results suggest that ENPP2 can induce not only migration but also invasion of A375SM cells, which implies that the aggressiveness of melanoma cells may be restricted by M1 macrophages. This occurs because exosomal miR-29c from M1 macrophages reduces the expression of ENPP2 in A375SM cells.

Knock-down of ENPP2 accumulated the cholesterol in melanoma cells

Next, we aimed to figure out which signaling pathways are affected to restrict the migration and invasion of melanoma cells as ENPP2 is down-regulated by M1 macrophages’ exosomal miR-29c-3p. To this end, we focused on the primary function of ENPP2 that is to produce lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) outside of cells. As a signaling molecule, LPA can regulate diverse biological processes in LPA receptor (LPAR)-dependent or -independent ways [21]. Therefore, decease in the expression of ENPP2 leads to reduced LPA levels, which, in turn, affects LPA- related pathways.

Among these pathways, we first investigated cholesterol metabolism, as we observed that genes related to cholesterol homeostasis were differently expressed by miR-29c-3p. HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and sterol regulatory element-binding protein 2 (SREBP2) are responsible for cholesterol biosynthesis while acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase 1 (ACAT1) and ATP-binding cassette transporters (ABCA1, ABCG1) regulate intracellular cholesterol concentration and cholesterol efflux respectively. The mRNA expression of all the these genes was significantly reduced by miR-29c-3p, with the largest decline observed in the mRNA expression of ABC transporters (Fig. 5A). Therefore, we focused on ABCA1 and ABCG1 for the further experiments. The mRNA expression of ABCA1 and ABCG1 decreased by approximately 50% after transfection with siENPP2; however, only the protein level of ABCA1 significantly decreased after siENPP2 transfection. This suggests that ENPP2 can down-regulate ABCA1 (Fig. 5B, C). To identify the biological processes involving ENPP2, LPA and ABCA1, we conducted PPI network analysis using STRING. The results indicated that ENPP2 may interact with ABCA1 through peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ)-mediated signaling (Fig. 5D). So, we speculated the existence of transcription factor that meets two characteristics; first, its expression is related to ENPP2-PPARγ signaling, and second, it is involved in the regulation of ABCA1 expression. Based on previous studies, Liver X receptor (LXR) satisfies both conditions as it is known to be upregulated by PPARγ, which is activated by LPA [22], and promote the ABCA1 expression [23]. For this reason, we examined the expression levels of LXRα/β in A375SM cells after transfection with siENPP2, hypothesizing that knock-down of ENPP2 would result in reduced LPA production, leading to decreased PPARγ-mediated LXRα/β expression. As expected, both mRNA and protein levels of LXRα/β reduced in siENPP2-treated group, compared to the si-NC group (Fig. 5E, F). These results suggest that down-regulation of ABCA1 following ENPP2 knock-down may occur via ENPP2-PPARγ-LXRα/β axis in A375SM cells. Additionally, we found that much more cholesterol accumulated in siENPP2-transfected A375SM cells than si-NC-transfected cells, as a consequence of the decreased expression of ABCA1, which is responsible for cholesterol efflux (Fig. 5G). Cholesterol accumulation reduces malignant cell migration by enhancing membrane rigidity and restricting fluidity [24]. Thus, these data indicate that migration and invasion of A375SM cells can be inhibited by reducing ENPP2.

Collectively, ENPP2 knock-down may inhibit migration and invasion of A375SM cells by reducing ENPP2-PPARγ-LXRα/β mediated cholesterol transporter expression which induces cholesterol accumulation.

Knock-down of ENPP2 modulated the ECM composition and remodeling

As another ENPP2-related pathway modulating aggressiveness of A375SM cells, we focused on ECM remodeling. This is because LPA, the product of ENPP2, has been reported to promote cell migration and invasion by increasing ECM remodeling enzyme and enhancing ECM stiffness through LPAR- dependent RhoA/ROCK signaling cascade [25]. Therefore, we examined whether ECM remodeling factors are also reduced by ENPP2 knock-down in A375SM cells.

First, we found less active RhoA in siENPP2-transfected cells compared to si-NC-transfected cells (Fig. 6A). These data imply that siENPP2 decreases LPA production, resulting in reduction of active RhoA, since RhoA is known to be activated by LPA in an LPAR-dependent manner. Once activated, RhoA makes ROCK activated, which upregulates the expression of its target genes. Therefore, we examined the mRNA expression levels of ROCK target genes, including ECM remodeling enzymes and key ECM components, in siENPP2 transfected A375SM cells. The results show decrease in mRNA levels of ECM remodeling enzyme, such as MMP2, MMP9 and uPA (Fig. 6B). These enzymes promote ECM degradation as proteolytic enzymes, facilitating cancer cell invasion [26]. Additionally, the mRNA levels of CCN4 and LOX were also decreased by siENPP2. CCN4 and LOX are ECM remodeling enzymes that increase ECM stiffness by influencing collagen linearization and crosslink, respectively, to enhance invasion and metastasis of cells (Fig. 6C) [27]. Therefore, we concluded that the ability of melanoma cells to remodel ECM for metastasis or invasion may be inhibited by ENPP2 knock-down. At the same time, the mRNA expression of collagen type 1 coding genes, COL1A1 and COL1A2, was decreased by siENPP2 in A375SM cells (Fig. 6C). Based on this result, we speculated that collagen type 1 levels in the ECM would be reduced after siENPP2 treatment. As expected, the immunofluorescence images show that collagen type 1 in the ECM was significantly decreased by siENPP2 (Fig. 6D). Since collagen type 1 is a major component of ECM, its quantity directly affects ECM stiffness. A reduction in collagen type 1 leads to reduced ECM stiffness or rigidity. Hence, these data suggest that ECM stiffness of A375SM cells is diminished by siENPP2 as it downregulates collagen type 1 expression.

Cancer cells migrate and invade more easily in a stiffer ECM environment, as they produce more ECM remodeling enzymes. Our findings indicate that downregulating ENPP2 releases migration and invasion of A375SM cells by reducing the expression of ECM remodeling enzymes and the key ECM component, collagen type1, through downregulation of the LPAR-RhoA-ROCK signaling cascade.

In silico analysis to estimate the effect of ENPP2 on aggressiveness of melanoma in vivo

To determine whether the aforementioned results can be generalized to the in vivo system, we performed bioinformatic analyses using GEO datasets, GSE29074 [28] and GSE7929 [29]. The GSE29074 dataset includes the gene expression profiles of normal melanocytes and genetically engineered mouse model of melanoma, a Met-driven model (iMet), which develops metastatic melanomas. First, uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) confirmed that the melanoma Meti group is clearly distinguished from normal melanocytes (Fig. S4A). It was also revealed that ENPP2 expression was significantly higher in melanoma (log2FC = 3.109) compared to the normal melanocytes, as indicated by differentially expressed gene analysis (Fig. 7A, B). Interestingly, lipid metabolic process and cell migration-related categories are enriched according to DAVID bioanalysis using GOBP (GO Biological Process) terms (Fig. 7C), and ECM-related terms were enriched in the GOCC (GO Cellular Components) (Fig. 7D). On the other hand, the chip of GSE7929 contained mouse xenograft melanoma model samples which were injected parental A375 melanoma cells or high metastatic A375 melanoma cells. The UMAP shows the two groups were clustered separately (Fig. S4B) indicating different gene expression profiles between the two groups. Similarly, the expression level of ENPP2 was significantly elevated (log2FC = 4.78) in high metastatic melanoma xenograft samples (Fig. S4C, D). DEG analysis using DAVID revealed that lipid biosynthesis and extracellular matrix related terms were highly raked in BP and CC categories, respectively (Fig. S4 E, F). When comparing DEGs from GSE29074 and GSE7929, there were seven common DEGs with ENPP2 among them (Fig. 7E). This serves as further evidence of the importance of ENPP2 in melanoma migration because the ENPP2 was significantly increased as melanoma cells become more metastatic, despite the different methods used to induce metastatic melanoma mouse model. Additionally, using TCGA public data, we also compared the survival rates between two groups of metastatic melanoma patients: one group with high ENPP2 expression and the other with low ENPP2 expression level (Fig. 7F). While the survival rate was slightly higher in the low ENPP2 expression group, the difference was not statistically significant. However, as it still shows tendency, that is at least suggests the possibility that ENPP2 may influence melanoma patient survival, particularly in cases where the cancer exhibits metastatic property.

In this study, the effect of ENPP2 on melanoma aggressiveness is explained by its involvement in abnormal lipid metabolism and extracellular remodeling, which were also observed in vivo in melanoma models through bioinformatics approach. Although the difference was not statistically significant, the tendency toward lower survival rate in metastatic melanoma patients with high ENPP2 expression was observed. Therefore, the mechanisms identified in the in vitro analysis with A375SM cells are expected to similarly occur in vivo.

Discussion

As melanoma is one of the immunogenic tumors, its relationship with host immune cells resident in TME is under investigation [30]. Especially, macrophages, the most abundant immune filtrating cells in solid tumor, act differently depending on their polarization status: M1 macrophages exhibit anti-tumor effects, while M2 macrophages have pro-tumoral effect [7]. At the same time, exosome-derived miRNA is believed to modulate various biological process in cancer as it is transferred from the donor cells to cancer cells [31]. In this context, we hypothesized that melanoma cells are suppressed by M1 macrophages delivering specific exosomal miRNA to cancer cells. First, we identified M1 macrophages secrete more miR-29c-3p packaged in exosomes to melanoma cells compared to M0 or M2 macrophages. We also showed that the exosomal miR-29c-3p leads to downregulation of ENPP2 in A375SM melanoma cells, which in turn releases the migration and invasion of the A375SM cells. The reasons why downregulation of ENPP2 reduces the aggressiveness of melanoma cells were further explained in two ways. First, as ENPP2 knock-down alters cholesterol homeostasis, which leads to accumulate cholesterol in A375SM cells, likely through the ENPP2-PPARγ-LXRα/β axis. Second, ENPP2 knock-down affects not only the ECM degradation ability but also ECM composition, reducing remodeling enzymes and ECM stiffness via the LPAR-RhoA-ROCK signaling cascade (Fig. 8).

Based on the exosomal miRNA sequencing data, we regarded miR-29c-3p as a key exosomal miRNA of M1 macrophages co-cultured with melanoma cells. The co-culture system allowed us to reflect the interaction between cancer cells and macrophages, an aspect that is limited in other systems that treat only isolated exosomal miRNA to cancer cells. About miR-29c-3p, previous studies have showed its tumor suppressive function in melanoma cell lines [32]. Clinical reports also demonstrated that serum levels of miR-29c were significantly lower in melanoma patients compared to normal controls [33]. However, there is limited data on the role of macrophage-derived exosome as a source of miR-29c-3p in the melanoma TME. Therefore, the current study provides novel findings suggesting that M1 macrophages exert anti-tumor effect through exosomal miR-29c-3p on melanoma cells. The up-regulation of exosomal miR-29c-3p may be a distinguishing characteristic of M1 macrophages in tumor suppression, given that M2 macrophages, which have pro-tumoral effect, did not express exosomal miR-29c-3p at levels comparable to M1 macrophages. However, further studies are required to determine whether melanoma cells affect the elevated level of miR-29c-3p in M1 macrophage-derived exosome.

We indicated ENPP2 as a meaningful target of exosomal miR-29c-3p from M1 macrophages. Since ENPP2 was initially discovered in melanoma cells as a motility stimulating factor [34], the ENPP2/LPA axis has been revealed to promote cancer aggressiveness, particularly in terms of migration and invasion [35]. Regarding miRNA, it has been reported that miR-29c-3p attenuates the expression of ENPP2 [36], a finding we corroborated in this study. Our results, however, further emphasized that ENPP2 is not only a target of exosomal miR-29c-3p but also controlled specifically by M1 macrophages, and not by M2 macrophages. To identify targets specifically affected by M1 macrophages in A375SM cells, we eliminated all the DEG whose expression changed in the same direction in M2 macrophage co-cultured case. We also confirmed that downregulating ENPP2 relieves the migration and invasion of A375SM cells, as mentioned by other studies. Based on these observations, we propose that ENPP2 -mediated mechanisms contribute to the aggressiveness of melanoma cells, which may be suppressed by exosomal miR-29c-3p, specifically derived from M1 macrophages.

In this study, we focused on cholesterol homeostasis among the ENPP2 related mechanisms that promote the migration and invasion of A375SM cells and might be adjusted by miR-29c-3p. It is well known that abnormal cholesterol homeostasis plays critical roles in aggressiveness of cancer cells. In particular, cholesterol content is crucial: increased cholesterol leads to its integration within the lipid bilayer, which reduces fluidity and enhances rigidity of cell membrane, resulting in restricted cell motility [37]. However, there are few reports about the effect of ENPP2 on cholesterol metabolism. We newly demonstrated that cholesterol is accumulated by knock-down of ENPP2 in A375SM cells, which is accompanied by diminished expression of cholesterol transporter, such as ABCA1. From PPI network analysis, we also verified that ENPP2 may be linked to cholesterol transporters in LPA related manner. Additionally, as we observed a reduced level of LXRα/β in ENPP2-knocked-down A375SM cells, we suggested ENPP2-PPARγ-LXRα/β axis as the signaling pathway to explain the correlation between ENPP2 and cholesterol transporter, ABCA1. Previous studies have shown that LXRα/β are expressed by PPARγ and function as transcription factor promoting the expression of ABCA1 [23]. Other studies have reported that LPA acts as a second messenger to activate the PPARγ [22]. Based on all the reports and our findings, we assume that ENPP2 contributes to the cholesterol efflux by increasing ABCA1 expression through producing LPA, which activates PPARγ to upregulate the transcription factor, LXR α/β in A375SM cells. Therefore, targeting ENPP2 with RNAi leads to cholesterol accumulation by disrupting ENPP2-PPARγ-LXRα/β axis, and ultimately attenuates the migration and invasion of melanoma cells. However, further studies are required to determine the precise mechanisms through which LPA induces PPARγ activation and whether the decrease in LPA is the primary factor responsible for PPARγ inactivation in A375SM cells.

We also investigated if ENPP2 affects outside of cells, such as ECM properties or remodeling. This is because LPA, the product of ENPP2, activates the RhoA-ROCK pathway to modulate ECM related genes [25]. As an important factor in the tumor microenvironment, the ECM is closely related to cancer cell migration and invasion. Tumor ECM is commonly stiffer than that of normal tissue, and this increased stiffness creates a more favorable environment for cancer cells to migrate and invade by stimulating integrin-dependent signaling pathways that are necessary for cell motility [27]. The stiffness of ECM is determined by two key factors: ECM deposition, such as collagen type 1, and the linearization or crosslink of ECM fibers by ECM remodeling enzymes, like as CCN4 and LOX [38, 39]. In this study we confirmed that not only collagen type 1 but also CCN4 and LOX are upregulated by ENPP2. Eventually it leaded to lower collagen type1 deposition in the ECM of A375SM cells. Accordance with these results, we suggest that ENPP2 promotes stiffer ECM in A375SM. This may be supportive evidence for previous reports proposing cancer cells themselves also play roles as ECM producers to change the composition of ECM, though fibroblasts are known as a major producer of ECM in tumor environment [40]. Beyond ECM stiffness, the expression of proteases, for example MMPs and uPA, is crucial for cancer cell movement. As different type of ECM remodeling enzymes, they degrade ECM and open the migration track for cancer cells. We observed that the MMP2, MMP9 and uPA, were also downregulated when ENPP2 was inhibited, which shows the additional effects of ENPP2 on ECM remodeling. The ENPP2 mediated ECM modulations have primarily been emphasized as therapeutic targets in fibrosis-related diseases, such as renal interstitial fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [41, 42]. Therefore, this study highlights the potential of targeting ENPP2 as an effective strategy to inhibit the migration and invasion of melanoma cells by reducing ECM stiffness and the activity of ECM remodeling enzymes.

To evaluate whether the in vitro data obtained from the melanoma cell line can be generalized to in vivo or clinical case, we conducted in silico analysis. First, from both GEO datasets on different type of mouse melanoma models, it was observed that the ENPP2 expression increased as the melanoma cells became more metastatic. Simultaneously, changes in lipid metabolism and ECM remodeling were also found in metastatic mouse melanoma models. Since the results of the bioinformatics analysis also support the in vitro findings of this study, it is expected that the in vitro data could be generalized in vivo systems. To confirm this, it would be beneficial to test whether an increase in miR-29c can reduce the elevated levels of ENPP2 in mouse metastatic melanoma models and suppress cancer progression in future studies. Regarding the clinical data analysis, we identified a trend toward decreased survival rate in patients group with relatively high ENPP2 levels, although the result was not statistically significant. Given the many subpopulations of melanoma, future studies could provide a better understanding of the effect of ENPP2 on melanoma patient’s survival by conducting analyses that consider each subtype of melanoma.

In conclusion, we discovered that M1 macrophage-derived exosomal miR-29c-3p may inhibit aggressiveness of melanoma cells through downregulating ENPP2. Further investigation revealed that both inside and outside of melanoma cells were altered in ways unfavorable for migration and invasion when ENPP2 was reduced by RNAi: cholesterol accumulated inside the melanoma cells, and the ECM was remodeled outside of the cells. Therefore, modulating tumor microenvironment to create conditions favorable for M1 macrophages is beneficial in suppressing melanoma. in this context, the pharmacologic therapies to prevent macrophages polarization to the M2 status might also be effectual approach [7]. Additionally, delivering miR-29c-3p or inhibiting ENPP2 could be potential strategies for controlling melanoma.

Limitation of the study

Despite the promising results, potential limitations exist in this study. First, there is the absence of animal experiments. Given that the TME is a highly complex milieu, the co-culture system used in this study may not fully replicate the melanoma TME. In the TME, M1 macrophages may be affected by other factors, such as different immune cells or heterogeneous cancer cells, which induces M1 macrophages to release different exosomal miRNA. Therefore, it is needed to confirm whether the consistent effects are observed in vivo system including vascular network and other types of immune cells. To address the lack of animal experiments, in silico analyses were conducted to complement this study. Second, the cholesterol accumulation may promote tumorigenic signaling which is contrary to the findings in this research. We propose that cholesterol accumulation caused by ENPP2 knock-down reduces the cell membrane fluidity thereby restricting metastatic potential. Although this correlation between cholesterol component and cell motility is well established, there are reports asserting that cholesterol accumulation has a pro-tumoral effect by abnormal activation of tyrosine kinase receptors. Thus, further rescue experiments are needed to clarify the effect of cholesterol accumulation in melanoma cancer cells, such as inducing cholesterol efflux through the overexpression of ABCA1 in ENPP2 knock down melanoma cells.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human melanoma cell line (A375SM), Human lung cancer cell line (A549), Human colon cancer cell line (HCT116), Human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line (Huh7), human gastric adenocarcinoma cell line (AGS) and human mononuclear macrophage line (THP-1) were obtained from the Korean Cell Line Bank (KCLB, Seoul, Korea) and American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), respectively. A375SM cells were incubated in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) while THP-1 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium. The complete culture medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2.

Differentiation of macrophages

For M0 macrophage differentiation, human THP-1 monocytes were treated with 12-O-tetradecanoylphorbol-l3-acetate (PMA; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 48 h in a humidified incubator at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. The M0 macrophages were treated with 100 ng/ml LPS (Sigma-Aldrich) and 20 ng/ml hIFN-ɤ (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) or 20 ng/ml hIL-4 (R&D Systems) and 20 ng/ml hIL-13 (R&D Systems) in complete RPMI media for 48 h for M1 or M2-like macrophages differentiation respectively.

Exosome purification

To isolate the exosomes, the conditioned media of M1 macrophages was harvested after co-cultured with A375SM melanoma cells. All experiments were performed at 4 ℃. Then the conditioned media was initially centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min to remove the cells and large debris. The supernatants were centrifuged subsequently at 2000 × g at for 20 min and 10,000 × g for 30 min then filtered through 0.22 μm poly-styrene vacuum filters (Merck Milliporem St. Burlington MA, USA) and additional centrifuge was performed at 100,000 × g for 70 min. The pellets containing exosomes were washed twice in PBS (pH 7.4) by centrifugation at 110,000 × g for 70 min and the washed pellets were resuspended in the PBS (pH 7.4) or RIPA buffer for western blot.

Characterization of exosomes

To analyze the size distribution of exosomes, dynamic light scattering was performed with the Zetasizer Pro Zsu3200 instrument (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, U.K) and the data was analyzed by ZS XPLORER v 3.00 software. To detect exosome-specific markers on isolated exosomes, purified exosome pellets were lysed with RIPA buffer and proteinase inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich) and western blot analysis was performed following the same procedures mentioned in western blot analysis section. The following antibodies were used: anti-63 (ab193349; 1:1000), anti-β-actin (#4967; 1:10000).

Affymetrix expression microarray analysis

The RNA was extracted and purified from the A375SM cells which were co-cultured with each type of macrophage. Then the purity and integrity of RNA was evaluated by ND-1000 Spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, Wilmington, USA) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, USA). The Affymetrix Whole Transcript Expression array was executed according to the manufacturer's protocol using GeneChip Whole Transcript PLUS reagent Kit (Affymetrix, Santa Clara. CA, US). In brief the cDNA was synthesized using the GeneChip WT (Whole Transcript) Amplification kit as described by the manufacturer. Then the cDNA was fragmented and biotin-labeled with TdT (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase) using the GeneChip WT Terminal labeling kit. Approximately 5.5 μg of labeled DNA target was hybridized to the Affymetrix GeneChip Human 2.0 ST Array at 45 °C for 16 h. Hybridized arrays were washed and stained on a GeneChip Fluidics Station 450 and scanned on a GCS3000 Scanner (Affymetrix). Signal values were computed using the Affymetrix® GeneChip™ Command Console software and Affymetrix® Expression Console™ Software was used to process raw data.

Small RNA sequencing

The exosomes were extracted and purified from the conditioned media after co-culture each type of macrophage and A375SM cells to conduct RNA sequencing on exosomal RNA. To construct cDNA libraries, SMARTer® smRNA-Seq Kit for Illumina® kit ((Illumina, San Diego, CA) and TruSeq SBS Kit v3 (Illumina)were used. Poly A–selected RNA extraction, RNA fragmentation, random hexamer-primed reverse transcription, and 100-nt single-end sequencing with the Illumina HiSeq2500 sequencer (Illumina) were per formed. Low-quality raw reads were eliminated, and data were aligned to Homo sapiens (hg19) using miRBase v21 and non-coding RNA database, Rfam9.1. The sorted readings were configured to estimate their abundance as fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped values of the transcript and gene expressed in each sample using miRDeep2 2.0.0.7 and Bowtie 1.1.1.

Transfection

The A375SM cells were plated in the 6-well plate at 3 × 105 cells/well before transfection. With 70–80% cell confluence, miR-29c-3p mimics, miRNA mimic Negative Control (miR-NC), Enpp2 siRNA (sense strand: CUAACUAAUGUGGAUGAUA; antisense strand: UAUCAUCCACAUUAGUUAG) or scramble-siRNA Negative control (Si-NC) (BIONEER, Daejeon, Korea) were transfected separately into A375SM using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were collected for in vitro analysis, 48 h post-transfection.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

The total RNA was extracted using RNA extraction kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cDNA was synthesized from total extracted RNA using SuperScript™ VILO™ Master Mix (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA). TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan) on an ABI 7500 system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) was employed to conduct RT-qPCR assay with gene-specific primers. GAPDH was amplified as an endogenous control and relative expression level of the target gene was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCT method. Results were presented as fold change when gene expression level in control sample was set as 1. All experiments were run in triplicate. The sequences of each primer were shown in Table S1.

miRNA isolation, miRNA cDNA synthesis, and miRNA RT-qPCR

The miRNA was extracted with RNAzol RT (catalog no. R4533; Sigma Aldrich) to isolate separate fractions of mRNA and miRNA. Then the MystiCq miRNA cDNA Synthesis Mix Kit (cat no. MIRRT; Sigma-Aldrich) was used to convert miRNA into cDNA templates for qPCR. Due to the small size of miRNA, it was subjected to polyadenylation through a poly-A polymerase reaction first. Then, the poly-A–tailed miRNA was converted into first-strand cDNA using reverse transcriptase and an oligo-dT adapter primer. The amplification of miRNA cDNAs was quantified in real-time SYBR Green RT-qPCR reactions according to the recommended two-step cycling procedure (preincubation/activation at 95 °C for 2 min followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for the 30 s). MystiCq miRNA Universal PCR Primer (cat no. MIRUP; Sigma-Aldrich) and MystiCq® microRNA qPCR Assay Primer hsa-miR-29c-3p (Cat No. MIRAP00077, Sigma-Aldrich) were used. MiRNA RT-qPCR was performed using the Applied Biosystems 7500 real-time cyclers (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). The target gene expression was quantified using a kit containing the human positive control primer, the small nucleolar RNA SNORD44.

Dual luciferase reporter gene assay

The targeting relationship of ENPP2 and miR-29c-3p was predicted with an online website and it was confirmed by the Dual luciferase reporter gene assay. To construct ENPP2 luciferase reporters, ENPP2 wild-type 3’UTR and mutant 3’UTR were inserted separately in multiple cloning regions of psiCHECK™-2 Vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA): psiCHECK-ENPP2-WT and psiCHECK-ENPP2-MUT. A375SM cells were co-transfected with recombined miRNA target-psiCHECK™-2 vector and miR-29c-3p mimics or miR-NC using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent and Lipofectamine® 2000 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA). After 48 h, the luciferase activity was measured by Dual-Glo® Luciferase Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) using microplate reader (GloMax DISCOVER, Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The activity was represented by the ratio of Renilla luciferase to Firefly luciferase. The assay was performed in triplicate.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

After 48 h of transfection, conditioned medium was collected from A375SM. The conditioned medium was centrifuged and only supernatant was collected. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was adopted to measure the ENPP2 level in cell culture supernatants according to the protocol in Human ENPP-2/Autotaxin Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). The concentration in each sample was analyzed using a microplate reader (GloMax DISCOVER, Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Western blot analysis

Total protein of cells was extracted using radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) with protease inhibitors (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). After 30 min of incubation on ice, cell lysates were centrifuged at 14240 × g at 4 °C for 20 min and supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations were determined using BCA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Equal amounts of protein were electrophoresed in a SDS–polyacrylamide gel and separated proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membranes were blocked by 5% skim milk and then immunoblotted with diluted primary antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight. Subsequently, the secondary antibody was attached to the membranes with appropriate dilutions by incubation for 2 h at room temperature. The specific proteins were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence western blotting analysis system and photographed using a iBright 1500 image analyzer (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA). The following antibodies were used: anti-ENPP2 (ab77104; 1:1000) from Abcam (Cambridge, UK); anti-LXRα/β (sc-377260; 1:1000) from Santa Cruz (Dallas, Texas, USA); anti-β-actin (#4967; 1:10000), horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled horse anti-mouse IgG (#7076; 1:1000) and HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (# 7074; 1:1000) from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA).

Transwell assays

Transwell insert with 8 μm pore size (Corning, NY, USA) were used. For migration assay, transfected A375SM cells were suspended in serum-free MEM and seeded into uncoated transwell upper chamber at 1 × 105 cell/mL of cell density. For invasion assay, the transwell inserts which are coated with Matrigel (Corning, MA, USA) were employed instead. At the same time, 10% FBS-MEM was added to the lower chamber to use serum as the chemoattractant. After incubation for 48 h, the inside of the inserts was wiped off with cotton swab and only the cells on the lower surface of the insert were fixed with 100% of cold methanol at 4 °C. To stain the fixed cells, 1% crystal violet solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added for 20 min. Under a light microscope, 3 fields were randomly selected and cells were counted. The experiment was conducted in triplicates.

RhoA activity assay

To measure the active RhoA, RhoA activation assay kit (Cell Biolabs, San Diego, CA, USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, transfected cells were lysed on ice and centrifuged. The supernatants were added to Rhotekin RBD-agarose bead followed by incubation at 4 °C for 1 h with gentle agitation. After through washing, proteins, binding to the beads, were eluted and subjected to Western blot analysis with anti-RhoA antibody provided by the kit.

Scratch wound healing assay

A375SM cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 3 × 105 cells/well and transfected once the cells reached 70–80% confluence. Straight wounds were generated on the monolayers of transfected cells by using 200 μl pipette tips. The cells were further incubated in the in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Wound gaps were photographed under the microscope at 0, 24 and 48 h respectively and the distance of cell-free wounds was measured.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis

To seek the potential interactions between ENPP2 and cholesterol-metabolism-related proteins, the research tool for retrieval of interacting genes (STRING ver.11.5; https://version-11-5.string-db.org/) was employed. As active interaction sources, textmining, experiments, databases, co-expression, neighborhood, gene fusion and co-occurrence were included and the species was limited to “Homo sapiens”. The minimum required interaction score > 0.4 was applied to construct the PPI networks. The k-means clustering method in STRING was used for cluster analysis. In the networks, the nodes represent proteins and the edges indicate the interactions.

Filipin staining

After 48 h of transfection, A375SM cells were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and washed three times with PBS. The cells were stained for 1 h with 50 μg/mL Filipin III (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in dark. Then cells were washed with PBS three times and mounted with Vecta Shield without 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Vector Lab, CA, USA). Fluorescence images were taken using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) with a UV filter set.

Immunofluorescence staining

The A375SM cells were plated on gelatin-coated coverslips and transfected. The cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After being washed with PBS three times, the cells were blocked by 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with anti-Collagen I antibody (ab34710; 1:300, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at 4 °C overnight. Then the secondary antibody, goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor™ 488 (A11008, Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA) was diluted at a ratio of 1:200 and incubated with the cells for 1 h at room temperature, while actin was probed by ActinRed™ 555 ReadyProbes™ Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Finally, the slides were mounted using vectashields® mounting medium containing DAPI (Vector Lab, CA, USA) for nucleic labeling. A confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) was used to capture the fluorescence images. For the quantification ImageJ (ver. 1.8.0) was used.

Bioinformatics prediction

The GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) was used to search the gene expression data dealing with the in vivo mouse melanoma model. For analyzing the GEO datasets, GEO2R (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r/) was used and to DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) was utilized to perform the functional annotation analysis. The TCGA database (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) was used to retrieve the melanoma patient derived gene expression data. Then the survival analysis was performed with R language (version 4.4.1).

Survival analysis

The datasets, Skin Cutaneous Melanoma dataset in January 2016, were collected from TCGA Firehose Legacy (http://firebrowse.org/?cohort=SKCM). The ENPP2 mRNA expression data was processed using RSEM based on RNASeqV2 from the mRNA expression read counts. And clinical profiles involving 439 human skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) samples were extracted and analyzed. Among the 439 ENPP2 mRNA expression data, 341 metastatic samples were extracted, and the upper 33% and lower 33% were defined and categorized into 113 high ENPP2 group and 113 low ENPP2 group, respectively. Subsequently, survival analysis was performed on the high ENPP2 and low ENPP2 group to determine if ENPP2 expression affected survival status of SKCM patients. From the clinical profile data, information on survival status (death or alive) and time to a particular event (days to death or last follow up) was extracted for each sample. Kaplan–Meier (KM) analysis was conducted for both the high and low ENPP2 groups, and the clinical characteristics of ENPP2 gene expression were estimated by the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Log-rank p-values, hazard ratios (HRs) and Hazard ratio 95% confidence intervals were calculated from the analysis, and survival rates were compared between the high and low ENPP2 groups. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad, MA, USA). Comparison between two dependent experimental groups was carried out by the Student t test. One-way ANOVA with Tukey multiple comparison test was employed for multiple test groups. All the data in this study were presented as the mean ± S.D. A p-value < 0.05 was regarded statistically significant. A statistical significance was denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.00001.
