Safety profile following mRNA-1273 COVID-19 primary vaccination in healthy adults in Indonesia
Authors: Bernie Endyarni Medise, Nastiti Kaswandani, Nina Dwi Putri, Elcha Leonard, Putra Fajar Angkasa, Ade Putra, Rini Muliasari, Viska Indriani, Prima Yosephine, Julitasari Sundoro, Hindra Irawan Satari, Sri Rezeki Hadinegoro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo evaluate the safety and benefit-risk profile of mRNA-1273 following the vaccine's authorization and the administration of over one million doses in Indonesia.DesignA retrospective analytical study was conducted to assess adverse events following immunization (AEFI) within 28 days after the latest dose of primary vaccine of mRNA-1273 COVID-19 vaccine. Data collection was conducted on November 3rd - December 31st, 2022 in 18 public health centers and one hospital from five regions in Jakarta, Indonesia. All of the participants' candidates will be contacted by trained interviewers via telephone to answer the questionnaire.ResultsA total of 1284 subjects (response rate = 31.05%) were enrolled and interviewed, with 13 drop out subjects. The most common solicited local reactions were local pain, erythema and swelling, meanwhile for systemic systemic reaction was fever. Most solicited and systemic local reactions showed an increase in reported events on days 1–3 after vaccination and rapidly decreasing afterwards. After dose 2 of mRNA-1273 COVID-19 vaccine, local pain, was reported more frequently in females and younger age. There were four serious adverse events (SAE) reported in this study which classified as coincidence.ConclusionmRNA-1273 COVID-19 vaccination in Indonesia is safe.

Introduction

On March 1, 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) officially declared coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) a global pandemic. [1] During the first year of the pandemic, more than 100 million people globally were infected. [2] As of August 2023, Indonesia has reported 6,812,670 confirmed COVID-19 cases and 161,895 deaths across 510 districts in 34 provinces. [3]

Despite the continued implementation of control and preventive measures to limit COVID-19 transmission, scientific focus on vaccine development has remained essential as a cornerstone of the global initiative to combat the virus. [2], [4], [5] The mRNA-1273 (Moderna) vaccine represents a novel platforms of COVID-19 immunization and has received Emergency Use Authorization from both the WHO and the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA). [6]

The mRNA-1273 vaccine has demonstrated high efficacy, providing 94.1% protection against COVID-19 in a phase III clinical trial. [4], [7] Evidence from phase I–III randomized controlled trials conducted across multiple countries indicates that the vaccine has a favorable safety profile. [5], [7], [8] Reported solicited adverse events were predominantly mild to moderate in severity, with the most common including fatigue, chills, headache, myalgia, and injection-site pain. [5], [6], [7] In addition to clinical trial data, post-marketing surveillance is essential for providing real-world evidence on the safety of mRNA COVID-19 vaccines across broader populations. For example, a study in pregnant individuals did not identify any clear safety signals following vaccination. [9]

In Indonesia, the mRNA-1273 vaccine was initially introduced as a booster program for healthcare workers only, followed by broader implementation in mass vaccination campaigns, including both primary and booster doses for all healthy adults and older populations. [10] As of August 24, 2023, about 16,733,895 doses of the mRNA-1273 vaccine have been administered across Indonesia. [11]

Although clinical trials have demonstrated a favorable and generally well-tolerated safety profile, a number of of case reports have described uncommon adverse effects following mRNA-1273 vaccination presented as localized erythematous rash surrounding the injection site, which manifests days after the first dose of the Moderna COVID-19 vaccine, called “COVID-arm”. [6], [12], [13]

In this Post-Authorization Safety Study, we aimed to evaluate the safety profile and describe the frequency and characteristics of participant-reported adverse events following immunization (AEFI) among healthy adults receiving primary mRNA-1273 vaccination in Indonesia.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective analytical study was conducted from November 3rd to December 31st, 2022, to assess adverse events following immunization (AEFI) within 28 days of the latest dose of the primary mRNA-1273 COVID-19 vaccine.

We analyzed the number and percentage of subjects with local and systemic reactions (local pain, tenderness, swelling, redness, and others) and systemic events (fatigue, headache, myalgia, arthralgia, chills, nausea, vomiting, fever, and others) following immunization with the mRNA-1273 COVID-19 vaccine.

Data sources and collecting procedures

This cross-sectional, registry-based safety surveillance study was conducted in Jakarta, Indonesia. Vaccination records were obtained from 18 public health centers and 1 government hospital (19 sites total) selected by the DKI (Daerah Khusus Ibukota) Jakarta Provincial Health Office based on the highest number of recipients of the COVID-19 mRNA-1273 vaccine during the study period.

We retrieved a total of 4294 records of individuals who received at least one primary dose from November 3rd to December 31st 2022, from facility vaccination registries. Eligible participants were adults (≥18 years) who received at least one primary dose of the COVID-19 mRNA-1273 vaccine at a participating facility and had a valid telephone contact recorded. Individuals were excluded if they (1) could not be reached after three contact attempts on different days and times; (2) declined or withdrew consent; (3) had incomplete vaccination records; or (4) were unable to provide reliable responses. All eligible individuals within the sampling frame were approached (total population sampling). No a priori sample size calculation was performed, as the objective was descriptive post-marketing safety surveillance.

Data collection and follow up

Trained interviewers contacted participants by telephone after ≥28 days of immunization to capture the full follow-up period in a single call. Up to three call attempts were made per participant. Verbal informed consent was obtained and recorded prior to data collection.

Participants were asked about adverse events occurring within 7 days post-vaccination (primary safety window) and any additional events occurring up to 28 days. Solicited local reactions included injection-site pain, axillary swelling/tenderness, swelling, and redness. Solicited systemic reactions included fatigue, headache, myalgia, arthralgia, chills, nausea/vomiting, and fever. Unsolicited adverse events within 28 days were captured through open-ended questioning. Participants who discontinued the interview after consent were classified as dropouts. The questionnaire used for data collection is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Safety assessment

Adverse events were defined as any unfavorable or unintended sign, symptom, or disease temporally associated with vaccination, regardless of causality. Events were graded as mild, moderate, or severe according to predefined operational criteria based on functional limitation and healthcare utilization. The operational definitions and grading criteria used in this study are presented in the Supplementary Materials [14].

Serious adverse events (SAEs) were defined in accordance with World Health Organization criteria as events resulting in death, life-threatening condition, hospitalization or prolongation of hospitalization, persistent or significant disability/incapacity, or congenital anomaly/birth defect. [15] Reported SAEs were reviewed by the certified national board of 21 physicians and experts trained in medicine and vaccine pharmacovigilance in Indonesia (National Committee of Adverse Events Follow Immunization – NC AEFI).

The causality assessments were performed using a standardized national algorithm amd predefined criteria consistent with WHO AEFI causality assessment guidelines. The evaluations were conducted collectively within the committee to ensure balanced judgment. The committee operates independently in its review process, and all members are required to declare potential conflicts of interest. [15]

Events were categorized as consistent causal association to immunization (vaccine product related reaction, vaccine quality defect-related reaction, immunization error related reaction, and immunization anxiety-related reaction/Immunization stress related response (ISRR)), indeterminate, inconsistent causal association (coincidental), or unclassifiable (if it requires additional information for further review of causality) [15].

In cases of disagreement, events were discussed among committee members until consensus was reached. When consensus could not be achieved, the final classification was determined based on majority opinion and/or consultation with additional independent experts within the committee framework.

Statistical analysis

Data were shown as frequencies and percentages. The chi-square test was used separately for each dose to evaluate the significance of adverse events by dose, sex, and age group. Analysis was performed using SPSS Software version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Data were collected from 19 study sites. Of the 4294 individuals who received the mRNA-1273 vaccine, 4134 with complete records were contacted. A total of 1284 subjects (response rate = 31.05%) were enrolled and interviewed. Thirteen subjects withdrew during the interview and were therefore considered dropouts.

Four SAEs were reported in this study; three occurred in pregnant women. Two subjects were unaware of their pregnancy status when receiving the vaccination at 2 weeks and 4 weeks of gestation, respectively. One experienced a spontaneous abortion, while the other delivered a low birth weight infant. Another pregnant subject who received the mRNA-1273 COVID-19 vaccine during the second trimester gave birth to an infant with a congenital defect (cleft lip and palate). The remaining SAE was reported in a woman with diabetic retinopathy. All SAEs were classified as coincidental and not causally related to vaccination, as assessed by the National Committee on Adverse Events Following Immunization (NCAEFI) in Indonesia.

Discussion

In this retrospective analytical study, we investigated adverse events following immunization with mRNA-1273. A total of 1271 subjects who have received either the first or second dose of mRNA-1273 in Jakarta were included in this study. Data were collected through structured questionnaires administered via telephone interviews. The interviewer recorded subjects' reported reactions at four time points: within 30 min, 1–3 days, 4–7 days, and 8–28 days after the most recent vaccine dose.

The most frequently reported solicited local reaction within 7 days after both the first and second vaccine doses was injection-site pain, predominantly of grade 1 severity. This finding is consistent with results from phase III clinical trials of the Moderna COVID-19 vaccine and a study among healthcare workers in Poland, in which infection-site pain was the most commonly reported local reaction.[13]Furthermore, the proportion of subjects reporting local pain in this study was lower than the result in other phase III clinical trials in Japan (dose 1: 77.1% vs. 83.7%; dose 2: 77.8% vs. 88.2%). [16] Most subjects reported local reactions, including difficulty lifting and moving their hands. This local pain reaction, commonly referred to as “COVID arm” in the United States or “Moderna arm” in Japan, is a delayed-onset, transient adverse cutaneous manifestation at the injection site. [6], [17] Our findings are consistent with a systematic review done by Agustin M. et al., which reported that the highest incidence of this “COVID arm” cases occurred following the first dose of mRNA-1273 vaccination. [18]

The most frequently solicited systemic reaction was fever; temperature measurement and monitoring following immunization were not applicable in this study. We found that the percentage of subjects with fever within 7 days in this study was higher than in the Japanese study (dose 1: 36.2% vs. 0.8%; dose 2: 44.8% vs. 15.5%).[16]Our result aligns with a survey of healthcare workers in Poland, which showed that fever was also fairly common among subjects who received the mRNA-1273 COVID-19 vaccine (42.67%). [13]

Females demonstrated a higher risk of pain, swelling, headache, chills, nausea/vomiting, and fever compared with males following the second dose of the mRNA-1273 COVID-19 vaccine. This observation is consistent with evidence indicating that females generally mount stronger immune responses to vaccination and exhibit greater reactogenicity than males. The variation is primarily due to differences in sex hormone levels and in chromosomes. [19]

Similarly, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) observed that more AEFIs were reported in females than males. Although females accounted for only 61.2% (8,436,863/13,794,904) of vaccinated individuals, they represented 79.1% (5413/6994) of the reported AEFI cases. [20], [21] Likewise, Swissmedic reported that 69.2% of AEFIs occurred in females, compared to 27.8% in males, with cases ranging from mild reactions (such as injection site redness) to severe outcomes (including death). [20]

There is an increased risk of experiencing local pain in young adults compared to the elderly. Consistent with our study, Wang et al. demonstrated that older individuals experienced fewer local and systemic adverse events than younger individuals. [22] It is associated with the concept of immunosenescence, which refers to changes in innate and adaptive immune functions with aging. [22], [23] The activity of thymocytes, thymic epithelial cells, and the immune response substances in the elderly is reduced. Therefore, the immune function is decreased in the elderly. As a result, immune function is reduced, leading to fewer adverse events following immunization in older adults than in younger adults. [22], [23] While compatible with prior evidence on age-related reactogenicity and potential immunosenescence, the observational design and limited sample size preclude causal inference.

In the initial analysis, we also observed an increased risk of fever among subjects with a history of autoimmune disease. Sumantri et al. reported an increased risk of AEFI after administration of an inactivated COVID-19 vaccine in women with autoimmune conditions compared to those without. [24], [25] Fever is a common manifestation of the innate immune response, mediated by the release of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF). However, after multivariate analysis, we found no correlation between a history of immune disease and any adverse events overall. Therefore, we recommend that further research is needed to clarify the nature of this association. [24]

There were four SAEs reported in this study, all classified as coincidental and thus unrelated to the vaccine by the NC AEFI. Three SAEs reported to be experienced by pregnant women with miscarriage, fetal defect (cleft lip and palate), and low birth weight baby. However, these two pregnant subjects did not know their pregnancy status when receiving the vaccination at 2 weeks and 4 weeks of gestation, respectively, and another subject who received vaccination in the second trimester also experienced diabetic retinopathy.

WHO has issued recommendations on vaccination during pregnancy and has implemented them in several countries, including Indonesia. The Ministry of Health of Indonesia has implemented COVID-19 vaccination for pregnant women with a minimum gestational age of 16 weeks (second trimester), using mRNA and inactivated platforms, since August 2nd, 2021. [26]

The spontaneous abortion that occurred after the second dose of the mRNA-1273 COVID-19 vaccine in this study was classified as a coincidental event. Spontaneous abortion occurs in approximately 10–20% of recognized pregnancies. [27] A case-control study in Norway comparing the incidence of spontaneous abortion in the first trimester between vaccinated and unvaccinated people showed that there was no increased risk of spontaneous abortion in early pregnancy after COVID-19 vaccination. [28] Similarly, another study comparing the incidence of spontaneous abortion between vaccinated people with mRNA vaccine platforms and unvaccinated in the first trimester also showed results that there was no difference in the risk of spontaneous abortion events between the two comparison groups. [29]

The low birth weight case reported in the study was the pregnancy outcome of a subject who had a history of epilepsy. The attending physician informed the participant that low birth weight was unlikely to be related to vaccination, but rather could be associated with antiepileptic treatment during pregnancy. Fell et al. and Lipkind et al. reported that there is no increased risk of preterm birth, small for gestational age at birth, or stillbirth after COVID-19 vaccination during any trimester of pregnancy. [30], [31] Conversely, several studies have demonstrated that exposure to antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) during pregnancy is associated with low birth weight and an increased risk of small for gestational age (SGA). [32], [33]

The SAE, which resulted in a cleft lip in the subject's newborn, was also classified as a coincidence by the NC AEFI. Craniofacial anomalies, in particular cleft lip and palate, occur at background rates of approximately 1 per 700–1000 live births. In this case, vaccination occurred outside the critical embryologic window for lip and palate formation (weeks 5–10 of gestation), supporting the biological implausibility of a causal relationship. [30], [34] Therefore, the vaccination after that gestational age did not contribute to the congenital defect in this case.

Diabetic retinopathy following immunization was reported in one subject. To the best of our knowledge, there have been no documented cases linking diabetic retinopathy to COVID-19 vaccination. Common risk factors of diabetic retinopathy include diabetes, high blood sugar level, high cholesterol, high blood pressure, smoking, and gestational diabetes. This subject had a history of diabetes and high blood sugar levels, which are risk factors for diabetic retinopathy. Thus, that condition is caused by exposure to something other than vaccines or is considered a coincidence.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. The retrospective design and reliance on self-reported outcomes introduce potential recall and reporting bias as participants may have difficulty accurately remembering the timing of events (e.g., whether an event occurred on day 3 versus day 4). The absence of an unvaccinated comparison group precludes estimation of relative risk and limits causal inference. The sample size, while sufficient for descriptive analyses, restricts the ability to detect rare adverse events, particularly among subgroups such as pregnant women or individuals with autoimmune disease. Finally, because the study included facilities selected based on high vaccination volume, the cohort may not be fully representative of the broader population.

Conclusion

In summary, the mRNA-1273 COVID-19 vaccination in Indonesia demonstrated a favorable safety profile. The most common solicited reaction within 7 days of immunization was grade 1 injection-site pain. Most reactions were reported within 1–3 days following immunization, and the number of reported reactions decreased over time. All reported adverse reactions resolved without sequelae. No safety concerns were identified during the study, and all reported SAEs were deemed unrelated to the vaccination. Following Dose 2, female sex and younger age were associated with higher odds of adverse events, while no significant association was observed with a history of immune-related disease. However, the limited number of events, particularly after Dose 1, restricts the precision of these estimates and highlights the need for further investigation.
