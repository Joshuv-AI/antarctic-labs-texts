Invited Perspective: Changing Places—How Moving Histories Can Help Map the Health Impacts of People’s Environmental Exposures
Authors: Meredith Pedde, Sara D. Adar
Published: 2024

The new work by Chen et al.1 in this issue of Environmental Health Perspectives offers an important contribution to the literature by assessing the associations of multiple air pollutants and the incidence of 14 different categories of disease and mortality among participants in the UK Biobank study. To answer this question, the authors focused their evaluation on information from people who changed residential locations during the follow-up period. Specifically, they compared the disease incidence and mortality experiences of people who moved between low-, medium-, and high-exposure areas to those who moved from a low-exposure area to another low-exposure area. In doing so, they consistently found that compared with individuals who moved from one low-exposure area to another low-exposure area, those who moved from a low-exposure area to a medium- or high-exposure area had increased incidence of all 14 disease categories studied for all pollutants that were studied. In contrast, for all-cause mortality these patterns were evident only for nitrogen dioxide and nitrogen oxide exposure.

As the authors discuss, a unique and interesting aspect of the work is its focus on movers and the comparison in exposures before and after a change in residence. By looking at within-individual changes in both exposure and health, the study design provides stronger evidence of causality because of its inherent control for potential confounding by characteristics that do not change over time. Importantly, this would include factors that may be both more easily observable or measurable (e.g., sex, race and ethnicity, and educational attainment), as well as those that are more difficult to observe or quantify (e.g., individual risk preferences and personal behaviors). As such, the design greatly mitigates concerns about residual confounding by known and unknown characteristics that are stable over time.

Although the design and scientific findings of this work add nicely to the literature, this is not the first study to use movers to study the role of air pollution on human health. For example, a recent review by Edwards et al.2 identified 15 studies that used a relocation design to study the association between ambient air pollution and health. Unlike Chen et al.,1 however, many of those studies had small sample sizes, studied short-term relocations, or focused on pregnancy outcomes.3–13 Only 4 of the identified studies assessed longer-term relocations among large cohorts to look at mortality and hospitalizations.14–17 Awad et al.,14 for example, studied the association between fine particulate matter and death among >10million US Medicare enrollees who relocated during follow-up, whereas Gan et al.16 studied the impact of relocating farther from or closer to a major roadway or highway with respect to mortality among nearly half a million residents of Vancouver, Canada. Krewski et al.17 also studied the mortality impacts of relocations among >8,000 participants in the Harvard Six Cities Study, and Lleras-Muney15 studied hospitalizations in >100,000 children of military families that were subject to compulsory relocations.

These add to findings from other quasi-experimental study designs that have been used to assess the relationship between air pollution and health in the past by leveraging major changes within a location, such as the sudden closure of a large industrial facility,18 the adoption of new regulations,19 or local pollution control policies like those implemented before the 2008 Beijing Olympics.20–22 Although these study designs also use changes over time to provide stronger evidence of causality, they are often conducted at the group level and thus do not share all the same advantages of residential relocation designs of individual-level data. Similarly, many of these studies were based on changes with shorter durations than residential relocations and therefore do not provide information on potential health effects of modulating long-term air pollution exposure.

It is important to note that in a residential relocation design not all confounding will be mitigated because, although an experiment of sorts, the treatment is not randomized. Most notably, temporally varying factors will not be accounted for by design. Although time trends and some other temporally varying factors may be easy to adjust for, others may not be. For example, more subtle characteristics that relate to why someone chose to move or that affected their choice of where to move to could still very much confound associations between air pollution and health. For example, suppose people with newly diagnosed health conditions, such as heart or lung disease—both of which have been shown to be related to air pollution23—move from higher- to lower-exposure areas out of concern for their health. Or maybe they move from urban settings to be nearer to their children and grandchildren in the suburbs. Healthy persons living in rural areas with lower exposures may move to higher-exposure urban areas to pursue economic opportunities. Without accounting for such factors, the results could be biased.

In these examples, the direction of the bias would be downward, suggesting a more protective association between air pollution and mortality. However, bias does not have to be toward the null. As another example, let us consider retirement as a time of increased residential relocation for older adults. Suppose healthy retirees in higher-exposure urban areas relocate to less hectic and lower-exposure areas, but retirees with health concerns stay in the higher-exposure area to remain close to medical services. Under these scenarios, the results could be biased toward a stronger adverse relationship between air pollution and health if these factors are not accounted for.

Although in theory time-varying confounding in a study of movers could be known and adjusted for, factors such as health status are not always available or known to the researcher, especially in the case of studies using administrative data. It is also common for researchers to have only health information at baseline and thus be unable to adjust for changes in health during follow-up. This was the case in Chen et al.,1 for example, where all of the many sociodemographic factors the authors adjusted for had been measured at baseline, before relocation occurred. Any factors that changed after baseline that may have been related to the health effect or air pollution, or both, were not accounted for. Given that their population ranged from 40 to 69 years of age at baseline, it is likely unrealistic to assume participants’ health and sociodemographic status remained stable throughout follow-up.

An alternative to adjusting for time-varying confounders is to study relocation among populations where moves are not by the participants’ choice. This was the approach used by Lleras-Muney,15 who studied air pollution and child hospitalizations among military families, where the military decides when and where a family relocates. Using this population would greatly reduce concerns about confounders related to why or where someone relocates given that the move is not determined by the individual. Of course, there could still be problems if the health of a child was a consideration in the parental assignment. In addition, it is uncommon for people’s locations to be truly randomized, and there are trade-offs to studying relocations among military families in terms of generalizability.

Although moving was not randomized in the new study by Chen et al.,1 the use of residential mobility still makes it a good addition to the literature on air pollution and health. The work also highlights the exciting potential for using natural experiments in longitudinal settings to get closer to a causal understanding of the health implications of environmental factors. Further, although the term natural experiment may suggest that the exposure contrast need come from a “natural” or random occurrence, the new study by Chen et al. highlights that instead, natural experiments can involve complex social, economic, or policy processes that may not be entirely natural. Therefore, alternative terms have been proposed, such as “quasi-experimental” or “observational study with a natural variation.” Regardless of the name, these designs also need not be limited to studying air pollution but could help us to better understand features such as water quality, radiation, climate, and much more. Any factor that differs over space is fair game—even the sky is no limit.
