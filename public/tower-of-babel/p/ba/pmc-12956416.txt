Transition from the posterior to the Superior Transverse Anatomic Reconstruction (STAR) approach for total hip arthroplasty
Authors: Alex J. Anatone, Isaiah K. Selkridge, Theofilos Karasavvidis, Eleftherios Tsiridis, Seth A. Jerabek, Jonathan M. Vigdorchik, David J. Mayman, Peter K. Sculco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsThe Superior Transverse Anatomic Reconstruction (STAR) approach for total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a modified posterior approach that preserves the piriformis and the quadratus femoris. This approach provides improved short-term outcomes, but few studies compare patients undergoing THA with STAR to the posterolateral approach (PA). The goal of this study is to compare the short-term perioperative outcomes of STAR with PA and evaluate the learning curve associated with the STAR approach.MethodsA total of 561 consecutive patients who underwent primary THA from 1 August 2023 to 30 April 2024 were included in the STAR approach cohort. Patients in the PA cohort underwent primary THA from 1 January 2017 to 31 July 2023 and were matched 1:1 with STAR patients by age, sex, BMI, and surgeon. Continuous outcomes (change in Hip disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (HOOS, JR), estimated blood loss (EBL), length of stay (LOS), operating time, opioid use, morphine milligram equivalents, and binary outcomes (transfusion, readmission, complications) were assessed with multiple linear regression at six weeks post operation.ResultsSTAR cohort patients used less postoperative opiods in the immediate postoperative period and at six weeks (MME 65 (SD 58) vs 107 (SD 126); p < 0.001, and 258 (SD 273) vs 364 (SD 308); p < 0.001, respectively) and experienced less EBL (127 ml (SD 55) vs 162 ml (SD 87); p < 0.001), lower operating time (82 minutes (SD 24) vs 90 minutes (SD 27); p < 0.001), and shorter LOS (0.9 days (SD 1.2) vs 1.7 days (SD 1.3); p < 0.001). There were no differences in the change in HOOS, JR score from pre- to postoperative, the rate of blood transfusion, readmission, or postoperative complications requiring revision.ConclusionThe STAR approach is a piriformis and quadratus preserving approach that can be used for primary THA and without a learning curve. The benefits of the STAR approach may include a shorter operating time, less EBL, shorter LOS, and less postoperative pain requiring opioids. Longer-term follow-up in necessary to determine if the STAR approach continues to be associated with a lower overall revision rate compared with the conventional posterior approach.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(3):303–308.

Introduction

Trends in surgical approaches for total hip arthroplasty (THA) have changed over the years, however, the a standard posterolateral approach (PA) remains one of the most commonly used surgical approaches worldwide.1,2 The PA provides excellent visualization of the acetabulum and femur which facilitates the preparation and implantation of components. Conventional posterior approach THA involves tenotomy of the piriformis tendon and short external rotator tendons, which are usually repaired during closure.3,4 However, standard transosseous repair of the piriformis distalizes its insertion and increases overall tension potentially leading to failure of the repair. Postoperative MRI studies demonstrate moderate to severe atrophy of the piriformis muscle after repair in ~65% of patients with 100% of piriformis tendons not intact with the greater trochanter at mid-term follow-up and, instead, are bridged by scar.5

The Superior Transverse Anatomic Reconstruction (STAR) approach is a modified posterolateral approach that preserves the piriformis tendon and the quadratus femoris muscle.6 The piriformis muscle is an important stabilizer of the hip and preservation of the insertion has been suggested to provide improved short-term outcomes in comparison with the direct superior approach and posterior approach,6,7 but there are no modern studies comparing large cohorts of patients undergoing THA with the STAR approach and with the PA.

The goal of this study is to: 1) compare short-term perioperative and postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing THA with the STAR approach versus the standard posterolateral approach; and 2) evaluate whether a learning curve is associated with transition in surgical practice from the posterolateral approach to the STAR approach.

Methods

Patient selection

After receiving approval from the institutional review board at Hospital for Special Surgery, a retrospective chart review was conducted on all THAs performed by four fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeons (SJ, JV, DM, PS) between 1 August 1 2023 and 30 April 2024. The patients identified within this timeframe constituted the STAR cohort, with the period defined by the date when the surgeons transitioned from a PA to a STAR approach THA. Patients in the PA cohort were identified through a chart review of THAs performed by the same surgeons from 1 January 2017 to 31 July 2023. These patients were matched 1:1 with STAR patients based on age, sex, BMI, and surgeon. Inclusion of patients in the PA cohort was prioritized based on the most recent dates to ensure a contemporary cohort. Technology, including navigation and/or robotics, was used in all cases for both cohorts. All patients adhered to the same postoperative pain regimen and followed a standardized rehabilitation protocol. Patients aged under 18 years, those undergoing bilateral THA, and patients with inflammatory arthritis or requiring hardware removal during THA were excluded from the study. Study variables were obtained from a chart review of the electronic medical records for all patients. Study variables included age, BMI, sex, latest clinical follow-up (minimum six weeks), laterality, preoperative and six-week postoperative Hip disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (HOOS, JR) scores,8 inpatient and six-week morphine milligram equivalents (MMEs), intraoperative estimated blood loss (EBL), need for blood transfusion, operating time, length of stay (LOS), readmission, and reasons for revision surgery.

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1,122 patients were included in the study, with 561 consecutive patients identified in the STAR approach cohort, each matched 1:1 with 561 patients in the PA cohort. The average time between STAR approach surgery and the matched PA approach surgery was 40.0 months. The mean age in the STAR group was 62 years (SD 10.3), compared with the PA group with a mean age of 62.1 years (SD 10) (p = 0.884). In each group, 54.9% (308/561) of participants were female. The mean BMI was 29.1 kg/m² (SD 5.9) in the STAR group and 29 kg/m² (SD 5.6) in the PA group (p = 0.912). Left THA was performed in 46% (258/561) of patients in the STAR group and 50.8% (285/561) of patients in the PA group (p = 0.091). Demographic characteristics are shown in Table I.

Surgical technique

All THAs were performed by four fellowship-trained surgeons (SJ, JV, DM, PS). THAs in the PA group were performed via the standard posterior approach with repair of the capsule, conjoint tendon, and piriformis tendon as previously described.9 The STAR approach, which is a modification of the standard posterolateral approach that preserves the insertion of the piriformis tendon, and has been previously described (Figure 1).6 The use of navigation or robot was entirely based on surgeon preference on a case-by-case basis.

Statistical analysis

The a-priori power analysis indicated that the included number of patients provided greater than 90% power to detect clinically meaningful differences in all continuous outcomes. For rare binary outcomes with a frequency of less than 2%, the analyses were primarily exploratory, as the sample size was sufficient to detect differences of only seven to ten-fold.

Descriptive statistics were used, with means and SDs used for continuous variables, and categorical variables represented using absolute and relative frequencies. Continuous outcomes (change in HOOS, JR, intraoperative blood loss, length of stay, operating room time, opioids) and binary outcomes (transfusion, readmission, revision, dislocation, periprosthetic fracture) were assessed with multiple linear and logistic regression equations at six weeks postoperative. The minimal clinical important difference (MCID) for the HOOS, JR score was determined at 18.0 or greater as previously described by Lyman et al.10 Statistical significance was established at p ≤ 0.05. Stata v. 17 (StataCorp, USA) was used as statistical software for all analyses.

Results

Function

Preoperative HOOS, JR score was recorded in 425 patients in the STAR cohort and 449 patients in the PA cohort. Postoperative HOOS, JR scores at six weeks were recorded for 194 patients in the STAR cohort and 290 patients in the PA cohort. Both preoperative and six-week postoperative HOOS, JR scores were available for 179 patients in the STAR Cohort and 252 patients in the PA cohort. The mean preoperative HOOS, JR score was 55 (SD 16.3) in the STAR cohort and 51 (SD 16.3) in the PA cohort (p = 0.001). The mean six-week postoperative HOOS, JR score was 78.4 (SD 13.6) in the STAR cohort and 74.7 (SD 14.3) in the PA cohort (p = 0.009; Table II). No significant difference was identified in the HOOS, JR score improvement from preoperative to six weeks postoperative between the two cohorts (21.8 (SD 17.6) vs 22.2 (SD 17.5), p = 0.820; Table II). MCID for HOOS JR scores was reached in 57.5% (103/179) of patients in the STAR cohort and 58.3% (147/252) of patients in the PA cohort at six weeks (Table II).

Pain

The STAR cohort was associated with significantly lower use of postoperative opioids compared with the PA cohort, as indicated by the mean prescribed MMEs. This difference was observed both in the immediate postoperative period while inpatient (65 (SD 58) vs 107 (SD 126), p < 0.001), and up to six weeks postoperative (258 (SD 273) vs 364 (SD 308), p < 0.001).

Intraoperative

Comparison of mean intraoperative EBL indicated that STAR patients experienced significantly less EBL compared with the PA patients (127 cm3 (SD 55) vs 162 cm3 (SD 87), p < 0.001). The mean operating time was also significantly lower in the STAR cohort compared with the PA cohort (82 minutes (SD 24) vs 90 minutes (SD 27), p < 0.001). The assessment of LOS showed a lower LOS for the STAR cohort (0.9 days (SD 1.2) vs 1.7 days (SD 1.3), p < 0.001), while there was no significant difference in the need for blood transfusion between STAR and PA patients (7 (SD 1.2%) vs 11 (SD 2%), p = 0.286).

Postoperative

When assessing complication up to six weeks after THA, it was demonstrated that STAR approach patients had a rate of revision surgery for any reason of 0.1% (n = 1) compared with 0.4% (n = 2) in the posterior approach cohort. Reasons for revision in the STAR group include prosthetic joint infection (n = 1). Reasons for revision in the PA group include dislocation (n = 1), and leg length discrepancy (n = 1). No significant differences were identified in readmissions (p = 0.097).

Discussion

The STAR approach is a piriformis, quadratus, and IT band sparing posterior-based approach for THA. Technically, this approach is very similar to a quadratus sparing PA with a modification to perform the posterior capsulotomy at the inferior border of the piriformis tendon to preserve its insertion. Repair of the capsule and conjoint tendon are performed in a similar fashion to the PA approach. This approach was adopted by several of the surgeons at the study institution and was implemented into practice immediately.

A posterior based surgical approach maintaining the piriformis and quadratus femoris attachments was first proposed by Khan et al7 in 2006 and later coined the STAR approach by Kenanidis et al6 in 2023. Other piriformis preserving approaches have been described in literature as well. Both studies suggested to have shorter LOS and less EBL with the STAR approach, and Kenanidis et al6 found improved early functional outcomes when compared with the direct superior approach.7 When comparing the STAR approach with the PA in this study, patients in the STAR group similarly experienced shorter LOS and less EBL. Furthermore, STAR patients in this study experienced decreased operating time and postoperative opioid use. STAR patients also had a similarly low rate of complications compared with PA patients, suggesting no increased risk of complications when adopting this approach.

The piriformis muscle plays an important role as an external rotator and stabilizer of the hip joint. Despite the improved rates of instability after surgical repair of the piriformis,4,11 an MRI study by Khan et al12 showed degenerative changes in the repaired piriformis muscle that remain years after surgery, and were not seen in piriformis sparing surgeries. Lalevée et al13 performed an MRI study on patients who underwent piriformis sparing posterior approach THA and similarly found preservation of the piriformis muscle in 19 of 21 patients. These data suggest that the function of the piriformis may not be fully restored with a healed repair, and that a piriformis sparing technique offers the best chances for preservation of the muscle. Arora et al14 recently performed a randomized prospective trial comparing the posterior approach with a piriformis sparing approach THA and found improvement in intraoperative stability as determined by increased internal rotation with hip flexion in the piriformis sparing group and no difference in component placement, supporting the clinical relevance of the piriformis muscle, and the safety of a piriformis sparing technique with regards to component placement.

Many different surgical approaches to the hip have been advocated over the years with the goal of improving outcomes in THA. Early joint arthroplasties by Sir John Charnley15 were performed through the trans-trochanteric approach. Although THA was a revolutionary surgery that brought pain relief and improved function to patients suffering from hip disease, the transtrochanteric and traditional posterolateral approaches, as described by Kocher-Langenbeck and Moore,16,17 were associated with high complication rates in comparison to modern standards and have since fallen out of favour.4,18,19 The traditional posterolateral approach was later modified to include a repair of the posterior tissues which improved dislocation rates significantly.4,11 Dislocation rates have continued to improve secondary to advances in implant materials and the evolution of enabling technology.20-22 More recently the direct anterior approach (DAA) has gained in popularity with some studies suggesting of improved dislocation rates and early functional outcomes, although there is conflicting evidence when compared with a modern PA technique.23-25 While the DAA has recently crossed the threshold to being the most commonly used surgical approach for THA in the USA, a recent survey of American Association of Hip and Knee Surgeon (AAHKS) members showed that anterior approach surgeons are generally earlier in their career.26 This may be due to the fact that the DAA demonstrates a relatively large learning curve, estimated at 50 to 150 cases,27,28 and established PA approach surgeons may be reluctant to take on the burden of complications associated with a large learning curve.26 In fact, only 3% of non-DAA surgeons surveyed by Patel et al26 planned to switch to the DAA in the future. Instead, the STAR approach offers an easy-to-adopt, more soft-tissue sparing, posterior-based approach that does not significantly impact the exposure and THA execution. In this study, the transition to the STAR approach for the four surgeons included in this study occurred overnight and without speciality training and without increase complication. Not only were there no increased complication rates in the first 561 patients, data from this study demonstrated an immediate improvement in operating time, EBL, LOS, and postoperative opioid use. This supports the idea that the STAR approach can be adopted by PA surgeons without risk of a learning curve and no increase in complications, and potentially see immediate early clinical benefits.

There are a number of limitations to this study. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, the accuracy of the data regarding complications is limited to the patient chart. Patients who were treated at outside institutions for complications may not be captured in this study. Additionally, all surgeons in this study use intraoperative technology for surgical planning and execution. While this may limit the generalizability of the results, the use of technology helps to control for inaccuracies in implant position that could otherwise lead to postoperative complications. Furthermore, all PA and STAR surgeries were performed by the same group of surgeons, with STAR approach surgeries being performed more recently. Despite efforts to match STAR patients with as contemporary PA cohort as possible, results of the PA cohort may be affected by evolution of postoperative pain and rehabilitation protocols. Additionally, with LOS decreasing recently in the setting of primary THA, this may have influenced the lower LOS found in the more recent STAR approach patients. Findings of decreased postoperative opioid pain medication or length of stay would be most notably affected by these differences. We believe that the time difference between the two cohorts is relatively minimal and that comparing cohorts from the same surgeons decreases the confounding effects of practice differences when comparing results between surgeons. Future studies should compare simultaneous cohorts undergoing THA with the STAR approach with PA to validate the findings of this study. It is important to note that across our entire study, less than 40% of patients had complete patient-reported outcome measure data. Although the data we received from this cohort are strong, more than half of the patients were not able to provide their PROMs for final analysis.

In conclusion, the STAR approach is a less invasive, piriformis, quadratus, and illiotibial band preserving, posterior-based approach that can be used for all primary THAs. The benefits of the STAR approach include less EBL, shorter operating time and length of stay, and less and postoperative pain requiring opioids compared with conventional PA. In addition, current posterolateral approach surgeons can transition their surgical practice to the STAR approach with ease, and avoid the risk of increased complications associated with the learning curves of other approaches.

Take home message

- The Superior Transverse Anatomic Reconstruction (STAR) approach is a less invasive, piriformis, quadratus, and illiotibial band preserving, and posterior-based approach (PA) that can be utilized for all primary total hip arthroplasties.

- The benefits of the STAR approach include less estimated blood loss, shorter operating time, and length of stay, and less and postoperative pain requiring opioids compared with the conventional PA.

- In addition, current PA surgeons can transition their surgical practice to the STAR approach with ease, and avoid the risk of increased complications associated with the learning curves of other approaches.
