Low levels of mouse sperm chromatin fragmentation delay embryo development
Authors: Hieu Nguyen, Jordi Ribas-Maynou, Hongwen Wu, Beverly Quon, Tracy Inouye, Brienne Walker, Caitlin Langaman, Thomas T F Huang, W Steven Ward
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe previously demonstrated that MnCl2 induces double-stranded DNA breaks in sperm in a process that we term as sperm chromatin fragmentation. Here, we tested if the levels of double-stranded DNA breaks were corelated to the concentration of MnCl2, and we compared this to another agent that causes single-stranded DNA breaks, H2O2. We found that both methods have the advantage of inducing DNA breaks in a concentration-dependent manner. Mouse sperm were treated with varying concentrations of either H2O2 or MnCl2, and the DNA damage was assessed by pulse-field gel electrophoresis, and the alkaline and neutral comet assays. Oocytes were injected with either treated sperm and the resulting embryos analyzed with an embryoscope to detect subtle changes in embryonic development. We confirmed that H2O2 treatment induced primarily single-stranded DNA breaks and MnCl2 induced primarily double-stranded DNA breaks, indicating different mechanisms of damage. These sperm were injected into oocytes, and the development of the resulting embryos followed with an embryoscope equipped with time lapse recording. We found that aberrations in early embryonic development by day 2 with even the lowest levels of DNA damage and that the levels of embryonic aberrations correlated to the concentration of either H2O2 or MnCl2. Low levels of H2O2 caused significantly more aberrations in embryonic development than low levels of MnCl2 even though the levels of DNA damage as measured by comet assays were similar. These data demonstrate that even low levels of sperm DNA damage cause delays and arrests in embryonic development.

Introduction

Male infertility impacts 50% of infertile couples and the majority of male infertility cases are diagnosed after the infertile couple has sought care for infertility [1]. Sperm DNA fragmentation is a common cause of male infertility and can result in impaired fertilization, disrupted preimplantation of embryo development, miscarriage, and birth defects in the offspring [2]. Damaged sperm DNA may also affect DNA replication, transcription, and cause translation errors [3], all of which can affect the future generations or offspring of children who survive to birth and adulthood. Sperm with DNA damage often retain their ability to fertilize oocytes, and sperm with the lowest levels of DNA damage may allow embryos to develop with some aberrations. Sperm DNA damage may be caused by aborted apoptosis, errors in chromatin remodeling during spermiogenesis, oxygen stress during sperm transport through the seminiferous tubules, radiotherapy and chemotherapy, and environmental toxicants [4]. This DNA damage is unrepairable in mature sperm as these cells lack the necessary DNA repair mechanisms [5].

The DNA in somatic cells is packaged by histones into nucleosomes in which the DNA is still accessible to many nucleases and other DNA damaging agents and is vulnerable to mechanical stress. Sperm DNA is much more protected because it is packaged by protamines that condense the DNA into compact toroids with 25–50 kb of DNA that are inaccessible to nucleases and many DNA damaging agents [6, 7]. However, even this compact chromatin has some vulnerability. Several studies have shown that hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) treatment induces single-stranded DNA breaks (SSBs) in sperm DNA of many different mammalian species, including mice [8–11]. Sperm DNA is also susceptible to degradation by exogenous nucleases [9, 12], demonstrating that the compact, protected sperm chromatin has some relatively exposed DNA that nucleases can access. We have shown that sperm chromatin has foci at the regions between the protamine toroids, termed toroid linkers, that are accessible to nucleases. An endogenous nuclease is present in the luminal fluid of the vas deferens in the mouse that digests sperm chromatin at these linker regions when sperm were incubated with MnCl2, but no further, digesting the entire chromatin into toroid-sized fragments, 25–50 kb [13–15]. We term this process as sperm chromatin fragmentation (SCF). The breaks that occur in SCF are largely double-stranded DNA breaks (DSBs), not SSBs [16]. In our previous experiments designed to understand the mechanisms and functions of SCF, in vitro [13, 14, 17] and in vivo [16], we digested all or most of the sperm chromatin to toroid-sized fragments. However, such a complete genomic degradation does not approach the much more minor fragmentation seen in clinical samples where some degradation is visible, but most of the DNA remains intact by this analysis [18].

Several studies have shown that sperm DNA damage in patient samples correlates with decreased fertility [2, 19, 20]. These have roughly correlated the overall levels of sperm DNA damage in an individual sample with the clinical outcome of that patient’s assisted reproduction technology (ART) cycle. In such clinical samples, the individual sperm cells have a wide variety of DNA damage as assessed by tests that measure individual sperm cells, such as the comet [21] and terminal deoxynucleotide transferase UTP nick end labeling assays [22]. While it is difficult to detect the level of DNA damage in an individual sperm that is also used for ART, we sought to develop a correlation in sperm samples in which the levels of DNA damage were experimentally induced, and therefore similar in all sperm, and embryonic development.

In this report, we sought to induce DNA damage which we thought would be more consistent with the types of damage patients experience. We used low levels of H2O2 to induce single-stranded DNA breaks (SSBs) and low concentrations of MnCl2 to induce DSB by SCF [21]. At least one other group used a similar approach to define the susceptibility of sperm chromatin from different mammalian species using peroxide and exogenous DNase I [9], but this group did not test embryonic development. Sperm DNA damage was assessed by the comet assay, which can differentiate between SSB and DSB and by pulse-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) which detects only DSBs. Sperm with DNA damage were injected into oocytes and the embryos were analyzed for development. We used a time lapse video analysis of developing embryos to detect more subtle aberrations during embryogenesis to test how low levels of sperm DNA damage affect the process without interrupting cultivation in a stable environment [23]. The stable culture environment is necessary and important because changes in temperature and pH may affect the embryo development. With standard incubation, the incubator is opened every time when then embryo is checked, and this disturbs the gas concentration and temperature inside the incubation. The change of gas concentration and temperature affect the optimal growth medium environments for the embryo [24]. We found that even low levels of sperm DNA damage resulted in decreased embryonic development, and these were different for sperm with SSBs and DSBs.

Materials and methods

Animals

Eight-to-ten-week-old B6 (C57BL) mice were used. Mice were obtained from Jackson labs. Animal care and experimental protocols for handling and the treatment procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Hawai’i.

Preparation of sperm

Spermatozoa were obtained from B6 8–12-week-old males by a caudal epididymis excision after sacrificing by using tweezers. The fluid from the epididymis was suspended in commercially obtained global total media with 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) media (Cooper Surgical, USA) patterned after human tubal fluid media, as described in [25], and the spermatozoa were allowed to disperse in the medium at 37°C for an hour. Motile sperm were extracted from the top of the tube and were used for treatments. The sperm suspensions were treated with either H2O2 or MnCl2 by adding the reagents to the appropriate concentrations. The suspension was incubated for 30 min at 37°C. The sperm were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 2 min to remove MnCl2 or H2O2 and then the treated samples were used for intra-cytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) by mixing with 10% polyvinyl pyrrolidone (Catalog #90123, Irvine Scientific, CA). Control sperm were treated in the same way but without additional MnCl2 or H2O2. It is possible that not all the H2O2 or MnCl2 were removed by centrifugation, but we did not want to risk further damage of the sperm by repeating it. The amount of time between sperm treatment and ICSI was limited to 15 min for all experiments. Because the pellets were small, the small concentrations of H2O2 or MnCl2 that might contaminate the final suspension would have been diluted >100-fold.

Sperm motility

The epididymis was collected and suspended in Global total with HEPES media and was then incubated for 45 min to an hour at 37°C to allow the spermatozoa to disperse in the medium. The suspension with motile sperm was used for MnCl2 and H2O2 treatments (described in the Preparation of sperm in the methodology). After each treatment, 5 μL of each treated sperm sample was added on Cell-Wu slice and the sperm motility was visually inspected under a microscope.

Preparation of oocytes

Oocytes were isolated as described [22]. Briefly, mature 8–10-week-old B6 female mice were induced to super-ovulate with injections of 5 IU equine chorionic gonadotropin and 5 IU human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) given 48 h apart. Oviducts were removed 14–15 h after the hCG injections and were placed in mineral oil. The cumulus cells were released from the oviducts into 0.1% bovine testicular hyaluronidase/Chatot, Ziomek, Bavister medium [26] for 10 min to disperse the cumulus cells. We evaluated mature oocytes, metaphase II (MII), based on the appearance of the first polar body. The cumulus-free mature oocytes were washed and kept in global total (Lifeglobal Group, LLC, USA) at 37°C under 5% CO2 in air, for the first 24 h. These oocytes were used for both control and treated samples of ICSI.

Intracytoplasmic sperm injection

ICSI was performed as described [25]. Briefly, ICSI was performed using Eppendorf Micromanipulators (Micromanipulator TransferMan, Eppendorf, Germany) with a piezoelectric actuator (PMM Controller, model PMAS-CT150; Prime Tech, Tsukuba, Japan). Oocytes were used for ICSI within 2 h after isolation. For each experiment, all oocytes were injected within this time period. Time choice of which sperm treatment injected was randomized, and repetitions of the same concentrations were performed on different days to account for slight variations in the age of the mice used. This was accomplished by one person preparing the sperm and oocytes while the other person injected. A single sperm head was sucked from tail into the injection pipette and was moved back and forth to remove the sperm tail. The sperm head was separated from the midpiece by applying one or more piezo pulses. We transfer 5 MII oocytes into 20 μL drops prepared next to sperm drops and the drops were covered by mineral oil. Only one MII oocyte for each time was held to the holding pipette. Eggs were held with the metaphase spindle close to the 12 o’clock or 6 o’clock position. After ICSI, oocytes were cultured individually in embryo scope slides equipped with a time lapse recording system in slides containing 12 culture wells (12 × 25 μL medium) (Vitrolife, Inc., Sweden), as described below. The embryos were then transferred to the embryoscope (see SCF treatment and pulse-field gel electrophoresis section) where they were cultured under 5% CO2 and 5% O2 for the remaining time of the study. The development of each embryo was recorded for 96 h.

Comet assays

Control and treated samples of sperm were counted and the concentration was adjusted to a concentration of 5 × 105/mL. Next, we mixed 50 μL of control or treated samples with 100 μL 1.5% agarose, and 8 μL was poured on the top of an agarose-pretreated slide. Then, 8 mm coverslip was added, and the slide was incubated at 4°C for 1 min. Next, the coverslip was gently removed, and the slide was incubated with solution L1 (800mM DTT, 800 mM Tris-base, 1% SDS, pH 7.5) and then with solution L2 (800 mM dithiothreitol, DTT, 400 mM Tris-base, 50 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 2 mM NaCl, 1% Tween 20, pH 7.5) for 30 min/each at room temperature. For the neutral comet assay, the slide was washed in H2O for 2 min to remove the solution and was electrophoresed in 0.5× Tris borate EDTA (TBE) buffer for 30 V for 10 min. Then, the slide was incubated in 0.9% NaCl for 2 min, in neutralization solution (0.4 M Tris–HCl, pH 7.5) for 5 min, and finally in 100% ethanol for 2–5 min. For the alkaline comet assay, the slide was incubated in cold alkaline for 25 min and electrophoresis was performed in alkaline buffer (0.03 M NaOH, pH 13) at 30 V for 4 min. Then, the slide was washed in neutralization solution for 5 min and finally in 100% ethanol for 2–5 min. Both neutral and alkaline slides were dried on paper overnight. The next day, the slides were incubated with SYBR green solution 1× for 15 min and were imaged under fluorescent microscopy.

Imaging and comet images analysis

Imaging and comet images’ analysis were performed as described [15, 16]. Briefly, an Olympus BX51 epifluorescence microscope (Tokyo, Japan) equipped with the FITC filter was used to visualize comets. At least 100 sperm cells per sample were captured using the CellSens software (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) coupled to an Olympus DP80 Dual-Sensor camera. For each capture, exposure time was adapted to avoid overexposure of any comet region, and images were obtained in a 14-bit monochrome format with a resolution of 1916 × 912 pixels. Analysis of the comet images was conducted through the open-access CometScore v2.0 software (Rexhoover, http://rexhoover.com/cometscoredownload.php), which quantifies the fluorescence of the comet heads and tails. Images were automatically evaluated, adjusting the background intensity to correctly visualize the comets. However, after the automatic analysis, a manual review of each comet was conducted to remove overlapping comets to eliminate captures not corresponding to comets and to correct head and tail detection for misanalysed comets. If the final number of pictures was <50, more pictures were performed until the number of correctly analyzed sperm reached this figure.

Olive tail moment (OTM) was chosen as an indicator of the amount of DNA breaks present in a specific cell, as it has been shown to be the most informative parameter [27, 28]. The OTM is defined by the following formula: (tail mean intensity − head mean intensity) × Tail DNA/100. Tail DNA is defined as the percentage of DNA that moved to the comet tail after electrophoresis. After OTM was computed for all samples, the OTM obtained for each H2O2 and MnCl2 treatment was relativized to each control to obtain the fold-increase caused by the treatment on the DNA damage.

SCF treatment and pulse-field gel electrophoresis

The MnCl2- or H2O2- treated samples and pulse-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) were performed as described [16]. Briefly, epididymal sperm samples were collected in 25 mM Tris and 0.25% Tx-100. The sperm concentration for each experiment is around 20 million sperm. Sperm samples were added to MnCl2 with concentrations of 0.1, 0.5, 1, and 10 mM or to H2O2 with concentrations of 0, 0.3, 0.75, 1.5, 15 and 30 mM. The samples were incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Following this, 50 mM EDTA was added, and the mixture was incubated for further 30 min at 37°C. Control sperm were first added to 50 mM EDTA and were incubated an hour at 37°C without the addition of MnCl2 or H2O2. Then, the samples were mixed with 2% low melting point agarose 37–39°C at concentrations of 1:1, and we immediately pipetted the mixture into plugs. After the plugs were solidified at 4°C for 30 min, they were incubated in a digestion buffer (10 mM Tris HCl, 10 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl, 20 mM DTT, 2% SDS and 20 mg/mL proteinase K, pH 8.0) for an hour at 55°C. Afterward, plugs were placed into the wells of the 1% pulse field gel in 0.5× TBE, containing 1 μg/mL ethidium bromide, and were run on a contour-clamped homogeneous electric field apparatus (CHEF-DR III Variable Angle System, BioRad, Hercules, USA) in circulating 0.5× TBE buffer at 14°C, for 16 h. The pulse field protocol was set to the auto-alignment algorithm for a molecular weight range of 5–200 kb, which included an electrode angle change of 120° with an initial switch time of 1 s and the final switch time of 25 s. The Midrange PFG Marker (15–291 kb) (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, USA) was included in each gel for molecular weight reference. After electrophoresis was run, gel was imaged in a LAS-3000 gel imager (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan), avoiding overexposure (1 s of exposure time). The Image Studio Lite (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, USA) software was used to quantify the intensity of the DNA in each lane, dividing the lane into four regions, taking into account the DNA ladder. The percentages of fragmented DNA with fragments in four quadrants were recorded: (1) undamaged DNA in the well; (2) >97 kb in the gel; (3) between 15 and 145 kb, corresponding to the size condensed in a sperm toroid; and (4) <15 kb. The percentages of DNA with fragments in the four quadrants were recorded.

Timelapse embryoscope recording and annotating

A 12-well embryoscope slide was used with an incubator equipped a camera and timelapse record system (Vitrolife, Inc.) and each of the individual 2-cell embryos were cultured in a single well with 35 μL of global total media. The two-cell zygotes were cultured in this system for 4 days from the two-cell up to blastocysts. The embryos were continuously recorded by video for 72 h, and the recordings were subsequently reviewed to analyze the number of hours each embryo took to reach each stage. The morphokinetic data were collected including the time it took for each embryo to reach the 2-cell, 3-cell, 4-cell, 8-cell, morula, and blastocyst stages for all four groups of the experiments. To document the timing of when each embryo reached each stage, the videos were reviewed at the end of the experiment. For the two, three, four, and eight-cell stages, the embryos were marked as at the stage as soon as the appropriate number of cells were seen. Embryos were identified as morula as soon as the eighth cell in the eight-cell stage had transformed into a mass in which the cells could no longer be counted. Embryos were identified as reaching the blastocyst stage only when the presence of the blastocoel was easily detected.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses for comet assays were performed with GraphPad Prism 8 (Graphpad Inc.). Data were assessed using non-parametric tests. Correlation coefficients, Rs, were calculated for each comet assay graph using the Spearman’s test.

For comparisons of developmental stages of embryo cultures, multiple comparisons among MnCl2 and H2O2 treatments were conducted using a non-parametric ANOVA using the Friedman’s test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons tests as post hoc analysis. For considering statistically significant differences, P < 0.05 was taken as the threshold value.

Results

Motility of sperm after treatments

We first tested the motility of sperm after treating with both H2O2 and MnCl2. We found that even the lowest concentrations of H2O2 completely inhibited all motility of sperm (Supplementary Figure S1A). Treatment with MnCl2 reduced the motility in a dose-dependent manner, but even the highest concentration did not inhibit motility completely (Supplementary Figure S1B).

Analysis of sperm DNA damage parameters through PFGE and comet assays

We first analyzed DSBs induced by H2O2 or SCF by PFGE (Figure 1, Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). The PFGE can only detect DSBs as the DNA is not denatured. Figure 1A shows that, even at the highest concentrations of H2O2, there are no DSBs detected in these sperm by PFGE. The only DNA that is visible is in the plugs, which is too large to migrate into the gel. However, with SCF, there are increasingly more visible amounts of DSBs in sperm DNA as seen by the fragments in the 248–97 kb range. At the lowest concentrations of MnCl2 used for SCF, there is less DNA that enters the gel, indicating fewer DSBs than at the highest concentrations.

PFGE analysis of DSB. Motile sperm were treated with increasing concentrations of H2O2 (A) or MnCl2 (B) then prepared for PFGE as described.

The comet assay is a more sensitive method to detect DNA breaks, so we used this assay to measure DNA breaks with lower H2O2 and MnCl2 concentrations. Furthermore, the comet assay can detect both SSBs (alkaline comet assay) and DSBs (neutral comet assay and alkaline assay). We emphasized these differences by differing electrophoresis times. For the alkaline assay, we used only 4 min, while for the neutral assay, we used 10 min. This allowed the (usually) smaller DNA fragments released by SDBs to be more visible in the alkaline assay while not allowing most of the usually larger fragments released by DSBs to be seen in the alkaline assay because there was not enough time for them to migrate away from the sperm nucleus [17]. Moreover, by comparing the alkaline and neutral assays, we could get a strong approximation of SDBs versus DSBs. We found that for the concentrations and time of incubation we used, there was a clear correlation between the concentration of H2O2 and the fold increase in SSBs in sperm chromatin (Figure 2A, Supplementary Figure S2A). We detected no significant DSBs in the peroxide-treated sperm (Figure 2B, Supplementary Figure S2B).

Time lapse recording demonstrated little delay of the development of embryos injected with SCF-induced sperm

Sperm with DNA breaks induced by H2O2 or MnCl2 were injected into oocytes, and the resulting embryos were continuously analyzed by an embryoscope. Representative video images taken from one control, and embryos injected with sperm treated with different concentrations of H2O2 (Figure 4) and MnCl2 (Figure 5), are shown. For these studies, we analyzed 30–45 embryos for each concentration in three different experiments. By analyzing the video of the development for each embryo, we were able to calculate the time that it took each embryo to develop to each stage and what percentage of the embryos arrested at each stage. We found that for both H2O2 and MnCl2 treatments, the embryos all took similar times to develop to each stage (Figure 6). One exception was that for the penultimate concentration of H2O2, 7.5 mM, the embryos required 20 h longer to reach the blastocyst stage.

Discussion

In this study, we used two different methods to induce DNA damage in live sperm: treatment with H2O2 that induced primarily SSBs, and treatment with MnCl2 to induce primarily DSBs in sperm chromatin. We used much lower concentrations than have been previously reported in an attempt to induce lower levels of DNA breaks to determine more subtle effects on embryo development in vitro from lower levels of DNA damage that might more accurately reflect clinical specimens. Previous studies used 0–1 mM H2O2 treatment [9, 10, 29], similar to our work, but none followed the development to the blastocyst stage. Our previous work used 10 mM MnCl2 [30, 31], more than 10-fold the maximum concentration used in this study.

While it is possible that the embryonic effects we characterized after treatment with MnCl2 or H2O2 are the result of something other than DNA damage, we view this as unlikely. Even though the sperm were only washed once before injection, the volume that was injected into the oocyte was minute when compared to the total volume of the oocyte cytoplasm. While it is possible that the H2O2 treatment, in particular, could directly alter proteins, sperm can withstand relatively severe manipulation, such as sonication [32]. Our data clearly demonstrate a correlation between the concentrations of MnCl2 or H2O2 and DNA damage, which is the subject of this report.

In both methods, the number of DNA breaks were proportional to the concentrations of the reactive agent used to induce the breaks. The H2O2 is an agent that is well known to induce breaks in DNA, directly, and its small molecular size allows it to access even the compact sperm chromatin [11, 33]. While it is unclear whether H2O2 can penetrate the DNA that is buried within the protamine toroids, it is reasonable to hypothesize that it could cause breaks in the DNA that is on the outer surface of the protamine toroids. The induction of DSBs by MnCl2 through a mechanism we term SCF activates an endogenous nuclease in the lumen of the epididymis and vas deferens [16, 22] to cause DSBs in the toroid linker regions of the sperm chromatin. The SCF thus exploits the few in situ structures in compact sperm chromatin that remain accessible to nuclease digestion. Here, we modified our previous methods to test the hypothesis that by lowering the concentration of MnCl2 used to activate SCF a 100-fold (from 10 to 0.1 mM), we could induce very low levels of DSBs to determine how this would affect embryonic development. We followed embryonic development with constant video surveillance to assess the timing and developmental potential of each embryo. In general, we found that DNA damage caused embryos to arrest at early stages but that the time it took to develop to each stage was not affected to the same extent.

Motility after treatments

Sperm treated with H2O2 lost motility even at the lowest concentrations we used (Figure S1A). It is well documented that peroxide treatment not only causes SSBs in sperm chromatin but also affects causes lipid peroxidation which results in loss of motility [29, 34, 35]. Treatment with MnCl2 at the concentrations used also reduced motility, but not as much as with H2O2 (Figure S1B). This suggests that the induction of SCF is more directed at chromatin degradation.

S‌SB and DSB caused by H2O2 and SCF

The concentrations of H2O2 used in this study caused almost exclusively SSBs (Figure 2A). The unwinding buffer for the alkaline comet assay is harsh and causes some SSBs (note that the OTM for untreated controls, 0 mM H2O2 is about 55), so we compared changes in SSBs with treatments. The H2O2 treatment caused an increase in SSBs, but MnCl2-treated sperm had no changes in SSB. The DSBs were almost undetectable in embryos created with H2O2-treated sperm (Figure 2B), indicating that the aberrations in embryonic development resulted from SSBs, not DSBs. The DNA damage caused by MnCl2 was limited to DSBs, as no difference from controls was noted. There was no increase in SSB with increasing concentrations of MnCl2 (Figure 3A).

There was a clear increase in DSBs with increasing concentrations of MnCl2 (Supplementary Figure S3B). Previously, we had shown that 10 mM MnCl2 induced the degradation of the entire sperm genome to 25–50 kb fragments by cleaving the protamine toroid linkers, the most susceptible component of sperm chromatin [14, 36]. This study demonstrates that much fewer DSBs can be induced with lower concentrations of MnCl2 and that the levels of DSBs correlate with the concentration of MnCl2 used. Our data are consistent with H2O2 causing primarily SSBs and MnCl2 causing primarily DSBs and that both reagents act in a dose-dependent manner.

Effects of DNA breaks

Our data indicate that SSBs caused by H2O2 caused the most inhibition to embryonic development and that DSBs caused by SCF caused the least (compare Figure 7A and B). The highest concentrations of H2O2 resulted in none of the embryos reaching the blastocyst stage (Figure 7A), while the highest concentrations of MnCl2 reduced blastocyst formation by 80.0% (Figure 7B). These data are consistent with clinical data that suggest that DSBs cause defects in later embryonic development that result in spontaneous abortions [18]. One significant weakness of this work is that we have not yet determined the fate of the development of these embryos to full term, a project that we will address in future work.

These data have important clinical applications. Sperm chromatin is widely considered to be more resistant to degradation than somatic cells because of its high degree of compaction by protamines, and it is less resistant, for example, to nuclease digestion [13, 37] and to sonication [32] than the chromatin in most somatic cells. However, our work and others, have shown that sperm DNA is susceptible to both peroxide-induced breaks and to external nucleases even if the DNA is not digested completely. In this work, we have shown that the treatment of sperm with very small concentrations of peroxide can prevent embryonic development when these sperm are injected into untreated, healthy oocytes. Furthermore, while we previously demonstrated that 10 mM MnCl2 can induce endogenous nucleases in the vas deferens luminal fluid to cause severe DNA damage, in which the entire genome is reduced to 25–50 kb fragments, here, we demonstrated that 100-fold less MnCl2 also significantly reduces the percentage of embryos that develop to blastocyst. Taken together, our data indicate that minute quantities of peroxide or divalent cations can affect the DNA integrity in sperm.
