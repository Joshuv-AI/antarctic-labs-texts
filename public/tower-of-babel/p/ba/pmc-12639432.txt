Protocol for in vivo assessment of glucose metabolism in mouse models
Authors: Titaya Lerskiatiphanich, Cedric S Cui, John D Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryMetabolic disturbances are common in motor neuron disease (MND), and elucidating their mechanisms may reveal therapies. Here, we present a protocol to assess glucose homeostasis in mice, including glucose tolerance, insulin tolerance, and glucagon challenge tests. We describe steps for fasting, intraperitoneal injections, and serial blood glucose measurements. The protocol also includes plasma collection for catecholamine analysis using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization (MALDI) mass spectrometry imaging and immunofluorescence of pancreatic hormones, enabling comprehensive metabolic profiling in aged and neurodegenerative mouse models.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to McDonald et al.1 and McDonald et al.2Subject areas: Metabolism, Metabolomics, Neuroscience

Before you begin

This protocol provides detailed methods for assessing glucose homeostasis and related glucoregulatory hormones in a mouse model of MND. To characterize metabolic alterations throughout disease progression, assessments were conducted at two defined stages: disease onset, identified by the initial decline in hindlimb grip strength and mid-to-late progression, marked by pronounced grip strength deficits.3,4 The data presented here focuses on the late stage of MND, specifically at 80 weeks postnatal. In a separate MND model, comparable glycemic responses were observed between female and male SOD1G93A mice.2 However, for reproducibility, only male mice were included in this protocol due to more pronounced metabolic alterations in this group. It is recommended to incorporate both sexes in future studies to ensure comprehensive and inclusive research outcomes.5,6,7 This protocol is adaptable and can be applied to other disease models with distinct progression stages. All mice were housed on Purachip bedding under a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 06:00 and off at 18:00), with ad libitum access to food and water, unless specified otherwise.

Innovation

In addition to standardized metabolic tests, this protocol incorporates functional metabolic assessments with spatially resolved catecholamine profiling and endocrine analysis, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of glucose regulation than conventional approaches. Our protocol provides detailed step-by-step instructions, includes troubleshooting notes, and highlights critical steps. Optimized for aged and neurodegenerative mouse models, its modular design enhances applicability for both mechanistic and translational research across a range of disease contexts.

27.Tissue fixation and cryoprotection.a.Transfer the pancreas to 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer in a 12-well plate in a fume hood to prevent exposure to 4% paraformaldehyde. Fix at 4°C for 2 h.CRITICAL: Paraformaldehyde is toxic and potentially sensitizing chemical. All handling of 4% paraformaldehyde must be performed in a certified fume hood while wearing appropriate PPE, including gloves, lab coat, and eye protection. Waste must be disposed of in designated hazardous chemical containers in accordance with institutional safety protocols. Always consult the safety data sheet for proper handling, storage, and disposal procedures.Alternative: 5 mL screw-cap tube may be used in place of a 12-well plate. Use only in well-ventilated areas to prevent the risk of asphyxiation due to nitrogen gas displacement of oxygen. Expected outcomes

ipGTT

Blood glucose concentrations obtained during the ipGTT should be plotted as glucose levels versus time. Calculating the area under the curve (AUC) provides a quantitative measure of overall glucose excursion. Several AUC methods can be applied depending on the nature of the glucose response. (1) The total AUC measures the entire area under the glucose curve, regardless of baseline. (2) The incremental AUC includes both positive and negative areas relative to baseline values. (3) The positive incremental AUC includes only the area above baseline values. Lastly, (4) the inverse AUC is determined by subtracting baseline values from subsequent measurements. The suitability of each method depends on the specific characteristics of the glucose response being analyzed.

In our study, we observed that WT mice exhibited normal glucose, as reflected by a characteristic glucose curve (Figure 2A), consistent with previous reports. Interestingly, TDP-43Q331K mice demonstrated enhanced glucose tolerance, as evidenced by faster glucose clearance and a reduced total AUC compared to WT mice (Figure 2B). Insulin secretions during ipGTT can also be assessed by plotting insulin levels over time (Figure 2C) and calculating insulin AUC (Figure 2D), providing further insights into the mechanisms driving glucose regulation. These principles are applicable to other tests, including ipITT and glucagon challenge, as well as the measurement of associated glucoregulatory hormones.

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (ipGTT) in WT and TDP-43Q331K mice

(A) Blood glucose concentrations over time during ipGTT, illustrating glucose clearance dynamics.

(B) Total area under the curve (AUC) for blood glucose, quantifying overall glucose excursion. TDP-43Q331K mice exhibited enhanced glucose tolerance, with faster glucose clearance and reduced AUC compared to WT.

(C) Plasma insulin concentrations over time during ipGTT.

(D) Total insulin AUC, providing insight into insulin secretion patterns and their role in glucose regulation. Data are presented as mean ± SEM; n = 3–4 per group. Two-way ANOVA was used to assess genotype differences over time, and unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare total AUC. ∗p < 0.05.

ipITT

Following administration of insulin, blood glucose levels typically decline from the fasting baseline, with glucose nadir occurring approximately 30 min post-injection. This nadir can be served as a measure of insulin sensitivity, with higher nadir values indicating impaired insulin responsiveness. Additionally, the slope of glucose concentration changes from baseline to the nadir, can be used to assess insulin sensitivity. However, this parameter was not applicable in the current study. Both TDP-43Q331K and WT mice failed to exhibit a significant decline in glucose levels following insulin injection, therefore, only glucose nadir values were analyzed (Figure 3B). Insulin resistant mice typically maintain elevated blood glucose levels throughout the test. However, in our study, both TDP-43Q331K and WT mice showed similar glucose profiles, indicating no marked differences in insulin responsiveness (Figure 3A). Additionally, glucagon secretion during ipITT can be quantified using ELISA, providing further insight into counter-regulatory hormone dynamics.

Intraperitoneal insulin tolerance (ipITT) test in WT and TDP-43Q331K mice

(A) Blood glucose concentrations over time during ipITT.

(B) Glucose nadir values, representing the lowest recorded glucose concentration post-injection, used as an indicator of insulin sensitivity. Both WT and TDP-43Q331K mice exhibited similar glucose profiles and comparable nadir values, suggesting no significant differences in insulin responsiveness. Data are presented as mean ± SEM; n = 3 per group. Two-way ANOVA was used to assess genotype differences over time, and unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare glucose nadir values.

Glucagon challenge

Blood glucose levels following glucagon administration should be plotted over time to assess glucagon tolerance. A typical response involves a rapid increase in glucose due to glycogenolysis. Both TDP-43Q331K and WT mice showed similar glucose profiles and AUC values (Figures 4A and 4B). A reduced glycemic response or lower AUC may indicate impaired glucagon sensitivity or disrupted signaling.

Intraperitoneal glucagon challenge test in WT and TDP-43Q331K mice

(A) Blood glucose concentrations over time following glucagon administration, reflecting hepatic glycogenolysis and glucagon responsiveness.

(B) Total area under the curve (AUC) for blood glucose, quantifying the overall glycemic response. Both WT and TDP-43Q331K mice exhibited similar glucose profiles and AUC values, indicating no significant differences in glucagon sensitivity. Data are presented as mean ± SEM; n = 4 per group. Two-way ANOVA was used to assess genotype differences over time, and unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare total AUCs.

MALDI imaging of plasma catecholamines

The MALDI workflow is expected to generate high-quality, reproducible spatial maps of epinephrine and norepinephrine from 0.5 μL plasma droplets. Following derivatization with FMP-10, dominant ions should appear at m/z 451.2016 for epinephrine (Figure 5A), and 437.1860 for norepinephrine (Figure 5C). These peaks can be semi-quantified using RMS-normalized average intensities (Figures 5B–5D). MALDI-TIMS-TOF imaging at 50 μm resolution should produce uniform, high-contrast ion maps, with blank regions showing no signal. Lock-mass correction using FMP-10 (m/z 288.1188) is anticipated to maintain mass accuracy within ± 5 ppm. Duplicate measurements from all biological replicates are expected to show less than 15% coefficient of variation, confirming sensitive detection and enabling publication-quality data and images.

(B and D) RMS-normalized average ion intensities for epinephrine and norepinephrine, comparing relative plasma catecholamine levels between WT and TDP-43Q331K mice. Lock-mass correction using FMP-10 (m/z 288.1188) maintained mass accuracy within ± 5 ppm. Duplicate measurements from biological replicates showed < 15% coefficient of variation, confirming reproducibility and sensitivity of detection. Data are presented as mean ± SEM; n = 5 per group. unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare RMS-normalized ion intensities.

Immunofluorescence analysis of pancreatic islets

Immunofluorescence staining of pancreatic sections provides structural insights into α-cell and β-cell populations (Figure 6A). Comparative analysis of the immunoreactive areas for insulin (Figure 6B) and glucagon (Figure 6C) between TDP-43Q331K and WT mice revealed no significant differences in islet morphology or size. These findings suggest that reduced circulating glucagon in TDP-43Q331K mice is not due to structural deficits in α-cells.

Immunofluorescence analysis of pancreatic islet hormones in WT and TDP-43Q331K mice

(A) Representative immunofluorescence images of pancreatic sections stained for nuclei (blue), insulin (green), and glucagon (red), highlighting β-cell and α-cell populations within the islets. Scale Bar = 100 μm.

(B and C) Quantification of the average immunoreactive area for insulin-positive β-cells and glucagon-positive α-cells, respectively. No significant differences were observed between WT and TDP-43Q331K mice, suggesting that reduced circulating glucagon in TDP-43Q331K mice is not attributable to structural deficits in α-cell populations. Data are presented as mean ± SEM; n = 4 per group. unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare immunoreactive areas.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± SEM. For time-course measurements of blood glucose and hormone levels during ipGTT, ipITT and glucagon challenge tests, two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni multiple comparisons were used. Bar graphs were analyzed using Student’s t-test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.1 and differences were deemed statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Limitations

While this protocol provides a robust approach to assess systemic glucose homeostasis and related glucoregulatory hormones in mice, it does not identify the precise mechanisms underlying the observed changes. Additional experiments such as ex vivo islet function assays and in vivo glucose uptake test may be necessary to further elucidate the pathways involved and to validate the physiological relevance of the findings.

Stress can significantly elevate glucose levels and affect test outcomes (step 1 of ipGTT, step 6 of ipITT, step 11 of glucagon challenge).

The glucometer may not be able to read extremely high glucose levels (step 3 of ipGTT, step 8 of ipITT, step 13 of glucagon challenge).

Potential solution

Problem 3: Hemolyzed (red) plasma after centrifugation

Hemolysis can interfere with downstream analyses.

Potential solution

•Gently stroke the tail to promote bleeding instead of applying excessive pressure (step 3 of ipGTT, step 8 of ipITT, step 13 of glucagon challenge).

Gently stroke the tail to promote bleeding instead of applying excessive pressure (step 3 of ipGTT, step 8 of ipITT, step 13 of glucagon challenge).

Process and centrifuge blood samples promptly after collection (step 5 of ipGTT, step 10 of ipITT, step 15 of plasma epinephrine and norepinephrine analysis).

Problem 4: Insufficient insulin dose to assess glycemic response

A low insulin dose may not produce a measurable glucose reduction (step 8 of ipITT).

Potential solution

Optimize the insulin dose based on the insulin sensitivity of the mice (typically 0.5 IU/kg – 2.0 IU/kg body weight).

Ensure the nebulizing nitrogen supply is connected and activated.
