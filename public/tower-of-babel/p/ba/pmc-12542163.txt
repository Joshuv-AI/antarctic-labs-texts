Macrolide-resistant Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in Chinese children: a retrospective study of clinical features and prognosis
Authors: Yu Zheng, Guitao Li, Hongchen Dai, Ying Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study systematically investigated the clinical features, imaging findings, and treatment outcomes associated with macrolide-resistant Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MRMPP) in children, aiming to support early clinical intervention and prevent disease progression.MethodsClinical data from 346 children with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) who underwent bronchoscopic treatment between May 2023 and April 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. Patients were stratified into a macrolide-resistant group (MRG) (n = 281) and a macrolide-sensitive group (MSG) (n = 65). Comparative analyses included demographics, clinical symptoms, laboratory findings, treatment protocols, bronchoscopic findings, pathogen load, and prognoses.ResultsAmong the 346 cases of MPP, 81.2% (281/346) were classified as MRMPP. Resistant strains predominated in autumn and winter (P < 0.05), with no significant differences in sex, age, or body mass index between groups (P > 0.05). The MRG showed significantly longer durations of fever, cough, abnormal lung sounds, macrolide and glucocorticoid use, and hospital stay and incurred higher hospitalization costs (all P < 0.05). Rates of oxygen therapy, ≥ 2 bronchoscopic interventions, second-line antibiotic use, and extrapulmonary complications were also higher in the MRG, while co-infections were more common in the MSG (P < 0.05). The MRG exhibited elevated neutrophil percentages and C-reactive protein, lactate dehydrogenase, and D-dimer levels (all P < 0.05). Pulmonary consolidation and bronchoscopic mucus plugs were more frequent in the MRG, while thin white secretions were typical of the MSG (P < 0.05). No significant difference in adverse prognosis was observed between groups (6.8% vs. 3.0%, P = 0.74). Multivariate logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic curve analyses identified mucus plug formation (odds ratio = 35.619, P = 0.001) and fever duration of > 6.5 days (area under the curve = 0.794, P < 0.001) as independent risk factors for adverse prognosis.ConclusionMRMPP is associated with a prolonged disease course, more complex clinical features, and greater therapeutic challenges. However, macrolide resistance gene mutations alone do not predict adverse outcomes. Special attention should be given to patients with mucus plug formation and fever lasting > 6.5 days because they are at greater risk of unfavorable prognosis and may benefit from early intervention.

Introduction

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is a leading cause of community-acquired pneumonia in children [1]. In recent years, infections have trended toward younger age groups, alongside a rising proportion of macrolide-resistant MP (MRMP). These shifts have contributed to a growing number of pediatric cases of severe and refractory MP pneumonia (MPP), which are more likely to result in adverse sequelae [2, 3]. A clear understanding of the clinical and imaging features of macrolide-resistant MPP (MRMPP) is essential for guiding treatment decisions and reducing the risk of poor outcomes. This study retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 346 children diagnosed with MPP and treated with fiberoptic bronchoscopic lavage. The overall goal was to identify the clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, and prognosis of MRMPP, thereby providing a reference for its diagnosis and management.

Materials and methods

General information

Children diagnosed with MPP and admitted to Fu Yang People’s Hospital between 1 May 2023 and 30 April 2024 were included in this retrospective study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (https://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/strobe/) and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Fu Yang People’s Hospital (Ethics Approval Number: 2024 − 240). Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all pediatric patients who underwent fiberoptic bronchoscopy.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were an age between 28 days and 18 years and having met the diagnostic criteria for MPP as defined in the Guidelines for the Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Macrolide-Resistant Mycoplasma pneumoniae Pneumonia in Children [4]. Specifically:

The exclusion criteria were underlying diseases (e.g., immunodeficiencies, chronic lung diseases, hematological disorders, or neurologic disease), incomplete clinical data, and loss to follow-up.

Management strategy

All hospitalized children with confirmed MPP received macrolide antibiotics as anti-infective treatment, along with general symptomatic and supportive therapies such as nebulization, chest percussion, sputum suction, and fluid replacement. For children aged ≥ 3 years, azithromycin was the first-line agent, administered intravenously at 10 mg/kg/day once daily. The initial and total treatment durations were determined by disease severity. Typically, the first course lasted 3–5 days, followed by a second course after a 3- to 4-day interval, with a total of two to three courses. For children aged < 3 years, erythromycin was used as the first-line therapy at 30 mg/kg/day, given twice daily via intravenous infusion for 10–14 days. If fever, cough, or wheezing persisted or worsened after a 3- to 5-day course of macrolide therapy, switching to second-line antibiotics was considered. Alternative agents included doxycycline (a tetracycline-class antibiotic) and moxifloxacin (a fluoroquinolone), selected after a thorough risk-benefit assessment and with written informed consent from guardians. For children aged ≥ 8 years, doxycycline was given intravenously or orally at 2 mg/kg per dose, twice daily, for 10 days. For those aged < 8 years, moxifloxacin was administered intravenously at 10 mg/kg per dose, once daily, for 7–14 days. In cases where BALF pathogen testing indicated bacterial co-infection, combination therapy was adjusted based on antimicrobial susceptibility profiles. For severe and critically ill children, glucocorticoids (methylprednisolone) were initiated at 2 mg/kg/day, with the dosage adjusted to 4–6 mg/kg/day based on clinical symptoms, pulmonary imaging progression, and inflammatory markers [e.g., C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin]; the total course did not exceed 14 days. For patients with D-dimer (D-D) levels exceeding five times the upper limit of normal, prophylactic anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin was administered subcutaneously at 50–100 U/kg per dose, twice daily, for 7–14 days. In children with marked pulmonary consolidation, suspected mucoid impaction, or plastic bronchitis, therapeutic fiberoptic bronchoscopy was strongly recommended to relieve airway obstruction. All treatment regimens followed the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Mycoplasma pneumoniae Pneumonia in Children (2023 Edition), available on the Chinese Health Commission website (https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2023-02/16/content_5741770.htm).

Bronchoalveolar lavage via fiberoptic bronchoscopy and pathogen detection

An appropriate bronchoscope model (PENTAX; outer diameter: 2.8 mm, 3.6 mm, or 4.9 mm) was selected based on the child’s age and weight. All procedures were performed under topical anesthesia combined with conscious sedation. Targeted lavage was conducted on the most severely affected bronchopulmonary segment or lobe. The lavage volume was determined by body weight: for patients weighing < 20 kg, a total of 3 mL/kg was used, divided into three equal aliquots for sequential instillation; for those weighing ≥ 20 kg, each aliquot was 20 mL, with the total volume not exceeding 3 mL/kg. After negative-pressure suction, 5–10 mL of BALF was collected into a sterile container, immediately refrigerated at 2–8 °C, and transported to the laboratory for analysis [5]. The testing protocol employed multiplex targeted amplification combined with high-throughput sequencing. More than 100 common pediatric respiratory pathogens—including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and MP—were targeted. Pathogen-specific primers were designed for conserved genomic regions, followed by multiplex PCR amplification and hybridization capture. The amplified products were sequenced on a tNGS platform (Model: MiniSeq). Sequencing data were filtered using bioinformatics software and compared to reference genomes to interpret pathogen profiles and detect mutation sites in the MP 23 S rRNA resistance genes (A2063G, A2064G, A2067G, C2017G). The assay included internal controls through comparison with human DNA data.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was an adverse prognosis, including necrotizing pneumonia, bronchiolitis obliterans, and bronchiectasis. All pediatric patients were followed up for 6 months after discharge, with chest radiographs or computed tomography scans performed every 2 months. The secondary outcomes were clinical features, laboratory test results, imaging findings, and bronchoscopy observations.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 25.0 software. Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared between groups using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous data are presented as median and interquartile range (M [P25, P75]), with group comparisons analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described as frequencies (%), and differences were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify risk factors for adverse prognosis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to determine the cutoff values of each index. Two-sided P-values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

In total, 346 pediatric patients with MP infection confirmed by tNGS of BALF were enrolled, including 182 males and 164 females, yielding a male-to-female ratio of 1.11:1.00. The patients were divided into two groups: the macrolide-sensitive group (MSG, n = 65), consisting of 34 males and 31 females (mean age: 6.07 ± 2.94 years; mean BMI: 16.50 ± 2.76 kg/m2), and the macrolide-resistant group (MRG, n = 281), consisting of 148 males and 133 females (mean age: 6.63 ± 2.58 years; BMI: 16.45 ± 3.16 kg/m2). A total of 281 macrolide-resistant strains were detected, corresponding to a resistance rate of 81.2% (281/346). Resistant strains predominantly circulated during autumn and winter, accounting for 72.6% (204/281) of resistant cases, while non-resistant strains were sporadically distributed throughout the year. A statistically significant difference in seasonal distribution was observed between the two groups (P < 0.05). However, no significant differences were found in the sex ratio, age, or BMI (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Clinical data

In comparing clinical characteristics between the two groups, children in the MRG exhibited significantly longer fever duration, delayed resolution of cough and abnormal lung sounds, prolonged macrolide antibiotic treatment, extended glucocorticoid use, longer hospital stays, and higher hospitalization costs. The MRG also had higher rates of oxygen therapy, multiple bronchoscopic interventions (≥ 2 sessions), second-line antibiotic use, and extrapulmonary complications. By contrast, the MSG showed a higher proportion of co-infections with other pathogens, a difference that was statistically significant (P < 0.05). Among the 42 co-infected cases in the MSG, viral infections were the most common (25 cases, 54.3%), followed by bacterial and fungal infections. The most frequent extrapulmonary complications included rash (19 cases), hepatic impairment (18 cases), and myocardial injury (14 cases). Less common complications included leukopenia (3 cases), thrombocytopenia (2 cases), myositis (1 case), and neuritis (1 case). No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups in peak body temperature, presence of abnormal pulmonary signs, or timing of bronchoscopic interventions during the disease course (P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Fiberoptic bronchoscopy findings

Comparison of fiberoptic bronchoscopic findings between the two groups revealed that the MRG had a significantly higher incidence of mucus plug formation, while the MSG predominantly showed thin white secretions—a difference that was statistically significant (P < 0.05). Adverse prognostic outcomes

The primary adverse outcomes in both groups were necrotizing pneumonia, bronchiectasis, and bronchiolitis obliterans. In the MRG, 19 patients experienced adverse outcomes, including 8 with necrotizing pneumonia, 4 with bronchiectasis, and 2 with bronchiolitis obliterans. By contrast, the MSG had only two patients with adverse outcomes: one with necrotizing pneumonia and one with bronchiectasis. However, no statistically significant difference was found in the overall incidence of adverse outcomes between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Table 6).

Analysis of risk factors for adverse prognosis

Based on prognostic evaluation, the 346 children were divided into an adverse prognosis group (n = 21) and a favorable prognosis group (n = 325). Discussion

MPP is one of the most common types of pneumonia in children. In earlier clinical practice, most patients achieved clinical cure after about 1 week of standard macrolide antibiotic therapy (e.g., azithromycin). However, in recent years, the rising incidence of MP infections has coincided with growing challenges in MPP management. Studies have shown that this trend is closely related to the emergence and spread of MRMP [6]. Macrolide resistance rates vary significantly by region. A meta-analysis reported the highest rates in East Asia (China, Japan, and South Korea), reaching 70%–90%, followed by the Western Pacific at 67.3%. By contrast, resistance rates in Europe, the Americas, and the Mediterranean regions remain much lower, typically ranging from 1% to 20% [7]. In this study, the macrolide resistance rate in Fuyang was 81.2%, with resistant strains predominantly circulating in autumn and winter. This seasonal pattern differs from previous findings; for example, studies in Beijing [8] and Ningbo [9] reported peak resistance rates in summer and autumn. These differences may reflect regional epidemiological variations and differing study periods, including sampling duration and climate influences. The primary mechanisms underlying MP resistance to macrolides are the widespread use of these antibiotics in pediatric respiratory infections and ongoing genetic mutations in the pathogen [10]. The most common resistance-associated mutations are A2063G and A2064G, with A2063G being the predominant variant globally [4]. Early diagnosis of MP infection and timely recognition of resistance patterns are essential for optimizing treatment strategies and improving clinical outcomes in the management of MPP.

Children in the resistant group exhibited slower resolution of clinical symptoms, a higher incidence of extrapulmonary complications, and longer, more complex treatment courses than did children in the non-resistant group. In MRMP infections, common features included prolonged fever and cough, delayed resolution of abnormal lung sounds, increased need for oxygen therapy, extended durations of antibiotic and anti-inflammatory treatment, and a higher likelihood of requiring second-line antibiotics. These factors collectively contributed to longer hospital stays and significantly higher hospitalization costs. A contemporaneous study by He et al. [11] in China similarly reported prolonged hospitalization and extended glucocorticoid use in children with MRMPP, aligning with our findings. However, unlike our study, He et al. [11] found no direct correlation between macrolide resistance genes and the resolution of clinical symptoms such as cough, fever, and abnormal lung sounds. These discrepancies may be explained by differences in patient selection criteria and study duration. Despite some divergence, most domestic and international studies agree that MRMP is associated with more severe imaging findings and worsened clinical manifestations. Moreover, macrolide resistance is widely recognized as a key contributor to refractory MPP [12–15]. The relationship between macrolide resistance genes and clinical inefficacy of macrolide therapy remains controversial. In our study, only 41 of the 281 patients with MRMPP (14.6%) required switching to second-line antibiotics. Notably, despite confirmed genetic resistance, continued macrolide therapy achieved clinical success in the majority of cases (85.4%), suggesting a potential discordance between genotypic resistance and phenotypic treatment outcomes. These discrepancies may be attributed to the immunomodulatory effects of azithromycin and the role of glucocorticoids. Azithromycin, a distinctive antibiotic, not only exhibits antimicrobial activity but also exerts notable immunomodulatory effects. These include promoting macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, enhancing macrophage phagocytic capacity, inducing neutrophil apoptosis, facilitating neutrophil degranulation, inhibiting oxidative stress, suppressing neutrophil extracellular trap formation, inducing dendritic cell polarization toward the immature phenotype, inhibiting T-lymphocyte proliferation and activation, and reducing cytokine secretion by lymphocytes. The critical role of glucocorticoids also warrants emphasis. Because MP lacks a cell wall, it directly damages lung tissue while simultaneously provoking an excessive inflammatory response. Through their potent anti-inflammatory action, glucocorticoids have been shown to improve clinical outcomes in MPP by mitigating this hyperinflammatory state and curbing disease progression [16, 17]. In the resistant group, we observed a significantly higher rate of glucocorticoid use (92.5% vs. 72.3%) and longer duration of therapy (5.93 ± 4.32 vs. 3.20 ± 2.62 days). Although MPP is generally a self-limiting disease, both groups achieved favorable treatment outcomes without severe complications. Adverse outcomes occurred in fewer than 10% of patients, primarily as necrotizing pneumonia and bronchiectasis, with no statistically significant difference in overall prognosis between the two groups. In summary, while MRMP may complicate treatment and prolong the disease course, our findings suggest that its impact on prognosis does not appear sufficient to significantly affect overall clinical outcomes.

Children in the resistant group exhibited higher inflammatory markers and a greater propensity for hypercoagulable states. MRMP strains not only reduce the effectiveness of macrolide antibiotics but also evade host immune responses, thereby sustaining excessive inflammatory activity during infection. Upon invasion, MRMP stimulates the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-6, which amplify both local and systemic inflammation and may contribute to systemic complications. This heightened inflammatory response is reflected in laboratory findings, with significantly elevated levels of CRP, LDH, and D-D observed in the resistant group compared with the non-resistant group [18, 19]. The worsening inflammatory status is often accompanied by abnormal activation of the coagulation system. Inflammatory mediators drive this hypercoagulable state by enhancing the synthesis of coagulation factors and suppressing natural anticoagulant pathways, ultimately leading to dysregulated clotting. In children with MRMP infections, this creates a predisposition to thromboembolic complications such as deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, further complicating clinical management and requiring close monitoring. In this study, CRP, LDH, and D-D levels were significantly higher in the macrolide-resistant group (P < 0.05). Prophylactic anticoagulation therapy was administered to 22 patients (7.8%) in the resistant group, while none in the non-resistant group required such treatment. These findings align with prior studies by Zhang et al. and Zhan et al. [20, 21]. Notably, Zhang et al. also identified CRP as an independent risk factor for multidrug resistance-associated gene mutations in pediatric patients.

Children in the macrolide-resistant group exhibited a significantly higher incidence of pulmonary consolidation and mucus plug formation than did children in the non-resistant group (P < 0.05), whereas the non-resistant group primarily showed thin white secretions on bronchoscopy. Pulmonary consolidation is generally associated with infection severity and the extent of lung involvement. A heightened immune response may aggravate airway mucosal injury, leading to increased secretion production and mucoid impaction, thereby raising the risk of airway obstruction [22]. The elevated rate of mucus plug formation in the resistant group highlights the extent of airway obstruction and impaired ventilation caused by MPP. In the non-resistant group, bronchoscopy typically revealed thin white secretions, indicating a milder infection with limited pathological changes. By contrast, children in the resistant group frequently exhibited thick, viscous secretions, reflecting more severe airway blockage. In advanced cases, this obstruction may progress to atelectasis, significantly compromising respiratory function. Consistent with this pathophysiology, we observed that children in the resistant group had a significantly higher incidence of requiring multiple bronchoscopic interventions (9.8% vs. 18.1%, P < 0.05). These repeat procedures were primarily performed to remove tenacious secretions and lavage inflammatory exudates within the affected bronchi, thereby improving ventilation, reducing localized inflammation, and accelerating pulmonary recovery. Multivariate analysis further identified mucus plug formation as an independent risk factor for unfavorable prognosis.

This study had two main limitations. First, it employed a single-center design, with all samples collected from Fuyang, Anhui Province. This may limit the generalizability of the epidemiological findings because MP resistance patterns can vary by region. Second, selection bias was present. One of the inclusion criteria was the completion of fiberoptic bronchoscopy. Some children—because of milder symptoms or guardian refusal—did not undergo the procedure and were therefore excluded, as accurate drug resistance gene data could not be obtained. This exclusion may have introduced bias, potentially inflating the reported resistance rate relative to the broader pediatric population.

Conclusion

Children with MRMPP exhibited more severe clinical symptoms, more complex radiological and bronchoscopic findings, and greater therapeutic challenges. However, our study found that macrolide resistance did not significantly affect the incidence of adverse outcomes. Pediatric patients presenting with mucus plug formation and a fever duration exceeding 6.5 days require close monitoring because they are more likely to experience unfavorable prognoses. In such cases, although macrolides remain the first-line treatment, clinicians should consider early escalation to second-line antibiotics when these warning signs are present. Timely implementation of effective treatment strategies can shorten hospital stays, reduce healthcare costs, and help alleviate the broader socioeconomic burden—ultimately protecting the physical and mental health of affected children.
