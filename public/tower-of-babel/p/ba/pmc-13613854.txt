PTGIS inhibited osteogenic and promoted angiogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells via endoplasmic reticulum stress mediated PI3K/AKT signaling
Authors: Han Bao, Jiaohong Wang, Haoqing Yang, Huina Liu, Xiao Han, Zhipeng Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) play a key role in ectopic mineralization. Effective treatment requires both inhibiting pathological calcification and promoting vessel growth. While prostacyclin synthase (PTGIS) is critical for vascular health, its role in regulating osteogenic and angiogenic differentiation in MSCs remains unclear.MethodsFollowing PTGIS modulation in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), osteogenesis was assessed by alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, Alizarin Red staining (ARS), and hydroxyapatite/tricalcium phosphate (HA/TCP) particles implantation. Angiogenesis was evaluated via a co-culture system of BMSCs and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Mechanistic studies involved Ca²⁺ dynamics, western blotting, and pharmacological inhibition.ResultsKnockdown of PTGIS enhanced ALP activity, mineralization, the expression of the osteogenic marker DMP1/BSP, and bone-like tissue formation both in vitro and in vivo. Conversely, PTGIS knockdown reduced tubule formation in vitro and impaired blood vessel formation, and the expression of the angiogenic marker CD31 in vivo. Mechanistically, the overexpression of PTGIS promoted Ca²⁺ efflux, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) dilation and vacuolization, increased the levels of the ER stress markers BIP/GRP75, and elevated PI3K/AKT phosphorylation. The inhibition of either ER stress or AKT activity restored osteogenic capacity by enhancing ALP activity and mineralization, and suppressed angiogenic capacity by inhibiting tubule formation in BMSCs.ConclusionPTGIS inhibited osteogenic and promoted angiogenic differentiation of BMSCs through ER stress mediated PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. This PTGIS-dependent regulatory pathway offers new insight into MSC fate determination and represents a potential therapeutic strategy for treating ectopic mineralization disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05144-9.Keywords: PTGIS, MSCs, Osteogenesis, Angiogenesis, ER stress

Introduction

Ectopic mineralization, also referred to as pathological mineralization, represents a prevalent pathological process characterized by the aberrant deposition of calcium-phosphate crystals in various soft tissues [1]. It is closely associated with aging and a spectrum of major chronic diseases, such as chronic kidney disease, articular cartilage degeneration, and cardiovascular disorders [2–5]. With its high global prevalence and involvement of multiple organ systems, ectopic mineralization poses a significant clinical burden [6]. The core mechanism driving its pathogenesis lies in the disruption of systemic and local mineralization homeostasis, which is prominently manifested as increased pathological calcification at lesion sites with insufficient angiogenesis to support tissue repair [7]. Current therapeutic strategies, however, such as surgical excision or bisphosphonate drugs, primarily target a single aspect of the problem, either removing existing calcifications or inhibiting further mineralization [8]. These approaches often fail to address the dual challenge of simultaneously inhibiting abnormal mineralization and promoting vascularized repair [9], and may even interfere with normal bone metabolism. Consequently, identifying a new approach regulating this balance is a promising direction for overcoming current limitations.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have garnered significant attention in the field of tissue regeneration due to their multilineage differentiation potential and paracrine signaling capacity [10, 11]. Among them, bone marrow‑derived MSCs (BMSCs) have been increasingly recognized as key players in the pathogenesis of ectopic mineralization, owing to their dual potential to differentiate along osteogenic and angiogenic lineages. For instance, BMSCs derived from patients with ankylosing spondylitis exhibit excessive osteogenic differentiation, and the upregulated integrin‑linked kinase (ILK) promotes aberrant mineralization via the Akt/GSK‑3β/β‑catenin pathway, thereby driving heterotopic ossification [12]. Moreover, in a post‑traumatic elbow heterotopic ossification model, inhibition of the TGF‑β/pSmad2/3 axis reduces M2 macrophage‑mediated BMSC migration and alleviates ectopic bone formation [13]. These findings highlight key pathogenic mechanisms of BMSCs in ectopic mineralization disorders and suggest potential intervention strategies targeting BMSCs. The pathological essence of ectopic mineralization diseases lies in an imbalance between aberrant mineral deposition and insufficient reparative angiogenesis. When BMSCs differentiate toward an osteogenic lineage, they can exacerbate pathological vascular calcification in the diseased microenvironment [14]. Conversely, their ability to support endothelial function and promote new blood vessel formation through paracrine mechanisms may offer therapeutic advantages for improving local blood supply [15]. Therefore, identifying core molecular switches governing BMSC fate choice between osteogenesis and angiogenesis is essential for developing mechanism‑based therapies for ectopic mineralization.

Our preliminary transcriptomic sequencing analysis revealed that prostacyclin synthase (PTGIS) was found to be significantly upregulated in BMSCs undergoing osteogenic induction compared with control BMSCs [16]. This finding suggested PTGIS may be involved in osteogenic regulation. During skeletal development, PTGIS catalyzes the conversion of prostaglandin H₂ (PGH₂) into prostacyclin (PGI2). The resulting PGI2 stimulates the proliferation of growth plate chondrocytes, thereby supporting normal bone formation [17]. PTGIS is highly enriched in the bone marrow endosteal niche where BMSCs reside [18], and its expression in BMSCs has been confirmed, but its functional role in osteogenic differentiation remains unexplored. Notably, PGI₂‑deficient mice exhibit increased bone mass due to accelerated bone turnover, suggesting that the PTGIS/PGI₂ pathway negatively regulates bone formation [19]. Beyond bone development, PTGIS also plays a crucial role in vascular homeostasis. Certain PTGIS variants that impair its function have been identified as new causes of idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension; such dysfunction compromises EC integrity and is linked to vascular pathologies [20]. As an endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane-bound enzyme from the cytochrome P450 family, PTGIS promotes blood vessel dilation and inhibits platelet aggregation [21, 22]. Considering its ER localization and its established functions in both bone formation and vascular health, we hypothesized that PTGIS might regulate the balance of osteogenic and angiogenic differentiation in BMSCs through ER associated mechanisms.

ER stress represents a state of disrupted ER homeostasis and is a recognized driver of ectopic mineralization. Sustained ER stress activates the unfolded protein response (UPR), which in turn upregulates key osteogenic transcription factors and promotes pathological calcium deposition [23, 24]. Disruption of ER calcium homeostasis further contributes to this process. This disturbance not only induces ER stress but also leads to cytoplasmic calcium overload and subsequent MSC injury [25, 26]. Conversely, inhibiting pathological ER stress has been shown to protect EC function. This protection helps maintain their ability to proliferate and form new blood vessels [27]. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway plays a pivotal, context-dependent role in modulating cellular responses under ER stress. In the skeletal system, ER stress induction upregulates PI3K/AKT phosphorylation in chondrocytes [28]. Within the vascular system, ER stress impairs EC-dependent vasodilation through PI3K/AKT pathway [29]. Interestingly, in conditions such as colorectal adenocarcinoma, bioinformatic analyses have identified PTGIS as a key gene associated with ER stress [30]. Another study has shown that PTGIS overexpression can protect insulin-producing cells from ER stress-induced damage [31]. However, the specific underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Moreover, research has shown that its enzymatic product PGI2 promotes EC proliferation via the PI3K/AKT pathway [32]. Despite these associations, it remains unclear whether PTGIS can functionally connect ER stress to the PI3K/AKT pathway by perturbing calcium homeostasis, thereby regulating the balance between osteogenic and angiogenic differentiation in MSCs.

In this study, we systematically explored the role and mechanism of PTGIS in regulating osteogenic and angiogenic differentiation in BMSCs. Our findings demonstrate that PTGIS activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway by inducing ER stress, which subsequently suppresses osteogenic capacity and promotes angiogenic potential in BMSCs.

Materials and methods

Isolation and culture of BMSCs

Human bone marrow samples were collected from the orofacial region of healthy adult donors. All procedures followed informed consent and were approved by the Ethical Committee of Beijing Stomatological Hospital, Capital Medical University (Ethical Review NO. CMUSH-IRB-KJ-PJ-2025-80 and CMUSH-IRB-KJ-PJ-2023-50). BMSCs were then isolated from the mandibular bone tissue using an established protocol [33]. For all subsequent experiments, cells from passages 3–5 were utilized.

HUVECs culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were used in this study. HUVECs were purchased from Procell (Cat No. CP-H082), along with their corresponding complete culture medium (Procell, Cat No. CM-H082). All experiments were performed using cells from passages 5–6.

Plasmid construction and viral infection

Plasmid construction and lentivirus production followed a previously established protocol [34]. For PTGIS knockdown, short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting human PTGIS (PTGISsh) and a non-targeting control shRNA (Consh) were synthesized by Genechem Company (Shanghai, China) and subsequently cloned and inserted into the pLKO.1-puro vector. To achieve PTGIS overexpression (HA-PTGIS), a DNA sequence encoding an N-terminal hemagglutinin (HA) tag fused to the full-length human PTGIS cDNA was synthesized and inserted into the NheI/NotI sites of the pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1a-puro vector, generating the HA-PTGIS plasmid. The corresponding empty vector was used as a control (Vector). All final plasmid constructs were verified through restriction enzyme digestion and DNA sequencing.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity assay and alizarin red staining (ARS)

BMSCs were cultured in osteogenic induction medium containing 100 µM ascorbic acid, 2 mM β‑glycerophosphate, 1.8 mM KH₂PO₄, and 10 nM dexamethasone. ALP activity was quantified on day 7 using a commercial kit (Beyotime, Cat No. P0321S). Following 2 weeks of induction, matrix mineralization was evaluated by ARS staining (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No. TMS-008) according to standard procedures [35]. To quantitatively assess calcium deposition after osteogenic induction, the cells were decalcified using 10% cetylpyridinium chloride dissolved in 10 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) for 60 min at room temperature. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader. To correct for any differences in cell number, the calcium content of each sample was normalized to the total protein concentration determined from a duplicate plate using a BCA assay. All assays were conducted in triplicate.

In vivo osteogenesis assay

Six nude mice (8 weeks old, female, nu/nu) were used with all experimental protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Beijing Stomatological Hospital (Approval No. KQYY‑202411‑002) in accordance with ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0. None of the animals received surgical or medical treatments. Before cell implantation, mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (1%, w/v, in sterile 0.9% saline) via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 50 mg/kg body weight. During anesthesia and recovery, mice were kept on a 37 °C heating pad and given ophthalmic ointment to both eyes to prevent hypothermia and corneal drying. For implantation, a cell‑scaffold construct was prepared by incubating 2.0 × 10⁶ cells with 20 mg of hydroxyapatite/tricalcium phosphate (HA/TCP) particles (Engineering Research Center for Biomaterials) at 37 °C for 3 h. Each construct was then implanted bilaterally into the subcutaneous dorsal region of a mouse. Following an 8‑week period, the grafts were harvested for analysis. All mice were humanely euthanized by pentobarbital sodium overdose (200 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection, 2% solution) at the experimental endpoint.

Histological and immunofluorescence analysis

The harvested grafts were subjected to standard histological processing. H&E staining was carried out to evaluate overall tissue architecture and quantify mineralized tissue, following a published protocol [35]. Collagen deposition and osteoid formation were visualized using Masson’s trichrome staining, performed according to established methods [36]. For immunofluorescence, tissue sections were labeled with an anti-CD31 antibody (Proteintech, Cat. No. 11265-1-AP) to mark vascular endothelium and an anti-BIP antibody (Proteintech, Cat. No. 11587-1-AP) to assess ER stress. Imaging was performed using an Olympus microscope. Analysis was conducted on randomly selected fields from systematic samples to ensure objective evaluation.

Western blot

Total protein lysates from BMSCs were separated by SDS-PAGE following a standard protocol [37]. The primary antibodies in this study were anti-HA (Proteintech, Cat No. 51064-2-AP), anti-PTGIS (Proteintech, Cat No. 27061-1-AP), anti-DMP1 (Bioss, Cat. No. bs-25502R), anti-BSP (Bioss, Cat. No. bs-2668R), anti-GRP75 (Bioss, Cat. No. bs-1469R), anti-PI3K (Bioss, Cat. No. bs-10657R), anti-Phospho-PI3K (Absin, Cat. No. abs147914), anti-AKT (Bioss, Cat. No. bsm-63324R), anti-Phospho-AKT (Bioss, Cat. No. bsm-60645R) and GAPDH (Rosemont, Cat No.60004-1-Ig).

In vitro angiogenesis assay

An in vitro angiogenesis assay was performed based on a published protocol [38]. Matrigel (BD Biosciences, Cat. No. 356234) was added to 96‑well plates (50 µL/well) and allowed to polymerize. BMSCs and HUVECs were co‑cultured at a 1:1 ratio (total 2 × 10⁴ cells/well) in 100 µL medium for 3 h. Tubule formation was then observed and imaged under an inverted microscope. The following parameters were defined and analyzed: Nodes: branching points where three or more segments intersect; Junctions: points where two segments connect; Segments: individual tubule fragments between two nodes or between a node and an endpoint; Meshes: enclosed polygonal areas bounded by segments, representing complete tube loops. Quantification of nodes, junctions, segments, and meshes was carried out using ImageJ software. Each condition was tested in triplicate.

In vivo angiogenesis assay

Twelve nude mice (8 weeks old, female, nu/nu) were obtained from the Beijing Stomatological Hospital Animal Laboratory (Beijing, China), and none of the animals received surgical or medical treatments. Mice were anesthetized using sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection, 1% solution) and then each received bilateral subcutaneous injections of 150 µL Matrigel plugs containing a co‑culture of HUVECs and BMSCs (1 × 10⁶ cells per cell type). Two weeks later, the implants were harvested for further analysis. All mice were humanely euthanized by pentobarbital sodium overdose (200 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection, 2% solution) at the experimental endpoint. Our work has been reported in accordance with ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0.

Measurement of cytosolic Ca²⁺ dynamics

Cytosolic Ca²⁺ levels were monitored using the fluorescent Ca²⁺ indicator Fluo-4 AM (Beyotime, Cat No. S1052). BMSCs were seeded in confocal dishes and cultured until reaching 70–80% confluence. Prior to staining, cells were washed twice with Ca²⁺-free PBS and incubated in Ca²⁺-free buffer containing 5 µM Fluo-4 AM at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Following incubation, cells were washed three times with Ca²⁺-free buffer to remove excess dye and then maintained in Ca²⁺-free buffer for imaging.

Fluorescence imaging was performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope with excitation at 488 nm and emission collected at 516 nm. Images were captured at 1-minute intervals for a total duration of 30 min. For quantitative analysis, fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader with the same wavelength settings. Relative fluorescence units were normalized to baseline values, and data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Three independent experiments were performed with triplicate samples for each condition.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For ultrastructural analysis of ER morphology, BMSCs were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer followed by post-fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide. After dehydration through a graded ethanol series, samples were embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin Sect. (70 nm) were prepared and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The sections were examined using a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope. Multiple fields were captured at appropriate magnifications to assess ER ultrastructure.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS software. Student’s t-test or one‑way ANOVA was applied for group comparisons where appropriate. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

PTGIS suppressed osteogenic capacity of BMSCs in vitro and in vivo

Stable PTGIS-knockdown BMSCs (PTGISsh) were generated using lentiviral transduction, with non-targeting shRNA serving as the control (Consh). Western blot analysis confirmed successful PTGIS knockdown in the PTGISsh group (Fig. 1A, B). Following 7 days of osteogenic induction, ALP staining revealed stronger osteogenic activity in the PTGISsh cells compared to controls (Fig. 1C). This was supported by Western blot results, which showed increased expression of the osteogenic markers DMP1 and BSP in the knockdown group (Fig. 1D, E). After 2 weeks of induction, ARS staining indicated enhanced matrix mineralization in PTGISsh cells (Fig. 1F). Consistently, these cells also exhibited a significant increase in calcium ion concentration (Fig. 1G).

C ALP staining showed enhanced osteogenic differentiation in PTGISsh after 7 days of induction. D, E The protein levels of osteogenic markers DMP1 and BSP were higher in PTGISsh. F ARS staining and (G) calcium quantification demonstrated increased mineralization in PTGISsh after 14 days. H, I Western blot confirmed PTGIS overexpression in HA-PTGIS. J ALP staining showed reduced osteogenic differentiation in HA-PTGIS after 7 days. K, L Western blot demonstrated reduced DMP1 and BSP expression in HA-PTGIS. M ARS staining and (N) calcium quantification demonstrated decreased mineralization in HA-PTGIS after 14 days. O, Q H&E and (P, R) Masson’s trichrome staining of implants in nude mice revealed increased osteoid formation and collagen deposition in PTGISsh grafts. Error bars represent SD (n = 6). Student’s t-test was performed to determine statistical significance. *** P < 0.001; **** P < 0.0001. Scale bar: 20 μm (O, P). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1, 2

To further examine the effects of PTGIS, HA-tagged PTGIS was introduced into BMSCs via lentiviral transduction, creating stable PTGIS-overexpressing cells (HA-PTGIS). Cells transduced with the empty vector served as controls (Vector). Western blot analysis confirmed the overexpression of PTGIS in the HA-PTGIS group (Fig. 1H, I). The osteogenic capacity of these cells was then evaluated. After 7 days of induction, ALP staining demonstrated that PTGIS overexpression significantly weakened osteogenic activity compared to the control (Fig. 1J). Western blot results were consistent, revealing lower levels of the osteogenic markers DMP1 and BSP in HA-PTGIS cells (Fig. 1K, L). Following 14 days of osteogenic induction, ARS staining further confirmed impaired mineralization capacity in the PTGIS-overexpressing group (Fig. 1M). Accordingly, these cultures also exhibited a significantly lower calcium ion concentration than the control group (Fig. 1N).

To assess the osteogenic effect of PTGIS in vivo, a transplantation model was performed in nude mice. Histological analysis 8 weeks post-transplantation revealed enhanced osteogenic differentiation in the grafts. H&E staining (Fig. 1O) showed more bone‑like tissue formation in implants with PTGIS‑knockdown cells compared to the control group. Semi‑quantitative analysis (Fig. 1Q) further supported this observation, indicating larger mature osteoid tissue area in the knockdown group. Similarly, Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 1P) images revealed greater collagen fiber deposition in the PTGIS‑knockdown implants. Semi‑quantitative evaluation of collagen content (Fig. 1R) confirmed more collagen formation in this group. Together, these in vivo results demonstrate that suppressing PTGIS promotes osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.

PTGIS enhanced in vitro tubule formation ability

To quantitatively assess the functional impact of PTGIS on angiogenesis in vitro, BMSCs stably overexpressing PTGIS or suppressing PTGIS were co-cultured with HUVECs at a 1:1 ratio on Matrigel for 3 h. Representative micrographs demonstrated that in co-cultures with HA-PTGIS, HUVECs self-organized into an extensive, well-connected tubular network characterized by numerous multicellular junctions and enclosed meshes compared to the Vector group (Fig. 2A, B). Quantitative analysis further confirmed HUVECs co-cultured with HA-PTGIS exhibited a significant increase in key angiogenic parameters, such as the number of nodes (779 vs. 567), junctions (239 vs. 155), segments (284 vs. 182) and meshes (77 vs. 35) compared to the control group (Fig. 2C-F).

G, H Microscopic analysis revealed tubule formation by HUVECs co-cultured with PTGISsh or Consh. Error bars represent SD (n = 3). *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01. Scale bar: 200 μm (A, G), 50 μm (B, H)

To confirm the specific role of PTGIS, we next examined the effects of PTGIS knockdown. After 3 h of co-culture, micrographs showed co-cultures with PTGISsh produced sparse, underdeveloped tubule structures compared to the more robust networks formed with Consh (Fig. 2G, H). Quantification confirmed this reduction, showing decreased numbers of nodes (442 vs. 570), junctions (128 vs. 162), segments (148 vs. 195), and meshes (26 vs. 42) in the PTGIS knockdown group (Fig. 2I-L). These results confirmed that PTGIS expression in BMSCs is both necessary and sufficient to potentiate their capacity to support endothelial cell network formation. PTGIS overexpression increased tubule formation, whereas its downregulation reduced tubule formation in vitro.

PTGIS promoted vascularization capacity of BMSCs in vivo

To evaluate the effect of PTGIS on angiogenesis in vivo, a Matrigel plug assay was performed in nude mice. Gross visualization of the grafts containing Vector and HA-PTGIS revealed apparent differences after a 14-day implantation period. Plugs containing HA-PTGIS appeared reddish, while Vector control plugs were pale (Fig. 3A). Histological analysis then demonstrated that PTGIS overexpression significantly promoted blood vessel formation. H&E staining and quantification confirmed plugs from the HA-PTGIS group contained a significantly higher density of functional vessels compared to the Vector controls (Fig. 3B, C). To specifically label HUVECs, we performed immunofluorescence staining for a canonical endothelial marker CD31. Representative confocal images showed intense and abundant CD31⁺ signal within the HA-PTGIS plugs. The signal in Vector was noticeably weaker and less organized (Fig. 3D).

B, F Representative H&E staining of sections from the Matrigel plugs, with yellow arrows indicating newly formed blood vessels. C, G Quantitative analysis of vessel number per microscopic field. D, H Immunofluorescence staining for CD31 in the plugs. Student’s t-test was performed to determine statistical significance. Error bars represent SD (n = 6). **P < 0.01; *** P < 0.001. Scale bar: 20 μm (B, D, F, H)

Conversely, analysis of the grafts containing PTGISsh revealed impaired vascularization. PTGISsh plugs were pale compared to Consh controls (Fig. 3E). H&E staining and vessel counting demonstrated a significant decrease in blood vessel formation in the PTGISsh group relative to the Consh group (Fig. 3F, G). As expected, PTGIS knockdown markedly reduced the CD31⁺ signal compared to the Consh group (Fig. 3H). These in vivo results indicate that PTGIS overexpression in BMSCs enhances the angiogenic capacity, while its suppression attenuates this function.

PTGIS promoted ER calcium efflux

To investigate the mechanistic link between PTGIS and calcium homeostasis, we performed live-cell calcium imaging to monitor dynamic changes in cytosolic Ca²⁺ concentration. BMSCs were loaded with the fluorescent Ca²⁺ indicator Fluo-4 AM under Ca²⁺-free conditions to isolate the contribution of intracellular stores. Representative fluorescence images captured at key time points (0, 10, 20, and 30 min) revealed a pronounced and time-dependent increase in Fluo-4 signal intensity in HA-PTGIS cells compared to Vector controls (Fig. 4A). Quantification of the fluorescence kinetics over the entire 30-minute recording period provided robust statistical evidence (Fig. 4B). The mean relative fluorescence intensity in the HA-PTGIS group was significantly elevated, reaching a peak increase of approximately 1.2-fold over the Vector control group.

PTGIS promoted ER calcium efflux. A, D Representative Fluo‑4 fluorescence images of HA‑PTGIS versus Vector (A) and PTGISsh versus Consh (D) at 0, 10, 20, and 30 min. B, C Quantitative kinetics of relative Fluo‑4 fluorescence intensity over 30 min, comparing HA‑PTGIS to Vector (B) and PTGISsh to Consh (C). Scale bar: 20 μm (A, D)

Conversely, suppression of PTGIS produced the opposite effect. Fluorescence microscopy showed a visibly weaker Fluo-4 signal in PTGISsh relative to Consh cells at all matched time points (Fig. 4D). Quantitative kinetic analysis confirmed a significant and sustained decrease in the mean relative fluorescence intensity in the PTGIS knockdown group (Fig. 4C). The intensity was consistently lower, with a maximal reduction of about 19.6% compared to the Consh group. Given that the ER serves as the primary intracellular Ca²⁺ reservoir, the significant elevation implies that PTGIS overexpression promotes the efflux of Ca²⁺ from the ER lumen into the cytosol.

PTGIS induced ER stress

To establish a direct link between PTGIS-mediated ER calcium efflux and the induction of ER stress, multiple experimental approaches were employed. TEM provided direct visual evidence of ER pathology. HA-PTGIS exhibited pronounced abnormalities in ER morphology. The ER lumen appeared markedly dilated and vacuolized, a classic ultrastructural hallmark of ER stress (Fig. 5A). In stark contrast, PTGISsh and their respective controls displayed a normal, compact ER morphology (Fig. 5B), indicating that PTGIS suppression preserves ER structural integrity.

E, F Quantitative analysis of relative BIP fluorescence intensity in BMSCs. G-J Western blot analyzed BIP and GRP75 expression in HA-PTGIS and PTGISsh. Error bars represent SD (n = 3). Student’s t-test was performed to determine statistical significance. ** P < 0.01;*** P < 0.001. Scale bar: 1 μm (A, B); 40 μm (C, D). Full-length blots/gels are presented in Supplementary Fig. 3

We next assessed the expression of key molecular mediators of the UPR. Immunofluorescence staining for the central ER chaperone BIP (GRP78) revealed a strong and diffuse cytoplasmic signal in HA-PTGIS, while PTGISsh exhibited lower fluorescence intensity (Fig. 5C, D). Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity demonstrated a significant increase in BIP signal in the HA-PTGIS group compared to Vector controls (Fig. 5E). Conversely, PTGISsh showed a significantly attenuated BIP signal compared to Consh cells (Fig. 5F). This finding was conclusively validated at the total protein level by Western blot analysis (Fig. 5G-J). Consistent with the immunofluorescence data, protein levels of both BIP and another major ER stress sensor, GRP75, were significantly elevated in HA-PTGIS group. Together, these results indicated that PTGIS overexpression is a potent inducer of ER stress in BMSCs.

Attenuation of ER stress reversed PTGIS-induced dysregulation of BMSC functions

To verify whether ER stress is the essential mechanistic mediator of PTGIS, we pretreated HA-PTGIS with a well-established ER stress inhibitor 4-PBA (5 mM). In the in vitro tubule formation assay, the extensive, well-connected endothelial network supported by HA-PTGIS was profoundly disrupted by 4-PBA pretreatment (Fig. 6A). Quantitative analysis of the network confirmed this reversal. The enhanced tubule formation caused by PTGIS overexpression was all significantly attenuated by 4-PBA pretreatment (Fig. 6B‑E). This was reflected by decreased numbers of nodes (689 vs. 881), meshes (46 vs. 75), junctions (175 vs. 252), and segments (208 vs. 296) compared to Vector controls.

A Microscopic analysis revealed tubule formation by HUVECs co‑cultured with HA‑PTGIS pretreated with or without the ER stress inhibitor 4‑PBA. Quantitative analysis of the formed tubules demonstrated that 4‑PBA pretreatment significantly reduced the number of (B) nodes, (C) meshes, (D) junctions, and (E) segments. F ALP staining after 7 days of osteogenic induction revealed increased activity in 4‑PBA‑pretreated HA‑PTGIS. G ARS staining after 14 days confirmed enhanced matrix mineralization following 4‑PBA. Error bars represent SD (n = 3). One-way ANOVA was performed to determine statistical significance. ** P < 0.01. Scale bar: 200 μm, 50 μm (A)

Conversely, inhibiting ER stress reversed the block in osteogenesis. After 7 days of osteogenic induction, ALP staining revealed that 4-PBA pretreatment markedly increased enzymatic activity in HA-PTGIS (Fig. 6F). The staining intensity and area in the 4-PBA- pretreated HA-PTGIS group were significantly greater than in untreated HA-PTGIS cells, approaching levels seen in Vector controls. ARS staining after 14 days showed that 4-PBA treatment robustly enhanced calcium nodule formation in HA-PTGIS (Fig. 6G). The size of mineralized deposits was significantly increased compared to the untreated HA-PTGIS cells. These results indicated that reducing ER stress could suppress its angiogenic phenotype and restore the osteogenic differentiation capacity that was dysregulated by PTGIS overexpression.

PTGIS induces ER stress to mediate PI3K/AKT signaling pathway activation in BMSCs

To delineate the downstream signaling pathway through which PTGIS and ensuing ER stress exert their effects, we systematically examined the activation status of the PI3K/AKT axis. Western blot analysis revealed that PTGIS overexpression directly influenced the PI3K/AKT pathway. Compared to Vector, HA-PTGIS exhibited a pronounced increase in the phosphorylation levels of both PI3K (p-PI3K) and its downstream effector AKT (p-AKT), whereas the total protein levels of PI3K and AKT remained unchanged (Fig. 7A, B). Conversely, PTGISsh produced the opposite effect. PTGISsh reduced p‑PI3K and p‑AKT levels without altering total PI3K or AKT expression (Fig. 7C, D).

PTGIS induces ER stress to mediate PI3K/AKT signaling pathway activation. A, B Western blot analysis showed increased p-PI3K and p-AKT in HA-PTGIS, while (C, D) PTGISsh reduced their phosphorylation. E, F Treating HA-PTGIS with the ER stress inhibitor 4-PBA reversed the increase in p-PI3K and p-AKT. Full-length blots/gels are presented in Supplementary Fig. 4, 5

To determine if ER stress is the necessary link between PTGIS and PI3K/AKT activation, we pretreated HA-PTGIS cells with the ER stress inhibitor 4-PBA (5 µM). This intervention effectively reversed the PTGIS-induced hyperphosphorylation of PI3K and AKT (Fig. 7E, F). The elevated levels of p-PI3K and p-AKT in HA-PTGIS were reduced to near-baseline levels comparable to the Vector upon 4-PBA pretreatment. This result definitively positions ER stress as the critical upstream signaling event that couples PTGIS overexpression to the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway.

Inhibition of AKT mitigated PTGIS-induced ER stress and rescued BMSCs functions

Furthermore, we treated HA-PTGIS cells with the specific AKT inhibitor MK2206 (3 µM). As expected, MK2206 treatment nearly completely abolished p-AKT without altering the p-PI3K or the total levels of either protein (Fig. 8A, B). To establish AKT as the critical functional effector downstream of the ER stress axis, we assessed its capacity to reverse the established angiogenic and osteogenic phenotypes. In the in vitro tubule formation assay, the endothelial network supported by HA-PTGIS was markedly disrupted upon AKT inhibition (Fig. 8C). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant attenuation across all key network parameters, including nodes (659 vs. 855), meshes (39 vs. 76), junctions (145 vs. 252), and segments (159 vs. 282) compared to untreated Vector controls (Fig. 8D-G).

On the other hand, blocking AKT signaling effectively reversed the suppression of osteogenesis. ALP staining showed that MK2206 pretreatment substantially increased enzymatic activity in HA-PTGIS after 7 days of osteogenic induction (Fig. 8H). The staining intensity in the MK2206 pretreated group was markedly stronger than in the untreated HA-PTGIS group. ARS staining after 14 days revealed that AKT inhibition significantly enhanced matrix mineralization in HA-PTGIS cultures (Fig. 8I). These targeted inhibitor experiments provide definitive functional validation that inhibition of AKT simultaneously abolishes the PTGIS-driven angiogenic phenotype and completely rescues the blocked osteogenic differentiation.

Discussion

Ectopic mineralization is a pathological process involving the abnormal deposition of calcium-phosphate crystals in soft tissues, posing a significant and growing clinical burden globally [39]. At disease sites, excessive pathological calcification occurs alongside insufficient new blood vessel formation [40, 41]. Current clinical strategies rarely address both problems at the same time. Ectopic mineralization shares similarities with physiological bone formation. In this study, we observed that knockdown of PTGIS strongly enhanced osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs both in vitro and in vivo. This finding offers a new perspective on the role of PTGIS within the bone metabolic microenvironment. A previous study has suggested that PGI₂, the catalytic product of PTGIS, can promote the proliferation of growth plate chondrocytes, thereby supporting bone development [17]. In contrast, another study found that PGI₂ deficiency leads to increased bone mass. In that context, the absence of PGI₂ accelerates bone turnover, with bone formation ultimately exceeding resorption [19]. These seemingly contradictory results precisely indicate that PTGIS has complex and even bidirectional regulatory effects under different physiological or pathological environments and different cell types.

Interestingly, our study found that PTGIS overexpression produced the opposite effect on angiogenesis. It markedly improved the ability of BMSCs to support tubule formation in vitro and to promote new blood vessel growth in vivo. This newly discovered role extends beyond the classic cardiovascular functions of PTGIS. Traditionally, PTGIS is known for dilating blood vessels and preventing platelet aggregation [42]. Supporting our findings, recent studies have shown that PTGIS deficiency leads to endothelial dysfunction by shifting prostaglandin production toward vasoconstrictive pathways [43]. Furthermore, PTGIS activity helps sustain key angiogenic factors such as VEGF, accelerates the migration and proliferation of ECs and promotes angiogenesis [32]. Our findings align with the therapeutic goal of simultaneously inhibiting pathological calcification and supporting vascular repair in ectopic mineralization.

PTGIS is localized to the ER membrane. The ER serves as the largest intracellular calcium store, and disruption of its calcium homeostasis is a known trigger for ER stress and the UPR [44–46]. In our study, overexpressing PTGIS in BMSCs triggered significant ER calcium efflux and ER stress. This caused observable ER structural dilation and upregulated the expression of the ER chaperone proteins BIP and GRP75. A potential mechanistic link between PTGIS and ER calcium efflux warrants consideration. PTGIS is the rate‑limiting enzyme for PGI₂ synthesis [47]. PGI₂ acts through its cognate IP receptor, a Gs protein‑coupled receptor, to elevate intracellular cAMP levels [48]. Elevated cAMP activates PKA, which can phosphorylate IP₃ receptors on the ER membrane, increasing their sensitivity to IP₃ or calcium and promoting ER calcium release into the cytosol [49]. Consistent with this, a PGI₂ synthase inhibitor has been shown to modulate cytosolic calcium mobilization in cardiomyocytes [50]. Thus, PTGIS overexpression may trigger ER calcium efflux via the PGI₂/IP/cAMP/PKA/IP₃R axis, which in turn induces ER stress. In the future, we will further explore the mechanism of calcium signaling mediated by PTGIS in BMSCs based on this hypothesis.

Our research further confirms that PTGIS overexpression induces ER stress, promoting angiogenesis and inhibiting osteogenesis in BMSCs. It is worth noting that the effect of PTGIS on ER stress appears to be highly context‑dependent. In β-cells, constitutively high PTGIS expression is essential for ER homeostasis and cell survival [31]. Whereas in our study, PTGIS overexpression disrupts calcium homeostasis rather than providing protection. BMSCs have different ER stress tolerance thresholds and signaling networks, the same level of PTGIS overexpression can have divergent consequences depending on the cell type’s inherent ER stress management capacity.

In our study, PTGIS-induced PI3K/AKT activation suppressed osteogenesis while promoting angiogenesis. Inhibiting AKT reversed the enhanced endothelial tubule‑forming capacity and the suppressed osteogenic ability of BMSCs. However, researchers hold different views regarding the role of the PI3K/AKT pathway in regulating osteogenesis in MSCs. For example, sulfated chitosan has been shown to promote osteogenic differentiation by activating PI3K/AKT in a model of osteonecrosis [51]. Similarly, natural products such as linoleic acid and exosomes loaded with Panax notoginseng saponins enhance osteogenesis through PI3K/AKT signaling in MSCs [52, 53]. In contrast, under high‑glucose conditions, PI3K/AKT activation exacerbates the inhibition of osteoblast differentiation [54]. In angiogenesis, however, the PI3K/AKT pathway in MSCs generally plays a positive regulatory role. Recent studies indicate that it enhances VEGF secretion from MSCs, promoting new vessel formation during wound healing [55]. Similarly, in fracture repair, exosomes from BMSCs activate PI3K/AKT in ECs, accelerating vascular regeneration [56], which is consistent with our observations.

In physiological bone repair, PI3K/AKT activation generally supports both osteogenesis and angiogenesis [57, 58]. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by considering upstream stress signals and signaling intensity of PI3K/AKT. First, PI3K/AKT can either promote or inhibit osteogenesis depending on the cellular context: it enhances BMSC-mediated bone formation when activated by exosome-loaded biomaterials [59], but inhibits osteogenesis when aberrantly activated in osteoporosis [60] or via NEDD4 suppression [61]. Moreover, the intensity of PI3K/AKT signaling may dictate downstream outcomes. A threshold level of AKT activity sufficient to support cell survival may be inadequate for driving differentiation [62], and the specific p-AKT increase observed in our study may favor angiogenesis over osteogenic transcriptional programs. Thus, within the PTGIS–ER stress–PI3K/AKT axis, pathway activation is functionally skewed toward angiogenesis and away from osteogenesis.

Moving forward, the mechanistic insights from this study open several avenues for clinical translation. Instead of direct gene editing, which faces significant safety and feasibility hurdles [63, 64], alternative strategies can be pursued. These include BMSC-derived exosomes as cell-free therapeutics [65, 66], small-molecule modulators targeting the enzymatic activity of PTGIS, and biomimetic nanocarriers for targeted delivery of PTGIS‑related cargo. In summary, our mechanistic insights lay the groundwork for multiple translationally viable strategies to concurrently suppress pathological calcification and promote functional angiogenesis in ectopic mineralization disorders. However, the long-term effects of these approaches require thorough investigation in preclinical models before clinical application.

Conclusion

This study elucidates that PTGIS overexpression modulates the function of BMSCs in regulating osteogenesis and angiogenesis by disrupting ER calcium homeostasis, inducing ER stress, and activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Specifically, PTGIS overexpression suppresses osteogenic capacity while enhancing angiogenic potential. This finding offers a new perspective for understanding MSC fate regulation in ectopic mineralization disorders. Further evaluation of its therapeutic potential may offer a promising direction for treating this condition.
