Child‐Directed Speech in Rural and Urban Households in a Low‐SES Afrikaans‐Speaking Community in South Africa
Authors: Carmen Defty, Frenette Southwood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMost studies on child language acquisition occur in the minority world (countries which make up the minority of the world's population). Their findings are not generalizable to majority‐world contexts, where the majority of the world's population lives. The limited research in the latter contexts reveals the presence of differing child language socialization practices, not generalizable across Global South contexts, and that socio‐economic status (SES) and geographic location (rural/urban) can affect these practices. This study examined the language input to 5‐month‐olds in a low‐SES, Afrikaans‐speaking community in South Africa. Half of the 10 participating households were urban‐situated and half rural‐situated. Each home had a 1‐h video recording of household members interacting naturally with and around the infant; these recordings were transcribed and coded in ELAN. The amount of child‐directed speech (CDS) and number of (a) different utterance types, and (b) instances of contingent speech were determined. CDS comprised 76% of all utterances. Although there were no significant differences between urban and rural households when all CDS was considered collectively, more questions were directed at rural than urban infants, and more instances of speaking on behalf of the child occurred in the case of rural infants: in each recording, caregivers used “typical” CDS features (higher pitch, repetition, etc.) to speak in the first person but on behalf of infants, an infrequently reported phenomenon in CDS research. The types and number of utterances and the amount of CDS used did not pattern as reported by scholars for other low‐SES, majority‐world communities.Keywords: child‐directed speech, language input, language socialization, low‐socioeconomic status, minority world

Overview of Child‐Directed Speech

Child‐directed speech 1 (CDS), that is, adjusted speech that caregivers 2 use when interacting with children (Gordon and Watson 2015), is typically described as having a higher pitch, exaggerated intonation, a highly restricted vocabulary (about 100 words when talking to very young children), and simpler sentence structures than adult‐to‐adult speech (Saxton 2009). Studies have shown a possible correlation between CDS and overall child language development (see Ma 2022, for an overview). However, most findings on CDS are from research conducted in minority‐world contexts. 3

Earlier research found that CDS contains many imperatives and questions (specifically yes/no questions) but few declaratives (see, e.g., Snow 1977; Newport et al. 1979; Kavanaugh and Jirkovsky 1982, for English; Toda et al. 1990, for English and Japanese; Soderstrom 2007; Saxton 2009).

Although an overview by Soderstrom (2007) reported that CDS is structurally simpler than adult‐to‐adult speech, CDS in some majority‐world cultures are more complex than and can contain the same types of sentence structures as adult‐to‐adult speech (e.g., Pye 1986, for Quiché Mayan cultures; Vogt et al. 2015, for rural and urban Mozambique communities; Terry 2010, for East Cree communities in Canada). 4 CDS has been found in a variety of contexts (e.g., Vogt et al. 2015, for Changana and Portuguese in Mozambique and Dutch in the Netherlands; Pye 1986, for Quiche Mayan in Guatemala; Cameron‐Faulkner et al. 2003, for British English in the UK; Terry 2010, for East Cree in Canada; Rosemberg et al. 2022, for Argentinian Spanish) but to varying extents and with varying characteristics. Some features of CDS register, such as alteration of prosody, have been found in both majority‐ and minority‐world contexts (see Broesch and Bryant 2014, for comparison across three cultures: Fijians, Kenyans, and North Americans; Hilton et al. 2022, for a large‐scale study across 21 communities).

CDS has been found to be prevalent in well‐educated, middle‐class contexts (see Newport et al. 1979, for the USA; Foulkes et al. 2005, for England; Saito et al. 2007, for Japan; Liu et al. 2002, for China), as well as in some majority‐world cultures (see Rabain‐Jamin 2001, for Senegal). However, some cultures use CDS minimally, such as the Luo in Kenya (Blount 1969), Inuit groups in Canada (Crago et al. 1993), the Tsimane forager‐horticulturalist community of lowland Bolivia (Cristia et al. 2019), rural villages of Botswana (Geiger and Alant 2005), Western Samoa communities (Ochs 1988), the Kaluli in Papua New Guinea (Schieffelin 1985), and some Guatemalan communities (Pye 1986).

Across communities, there are differences in not only the quantity of CDS but also the nature thereof–for instance, in utterance types contained in CDS. Contingent speech, a form of CDS, comprises “utterances which are […] expansions (complete, incomplete, transformed or elaborated), and semantic extensions of the child's preceding topic or predicate” (Cross 1977, p. 158). The infants in this study are pre‐linguistic, and thus, their utterances, which are vocalizations, do not have discernible semantic information on which contingent utterances can be built. However, the caregivers interpret the infants' vocalizations as having meaning, and thus they prescribe contingent utterances on their interpretations of the infants' vocalizations. Golinkoff et al. (2019) state that behaviors within CDS, like contingent speech because of increased parental responsiveness toward and focus on the child, could play a role in “fostering infants' language knowledge” (p. 341; see also Baumwell et al. 1997). The question arises as to whether features of CDS, including contingent speech, are equally prevalent in cultures where children and their utterances are typically not the focal point in interactions.

Because CDS varies extensively across cultures, one‐size‐fits‐all generalizations regarding child language acquisition, socialization and interaction should be avoided. It follows that communities, including those of the majority‐world, need to be studied on a case‐by‐case basis to determine whether they make use of CDS and, if so, how CDS presents itself in each case. Careful documentation of CDS in many, varied, majority‐world contexts and underrepresented language and culture groups will allow for (a) the expansion of our minority‐world‐dominated knowledge base on child language socialization and its possible effect on child language development across cultures and contexts, and (b) theory building based on more representative evidence.

To this end, this study focused on language input given to Afrikaans‐speaking 5‐month‐olds by caregivers in their homes in South Africa, a majority‐world context. Five‐month‐olds were selected as our interest was particularly in pre‐linguistic infants. Norms for the onset of first words are not yet available for Afrikaans, but data from American English (Moore et al. 2019) showed that children typically produce their first words at around 12 months and comprehend first words between 6 and 9 months. Below follows a discussion of what is known about CDS in majority‐world and understudied contexts.

Child‐Directed Speech Across the Globe

CDS seems to be present in all studied cultures from both minority‐ and majority‐world contexts but to greatly varying extents and with varying characteristics. In some Mayan cultures, infants are considered extremely vulnerable and are therefore not to be overstimulated or interacted with extensively (Brown 1998). Accordingly, almost no verbalizations are directed at them in their first 6 months of life (Brown 1998). When indeed addressed directly, infants are usually given commands in the form of imperatives. In his study of caregiver‐child interactions in a Quiché village (n = 3 infants), 5 Pye (1986) found that CDS is sometimes more complex than adult‐to‐adult speech; for example, CDS had sounds and verbal affixes that did not occur in adult‐to‐adult speech. Additionally, the CDS register used in this community exhibited a high pitch (a common feature of CDS registers in other contexts as well) that is not reserved exclusively for use with children. Similarly, Brown (1998) found that a slower speech rate (another reportedly common feature of CDS register) was rarely used when talking to infants in this Quiché community (n = 5 infants). In their study of Kaluli (n = 4) and Somoan infants (n = 6), Ochs and Schieffelin (2009) found that CDS was not common, and when children were addressed, those simplifications that are sometimes observed in CDS registers in other cultural contexts were absent. In contrast to these cultures with their more complex CDS, Kess and Kess (1986) found that the Nuutka people used a baby register which was a simplified version of adult‐to‐adult speech (n = 3).

Utterance Types in CDS

Research across majority‐world communities indicates that not only the quantity of CDS present but also the nature thereof varies across communities. For instance, Blount and Padgug (1977) described an interplay between cultural expectations and CDS content in Samoan communities: the speech from adults to children seemed to adhere to cultural parameters instead of being tailored to the specific child's perceived linguistic capabilities. For instance, infants were rarely asked yes/no questions because this question type asks for an opinion or observation from the addressee (in this case, a child), which implies that the interlocuters are equal, and considering a young child an equal would contravene the social order.

Contrastively, in their work among the Romani in 13 settlements in Hungary–Hungary being a minority‐world context geographically but the Romani a marginalized, understudied community–Réger and Gleason (1991) reported that infants were asked a considerable number of questions, specifically test questions, defined as “questions whose answers are well known to the questioner but that are asked in order to test or display the knowledge of the child” (p. 604). 6 In the Northern East Cree community in Canada–again, a minority‐world country but an understudied community–questions were also common in CDS. Terry (2010) found that caregivers use a high proportion of questions and commands/requests, engage with the child as a conversational partner, practice several language socialization routines, and use special child‐form lexical items. In research among Mayan cultures, Brown (1998) also found a high number of commands in CDS; although infants were rarely addressed directly, when they were, they were usually given commands, a feature documented in minority‐world contexts as well (see, e.g., Snow 1977; Newport et al. 1979; Soderstrom 2007).

CDS in Urban and Rural Communities

Limited research is available on the relationship between geographic location (urban/rural) and CDS. Relevant studies include that of Vogt et al. (2015; n = 40), who examined CDS in three contexts: urban, middle‐class families from the Netherlands; and rural, subsistence‐farming families and urban families from Mozambique. The number of utterances in the CDS of the Dutch participants significantly exceeded that of both Mozambique groups, and the rural Mozambiquans produced significantly less CDS than both urban groups. CDS also differed qualitatively between contexts, with rural interlocutors using more imperatives than urban interlocutors.

Similarly, Yang (2021) examined two Chinese children: a 3‐year‐old male from a low‐SES, rural background, and a 2‐year‐old female from a middle‐class, urban background. Yang (2021) reported that the urban‐situated caregivers produced more words per minute, and used a larger vocabulary and more interrogatives than their rural counterparts. As in the Vogt et al. study (2015), rural participants in Yang's study produced more imperatives than urban participants.

CDS in Different SES Groups

In their seminal study of language development of 42 American children (aged 10 months to 3 years), Hart and Risley (1995) found vast differences between higher‐SES and lower‐SES families in terms of CDS. The participating families were divided into three SES categories: welfare, working, and professional. Children in the highest income brackets heard a mean of 2,153 words per hour whereas those from the lowest bracket heard 616 (Hart and Risley 1995). Hart and Risley suggested that by the time a child from a low‐income family turned three, they would have heard 30 million fewer words than a child from a family in one of the higher income brackets. This study and its findings have been criticized widely, and findings from other studies on a possible SES disparity do not concur with those of Hart and Risley. For instance, Sperry et al. (2018a; n = 42), when examining CDS across lower and middle‐class communities (the same lower income bracket as that of Hart and Risley 1995), did not find evidence of this disparity. In fact, they found that the number of words primary caregivers in the lower income bracket produced was nearly that of the families in Hart and Risley's higher income bracket. Accordingly, Sperry et al. (2018b) concluded that SES cannot be used as sole predictor of the amount of CDS any one community will use. However, Golinkoff et al. (2019) point out that even if the quantity of CDS does not differ across SES levels, the significance of the link between input and SES level should not be discarded as research across both minority‐ and majority‐world contexts have found other SES‐related differences in input. Findings in this regard include mothers with lower SES using more speech directed at child behavior than mothers with higher SES (see Rowe 2008, for the USA; n = 94), and the CDS used in lower‐SES households containing fewer declaratives and more imperatives (see Vogt et al. 2015 for the Netherlands and Mozambique). Similarly, Rosemberg et al. (2022) found SES‐related differences in quantity, lexical diversity, and syntactic complexity of CDS (n = 30).

In sum, CDS exhibits vast variation–to such an extent that the study of CDS cannot be approached with a one‐size‐fits‐all research method, but that each case requires considerations that fit its context. The present study set out to describe the CDS used in one such context, considering both the quantity and quality of the CDS.

Study Context

Lieven and Stoll (2009) noted that, at the time, language acquisition had been studied in around 70 to 80 languages, most from the minority world. In 2022, Kidd and Garcia reported that this number had grown to 103, but still few languages were from majority‐world countries. The lack of research on child language development in majority‐world contexts therefore persists. This is especially the case for language socialization practices such as CDS, which have been shown to exhibit great variation across majority‐world contexts (as discussed above).

South Africa is a majority‐world country 7 with 12 official languages, which include one sign language. Afrikaans is one of its official Germanic languages (the other being English), with approximately 10.6% of the 2018 population speaking Afrikaans in the home (Statistics South Africa 2018). Afrikaans is a morphologically poor language in which few grammatical features are realized phonetically. Declaratives demonstrate a verb‐second surface order, and the language shows more word order variation than, for instance, English, with scrambling being possible and left dislocation occurring frequently. The equivalent of the following English sentences (some ungrammatical) would be grammatical in Afrikaans: They see me; I think that they me see; I wonder if they me see (but not I think that / wonder if they see me); That girl see they often at the beach. The combination of sparse grammatical morphology and significant word order variation makes Afrikaans atypical amongst Germanic languages.

All South African languages are under‐researched in terms of child language acquisition and language socialization practices, especially in lower‐SES communities (Pascoe and Singh 2023). Studies of interaction patterns in South Africa are Messerschmidt et al. (2008) for Afrikaans and Sesotho (focusing on strategies used to facilitate language development and not on CDS per se), and John et al. (2024) for isiZulu.

There are very few studies on Afrikaans' acquisition as a first language, particularly amongst infants and toddlers (but see Messerschmidt et al. 2008; Brink 2020, 2023; Southwood et al. 2021). Within this already limited body of research, there is almost no data on the child language practices of low‐SES Afrikaans‐speaking communities; hence, this community was selected for study.

Research Questions

RQ1: What is the nature of CDS within Afrikaans‐speaking households in which a 5‐month‐old is raised, in terms of mean number of utterances, utterance types (declaratives, exclamations, imperatives, that is and questions), and instances of contingent speech?

RQ2: Are there any differences between urban and rural households in terms of the mean number of utterances, utterance types, and instances of contingent speech in our sample?

RQ3: What proportion of the speech produced around the infant is child‐directed, and what proportion thereof is contingent speech?

Methods

Study Design and Analytical Procedure

This quantitative study focused on the volume of CDS as a whole and on the number of each type of utterance in the CDS. Three main categories of analysis were CDS, speech about the infant but not directed at the infant, and speech neither about the infant nor directed at the infant. The latter two are both forms of overhearable 8 speech, which has been shown to affect language development (Shneidman et al. 2009), and therefore we placed them in one larger category, namely overhearable speech (OS). Utterances by adults directed at children other than the study infants were classified in the same manner as were utterances directed at adults. In the video footage, the study infant was in all cases the only infant present, and when other children were addressed by adults, the typical features of the CDS register were absent. The main categories, namely CDS and OS, were subdivided into declaratives, exclamations (that is interjections), 9 imperatives, and questions, some of which were further subdivided (see below and Table 1; Tables A1, A2, A3, A4, A5 in Appendix A for examples).

Declaratives were subdivided into statements of fact, comforting statements and greetings, and imperatives into attempts to regulate the infant's behavior and attempts to regulate the infant's vocalizations. Each question was categorized into one of Fraser's (1996) question categories: wh‐, yes/no, information‐eliciting, and information‐checking questions. Another question category was added, namely using the infant's name or nickname in isolation with rising intonation (only for CDS) because it forms part of what Abels et al. (2020) refer to as vocatives of which the main goal is addressee identification (i.e., distinguishing the recipients from others). Thus, hearing one's own name in CDS is part of self‐recognition. Abels et al. (2020) also state that vocatives are an understudied aspect of CDS as a whole, especially in lower‐SES communities.

While CDS and OS were the main categories, there were also the following subcategories: (a) instances of contingent speech (a subcategory of CDS), which were subdivided into speech contingent on the infant's vocalizations and speech contingent on the infant's actions; and (b) instances of speaking on behalf of the child while engaging in CDS (where caregiver utterances with the typically reported features of a CDS register were used to speak in the first person, as if the caregiver were the infant, while simultaneously addressing the infant). These subcategories, namely contingent utterances and speaking on behalf of the child, were included in the tallies for CDS but were also presented separately (see Table 2) to better answer the research question pertaining to contingent speech, and to highlight the underreported feature of speaking on behalf of the child. A third main category in addition to CDS and OS was infant vocalizations, which, of course, do not form part of CDS or OS, but of which the numbers were reported to contextualize findings on (a) the number of times interlocutors attempted to modulate infant vocalizations and (b) the number of times interlocutor's utterances were contingent on child vocalizations.

Participants

The participants were 5‐month‐olds (n = 10) from 10 different households being raised monolingually in Afrikaans, and those of their household members present during data collection. Half of the infants (three females, two males) were from urban‐situated families; the others (two females, three males) lived in rural areas. The rural households were in farm‐laborer communities in which laborers live on farmlands in small dwellings owned by their farmer employers. Living in these dwellings formed part of their remuneration. For both geographic categories, the households were often multi‐generational, with many family members residing together and sharing bedrooms. The urban households were all from one high‐density suburb. All participating households were recruited from areas with low SES. The low SES was confirmed by means of a background questionnaire completed by the infant's primary caregiver which included questions about the average monthly household income.

The infants' primary caregivers were recruited by a fieldworker who lived in one of the farm‐laborer communities of the town in which data was collected and who had acquaintances in the high‐density urban area. He is a trusted member of his community and was involved in previous child‐language studies in the town and surrounds.

Data Collection

The data was collected with the assistance of the abovementioned fieldworker who had previously been trained in the ethics of child‐language research and had experience in data collection amongst households with young children. The primary caregiver of each infant, with the assistance of the fieldworker, completed a background questionnaire which was used to verify that the infant was being raised monolingually in a low‐SES Afrikaans‐speaking household. In total, an hour's worth of video recordings was made by the household members after receiving instructions and a demonstration from the fieldworker. The household members were informed that (a) they could record for one consecutive hour or for two or more shorter periods, (b) they could also delete any footage, and (c) the footage was meant to be of naturalistic interactions between the infant and those in their surroundings. To ensure that we obtain the same amount of footage for each household, we asked the household members to ensure that their recordings added up to an hour. Upon receipt of a family's video footage, the first author checked the total length thereof, and where it did not total 1 hour, they were asked to submit more video footage. In all cases, the households obliged.

Recordings were mostly made on a tablet that the authors provided to the fieldworker. Typically, the fieldworker would leave the tablet with the household members for the duration of the recording day. Households could supplement these recordings by using their personal devices.

Data Transcription and Coding

Prior to the commencement of coding, the authors jointly determined the coding categories and subcategories. All of these were retained, and an additional category (speaking on behalf of the infant) was added during transcription. Hereafter, the first author transcribed the recordings in ELAN. The transcriptions were checked by an Afrikaans‐speaking research assistant proficient in ELAN. The only content changes made during checking involved the assistant replacing some of the speech transcribed as xxx (i.e., unintelligible according to the first author) with words. All other changes pertained to punctuation and the precise onset of overlapping speech.

The first author coded the transcribed data, and coding was checked for consistency by the second author and thereafter by the assistant. Any differences in coding between the authors were resolved via discussion. Code‐recode reliability was not calculated. The transcriptions took 3 months to complete, and the coding and two rounds of checking took another month. Table 1 outlines the coding categories used, and Appendix A contains examples of utterances.

An utterance was classified as a speech segment with an identifiable sentence structure. For example, “Where is mommy?” was deemed a single utterance as it is identifiable as a question construction. Compound sentences such as “Where is mommy, and why is she gone?” were counted as two utterances as “Where is mommy” and “and why is she gone” each comprise an individual question construction. All proportions were calculated based on the total number of individual utterances in the respective categories outlined in Table 1 and Appendix A.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical clearance for this study was granted by the Humanities Research Ethics Committee at Stellenbosch University (SU‐HSD‐004080). The study was conducted according to the ethical guidelines and principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, South African Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice, and the South African Medical Research Council's Ethical Guidelines for Research. Consent and assent for participation were obtained with the assistance of the fieldworker for all persons present in the recordings. Where minors (including the infant participants) appeared in recordings, their parent or legal guardian consented in writing to such appearance. In addition, older children assented in writing to their own participation. Prior to data collection, participants were made aware, in writing and verbally by the fieldworker, that no identifying information (names, addresses, etc.) will be disclosed, to preserve anonymity and privacy. Accordingly, participant codes were used to refer to the infants, where the first letter is R or U (for rural or urban) and the second F or M (for female or male), followed by the participant number (1–10). Each household was given a supermarket voucher of R300 (approximately US$16 at the time) after data collection to thank them for their time and effort.

Results

Overview of Data

Collectively, 12, 418 utterances were produced across the 10 h of recording. This includes CDS and OS. The 10 infants collectively produced 2, 445 vocalizations.

Of the 12, 418 utterances, 9, 445 (76% combined across all 10 infants) 10 comprised CDS, including contingent speech and speaking on behalf of the child. Regarding utterance types present in this CDS, 30% were declaratives, 12% exclamations, 30% imperatives, and 29% questions.

There were 529 instances of contingent speech, 50% thereof produced in reaction to the infant's (nonverbal) actions and 50% to infant vocalizations. Of the three main categories (see Table 2), the largest differences between the urban and rural groups were in number of (a) infant vocalizations (urban‐situated infants on average produced more vocalizations (299.8 vs. rural 189.2), and (b) instances of speaking on behalf of the infant (mean: rural 67.2 instances vs. urban 35.4). The effect sizes, as measured by Cohen's d, was 0.1 for CDS, 0.3 for OS, 0.3 for contingent speech, 0.6 for speaking on behalf of the infant, and 0.9 for infant vocalizations, all indicating ranging effect sizes. The large standard deviations could, however, have contributed to the effect sizes.

CDS in the Urban and Rural Groups

Rural‐urban comparisons are made cautiously as considering central tendencies alone masks the large intragroup differences. Figures 1, 2, 3, 4 show the sentence subcategories. The CDS subcategories with the largest difference between the urban and rural groups were questions, exclamations, and imperatives. On average, when comparing means, 22% more questions, and also more of each type of question (bar information‐checking questions), were directed at the rural group. This included eliciting questions, of which 39% more were directed at rural infants. The exception was information‐checking questions, of which almost three times more were directed at the urban group. Although more questions in total were directed at the rural infants, 19% fewer exclamations were directed at them than at their urban counterparts. The third notable difference in CDS was in the number of imperatives, specifically those aimed at modulating infant vocalizations: 44% fewer were directed at the vocalizations of the rural than the urban group.

The urban and rural groups also differed in number of infant vocalizations. The former produced on average 23% more utterances than the latter and also showed more intragroup differences. The most vocal urban infant produced more than double the number of vocalizations than the most vocal rural infant (see Figure 2). Lastly, rural‐situated caregivers presented 30% more instances than urban caregivers of speaking on behalf of the infant.

Contingent Speech

Instances of contingent speech were present in all 10 recordings, 529 in total, of which 264 (50%) were responses to the infant's actions and the other 265 to vocalizations. Examples of responses to vocalizations and to actions are provided in Appendix A. Of all the vocalizations by the infants (2, 445 in total), 11% were followed by responses from others. That is, almost 90% of all infant vocalizations did not elicit responses. Both groups had instances of contingent speech, but the rural group received 10% more than the urban group.

Speaking on Behalf of the Infant

During transcription, what seemed like instances of people speaking as if they were the infant while addressing the infant was noticed. These instances had the typically reported features of a CDS register (a slower pace, lengthened vowels, etc.) but were produced by the caregivers as though it were the infant speaking. Discussion

Types of Utterances in the CDS

This study examined the input provided to 5‐month‐olds in low‐SES Afrikaans‐speaking households in a community in South Africa, posing three research questions. Regarding the first, “What is the nature of CDS in low‐SES, Afrikaans‐speaking households in terms of utterance types?,” an average of 944.5 instances of CDS occurred per hour, and CDS constituted over three‐quarters of speech produced overall. Regarding utterance types present in the CDS, about a 10th were exclamations, with the other 90% distributed almost equally across declaratives, imperatives, and questions. In this CDS, there was an average of 52.9 instances of contingent speech per hour, half thereof produced in reaction to the infant's (non‐verbal) actions and the other half to infant vocalizations.

For minority‐world communities, the number of declaratives and questions used in CDS is typically reported to exceed the number of imperatives (e.g., Newport et al. 1979, for English). In contrast, some descriptions of CDS in majority‐world contexts have stated that CDS includes many imperatives and fewer declaratives (e.g., Brown 1998; Gaskins 2000, for Mayan culture; Pye 1986, for Guatemala; Rabain‐Jamin 2001, for rural Senegal). De Leon (1998) found that in the Zinacantán culture of the central highlands of Mexico, declaratives, questions and imperative occurred at a rate of 17%, 15%, and 68% in CDS, respectively; that is, imperative constructions formed the bulk of the language directed at the children. Some studies in rural Africa and Guatemala also found that approximately 50% of utterances in CDS were imperatives, that is, far exceeding declaratives and questions (Pye 1986, for Guatemala; Rabain‐Jamin 2001, for rural Senegal). The findings of our study did, however, not concur with those previously reported for caregivers from some majority‐world backgrounds (or even low‐SES background), namely that they use fewer declaratives and more imperatives in CDS. Rather, the number of declaratives and imperatives in the CDS in our study was equal (at 30%). Note, however, that infant participants in our study were 5‐month‐olds, and the observed input patterns could change as they grow older (see Clarke‐Stewart and Hevey 1981, for American English). The difference between the results of our study and those of others underline the need to study CDS in many majority‐world contexts rather than generalizing from one majority‐world context to the next.

Overall, our data showed that questions comprised 29% of CDS. Recall that, of these, 38% were wh‐questions, 25% yes/no questions, 22% eliciting questions, 4% information checking questions, and 11% instances of the infant's name used with rising intonation. In a review of CDS as a feature of some socialization practices, Soderstrom (2007) found that interrogative utterances formed a large part of CDS, especially yes/no questions. However, in our study, questions were not found to be the most common sentence construction in CDS, and yes/no questions (at an average of 61.7 instances per hour) were not the most prevalent question type–wh‐questions were (92.2 instances per hour); however, yes/no questions did comprise the second largest category. This is in contrast with what had been found in certain other majority‐world contexts, such as in Samoan and Luo communities where children are rarely asked yes/no questions (Blount and Padgug 1977). Blount and Padgug (1977) state that this could be because a yes/no question asks for an “opinion or observation from the addressee (in this case, a child) which implies that the interlocutors are equal” (p, 331), and in Samoan and Luo communities, children are not seen as adults' equals. From the studies discussed here and the findings of our study, it is evident that variation in CDS between majority‐world communities is too vast for making blanket statements regarding how caregivers in the majority‐world verbally interact with young children.

Urban‐Rural Comparison

The second research question was whether there are differences 11 between urban and rural households in terms of number of utterances, utterance types, and instances of contingent speech in CDS. This question was posed because previous studies (e.g., Vogt et al. 2015; Yang 2021) reported that urban‐ versus rural‐situatedness can affect CDS content. Although there were some differences in our study between urban and rural households in terms of the number of questions, exclamations, imperatives, and instances of contingent speech, the overall number of utterances in the CDS did not differ extensively between the two geographic locations. In both urban and rural households, there were instances of contingent speech (to be discussed below) but more so in rural households.

The data from our study did not show as great a difference between geographic groups as Vogt et al.'s (2015) did, with our urban infants hearing only 0.4% more utterances in CDS than rural infants. Regarding utterance types, there were differences between the groups in terms of questions, imperatives, and exclamations. Twenty‐two percent more questions were directed at rural infants, as well as 12% fewer imperatives and 19% fewer exclamations. The overall mean number of imperatives across the groups was 22.5 per hour for rural and 28.4 for urban infants (a 12% intergroup difference). However, between the imperatives directed at infant vocalizations, there was a larger difference. Rural infants had 44% fewer imperatives directed at their vocalizations than their urban counterparts. Therefore, although there were differences, it is recommended that this study be repeated with a larger sample size before drawing conclusions regarding rural and urban contexts.

Contingent Speech

The third research question was regarding contingent speech and the overall proportion of speech being child‐directed. CDS comprised 76% of overall speech produced, a considerable amount. This large volume of CDS is not in line with what has been reported for some other majority‐world communities, in which CDS is minimal (see Blount 1969; Crago et al. 1993; Cristia et al. 2019; Geiger and Alant 2005; Ochs 1988; Pye 1986; Schieffelin 1985).

Regarding contingent speech, several instances (529 in total) were found for both geographic groups combined; however, 10% more contingent speech were directed at rural than urban infants. Relatedly, more exclamations were found in the CDS in urban than in rural households. Some but not all exclamations were contingent speech–for instance, if an infant sneezed and the interlocutor said “Oh!” in response, “Oh!” is both an exclamation and an instance of contingent speech. This finding should be viewed against the fact that rural infants collectively produced 946 vocalizations whereas urban infants produced 1,499; at least for CDS contingent on child vocalizations, interlocutors in urban households thus had more opportunity to use contingent speech, yet those in rural households did so more frequently. Of the 1,499 infant vocalizations in the urban data, only 108 were reacted to with contingent speech; for the 946 vocalizations from rural infants, 157 were followed by a contingent utterance. Our analysis of contingent speech in this paper is simply quantitative and does not go beyond subcategorization based on whether it was contingent on actions or vocalizations. Future research entailing qualitative analysis of these instances of contingent speech is recommended.

Speaking on Behalf of the Infant

During transcription of the recordings, evidence of an unexpected type of CDS emerged, namely instances of caregivers speaking to the infant but at the same time speaking in the first person as if on the infant's behalf. As stated further above, these instances showed often‐reported features of a CDS register and were directed at the infant. For the 10 infants, there were collectively 513 instances of speaking on behalf of the infant, of which 55% occurred in rural households.

This type of CDS has not been widely reported. One example of a similar type of CDS is found in De Leon's (1998) study amongst Zinacantán communities (n = 2), where children were at times “embedded” into CDS by inserting them as virtual speakers. An example of what De Leon (1998) refers to as a “she said” frame (p. 140) is where the caregiver reports on what the child is doing or presumably wants by saying, “She says, ‘Arrr!’”. In our data, the child is also embedded into the conversation but more directly–for example, instead of “She wants milk”, it would be “I want milk, Mommy”. That this type of CDS was found in all households indicates that it could be common in low‐SES, Afrikaans‐speaking communities. A larger‐scale study is needed to determine whether this is in fact a prevalent element in this community's language practices.

One other community exhibited a practice somewhat similar to the practice of speaking as if they were the child reported in this paper. In their research amongst the Kaluli people in Papua New Guinea, Ochs and Schieffelin (2009) found that adults did not often directly address infants, but when older children addressed an infant, the mother will respond “for” the infant, using a high‐pitched, nasalized voice. This type of interaction continues until the child is 4–6 months old. The mother still uses language that is well‐formed and suited for an older child when speaking for the infant, and it is only the high pitch and nasality that mark the utterance as “the infant's”. These interactions are primarily used to strengthen relationships between older children and the infant and are not based or dependent on anything the infant might initiate (Ochs and Schieffelin 2009).

Instances of speaking on behalf of the infant observed in the Afrikaans‐speaking community did not always have a clear reason for occurring (unlike in the Kaluli community). Such instances could be meant as vocabulary‐building exercises and/or a model for turn‐taking. Regardless of the reason for engaging in this practice, its observed frequency of occurrence suggests that it is not a random practice in this community.

Limitations of the Study

Most of the limitations of this study are related to the small sample size (which limited the generalizability of the findings) and the cross‐sectional nature of the data.

This study was solely quantitative and would have benefitted from an additional qualitative analysis of the contingent speech data. Specifically, the types of infant actions and vocalizations eliciting a contingent response from the interlocuter should be examined.

Participants were recorded once, only when the infant was 5 months old. Because input can change over time (Behrens 2006), the findings of this study cannot necessarily be generalized to older children.

As with all studies which require video or audio recordings, it is a challenge to capture truly naturalistic interactions while any sort of recording is occurring as the presence of a recording device (or recording person, for that matter) could influence the language used by household members. In this study, these effects were reduced by having the household members themselves record their interactions and by allowing them to do so over the course of a day if they so preferred, thereby ensuring the participants were only recording when they were comfortable.

Given these limitations, the findings cannot be claimed to be representative of all low‐SES Afrikaans‐speaking communities and of households with infants of a range of ages. This emphasizes the need for larger‐scale studies on CDS in similar communities.

Conclusion

Our findings lend support to the existence of vast differences in child language input and socialization practices across majority‐world contexts. The implication of our findings is that research on CDS should be diversified by means of studies of majority‐world contexts, and premature generalizations should be avoided. To this effect, the findings of this study–the first of its kind amongst Afrikaans speakers–contribute to the diversification of existing knowledge on child language input. Given the small scale of this study and its exploratory nature, its findings need to be interpreted with caution and should be tested against those of larger‐sample studies of the language practices in these communities. Also, Vogt et al. (2015) found that from age 1;1 12 to 1; 6, the number of utterances in CDS addressed to infants in the Netherlands and rural Mozambique increased significantly, but not in urban Mozambique. Longitudinal studies of urban and rural Afrikaans‐speaking households will show if such an increase in CDS also exists in Afrikaans‐speaking communities. If so, explanations for the increase should be sourced. Such research will contribute to increased representation of the socialization practices of the majority world and the knowledge on which theories of CDS draw.
