High-voltage and high-safety lithium-ion batteries enabled by nonflammable electrolyte with enhanced Li+ desolvation behavior
Authors: Jiahe Chen, Fangkun Li, Lei Xi, Shiman He, Linwei Zhao, Jun Zeng, Jinpeng Lin, Zhaoyu Sun, Conghui Zhang, Yihong Tong, Chenchen Li, Zhijun Wu, Hongge Pan, Jingwei Zhao, Min Zhu, Jun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnhancing high-capacity silicon-graphite negative electrodes and high-voltage positive electrodes is crucial for improving specific energy of lithium-ion batteries. However, challenges like large volume change in silicon-graphite electrode and electrolyte decomposition at high voltage compromise electrochemical stability. Furthermore, sluggish Li+ exchange between electrolytes and electrodes severely hinders electrochemical kinetics. Herein, we design an anion-rich electrolyte with high oxidation resistance and rapid desolvation behavior, which enabling the formation of effective interphases for improving electrochemical performance. The optimized electrolyte allows silicon-graphite electrode to achieve 1838.9 mAh g−1 capacity at 0.1 C with a long-term electrochemical stability, while a lithium-rich layered oxide positive electrode maintains 72.3% retention after 600 cycles at 1 C. The designed electrolyte with nonflammability also enhances safety in pouch cells after nail penetration. This design significantly improves cycling stability in high-specific-energy batteries, reaching specific energy of 278 Wh kg−1 for graphite | |lithium-rich layered oxide and 355 Wh kg−1 for silicon-graphite | |Ni-rich layer oxide pouch cells, calculated based on all cell components (positive electrode, negative electrode, separator and electrolyte). The design of the high-voltage electrolyte introduces a feasible approach to formulate a suitable solvation structure for high-specific-energy batteries.Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries, Batteries

Introduction

Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have been considered the major component of large-scale energy storage stations and electric vehicles1,2. Particularly, the manufacturing scale of graphite | |LiFePO4 batteries has gradually expanded due to their low cost and high performance3,4. Nevertheless, the specific energy of graphite | |LiFePO4 batteries is inhibited by both the low-capacity positive electrode and negative electrode and does not meet the urgent demand for increasing specific energy in modern society5,6. Therefore, the pursuit of alternative electrodes with high capacity and suitable working voltage is critical for developing next-generation and high-specific-energy LIBs7–9. Simultaneously, the matched component and technology of LIBs should be innovated and updated.

As promising candidates in building next-generation LIBs with high specific energy, lithium-rich layered oxide materials, xLi2MnO3·(1-x)LiMO2 (LRLO, M=Ni, Co, Mn, etc.) can expand voltage window up to 4.8 V and deliver high specific capacity (>250 mAh g−1) due to the extra capacity from oxygen anionic redox reaction triggered by high voltage10–12. Nevertheless, the anionic redox reaction is responsible for irreversible lattice oxygen release, voltage hysteresis, and structure evolution during cycling, thereby impeding the commercialization of LRLO electrode materials13–15. In addition, another obstacle to the commercial application of LRLO is that commercial carbonate electrolytes are susceptible to attack by highly oxidized species at the LRLO interface, resulting in severe electrolyte decomposition and interfacial instability16–18. Meanwhile, efforts to enhance the specific capacity of negative electrodes have been achieved by doping Si elements into graphite to create silicon-graphite (Si@C) electrodes19,20. Silicon, a low-cost and earth-abundant element, offers a higher theoretical specific capacity (4200 mAh g−1) and a suitable reduction potential (0.2 V vs Li/Li+), making it a promising candidate to partially replace graphite in electrode materials and create a range of Si@C materials with specific capacities range from 450 to 2000 mAh g−1. However, the implementation of Si@C electrodes is plagued by their large volume change and unstable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) during Li-intercalation and de-intercalation processes21. Typically, the SEI derived from carbonate electrolytes can tolerate the strain caused by 4% volume expansion of graphite, but it tends to break easily once the Si doping in graphite22,23. The continuous electrolyte decomposition and SEI formation render poor cycling performance of Si@C electrodes24,25. These detrimental effects on Si@C electrodes become increasingly apparent as Si content increases21. In addition, the large volume expansion leads to electrode pulverization and electric disconnection, which eventually results in cell failure26. Eventually, it is important to highlight the common challenges associated with high-capacity electrodes, specifically the LRLO electrode and Si@C electrode. The interface of high-capacity electrodes will experience a high Li+ flux exchange17. This implies that efficient Li+ transportation within the electrolyte system and rapid Li+ desolvation on the electrode surface are essential for minimizing the polarization of batteries and fully utilizing the capacity27,28.

Electrolyte engineering is considered one of the most promising and crucial strategies to address these problems of both positive and negative electrodes29,30. This is because the direct contact between electrolyte and electrode makes the electrolyte a key factor influencing interfacial formation and the kinetics of charge transport. Commercial carbonate electrolytes with cyclic and linear components (ethylene carbonate, dimethyl carbonate, etc.) exhibit poor anti-oxidant performance and form an unstable interphase on electrodes, leading to incompatibility with high-voltage LRLO electrodes and high-capacity Si@C electrodes31,32. To tackle these challenges, among many candidates for electrolyte systems, high-concentration electrolytes (HCE)33,34, localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCE)35,36, and weakly solvated electrolytes (WSE)37,38 have been widely reported for their effectiveness in enhancing electrochemical performance. Zheng et al. formulated a nonflammable LHCE electrolyte, 2 M lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) in 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl methyl carbonate (FEMC): 1H,1H,5H-octafluoroamyl-1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroether (OTE) in 1:1 by volume, to form a robust and stable F-rich inorganic-organic bilayer solid-electrolyte interphase on Si microparticle electrodes39. The FEMC-based LHCE electrolyte with high anodic stability enabled cycling stability of high-specific-energy batteries, coupling with high-capacity macro-Si electrodes and high-voltage LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM811) or LiNi0.5Mn1.5O4 electrodes. Additionally, to resolve the challenges of the interphases reformation after huge electrode swelling and cracks, Wang et al. proposed a high-voltage electrolyte containing 1 M lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6) in fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC): sulfolane (SL): 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethylene-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) with volume ratio of 2: 6: 2 to enhance the electrochemical performance of micro-sized silicon electrodes24. The designed electrolyte built silicon-phobic interphases on lithium-silicon alloys, which enabled the integrity of macro-sized Si electrodes after repeated cycles. The high-voltage electrolyte also showed high feasibility in Si | |LiNi0.8Co0.15Al0.05O2 pouch cell and provided an effective way to realize the commercialization of LIBs with Si electrodes. However, the reported studies primarily focus on the effective SEI formation and overlook the process of Li+ transport from the electrolyte to the electrode40,41. Specifically, Li+ desolvation on the electrodes takes place on high-capacity Si electrodes during the charging process and on LRLO electrodes during the discharging process of the batteries.

Herein, we present a nonflammable and high-voltage electrolyte for LIBs through formulating the solvation configuration of the electrolyte. This work selected SL with a moderate dielectric constant and dimethyl carbonate (DMC) as the main solvents, while introducing difluoro ethylene carbonate (DFEC) additive to modify the interphase formation. The adjustable solvation structure was achieved by decreasing the SL content to form P-SDD electrolyte (1 M LiPF6 in SL: DMC: DFEC with a 2: 6: 2 volume ratio). Additionally, lithium tetrafluoroborate (LiBF4) with strong association of BF4- anions can directly enter the inner solvation shell to form anion-rich solvation structure and weaken the Li+-solvents interaction. Thereby, 0.2 M LiBF4 was added to the P-SDD electrolyte to form the PB-SDD electrolyte with rapid Li+ desolvation property. Eventually, we demonstrate that PB-SDDF electrolyte, consisting of 1 M LiPF6 and 0.2 M LiBF4 in a mixture of SL: DMC: DFEC: ethoxy(pentafluoro)cyclotriphosphazene (PFPN) (2: 4: 2: 2 by volume), features an anion-rich inner solvation for rapid Li+ desolvation and anti-oxidized outer solvation. The PB-SDDF electrolyte is designed to facilitate the Li+ desolvation process on Si@C and LRLO electrodes, prompting the electrochemical reaction kinetics. Moreover, the PB-SDDF electrolyte enables the formation of effective interphases on both electrodes, thereby enabling high electrochemical performance of high-capacity Si@C electrodes and high-voltage LRLO electrodes. Particularly, Si@C electrodes with PB-SDDF electrolyte deliver 1838.9 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C and maintain a high capacity retention of 74.9 over 300 cycles at 0.3 C. The PB-SDDF electrolyte enables LRLO electrodes to retain 72.3% capacity after 600 cycles at 1 C, while also ensuring long-term cycling stability at temperatures of −10 and 45 °C. The nonflammable PB-SDDF electrolyte ensures enhanced safety in the pouch cell during the nail penetration test, preventing any thermal runaway. Based on all cell components (positive electrode, negative electrode, separator, and electrolyte), the graphite | |LRLO and Si@C | | NCM pouch cells using the PB-SDDF electrolyte achieve high specific energy of 278 and 355 Wh kg−1, respectively, along with an enhanced cycling stability.

Results

Li+ solvation structure with fast Li+ desolvation behavior

To develop an optimal electrolyte system for next-generation LIBs with high specific energy, we investigated the challenges associated with the interaction between Li+ and other electrolyte components. Commonly, the solvents, with a high dielectric constant, have good lithium salt dissolution properties and show strong interaction with Li+ in the electrolyte, which domain the Li+ solvation structure (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1, and Supplementary Data 1). For reducing the viscosity, linear solvents with moderate Li+ binding energy were introduced to modulate the Li+ coordination environment. Currently, fluorinated solvents show weak or non-solvating capacity and are used as bifunctional additives32,42. Based on this, we designed an electrolyte system featuring fast Li+ desolvation kinetics and good oxidation resistance. This work selected SL as a solvent, due to its moderate constant dielectric constant and high safety, to substitute the EC component in the commercial carbonate electrolytes36,43 (Supplementary Fig. 2). And the amount of SL was controlled in a low volume ratio to enhance Li+ desolvation behavior. The electrochemical performances in LRLO electrodes and Si@C electrodes illustrate that the reducing amount of SL in electrolyte benefit for deliver high reversible capacity (Supplementary Fig. 3). In addition, to further weaken the interaction between Li+ and solvents, the strategies of anion substitution and weakly solvated solvent introduction were proposed. Compared to LiPF6, the Li+-BF4-, with lower binding energy, encourages the incorporation of the BF4- anion into Li+ solvation structure, thereby weakening Li+-solvent interaction (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data 1). The optimal concentration of LiBF4 was adjusted at 0.2 mol L−1 based on the electrochemical performance of LRLO electrode and Si@C electrode (Supplementary Fig. 4). The addition of weakly solvated solvents (DFEC and PFPN) with high oxidation resistance forms an outer solvation structure that compresses the inner solvation shell, prompting more anion coordination, while also offering flame-retardant properties (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 5, and Supplementary Data 2).

To verify the above Li+ solvation process, the radial distribution functions (RDF) and corresponding coordination numbers were obtained from the molecular dynamics (MD) simulation (Fig. 1d−g, Supplementary Fig. 6, and Supplementary Data 3). In the P-SDD electrolyte as a comparison, all the peaks corresponding to Li+-O(SL), Li+-O(DMC), and Li+-P(PF6-) species appear around 0.204 nm, while the coordination number of SL and DMC is about 2.1, larger than that of PF6- (1.32) (Supplementary Table 1). The weakly solvated DFEC solvent shows a large distance of the RDF peak (0.212 nm) and a small coordination number of 0.35. The result demonstrates that the Li+ solvation structure in P-SDD electrolyte is primarily dominated by SL and DMC solvents, while PF6- anion and DFEC are still partially coordinated with Li+ (Supplementary Fig. 6a). In the PB-SDD electrolyte, formed by adding LiBF4 in the P-SDD electrolyte, the positions of the peaks (Li+-solvents and Li+-PF6) remain unchanged, but the initial peak for Li+-F(BF4-) appears at 0.196 nm, which is closer to Li+ nucleus (Fig. 1d, e and Supplementary Table 2). Notably, the total coordination number of solvents decreases from 4.48 to 4.19 after the addition of LiBF4, with a significant reduction in the number of coordinated SL solvents within the Li+ coordination environment of the PB-SDD electrolyte (Fig. 1f). This phenomenon demonstrates that the strong Li+-BF4- interaction can squeeze the coordinated SL out of the inner solvation structure, thereby prompting Li+ desolvation process. Moreover, a Li+ solvation structure with improved desolvation behavior was created by substituting some of the DMC solvents with PFPN solvents. The non-solvated PFPN solvents in outer solvation structure do not participate in Li+ coordination, as evidenced by the absence of peaks in the RDFs of Li+-O(PFPN) and Li+-F(PFPN) (Supplementary Fig. 7). However, they induce a significant change in the inner solvation structure. The tight contact between Li+ and anions (PF6- and BF4-) increases, while the coordination number of SL and DMC solvents decreases. The coordination numbers of PF6- and BF4- increase to 1.41 and 0.39, respectively, indicating more enhanced Li+-anions interaction in the PB-SDDF electrolyte (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Table 3). And the coordination numbers of SL and DMC simultaneously decrease to 1.70 and 1.71, respectively, implying that the Li+-solvents interactions are weakened due to the anions coordinating with Li+ (Fig. 1f, g). The compressed inner solvation structure formed by anion substitution and weakly solvated solvent introduction facilitates the escape of Li+ from the solvation structure on the electrode surface.

Additional insights into the solvation process were obtained through Raman spectroscopy and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8a, typical stretching vibration peaks of -CH2- in SL at 788 cm−1 are observed in the P-SDD electrolyte. After the addition of LiBF4, the new peaks emerged at 783 cm−1 compared to the P-SDD electrolyte, corresponding to coordinated BF4- with Li+44. In the PB-SDDF electrolyte, the Li+-BF4- interaction strengthens due to the addition of non-coordinated PFPN solvent, as indicated by increased intensity of Raman peaks. Furthermore, the absorption band of the free PF6- appears around 843 cm−1, overlapping with the P-F vibration of PFPN solvent45. Simultaneously, the contact ion pairs (CIPs) of Li+-solvent-PF6- can be found at two wide bands at around 860 and 830 cm−1. The increasing intensity of these CIPs in FT-IR spectra suggests that the PF6- anion likely contributes to the formation of the first solvation structure. The coordinating condition of solvents is also confirmed by FT-IR spectra. The free DMC and the coordinated DMC are determined at around 1753 and 1727 cm-1, respectively46. The suppressed peaks of Li+-DMC and the strengthened peaks of free DMC in the PB-SDDF electrolyte indicate a transition of more coordinated DMC to the free state within the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 8b). The same phenomenon occurs in the FT-IR spectroscopy of SL solvents. The peak corresponding to the antisymmetric O = S = O stretching vibration is observed at 1298 cm–1. As the anions progressively take over the first solvation structure, the coordinated SL solvents move away from Li+, resulting in a redshift of the O = S = O stretching vibration peak47. More evidence can be obtained from nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. The 7Li NMR spectra change can confirm the coordination environment change of Li+ resulting from the anion and solvent changes. The more anions with highly electronegative, like PF6- and BF4-, participate in Li+ solvation structure of the PB-SDD and PB-SDDF electrolytes, rendering an increased surrounding electron cloud around the Li+ nucleus and an upfield shift in 7Li NMR spectra (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Also, in the PB-SDD and PB-SDDF electrolytes, the 19F signals of PF6- and BF4- show upshield shifts, indicating the strong interaction of Li+-anions compared to the P-SDD electrolyte45 (Supplementary Fig. 9b and 9c). The designed PB-SDDF electrolyte exhibits suitable ion conductivity (5.86 mS cm−1, Supplementary Fig. 10) and Li+ transfer number (0.67) to ensure the Li+ transportation in the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 11a and 11b). Compared to carbonate electrolyte, the PB-SDDF electrolyte shows stronger oxidation resistance with an electrochemical oxidation window of 5.26 V (Supplementary Fig. 12). Additionally, the PB-SDDF exhibits a reduction in viscosity to 3.17 mPa s−1 and an improved wettability with the separator (Supplementary Fig. 13).

To elucidate the dynamic interfacial evolution of LRLO electrodes and the underlying mechanism of cathode/electrolyte interphase (CEI) formation, in-situ FT-IR spectroscopy was conducted to monitor the real-time structural and compositional changes at the CEI during galvanostatic charge-discharge cycling of LRLO electrodes with different electrolytes. The characteristic vibrational bands were assigned as follows: the asymmetric stretching of O = S = O in SL is located at ~1298 cm−1, the C-O-C stretching vibration of DMC is centered at ~1274 cm−1, and the C-F stretching vibration of DFEC is observed at ~1081 cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 14). The PF6- anion from LiPF6 salt exhibits characteristic absorption in the 800–900 cm−1 region. In the P-SDD electrolyte, the continuous attenuation of PF6- and DFEC characteristic bands during the charging process demonstrates the continuous decomposition of electrolyte components and the formation of a CEI film on the LRLO electrode surface (Fig. 1h). The persistent decomposition of PF6- throughout the charge-discharge cycle, as evidenced by the sustained intensity change of its characteristic band, indicates that the irreversible decomposition of LiPF6 is a major contributor to the electrochemical degradation of LRLO electrodes, including transition metal dissolution, oxygen release, and capacity fading. Upon the introduction of LiBF4 to form the PB-SDD electrolyte, a significant decrease in the intensity of PF6- characteristic absorption was observed in the in-situ FT-IR spectra. This phenomenon confirms the preferential and strong adsorption of BF4- anions on the LRLO electrode surface, which effectively isolates PF6- anions from the high-voltage LRLO electrode and suppresses their oxidative decomposition (Fig. 1i). Concurrently, the gradual attenuation of DMC and DFEC absorption bands during cycling was significantly mitigated, indicating that the BF4--derived CEI film effectively inhibits the continuous decomposition of carbonate solvents. In the PB-SDD electrolyte, the CEI formation is dominated by the preferential decomposition of BF4- anions, with suppressed solvent decomposition. In contrast, in the PB-SDDF electrolyte, the enhanced attenuation of solvent bands and the more pronounced formation of stable CEI components become more obvious, confirming that BF4- anions fundamentally alter the interfacial reaction pathway on LRLO electrodes, promoting the formation of a dense and ion-conductive CEI film that stabilizes the structure of high-voltage LRLO electrode and improves cycling performance (Fig. 1j). The combination of enhanced desolvation kinetics and robust CEI film formation facilitate the Li+ transportation and electrochemical stability of electrodes (Fig. 1k).

Promotion of Li+ desolvation behavior on Si@C electrodes

To provide additional insights into the Li+ desolvation kinetics, temperature-dependent electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed. Li | |Li Symmetric cells were tested over a temperature range from 283 to 303 K without pre-cycling activation in order to prevent interference from the SEI formation (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 15a–15e). In this work, two types of control electrolyte are used: 1 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate (EC): DMC: DFEC with volume ratio of 2: 6: 2 (P-EDD) and 1 M LiPF6 in SL: DMC: DFEC with volume ratio of 7: 1: 2 (P-SDD712). Compared with the Li+ desolvation energy of P-EDD (80.1 kJ mol-1) and P-SDD712 (83.6 kJ mol-1) electrolytes, the lower Li+ desolvation energy of P-SDD (74.2 kJ mol−1) is attributed to the reduced content of solvents with high dielectric constant. The addition of LiBF4, which exhibits strong ion-ion interactions, allows for adjustment of the inner solvation structure, resulting in a coordinated environment for Li+ with strong Li+-anion interaction and weak Li+-solvent interaction. This adjustment helps to gradually reduce the Li+ desolvation energy in the PB-SDD electrolyte (73.7 kJ mol−1). Furthermore, the non-coordinated solvent PFPN prompts Li+ coordination with BF4- and PF6-, leading to the formation of an anion-rich solvation structure. PB-SDDF electrolyte, with the lowest desolvation energy of 70.2 kJ mol−1, accelerates the detachment of Li+ from the solvation structure at the electrode interface.

The kinetics of Li+ desolvation at the electrode have a significant impact on the capacity of Si@C electrodes, particularly for high-capacity Si@C electrodes. During the Li+ intercalation and deintercalation process in high-capacity Si@C electrodes, rapid Li+ exchange takes place within the restricted interface or space between electrolyte and electrode. The initial discharge-charge curves of the Li | |Si@C half cells with different electrolytes at a current density of 0.1 C in the potential window of 0.005-2 V are presented in Fig. 2b. For the electrochemical tests of Si@C electrodes, the 1 C rate is defined as 1800 mA g−1, corresponding to a charge/discharge duration of 60 min. Si@C electrode exhibits a large polarization and a low discharge capacity of 756.5 mAh g−1 (0.1 C) in the P-SDD712 with high Li+ desolvation energy. Additionally, a low reversible capacity of 606.5 mAh g–1 is released from the Si@C electrode with a low Coulombic efficiency (CE) of 80.2% at 0.1 C. After the rational electrolyte manipulation, the Li | |Si@C half cell using PB-SDDF electrolyte demonstrates a high initial capacity of 1838.9 mAh g–1 with an initial CE of 90.3% at 0.1 C, outperforming those using P-EDD (1492.5 mAh g–1, 90.8%) and P-SDD262 (1567.5 mAh g–1, 90.2%) electrolyte. The cycling performance of Si@C electrodes with different electrolytes at the current density of 0.3 C is shown in Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 16. A low capacity observed in the Si@C electrode with P-SDD712 electrolyte indicates slow electrochemical kinetics. And the Si@C electrode with P-EDD electrolyte experiences rapid capacity fading with a capacity retention of 40.0% after 100 cycles. The results can be interpreted through the structural evolution observed in the subsequent characterization. In comparison, the optimal PB-SDDF electrolyte enables a stable and long-term performance of the Si@C electrode compared to P-SDD and PB-SDD electrolytes. After 300 cycles, the Si@C electrode with PB-SDDF electrolyte retains a reversible capacity of 1239.1 mAh g–1 and a high capacity retention of 74.9% at 0.3 C. The rate performance demonstrate that the Si@C electrode in the PB-SDDF electrolyte deliver a high capacity of 1246.8 mAh g–1 at 1 C (Supplementary Fig. 17). Additionally, Fig. 2d reveals the cycling performance of Si@C electrode with the P-EDD and PB-SDDF electrolyte at a lager current density of 0.5 C. A reversible capacity of 1311.6 mAh g–1 was retained, corresponding to 78.4% capacity retention after 300 cycles at 0.5 C, which is much better than the performance of Si@C electrode cycled in the P-EDD electrolyte with a reversible capacity of 1060.8 mAh g–1 and a low capacity retention of 62.3% after 170 cycles. Furthermore, high mass loading (2.5 mg cm–2) Si@C electrodes are used to demonstrate the versatility of the PB-SDDF electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 18). The Li | |Si@C cell with the PB-SDDF electrolyte delivers a reversible capacity of 1125.5 mAh g–1 with a high capacity retention of 85.3% over 200 cycles at 0.3 C. Reviewing the electrolyte engineering research for Si-based electrode, PB-SDDF electrolyte ensures a good electrochemical performance of Si-based electrode with a specific capacity over 1800 mAh g-1 and high mass loading (Supplementary Table 4).

To investigate the correlation between the Li+ desolvation process and electrochemical reaction kinetics of Si@C electrode, the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) was performed on the initial cycle of Li | |Si@C half cells with P-EDD and PB-SDDF electrolytes, involving charge/discharge at 0.1 C with a 10-min pulse time followed by a 1-h relaxation period. As illustrated in Fig. 2f, during the discharge process of Si@C electrode, greater polarization is observed in the Si@C electrode using P-EDD electrolyte compared to PB-SDDF electrolyte. The same phenomenon occurs during the charge process of Si@C electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 19). The calculated Li+ diffusion coefficient (DLi+) based on the discharge-charge curves is presented in Fig. 2e. The higher DLi+ of the Si@C electrode in PB-SDDF electrolyte indicates enhanced reaction kinetics, likely resulting from the rapid Li+ desolvation at the electrolyte/electrode interface.

The effect of the designed electrolyte on electrochemical kinetics was further validated through an analysis of the distribution of relaxation times (DRT), derived from in-situ EIS data corresponding to different states of discharge/charge curves. DRT analysis differentiates various electrochemical processes based on their time dependence, enabling a deeper understanding of electrochemical reactions48. In Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21, the DRT curves generally exhibit three main kinetic processes, including the impedance of Li+ passing through the interfacial film (RSEI), charge transfer (Rct), and Li+ diffusion in the electrodes (RD)15,49,50. In the initial discharge process, the parasitic SiOx and the newly formed SEI film will obstruct the movement of Li+ across the interface of Si@C electrodes. The higher RSEI value observed during the whole discharge process of Si@C electrode cycled in P-SDD712 electrolyte may be attributed to the formation of high interfacial resistance resulting from SL decomposition. Interestingly, in Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 21, DRT mapping of Li | |Si@C half cells in both P-EDD and P-SDD712 electrolyte reveals enhanced peaks in the charge transfer region throughout the entire process of Li+ intercalation into Si@C particles, suggesting a challenging electrochemical reaction. Typically, the Li+ intercalation into Si@C particles denotes the transfer of Li+ from electrolyte to electrode, a process that includes Li+ desolvation from electrolyte and charge exchange at the electrode interface. The presence of high-dielectric-constant solvents in the electrolyte increases the resistance to Li+ desolvation from the solvation structure, thereby further delaying the electrochemical reaction. After substituting SL with a large amount of low-dielectric-constant DMC or introducing LiBF4 to construct P-SDD and PB-SDD electrolyte, the relaxation time distribution in the charge transfer region exhibits a decreasing trend as Li+ intercalation processes, suggesting an acceleration of charge transfer at the Si@C interface and a reduction in the impedance of electrochemical performance (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 21b). Although the DRT curves of the Li | |Si@C half cell with PB-SDDF electrolyte show strong peaks in the charge transfer region at the voltage above 0.4 V vs. Li/Li+, associated with the initial SEI formation, the suppressed peaks below 0.2 V vs. Li/Li+ indicate rapid charge transfer during the plateau of Li+ intercalation (Fig. 2i). Notably, the Li+ de-intercalation process of Si@C electrode can be improved with the PB-SDDF electrolyte, which shows smaller peaks in the RSEI and Rct regions compared to P-SDD712 and P-EDD electrolytes. This implies that the designed PB-SDDF electrolyte is characterized by rapid Li+ desolvation properties, which enhance Li+ transportation and, consequently, improve the electrochemical kinetics of large-capacity Si@C electrodes.

Electrochemical performance of LRLO electrodes

The comparative electrochemical performance of Li | |LRLO half cells using various electrolytes has been examined to evaluate the compatibility of different electrolytes and LRLO electrodes. As for the electrochemical tests of LRLO electrodes, the rate of 1 C is set as 250 mA g–1. The Li | |LRLO half cell with the P-EDD electrolyte exhibits a low discharge capacity of 283.3 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C, yet shows a high Coulombic efficiency of 91.1% (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 22a). Notably, the discharge process of LRLO electrodes involves the intercalation of Li+ ions into the LRLO crystal structure, occurring after the desolvation of Li+ ions from the electrolyte. Therefore, the substitution with low-dielectric-constant solvents can improve the reversible discharge capacity of LRLO, like Li | |LRLO cells with P-SDD712 (288.9 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C) and P-SDD262 (301.0 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C) electrolytes. However, attention should be given to the prevention of electrolyte decomposition, particularly at the high voltage of 4.8 V. With the addition of LiBF4 and PFPN additives, the LRLO electrode cycled in PB-SDDF electrolyte demonstrates a high reversible capacity of 292.5 mAh g−1 and a CE of 90.6% at 0.1 C. As shown in Fig. 3b, the peak at around 4.2 V in cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves represents the deintercalation of Li+ ions from the LiMO2 layered structure, while the peak at around 4.7 V corresponds to the anion redox reaction and electrolyte decomposition. Consistent with the results for reversible capacity and initial CE, the CV curve of Li | |LRLO with PB-SDDF electrolyte displays a left-shift peak for Li+ deintercalation and a suppressed peak for the anion redox reaction, indicating fast interfacial kinetics and reduced electrolyte decomposition. The improved kinetics in the PB-SDDF system are further validated by GITT tests (Fig. 3c). The Li+ diffusion coefficients of the LRLO electrode during the charging process typically exhibit a high value during the rapid Li+ deintercalation process and a low value during the sluggish anion redox reaction. This suggests the low transformation of Li2MnO3, accompanied by the formation of highly oxidized O-/O2- species11. In addition, the Li+ diffusion coefficients demonstrate a decreasing trend during discharge. The diffusion coefficients of LRLO in PB-SDDF electrolyte are slightly higher than those in P-EDD electrolyte, indicating enhanced kinetics during the charging process, which is consistent with the CV results. The PB-SDDF electrolyte notably facilitates the Li+ diffusion in the LRLO crystal structure after the Li+ desolvation from the solvation structure. This effect can be attributed to the rapid electrochemical kinetics occurring at the electrolyte/electrode interface.

The comparative electrochemical performance of the Li | |LRLO half cells with various electrolytes has been evaluated within a voltage window of 2–4.8 V and at a 1 C symmetric charge/discharge rate (Fig. 3d). An enhancement in performance is observed for cells with the P-SDD262 electrolyte compared to the P-EDD electrolyte, although the capacity retention of 75.5% after 250 cycles at 0.1 C remains unsatisfactory (Supplementary Fig. 22d). This improvement is attributed to the prevention of catalytic decomposition of EC components at the LRLO interfaces. With the formation of a more stable interface, the Li | |LRLO cell using the PB-SDDF electrolyte demonstrates significantly more stable cycling performance under 4.8 V, retaining 72.3% of its capacity (241.5 mAh g−1) after 600 cycles at 1 C (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 22f). In contrast, the cells with P-EDD and P-SDD712 electrolytes experience a sharp capacity loss, failing to maintain measurable capacity after 250 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 22b-22c).

Voltage hysteresis and capacity degradation of LRLO during cycling are the primary factors responsible for the loss of its electrochemical activity. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the changes in redox couples throughout the cycling process to identify the main cause of capacity degradation in LRLO. The differential capacity (dQ/dV) curves of LRLO cycled in P-EDD and PB-SDDF electrolytes over 250 cycles at 1 C are presented in Fig. 3f, g. The capacity contribution in the early stages of cycling is primarily attributed to the oxidation and reduction of oxygen (O2-/On- at ~4.5 V) and nickel (Ni3+/Ni4+ at ~4.0 V)17,51. During further cycling, the activation of the manganese (Mn3+/Mn4+ at 3.0 ~ 3.5 V) and cobalt (Co3+/Co4+ at 4.0 V) redox couples compensates for the capacity loss caused by irreversible oxygen and nickel migration. The Mn3+/Mn4+ oxidation peaks of the LRLO electrode with P-EDD electrolyte shift to higher voltages, while the reduction peak shifts to lower voltages during cycling. Additionally, the intensity of the manganese redox couple gradually decreases and eventually disappears, which may be due to the Mn dissolution and continuous phase transition that hinders redox activity. The LRLO with PB-SDDF electrolyte still maintains the reversible Mn3+/Mn4+ redox reaction during the long-term cycling, indicating the highly reversible electrochemical activity.

To verify the interfacial stability of LRLO electrodes in various electrolytes during cycling, EIS spectra was conducted at different cycling states (Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24). Nyquist plots consist of several components, including the solution resistance (R1), surface film resistance (RCEI), charge transfer resistance (Rct), and Warburg impedance (Zw). As shown in Fig. 3h and Supplementary Table 5, during the early stages of cycling, such as the 50th cycle, the LRLO with P-EDD electrolyte exhibits a small RCEI and a large Rct, indicating a sluggish charge transfer due to spinel phase formation. As the cycling processes, more side reactions and severe collapse of the LRLO structure render an increase in both RCEI and Rct. However, the stable interface formed on the LRLO electrode cycled in PB-SDDF electrolyte prevents the rise in RCEI and effectively maintains a low impedance of charge transfer throughout the subsequent cycles.

To investigate the rate performance of different electrolytes, a symmetric charge/discharge process of 2 C rate was applied in the cycling performance test of Li | |LRLO half cells (Fig. 3i). The Li | |LRLO cells with the PB-SDDF electrolyte deliver a discharge capacity of 215.5 mAh g−1, higher than that of the P-EDD electrolyte (207.6 mAh g-1) and P-SDD712 electrolyte (149.4 mAh g–1). Additionally, Li | |LRLO cells with the PB-SDDF electrolyte demonstrate robust stability, achieving 72.3% capacity retention over 400 cycles at 2 C. To illustrate the compatibility of PB-SDDF electrolyte with other positive electrode materials, the PB-SDDF electrolyte was adapted to high-voltage NCM811 with a cut-off voltage of 4.6 V (Fig. 3j). As for the electrochemical tests of NCM811 electrodes, the rate of 1 C is set as 200 mA g–1. The P-EDD electrolyte experiences severe decomposition due to the breakdown of carbonate solvent at the highly catalyzed interface with the transition metal. Therefore, rapid capacity degradation is observed in the electrochemical performance of Li | |LRLO half cell with the P-EDD electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 25a). However, the enhanced cycling performance of the LRLO electrode cycled in the PB-SDDF electrolyte exhibits a high residual capacity of 171.9 mAh g−1 with a capacity retention of 80.9% over 500 cycles at 1 C. To highlight the importance of the well-developed PB-SDDF electrolyte in the LRLO electrode, the relevant references published in recent years have been summarized in Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 6. The PB-SDDF electrolyte enables the LRLO electrode to maintain high capacity retention during 200–300 cycles, or to exhibit a high capacity for more than 600 cycles. The long-term cycling stability offered by this designed electrolyte is competitive with that of recently reported electrolytes for high-voltage LIBs.

Interfacial component and structural evolution of electrodes

The chemical composition of the SEI film was analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) with an Ar+ sputtering depth profiling. As shown in Fig. 4a, c, the top surface of the SEI on Si@C electrodes, cycled in both P-EDD and PB-SDDF electrolytes, was composed of both organic (ROLi) and inorganic components (LiCO3, LiF). The ratio of specific elements of Li and O remains consistent on the surface of Si@C electrodes cycled in both electrolytes. However, the SEI film formed by the PB-SDDF electrolyte contains fewer C elements and more F elements compared to the SEI film formed by the P-EDD electrolyte (Fig. 4b, d). With increasing sputtering time, the ratio of C elements in the SEI film derived from the P-EDD electrolyte increases, while the ratio of residual elements decreases. This indicates that solvent decomposition becomes aggravated during the repeated discharge/charge processes of Si@C electrodes in P-EDD electrolyte. The accumulation of solvent derivative (ROLi, LiCO3, and O-C = O) on the Si@C surface slows down Li+ charge transportation and degrades electrochemical performance in P-EDD electrolyte. Typically, the solvent-derived SEI film lacks sufficient strength to withstand large stress from the expansion of Si@C electrodes, leading to SEI breakage and excessive electrolyte consumption. The morphology and structural features of the Si@C electrode cycled in P-EDD electrolyte were characterized in Fig. 4e. scanning electron microscope (SEM) images confirmed the accumulation of solvent derivatives in P-EDD electrolyte, revealing a rough surface and the pulverization of primary particles. Moreover, the repeated expansion and contraction cause a disconnection between primary particles and conductive reagents, triggering the loss of active materials and rapid capacity degradation. More pores and gaps develop in the internal structure of the Si@C electrode cycled in P-EDD electrolyte. In contrast, the atomic ratio of the C element in the SEI film obtained from the PB-SDDF electrolyte exhibits a decreasing trend, ultimately stabilizing at about 20% with increasing sputter time. The rising levels of Li and F, coupled with the declining levels of O, illustrate that a great amount of LiF is present in the inner SEI film, while a reduced quantity of Li2CO3 is generated from solvent decomposition. These findings are in agreement with the etched XPS spectra of F, O, Li, and C (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 26b). Additionally, the additional LixByOz inorganic component can easily form on the Si@C electrodes when using the PB-SDDF electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 26c). Based on the above XPS results, a stable film that consists of an inner inorganic layer (LiF, Li2O, and LixByOz) and an outer organic structure is generated on the Si@C electrodes cycled in PB-SDDF electrolyte. The effective SEI film prevents electrolyte decomposition and minimizes side products on the Si@C electrodes, contributing to enhanced electrochemical performance. The Si@C electrodes cycled in the PB-SDDF electrolyte maintain their integrity with a thickness of 15 μm, without any cracks and pores, whereas those cycled in the P-EDD electrolyte exhibit an increased thickness of 28 μm (Fig. 4e, f). Finally, high-resolution transmission electron microscope (TEM) was performed to detect the morphology of the cycled Si@C electrodes in different electrolytes (Supplementary Fig. 27). The loose and uneven SEI film was detected in the Si@C electrodes when cycling in P-SDD electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 27a). Additionally, the different contrast in the TEM image of Si@C particle demonstrates the producing cracks and holes in Si@C particle during cycling. When using PB-SDD electrolyte, a dense SEI film cover on the Si@C particle to inhibit electrolyte corrosion and maintain the integrity of Si@C particle during cycling (Supplementary Fig. 27b). The enhancing structural stability without any defect was found in the Si@C particle cycling in PB-SDDF electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 27c). Therefore, the PB-SDDF enables a stable electrochemical performance in Si@C electrodes.

The composition of CEI film on the LRLO electrodes cycled in different electrolytes was also investigated by the XPS technique. As shown in Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 28, for the C 1 s and O 1 s spectra, similar organic components (C-C, C-O, and C = O) derived from solvent decomposition were detected on the LRLO electrodes cycled in both P-EDD and PB-SDDF electrolytes. However, the O 1 s spectra of LRLO cycled in P-EDD electrolyte exhibit stronger metal-O peaks compared to those in PB-SDDF electrolyte, suggesting the formation of a thin CEI film from P-EDD electrolyte without sufficient protective effect. It can be observed that the inorganic components, such as LiF, LiPxOyFz, LixByOz derived from the decomposition of Lithium salt (LiPF6 and LiBF4), form a robust layer on the LRLO cycled in PB-SDDF electrolyte to reduce continuous electrolyte decomposition. Additionally, to illustrate the mechanism of the film formation, the analysis of chemical component was performed in the LRLO electrodes cycled in different electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 29). The similar CEI component was detected on the surface of LRLO electrodes cycled in P-SDD, PB-SDD, and PB-SDDF electrolytes. The noticeable difference occurs on the decreasing LiPxFy contents and increasing LiPxOyFz contents when using PB-SDDF electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 29d). This indicates the inhibiting decomposition of LiPF6 when adding LiBF4 and PFPN. Additionally, the increasing intensity of LixByOz in B 1 s spectra demonstrates that more LiBF4 participate to form CEI film Supplementary Fig. 29f. In the comparison of the atomic percentage of the CEI film in Supplementary Fig. 29g–29i, the increasing Li content and decreasing C content in the CEI film derived from PB-SDDF electrolyte indicates the formation of more inorganic Li-contained species, which strengthen the mechanical properties of the interface. Furthermore, the Young’s modulus was evaluated by atomic force microscope (AFM) in Supplementary Fig. 30. The CEI formed by PB-SDDF electrolyte exhibits a high Young’s modulus of 13.6 GPa than that of P-SDD electrolyte (7.7 GPa).

To verify the distribution of chemical components in CEI layers formed in different electrolytes, time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) has been employed on the LRLO electrodes cycled in different electrolytes for 50 cycles. The species containing C, H, and O elements, such as CH3O-, are identified as organic components resulting from solvent decomposition, while the characteristic mass-to-charge ratios of 45 and 63 correspond to the inorganic components LiF2- and PO2-, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5b, the amounts of CH3O-, LiF2-, and PO2- decrease once the ion sputtering begins. The horizontal mapping in Fig. 5b of the aforementioned species further confirms that the thickness of the CEI film is insufficient in the P-EDD electrolyte. This observation suggests that the CEI film formed from the P-EDD electrolyte is too thin to withstand the intense oxidation reaction occurring at high-voltage states. However, in the CEI from PB-SDDF electrolyte, the intensity of LiF2- peaks reaches its maximum before gradually decreasing (Fig. 5c). Additionally, the initial intensities of CH3O- and LiF2- are stronger than those in the P-EDD electrolyte. Notably, the CEI film from the PB-SDDF electrolyte consists of another effective inorganic component, BO2- and SO2-, to inhibit further electrolyte decomposition and structural collapse (Supplementary Fig. 31). This indicates that an effective CEI film, with a well-designed structure and appropriate thickness, is essential for creating a stable interface and ensuring long-term cycling stability.

To further validate the structural evolution of the LRLO electrodes cycled in P-EDD and PB-SDDF electrolytes, X-ray diffraction (XRD) characterization was performed on the cycled LRLO electrodes after the selected cycles (Fig. 5d). For LRLO materials with a layered structure, the intensity ratio of (003)/(104) indicates the extent of transition ion mixing. The higher value signifies a well-preserved layered structure, with the pristine LRLO material exhibiting a high I(003)/I(104) ratio of 1.51 (Supplementary Fig. 32)52. After the initial activation process with three cycles at 0.1 C charge/discharge, the intensity ratio of (003)/(104) for the cycled LRLO with the PB-SDDF electrolyte is 1.29, higher than that with the P-EDD electrolyte (1.13), which indicates that severe side reactions prompt the structural evolution in the LRLO electrodes cycled with the P-EDD electrolyte. During the following long-term cycling, the LRLO electrodes with both P-EDD and PB-SDDF electrolytes display a decreasing trend in I(003)/I(104). However, the LRLO with the PB-SDDF electrolyte still maintains an I(003)/I(104) ratio greater than 1.0, in contrast to the LRLO with the P-EDD electrolyte. This demonstrates a reduced formation of rock-salt/spinel structure with smaller spacing, which is induced by the structural degradation of LRLO materials, as well as a suppression of electrolyte decomposition. Simultaneously, the structural evolutions of the LRLO with different electrolytes were further proved by Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 5e). In the Raman spectroscopy, the characteristic peak of the layered structure, which is located at around 600 cm−1, is well maintained in the cycled LRLO with PB-SDDF electrolyte after 200 cycles. In contrast, the spinel phase (656 cm–1) predominates the crystal structure of the LRLO cycled in the P-EDD electrolyte. The result of inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES) demonstrates that TMs dissolution is obviously inhibited in the LRLO electrode cycled in PB-SDDF electrolyte and the structural integrity of LRLO electrode can be maintained (Fig. 5f).

The morphologies and structural evolution of the cycled LRLO using P-EDD and PB-SDDF electrolyte were analyzed by SEM and HR-TEM. After 200 cycles, the byproduct of side reactions has nearly entirely coated the LRLO primary particles, indicating the LRLO electrode withstands drastic electrolyte decomposition in the P-EDD electrolyte (Fig. 5g). Additionally, the CEI formed on the LRLO electrodes exhibits an uneven thickness ranging from 3 to 11 nm in the HR-TEM image (Fig. 5h), which is insufficient to prevent electrolyte decomposition on the highly charged LRLO surface. The lack of interfacial stability renders structure degradation in the LRLO electrode during long-term cycling. In the HR-TEM and corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis (Fig. 5i, j), the distinct diffraction spots from both the spinel (Fd-3m) and layered (R-3m) structure can be observed in the LRLO particle cycled in the P-EDD electrolyte, which is consistent with the results from XRD and Raman spectra mentioned above. By contrast, the LRLO cycled in PB-SDDF electrolyte still maintains a clear and smooth surface with low resistance for charge transportation (Fig. 5k). HR-TEM images reveal a robust and thin CEI film, approximately 4 nm thick, on the surface of the primary particles (Fig. 5l). Simultaneously, the clear lattice fringe of the characteristic lattice plane (003) in HR-TEM image, along with the diffraction spots of the layered structure in FFT image, demonstrate that the LRLO cycled in PB-SDDF electrolyte effectively maintains its layered structure during cycling performance (Fig. 5m, n). This accounts for the long cycle stability of cells using the PB-SDDF electrolyte. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 33, the leakage current of the LRLO electrode in PB-SDDF electrolyte closely resembles that in P-EDD electrolyte at the voltage of 4.8 V. When the voltage is increased, the 5.0 V leakage current for PB-SDDF electrolyte is 2.6 μA, significantly lower than that of both P-EDD (6.2 μA). This leads to a stable surface for diminishing the side reaction between electrolyte and electrode. In summary, the designed electrolyte builds a uniform and robust CEI layer on the LRLO particles, effectively preventing side reactions from electrolyte decomposition at the interface of high-voltage electrode. Simultaneously, by suppressing electrolyte decomposition, the transformation from a layered to a spinel structure is also hindered, which contributes to the long-term stability of electrochemical performance (Supplementary Fig. 34).

The wide application of PB-SDDF electrolyte in the pouch cell

Electrolyte solvents are one of the primary causes of fires during thermal runaway in a cell. Generally, self-distinguishing time (SET) is utilized to evaluate the flammability of experimental material. The flammability of electrolytes is classified into flammable state (SET > 20 s g–1), flammable-retardant state (6 < SET < 20 s g–1), and non-flammable state (SET < 6 s g−1)42. In Fig. 6a, b, traditional electrolytes, particularly those based on carbonates, like the P-EDD electrolyte, are highly flammable and pose significant hazards due to the high SET value of 73.8 s g−1 and low limited oxygen index of 20.4%. Therefore, the highly effective flame retardant (PFPN) can ensure the high safety of the PB-SDDF electrolyte, which has a low SET of 3.7 and a high limited oxygen index (LOI) of 30.5%, indicating the difficulty of burning in the air. For simulating the thermal runaway of the delithiated LRLO electrode in different electrolytes, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed by placing a fixed amount of delithiated LRLO electrode and electrolyte into a stainless steel crucible, with a heat rate of 5 °C min-1 (Fig. 6c). During the DSC measurements, the delithiated LRLO electrode with the P-EDD electrolyte exhibits a lower onset temperature (237 °C) and higher exothermic peaks compared to that with the PB-SDDF electrolyte (onset temperature: 246 °C). This suggests that the PB-SDDF electrolyte can enhance the stability of the battery during operation.

Under the thermal abuse condition, the fully charged 1 Ah Si@C | | NCM811 pouch cell, measured 58×77 mm and injected with PB-SDDF, was subjected to a nail penetration test (Supplementary Video 5). As shown in Fig. 6d, e, there is no explosion or flame at the beginning of the nail penetration (Time = 14 s). Under short-circuit conditions, the cell with the PB-SDDF electrolyte exhibits a slight voltage drop from 4.14 to 3.77 V for 1 h, accompanied by increasing temperature. the temperature rise of the cell is 2.3 °C without any thermal runaway. After the nail retraction, the cell recovers to a safe condition with decreasing temperature (Fig. 6f). This suggests that the PB-SDDF electrolyte provides a guarantee of high safety during both cell operation and thermal abuse. Additionally, gas release is a common safety concern for LRLO materials. The PB-SDDF electrolyte delays the onset of CO2 and O2 release while also reducing the peak intensity, in contrast to the P-EDD electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 35). Additionally, the obvious expansion of the LRLO pouch cell with P-EDD electrolyte can be observed after cell aging (Supplementary Fig. 36).

The DSC curve shown in Supplementary Fig. 37a confirms that the PB-SDDF electrolyte maintains a liquid state during the temperature range from -80 to 40 °C without any heat release. In contrast, the P-EDD and P-SDSD262 electrolytes exhibit freezing points of –35 and –47 °C, respectively. To expand the potential applications of the PB-SDDF electrolyte, we conducted further assessments of the electrochemical performance of the Li | |LRLO half cells across a wide temperature range of –30 to 60 °C. For the low-temperature evaluation, the Li | |LRLO half cells with different electrolytes were initially cycled for 3 cycles at 30 °C using a 0.1 C charge/discharge rate. After this, the cells were moved to the respective temperature for subsequent testing. Representative voltage curves of Li | |LRLO cells with the P-EDD and PB-SDDF electrolytes at different temperatures are shown in Supplementary Fig. 37b and 37c. The LRLO electrode with PB-SDDF electrolyte can deliver high capacities of 323.1 and 338.8 mAh g–1 at the temperatures of 45 and 60 °C, respectively. However, the LRLO in the P-EDD electrolyte at 60 °C exhibits a similar discharge capacity of 318.8 mAh g-1 as under 45 °C, indicating that the severe side reaction occurs in the P-EDD electrolyte at elevated temperature. For the low-temperature condition, the PB-SDDF electrolyte with lower desolvation energy ensures the LRLO electrode yields a discharge capacity of 180.6 mAh g–1 at –10 °C (more than half the capacity at 30 °C) and 92.7 mAh g–1 at -30 °C. Specifically, the LRLO with the P-EDD electrolyte displays lower discharge capacity at low temperatures (205.5, 161.7, and 74.8 mAh g–1 at 0, –10, and –30 °C, respectively) due to the slow desolvation kinetics of the electrolyte.

Furthermore, the Li | |LRLO cells were tested under standard cycling conditions to assess their long-term stability at various temperatures. The Li | |LRLO cell with PB-SDDF electrolyte demonstrates enhanced cycling stability with a capacity retention of 84.3% after 600 cycles at 88.3% after 450 cycles at 0 °C (Supplementary Fig. 38a) and –10 °C (Supplementary Fig. 38b). Clearly, the LRLO electrode in the P-EDD electrolyte exhibits lower cycling capacity under 0 and –10 °C. Under the more extreme condition of –30 °C, the PB-SDDF continues to function normally during cycling, whereas the P-EDD electrolytes failed to perform due to the obstacle of charge transfer (Supplementary Fig. 38c). Thanks to the formation of a robust and inorganic CEI film derived from PB-SDDF electrolyte, the LRLO electrolyte exhibits much better high-temperature tolerance with the high retention of 70.2% after 300 cycles at 45 °C (Supplementary Fig. 39a). The LRLO electrode in P-EDD electrolyte experiences rapid capacity loss due to the insufficiency of CEI film in preventing LRLO from the corrosion of acid species. However, the elevated temperature (60 °C) triggers more severe side reactions, which render the instability in the whole cells (Supplementary Fig. 39b).

The practicality of the PB-SDDF electrolyte is further demonstrated in pouch cells with high specific energy. A pouch cell consisting of graphite | |LRLO was fabricated and cycled within the voltage range of 2–4.6 V. The graphite | |LRLO pouch cell with the PB-SDDF electrolyte delivers a high reversible capacity of 4.28 Ah at 0.1 C and achieves a specific energy of 278 Wh kg-1 (Supplementary Figs. 40 and 41). The PB-SDDF electrolyte enables a stable performance of graphite | |LRLO pouch cell with a high reversible capacity of 3.53 Ah and a high capacity retention of 91.4% after 250 cycles at 0.2 C charge/0.5 C discharge (Fig. 6g). Additionally, the pouch cell with high specific energy is capable for supplying power for the electric fan (Supplementary Fig. 42 and Supplementary Video 6). In contrast, the pouch cell with P-EDD electrolyte experiences a rapid capacity fading due to its poor oxidation resistance. These results reveal the good compatibility between the PB-SDDF electrolyte and high-voltage LRLO electrodes. Furthermore, we injected the PB-SDDF electrolyte into an 8.0 Ah Si@C | | NCM pouch cell to further highlight its broad applicability in different types of LIBs. The detail parameters of the pouch cell are listed in Supplementary Table S7. The Si@C | | NCM pouch cell can deliver a discharge capacity of 7.97 Ah with a mid-discharge voltage of 3.50 V, and the specific energy of the pouch cell was calculated to be 355 Wh kg-1 (Fig. 6j and Supplementary Fig. 43). The Si@C | | NCM pouch cell maintains 90.1% capacity after 120 cycles with an asymmetric process of 0.2 C charge/0.4 C discharge (Fig. 6i). For the next-generation battery system, combining Si@C electrodes with LRLO electrodes, the PB-SDDF also ensures stable cycling performance over 100 cycles, with a capacity retention of 85.6%, outperforming the P-EDD electrolyte (Fig. 6h). The results indicate that the designed PB-SDDF electrolyte can offer both safety assurance and high compatibility for practical battery applications.

Discussion

In summary, we designed the nonflammable PB-SDDF electrolyte, which demonstrates good compatibility with both high-capacity Si@C electrodes and high-voltage LRLO electrodes. The decreasing SL contents and the introduction of LiBF4 with low dissociation facilitate the entrance of anions into the Li+ solvation structure, thereby isolating more solvents and accelerating the Li+ desolvation process. The Li+ solvation structure, enriched with more anions, forms the stable and robust interphase on the electrode surfaces, resulting from anion decomposition. The PB-SDDF electrolyte allows the Si@C electrode to achieve a high reversible capacity of 1838.9 mAh g-1 at 0.1 C while improving the electrochemical reaction kinetics. The bilayer SEI structure on Si@C electrodes, comprising an inorganic inner layer and an organic outer layer, accommodates the expansion of Si@C electrodes and enhances the electrochemical performance, achieving a high capacity retention of 74.9% after 300 cycles at 0.3 C. Simultaneously, the stable inorganic CEI film formed on the LRLO electrode effectively prevents electrolyte decomposition and structural evolution in LRLO, ensuring promising performance with 72.3% retention after 600 cycles of 4.8 V Li | |LRLO cells at 1 C. The designed nonflammable electrolyte provides a high safety guarantee in the nail penetration test of the pouch cell without any thermal runaway. Based on all cell components (positive electrode, negative electrode, separator, and electrolyte), the practical graphite | |LRLO and Si@C | | NCM pouch cells with the PB-SDDF deliver high specific energy of 278 and 355 Wh kg-1, respectively, and demonstrate a stable electrochemical performance. The electrolyte design by balancing high voltage and rapid Li+ desolvation gives a reference for developing next-generation high-specific-energy Li-ion batteries, providing an effective way to formulate the solvation structure. In addition, our proposed PB-SDDF electrolyte holds significant potential in the commercialization of Li-ion batteries after minor adjustment or solvent replacement.

Methods

Materials

Lithium hexafluorophosphate (99.9%), lithium tetrafluoroborate (99.9%), tetramethylene sulfone (99.9%), ethoxy(pentafluoro)cyclotriphosphazene (99.5%), dimethyl carbonate (99.99%), difluoro ethylene carbonate (99.5%) were purchased from DodoChem Co., Ltd. Ethylene carbonate (99.9%) was provided by Macklin Inc. Si@C electrode materials (specific capacity: 1800 mAh g–1) and polyacrylate lithium (PAALi) binder were purchased from Canrd Technology Co. Ltd. The solvents were further dehydrated by the 4 Å activated molecular sieves (DodoChem Co.) before electrolyte preparation. Li1.12Mn0.49Ni0.16Co0.16O2, LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2, and Si@C electrode materials were provided by Youyan Technology Co., Ltd. without any treatment. The 14 mm-diameter Li foil (99.9%) with 400 μm thickness was bought from China Energy Lithium Co. Ltd. The Li foils are stored at the argon-charged glove box with O2 and H2O contents below 0.1 ppm and at 30 °C. The Li foils are refreshed after one month of storage.

Electrolytes and electrodes preparation

Typically, the electrolytes were prepared by dissolving lithium salt (e.g., LiPF6 and LiBF4) into mixed solvents based on the volume ratio in the argon-charged glovebox with O2 and H2O contents below 0.1 ppm and at 30 °C. The prepared electrolytes were stirred mechanically before storing on the aluminum-plastic bottle. The positive electrode slurry was combined with positive electrode material powder (LRLO and NCM811), KS-6 conductive regent, Super-P conductive regent, and poly(vinylidenefluoride) binder(PVDF, 99.9%, ARKEMA) in an 8: 0.5: 0.5: 1 weight ratio, as well as N-methyl−2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 99.9%, Macklin Inc.). The slurry was mixed in automatic mixer under vacuum condition. Then, the slurry was coated on Al foil by blade coater. The obtained electrodes were baked at 60 °C to remove residual NMP and then placed at 120 °C for 12 h under vacuum condition. The mass loading of the positive electrodes is controlled at around 3–3.5 mg cm–2. The Si@C electrodes were prepared with a slurry containing active materials, Super-P, and PAALi with a weight ratio of 8: 1: 1 in deionized water, with Cu foil serving as current collectors. The dried electrodes were further treated at 80 °C under a vacuum for 12 h. The mass loading of the Si@C electrodes is controlled at around 0.8–0.9 mg cm–2. The electrodes were sliced into 12-mm-diameter discs by manual slicer (MSK-T10, Kejing Co. Ltd.) and dried at 60 °C before electrochemical tests. The Celgard 2325 separators were sliced into 19-mm-diameter discs.

Electrochemical measurements

The cycling performance and electrochemical tests of batteries were conducted with 2025-type coin cells, consisting of Li foil, as-prepared electrode, and Celgard 2325 separators soaked with 50 μL electrolyte. The electrolyte preparation and cell assembly were conducted in the same Argon-charge glove box. The electrochemical performance was tested in environmental chamber with different temperatures, which the actual temperatures were noted on the specific graphs. The electrochemical performance of half cells (e.g., Li | |LRLO and Li | |Si@C) was performed on a CT2001A battery test system (Wuhan LAND Electronic Co., Ltd.). The Li | |LRLO cells were charged to 4.8 V and discharged to 2.0 V at 0.1 C rate (1 C = 250 mA g−1) in the initial three cycles for activation. After that, the cells were cycled at different rates for the subsequent cycles in galvanostatic charge/discharge method. The Li | |Si@C cells were initially cycled at 0.1 C rate (1 C = 1800 mA g−1) for two activated cycles before the long-term cycling during cell voltage of 0.005-2 V in galvanostatic charge/discharge method. The cycling performance of the pouch cells (e.g., Si@C | | NCM, and graphite | |LRLO) was recorded on a multichannel CT-4008Tn-5V6A battery cycler (Neware Co., Ltd.). The cut-off voltages of Si@C | | NCM and graphite | |LRLO pouch cells are 2.5–4.35 V and 2–4.6 V. The oxidation potential was evaluated through the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) test with Li | |Pt cells scanning from the open circuit to 6 V vs. Li/Li+ wih a scan rate of 0.2 mV s–1, performed on Gamry Interface 1000E. Cyclic voltammetry was conducted to determine interfacial kinetics of LRLO from 2.0 to 4.8 V vs. Li/Li+ at a scan rate of 0.2 mV s–1. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy of cycled electrodes was tested in potentiostatic mode with the frequency range from 0.01 Hz to 1 MHz and the amplitude voltage of 5 mV. Ten points were recorded at each decade. The distribution of relaxation time analysis was processed by the Matlab Graphical used DRTtools, which was developed by Ciucci’s research group53.

Characterizations

The morphologies of cycled electrodes were investigated by scanning electron microscopy (TESCAN GAIA 3 GMH) equipped with time of flight secondary ion mass spectrometry and transmission electron microscopy (Thermo Fisher Talos F200x). The structural characterization of cycled electrodes was carried out by X-ray diffraction. The surface chemistry of cycled electrodes was analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Thermo Scientific ESCALAB XI + ), excited by an Al Kα radiation source. All the cells were disassembled in an argon-filled glovebox with O2 and H2O contents below 0.1 ppm. The disassembled electrodes were washed in pure DMC solvent three times to remove the electrolyte, and then placed in the glovebox to evaporate DMC solvent. The Raman spectra for as-prepared electrolytes were collected with a Raman spectrometer (Renishaw in Via reflex) at an excitation wavelength of 532 nm and 785 nm. 7Li and 19F NMR spectra of the as-prepared electrolytes were recorded on Bruker AVANCE Ш HD 600 MHz liquid NMR at room temperature. Ion conductivities of as-prepared electrolytes were measured by a conductivity analyzer (Leici DDB-303A) under an argon-filled glovebox after calibration with saturated KCl aqueous solution. The simulated safety of electrolytes in cell operation was evaluated by differential scanning calorimetry with charged samples and different electrolytes. DSC experiments were performed using a TA Instrument Q20 differential scanning calorimeter at a heating rate of 5 K min–1 in an argon atmosphere over a temperature range of 40–350 °C. Besides, the freezing point of electrolytes was determined by DCS with the decreasing temperature from 40 to –80 °C. The flammability of as-prepared electrolytes was determined by self-extinguishing time, tested on the electrolyte-soaked glass fiber. The flame torched the 19-mm-diameter glass fiber soaked with 200 μL electrolyte for 3 s. The time was recorded from flame ignition to flame extinguishing and then standardized according to the electrolyte weight. The limited oxygen index of as-prepared electrolytes was determined by the TTech-GBT2406-2 oxygen index apparatus and vertical burning tester (TESTech Instrument Technologies Co., Ltd.). Nail penetration tests of pouch cells were performed in the battery nailing tester with a stainless steel needle with a diameter of 5 mm and a falling rate of 25 mm s–1. Gas evolution of Li | |LRLO cells in different electrolytes was performed using online differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (Pro-tech) at the electrochemical voltage range of 2–4.8 V. Atomic force microscopy (Bruker Dimension Icon) was applied to measure the Young’s modulus of the SEI film of cycled Si@C electrodes in different electrolytes. The concentrations of transition metal ions deposited on the lithium metal electrodes were determined by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES, iCAP 7200 Duo, Thermofisher Scientific). The in situ Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) were recorded in the cell instrument, purchased from Shanghai Linglu Instruments. The cell was assembly by Li foil (14 mm-diameter and 400 μm thickness), glass fiber, and LRLO-coated Al net.

Theoretical simulation

HOMO and LUMO energies were calculated by density function theory. Ground-state geometries were optimized at the B3LYP/6-31 G* level. And then the energy levels of HOMO and LUMO were calculated at the B3LYP/6-311 + + G(d,p) basis set54. Besides, the binding energies of Li+ and different solvents were calculated in the B3LYP/6-311 + + G(d,p) basis set according to density functional theory after geometry optimization at B3LYP/6-31 G* level55,56. All atomistic molecular dynamics simulations were carried out using the GROMACS 2018.8 simulation package58 combined with the GAFF force field59. The atomic charges of solvent molecules were derived via the restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) fitting algorithm, which was implemented using the Multifwn software60. We adopted Sobtop to generate topology files as well as bonded and Lennard-Jones parameters for simulated molecules. The initial simulation configuration was constructed with the Packmol package61, where molecules were randomly distributed within a cubic box with dimensions of 7 nm along length (x), width (y), and height (z). The steepest-descent algorithm was utilized to perform energy minimization for the entire simulation system62. Afterwards, both equilibrium and production simulations were performed under the NPT ensemble at constant pressure and temperature (298 K) with three-dimensional periodic boundary conditions in all xyz Cartesian direction. During the equilibrium stage, the V-rescale thermostat was applied to maintain the system temperature with a relaxation time of 0.2 ps, while the Berendsen barostat was used to regulate system pressure. The equilibrium simulation lasted for 2 ns. For the subsequent production simulation, the temperature and pressure were controlled by the V-rescale63 coupling and Parrinello-Rahman64 barostat, respectively. The trajectories of the 20 ns production run were recorded every 2 ps for subsequent data analysis. The linear constraint solver (LINCS) algorithm was used to constrain all covalent bond lengths65. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method was adopted to compute long-range electrostatic interactions66. The Visual Molecular Dynamics software was used for post-processing and visualization of simulation configurations67.
