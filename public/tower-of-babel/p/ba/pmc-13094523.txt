Recovery and Survival of Prototheca Microalgae During Wastewater Treatment Process
Authors: Mateusz Iskra, Marta Romaszuk, Mariusz Dyląg, Henryk Krukowski, Jacek Bielecki, Tomasz Jagielski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Prototheca are unicellular, non‐pigmented, yeast‐like microalgae widely distributed in natural and anthropogenic environments. While typically saprophytic, these organisms are the only plant species known to have emerged as opportunistic pathogens for vertebrate animals including humans. The algae have been recognised as ubiquitous inhabitants of domestic and municipal sewage, considered as point sources of Prototheca contamination in natural aquatic systems. These observations, however, have been based on data from a very limited number of historical studies. The purpose of this work was to readdress the question of wastewater as a habitat for Prototheca, using a new and refined experimental methodology, and in light of the recently updated taxonomy and nomenclature of the Prototheca genus. Over a three‐month period, 96 samples were collected from three municipal wastewater treatment plants, at four different stages of the treatment process. The algae were recovered from 74 samples, which translates into an overall prevalence of 78.1%. The highest Prototheca population was found in raw sewage, and decreased progressively with each purification stage. The Prototheca cell count in the effluent was 100‐fold lower than in the untreated sewage, resulting in an algal removal efficiency of up to 99.9%. The occurrence of Prototheca in treated wastewater suggests that wastewater treatment plants may act as a pathway for the release of these algae into receiving natural environments. In both influent and effluent, species distribution was dominated by Prototheca species previously reported as pathogenic, with P. wickerhamii detected most frequently. The study concludes by advocating for regular monitoring of pre‐ and post‐treatment wastewater for Prototheca species and emphasising the need for guideline limits on their acceptable levels at both treatment stages.Keywords: algae, effluent, influent, Prototheca, sewage, wastewater treatment plants

Introduction

Prototheca species are unicellular, achlorophyllous, saprophytic and occasionally pathogenic microalgae with a wide natural habitat. After a century‐long debate over the Prototheca taxonomy, they have been eventually accepted as members of the Chlorophyta (Viridiplantae) phylum, sharing a clear evolutionary descent from green algal‐like ancestors (Jagielski and Lagneau 2007; Jagielski, Bakuła, et al. 2019). Prototheca are part of the CHAP (Chlorella, Helicosporidium, Auxenochlorella and Prototheca) lineage, as evidenced by recent phylogenies based on the mitochondrially‐encoded cytochrome b (cytb) gene, which now serves as the ‘gold standard’ marker to differentiate species within the Prototheca genus (Jagielski, Bakuła, et al. 2019; Jagielski et al. 2018; Shave et al. 2025). Of a total of 18 currently recognised species, six (P. wickerhamii, P. blaschkeae, P. cutis, P. miyajii, P. ciferrii and P. bovis ) have been implicated as the etiological agents of opportunistic infections in both humans and animals, collectively referred to as protothecosis (Jagielski, Bakuła, et al. 2019; Jagielski et al. 2022). Although some Prototheca species have previously been associated with disease, their opportunistic nature warrants caution when inferring pathogenicity, particularly for environmental isolates.

Prototheca were originally isolated from the slime flux of deciduous trees (Krüger 1894). Since then, the algae have been recovered from a broad range of natural sources, including soil, marine and fresh waters, as well as the tissues of plants and animals and their waste products (Jagielski et al. 2022; Pore 1986; Iskra et al. 2025; Pore et al. 1983). Prototheca algae have also been found in many agricultural environments, such as pastures, rice paddy fields, but above all, areas used for livestock and dairy milk production (Pore et al. 1983; Jagielski, Roeske, et al. 2019; Jagielski, Krukowski, et al. 2019). Although the ecology of Prototheca is still poorly explored, it exhibits a conspicuous predilection for humid environments with high organic matter content (Jagielski et al. 2022).

Wastewater as a source of Prototheca algae was first demonstrated by Cooke in the late 1950s (Cooke 1954). In these early studies from the USA, the algae were found to occur abundantly in raw sewage and different stages of its treatment process (Cooke 1954; Pore et al. 1983). The issue of sewage habitats as a niche for Prototheca spp. was revisited two decades later in a series of studies, again from the United States, yet with samplings performed also in the Philippines, Thailand, Haiti, Peru and Costa Rica (Pore et al. 1983; Pore et al. 1986; Barnett 1977; Woollett and Hedrick 1970). The results clearly showed that Prototheca spp. are ubiquitous inhabitants of domestic and municipal sewage and persist through all stages of primary and secondary (both aerobic and anaerobic) treatment processes. This conclusion was confirmed by later reports from other countries, including France, Spain and Brazil (Pidoux 1977; Casal and Solis 1981; Camargo and Fischman 1981). The consistent observations of the common occurrence of Prototheca in sewage water were provocative and contributed to providing insight into the ecological cycle of the algae. It was postulated that a major reservoir of Prototheca spp. is wastewater from the primary domestic sewers and secondary sewer networks, as it carries large amounts of nutrients, thus supporting significant growth and reproduction of the algae (Pore et al. 1983; Pore et al. 1986). Both pre‐ and post‐treatment wastewater are the sources from which Prototheca enter aquatic systems and drinking water supplies. The ability of Prototheca to escape killing in sewage digesters assumes particular importance in the context of the pathogenic potential of these organisms. Prototheca‐contaminated water may provide a vehicle of infection either through inoculation of wounded or otherwise traumatised tissues (Todd et al. 2012), which is the most common mechanism of disease transmission to humans, or by oral exposure, often surmised in dogs (Jagielski et al. 2025). The epidemiological aspects of the presence of Prototheca spp. in sewage and treated wastewater urge its close monitoring and management. Yet, after only a few studies from the turn of the 1970s and 1980s, little or nothing has been added to our knowledge of Prototheca in wastewater. Therefore, the purpose of this work was to reapproach this topic, after a four‐decade break, with new and refined experimental methods, designed to detect Prototheca more accurately and effectively. The prevalence of Prototheca algae in wastewater was assessed both quantitatively and qualitatively, by describing the species composition by using, for the first time, molecular typing tools and in the context of the updated taxonomy. Moreover, unlike most previous studies, which were typically confined to a single wastewater treatment plant (Pore et al. 1986; Woollett and Hedrick 1970), the present work examines the occurrence of Prototheca in three different facilities concurrently. The comparative framework of this study, further supported by physicochemical analysis of wastewater samples, provides a much more detailed and comprehensive research perspective compared to earlier studies. All this provides a more accurate insight into the ecological significance of Prototheca algae in wastewater systems.

Materials and Methods

Description of WWTPs

Three wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), located in three major voivodeships, that is, Lower Silesia (WWTP1), Lublin (WWTP2) and Masovia (WWTP3) along the Eastern‐Western axis of Poland were visited (Figure 1). Each plant is designed as a conventional mechanical and biological installation and serves, for over 15 years, as a municipal facility to treat domestic sewage from family households, agricultural farms, public utility buildings and other public infrastructure in both urban and rural locations. The three plants differ in terms of their size, the size of the catchment area that each plant covers, the treatment capacity of the plant or the population equivalent. Sampling

The study was performed over a 3‐month period (i.e., from March to May 2023). From each WWTP, a total of 32 samples were collected, giving a total of 96 samples overall. The samples, at each plant, were taken from (i) raw sewage (RS, influent) at the inlet point, (ii) primary effluent (PE), after primary clarification (i.e., after mechanical removal of coarse solids, followed by flowing through grit chambers allowing for settling of sand and other small particles), (iii) secondary effluent (SE), at the outlet of the aeration tank, where the biological level of sewage treatment occurred, and (iv) final (treated) effluent (TE), when, after sludge separation, it is discharged to the environment. Sectors wherefrom wastewater/treated effluent samples were collected are schematically shown in Figure 2. Each WWTP was sampled once a week, on the same day and around the same hour (between 8:00 AM and 10:00 AM) for a period of 8 weeks in total. The samples were collected into 1‐L sterile containers with a long‐handled water sampling dipper with a replaceable, sterile collection beaker. All samples were kept chilled at 4°C–8°C in a portable transportation box and transferred to the laboratory within 24 h after collection, split into two volumes upon delivery, and processed for physicochemical and microbiological analyses immediately afterward.

Quantification of the Yield of Prototheca spp. and Removal Efficiency

Quantification of Prototheca colony‐forming units (CFU) was performed by serial dilution plating of wastewater samples onto selective Prototheca Isolation Medium (PIM). Colonies exhibiting the characteristic morphology of Prototheca spp., upon both macro‐ and microscopic inspection, were counted. From three independent replications (three plates), the average value was determined and expressed as CFU per mL of the original sample (undiluted).

Prototheca spp. Cultivation

One millilitre of each sample was suspended in 9 mL of liquid PIM, whose formulation was described elsewhere (Pore 1973), and preincubated for 48 h at 30°C with horizontal shaking (200 rpm). Every sample was cultured in PIM in triplicate. After preincubation, an aliquot of 0.1 mL of culture suspension was spread on PIM agar plates and incubated at 30°C for up to 72 h. Colonies suspected to be Prototheca spp. were subcultured on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) and subjected to identification at the genus level, based on macro and micromorphology, as assessed using a Primo Star light microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Physicochemical Analyses

Each wastewater sample, of a volume of at least 800 mL, was analysed by an array of physicochemical methods according to the standardised protocols. The biochemical oxygen demand (BOD5), chemical oxygen demand (COD), ammonia (NH4 +) and nitrates (NO3 −) were determined using NANOCOLOR test kits (Macherey‐Nagel, Germany), following the manufacturer's instructions and in agreement with relevant international standards (EN 1899‐1; ISO 15705:2002; ISO 23695:2023‐02; ISO 23696‐1:2023‐02). Total carbon (TC), total nitrogen (TN) and total inorganic carbon (TIC) were measured with a Multi N/C 3100 TOC/TN analyser (Analytik Jena, Germany). The quantity of organic carbon (OC) was calculated as the difference between the TC measured and the TIC. For total phosphorus (TP) determination, samples were digested with nitric acid in an UltraWave (Milestone, Italy) microwave digestion system. TP content was then measured using SAN++ Skalar (Skalar Analytical, The Netherlands) continuous flow analyser, using the molybdate method (Estela and Cerdà 2005). The pH was measured with the use of an Elmetron analyser (Elmetron, Poland).

Molecular Species Identification

Species identification was performed by molecular typing using a fragment of the cytb gene as a genetic marker (Jagielski et al. 2018). Genomic DNA was isolated according to a previously described protocol (Jagielski et al. 2017). In short, a loopful of cells from a fresh colony grown on SDA medium was suspended in a buffer containing β‐mercaptoethanol, lyticase and proteinase K, along with 1–2 mm glass beads. The mixture was vigorously vortexed and centrifuged, and the aqueous supernatant was transferred to a new tube. DNA extraction was then continued with the GeneMATRIX Environmental DNA and RNA Purification Kit (EURx, Gdańsk, Poland), according to the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted DNA was eluted in TE buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0), quantified using a NanoDrop ND‐1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and either used immediately for PCR amplification or stored at −20°C for later use. For amplification of the partial cytb gene, the primer pair cytb‐F1 and cytb‐R2 was used (Jagielski et al. 2018). 20‐μL PCR mixtures contained 0.2 μM of each primer, ca. 10 ng of template DNA and 0.5 U of OptiTaq DNA polymerase (EURx, Gdansk, Poland). PCR product amplification was performed under standard conditions including 3 min at 95°C, followed by 35 cycles of 30 s at 95°C, 30 s at 50°C and 30 s at 72°C, with a final extension of 5 min at 72°C.

All Prototheca isolates cultured underwent cytb‐based PCR‐restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis. Whenever PCR‐RFLP profiling was inconclusive for species identity, sequencing of the partial cytb gene was performed, with the same primers as those used for PCR amplification (Jagielski et al. 2018). Single‐strand reads were assembled into a consensus sequence using Clone Manager software ver. 9.0 (Sci‐Ed Software, USA). The assembled sequences were then aligned against those available in the Prototheca‐ID database (Dziurzyński et al. 2021) (https://prototheca‐id.org) and GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/), using a 98% sequence similarity cut‐off for unambiguous species identification.

Statistical Analysis

The Spearman rank correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationships between algal culture yield (CFU/mL) and the physicochemical parameters analysed. This test was chosen due to the non‐normal distribution of the data and the presence of outliers, as confirmed by visual inspection (histogram, Q–Q plot) and the Shapiro–Wilk test. The significance level was set at the conventional threshold of p = 0.05.

The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to evaluate whether overall removal efficiency (RE) differed between wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) or among sampling periods. The significance threshold was set at p = 0.05.

PCA analysis was carried out in R (R Studio ver. 2023.12.1) using the factoextra library. The dataset was normalised prior to PCA analysis.

Results

Prevalence and Distribution of Prototheca spp. During Treatment Process

Prototheca algae were isolated from 74 (77.1%) out of 96 wastewater samples collected at all stages of the treatment process in the three WWTPs investigated. The overall recovery rates were similar across the facilities, calculated at 78.1% in WWTP1 and WWTP3, and 75% in WWTP2 (Table 2). Over the course of the treatment process, the frequency of Prototheca isolation decreased decrementally with each purification stage. The algae were consistently recovered from raw sewage (RS; 100% across all WWTPs), somewhat less frequently from primary effluent (PE; 93.3%), and slightly less further from secondary effluent (SE; 90.6%). The lowest algae isolation was observed for treated effluent, where the decline was most significant (TE; 21.9%). At the level of individual WWTP, the first drop in the Prototheca isolation was observed at different treatment stages. In WWTP3, it was as early as in PE. In WWTP2 this drop occurred in SE. Whereas, in WWTP1, a decline in Prototheca isolation only became apparent after the final treatment stage, namely in TE (Table 2).

Removal Efficiency of Prototheca Algae

The quantity of Prototheca decreased substantially from influent (RS) to final effluent (TE), as evidenced by a 3.5‐fold reduction in the frequency of isolation throughout sewage treatment. The clearing effect of the treatment process on the Prototheca population level was best illustrated by removal efficiency (RE) values (Equation (1)). The mean REs of Prototheca algae, based on algal CFU counts, were 96.1%, 98.4% and 99.7% for WWTP3, WWTP2 and WWTP1, respectively (Table 4). The coefficient of variation (CV) in the algal quantity, calculated for RS was low (3.7%–4.3%), and even lower for TE (2.2%–2.5%). The CV of the RE differed between the plants and was the lowest at WWTP1 (0.8%) and the highest at WWTP3 (10%).

Species Composition of Prototheca Algae in Wastewater Samples From Different WWTPs in Relation to the Treatment Stage and Sampling Period

A total of 95 isolates were retrieved from the three WWTPs under the study, with comparable numbers obtained from each facility (Figure 4B, Table S1). As with the CFU counts, the number of isolates decreased progressively along the treatment process. RS was the most abundant source of Prototheca isolates, accounting for 36.8% (35/95) of their total count. A stepwise decline was observed through the PE (31.6%; 30/95) and SE (23.2%; 22/95) and continued into the TE, which yielded the smallest fraction of the strains (8.4%; 8/95) (Figure 4C). The predominant species was P. wickerhamii (37/95; 38.9%), followed by P. bovis (18/95; 18.9%), P. cookei (15/95; 15.8%), P. ciferrii (14/95; 14.7%), P. pringsheimii (9/95; 9.5%) and P. vistulensis (2/95; 2.1%) (Figure 4A). The first four species were found in all WWTPs, yet their relative abundance slightly varied. For example, P. wickerhamii made up almost half (47%) of the algal species structure in WWTP2 and WWTP3, whereas in WWTP1 it ranked third after P. ciferrii (35%) and P. cookei (31%). As for P. pringsheimii and P. vistulensis, the former was absent from WWTP1, whereas the latter was isolated only from WWTP3 (Figure 4B).

Species structure of Prototheca isolates overall across all wastewater treatment plants (A) and categorised by individual treatment plant (B), treatment stage (C), and week of experiment (D).

All six Prototheca species were isolated from wastewater samples (RS, PE, SE) except that RS did not yield P. vistulensis in culture. In the treated effluent only three species (P. wickerhamii, P. bovis , P. ciferrii ), previously associated with disease, were recovered. P. wickerhamii predominated across all sample types (Figure 4C). The composition of Prototheca species varied also over time. P. wickerhamii, as the only species, was isolated in every week, and through most of the sampling period it was the most frequently isolated species, showing peaks during weeks III–V. P. bovis, P. ciferrii and P. cookei occurred, at lower frequencies, regularly, with each missing in a single week only. The remaining two species appeared intermittently. P. pringsheimii was isolated in four out of 8 weeks of sampling, whereas P. vistulensis was isolated only once, in the sixth week of the experiment (Figure 4D).

Physicochemical Determinants of Prototheca Abundance in Wastewater

Key physicochemical parameters showed clear stage‐dependent dynamics across all treatment plants (Tables S2 and S3). Of all the parameters analysed, only the ammonium and IC concentrations showed consistently decreasing patterns through all treatment stages in all facilities. A consistent pattern across all WWTPs was also observed for nitrates, TC, OC, TP and TN. Here, following a slight decrease after primary treatment, the concentrations of all these parameters peaked in the SE, and then dropped sharply at the final treatment stage (TE). Two distinct trends were noted for the BOD5 and COD parameters. In WWTP1 and 3, the values increased steadily until the secondary clarification was reached, with the drastic decline in the treated samples (TE). In WWTP2, however, both BOD5 and COD decreased progressively in the subsequent four water samples (Table S2). In the pooled dataset, the influent BOD5/COD ratio was 0.47. pH remained stable at 6.8–7.0 throughout all treatment stages, except that in WWTP1, the pH was slightly acidified once the secondary treatment was completed (Tables S2 and S3).

Principal component analysis (PCA) was used as an exploratory tool to visualise patterns of co‐variation between physicochemical parameters and Prototheca abundance in the pooled datasets across all WWTPs. The PCA analysis indicated an association between higher Prototheca CFU counts and elevated nutrient and organic matter indicators (TP, OC, TC, TN, BOD5, COD), reflecting joint patterns within the data. Two major clusters of variables were identified in the PCA space: (i) NH4 and TIC, and (ii) TP, OC, TC, BOD, TN and COD (Figure 5).

Discussion

In Poland, nearly 8 billion m3 sewage are produced every year, of which over 80% (ca. 6.5 billion m3) originate from industrial sources, while the remaining 20% (ca. 1.4 billion m3) comes from household activities (Statistics Poland 2022). These figures position Poland as an important contributor to wastewater management in the European Union. The annual sewage production in Poland represents more than 13% of the total wastewater for the EU countries, although domestic wastewater accounts for only 4% of such flow in the region (WHO 2025). Around 75% of the Polish population is connected to the municipal wastewater treatment system, which is slightly below the EU average (80%) (Kacprzak and Kupich 2023; Eurostat 2023).

Domestic wastewater contains high microbial loads that pose significant challenges to both infrastructure and ecological resilience. Typical municipal influent (raw sewage) carries viable bacteria in the amount of 8.4 × 107 CFU/mL, on average (Foladori et al. 2010), whereas, the fungal counts in untreated sewage reach up to 1.7 × 105 CFU/mL (Kacprzak et al. 2005). Comparable estimates for microalgae are rarely made due to the lack of routine monitoring protocols in wastewater treatment facilities.

Prototheca algae have long been recognised as common sewage dwellers, albeit only in a very limited number of studies. These studies that examined domestic and municipal wastewater, have reported Prototheca abundance ranging from 102 to 106 CFU/mL across all stages of treatment (Pore et al. 1983; Pore et al. 1986). Within this range fell the Prototheca CFU counts determined in this study, spanning from 0.3 × 102 to 6.6 × 104 CFU/mL. Noteworthy, the mean CFU count calculated for treated effluent (7 × 101 CFU/mL) in this work was considerably lower than that from a previous investigation (2.4 × 103 CFU/mL) (Pore et al. 1983). Since the mean Prototheca counts in RS did not differ significantly between the two studies (7 × 103 vs. 1.3 × 104 CFU/mL) the difference in the algal burden in the final effluent can be explained either by sampling variances or, more plausibly, by the performance and efficiency of the wastewater treatment technology used. The treatment plants visited during this study were established at least four decades after those inspected in the earlier study.

No marked differences in the Prototheca abundance were observed along the treatment line among the three WWTPs investigated. Only WWTP3 showed slightly elevated CFU values at each treatment stage, compared to the other two facilities. This may relate to the fact that WWTP3 exceeded the other two multiple times in terms of the catchment area, population equivalent and annual sewage treatment volume. Still, these parameters seem to affect the Prototheca burden to a much lesser extent than the origin of the sewage or its nutrient composition.

Some fluctuations in the Prototheca CFU counts from week to week (Figure 6) can be explained by hourly variations in sewage flow rate (Pore et al. 1983; Barnett 1977).

Temporal dynamics of Prototheca spp. abundance in the wastewater treatment plants under the study (WWTPs 1–3).

When analysing the Prototheca species composition of the wastewater, P. wickerhamii has consistently been identified as the overwhelmingly dominant species, often representing more than 90% of all isolates (Pore et al. 1983; Pore et al. 1986). P. wickerhamii was the most prevalent species also in this study, yet it accounted for somewhat less than 40% of the isolates. Among the other five species detected were P. bovis and P. ciferrii , which had previously been found in sewage under the collective name of P. zopfii, as well as P. cookei , P. pringsheimii and P. vistulensis, whose presence in wastewater was demonstrated for the first time. Our results show a higher species diversity compared to what was previously reported in the literature. This may be partly attributed to recent advancements in diagnostic methods along with revisions in the taxonomy and nomenclature of the Prototheca genus. Captivatingly, certain shifts in the species composition could be observed at the level of individual treatment plants. Although three species, namely P. wickerhamii, P. bovis and P. cookei , always formed the backbone of the population structure, the share of other species varied significantly. For instance, in WWTP1, P. ciferrii predominated, outnumbering other species, whereas P. pringsheimii was markedly overrepresented in WWTP2 relative to the other facilities. As with Prototheca abundance, the differences in their species composition may relate to operational parameters, catchment‐specific inputs, or seasonality (Kim et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2024; LaMartina et al. 2021). The relevance of the latter is further highlighted by the shifts in the species population structure, which were observed weekly over a short, three‐month sampling period.

More importantly, the influent wastewater samples clearly differed between the three WWTPs studied, in all parameters defining their organic matter content, with WWTP2 showing the highest and WWTP1—the lowest organic loads. In this context, it is worth noting that the catchment area for WWTP2 had the highest proportion of farm households, thus increasing the possibility of agricultural contamination, including organic‐rich leachate from animal production. While this may explain the overinclusion of P. pringsheimii, whose natural habitat has been poorly described so far, it adds some confusion to the paucity of P. bovis , which is a commonly animal‐associated species. WWTP3, on the other hand, serving the most urbanised and socially atomised catchment area, showed the most heterogeneous Prototheca species structure, with the highest yield of P. wickerhamii, previously reported from human‐engineered environments and clinical cases. Overall, it appears that the Prototheca composition in wastewater is influenced by the catchments area and its organic pollution, although the general patterns observed remain consistent with earlier reports describing widespread Prototheca occurrence in wastewater environments. This is in line with previous findings demonstrating the strong influence of catchment area on micropollutant composition (Kilpinen et al. 2023).

Wastewater provides an exceptionally favourable ecosystem for Prototheca growth consistent with previous observations of their persistence in nutrient‐rich wastewater environments. The high concentration of organic matter, as reflected by the high BOD5 and COD values, clearly elevated TC, TN and TP levels, along with the high index of wastewater biodegradability, proxied by the BOD5/COD ratio (ca. 0.5) of the raw sewage, create the conditions for efficient growth and proliferation of Prototheca spp. Another advantageous aspect of the wastewater nutrient profile, in terms of Prototheca metabolism, was the relatively high concentration of ammoniacal nitrogen, which, unlike nitrate, can be assimilated by the algae as a sole nitrogen source. This incidentally points to the potential use of Prototheca algae for ammonium removal from wastewaters and aligns with the broader discussion on the exploitation of microalgae for wastewater recycling (Salbitani and Carfagna 2021). Our results suggest a correlation between Prototheca growth and sewage nutrient composition. The algal abundance appears to be sustained in tandem with organic and nutrient loads and tends to diminish as these resources are stripped away during treatment.

An important observation from this study is the high removal efficiency of WWTPs with respect to the elimination of Prototheca algae. For the three facilities under this study, the mean RE of Prototheca ranged from 96.1% to 99.9%. This contrasts with previous observations that the Prototheca populations measured for raw sewage and treatment plant effluent did not change substantially and that the Prototheca count decreased by a little more than an order of magnitude (1 × 10−1) from influent to effluent (Pore et al. 1983; Pore et al. 1986). This was explained by the breakdown of the biofilm at the later stages of the treatment, possibly due to the depletion of nutrients or changes in other environmental parameters (temperature, pH, ionic strength etc.) which are known to affect biofilm formation (Kwiecinski 2015; Saini et al. 2023). The differences between the treatment plants in the Prototheca removal rates may stem from the same underlying factors that might have accounted for vastly different interstudy algal counts in the treated effluent, namely the technological and operational aspects of the wastewater treatment systems applied.

A limitation of this study is that CFU‐based enumeration captures only the cultivable fraction of Prototheca populations and may therefore underestimate their total abundance and bias the observed species composition. Nonetheless, this approach was chosen to ensure the detection of viable cells and to enable subsequent isolation and species‐level identification. At present, the application of molecular quantification methods for Prototheca in complex wastewater matrices remains constrained by the lack of validated, species‐specific quantitative assays.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of limitations related to the sampling design. Although the present study provides new insight into Prototheca occurrence, their species composition and removal in municipal wastewater treatment systems, it does not capture potential variability associated with different climatic zones, seasonal patterns or other than mechanical–biological wastewater treatment technologies. Factors such as temperature, seasonal changes in organic load, and the application of alternative treatment or disinfection regimes may influence the behaviour and fate of Prototheca in wastewater systems. Addressing these aspects will require future multi‐site and multi‐season studies encompassing a wider range of environmental conditions and treatment configurations.

In Poland, as in many countries across the world, insofar as there are regulatory limits for the physicochemical parameters of discharged effluents, no microbiological standards for treated wastewater have been established in the legislation. The statutory guidelines on the microbiological quality are mandated for treated wastewater that is used in agriculture (e.g., for crop irrigation) (Blumenthal et al. 2000). Otherwise, strict microbiological quality criteria apply only to drinking and recreational waters, with the latter considered mostly as human‐made systems (e.g., swimming pools, spa pools, artificial lakes) (Table S4). However, water intended for human use (consumption or bathing) is typically subject to various treatment processes with a crucial purification step of disinfection carried out by technologies, such as chlorination, ozonation, ultrafiltration etc. Even if microbiological standards are available they typically pertain to merely a few selected bacterial indicators, such as total coliforms, Escherichia coli or Clostridium perfringens , which serve as canonical markers of faecal contamination. Noteworthily, irrespective of whether it is treated wastewater or potable and recreational water no guideline limits have been set for fungi or algae, with a likely rationale behind this being that neither of these microorganisms have been normally associated with waterborne disease. Consequently, the concentrations of Prototheca sp. detected in the effluent in this study cannot be assessed against any standards, since no such standards exist. Still, the presence of Prototheca algae in treated sewage highlights an environmental concern, as their persistence and ecological implications have yet to be elucidated.

And even if the levels of Prototheca sp. may appear relatively low compared to bacterial pollution reported for treated wastewater, with the coliform counts exceeding 103 CFU/mL (Ajonina et al. 2015; Osuolale and Okoh 2015), the finding of Prototheca algae in the final effluent should not be ignored. Prototheca‐infested water is surmised as a major source of infection in humans, dogs and livestock, with the most probable modes of infection being post‐traumatic inoculation, ingestion and passing into the udder through exposed teat orifices, respectively (Jagielski, Krukowski, et al. 2019; Todd et al. 2012; Jagielski et al. 2025). Attempts to experimentally induce Prototheca infection in laboratory animals, using a variety of inoculation doses, have met with mixed success. The infection dose sufficient to develop infection under natural conditions has not yet been determined. Additionally, four further arguments support the necessity of monitoring for the occurrence of Prototheca in treated wastewater. First, the levels of Prototheca in the effluent discharged into the environment can far exceed those identified in our study when heavy sewage flow occurs. During such peak flows, the retention time decreases significantly, and a portion of the raw sewage with the highest Prototheca counts may bypass the treatment plant and enter directly local waterbodies (Pore et al. 1983; Barnett 1977). Second, the algae can rapidly increase in number shortly after the effluent is discharged into the environment, as evidenced by finding higher population levels downstream of the sewage treatment plant compared to upstream, a pattern linked to the large amounts of organic matter in the sewage effluent, easily utilised by Prototheca (Barnett 1977). Third, among the species recovered from the treated effluent were only those previously associated with Prototheca disease, implying that pathogenic Prototheca species exhibit a pronounced survival advantage during the treatment process. Fourth, Prototheca species have been shown to withstand various harsh environmental conditions, such as wide pH, high salinity and high temperatures, including those applied in milk pasteurisation (Marques et al. 2010; Lassa et al. 2011). Moreover, although Prototheca spp. in the effluent of municipal wastewater treatment systems are usually killed by chlorination, they may, at some number, escape this process and contaminate chlorinated tap water (Pore et al. 1983; Pore et al. 1986; Barnett 1977). This remarkable refractoriness to physicochemical stresses, most likely conferred by the sporopollenin‐rich architecture of the Prototheca cell walls, facilitates the persistence of the algae in the environment.

Conclusions

In summary, this study is the first of its kind in Poland, and the first worldwide in over four decades to investigate the prevalence of Prototheca algae in sewage and treated wastewater. The major conclusions can be recapitulated as follows. Prototheca spp. occur abundantly in raw sewage, confirming this environment as a suitable, refuge habitat. The algae follow the nutrient–organic gradient and thrive in wastewater throughout its treatment until a sufficient amount of organic matter is provided. The treatment process, particularly during its biological phase, effectively removes Prototheca, reducing their concentrations by about two orders of magnitude to an average of 70 cells/mL in the final effluent, which corresponds to a removal efficiency of up to 99%. Still, the presence of Prototheca in the treated wastewater may pose a potential concern to the environment and to human health. This statement gains additional weight from the fact that the algae, unlike most bacteria, are highly resistant to inimical environmental conditions, which supports their survival in natural aquatic ecosystems from which they can contaminate urban or agricultural water systems. Here, under heavy organic pollution, the algae can form densely populated micro‐niches, which may facilitate their local persistence and accumulation in the environment. Species previously reported as pathogenic, with P. wickerhamii predominating, clearly outnumber other typically saprophytic species in both influent and effluent from treatment plants. All this underscores the need for regular monitoring of wastewater, both untreated and treated, and the downstream environment for the occurrence of Prototheca species. We advocate for the establishment of guideline limits for Prototheca in both pre‐ and post‐treatment water samples. This will allow to better delineate the ecological circulation of the algae, and thereby more accurately assess the reservoir and the risks of Prototheca infection.
