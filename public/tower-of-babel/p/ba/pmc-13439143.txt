When the usual spaces fail: the role of L5–S1 spinal anesthesia in the difficult spine
Authors: Ji Seon Jeong
Published: 2026

Neuraxial anesthesia remains the preferred anesthetic technique for many lower extremity orthopedic procedures because of its favorable effects on perioperative outcomes, recovery, and patient satisfaction. In hip and knee arthroplasty, the consensus recommendations continue to support neuraxial anesthesia over general anesthesia when no contraindication exists [1].

However, patients most likely to benefit from spinal anesthesia are often those in whom it is technically difficult to perform. For older adults with degenerative spinal disease, scoliosis, calcified ligaments, prior spinal change, or restricted flexion, conventional lumbar interspaces tend to be difficult to access [2].

Traditionally, the difficulty with spinal anesthesia has been framed as a problem of access. This perspective has shifted substantially with the growing role of neuraxial ultrasound. Contemporary evidence now supports ultrasound-guided neuraxial puncture over the reliance on anatomical landmarks alone, with improved first-pass success and overall procedural performance [3]. Similarly, systematic review data show that preprocedural lumbar neuraxial ultrasound improves first-attempt success and reduces the need for needle redirection, with particularly meaningful benefits in obese patients and those with difficult spinal anatomies [4]. These developments have shifted neuraxial anesthesia from a purely landmark-based technique to a more image-assisted and strategy-driven procedure [3,4].

However, successful neuraxial anesthesia requires more than a successful dural puncture. Even when intrathecal access is obtained, inadequate cephalad spread of the local anesthetic may result in insufficient block height and clinical failure. This issue is especially relevant at the lower lumbar levels. In an 8-year arthroplasty cohort, Colish et al. [5] found that spinal anesthetic failure was associated with lower insertion levels, specifically with the odds of failure increasing from 4.61 (95% CI [2.02–10.54]) at L4–L5 to 7.66 (95% CI [2.47–23.7]) at L5–S1, relative to L2–L3. Due to this concern, enthusiasm for the L5–S1 interspace has historically been limited [5], despite its obvious practical appeal as a rescue site when spaces that are more cephalad are difficult or impossible to access [2].

In this issue of the Korean Journal of Anesthesiology, Khalid et al. [6] offer important prospective observational data that challenge the assumption that the L5–S1 access compromises anesthetic reliability. In 54 patients undergoing total hip or knee arthroplasty with ultrasound-assisted spinal anesthesia at L5–S1 with 10 mg of hypobaric 0.33% bupivacaine, surgical completion was achieved without anesthetic supplementation in 92.6% of the patients, and adequate anesthesia for surgical incision was achieved in 98.2%. No patient required conversion to general anesthesia, and full motor recovery occurred at 198 ± 53 min. These findings suggest that under appropriate technical and pharmacological conditions, L5–S1 may serve as a clinically useful fallback option rather than merely a last resort.

The broader significance of this study lies in its reframing of difficult spinal anesthesia as both an anatomical and pharmacological problem. Ultrasound guidance helps with the anatomical issue by improving the identification of a usable interspace and facilitating needle placement. The manipulation of solution baricity addresses the pharmacological issue by influencing intrathecal distribution and block height. Previous studies have shown that hypobaric spinal local anesthetics can be advantageous in arthroplasty settings, particularly when patient positioning is used deliberately to shape the spread of the drug [7]. More recent prospective observational data further support the favorable anesthetic and recovery profiles of low-dose hypobaric mepivacaine and bupivacaine in fast-track hip and knee arthroplasty pathways [8]. Taken together, these studies strengthen the biological and clinical plausibility of the approach used by Khalid et al. [6–8].

The attractiveness of the L5–S1 interspace is also practical. Contemporary reviews of lumbar neuraxial anesthesia in patients with challenging anatomies emphasize that technical success depends on flexible strategy selection rather than rigid adherence to conventional mid-lumbar targets [2]. In this context, L5–S1 remains highly relevant as a rescue or alternative route. Recent randomized data comparing L5–S1 with L3–4 spinal anesthesia under real-time ultrasound guidance in total knee arthroplasty suggest that L5–S1 may offer a wider and more clearly identifiable target, lower sensory spread, and greater hemodynamic stability, albeit with different block characteristics [9]. These observations complement the present study by underscoring that L5–S1 should not be viewed simply as an inferior low lumbar option but rather as a distinct neuraxial strategy with its own technical and pharmacodynamic profile [2,9].

Nevertheless, caution should be exercised. Khalid et al. [6] conducted a single-center observational study with a modest sample size and no control group. In addition, three of the four patients who required supplemental opioids requested them after prolonged injection-to-incision intervals due to unanticipated operating room delays, suggesting that workflow and anesthetic duration remain important determinants of success. The lower rate of completion without supplementation in hip compared to knee arthroplasty also suggests that procedure-specific demands may matter, even if the study was not powered for subgroup comparisons.

Regardless, this study highlights an important evolution in regional anesthesia practice. The future of neuraxial anesthesia in difficult spines is unlikely to depend on needle placement alone. Rather, it will depend on integrating anatomical imaging, informed interspace selection, and the deliberate control of intrathecal drug behavior [2–4]. In this framework, the L5–S1 interspace deserves reconsideration, not as a desperate fallback but as a strategically valuable option for preserving spinal anesthesia in patients with challenging anatomies [2,6,9].

Future research should not consider simply whether spinal anesthesia can be performed in a difficult spine, but how it can be optimized once access is achieved.
