Sudden Painful Proptosis following Orbital Evisceration: Early Implant Hemorrhage in the Anophthalmic Socket
Authors: Mehmet Serhat Mangan, Yusuf Ziya Sahinoglu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To report a case of early orbital hemorrhage following four-petal evisceration and highlighting the clinical presentation, diagnostic process, and surgical management.Methods:A 49-year-old male patient, with a history of chronic renal insufficiency on renal replacement therapy and anticoagulation treatment, presented with sudden painful proptosis 1 week after four-petal evisceration. Orbital magnetic resonance imaging revealed a hyperintense area suggestive of hemorrhage around the implant. Initial management involved intravenous steroid therapy to alleviate symptoms.Results:Despite the administration of steroids, the patient’s proptosis and associated symptoms persisted. This led to the decision to perform surgical intervention. The surgical procedure encompassed the removal of the silicone implant, evacuation of the hemorrhage, and subsequently reinsertion of the same implant. Postoperatively, the patient’s symptoms improved significantly.Conclusions:To our knowledge, this report represents the first case of early orbital hemorrhage developed following four-petal evisceration. This case underscores the potential for the development of early orbital hemorrhage following four-petal evisceration. Prompt recognition and appropriate surgical intervention are crucial for the management of the hemorrhage and preserving orbital function.

INTRODUCTION

Evisceration, a surgical procedure involving the comprehensive extraction of ocular contents through either corneal or scleral incisions, aims to retain the integrity of the conjunctiva, sclera, extraocular muscles, and orbital fat.12 Evisceration can be performed via various techniques, with the four-petal and two-scleral flap techniques being the most commonly used ones. To the authors’ best knowledge, limited number of studies exist in the available literature reporting hemorrhage as an early complication of the evisceration, and no report could be found for four-petal technique in particular. The need to fulfill this gap was the main triggering factor of the current case report. Therefore, the authors present the first case of early implant hemorrhage with sudden painful proptosis following four-petal evisceration.

CASE REPORT

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report and any accompanying images. This case report is in compliance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

A 49-year-old male patient who underwent an evisceration was referred to our clinic 1 week after the procedure for developing symptoms of sudden painful proptosis. The patient had experienced penetrating ocular trauma to the left eye 20 years prior to surgery, and it had no light perception. As phthisis bulbi had developed, the patient had been uncomfortable cosmetically and surgical intervention had been decided. Four-petal evisceration had been performed with 20 mm silicone implant by another oculoplastic surgeon in another healthcare facility. The patient had diabetes mellitus, chronic renal insufficiency. He was undergoing renal replacement therapy 3 times a week and using anticoagulation medication. Following the developing symptoms, the patient had been referred to our clinic. Physical examination revealed chemosis, subconjunctival hemorrhage, and proptosis [Figure 1a]. There was no history of trauma following the surgery and anticoagulant therapy had been cessated 1 week before the surgery.

(c) Image of the patient after hemorrhage was evacuated. Proptosis regressed. (d) Image of the patient after the ocular prosthesis implantation

Orbital magnetic resonance imaging was obtained, and T1 and T2 images revealed hyperintense area surrounding the implant which was consistent with hemorrhage [Figure 2a-d]. Consulting with the nephrologist, images with contrast enhancement could not be obtained as the patient had chronic renal insufficiency. As initial therapy attempts, 1 g intravenous methylprednisolone was administered for 3 consecutive days; however, the symptoms failed to regress and even deteriorated [Figure 1b]. After the failure of medical treatment, surgical intervention was immediately planned under general anesthesia. During the surgery, the scleral flaps were reached following the dissection of conjunctiva and tenon. Immediately after dissecting the anterior scleral flaps, the hemorrhagic material started to ooze. After the posterior flaps were dissected, the hemorrhage was clearly seen. The implant was taken out from the socket and the hemorrhage was evacuated. Local bleeding areas were cauterized and tamponed [Video 1].

(a) Coronal, (b) Axial T1, (c) Sagittal, (d) Axial T2 magnetic resonance image of the left orbit demonstrating the implant and hyperintense surrounding hemorrhage

After the socket was cleared from the hemorrhage and ensured to be free of bleeding, the same implant previously taken out at the beginning of the procedure was reinserted. The orbit was closed with posterior scleral flaps to be sutured first and anteriors to be second. In addition, a conformer was placed. In the early postoperative stage, the patient’s symptoms improved and proptosis regressed [Figure 1c]. Following the recovery of the anophthalmic socket, the patient was able to use ocular prosthesis [Figure 1d].

DISCUSSION

This report presents a case of orbital hemorrhage in the anophthalmic socket, which is one of the rare complications of evisceration, and the authors discuss the possible underlying causes of hemorrhage development. A study comparing the complications and outcomes of enucleation and evisceration identified 8 types of complications yet hemorrhage was not listed among the expected ones.2 Holmes et al. also evaluated the surigical outcomes of patients underwent either evisceration or enucleation and did not report orbital hemorrhage or hematoma.3 On the other hand, two seperate large case series studies by Sobti et al. and Lang et al., reported hemorrhage in 2 (0.6%) and 5 (4.9%), respectively.45 In addition, Chamberlain et al. recently reported orbital late hemorrhage developed after 3 years masquerading an orbital mass.6 However, in contrast to our report in these studies, surgical technique was enucleation with implantation of porous material and detailed information regarding the management of the hemorrhage was not mentioned.

Orbital hemorrhage can occur spontaneously and its relationship with systemic conditions and vascular abnormalities have been reported.123 It is a well-accepted and documented complication of orbital surgeries and it is not uncommon for oculoplastic surgeons to encounter bleeding after various orbital surgeries such as blepharoplasty and deep orbital surgeries.7891011 Custer and Trinkaus conducted a study to identify potential risk factors for bleeding after orbital surgeries and reported two cases following enucleation with neither of them requiring intervention.10 They also reported two cases, one being blepharoplasty and other lateral orbitotomy, treated with drainage of hematoma as we performed in our case.

As discussed previously, our patient had numerous comorbidities such as renal insufficiency, diabetes and anticoagulation therapy which might have contributed to postoperative hemorrhage. A cohort analyzing the relationship between comorbidities of the patient and implant extrusion revealed that although the comorbidities of patients were not directly associated with implant exposure, they affected optimal wound healing by interfering with inflammatory processes, which eventually led to insufficient healing of the socket and implant extrusion.12 Guidance from expert panels in oculoplastic surgery suggests discontinuing antithrombotic agents before procedures. Considering evisceration as a deep orbital surgery, in our case, although the anticoagulation treatment had been cessated prior to primary surgery, incomplete hemostasis combined with continuation of the drug after the procedure might led to hemorrhage.131415

The surgical technique used in this case was four-petal technique, and while there are reports of occurence of orbital hemorrhage associated with the 2-scleral flap techniques, the four-petal technique lacks such evidence in the literature. Four-petal evisceration was introduced by Sanz López and Sales Sanz in 2003 and described as “double scleral covering evisceration”.16 In 2007, they revealed the long-term results of it, rebranding as four-petal technique, and they did not report orbital hematoma as a complication of their operations.17 A study compared four-petal evisceration with the 2-scleral flap evisceration and reported multiple types of complication, yet hemorrhage was not encountered in either techniques.18 However, in a larger series of 201 patients undergoing 2-scleral flap evisceration, orbital hemorrhage was reported in one case which occurred within 24 h and treated by just drainage.19

Implant materials were found to interact with their surroundings at histopathological and biochemical level. They trigger local inflammation and interfere with the physiologic pathways such as coagulation cascade.2021 Several studies also were conducted about surgical complications of orbital implants. Formerly mentioned study by Smith et al. reported one case of hemorrhage after evisceration and the implant material was alumina.19 A case series by Lin and Liao analyzed 256 patients that underwent enucleation or evisceration with porous implants. Implant exposure rates were found to be different for each type of material. Nevertheless, no orbital hemorrhage was reported.22 In another study evaluating the complications associated with four types of porous implants, 86 patients underwent 2-scleral flap evisceration and none of the patients developed orbital hemorrhage as a complication.23 Although no histopathological data were either analyzed or provided in these studies, we can infer that the interactions at cellular level take part in the occurrence of these complications. Hence, we cannot certainly claim that the cause of the hemorrhage in our patient was directly related to the silicone implant. In addition, similar to previous studies, we were unable to perform histopathological examinations to prove otherwise. On the other hand, the current report provides future insights for researchers to evaluate silicone materials’ hemostatic properties as this is the first study to report orbital hemorrhage following evisceration with a silicone implant.

In conclusion, orbital hemorrhage has rarely been encountered in evisceration and literature seems to lack evidence in terms of this particular complication. To the authors’ knowledge, there is no previous report of a patient with orbital hemorrhage developed following four-petal evisceration using silicone implant. The authors of this report speculate that the patient’s comorbidities, surgical technique, or the implant type might have individually or collectively played a role in the development of orbital hemorrhage. Further studies are needed to provide additional evidence to understand the causative agents of developing hemorrhage.
