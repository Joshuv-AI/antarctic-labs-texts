Atypical behavior of recurrent glioblastoma tumor cells with a highly adherent radial glial phenotype
Authors: Manjusha Vaidya, Jonhoi Smith, Julia Pessaia, Melvin Field, Kiminobu Sugaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma (GBM) is the most common malignant primary brain tumor, characterized by a high recurrence rate despite aggressive therapy. We present a case of a 32-year-old male with a recurrent WHO Grade IV IDH1-mutant astrocytoma after undergoing standard Stupp protocol chemoradiation and tumor-treating field therapy. Repeat surgery was performed where in vitro analysis of recurrent GBM cells revealed atypical behavior, rapid adhesion within minutes of plating, and the formation of radial glial-like cells (RGCs) with 3D aggregated cells, phenotypes absent in the primary tumor. These brain lipid-binding protein positive RGCs exhibited elongated processes that facilitated cancer cell migration, potentially contributing to tumor invasiveness. Extensive treatment between the primary and recurrent tumors may have induced this phenotypic shift, highlighting therapy-induced plasticity as a key factor in recurrence. The emergence of RGCs in recurrent GBM underscores the need for targeted therapies addressing tumor adaptability to improve treatment outcomes.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is an incurable cancer of central nervous system with a median survival of 8–9 months as reported by the Central Brain Tumor Registry of the United States (CBTRUS) database. According to CBTRUS, glioblastoma comprises 50.9% of all malignant brain tumors [1]. The fifth edition of 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of central nervous system (CNS) tumors categorizes glioblastoma as a Grade 4 astrocytoma, a highly aggressive tumor with a heterogenous population of cancer cells [2]. The current standard treatment follows Stupp protocol that includes safe, maximal, surgical resection of the tumor followed by concomitant radiation and chemo-therapy [3]. Despite these interventions, the prognosis remains poor. Advances in molecular-targeted therapies, immunotherapy, and noninvasive technologies, such as tumor-treating fields (TTF) and sonodynamic therapy, offer new treatment avenues, though none have yet proven curative [4,5].

A key challenge in GBM is preventing recurrence despite surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy which is thought to be driven by therapy-resistant cancer stem cells (CSCs) and the tumor’s ability to adapt under treatment pressure. Even after intensive therapy, including temozolomide (TMZ) and radiation, a subset of tumor cells survives, undergoing genetic and epigenetic changes that promote a more aggressive phenotype [3,6]. Studies show that standard treatments can reshape the transcriptomic landscape, enhancing stemness, altering cell morphology, and increasing tumor plasticity [7–10]. As a result, recurrent GBMs often contain a higher proportion of CSCs compared to their primary counterparts, making them furthermore resistant to conventional therapies [6]. According to the researchers, the stem cells responsible for the recurrence and the invasiveness of GBM are located in neurogenic regions of the brain and should be targeted for treatment to avoid recurrence. The primary tumors in close contact with subventricular region (SVZ) or subgranular region (SGZ) have high recurrence rates even out of the radiation treatment field highlighting the relationship between the location of the lesion and the neurogenic niche [11–13]. The periventricular zone is implicated in prognosticator predicting the survival outcome of the GBM patients contributing to the tumor recurrence and should be targeted for surgical removal of the lesions to avoid the relapse [14,15].

While CSCs pose a serious hindrance to the efficacy of the GBM treatment, in-depth investigation into their biogenesis, genomics, and molecular networks will help in designing highly effective treatments thereby improving patient survival or even in finding a cure for the disease.

Dekker et al. conducted a multiomics study with primary and recurrent GBM tumors comparing signaling pathways, transcriptomics, proteomics, and phosphor-proteomic analysis. They found substantial molecular differences between primary and recurrent tumors, suggesting that standard therapy exerts selective pressure, altering the tumor at multiple biological levels [16]. Actively dividing tumor cells are highly susceptible to chemo-radiation therapy, while a less proliferative, stem-like subpopulation evades treatment, acquiring genetic and epigenetic modifications that promote therapy resistance and recurrence [10,17,18].

In this study, we report an unusual cellular transformation in recurrent GBM, where tumor cells acquired a radial glia-like phenotype (RGCs)—a feature absent in the primary tumor. These RGCs exhibited rapid adhesion, 3D aggregation, and elongated processes that may facilitate tumor invasion and recurrence. Given that the patient underwent fractionated radiation, temozolomide, and TTF therapy, these findings suggest that therapy-induced plasticity may contribute to tumor progression by promoting novel migratory phenotypes. Understanding this transformation could provide new insights into recurrence mechanisms and help identify therapeutic strategies targeting therapy-induced tumor adaptability. As recurrent tumor samples were obtained following concomitant and adjuvant treatments, it is not feasible to determine or speculate on the individual effects of these therapies on the observed phenotypic characteristics.

This report should be considered a hypothesis-generating observational case study with potential clinical relevance. Further investigations using molecular analyses and orthotopic xenograft models are warranted to establish robust mechanistic insights and translational implications. Further studies involving rigorous molecular analyses and orthotopic xenografts for example, would be required to establish robust clinical relevance.

Case presentation

In June 2022, the patient presented to AdventHealth’s Emergency room unresponsive after a witnessed seizure. The patient was a 30-year-old right-handed married male with a 4-week history of progressive headache, nausea with emesis and worsening balance with falls. In the emergency room, the patient was intubated for airway protection as he was unconscious. He was given Keppra for seizure and then versed after a second seizure was witnessed while in the emergency room. A CT of the brain was performed which showed a large bifrontal brain mass eccentric to the right with edema, mass effect, and mild obstructive hydrocephalus (Figure 1).

Presenting CT Brain without contrast showing a large bifrontal brain mass with shift.

The patient was admitted to the Neurological Intensive Care Unit (NICU) for monitoring and stabilization. The remainder of his medical history was unremarkable with no routine home medications, illnesses, or risk factors. While intubated, his best neurological exam revealed localization to stimulation, but he did not follow commands off sedation and his eyes would only open to stimulation. His pupils were reactive and his fundoscopic exam did not suggest papilledema. His Glasgow Coma Score was 8. An MRI brain with and without contrast was performed showing a bifrontal mass measuring approximately 8 cm × 7 cm × 5 cm eccentric to the right, involving the corpus callosum anteriorly. The mass had central necrosis with heterogenous enhancement that was compressing the right frontal horn of the lateral ventricle and was trapping the left lateral ventricle causing some obstructive hydrocephalus of the temporal horn. The mass caused over 1.5 cm of right to left shift and had extensive flair and T2 changes within it (Supplementary Figure 1).

Based on the patient’s young age and overall excellent health prior to admission, surgery was recommended to obtain a tissue diagnosis and relieve mass effect and pressure. The following day, the patient underwent an Image-guided stereotactic right frontotemporal craniotomy and resection of the mass. Preoperatively, the patient’s wife provided consent for him to be enrolled in our IRB approved study (Advent Health IRB- HBTT_1543713. University of Central Florida IRB-STUDY00004437) evaluating stem cells and stem cell markers in malignant brain tumors. Intraoperatively, fresh tumor samples were submitted for study evaluation. Figure 2 describes the details of the tissue collection, storage, transportation, and the processing for in vitro cultures.

Storage, transportation, and processing of the resected tumor tissue.

The surgery was uneventful and postoperatively the patient returned to the NICU. The following day, the patient was awake, following commands, and was extubated. He was neurologically intact with a Glasgow Coma Scale of 15. His headaches, nausea and balance were improved. A postoperative MRI was completed and showed a gross total resection of the enhancing disease and a small amount of persistent flair/T2 disease involving the corpus callosum and right frontal horn of the lateral ventricle. The mass effect and hydrocephalus were improved (Supplementary Figure 2).

The patient was started on Decadron postoperatively and continued on Keppra. His postoperative course was unremarkable, and he was discharged home on post-operative day 7.

The final pathology from his tumor found that the patient had a MGMT unmethylated, IDH1/2 IDH R132H mutated CNS WHO Grade 4 Glioblastoma brain tumor with ATRX loss of nuclear expression, prominent gemistocytic morphology, p53 positive staining and a MIB-1 index of approximately 15%. Based on the pathology, the patient was referred to radiation oncology and neuro-oncology and was started on Stupp protocol chemoradiation 6 weeks postoperatively during which he was maintained on low dose Decadron and Keppra.

Systemic treatment comprised fractionated radiation therapy to the tumor bed and surrounding tissues at a dose of 2 Gy – 5 times per week for 6 weeks for a total dose of 6000cGY. At the same time, he was started on oral Temozolomide chemotherapy at 75 mg/m2 BSA per day for 6 weeks. Once completed, the patient was maintained on Temozolomide chemotherapy at 150 mg/m2 BSA 5 days per month for 6 months. The patient was also enrolled in the Optune TRIDENT trial (EF-32 TRIDENT) utilizing TTF-field therapy during his chemoradiation treatment. The patient continued Temozolomide until January 2023 and then was monitored with serial MRI imaging every 2 to 3 months.

The patient remained stable until May 2024 when routine imaging suggested some changes in the area of surgery interpreted as slight worsening concerning for recurrence versus changes related to treatment (Supplementary Figure 3).

Patient was presented at the hospital’s multidisciplinary tumor board and recommendation was to rechallenge with temozolomide and continue Optune with repeat MRI including perfusion in 2 months. Repeating MRI with perfusion showed continued progression and increased metabolism in the region adjacent to the previous surgery and it was recommended the patient undergo a second surgery for tumor resection and molecular reclassification (Supplementary Figure 4). It was also recommended to implant Gamma Tile Cesium 131 radiation seeds along the resection cavity if viable tumor was encountered during surgery to aid in local tumor control postoperatively.

In August 2024, the patient underwent re-exploration craniotomy. A recurrent tumor was found at the time of surgery and was resected (Supplementary Figure 5). 8 Gamma Tile wafers were implanted, each containing 4 Cesium 131 3.5 U radioactive seeds per wafer (Supplementary Figure 6). Tumor was sent for pathology and reanalysis of stem cell markers and tumor stem cell characteristics. Post-operative imaging showed a gross total resection of the residual disease and excellent placement of Gamma Tile seeds along the resection margins.

The patient went home on postoperative day #1 with no neurological deficits. Two weeks after surgery he developed a wound dehiscence over an area where his glasses were pressing on his incision. This was repaired in the operating room and subsequently healed without complication. The pathology from the second surgery in August 2024 showed a recurrent IDH1 R132H (+), MGMT unmethylated WHO Grade 4 Glioblastoma with ATRX loss of nuclear expression, p53(+), CDKN2A homozygous deletion (−), and a MIB-1 index of 10–15%.

He was subsequently restarted on temozolomide at 75 mg/m2 BSA for 6 weeks and then began vorasidenib (Voranigo) and lomustine (Gleostine).

The hyper-adhesive phenotype of the cultured cells

The primary and the recurrent GBM cells grown under adherent conditions were incubated with commercially available enzyme cell dissociation medium, ACCUTASETM, for further maintenance. The cell lines showed differences in the time for detachment from the culture flask as well as cell-cell dissociation. Where 95% cells from the primary tumor detached within 5 min of incubation (at 37 °C), over half of the cell population from the recurrent GBM remained adhered to the surface of the culture flask. The adhered cells of the recurrent GBM did not detach in spite of a continued incubation of an additional 4 min. Even a gentle rinse of the bottom of the flask with the cell culture media could not remove the cells. The image below shows the cells before dissociation as well as various time points as the incubation in the dissociation reagents progressed (Figure 8).

Dissociation of primary and the recurrent GBM cells grown under adherent culture conditions. The cells from the recurrent GBM could not be dissociated with the cell dissociation reagent even after 9 min of incubation and at a rinse at the end of the incubation period. However, the primary GBM cells detached from the flask within 5 min and dissociated after gentle pipetting.

Results and discussion

In the current case, recurrent GBM cells exhibited distinct morphological and behavioral differences compared to their primary counterpart. For example, some recurrent GBM cells, adhered to the culture flask within 3–5 min of seeding, and rapidly developed processes (Figures 3, 5). Given the intrinsic biomechanical heterogeneity of GBM, it is possible that these adherent cells possessed upregulated adhesion molecules, contributing to their rapid attachment. Rezk et al. previously demonstrated that tumor core cells exhibit stronger adhesion properties, whereas peripheral cells tend to be more migratory [19]. So far, we have processed and cultured the tumor cells from 25 other samples, including 3 recurrent ones. Even after 10–15 min of seeding, the cells dissociated from these tumors did not display the unique behavior of the cells described in the present case study. It is unlikely that the atypical cellular behavior observed in this case is due to technical artifacts, as similar phenomena were not observed in other glioblastoma samples processed and analyzed under comparable conditions.

A particularly striking observation was the formation of RGCs (Figures 4, 7). Additionally, unlike the primary tumor, where cells adhered individually, in a monolayer, the recurrent tumor cells formed spheroids on top of the adherent layer within three days (Figures 3, 4). When these spheroids were transferred to adherent conditions, they differentiated into the RGCs, as confirmed by BLBP expression, a known radial glial marker (Figure 7) [20]. Interestingly, peripheral cells from the spheroid exhibited strong adhesion and differentiation into RGCs, suggesting that these spheroids contained a stem-like subpopulation unique to recurrent GBM. Small, rounded cells were observed moving along the elongated processes of RGCs, supporting their potential role in tumor cell migration and tumor invasiveness.

Under normal physiological conditions, radial glia serve as neural stem cells (NSCs) during development, also guiding neuronal migration, synapse formation, and cortical organization [21]. However, in the context of GBM, the RGCs are a subpopulation of CSCs that stay dormant, but may become activated by inflammatory signals, promoting tumor progression [22,23]. Given that the patient underwent radiation and TTF therapy, both of which are known to induce inflammation, it is possible that therapy-induced changes triggered the emergence of RGCs [24,25].

Beyond increasing tumor heterogeneity, GBM-derived RGCs may contribute to tumor invasion through mitotic somal translocation (MST), a process observed in outer subventricular zone expansion during corticogenesis [26]. TTF therapy disrupts mitotic division by interfering with tubulin and septin alignment, primarily targeting actively proliferating cells [27]. However, quiescent CSCs may have evaded this effect, possibly undergoing genetic and epigenetic modifications later on. These alterations likely promoted their differentiation into RGCs, facilitating tumor cell migration. Notably, outer RGCs (oRG) exhibit MST independently of mitosis, meaning this invasive behavior does not rely on intact microtubules, further supporting a treatment-induced phenotypic shift [28].

Additionally, recurrent GBM cells demonstrated increased adhesion and intercellular attachment, resisting dissociation attempts significantly longer than their primary counterparts (Figure 8). Post-radiation GBM cells are known to develop heightened resistance and increased invasion potential, likely due to upregulated adhesion proteins in areas of cell-to-cell contact [29]. Given that this patient underwent extensive radiation therapy, it is plausible that the observed hyper-adhesive phenotype resulted from radiation-induced molecular changes, enhancing tumor cell interactions and possibly increasing invasive capabilities [29,30].

These findings suggest that recurrent GBM adapts through therapy-induced plasticity, giving rise to radial glial-like phenotypes and enhanced adhesion properties, which may contribute to greater invasiveness and treatment resistance. Targeting these therapy-driven adaptations could be crucial for improving treatment strategies and preventing recurrence.

This case study is limited to qualitative observations. However, the findings hold clinical significance, warranting future studies incorporating quantitative analysis and functional validation.

Conclusion

In this case study, our patient received concomitant treatment of radiation, TMZ and TTF, followed by a year-long adjuvant TMZ regimen. While these therapies likely eliminated a significant portion of cancer cells, they may have spared CSCs, which are known for their inherent resistance to treatment. Persistent CSCs, already harboring mutations in key genes such as IDH1/2 and ATRX, may have undergone further genetic and epigenetic modifications due to treatment-induced stress. These changes likely contributed to the emergence of a more aggressive, therapy-resistant, hyper-adhesive phenotype, characterized by RGC differentiation. These findings underscore the conceivable role of therapy-induced plasticity in GBM progression and highlight the need for targeted approaches to prevent CSC-driven recurrence.
