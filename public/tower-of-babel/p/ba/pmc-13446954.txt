Efficacy and Safety of Polyethylene Glycol versus Placebo in Pediatric Foreign-body Ingestion: A Randomized Pilot Trial
Authors: Veerabhadra Radhakrishna, Nitin Kumar, Raghunath Bangalore Vasudeva, Mamatha Basavaraju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Foreign body ingestion (FBI) is common in children, with 10%–20% requiring endoscopic intervention and 1% requiring surgery. Despite its frequency, evidence supporting the use of laxatives in FBI is scarce, limited to case reports, case series, and expert opinions. This pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluated the feasibility of a larger RCT assessing polyethylene glycol (PEG) versus placebo in pediatric FBI.Methods:Children aged 1–18 years with the FBI were randomized to receive PEG (1 g/kg/day) or aspartame placebo (8 mg/kg/day) until foreign body passage or a maximum of 7 days. Feasibility outcomes included recruitment, retention, adherence, acceptability, and tolerability. The primary efficacy outcome (hypothesis-generating) was foreign-body transit time.Results:Among 69 children (71 FBIs), retention was 100%, and adherence was 96%. The acceptability and satisfaction scores were high (4.9/5 and 4.7/5, respectively). No adverse events were reported. The mean FB transit time was shorter in the PEG group than in the placebo (55.8 ± 39.3 vs. 86.8 ± 66.9 h; P = 0.032; Cohen’s d ≈ 0.54). The reduction in FB transit time appeared more pronounced in children >10 years (53.7 ± 12.7 vs. 112.8 ± 45.3 h; P < 0.001; Cohen’s d ≈ 1.77).Conclusion:A randomized trial of PEG in pediatric FBI is feasible, well accepted, and safe. Preliminary findings suggest PEG may enhance foreign body transit, particularly in older children, supporting the rationale for a larger, adequately powered RCT.

INTRODUCTION

Foreign body ingestion (FBI) is a common problem in children, peaking between 6 months and 3 years of age, when oral exploration is frequent.[1] Although most ingested objects pass spontaneously, about 10%–20% require endoscopic removal, and nearly 1% need surgical intervention for complications.[12]

Despite its prevalence, the role of laxatives in facilitating foreign body passage remains unclear. Available evidence consists mainly of case reports, small case series, and expert opinions.[2345] Robust, randomized controlled data are lacking, leaving clinicians without standardized guidance for conservative management.

Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is a widely used osmotic laxative with a proven safety and efficacy profile in both pediatric and adult constipation.[678] Studies, including a Cochrane review and systematic analyses by ESPGHAN and NASPGHAN, have proved PEG’s superiority over lactulose in managing constipation, fecal impaction, and bowel preparation.[78] It is hydrophilic, inert, and non-absorbable, producing soft stools without causing inflammation.[910] Its favorable tolerability and low cost makes it an attractive agent for use in gastrointestinal (GI) treatment, drug delivery systems, and artificial blood cell substitutes.[910] Although PEG has occasionally been mentioned in case reports and expert opinions for managing ingested foreign bodies, the study is one of the first prospective pilot trials evaluating PEG as an active intervention in pediatric FBI.

Aspartame was selected as the placebo due to its nonlaxative properties and established safety profile.[111213] It is a commonly used artificial sweetener composed of L-phenylalanine and L-aspartic acid and is contraindicated in phenylketonuria (PKU). Safety evaluations, including large controlled studies, have demonstrated no neurotoxic or carcinogenic effects even at higher doses.[14151617]

This pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) aimed to assess the feasibility of a larger RCT by evaluating the acceptance, adherence, tolerability, and preliminary efficacy of PEG compared with placebo in children with FBI.

METHODS

Study design and participants

A pilot RCT was conducted in the Department of Pediatric Surgery at a tertiary center between June 2024 and May 2025. Children aged 1–18 years who presented with foreign body (FB) ingestion were screened for eligibility. Children were excluded if they presented more than 24 h after ingestion; had FBs in the esophagus; had multiple magnets; had a combination of a magnet and a metal; or had radiolucent or impacted FBs. Children with GI pathology, prior GI surgery, PKU, or seizure disorders were also excluded.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Reg No. BMCRI/PS/EC/55/2024) and registered with the national clinical trial registry (No. 2024/02/079000). Written informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians, and assent was obtained from children aged 7 years or older.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomized using computer-generated block randomization (block sizes of 4, 6, and 8) to receive either PEG or aspartame. Allocation concealment was ensured through sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Both syrups were identical in color, consistency, and taste. Commercially available syrup PEG (LAXOPEG®-FC) was used after masking the labels. Syrup aspartame was prepared and dispensed by a pharmacist not involved in the study. Both participants and treating physicians were blinded using a unique randomization code.

Intervention

Children received syrup PEG (1 g/kg/day, up to 26 g/day) or syrup aspartame (8 mg/kg/day, up to 200 mg/day) in two divided doses until FB passage or for a maximum of 7 days, whichever occurred first. Both groups received standard supportive care, including observation, symptomatic treatment, and endoscopic or surgical retrieval when indicated.

Assessment and monitoring

At presentation, an abdominal radiograph confirmed FB location. Demographic and clinical data, including the child’s age, sex, weight, type of ingested foreign body, mode of presentation, stooling pattern, history of constipation, and relevant investigations, were recorded. Constipation was defined as the presence of two or more of the following symptoms lasting more than 4 weeks: frequency of stools ≤2 times a week, excessive stool retention, fecal incontinence more than once a week after the acquisition of toileting skills, presence of a large mass on abdominal or per rectal examination, hard or painful bowel movements, or large-diameter stools obstructing the toilet.[6]

The Leech score was used to assess fecal loading on abdominal radiograph.[18] The colon was divided into three segments, each scored 0–5, based on stool burden. A combined score of more than eight indicated a significantly loaded colon.[18]

The foreign bodies were managed by a structured approach, as depicted in Figure 1.[7] Parents were instructed to monitor stools for FB passage, confirmed by direct visualization. If the FB was not identified and a follow-up radiograph was negative, the time of passage was recorded as “unknown,” and the case was excluded from efficacy analysis.

If a child had more than five bowel movements a day, the dose of medication was halved; if no stool was passed after 48 h, the dose was increased by 50%. Parents were instructed to return if the child developed abdominal distension, pain, vomiting, or fever. Adverse events were defined as previously reported for PEG[6] (abdominal distension, vomiting, nausea, abdominal pain, loose stools, and diaper rash) and aspartame[14] (headache, nausea, abdominal pain, dizziness, and urticaria). Children with sharp FBI carry a higher risk of mucosal injury and perforation; hence, they were more closely monitored [Figure 1]. Details of endoscopic or surgical procedures, the date and time of spontaneous passage of FB, and any complications were meticulously recorded. Children were followed up for at least 3 months after the spontaneous passage or retrieval of the FB.

This pilot trial was primarily designed to assess the feasibility, with efficacy outcomes evaluated in a hypothesis-generating manner. Feasibility outcomes included willingness to participate in the study, assessment of execution of randomization and blinding, acceptability of intervention, ease of medication use, adherence to the treatment rate, lost to follow-up rate, adverse events related to the drug or placebo, satisfaction rate with the treatment process, and recommendation of the treatment program to others.

Primary efficacy outcome (hypothesis-generating) was FB transit time, defined as the time from ingestion of the FB to its documented passage in stools. Secondary efficacy outcomes (hypothesis-generating) included the need for endoscopic or surgical intervention, and subgroup-specific FB transit time based on age-group, weight centile, FB characteristics (shape), and radiographic findings. The subgroup analyses were pre-specified a priori, given the known influence of these factors on GI transit. The effect size derived from the pilot data was used to calculate the sample size for the future RCT. The anticipated effect size was based on the difference in mean FB transit time between the PEG and placebo groups, using the pooled standard deviation obtained from the pilot study. Sample size estimates for the future RCT were calculated assuming a two-sided α level of 0.01 and 90% power, with adjustment for potential attrition.

Statistical analysis

As this was a pilot feasibility study, statistical analyses were primarily descriptive and exploratory. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages and compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with range and compared using the independent sample t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Parametric tests were used to provide preliminary effect-size estimates and to generate hypotheses; normality assumptions were assessed descriptively. Given the exploratory nature of the analyses, no multiple-comparison adjustment was performed, and all efficacy findings were interpreted cautiously. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Acceptability and satisfaction were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale.

RESULTS

A total of 114 children presented with 116 FBIs during the study period. After excluding 45 children, 69 children with 71 FBIs were enrolled [Figure 2]. Thirty-seven (54%) were boys, and 31 (45%) were between 5 and 10 years of age.

Study flow chart

Blunt objects accounted for 43 (61%) cases, with coins being the most common (n = 30, 42%). Other ingested FBs included paper pins (6), magnets (5), scarf pins (3), button batteries (3), bangle pieces (3), bolts (2), screws (2), and keys (2). Less frequently ingested objects (one case each) included a button, a silver ring, a marble, an earring, an endodontic files, an air pump pin, a hair clip, a charging pin, a nail, a needle, a pen nib, a safety pin, a stapler pin, a zip, and a sharpener blade [Figure 3]. All children ingested a single object, except for a 17-year-old girl who ingested three bangle pieces.

The diameter of the blunt foreign body ranged from 0.5 to 3 cm, and the length of the sharp objects ranged from 1 to 4 cm. The mean time to presentation was 7.8 h ± 6.7 h (range: 0.5–23.5 h). Seven (10%) children presented with abdominal pain, and the rest were asymptomatic. One child had a history of constipation, and radiographic evidence of fecal loading was seen in 48 (70%) children.

All eligible children and parents consented to join the study [Figure 2]. Randomization and allocation procedures were conducted without difficulties. Thirty-four children were randomized to the PEG group and 35 to the placebo group. All 69 children completed follow-up (100% retention rate). Three children missed one dose each, giving an adherence rate of 96%. The mean parental acceptance score was 4.9/5, and the ease-of-use score was 4.8/5. Overall, 98% of the parents reported they would recommend the treatment program to others, and 94% expressed satisfaction with the treatment program (score: 4.7/5).

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the PEG and placebo groups [Table 1]. No adverse effects related to PEG or aspartame were reported. No child showed aversion, crying, or spitting during drug administration. Both drugs were well tolerated, and there was no protocol violation. Follow-up ranged from 5 to 17 months.

Using the pilot-derived estimates, a future RCT designed to detect a clinically meaningful reduction in transit time would require approximately 100 participants per group (α = 0.01, 90% power). Allowing for an anticipated 10% attrition rate, a total of 220 children would be required to power a larger RCT and prespecified subgroup analyses.

DISCUSSION

Unlike previous reports that relied solely on case reports and expert opinion, this pilot trial prospectively evaluated PEG use, with predefined feasibility and safety outcomes, thereby addressing an important gap in the existing literature. The study protocol was feasible, acceptable, and safe for conducting a larger RCT. All eligible parents and children showed willingness to participate in the study, reflecting high perceived benefit, low perceived risk, and confidence in the treating team.[19] Randomization and allocation procedures were conducted without protocol violations or breaches in allocation concealment. None of the participants or investigators were aware of treatment assignments, ensuring robust blinding and procedural integrity.

The retention rate was 100%. This can be attributed to the simple study design, the investigator’s constant engagement, the short follow-up duration, the shorter outcome assessment period (days to weeks), the ease of follow-up methods, and participants’ and parents’ confidence in the investigator, consistent with prior pediatric feasibility studies.[1920]

Treatment adherence rate was 96%, indicating strong caregiver understanding and cooperation. A high adherence rate was attributed to the simple dosing regimen, good tolerability, minimal adverse events, and no cost to participants.[21] Similarly, the high treatment acceptance (4.9/5) and ease of use (4.8/5) reflect the intervention’s noninvasive and palatable nature. Caregivers generally prefer a safe, orally administered regimen over invasive procedures.[2223] High satisfaction scores (94%; mean 4.7/5) also highlight effective counseling, clear communication, minimal disruption to daily activities, and a positive research experience.[24]

PEG has been widely used for bowel preparation and constipation in children and adults. Reported adverse effects include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, flatulence, bad palatability, and rectal bleeding.[25] None of these were observed in our study. By drawing fluid into the bowel lumen, PEG increases the bulk of the luminal content, thereby facilitating forward movement and reducing the surface contact time of FBs. In addition, as an inert substance, PEG may coat the FB, potentially minimizing mucosal irritation and related complications. However, in cases of sharp FBs, there is a theoretical risk of mucosal injury or perforation. Therefore, such children were closely monitored, with prompt and timely intervention undertaken in the event of symptoms or failure of FB progression [Figure 1]. Children tolerated PEG syrup well, without resistance, crying, or spitting during administration. Concerns regarding the safety of aspartame, used as the placebo vehicle, have been raised in the past; however, recent reviews by the International Agency for Research on Cancer, the World Health Organization, and the Food and Agriculture Organization Joint Expert Committee on Food Additives have confirmed its safety even at higher doses.[14151617] Consistent with this, none of the children in the placebo group experienced any adverse effects or intolerance.

A preliminary hypothesis-generating analysis suggested that PEG administration was associated with a shorter FB transit time compared with placebo, with a moderate effect size overall (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.54). These findings should be interpreted with caution. Given the differing physical characteristics and natural histories of various FBs, a uniform biological treatment effect of PEG across all FB types cannot be assumed, and the pooled efficacy findings should, therefore, be regarded as exploratory. The reduction in transit time appeared more pronounced among children older than 10 years, where it was substantial (Cohen’s d ≈ 1.77); however, this age-based subgroup analysis was exploratory, and the study was not powered to draw definitive conclusions regarding efficacy. Similar trends were observed among those who ingested blunt FBs or had fecal loading on radiographs; however, these subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance. Collectively, these findings support the feasibility of PEG use and provide a rationale for conducting a larger, adequately powered RCT to evaluate its efficacy and safety.

The pilot study has certain limitations. Reliance on parental visualization to determine FB passage may introduce ascertainment bias, and excluding children with unknown time of FB passage may result in post-randomization and attrition bias. These exclusions were undertaken to avoid outcome misclassification and measurement bias; however, these children were retained in safety and feasibility analyses. The study was not powered for confirmatory efficacy analyses, and intention-to-treat or sensitivity analyses were not pre-specified. The use of parametric statistical tests despite variable transit time data, together with multiple unadjusted subgroup analyses, increases the risk of type I error. Parametric tests were employed for simplicity and to provide preliminary effect size estimates; normality assumptions were assessed descriptively, and analyses were intended to be exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Routine fecal disimpaction of constipated children was not performed in this study to allow isolated evaluation of oral PEG and to avoid introducing an additional intervention that could confound the assessment of PEG-related outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Both PEG and aspartame were safe, well-tolerated, and acceptable to children and caregivers. Preliminary results demonstrated faster foreign body transit with PEG, particularly among children older than 10 years. A larger RCT is feasible and warranted to establish the efficacy and safety of PEG in pediatric FBI.
