Prediction of weaning outcomes from mechanical ventilation in critically ill patients based on the combination of ultrasound parameters of the heart, lung, and diaphragm: a prospective observational cohort study
Authors: Qian Zhou, Ying Xu, Jun Chen, Qin Gu, Wentao Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostextubation distress in critically ill patients with successful spontaneous breathing trials (SBTs) is unfavorable for prognosis. This study aimed to determine whether the combined application of multimodal ultrasound parameters of the heart, lung, and diaphragm can predict the mechanical ventilation weaning outcome among critically ill patients.MethodsFrom December 2022 to December 2023, a total of 74 patients (aged over 18 years old) mechanically ventilated for more than 48 hours and prepared for an SBT were selected from the Department of Critical Care Unit, Drum Tower Hospital, The Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University Medical School. Patients who met the criteria for weaning were prepared to undergo a 30-minute SBT, during which the heart, lungs, and diaphragm of the patients were examined via ultrasound. According to the weaning outcomes, patients were divided into a success group and a failure group. Ultrasound parameters with statistical significance in univariate analysis were incorporated into a binary logistic regression model to identify the independent influencing factors of weaning outcomes. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for statistical analysis.ResultsOut of the 74 included patients, 23 failed weaning. There were significant differences in diaphragm excursion (DE), diaphragm thickening fraction (DTF), bilateral lung ultrasound score (LUS), total LUS, and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) between the success and failure groups (P<0.05). The predictive performance of individual ultrasound parameters was as follows: LVEF achieved an AUC of 0.742 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.642–0.842; P<0.001], with optimal thresholds of 58.05% (sensitivity: 76.5%; specificity: 69.6%); LUS achieved an AUC of 0.837 (95% CI: 0.738–0.936; P<0.001), with an 80.4% sensitivity and 82.6% specificity at a cutoff value of 17.50. DE yielded an AUC of 0.895 (95% CI: 0.821–0.969; P<0.001), with an 82.4% sensitivity and a 95.7% specificity at a cutoff value of 1.205 cm; DTF reached an AUC of 0.896 (95% CI: 0.827–0.965; P<0.001), with a 68.6% sensitivity and a 100% specificity at a cutoff value of 22.75%. A composite model integrating LVEF, LUS, DE, and DTF achieved an AUC of 0.951 (95% CI: 0.907–0.996; P<0.001), with an 88.2% sensitivity and a 95.7% specificity.ConclusionsUltrasound parameters of the heart, lungs, and diaphragm provide critical information on cardiopulmonary and diaphragmatic function during SBT. Weaning failure is more common when LUS is increased and LVEF, DE, and DTF are decreased. The combination of these three aspects can improve the accuracy of predicting weaning outcomes.

Introduction

Mechanical ventilation is one of the most important life support technologies for critically ill patients (1). When the patient’s primary disease or the cause of mechanical ventilation improves, mechanical ventilation should be discontinued as soon as possible. Previous studies have shown that weaning failure correlates with prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) hospitalization and elevated mortality risk (2,3). In the process of changing from mechanical ventilation to spontaneous respiration, the changes in the functions of various organs have a substantial impact on the weaning outcome (4). Spontaneous breathing trials (SBTs) are performed before the patient is ready to be weaned from ventilation. The mechanism of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) can affect the weaning process in the following ways: (I) systemic inflammatory response can result in a decrease in myocardial contractility [interleukin (IL)-6-mediated troponin phosphorylation]; (II) intra-abdominal hypertension (IAH; >12 mmHg) can restrict the mobility of the diaphragm; and (III) uremic toxins may inhibit the drive of the respiratory center. However, it has been shown that the prediction accuracy of weaning outcomes based solely on SBT results is limited (5), and the incidence of reintubation after successful spontaneous breathing is between 3% and 30% (6,7). SBTs alone cannot effectively predict weaning failure due to cardiopulmonary decompensation and diaphragmatic dysfunction, making it challenging for intensivists to decide when to remove patients from mechanical ventilation.

Numerous indicators and parameters have been developed to assess patients’ ability to breathe unassisted. The rapid shallow breathing index (RSBI) is the most commonly used predictor in clinic (8) and is calculated by dividing respiratory rate by tidal volume. The RSBI reflects the equilibrium between the mechanical load imposed on inspiratory muscles and their capacity to adapt to this load during SBTs (9); however, its limited specificity and suboptimal positive predictive value remain critical shortcomings that may compromise the accuracy of weaning decisions (10,11). During the SBT, when patients switch from positive pressure ventilation to spontaneous breathing, various organs in the body may cause dysfunction of the heart, lungs, and diaphragm, resulting in weaning failure (12,13). Ultrasound has emerged as a validated modality for the real-time monitoring of cardiopulmonary function and morphological alterations across all phases of the weaning process (14-16). A previous study proved that echocardiography combined with pulmonary ultrasound can improve the accuracy of weaning outcome prediction (17). In addition, for patients with normal cardiopulmonary function but diaphragmatic dysfunction, assessing diaphragmatic status can help accurately predict weaning outcomes (18).

Bedside ultrasound is becoming increasingly common in ICUs, and ultrasound can visually assess patients’ heart, lung and, diaphragm function during spontaneous breathing (19), which can identify and treat patients with insufficient cardiopulmonary reserve and diaphragm dysfunction at an early stage. Therefore, the combination of transthoracic echocardiography, pulmonary ultrasound, and diaphragm ultrasound as routine clinical predictors may improve the accuracy of predicting weaning outcomes and extubation failure (20). The objective of this study was to determine which ultrasound parameters are associated with weaning outcomes and to assess the value of multimodal ultrasound in predicting weaning outcomes in mechanically ventilated patients. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-167/rc).

Methods

Participants

This single-center, prospective observational study was conducted in the ICU of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital from December 2022 to December 2023. This study adhered to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study protocol received formal approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (Approval No. 2024-693-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Participants were enrolled in the study only if they fulfilled the following predefined inclusion criteria: (I) age >18 years; (II) mechanical ventilation >48 hours; and (III) assisted ventilation through oral tube intubation and ventilator. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) tracheotomy or noninvasive ventilation; (II) external chest fixator or open wound; (III) neuromuscular or central nervous system diseases; (IV) paralysis of diaphragm; and (V) poor ultrasonic image display.

Weaning trials

Participants were enrolled only if they had undergone invasive mechanical ventilation for >48 hours and satisfied all the following predefined eligibility criteria for SBTs. (I) resolution or significant improvement in the underlying condition that necessitated mechanical ventilation; (II) adequate oxygenation, arterial oxygen saturation (SaO2) >90%, inhaled oxygen fraction (FiO2) ≤0.5, or PaO2/FiO2 ≥150 mmHg, and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) ≤8 cmH2O; (III) good lung function, respiratory rate <30 breath/min, tidal volume ≥5 mL/kg ideal body weight, and no obvious respiratory acidosis; (IV) stable hemodynamic state, a systolic blood pressure of 90–160 mmHg, no or a small amount of vasopressor drugs (dopamine or dobutamine <5 µg/kg/min or norepinephrine <0.05 µg/kg/min), and a heart rate of <120 beats/min; (V) alert and free from sedation; (VI) minimal or manageable mucus accumulation in the lower airways; and (VII) preserved airway clearance reflex (21). All the enrolled patients received standardized diuretic treatment within 24 hours before the SBT (intravenous injection of furosemide, with the dosage being adjusted according to the central venous pressure at the time of enrollment, ranging from 20 to 40 mg). Ultrasound examinations

The ultrasonic examination was carried out with an intelligent ultrasonic system (Nervus 9S, Shenzhen Wisonic Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China), equipped with a C6-2 convex array probe (frequency range, 2–6 MHz), an L15–6 linear array probe (frequency range, 6–15 MHz), and an SP5-1 phased array probe (frequency range, 1–5 MHz). SBT assessment was performed by critical care physicians with more than 5 years of clinical experience.

Transthoracic echocardiography

All patients were placed in a supine or left lateral decubitus position. The Carnation system (Shenzhen Wisonic Medical Technology Co., Ltd.) equipped with an SP5-1-H phased array probe (frequency range, 1–5 MHz) was used. In the long-axis view of the left ventricle at the level of the chordae tendineae along the parasternal line, M-mode ultrasound was employed to obtain the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). In the four-chamber view at the level of the mitral valve annulus along the apex, pulsed Doppler was used to record the early diastolic peak velocity (E peak) and the systolic peak velocity of the left atrium (A peak). Through the use of tissue Doppler imaging mode, the early diastolic motion velocity (e') of the mitral annulus septa and lateral walls was measured, and the arithmetic mean of these two measurements was used to calculate the E/e' ratio.

Lung ultrasound

A convex array probe (C-6-2-H) was used to scan the lungs, and the bedside lung ultrasound in emergency (BLUE) protocol was used to locate each point of the lung scan. In step 1, the operator compared the palm of the hand with the patient’s palm, and adjusted according to the size gap. In step 2, both palms were placed on the chest wall of the patient, the index fingers were adjacent, the little fingers of the upper hand were parallel to the collarbone of the patient, and the fingertips were aligned to the level of the sternum. As shown in Figure 2, lung ultrasound scanning is performed at the following four points, with their positioning as shown: (I) the upper BLUE point was located in the metacarpophalangeal joint between the upper indicating finger and the ring finger; (II) the lower BLUE point was located in the palm of the lower hand; (III) for the diaphragm point (diaphragmatic point), the lateral margin of the lower little finger was the diaphragm line, while the intersection point was its extension line and the midaxillary line; and (IV) the posterolateral alveolar and/or pleural syndrome (PLAPS) was located under the blue dot extension cord, with the axillary line following after the vertical intersection point. The scoring scheme (Figure 3) (21-23) was as follows: 0 points, normal ventilation, presence of horizontal A line (or no more than two B lines); 1 point, moderate loss of ventilation, presence of multiple B lines, regular intervals (interval ≥7 mm) or irregular intervals, or even fusion but only visible in a limited area of the intercostal space; 2 points, heavy ventilation loss, presence of multiple fused B lines (interval <5 mm); and 3 points, lung consolidation or atelectasis. For the 8 BLUE points in the blue scheme, the most severe performance was taken and scored as 0, 1, 2, or 3. The scores of each region were added together, with the total score ranging from 0 to 24 points. The lung ultrasound score (LUS) of the patients was the sum of the scores of eight lung areas.

Diaphragmatic ultrasound

For diaphragmatic ultrasound, the patient was placed in a supine position and scanned under the costal margin with a convex array probe (C6-2-H). With the standard incision to the second portal of the liver, the M-mode was used. The sampling line was perpendicular to the diaphragm, and the degree of diaphragm movement was recorded; that is, the vertical distance of the diaphragm toward the hepatic region during aspiration. Subsequently, a high-frequency linear array probe (L15-4-H) was used for an intercostal scan at the eighth to tenth ribs. The diaphragm thickness at the end of inspiratory and expiratory periods was recorded under the M mode with the sampling line perpendicular to the diaphragm, and diaphragmatic thickness fraction (DTF) was calculated as follows (Figure 4): DTF = (diaphragm thickness at the end of inspiration − diaphragm thickness at the end of expiration)/diaphragm thickness at the end of expiration × 100%. Diaphragm movement and diaphragm thickness were measured continuously for three cycles.

Diaphragmatic excursion and diaphragm thickening fraction.

Clinical data collection

Patients’ age, gender, length of stay in the ICU, duration of mechanical ventilation, primary diseases, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, mechanical ventilation, blood gas analysis before and after the SBT, and RSBI. During the SBTs, the electrocardiogram monitor observed changes in patients’ vital signs.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was determined based on feasibility within the study period, consistent with similar exploratory studies aimed at identifying potential predictors for further validation. All data were analyzed with SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages and were compared with the chi-squared test. Numerical data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation or as the median and interquartile range (IQR) as appropriate and were compared with independent samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney test. The association between potential variables and the risk of weaning failure was analyzed via multivariate logistic regression, in which all variables with P<0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into the stepwise model. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were established to evaluate the accuracy of potential indicators for predicting weaning outcomes. Ultrasonic variables were screened through least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression (the λ value was determined via 10-fold cross-validation), and the screened variables were incorporated into the logistic regression model to generate a composite prediction probability. Finally, this probability value was used for the ROC curve analysis. A combination model of multiple indices was established via logistic regression, and its value in predicting weaning outcomes was evaluated.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics of patients

During the study period, a total of 108 patients received >48 hours of invasive mechanical ventilation and were prepared for their first SBT. Ultimately, 74 patients were enrolled in the study, including 52 men, and the median age was 64.7±18.4 years. At the time of inclusion, the median length of stay in the ICU was 20 [8–32] days, and the median duration of mechanical ventilation was 7.5 [3–15]days. Of the 74 patients, 51 (69%) who were successfully extubated were included in the successful weaning group. Meanwhile, 23 (31%) patients who failed extubation in the subsequent 48 hours were enrolled in the failed weaning group. Among these 23 patients, 11 were intubated again, 8 tracheostomy, and 4 died. Accuracy of variables for predicting weaning outcome

The ROC curve was used to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of ultrasound parameters for predicting mechanical ventilation of weaning failure (Figure 6). The area under the ROC (AUC) values of LVEF, LUS, DE, and DTF were 0.742 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.642–0.842; P<0.001], 0.837 (95% CI: 0.738–0.936; P<0.001), 0.895 (95% CI: 0.821–0.969; P<0.001), and 0.896 (95% CI: 0.827–0.969, P<0.001), respectively. The optimal cutoff values for predicting weaning failure of LVEF, LUS, DE, and DTF were >58.05%, <17.5, <1.205 cm, and <22.75%, respectively (Table 5). The combined AUC of ultrasound parameters with statistically significant differences was 0.951 (95% CI: 0.907–0.996; P<0.001).

Discussion

In this prospective study, critically ill patients in the ICU underwent echocardiography, pulmonary ultrasound, and diaphragmatic ultrasound during SBTs. The key findings from our analysis are as follows: (I) Patients who were successfully weaned from mechanical ventilation had better left ventricular systolic function during the SBT. The lung scores of patients who failed weaning were higher than those of patients who succeeded in being weaned, and the success rate of patients with greater diaphragmatic mobility and diaphragmatic thickening was higher. (II) Among the ultrasonic parameters, LVEF <58.05%, LUS >17.5, DE <1.205 cm, and DTF <22.75% were independent predictors of weaning failure. (III) The diagnostic efficacy of the combination of multi-organ ultrasound parameters for weaning outcome was higher than that of each ultrasound parameter used alone, as were longer ICU stay and mechanical ventilation time.

Although these preliminary findings need to be validated in larger studies, the combined cardiopulmonary ultrasound parameters can help doctors more fully assess the condition of the patient’s heart and lungs and identify pathological changes. These changes may occasionally affect only one organ, while other times both the circulatory system and the respiratory system may be involved. In particular, when the echocardiogram shows heart failure, if the lung ultrasound also detects interstitial edema, this may indicate a more serious condition. This combined approach not only enhances the accuracy of diagnosis but may also provide important indications for assessing the severity of the condition. Future studies should further examine the clinical application value of this comprehensive ultrasonic evaluation method.

The transition from mechanical ventilation to spontaneous breathing may increase the preload and afterload of the heart, especially in patients with fluid overload or left ventricular dysfunction, which may induce cardiogenic pulmonary edema (22,23). Echocardiography is an important tool for assessing cardiac function during this transition (24,25), and the application value of its parameters in predicting weaning outcomes has been investigated in previous studies (12,26-28). Recent meta-analyses have highlighted the pivotal role of diastolic dysfunction markers—specifically E/e', e' wave velocity, and E wave velocity—in predicting weaning failure, while the contribution of impaired systolic function remains inconclusive (14). In contrast to prior evidence, we found no significant correlation between diastolic dysfunction markers (e.g., E/e' and e' wave) and weaning failure. Instead, there was reduced left ventricular systolic function, with LVEF below the 58.05% threshold emerging as a critical predictor of unsuccessful weaning. This may be related to therapeutic intervention, as a large number of patients were treated with diuretics prior to the SBT, which may ameliorate the increase in left ventricular congestion pressure during SBT by reducing cardiac preload (29,30). In addition, E/e', an indicator of left ventricular filling pressure, did not rise significantly during SBT, which may indicate that patients treated with diuretics are better able to adapt to the hemodynamic changes and more negative fluid balance induced by SBT. These findings suggest that optimal treatment and fluid management for patients may help improve weaning success.

The weaning process may lead to significant physiological changes in the patient, which may cause symptoms such as alveolar dehydration, pulmonary edema, and atelectasis, resulting in reduced parenchymal ventilation and ultimately weaning failure (4,13,20,30). Lung ultrasound has gained prominence as a pivotal bedside tool for assessing pulmonary ventilation in critically ill patients, owing to its diagnostic precision, user-friendly application, and noninvasive nature (31,32). LUS has shown several advantages in detecting lung problems during the weaning process. In a prospective cohort analysis of 40 high-risk geriatric patients undergoing weaning trials, Bouhemad et al. demonstrated that elevated LUS scores following SBT completion independently predicted extubation failure, establishing post-SBT LUS as a standalone prognostic marker for weaning outcomes (33).

In addition, lung ultrasound is of considerable value in predicting weaning failure. One study found that at the end of SBT, an overall LUS ≥17 or an anterolateral LUS ≥5 has good predictive value for weaning failure, while an LUS ≤13 has good predictive value for weaning success (34). Consistent with this, we found that an overall LUS of >17.5 was a good predictor of weaning failure, and taken together, these findings underscore the clinical utility of LUS as a bedside modality for both risk stratification of weaning failure and prognostication of extubation outcomes. The LUS score in this study was not corrected for patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) because the chronic changes in their lung structure (e.g., bullae and pleural adhesions) may affect the pathological significance of B-line count (chronic fibrosis vs. acute exudation) and the interpretation of pleural sliding sign (pseudoreduction of pleural sliding). It is recommended that future studies use the quantitative computed tomography emphysema index for hierarchical analysis of LUS.

The diaphragm, as the primary inspiratory muscle, is critical for respiratory mechanics. Evidence indicates that 44% of mechanically ventilated patients exhibit diaphragm thinning within the first 72 hours of initiation, with such atrophy strongly correlating to diminished contractile function (35). Diaphragmatic atrophy is significantly associated with unsuccessful weaning attempts and prolonged dependence on invasive mechanical ventilation (36). Evaluation of diaphragmatic performance serves as a prognostic indicator of respiratory muscle reserve in managing elevated ventilatory demands. In Kim et al.’s study (19), the prevalence of diaphragmatic dysfunction in critically ill patients without a history of neuromuscular or diaphragmatic myopathy was 29%. In our study, the cutoff value of DE and DTF for extubation failure was 1.205 cm and 22.75%, respectively. In addition, patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction had longer periods of ventilation. In other research, DE was shown to be a predictor of successful extubation (37). We further found that DE and DTF were independent risk factors for predicting success in weaning. However, due to the small sample size, the role of diaphragm in weaning still needs to be further explored.

For the prediction of weaning outcomes, the ROC curves in this study indicate that LUS and diaphragm ultrasound function indicators had greater sensitivity, while diaphragm function had greater specificity. However, none of these indicators could predict weaning outcome independently (AUC <0.9). To leverage the complementary strengths of individual predictors, we integrated cardiopulmonary and diaphragmatic indices into a multiparameter logistic regression model. The combined model demonstrated superior discriminative capacity, achieving an AUC of 0.951 (95% CI: 0.907–0.996; P<0.001), with sensitivity and specificity rates of 88.2% and 95.7%, respectively. This multiparametric approach outperformed individual parameters in predicting weaning outcomes, underscoring the synergistic value of holistic physiological evaluation. In clinical work, joint predictive models can be applied to calculate the probability of successful weaning and subsequent management.

This study has certain limitations that should be noted. First, diaphragmatic assessments were exclusively performed on the right hemidiaphragm due to frequent acoustic interference from gastrointestinal gas which obscured the left hemidiaphragm. Although this approach aligns with current clinical guidelines—which advocate for unilateral measurements unless phrenic nerve injury is suspected—it may overlook potential bilateral dysfunction in select populations (38,39). Second, we employed a single-center design with a small sample size. Therefore, a large-scale, multicenter, randomized controlled trial is warranted to definitively establish the predictive validity of integrating cardiopulmonary and diaphragmatic assessments. Third, the SBTs implemented in this study involved low-pressure support or ventilator disconnection, which imposes less hemodynamic stress as compared to T-tube–based SBTs. Consequently, these findings may not be generalizable to patients undergoing T-tube weaning protocols. Fourth, ultrasound imaging may be technically challenging in patients with suboptimal acoustic windows, particularly in the presence of obesity, subcutaneous emphysema, or chest dressings. In this study, 19 (17.6%) cases were excluded due to image quality. Although the robustness of the results has been verified through multiple imputations (with the change in odds ratio being less than 10%), the impact of this threshold on actual clinical applications still needs to be interpreted with caution. Finally, although the sample size (n=74) was sufficient for an exploratory analysis, it may be limited in statistical power for the detection of subtle associations and reduce the generalizability of the results. Larger multicenter cohorts are needed to validate the predictive model and cutoff values derived from this preliminary investigation.

It should also be mentioned that this study used the fractional shortening method to evaluate ejection fraction (EF), but this method has the following limitations: (I) It relies on an assumption of the geometric shape of the left ventricle, which may overestimate the true EF value in patients with asymmetric contraction. (II) It has not been cross-validated with other gold standard methods such as cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. (III) This method cannot capture early abnormalities in systolic function (such as a situation in which global longitudinal strain is abnormal while EF is preserved). (IV) The selection of 58% as the EF cutoff value was based on the results of univariate ROC analysis (sensitivity =76.5% and specificity =69.6%). Although this value was lower than the traditionally defined lower limit of normal (55%), its clinical significance needs to be interpreted with caution. It is recommended that future studies use three-dimensional echocardiography for verification. Moreover, a relatively small sample size, while adequate for hypothesis generation, necessitates caution in the interpretation of cutoff values (e.g., LVEF <58.05% and LUS >17.5). Future studies with larger populations are critical to refine these thresholds.

Conclusions

The findings of this exploratory study suggest that multimodal ultrasound parameters may aid in predicting weaning outcomes. However, the results require validation in larger, prospective cohorts to confirm their clinical applicability. Overall, this indicates that ultrasound features of the heart, lungs, and diaphragm provide critical information on cardiopulmonary function and diaphragm function during SBTs. During the spontaneous breathing test, LVEF <58.05%, LUS >17.5, DE <1.205 cm, and DTF <22.75% were independent predictors of weaning failure. We hope that the combined predictive model will aid physicians in optimizing heart and respiratory function before initiating weaning and disconnecting patients from the ventilator. Further large-scale, multicenter studies are warranted to validate the clinical efficacy of ultrasound-guided mechanical ventilation weaning protocols across diverse patient populations.
