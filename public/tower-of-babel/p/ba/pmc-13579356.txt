Molecules of lung cancer – fusion alterations in nonsmall cell lung cancer: biology and diagnostic considerations
Authors: Marie Talty, David O'Reilly, Jasmine Dunne, Jarushka Naidoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fusion alterations have transformed outcomes for a subset of patients with nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC), as part of the evolution of precision oncology. While the role of molecular pathology in the diagnosis and treatment of NSCLC has rapidly evolved, it remains the responsibility of the pulmonologist to coordinate the diagnostic evaluation of patients with lung cancer, including clinical assessment, obtaining a histological diagnosis and coordination of staging procedures. This review focuses on the biology, clinical significance and diagnostic considerations of fusion alterations in NSCLC. We summarise current knowledge regarding the role of actionable gene fusion translocations, including anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), ros-protooncogene 1 (ROS1), retinoblastoma rearranged during transfection (RET) and neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK) fusions, while outlining clinical decision-making considerations and testing frameworks. For respiratory physicians, it is essential to prioritise suitable diagnostic modalities by taking into account tissue availability, turnaround time, and consideration of next generation sampling methods and noninvasive sampling such as blood-based circulating tumour DNA assays.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer deaths worldwide, with nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for the majority of cases [1]. The therapeutic landscape of NSCLC treatment has undergone a seismic shift in oncology in the past 20 years, with a move away from its foundational histological definitions to characterisation by its underlying molecular driver [2, 3]. Historically, the treatment of advanced NSCLC, which represents the majority of cases at diagnosis, has involved cytotoxic chemotherapy, later supplemented by immune checkpoint inhibitors. The discovery of activating epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations in 2004 and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) rearrangements in 2007 fundamentally redefined this approach, introducing the concept of targeted therapy and establishing the foundation for precision oncology [4, 5]. Subsequently, the number of clinically relevant fusion alterations and corresponding driver alteration-based treatments have expanded to include: ALK, c-ros-protooncogene 1 (ROS1), retinoblastoma, rearranged during transfection (RET), neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK) and neuregulin 1 (NRG1) amongst others [6–8]. This targeted therapy has greatly improved disease response and survival among patients with a druggable fusion alteration. Baseline molecular profiling is now considered a standard of care for all patients with non-squamous NSCLC and for selected patients with squamous cell carcinoma (patients of younger age, or those with a never/light smoking history) [9, 10].

The dramatic change in the level of knowledge required regarding each specific lung cancer patient's molecular biology demands more of the respiratory physician, who is now acting as a gatekeeper to highly effective targeted treatments where applicable. This is based on obtaining high quality tissue and initiating reflex molecular testing, as well as having a high index of suspicion of fusion alterations where appropriate and communicating results across a multidisciplinary setting in a timely fashion so patients can start treatment [11]. This narrative review will discuss the molecular biology of fusion alterations, current diagnostic testing options, clinical and therapeutic implications, and development of algorithms for testing prioritisation, with an emphasis on practically integrating such algorithms into respiratory physician-led care pathways.

Biology of fusion alterations

Gene fusion alterations were first demonstrated in haematological malignancies such as chronic myeloid leukaemia, where recognition of the Philadelphia chromosome became one of the earliest examples of a druggable oncogenic fusion alteration [12]. In NSCLC, the broad principles remain the same, chromosomal rearrangements lead to activation of a kinase domain under the control of a strong promoter or the creation of a fusion protein [6]. The resultant aberrant signalling promotes transformation of normal bronchial cells to malignant clones.

This abnormal activity leads to downstream oncogenic cascades, such as rat sarcoma virus (RAS), rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma (RAF), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), protein kinase B (AKT), mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK–STAT), activating uncontrolled proliferation, survival and metastasis [13–15]. These rearrangements act as driver events, and are vital for tumour growth and maintenance. This phenomenon means ongoing tumour survival depends on the fusion-driven pathway and as such pharmacological inhibition frequently produces profound and durable responses, often termed oncogene addiction [16].

Clinical characteristics

Overall, fusion altered tumours represent ∼10% of lung adenocarcinomas globally, and a higher proportion in never-smokers and young patients (see table 1) [17]. There are significant regional variations, however, with an incidence of up to 20% in Asia, 10% in a European population, and ∼5% in an Irish population [18, 19]. However, they are all associated with highly effective first-line targeted therapies for oncogene-addicted tumours, unlike most mutations (excluding EGFR) [7]. Therefore, an accurate molecular based diagnosis of NSCLC is essential to ensure patients are treated with an appropriately molecular-targeted therapy.

Consequently, the role of the respiratory physician is not just to be aware of such fusion alterations, but to facilitate their discovery, through having an appropriate higher index of suspicion in younger and never-smoking cohorts, and by integrating appropriate testing modalities and plasma and tissue assays into diagnostic algorithms.

Considering the advances in the diagnostic and treatment landscape of a lung cancer diagnosis, the introduction and implementation of lung cancer screening across Europe potentially enables a greater proportion of lung cancers to be diagnosed at an early and curable stage [20–22]. Historically, molecular testing was not required for this subset of patients, but as treatment of earlier stage cancers becomes more individualised, with targeted therapies in both the neoadjuvant and adjuvant setting, molecular testing is becoming essential in early stage disease [23].

ALK rearrangements

The ALK gene encodes a receptor tyrosine kinase whose physiological role is largely confined to neural development. The discovery of EML4(13)::ALK(20) in 2007 established ALK rearrangement as one of the first fusion drivers in NSCLC, with many other fusion partners identified since then [5, 24]. The resulting fusion protein dimerises spontaneously, activating downstream signalling independent of ligand binding. Clinically, ALK-rearranged tumours typically occur in younger, nonsmoking patients with adenocarcinoma histology and a high association with brain metastasis. ALK inhibitors include crizotinib (first-generation), certinib, alectinib and brigatinib (second generation) and lorlatinib (third generation) [7, 25]. The CROWN trial has recently presented unprecedented survival data with 5-year progression-free survival in excess of 50% in treatment naïve patients receiving lorlatinib – a landmark in the management of advanced NSCLC [26]. Furthermore, the novel agent NVL-655, currently under investigation in the ALKOVE phase 1/2 study, and anticipated to be approved for ALK rearranged NSCLC, has demonstrated promising recent data [27]. Compared with earlier generation ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), NVL-655 was engineered to be a highly selective and brain penetrant inhibitor, targeting the full spectrum of ALK fusions as well as resistance mutations. This more precise fusion targeting, aims to maximise efficacy including intracranially, while minimising off-target toxicities such as tyrosine receptor kinase (TRK)-associated neurological adverse effects. Preliminary clinical results from the early-phase trial (ALKOVE-1) in heavily pretreated ALK-positive NSCLC (including patients previously exposed to multiple ALK TKIs, with central nervous system metastases or resistance mutations) have shown encouraging anti-tumour activity, with an overall response rate of 42% in pre-treated group and 71% in loratinib-naïve patients [28].

ROS1 rearrangements

ROS1 fusions occur in 1–2% of patients with advanced NSCLC and encode for a receptor tyrosine kinase of the insulin receptor family. Its rearrangements have very significant clinical and biologic parallels with ALK, including mutual exclusivity with other drivers and sensitivity to the same class of TKIs [29, 30]. Crizotinib, entrectinib and repotrectinib are all highly effective, achieving response rates exceeding 70%. Whilst these rearrangements may occur in young people, they are more frequently identified in those aged >60 years and with a smoking history than ALK rearrangements.

RET rearrangements

RET fusions, most commonly KIF5B–RET or CCDC6–RET, activate a transmembrane receptor involved in cellular growth and differentiation. The prevalence is ∼1–2%, with enrichment in never-smokers. After years of limited success with multi-kinase inhibitors, selective RET inhibitors (selpercatinib and pralsetinib) have yielded response rates exceeding 60% and durable intracranial control [31, 32]. In the LIBRETTO trial, selpercatinib demonstrated an improved progression-free survival compared to platinum-doublet and chemotherapy (24.8 months versus 11.8 months). Similarly in the ARROW trial, pralsetinib produced a robust response, with an objective response rate of 70% in treatment naïve patients, and a median progression-free survival of 16.5 months in patients with prior platinum-based chemotherapy. These are typically but not always diagnosed in never/light smokers.

NTRK fusions

NTRK1–3 fusions are exceedingly rare (<1%), but clinically significant due to tumour-agnostic approval of TRK inhibitors [33]. In the European Union (EU), both larotrectinib and entrectinib are approved for use for patients with NTRK fusions with response rates in excess of 70% for both agents [34, 35]. In cases where intolerance to selective NTRK inhibitors precludes their use, off-label use of crizotinib, although less potent, demonstrates modest activity and can be considered as an alternative [36].

NRG1 fusions

NRG1 fusions are the newest fusion with an approved targeted therapy available (approved in the USA but not the EU) [37]. This is an uncommon fusion in NSCLC, but is also found in pancreatic and other solid organ tumours. Activity of zenocutuzumab was demonstrated in the single-arm, phase II basket study which demonstrated a response rate of 30%. Whilst this response rate is significantly lower than what we see when targeting other fusion alterations, it is likely that it will be improved upon with second and third-generation targeting agents.

Summary

In summary, targeted therapy yields higher objective response rates ranging from 60% to 80%, with prolonged progression-free survival and improved quality of life. For example, second-generation ALK inhibitors such as alectinib and brigatinib demonstrate superior central nervous system penetration and have displaced crizotinib as the first-line standard. Similarly, selpercatinib and pralsetinib for RET fusions produce durable systemic and intracranial control. However, nearly all patients eventually develop acquired resistance. Resistance mechanisms are broadly categorised as on-target (secondary kinase domain mutations that impair drug binding) or off-target (activation of bypass signalling pathways). In ALK-altered NSCLC, common on-target mutations include L1196M and G1202R, while bypass pathways often involve EGFR, MET or Kirsten rat sarcoma virus (KRAS) activation. For ROS1, secondary mutations such as G2032R commonly confer resistance to first-generation inhibitors but may remain sensitive to lorlatinib or repotrectinib. Understanding the mechanisms underpinning this loss of treatment effect highlights the potential role for repeat molecular analysis if patients develop progression. Liquid biopsy, with circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA), may offer serial sampling and tracking of emerging clones as an alternative to repeat tissue biopsy upon development of treatment resistance [38].

Diagnostic approaches

The key role of the respiratory physician in this process relates to clinical suspicion and selection of appropriate biopsy method (figure 1). Related decisions with regard to biopsy site, method of sampling and specimen preservation directly affect the availability and suitability of tissue for molecular testing as highlighted by the American Association of Bronchology and Interventional Pulmonology and the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer in their clinical practice guideline [39]. Endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration, computed tomography-guided core biopsy and transbronchial cryobiopsies are typically best to offer sufficient tissue for molecular analysis, while other cytological samples, such as bronchoalveolar lavage, endobronchial brushings or pleural fluid, or smaller histological samples such as endobronchial biopsies are often insufficient for the plethora of testing required [40]. Recent evidence from the VERITAS trial comparing navigational bronchoscopic sampling and transthoracic needle biopsy should also be considered, as navigational bronchoscopy becomes increasingly used in the investigation of lung nodules [41]. Though this demonstrated non-inferiority with regard to diagnostic accuracy, it did not assess sufficiency of material for molecular testing, and this is an area for further study as pulmonologists sample smaller and smaller nodules bronchoscopically. Close collaboration with pathology and so-called “molecular stewardship” optimises resource and tissue usage, while reducing waste and optimising sample handling. In lower to middle income countries, where routine next generation sequencing (NGS) is not feasible, assessment based on epidemiological likelihood (i.e. EGFR and ALK testing first) remains a reasonable approach. Implementing standardised fusion alteration testing in lower income countries can be challenging due to lack of infrastructure and budgetary constraints, but should be prioritised where access to targeted treatments is available. This can be achieved through prioritising the highest impact fusions (e.g. ALK and ROS1) and using lower cost surrogate techniques, such as immunohistochemistry (IHC) and fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH), if NGS is not available [42]. It is vital that greater collaboration with and amongst colleagues in Africa and the Middle East improves understanding of the genomic landscape of NSCLC in these areas and increases access to testing and targeted treatments.

Assay modalities

Next-generation sequencing

NGS is now considered the optimal modality for comprehensive genomic profiling for patients with newly diagnosed advanced NSCLC [3, 43]. It allows parallel assessment of multiple genes with minimal tissue. DNA-based panels capture point mutations and some rearrangements, while RNA-based methods directly identify expressed fusions. Hybrid capture and anchored multiplex PCR are the dominant RNA technologies. RNA methods are essential for fusion detection as the variety of fusion breakpoints mean that DNA methods may only detect “known fusions” and rarer fusion events are likely to be missed. RNA circumvents this challenge by evaluating the transcriptome – thus providing a highly sensitive and specific method for identifying all fusion alterations. With an increasing repertoire of fusion alterations known to be associated with therapies, an RNA assay provides a “one-stop” approach which can assess all fusion alterations as well as the splicing variant MET exon 14 skipping [44].

Immunohistochemistry

IHC remains a rapid and cost-effective frontline assay [3, 15]. The ALK D5F3 clone demonstrates >95% concordance with FISH and NGS. ROS1 and pan-TRK IHC are useful as a screening test but require confirmatory testing due to their variable specificity. RET fusions cannot be reliably detected with IHC. Its other key disadvantage (similar to FISH and reverse-transcriptase (RT)-PCR) is that it consumes significant tissue and does not provide a comprehensive assessment of fusion alterations.

Fluorescence in situ hybridisation

FISH uses fluorescent probes to detect chromosomal break-apart signals and remains a reference method for ALK and ROS1 [15]. Due to its single-target nature and significant labour intensity it is now largely used as confirmatory testing; however, it remains the gold standard test for ALK and ROS1 alterations. Similar to RNA-based assays, it is agnostic to the fusion partner and thus is a highly sensitive and specific technique.

Reverse-transcriptase PCR

RT-PCR is highly specific for known fusions but limited by the need for predefined breakpoints, making it less useful for broad discovery [3, 15]. It has been most commonly used for ALK or ROS1 fusion detection.

Prioritisation of testing

When NGS is not immediately available, rational prioritisation helps conserve tissue and costs. It is also an option in severely unwell patients with appropriate patient factors, such as young age and never-smoking status, where there is a clinical need and clinician preference to commence treatment without waiting for results of NGS. Cytology and small biopsy specimens

As outlined previously, multiple bronchoscopic and needle-based sampling methods provide cytological samples rather than histological samples, which can be challenging for further molecular testing[40]. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded cell blocks and even direct smears can support NGS if handled correctly[45]. Respiratory physicians should coordinate with pathology teams to preserve unstained slides and minimise pre-analytical artefacts such as air-drying or over-fixation. There is an expanding role for NGS in cytological samples, with use of Papanicolaou-stained slides recommended [46]; although FISH and immunocytochemistry can still be used their results are not always concordant [47].

Liquid biopsy

Liquid biopsy has an established role in the molecular diagnosis for patients with advanced NSCLC, with recommendations from both the National Cancer Comprehensive Network and International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer [48, 49]. Sensitivity for mutations such as KRAS and EGFR typically ranges between 75% and 80% in the pre-treatment advanced setting [49, 50]. Particular challenges emerge in the setting of detection of fusion alterations with liquid biopsy, for similar reasons that there are challenges in the detection of fusion alterations with DNA-based NGS. Almost all liquid biopsy ctDNA assays rely on using hybrid capture DNA-based NGS assays to detect fusion alterations but these are limited to targeting only known breakpoints. However, sensitivity remains lower than with mutation detection. For example in the CROWN study, the Guardant360 assay had a sensitivity of 50% for detection of fusions in pre-treatment patients with ALK-altered NSCLC [51]. Thus, whilst liquid biopsy may detect fusions and a positive finding in a ctDNA test is unlikely to represent a false positive – a negative liquid biopsy does not exclude fusion alterations. The sensitivity of these tests in the early stage setting (e.g. neoadjuvant setting where ALK testing is essential) is lower again. Future developments in this field include the assessment of cell-free RNA with either NGS or direct hybridisation technologies (e.g. NCounter) [52, 53]. This will overcome some of the challenges associated with the use of ctDNA for liquid biopsy detection but other challenges with RNA analysis include low cell-free RNA in plasma and the requirement for rapid processing to ensure stability. As plasma techniques improve in sensitivity of detection of circulating tumour DNA and RNA, there are likely to be improved technologies available for noninvasive fusion detection [54]. Developments in ctDNA technology may have applications outside of fusion detection including as a screening test in high-risk individuals, assessment as part of lung cancer screening pathways and potential use of serial assessments in surveillance after early-stage resection. In the future, plasma testing could be the primary method, with tissue testing reserved for confirmation or discordant results. This expanded use of liquid biopsy will require greater awareness and education among respiratory clinicians on interpretation of results throughout lung cancer diagnosis, treatment and follow-up [6, 49].

Turnaround time and reflex testing

Guidelines recommend that molecular results be available to clinicians within 10 working days from laboratory receipt of an adequate sample [3]. Reflex initiation of testing by pathology upon NSCLC diagnosis, without waiting for an oncologist's request, shortens turnaround time and increases detection of actionable alterations. Incorporating reflex orders into bronchoscopy or biopsy workflows ensures that the respiratory physician's initial intervention enables seamless progression to targeted therapy.

Special considerations and populations

Early-stage disease and screening-detected lesions

The expansion of low-dose computed tomography screening has resulted in increased detection of small peripheral nodules and early-stage adenocarcinomas. While targeted therapy remains primarily indicated for advanced disease, adjuvant alectinib has demonstrated a significant improvement in disease-free survival in the recently published ALINA trial [55]. As such, molecular characterisation of resectable NSCLC is increasingly relevant in stage I–II disease. Respiratory specialists involved in lung-cancer screening programmes should therefore ensure that early-stage resection specimens undergo comprehensive profiling to identify candidates for potential standard of care targeted therapy or immunotherapy in the neoadjuvant or adjuvant settings, for suitability for clinical trials, and to understand this area of evolving clinical practice. Tissue may be particularly scant in the early-stage setting, where percutaneous biopsy is the primary modality for sampling. The sensitivity of liquid biopsy in early-stage disease ranges from 20% to 50% and thus there is an unmet need for the development of novel biomarker strategies for patients with early-stage disease for fusion detection [49]. This may involve ultra-sensitive liquid biopsy assays, improved tumour sampling techniques (e.g. robotic bronchoscopy) or the use of novel liquid biopsy techniques (e.g. exhaled breath condensate) [56–58].

Comorbidities and drug safety

Respiratory clinicians are certainly familiar with the potential pulmonary toxicities of modern lung cancer treatment, both with checkpoint inhibitors and TKIs [59]. There should be a low threshold for consideration of drug-induced pneumonitis, particularly with the use of EGFR, ALK or RET inhibitors, with withholding TKIs where suspected, out-ruling other potential causes and commencing corticosteroids where appropriate.

In spite of the demonstrated evidence of benefits of identification of fusion alterations, real-world data outlines significant gaps in testing rates. There are multiple barriers including access to NGS, cost/funding across different healthcare systems, insufficient tissue for molecular analysis and lack of clinical awareness regarding the role of testing. There is a paucity of data on availability of molecular testing in low-resource settings. In a retrospective single-centre review of patients from Kenya, fusion alterations were identified with a high frequency of 19% [60]. Much work is needed to ensure equity of access to both molecular diagnostics and therapeutics so that we can effectively address the global burden of lung cancer morbidity and mortality.

Future directions in fusion alterations

Expansion of actionable mutations

Developments in sequencing techniques continue to shed light on newly identified and rare fusion alterations in NSCLC, such as NRG1 and BRAF fusions, which all have potential therapeutic inhibitors. Developments in recent treatments such as tumour-agnostic approval of NTRK- and NRG1-targeted TKIs is in line with the overall shift in modern oncological care towards tumour-agnostic molecularly defined treatments. As the technology develops it is likely that comprehensive NGS, including both DNA- and RNA-based panels, will become standard at diagnosis of lung cancer. We may be moving beyond targeted panels to whole-genome or whole-exome sequencing of tumour specimens, which may allow the prospective and retrospective evaluation of molecular alterations yet to be discovered [61]. In the liquid biopsy setting, we see the development of both tumour-naïve and tumour-informed minimal residual disease assays, which may be used to guide both neoadjuvant, adjuvant and palliative therapy in fusion-altered NSCLC [62].

Development of multi-omic integrated pathways

With further iterations of next generation molecular diagnostics, the approach is likely to expand from current sequencing to integration of multi-omic data (DNA, RNA, methylation, proteomics) [63]. There is a clear and growing role for assessing genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, epigenomics and metabolomics to further characterise tumour heterogeneity, biology and the tumour microenvironment [64]. This promotes improved molecular subtyping, identification of novel biomarkers and stratification of patients for personalised therapy, including immunotherapy and targeted agents.

Education and multidisciplinary collaboration

Lung cancer diagnosis is truly multidisciplinary, with ongoing engagement in professional development and education around molecular diagnostics and techniques required for all specialists involved [65]. Early identification of insufficient tissue for molecular testing and the use of noninvasive approaches is best led by respiratory physicians – prior to the patient meeting a medical oncologist. Ultimately, the aim of respiratory physicians leading the molecular diagnostic pathway for patients with lung cancer is to translate the emerging developments in molecular therapeutics into outcomes that matter for our patients.

Conclusion

Fusion alterations in lung cancer exemplify the benefits of relatively recent translational research discoveries on patient outcomes and survival. However, this increases complexity in lung cancer subtypes and treatments, and requires consideration throughout the lung cancer diagnostic pathway, in particular with regard to tissue sampling collection and stewardship to ensure limited tissue is best utilised for the appropriate patient. For respiratory physicians this is no longer an optional or research interest area of lung cancer care, it is absolutely vital to delivering the optimal and precision-based treatment approaches for our patients. The pulmonologist remains the gatekeeper in lung cancer diagnosis and the clinician holding the bronchoscope, endobronchial ultrasound probe or core biopsy needle holds the key in ensuring sufficient tissue in quantity and quality, and using and establishing efficient and reflex based testing algorithms.

Combining tissue-based and liquid biopsy approaches, emphasising comprehensive NGS at initial diagnosis, and fostering effective communication among multidisciplinary teams can together reduce diagnostic delays and enhance the detection of actionable molecular alterations.

As the scope of molecular profiling widens and technological capabilities advance, the role of the pulmonologist – navigating the balance between clinical practicality and the expanding field of genomic medicine – will continue to be crucial. Progress in this area will rely not only on the discovery of new targeted treatments but also on collaborative infrastructure, professional education and fair access to molecular testing. Through these combined efforts, fusion-driven therapy in NSCLC will remain a leading example of how translational research can transform patient care.
