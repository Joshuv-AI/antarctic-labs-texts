Angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor treatment in people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and heart failure
Authors: Wan-Hsuan Hsu, Bo-Wen Shiau, Jheng-Yan Wu, Min-Hsiang Chuang, Ya-Wen Tsai, Po-Yu Huang, Khai-Chi Hu, Wei Hsu, Ting-Hui Liu, Kuang-Ming Liao, Chih-Cheng Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RASi) have shown benefits for people with both heart failure (HF) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), limited data exists on the use of angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs) in this population. This study compares the effectiveness of RASi and ARNIs in people with coexisting COPD and HF.MethodsWe identified individuals who started treatment with either ARNI or RASi since August 1, 2015. We assessed outcomes such as COPD exacerbations, acute respiratory failure, and lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) over 30 days to 3 years. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox regression models were applied to estimate survival probabilities and hazard ratios (HR).ResultsAmong 9,071 ARNI users and 71,836 RASi users, the ARNI group has fewer respiratory complications. The ARNI group has a higher proportion of females compared to the RASi group (38.2% vs. 31.5%). Specifically, ARNI users have a lower incidence of COPD exacerbations (13.1% vs. 18.7%; HR, 0.84), acute respiratory failure (16.2% vs. 22.0%; HR, 0.90), and LRTIs (16.9% vs. 22.9%; HR, 0.91).ConclusionsIn people with both COPD and HF, ARNI treatment is associated with fewer respiratory complications compared to RASi.Subject terms: Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Heart failure

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and heart failure (HF) are global health challenges, which were associated with increasing morbidity and mortality. Both COPD and HF not only share common etiological factors including age, smoking, inflammation and oxidative stress but also demonstrate intricate pathophysiological connections1–4. COPD, characterized by persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow limitation, is increasingly recognized as a systemic disorder rather than a purely pulmonary disease. People with COPD frequently present with a spectrum of comorbidities, with cardiovascular complications being particularly prevalent. Among these, coronary artery disease, heart failure, and various cardiac arrhythmias are notably common1,3. Conversely, the prevalence of COPD in the HF population is substantial, with estimates suggesting that approximately one-third of HF people also have COPD, and this association is especially pronounced in people diagnosed with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF)5–7. The high prevalence of this comorbidity underscores the need for clinicians to maintain a high index of suspicion for both conditions when evaluating people with either diagnosis.

The interplay between COPD and HF is multifaceted and bidirectional, with each condition potentially exacerbating the other. In individuals with HF, concomitant COPD is associated with poorer outcomes, including increased rates of hospitalization for HF and mortality, especially for those with reduced ejection fraction8–10. Similarly, HF complicates the clinical course of COPD, with affected individuals experiencing more frequent exacerbations, increased emergency department utilization, and higher rates of hospital admission10,11. Given the substantial overlap and mutual exacerbation of these conditions, developing effective strategies for the concurrent management of COPD and HF has emerged as a critical priority in contemporary medicine.

Compared to renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RASi), angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI) have been associated with improved clinical outcomes in people with HFs and are now recommended as a pivotal treatment option for this population12–16. However, the effect of ARNI on the respiratory outcomes of people with both COPD and HF is limited17. In this study, we hypothesis ARNI as an promising medication for HF, could provide additional respiratory benefit for people with coexisting HF and COPD. Therefore, this large retrospective cohort study is conducted to compare the effectiveness of ARNI versus RASi in the treatment of people with HF and COPD. Collectively, these results highlight that the coexistence of COPD and HF complicates patient management, as each condition exacerbates the other. While ARNIs have been shown to improve clinical outcomes in HF individuals compared to traditional RASi, their effect on respiratory outcomes in people with both COPD and HF remains unclear.

Methods

Data source

The cohort study utilized data from the TriNetX database, a platform that aggregates deidentified patient-level information from electronic health records (EHRs). Access to the TriNetX platform was obtained through our institution’s existing subscription and data use agreement with TriNetX. As researchers affiliated with Chi Mei Medical Center, we were granted access after completing the necessary internal approvals and compliance requirements, including ethics and data privacy training, as mandated by our institution. This data is provided by over 130 healthcare organizations (HCOs), primarily academic centers, which consolidate records from their affiliated hospitals and outpatient clinics. The database includes detailed information on demographics, diagnoses (based on International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification [ICD-10-CM] codes), procedures (classified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision Procedure Coding System [ICD-PCS] or Current Procedural Terminology [CPT] codes), medications (documented by Veterans Affairs Drug Classification System and RxNorm codes), laboratory tests (standardized with Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes codes), and health care utilization data18,19. This study accessed the Global Collaborative Network within TriNetX, encompassing over 150 million patient records from 134 HCOs across 19 countries in North and South America, Asia-Pacific, Europe, the Middle East, and Africa. Ethical approval for using the TriNetX database in this study was granted by Chi Mei Hospital’s Institutional Review Board (approval number: 11305-E01). The need for informed consent was waived because this study was conducted using only aggregated statistical summaries of deidentified information.

Study design and participant selection

Individuals aged 18 years or older with concomitant HF and COPD who received ARNI or RASi since August 1, 2015, were included in the study. The index date was defined as the date of the first prescription of ARNI or RASi after the individual met all inclusion criteria, including confirmed diagnoses of both HF and COPD. This start date of enrollment aligned with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approval of ARNI in July 201520. To ensure a new-user design, we included only patients who initiated ARNI or RASi therapy after receiving confirmed diagnoses of both HF and COPD, thereby reinforcing treatment-naïve status for both groups. Medication compliance was addressed by selecting patients with two consecutive records of ARNI or RASi use within a year, while those who received both ARNI and RASi were excluded from the study. Additionally, as liver dysfunction was a contraindication for ARNI, patients with liver dysfunction were also excluded from the study. Coding or patient selection is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and existing literature, and all were assessed during the three-year period prior to the index date to minimize the risk of post-exposure bias. These included demographics (age, sex, race, and ethnicity), lifestyle factors (e.g., nicotine dependence, socioeconomic status), comorbid conditions (e.g., COPD exacerbation, cardiovascular diseases, metabolic disorders, chronic kidney diseases, obstructive sleep apnea, lung cancer, liver cirrhosis), and medications (e.g., diuretics, beta-blockers, thiazides, angiotensin II inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, and vericiguat). Laboratory data included left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF, %) and HbA1c (%). Coding details are in Supplementary Table 2.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the risk of COPD exacerbation during the 30-day to 3-year follow-up period. Secondary outcomes included the risk of acute respiratory failure and lower respiratory tract infection within the same follow-up period. Stratified analyses were conducted based on age (<65 years vs. ≥65 years), sex (female vs. male), history of COPD exacerbation in the past year (absence vs. presence), type of COPD inhalant used (long-acting beta-agonists [LABA] or long-acting muscarinic antagonists [LAMA], LABA + LAMA, LABA + inhaled corticosteroid [ICS], or LABA + LAMA + I CS), history of heart failure with acute exacerbation (HFAE) in the past year (absence vs. presence), and beta-blocker use (absence vs. presence). Codings used for outcomes and subgroup identification are provided in supplementary table 3 and 4, respectively. Index date was the first date of receiving ARNI for the ARNI group or RASi for the RASi group. The outcome time window ranged from 3 months to 3 years after the index date.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of the ARNI and RASi groups were summarized as means with standard deviations for continuous variables and as counts with percentages for categorical variables. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test, and continuous variables were analyzed with the independent two-sample t-test. To balance baseline characteristics between the two groups, one-to-one PSM was performed using the greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper of 0.1 pooled standard deviations. Adequate matching was confirmed with standardized differences below 0.1, using TriNetX’s built-in analytic tools.

Following PSM, the Kaplan-Meier method calculated survival probabilities, and Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to determine hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for each outcome. Survival probabilities for COPD exacerbation, acute respiratory failure, and lower respiratory tract infection were assessed, with survival curve differences evaluated using the log-rank test through TriNetX’s analytic tools. Additionally, reverse Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed in Stata, version 18.0, using data from the primary Kaplan-Meier analysis to assess cumulative probability. Further stratified analyses were conducted in Stata, version 18.0, with preliminary data obtained from TriNetX’s statistical module. All subgroup analyses were conducted as 14 separate studies, each with PSM performed independently. Interaction p-values for each subgroup were calculated using the Wald test.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Statistical analyses were conducted using both the TriNetX platform and Stata software (version 18.0). Baseline characteristics were compared using chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent two-sample t-tests for continuous variables. One-to-one propensity score matching was applied using the greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper of 0.1 pooled standard deviations to balance covariates between groups. Adequacy of matching was confirmed with standardized differences below 0.1. Primary and secondary outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Survival differences were assessed using the log-rank test.

Reproducibility was ensured through the use of a large, multi-institutional cohort drawn from over 150 million patient records within the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network. Sample sizes were determined by the number of eligible patients meeting the inclusion criteria. Patients receiving both treatments or with contraindications were excluded to minimize bias. Replicates in this retrospective study were defined by the independent patient records included after matching, with all statistical analyses conducted on these matched cohorts.

Results

Participant’s selection

Based on screening of 134 health care organizations (HCOs) from the Global Collaborative Network of the TriNetX platform on October 8, 2024, 399,664 people with both COPD and HF were identified. After excluding 2,088 people with liver dysfunction or hepatic failure, 9,071 new users of ARNI and 71,836 new users of RASi were identified, each with two consecutive drug prescription records within one year and mutually exclusive drug use prior to the enrollment period. Following PSM, 9,055 patients were classified into the ARNI and RASi groups, respectively (Fig. 1).

Demographic characteristics of included individuals

There was no significant difference in age between the ARNI and RASi groups (69.4 ± 10.7 vs. 69.7 ± 11.4 years); however, the ARNI group had a higher proportion of female patients (38.2% vs. 31.5%) (supplementary table 5). Compared to the RASi group, the ARNI group had a higher percentage of individuals with unknown race (14.5% vs. 10.7%), but lifestyle factors were similar between groups. Regarding comorbidities, the ARNI group showed lower prevalence of COPD exacerbation, hypertensive disease, cerebrovascular disease, mood disorders, and schizophrenia. In terms of medications, the ARNI group had higher utilization of SGLT2i but lower utilization of beta-blockers. Laboratory examinations revealed that the ARNI group had lower LVEF compared to the RASi group, while HbA1c levels were similar between groups. After PSM, it yielded 9,055 matched patients in each cohort (supplementary table 5) with the mean follow-up duration of 425 ± 383 days for the ARNI group and 582 ± 434 days for the RASi group.

Primary outcome

In this retrospective cohort analysis, use of ARNI was associated with a lower risk of COPD exacerbation compared to RASi (13.1% vs. 18.7%; HR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.78–0.91; Table 1). Survival analysis showed a lower cumulative incidence of COPD exacerbation among patients in the ARNI group (log-rank test, p = 2.2 × 10–¹¹) (Fig. 2, Supplemental Data). This association persisted across all stratified analyses (HR < 1), including subgroups such as adults <65 years (HR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.68–0.93), adults ≥ 65 years (HR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.79–0.94), females (HR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.70–0.90), males (HR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.81–1.00), patients without prior COPD exacerbation (HR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.78–0.97), those using triple therapy (HR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.75–0.93), patients without recent HFAE (HR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.61–0.85), those with recent HFAE (HR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.80–0.94), and those on beta-blockers (HR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.80–0.94) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

People with COPD frequently have cardiovascular comorbidities, including HF, which requires a comprehensive, multidimensional treatment approach. Management should extend beyond inhaled bronchodilators, the mainstay of COPD therapy, to include medications for HF. This combined approach is crucial for alleviating dyspnea and reducing the risk of acute exacerbations, ultimately improving patient outcomes. In this study of 18,110 people with concurrent COPD and HF, ARNI was associated with a 16% lower risk of COPD exacerbation compared to RASi, a finding that remained consistent across stratified analyses. Furthermore, ARNI users were also associated with 10% reduced risk of acute respiratory failure and a 9% lower risk of LRTI. These findings demonstrate the additional clinical benefits of ARNI in patients with HF and COPD, suggesting its potential therapeutic role in this specific population.

The key goals in the treatment of COPD are to reduce the frequency of acute exacerbations and alleviate symptoms. Among these symptoms, dyspnea is the most common and distressing21, markedly impacting patients’ quality of life and daily functioning. Therefore, addressing dyspnea is a central focus in managing COPD, alongside efforts to prevent exacerbations. Dyspnea, physical activity, exercise capacity, and COPD exacerbations are closely interconnected, forming a vicious circle that can worsen over time if not properly managed22. Dyspnea from COPD or heart failure can limit the patient’s activity, quality of life, and physical function. Physical activity also plays an important role in therapy23. Patients with COPD have a higher prevalence of heart failure compared to individuals without COPD24. Properly tailored treatment for patients with both COPD and HF, particularly with the use of ARNI, not only helps reduce respiratory failure and LRTIs but also has a beneficial effect on preventing acute exacerbations, as demonstrated in our study.

Although ARNIs are not designed to directly target the pathophysiological mechanisms of COPD, their use may confer indirect benefits in COPD patients, particularly those with coexisting heart failure. ARNIs have been shown to improve cardiac function and optimize hemodynamic stability. These cardiovascular improvements may positively influence respiratory health in several ways. Improved cardiac output can enhance pulmonary perfusion and systemic oxygen delivery, potentially leading to better oxygenation and reduced sensation of dyspnea. As a result, patients may experience improved exercise tolerance and functional capacity, which are key determinants of health status and resilience in COPD. Additionally, better control of heart failure symptoms may reduce pulmonary congestion and prevent fluid accumulation in the lungs, which could otherwise exacerbate respiratory symptoms. These physiological improvements, although not directly targeting COPD, may contribute to a more stable clinical course by decreasing the frequency or severity of acute exacerbations. Overall, the cardiopulmonary interactions suggest that ARNI therapy might offer a protective effect against COPD exacerbations in patients with concurrent heart failure, through mechanisms that support respiratory function and general health.

Neprilysin inhibition plays a key role in increasing the bioavailability of natriuretic peptides by preventing the breakdown of endogenous vasoactive peptides such as atrial natriuretic peptide, brain natriuretic peptide, and C-type natriuretic peptide. This mechanism helps protect against myocardial cell death, providing a positive impact on HF patients, even in the presence of COPD. Natriuretic peptides enhance natriuresis and removal of excess fluid, while also acting as vasodilators in the cardiovascular system, which can improve overall circulation. Moreover, elevated natriuretic peptide levels can reduce renin secretion, thereby inhibiting the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, leading to a decrease in arterial blood pressure. It is important to note that a local renin-angiotensin system is present in various human tissues, including the lungs, where it may also influence disease progression in both HF and COPD25. Angiotensin-converting enzyme is found in particularly high concentrations in the lungs, where its activity is further amplified by chronic hypoxia, a condition commonly seen in patients with COPD26. Additionally, type 1 angiotensin II receptors are highly expressed in lung tissue, where they play a critical role in regulating various cellular processes, including controlling alveolar epithelial cell programmed cell death and the proliferation of lung fibroblasts, both of which contribute to lung remodeling27–29. These factors together suggest that Valsartan acts as a central mediator in the processes of lung injury and apoptosis. In an animal model, Valsartan alleviates the severe condition of COPD in rats co-exposed to cold stress and fine particulate matter (PM2.5)30. For the myocardium, the mechanisms of Valsartan are closely linked to the prevention of cardiac muscle hypertrophy. When combined with Sacubitril, these two drugs may work synergistically at the molecular level to reverse or halt left ventricular extracellular matrix remodeling, reduce cardiomyocyte cell death, and, through Valsartan, enhance the effects of Sacubitril. Notably, neither Sacubitril nor Valsartan alone is sufficient to reduce left ventricular extracellular matrix remodeling; it is the combination of both medications that activates these beneficial molecular processes31. The combination of Sacubitril and Valsartan may provide pleiotropic effects on the cardiovascular system, due to the broad, multifaceted actions of these drugs. Research has demonstrated that Valsartan can help prevent myocardial remodeling by inhibiting the guanine nucleotide-binding protein family. At the same time, Sacubitril reduces myocardial cell death by inhibiting the phosphatase and tensin homolog. Together, these effects make the combination therapy a powerful treatment option for patients with cardiovascular conditions32. The use of this new class of drugs in patients with both COPD and HF is supported by limited scientific data, highlighting the need for further research in this area.

Only a small number of studies have explored the combination of ARNI therapy with COPD and HF, underscoring the potential for additional investigation into its benefits and efficacy in this patient population. A study33 was conducted to evaluate outcomes in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, specifically considering their COPD status, within the context of the PARAGON-HF trial (Prospective Comparison of Angiotensin Receptor Neprilysin Inhibitor With Angiotensin Receptor Blocker Global Outcomes in Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction). The trial aimed to assess the effectiveness of various treatments in HF with preserved ejection fraction patients with COPD. After adjusting for multiple variables, the results revealed that COPD was associated with poorer clinical outcomes in this patient population, showing a 51% higher risk for a composite of first and recurrent hospitalizations for heart failure and cardiovascular death in patients with COPD. Additionally, COPD patients had a 54% increased likelihood of hospitalization due to heart failure. The analysis also found that COPD was linked to a higher risk of all-cause mortality. However, the study did not assess acute exacerbations, respiratory failure, or LRTI in COPD patients. Another large-scale study34 included 8,399 patients diagnosed with HF with reduced ejection fraction as part of the PARADIGM-HF trial (Prospective Comparison of Angiotensin Receptor Blocker–Neprilysin Inhibitor With Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitor to Determine Impact on Global Mortality and Morbidity in Heart Failure). This trial aimed to compare the clinical outcomes of patients treated with sacubitril/valsartan versus those treated with enalapril. After adjusting for multiple variables, the results showed that patients with COPD faced a higher risk of hospitalization due to HF, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.32 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.13–1.54). Additionally, COPD was associated with a higher risk of the composite outcome of cardiovascular death or HF hospitalization (HR, 1.18; 95% CI, 1.05–1.34). Patients with COPD also had a greater likelihood of any cardiovascular hospitalization (HR, 1.17; 95% CI, 1.05–1.31), and an even higher risk of non-cardiovascular hospitalizations (HR, 1.45; 95% CI, 1.29–1.64). Despite these increased risks in the COPD group, the benefit of sacubitril/valsartan over enalapril was consistent across both COPD and non-COPD patients for all evaluated endpoints, suggesting that sacubitril/valsartan provided superior outcomes regardless of COPD status. These findings support the efficacy of ARNI treatment in patients with both COPD and HF. However, the studies conducted have not specifically focused on COPD acute exacerbations or respiratory failure. This gap in the literature prompted the authors of the present study to undertake a detailed analysis of the role of ARNI therapy in managing both COPD and HF, particularly in relation to exacerbations and respiratory complications.

We believe that HF is often underrecognized as a comorbidity in COPD, particularly in relation to its markedly impact on quality of life, hospitalization rates, and mortality outcomes. Our findings suggest that ARNI therapy plays crucial roles in improving key clinical outcomes for patients with COPD and HF. Specifically, ARNI proved beneficial in this high-risk subgroup, highlighting the potential value of comprehensive management for COPD patients with concurrent HF. Therapeutic management of COPD should, therefore, be carefully considered and consistent, taking into account all comorbidities that may contribute to disease progression. It is important to address these comorbid conditions to prevent worsening symptoms such as dyspnea. For COPD patients with HF, improving overall body function through targeted treatment can lead to profoundly benefits, including the alleviation of dyspnea symptoms and a reduction in disease progression.

There are several limitations to our study. First, as a retrospective analysis, the study’s design inherently limits the ability to establish causality. Although we matched for factors such as heart failure severity, lung function, comorbidities, and bronchodilator use, treatment protocols may vary across different regions and among physicians, which could introduce inconsistency in management approaches for COPD and HF. Second, patient adherence data were not available, which is a key consideration in assessing the real-world effectiveness of treatments. Third, patients with a history of heart transplantation, ventricular assist device implantation, endomyocardial disease, congenital heart disease, chemotherapy-induced heart failure, cardiac amyloidosis, or sarcoidosis were not excluded during the initial cohort construction. As these conditions may influence treatment selection and clinical outcomes, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis excluding these patients (coding listed in Table 2). The results remained consistent, with both primary and secondary outcomes showing the same trend and maintaining statistical significance (Table 3). However, we identified heart failure patients using ICD-10 codes for systolic heart failure (I50.2 and I50.4), which are generally associated with reduced or mildly reduced ejection fraction (LVEF < 50%)35. This may have limited our ability to differentiate treatment effects across specific heart failure phenotypes (HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF). Fifth, excluding exacerbations that occurred within the first 30 days following treatment initiation may have implications for the interpretation of our study findings. This 30-day window was intentionally applied to reduce potential confounding from early events that may not reflect the true effect of ARNI or RASi treatment. Such early events could be driven by underlying acute illness, clinical instability at the time of therapy initiation, or residual effects of prior treatments. By excluding this period, we aimed to better isolate associations reflective of the longer-term impact of the medications. However, we acknowledge that this methodological choice may result in an underestimation of early exacerbation events and potentially overlook clinically meaningful outcomes if exacerbation occur shortly after treatment initiation. As such, this could limit the generalizability of our findings to the immediate post-initiation phase, where treatment transitions or acute clinical changes may be particularly relevant. Lastly, relying solely on ICD-10-CM codes to identify COPD exacerbations may not capture all episodes, particularly those classified as mild exacerbations and managed without formal diagnostic coding or hospitalization. The primary outcome in our study reflects moderate-to-severe COPD exacerbations, as identified through ICD-10-CM codes. These events generally represent exacerbations that require healthcare encounters, including outpatient management, emergency department visits, or hospital admissions. We acknowledge that this approach may underestimate the incidence of mild exacerbations, which are often treated solely with short-acting beta-2 agonists or other medication adjustments, without a corresponding diagnostic code or formal healthcare visit. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted in the context of clinically remarkable exacerbations - those that result in healthcare utilization and are captured in administrative data through diagnostic coding. In conclusion, this large retrospective cohort study, the use of ARNI in patients with coexisting COPD and HF was associated with a lower risk of clinically COPD exacerbations compared to RASi. This association was consistent across multiple patient subgroups and remained robust in stratified analyses. Additionally, ARNI use was linked to reduced risks of acute respiratory failure and lower respiratory tract infections. These findings suggest that, in real-world settings, ARNI may provide potential clinical benefits beyond cardiovascular outcomes in patients with concurrent COPD and HF. However, given the observational nature of the study, these associations warrant further confirmation through prospective research.
