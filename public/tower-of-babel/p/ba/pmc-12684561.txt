Disparities in Geographic Access to Cardiac Rehabilitation Among Socially Vulnerable Communities
Authors: Edward W Chen, Wen‐Chih Wu, Ling Han, Parul U Gandhi, Lori A Bastian, Cynthia A Brandt, Kristin M Mattocks, Joyce Oen‐Hsiao, Merilyn S Varghese
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is a dearth of research examining geographic disparities in access to cardiac rehabilitation (CR). We evaluated whether disparities in geographic access to CR exist among socially vulnerable communities, using the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI).MethodsAmong 3113 US counties, we investigated relationships between SVI and number of hospitals with CR per 100 000 adults, modeled with SVI as a linear variable, and SVI and distance to the nearest county with CR facilities, modeled using spline terms for SVI. We used multivariable zero‐inflated negative binomial regression modeling adjusted for county population, CR eligibility, percentage without health insurance, metropolitan status, and availability of existing health care infrastructure. We explored whether these relationships changed in the context of rurality.ResultsAfter adjustment, as SVI increased by 1 percentile, number of hospitals with CR per 100 000 adults decreased by 1.2% (P<0.001). For distance to CR, individual spline terms were not significant in our adjusted model; however, the overall predicted distance to CR increased as SVI increased. When stratified by metropolitan status, these relationships not only persisted but also intensified in rural counties. As SVI increased by 1 percentile, the odds of a county containing a CR facility decreased by 0.9% in metropolitan counties (P=0.002) versus 2.2% in rural counties (P<0.001).ConclusionMore socially vulnerable communities experienced worse geographic access to CR facilities. This access disparity intensified in rural communities. Our findings call for reducing barriers to CR access among socially vulnerable and rural communities, potentially through virtual or hybrid models of CR.Keywords: cardiac rehabilitation, geographic disparities, socially vulnerableSubject Categories: Secondary Prevention, Risk Factors, Race and Ethnicity, Social Determinants of Health

Cardiac rehabilitation (CR), a multidisciplinary care model consisting of supervised exercise, risk factor modification, and psychosocial counseling, is a fundamental component of improving health outcomes in patients with cardiovascular disease. 1 However, CR is strikingly underused. In 2016, approximately one quarter of all CR‐eligible Medicare fee‐for‐service beneficiaries participated in CR, of whom only 26.9% completed CR through 2017. 2 CR use has further declined, hastened by the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic. After declaration of the national emergency, CR participation sharply declined in April 2020 (56 CR sessions per 100 000 Medicare beneficiaries); though participation rates gradually recovered through December 2021, they remained lower than prepandemic levels (698 monthly CR sessions postpandemic versus 895 sessions prepandemic per 100 000 Medicare beneficiaries). 3

Disparities in CR use among socially vulnerable populations are well‐documented. 3 Populations such as older adults, 2 racially or ethnically underrepresented groups, 2 , 4 people of lower socioeconomic status, 5 people with limited educational attainment, 5 , 6 and other marginalized groups 7 have disproportionately low rates of CR participation and completion. The mechanisms underlying these disparities include provider‐related factors, such as inadequate referral strategies and poor interprovider communication, 8 as well as patient‐related social determinants of health, such as limited financial resources, 9 lack of insurance coverage, 10 lower health literacy, 8 and language/cultural barriers. 8 , 9 These same populations also have disproportionately worse cardiovascular disease and health outcomes. 11 , 12 , 13 Rural patients in particular experience significantly higher mortality rates related to acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, and stroke compared with their urban counterparts 14 , 15 ; however, they also have lower rates of CR referral and participation. 16

Contemporary research examining geographic disparities in access to CR is sparse. A prior study suggested that underrepresented minorities, those of lower socioeconomic status, and inhabitants of rural areas are disproportionately located in areas without CR facilities—known as CR deserts. 17 Rural inhabitants have also reported commute distance as a significant geographic barrier to CR use. 16 Whether geographic access to CR is a barrier among urban socially vulnerable populations is also unclear.

In response, our goal was to perform a nationwide analysis to investigate the relationship between social vulnerability and geographic access to CR, and the impact of rurality. Specifically, our aim was to evaluate whether socially vulnerable communities have worse geographic access to CR and how this relationship changes when viewed in the context of metropolitan status.

METHODS

We conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of 3113 counties in the continental United States to determine the relationship between social vulnerability and geographic access to CR. All data used are publicly available and thus met criteria for exemption by the Veterans Affairs Connecticut Healthcare System Institutional Review Board.

Social Vulnerability Index

Our primary exposure for this study was county Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) in 2018. SVI was created by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry 18 ; it is a composite variable derived from 15 US census variables related to 4 themes: (1) socioeconomic status, (2) household composition and disability, (3) minority status and language, and (4) housing type and transportation. Every county is assigned a percentile ranking for each theme, and an overall SVI percentile is calculated by ranking counties based on their composite across all themes. A higher SVI percentile indicates a higher degree of social vulnerability relative to all other counties.

Geographic Availability of CR

We used 2 outcome variables as measures of geographic availability of CR: (1) number of hospitals with CR per 100 000 adults within the county and (2) distance to nearest county with CR.

The number of hospitals with CR per 100 000 adults (2019) was calculated by dividing the number of hospitals with CR per county by the number of county residents aged 18 years and older in hundred‐thousands (obtained from the US Census Bureau 19 ). If a county did not have a hospital with CR, the resulting value would be zero.

Distance to nearest county with CR was calculated by combining county distances to one another and county data for hospitals with CR. Every county’s distance in miles to all other counties was obtained from the National Bureau of Economic Research. 20 The Haversine formula was used to calculate the orthodromic distances between counties; the internal points designated by the US Census Bureau located at or near the geographic center of each county were used as measuring points. County data for the number of hospitals with CR in 2019 were obtained through the CDC 21 from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse. 22 Using the Python 3.12.4 programming language, we developed a code (available in Supplementary File 1) to calculate each county’s distance to another county that housed at least 1 hospital with CR. If a county itself contained at least 1 hospital with CR, the resulting distance would be zero. If a county did not contain any hospitals with CR, the program would iterate through the county’s distance to all other counties, identifying the nearest county that housed at least 1 hospital with CR and the corresponding distance in miles.

CR Eligibility and Percentage Without Health Insurance

We adjusted for county CR eligibility rate and percentage without health insurance in our regression analyses to ensure any relationships observed were not due to differences in eligibility for CR services or insurance status. Patients were considered eligible if they experienced a qualifying cardiovascular event, such as acute myocardial infarction, percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary artery bypass surgery, valve surgery, heart transplantation, stable angina, or heart failure. 1 , 21 , 23 County data for CR eligibility rate per 1000 Medicare fee‐for‐service beneficiaries (2018–2019) and percentage without health insurance (2014–2018) were obtained from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 21 and US Census Bureau, 19 respectively.

Metropolitan Status

We adjusted for metropolitan status, as rural inhabitants have been reported to experience significant geographic barriers to using CR. 16 , 17 County metropolitan status was obtained from the Office of Management and Budget, 21 which classified counties as large central metro, fringe metro, medium/small metro, or nonmetro. In our study, counties were divided into 2 groups: metro (including large central metro, fringe metro, and medium/small metro) and rural (nonmetro).

Availability and Proximity of Existing Health Care Infrastructure

We adjusted for distance to the nearest county with a hospital capable of cardiac catheterization (2019) and population per primary care physician (2017) to reduce potential confounding caused by existing health care infrastructure. Hospitals capable of cardiac catheterization were chosen because coronary artery disease and its acute and chronic complications are among the most common indications for CR. 1 , 23 Distance to the nearest county with cardiac catheterization was calculated similarly to distance to the nearest county with CR. County data for these variables were obtained through the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 21 from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse.

Statistical Analysis

The unit of analysis was continental US counties (N=3113). US territories were excluded due to insufficient data. Alaska was also excluded due to extreme outlier values in distance to nearest county with CR. County variables were described as median with interquartile range (IQR). All variables were stratified by SVI quartiles, for which linear trend significance was calculated.

Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to measure the correlations of SVI with the outcomes of interest as well as county population, percentage without health insurance, distance to nearest county with cardiac catheterization, and population per primary care physician. As a supplementary analysis, we measured the correlations between the outcomes of interest and each of the 4 themes that compose SVI.

Given the presence of substantial overdispersion (variance exceeding the mean) and a high proportion of structural zeros in our outcome variables (1806 of 3113 US counties lacked a CR facility), we conducted multivariable zero‐inflated negative binomial (ZINB) regression analyses to further assess the relationships between SVI and number of hospitals with CR per 100 000 adults as well as distance to nearest county with CR. We first evaluated the linearity of these relationships using likelihood ratio tests comparing linear and nonlinear spline models. For the association between SVI and number of hospitals with CR per 100 000 adults, the likelihood ratio test yielded a P value of 0.578, supporting the use of a linear model. In contrast, the association between SVI and distance to nearest county with CR demonstrated a statistically significant likelihood ratio test (P<0.05), indicating better fit with a nonlinear model. To further assess model performance for this outcome, we compared Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion between the linear and spline specifications. Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion values favored using splines for the unadjusted model; however, in the adjusted model, Akaike information criterion favored linear SVI whereas Bayesian information criterion favored spline. Given the mixed results for the adjusted model, we proceeded with the nonlinear specification, modeling SVI using restricted cubic splines with 4 knots to capture potential nonlinearity. For comparison, results from the linear specification are also presented in the Supplementary Results. All models were adjusted for county population, CR eligibility rate, percentage without health insurance, metropolitan status, distance to the nearest county with cardiac catheterization, and population per primary care physician.

We tested for statistical interaction between metropolitan status and the associations of SVI with both outcomes by using an interaction term in our regression models and by conducting a subgroup analysis by metropolitan status. The interaction between metropolitan status and the associations of SVI with number of hospitals with CR per 100 000 adults (P<0.001) was significant. The interaction terms between SVI splines and metropolitan status with distance to nearest county with CR were not significant. We performed a stratified analysis to further evaluate whether the relationship between SVI and our outcomes varied by metropolitan status.

To visualize the association between SVI and predicted distance to nearest county with CR, we constructed a hypothetical data set in which SVI was varied from 0 to 100, and all other covariates were fixed at their mean values. Using the fitted ZINB model, we generated predicted distances across the range of SVI values and calculated 95% CIs based on SEs of the predictions. This approach was applied to the unadjusted model, fully adjusted model, and the fully adjusted model stratified by metropolitan status.

To illustrate the relationship between social vulnerability and geographic access to CR, we also created a composite index that incorporated SVI and both outcomes of interest, with details of the calculation included in the Supplementary Methods. Counties with higher SVI, greater distance to nearest county with CR, or fewer number of CR per 100 000 adults correspondingly had higher composite indices, indicating greater risk for social vulnerability as well as disparate geographic access to CR. Using the Python 3.12.4 programming language in conjunction with the GeoPandas, Matplotlib, and Pandas libraries, we plotted each county’s composite index, categorized into quartiles, onto a national map obtained from the US Census Bureau’s TIGER/Line shapefiles. 24

County population was used as analytic weights when applicable. All analyses were performed using STATA SE v18.0. Statistical significance was defined as a 2‐sided P value <0.05.

RESULTS

Among 3113 US counties, the overall median number of hospitals with CR per 100 000 adults per county was 0 (IQR, 0–2.13), and the overall median distance to the nearest county with CR was 16.48 miles (IQR, 0–27.94) (Table 1). The overall median county population was 26 031 (IQR, 11 178–68 024). The overall median county CR eligibility rate per 1000 Medicare fee‐for‐service beneficiaries was 17.70 (IQR, 14.80–21.50). The overall median county percentage without health insurance was 9.1% (IQR, 6.20–12.50). The overall median distance to the nearest county with cardiac catheterization was 26.98 miles (IQR, 15.13–42.59), and the overall median population per primary care physician was 2100 (IQR, 1400–3100). When stratified by SVI quartile, with increasing quartiles of social vulnerability, the median number of hospitals with CR per 100 000 adults per county decreased, and the median distance to the nearest county with CR increased in significant linear trends. Median county CR eligibility rate, percentage without health insurance, and population per primary care physician also increased in significant linear trends. There were no significant trends between SVI and either county population or distance to the nearest county with cardiac catheterization across quartiles.

In the multivariable ZINB regression analyses between SVI and the number of hospitals with CR per 100 000 adults (Table 3), each 1‐percentile increase in SVI was associated with a 1.6% decrease in the expected number of hospitals with CR per 100 000 adults in the unadjusted model (incidence rate ratio [IRR], 0.984 [95% CI, 0.981–0.987]; P<0.001) and a 1.2% decrease in the adjusted model (IRR, 0.988 [95% CI, 0.985–0.990]; P<0.001), after controlling for county population, CR eligibility rate, percentage without health insurance, metropolitan status, distance to nearest county with cardiac catheterization, and population per primary care physician. In the adjusted zero‐inflation component, each 1‐percentile increase in SVI was associated with a 2.2% increase in the odds of a county lacking a hospital with CR (odds ratio [OR], 1.022 [95% CI, 1.018–1.026]; P<0.001). Stratified analyses by metropolitan status (Table 4) showed that, in the adjusted model, each 1‐percentile increase in SVI was associated with a 0.6% decrease in number of hospitals with CR per 100 000 adults in metro counties (IRR, 0.994 [95% CI, 0.990–0.998]; P=0.001) and a 0.5% decrease in rural counties (IRR, 0.995 [95% CI, 0.993–0.996]; P<0.001). Corresponding odds of lacking a hospital with CR increased by 1.7% in metro counties (OR, 1.017 [95% CI, 1.008–1.026]; P<0.001) and 2.5% in rural counties (OR, 1.025 [95% CI, 1.021–1.029]; P<0.001).

In the multivariable ZINB regression analyses between SVI and distance to the nearest county with a CR facility (Table 5), SVI was modeled using restricted cubic splines with 4 knots, resulting in 3 spline terms. In the unadjusted analysis, none of the spline terms was statistically significant: spline 1 (IRR, 0.997 [95% CI, 0.993–1.001]; P=0.147), spline 2 (IRR, 1.002 [95% CI, 0.989–1.014]; P=0.809), and spline 3 (IRR, 1.013 [95% CI, 0.977–1.049]; P=0.474). This pattern remained in the adjusted model, with no spline terms reaching significance. In the adjusted zero‐inflation component, however, each 1‐percentile increase in SVI was associated with a 1.7% decrease in the odds that a county had a CR facility within its borders (ie, a distance of zero) (OR, 0.983 [95% CI, 0.980–0.986]; P<0.001). Stratified analysis by metropolitan status (Table 6) revealed that, in metro counties, the adjusted model showed a statistically significant association only for spline 3 (IRR, 1.065 [95% CI, 1.007–1.123]; P=0.024), whereas spline 1 (IRR, 1.005 [95% CI, 0.999–1.011]; P=0.078) and spline 2 (IRR, 0.982 [95% CI, 0.964–1.000]; P=0.059) did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, none of the spline terms were statistically significant in rural counties: spline 1 (IRR, 0.999 [95% CI, 0.994–1.005]; P=0.807), spline 2 (IRR, 1.002 [95% CI, 0.986–1.019]; P=0.761), and spline 3 (IRR, 1.001 [95% CI, 0.957–1.046]; P=0.955). Corresponding odds that a county had a CR facility within its borders decreased by 0.9% in metro counties (OR, 0.991 [95% CI, 0.986–0.997]; P=0.002) and 2.2% in rural counties (OR, 0.978 [95% CI, 0.974–0.982]; P<0.001). When SVI was modeled as a linear variable (Table S2), each 1‐percentile increase in SVI was associated with a 0.1% increase in distance to the nearest county with CR in the unadjusted model (IRR, 1.001 [95% CI, 1.000–1.002]; P=0.002) and a 0.3% increase in the adjusted model (IRR, 1.003 [95% CI, 1.002–1.004]; P<0.001). Stratified analysis by metropolitan status (Table S3) showed that, in the adjusted model, each 1‐percentile increase in SVI was associated with a 0.2% increase in distance in metro counties (IRR, 1.002 [95% CI, 1.001–1.004]; P=0.003) and a 0.2% increase in rural counties (IRR, 1.002 [95% CI, 1.001–1.003]; P<0.001).

DISCUSSION

Our study is among the first to evaluate the relationship between social vulnerability and geographic access to CR on a national level. There were 3 key findings. First, we found that increasing degrees of social vulnerability were associated with a decrease in the number of hospitals with CR facilities and higher odds of lacking a CR facility entirely, independent of county population, CR eligibility rates, percentage without health insurance, and availability of existing health care infrastructure. Second, the relationship between SVI and distance to a county with CR appeared more nuanced. None of the individual spline terms reached significance in our adjusted model, though predicted distances overall suggested that higher SVI values were associated with greater distances to CR. Third, when examined by metropolitan status, many of these relationships not only persisted but also intensified in rural counties compared with metro counties.

Our study found that socially vulnerable communities have disproportionately worse geographic access to CR, identifying a critical population that requires improvement in health equity. Potential mechanisms driving these disparities in socially vulnerable communities include limited existing health care infrastructure, 17 lack of financial incentives to establish CR facilities including flawed financing and reimbursement structures, 25 a perceived lack of demand for CR due to disproportionately low referral rates of ethnic and racial minority patients, 25 and workforce shortages of health care providers in underserved areas. 26 Our findings come amid recent advocacy efforts to restore and implement virtual and remote CR. These novel care models were introduced during the COVID‐19 pandemic to improve access to CR but have since been defunded by the federal government. 27 Current data show that hybrid CR, a combination of in‐person CR and virtual or remote CR, is equally as effective and safe compared with traditional in‐person CR. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 In addition, recent research indicates that the majority of the population living in CR deserts may have internet capabilities to benefit from virtual or remote CR. 17 Our findings spotlight an opportunity to improve CR access in a disproportionately marginalized population.

We are among the first to investigate the intersection of social vulnerability and rurality in relation to geographic access to CR. We found a persistence in disparate geographic access to CR among socially vulnerable communities in both metropolitan and rural areas. However, the magnitude of the disparity is greater in rural areas, where we found that counties had significantly higher odds of lacking a CR facility altogether. This difference in effect mirrors current rural–urban disparities in CR use. Rural inhabitants have lower rates of CR referral and participation compared with urban inhabitants 16 , 32 ; they also experience more significant geographic barriers to accessing in‐person care, such as farther commutes, lack of public transport, poor road quality, hazardous weather conditions, health conditions that prevent lengthy travel, and competing obligations (eg, family care, occupation). 33 , 34 , 35 Our results imply that many of the patients adversely affected by rural–urban disparities in geographic access to CR may also be among the most socially vulnerable, highlighting the potential dangers of these compounding risk factors. Indeed, disparate geographic access to CR in rural communities has shown to be associated with poor cardiovascular health outcomes. 36 These findings are especially relevant as the COVID‐19 pandemic has led to significant and ongoing reductions in CR participation as well as closures of CR facilities in rural communities. 3 , 37 Therefore, policy and health system changes are crucial to prevent widening of these disparities.

Improving access to CR in socially vulnerable communities among both urban and rural areas requires multifaceted policy interventions and health care system reforms. Potential strategies include increasing funding for health care infrastructure in underserved and rural areas, implementing automated referral processes, and providing financial and logistical support (eg, subsidized transportation). 38 , 39 Moreover, virtual and hybrid CR models have the potential to mitigate logistical and geographic barriers to accessing CR. However, there are several potential limitations in implementing these programs, particularly in socially vulnerable communities. Digital literacy remains a significant concern, as many socially vulnerable populations, including older adults and impoverished individuals, have limited experience with telehealth platforms or lack the necessary technology. 40 , 41 Older adults in particular also suffer from social isolation and lack of peer support needed for successful follow‐up. 41 Virtual CR may also be subject to financial constraints, especially with unclear reimbursement plans by the federal government. 27 Furthermore, virtual CR requires reliable internet access, which may not be available in disparate communities. 17 To enhance feasibility, targeted strategies such as supplying user‐friendly technology (eg, with a preloaded CR application), expanding reimbursement structures, and improving access to reliable internet are crucial. By addressing these barriers, virtual and hybrid CR have the potential to significantly reduce disparities in CR access among socially vulnerable communities.

Though to a lesser degree than in rural areas, the persistence in disparate geographic access to CR among socially vulnerable communities in metropolitan areas is a unique finding that accentuates the potential driving force of social vulnerability in mediating these disparities. Metropolitan areas likely share similar barriers to accessing CR as rural areas; indeed, CR deserts have been shown to exist in both metropolitan and rural settings. 42 Metropolitan areas may have their own unique geographic challenges that disproportionally affect socially vulnerable populations, such as limited and expensive parking availability 43 and higher rates of housing insecurity. 44 Metropolitan areas may also have unique cultural barriers in accessing CR. Immigrant populations, some with limited English proficiency, disproportionately reside more in metropolitan areas and experience challenges in participating in CR. 8 , 45 Urban low‐income individuals are also more likely to work multiple jobs or have unstable work schedules, posing significant time barriers to attending structured CR. 46 Together, these socioeconomic and geographic factors likely contribute to the underuse of CR in metropolitan areas. These findings continue to emphasize the critical need for policy changes that increase CR access among socially vulnerable communities.

Although our study has several novel findings, there are a few key limitations. First, we only accounted for hospital‐based CR and not office‐based CR. Though hospital‐based CR accounts for 97% of CR settings in the United States based on Medicare claims data in 2021, the true effect size of our results may have been diluted without accounting for office‐based CR facilities, possibly inflating observed geographic disparities in CR access. Second, CR eligibility and participation rates were derived from Medicare beneficiaries, which may limit the generalizability of our results. Third, our methodology for calculating distance to the nearest county with CR does not account for individual county size or exact positioning of CR facilities and thus may not accurately reflect individual access to CR, especially in larger counties. An individual living in a county with nonzero CR facilities may still experience significant geographic barriers to using CR if the county area and commute distance are sufficiently large. Conversely, an individual living in a county without CR facilities may need to travel only a short distance to the border of a neighboring county to access a CR facility. Thus, the true effect size of our results on the individual‐level may be larger or smaller than demonstrated. To improve generalizability, future studies should incorporate more precise methods to investigate geospatial relationships, such as using population‐weighted centroids or subcounty level data (eg, census tracts or zip codes). Relatedly, this is an ecological study; therefore, no inferences can be made at the individual level. Lastly, this is a cross‐sectional analysis and therefore cannot establish temporality or causality.

CONCLUSIONS

In this nationwide cross‐sectional analysis, we demonstrated that counties with a higher degree of social vulnerability had worse geographic access to CR facilities even when accounting for county population, CR eligibility, insurance status, and availability of existing health care infrastructure. This disparity persisted in both metropolitan and rural counties and intensified in rural counties. Our findings call for reducing barriers to CR access among socially vulnerable and rural communities using targeted policy interventions such as expanding virtual telehealth options, increasing funding for CR, and improving health care infrastructure in underserved areas.
