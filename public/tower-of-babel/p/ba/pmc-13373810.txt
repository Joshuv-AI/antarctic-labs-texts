Genomic forecasting for climate‐resilient fruit trees
Authors: Maxime Criado, Mathieu Brisson, Karine Alix, Yann XC Bourgeois, Olivier François, Élodie Marchadier, Amandine Cornille
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFruit trees – long‐lived perennial crops cultivated for their edible fruits or nuts and frequently propagated clonally – are increasingly exposed to climate extremes that threaten their productivity and survival. Yet their capacity to adapt to rapid environmental change remains poorly understood. We argue that fruit trees and their wild relatives are powerful but underused systems for advancing genomic forecasting in perennials, with a focus on genomic offset analyses. Genomic offset estimates the mismatch between current genomic variation and that predicted to be optimal under future climates, offering a promising framework to anticipate maladaptation and guide conservation, breeding, and management strategies. Although its application is expanding rapidly in annual crops and forest trees, its interpretation and predictive value remain actively debated and require stronger empirical validation. Fruit trees are particularly well suited to address these challenges as they combine distinctive biology – including long generation times, clonal propagation and intensive management practices – with expanding genomic resources and common garden networks. Using emblematic Mediterranean and temperate species, we outline a roadmap that combines genomic offset with common‐garden networks, high‐resolution climate data, and trait‐based fitness proxies. Together, these resources position fruit trees as an powerful model to evaluate and refine genomic forecasting into a practical tool for biodiversity‐informed breeding and conservation under global change, and better understand plant adaptation and maladaptation processes.Keywords: climate adaptation, fruit trees, genomic offset, maladaptation, perennial crops, phenotypic plasticity

Fruit trees: a missing link in the genomic forecasting landscape

Climate change threatens global food systems by reducing productivity and increasing uncertainty. Biodiversity underpins agricultural resilience, yet it is increasingly at risk (FAO, 2019). Wild relatives of crops have historically contributed to breeding for traits associated with adaptation to extreme environments, including high‐altitude conditions (Hufford et al., 2013; Burgarella et al., 2018), and remain key resources for resilience (Zhang et al., 2017; Hübner & Kantar, 2021; Pironon & Soto Gomez, 2021; Chen et al., 2026), although their use can also disrupt co‐adapted gene complexes or lead to outbreeding depression (Veltman et al., 2025). Key questions, therefore, remain. How will crops and their wild relatives respond to rapid climate change, given their levels of standing genetic and epigenetic variation? What can wild populations reveal about the processes of adaptation and maladaptation? And how can these genetic resources be mobilized to enhance resilience? More broadly, to what extent can crop wild relatives serve as informative systems for anticipating the impacts of global warming on perennial crops?

Genomic offset offers a predictive framework by estimating the mismatch between the current genomic composition of a population and that expected to be optimal under future climates (Fitzpatrick & Keller, 2015; Capblancq et al., 2020a; Bernatchez et al., 2024) (Fig. 1). By linking genomic variation to environmental gradients and projecting these relationships into future climates (Fig. 1; Box 1), genomic offset provides a proxy for potential maladaptation. Although increasingly applied in annual crops and forest trees (Aguirre‐Liguori et al., 2019; Jia et al., 2020; Rhoné et al., 2020; Capblancq et al., 2020b; Lazic et al., 2024; Varas‐Myrik et al., 2024; Archambeau et al., 2026) (Table 1), and more recently in perennial crops such as grapevine (Aguirre‐Liguori et al., 2022; Fiscus et al., 2025), its use remains debated due to assumptions and uncertainties (Lachmuth et al., 2023; Lotterhos, 2024; Ahrens et al., 2026) that require empirical validation and integration with ecological and phenotypic data. Strikingly, fruit trees remain largely absent from the genomic offset literature (Table 1).

Specific considerations for genomic offset analyses in fruit trees

Genomic offset integrates genomic, environmental, and phenotypic data to predict maladaptation under climate change. While the conceptual framework (Fig. 2) is widely applicable (Table 1), fruit trees present specific biological and methodological challenges that influence both inference and interpretation.

Step 1: Data acquisition in perennial systems

Sampling: Vegetative maintenance through grafting blurs the concept of local landraces, while wild relatives are often scattered and admixed with cultivated populations. Robust sampling requires genomic assignment approaches combined with targeted selection of well‐characterized populations and reference genotypes.

Genomic data: High‐quality reference genomes and single‐nucleotide polymorphism datasets are available for many crops (https://www.rosaceae.org; Table 1) but remain limited for wild relatives. Structural variants, such as transposable element insertions (Groza et al., 2024; Saidi et al., 2026), are likely important contributors to adaptation but remain underexplored.

Climate data: Human‐mediated dispersal and grafting decouple genotypes from their original environments, complicating the reconstruction of climatic niches. Rootstocks may further buffer environmental constraints, weakening genotype–environment signals.

Phenotypic data: Common garden experiments remain essential (Box 2) for quantifying fitness and validating genomic predictions, despite logistical constraints linked to long generation times and management sensitivity. Nonetheless, they offer a rare opportunity to compare fitness traits under controlled environments across wild and cultivated populations.

Step 2: Estimation of genome–environment relationships

Domestication history and population structure can bias inference. Genome–environment association approaches (e.g., LFMM (Caye et al., 2019), RDA (Oksanen et al., 2007), BAYPASS (Gautier, 2015)) estimate statistical relationships between genomic variation and environmental gradients, which serve as the basis for genomic offset predictions. Because population structure and demographic history can generate spurious associations, careful correction for structure remains essential (Gain et al., 2023). Integrating G × E models (De Walsche et al., 2025) and combining GEA with trait‐based analyses or genome‐wide association studies can improve the biological interpretation of adaptive variation, particularly when linking climate gradients with phenology‐, stress‐, or yield‐related traits in fruit trees. Performing simulations to assess the power and robustness of selected methods is also valuable, although few tools currently support generating pseudo‐observed datasets or flexible simulation frameworks adapted to perennial systems (Gain et al., 2023).

Step 3: Interpreting genomic offset

Genomic offset estimates reflect predicted mismatches between current and future allele frequencies but must be interpreted in light of slow generation times, strong human selection, and potential trade‐offs between climate adaptation and agronomic traits.

Step 4: Applications for breeding and conservation

Using future climate scenarios (e.g. representative concentration pathways (RCP)‐based climate scenarios) (Meinshausen et al., 2011), genomic offset analyses can help identify and inform vulnerability assessments and guide breeding and conservation strategies. However, plastic and epigenetic responses may also contribute to resilience, and their stability and heritability remain key open questions.

Fruit trees are long‐lived, domesticated perennials that combine sexual reproduction – typically via pollination – with subsequent vegetative propagation often via grafting to maintain selected genotypes. Most cultivars therefore arise from sexual recombination but are subsequently maintained through clonal propagation; as a result, a tree combines scion and rootstock genotypes within a single individual. This system preserves selected genotypes and can buffer environmental variation, but it limits evolutionary responses once cultivars are deployed (Gaut et al., 2015). Grown in highly managed agroecosystems (Herzog, 1998), fruit trees are also subject to standardized practices that can further decouple genotype–environment relationships. In parallel, their wild relatives harbor substantial adaptive diversity that could support resilience to climate change (Zhang et al., 2017; Greene et al., 2018; Hübner & Kantar, 2021), although this diversity is increasingly threatened by habitat loss and gene flow from cultivated taxa (Delplancke et al., 2012; Cornille et al., 2013, 2015; Savolainen et al., 2013; Diez et al., 2015; Feurtey et al., 2017; Flowers et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019). Together, these features raise important questions about the capacity of fruit trees and their wild relatives to adapt to future climates and highlight the need for predictive frameworks that can anticipate maladaptation. Despite this need, fruit trees remain largely overlooked in genomic offset studies. This omission is striking because they constitute an ideal system for evaluating genomic forecasting approaches, owing to their well‐documented pedigrees, expanding genomic and phenotypic resources, and growing networks of common gardens established across climatic gradients.

In the following sections, we argue that genomic offset provides a promising framework for understanding adaptation and maladaptation to climate change in fruit trees and that these species constitute powerful yet underused model systems for genomic forecasting in perennial crops. We focus on emblematic Mediterranean and temperate species – including apple (Malus domestica), apricot (Prunus armeniaca), peach (Prunus persica), olive (Olea europaea), and grapevine (Vitis vinifera), which, despite differences in growth form, share key features such as perenniality, clonal propagation, and strong human influence. We identify key knowledge gaps and methodological challenges at the interface of genomics and climate adaptation, and discuss how fruit tree systems can improve ecologically grounded predictions of plant resilience under climate change.

Gaps in our understanding of climate adaptation in fruit trees

Understanding how fruit trees have responded to environmental change is essential for predicting their future resilience. Wild relatives of cultivated fruit trees offer a key window into these dynamics because they retain substantial reservoirs of adaptive genetic diversity shaped by historical climatic fluctuations. Gene flow from wild populations has also contributed to the genomes of many domesticated fruit trees, introducing adaptive alleles that may persist in modern cultivars (Chen et al., 2026) or be reintroduced through targeted breeding (Cornille et al., 2012; Gélinas Bélanger, 2025). Identifying the genetic basis of local adaptation in wild relatives is therefore a critical step before applying genomic offset approaches to cultivated systems. Yet local adaptation is still poorly characterized in wild fruit trees and cannot be assumed a priori. Patterns of genetic differentiation, especially in fragmented landscapes with limited gene flow, may reflect not only adaptation but also neutral processes such as genetic drift or isolation‐by‐resistance (Le Corre & Kremer, 2003; Kawecki & Ebert, 2004; Cornille et al., 2013; Savolainen et al., 2013; Wang & Bradburd, 2014). Consequently, disentangling adaptive from nonadaptive genomic signals remains challenging. Empirical evidence is still scarce. In the European crabapple (Malus sylvestris), a wild relative of the cultivated apple, for example, some populations appear better adapted to past climatic conditions than to current ones, suggesting ongoing maladaptation (Chen et al., 2023). These observations highlight how little is still known about the extent, genetic basis, and evolutionary dynamics of climate adaptation in wild fruit trees. Addressing these questions is a prerequisite for evaluating the potential of genomic offset approaches in both wild and cultivated systems.

Investigating climate adaptation in cultivated fruit trees is further complicated by breeding practices. Most cultivars originate from sexual recombination during breeding and are subsequently maintained vegetatively through grafting, reducing effective recombination once deployed. Some traditional systems still maintain locally selected varieties that may occasionally incorporate seedlings or derive from historical selection under local environmental conditions (e.g. in parts of Central Asia or Iran) (Brite, 2021; Bina et al., 2022). Thus, while adaptive potential may be limited within established clones, it persists at the level of breeding populations, cultivar turnover, and introgression from wild or diverse germplasm. In cultivated apples, recent genomic analyses have identified signatures of selection associated with adaptation to northern environments, indicating that climate‐driven differentiation can also occur in domesticated systems (Skytte Af Sätra et al., 2024). More broadly, studies in forest trees indicate that long‐lived perennials can track environmental changes through polygenic adaptation (Kremer et al., 2025), but whether similar dynamics operate in fruit tree systems shaped by domestication, grafting, and management remains largely unresolved. In this context, genomic offset may be more informative for quantifying the climatic mismatch that breeding and germplasm mobilization must overcome than for predicting autonomous evolutionary responses in deployed cultivars.

Beyond these system‐specific constraints, a key unresolved issue concerns the genetic architecture underlying climate adaptation in fruit trees and how it conditions genomic offset predictions. Although climate adaptation in plants is generally assumed to be polygenic (Savolainen et al., 2013; De La Torre et al., 2019; De Miguel et al., 2022) – driven by many small‐effect loci – it remains unclear whether this framework applies to long‐lived fruit crops maintained largely as fixed cultivars. Historical breeding has focused on traits such as fruit size, flavor, and shelf life, typically under stable environmental conditions. Consequently, while genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) have successfully identified loci linked to these traits (Cao et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019; Groppi et al., 2021), the genetic basis of climate resilience in fruit trees remains comparatively understudied (Gros‐Balthazard et al., 2019; Sánchez‐Pérez et al., 2019; Groppi et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2021; Dong et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2024). Nevertheless, some progress has been made in understanding the genetic architecture of key phenological traits associated with climate responses, particularly flowering time and bud break (Celton et al., 2011; Urrestarazu et al., 2017; Jung et al., 2020; Song et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). Recent studies have also identified genomic regions and regulatory mechanisms associated with responses to abiotic stress (Tian et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2024; Dadole et al., 2026). However, signals remain limited and often indirect, leaving unresolved whether current cultivars retain sufficient standing genetic variation to support rapid adaptation to ongoing climate change. This limitation is further exacerbated by vegetative propagation, which restricts recombination and slows the incorporation of adaptive alleles. Consequently, many modern cultivars may already be partially mismatched to current or emerging climatic conditions, having been selected under past or relatively stable environments. Importantly, the magnitude of this mismatch is likely to vary among fruit tree species depending on their domestication history, breeding systems, and reliance on clonal propagation and grafting, with some crops potentially retaining stronger signals of local adaptation than others (Li et al., 2021; Dai et al., 2024). Although gene flow and introgression from wild relatives could theoretically introduce beneficial alleles, their contribution may remain constrained in commercial orchards dominated by fixed cultivars and controlled pollination practices (Savolainen et al., 2013; Kremer et al., 2025). In addition, introgression between cultivated and wild genotypes may disrupt co‐adapted gene complexes and epistatic interactions, potentially leading to outbreeding depression depending on the level of genetic divergence (Ellstrand et al., 2013). Consequently, the adaptive potential of fruit trees under climate change remains poorly resolved.

Additionally, the relative contributions of phenotypic plasticity – the ability of a genotype to express different phenotypes across environments – and genetic adaptation through selection or introgression are not well‐understood. These mechanisms may act independently or covary through patterns such as cogradient variation (Palacio‐López et al., 2015), but have yet to be disentangled in fruit trees. While phenotypic plasticity has been extensively studied in forest trees, particularly through multisite common‐garden experiments that reveal strong genotype‐by‐environment (G × E) interactions, comparable approaches remain limited in fruit trees, especially among wild relatives (Li et al., 2017; Park & Rodgers, 2023). This picture is further complicated by genetic load – the accumulation of deleterious mutations that reduce fitness regardless of environmental conditions – and its interaction with selection and gene flow. Although the evolutionary consequences of these interactions are well‐characterized theoretically, empirical evidence in perennial crops remains scarce (Gaut et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2017). Notably, studies in forest trees indicate that elevated genetic load does not necessarily constrain climate adaptation, particularly when buffered by plastic responses (Kremer et al., 2025).

In sum, our understanding of adaptation and maladaptation to climate change in fruit trees remains fragmented. Addressing this gap will require integrating approaches combining genomic, phenotypic, and environmental data – notably through common‐garden experiments – alongside predictive frameworks such as genomic offset. Paradoxically, fruit trees offer a powerful yet underexploited system for this purpose, given the growing availability of genomic resources and multi‐environment trial data. Realizing this potential will require overcoming biological and methodological challenges inherent to perennial crops that are largely maintained as fixed cultivars.

Methodological and conceptual challenges in applying genomic offset to fruit trees

Despite growing interest in genomic offset approaches, their application to fruit trees remains challenging because genotype–environment relationships are often blurred by fragmentation of wild populations, crop–wild admixture, vegetative propagation, grafting, and intensive management. These features do not preclude genomic offset analyses, but they require careful sampling, explicit control of confounding factors, and empirical validation.

Sampling constraints and genomic data limitations

A major hurdle is detecting local adaptation across environmental gradients, which is essential for robust genomic offset analysis (Box 1). In wild fruit tree populations, this task is challenged by fragmented distributions, low population densities, and frequent gene flow from cultivated individuals, as observed in Malus, Prunus, and Olea (Cornille et al., 2015; Besnard et al., 2018; Groppi et al., 2021). Without prior genotyping, distinguishing true wild individuals from crop‐wild hybrids is difficult (Delplancke et al., 2012; Cornille et al., 2015; Li et al., 2017), limiting the design of representative sampling strategies. Although genome–environment association (GEA) methods can account for population structure, their biological interpretation still depends on accurate individual classification and sampling schemes that capture both genetic and ecological variation.

In cultivated systems, genotype–environment relationships are further altered by grafting and management practices. A grafted fruit tree combines two genotypes – the scion and the rootstock – whose interaction can shape whole‐plant responses to climate (Box 2). Rootstocks may buffer environmental constraints and allow scions to persist outside their optimal climatic range, complicating both GEA and genomic offset inference. In principle, genomic offset could be estimated separately for scion and rootstock genotypes, thereby identifying distinct sources of climatic mismatch. Such systems also offer an opportunity to extend concepts such as local, forward, and reverse offset (Gougherty et al., 2021) to composite perennial systems, in which mismatch may arise independently in the rootstock or the scion, or from their interaction. In practice, however, rootstock identity is often missing from historical records or confounded with site, cultivar, or management practices. Experimental designs that use standardized rootstocks or explicitly model rootstock effects in replicated trials therefore offer the most robust way forward (Box 2).

Assessing fitness proxies in temperate fruit trees under climate change

Evaluating the climate resilience of fruit trees requires robust fitness proxies that capture both survival and reproductive performance. These proxies must be biologically meaningful, adapted to species' life‐history traits (e.g. deciduous vs evergreen, wild vs cultivated), and measurable under controlled conditions such as common gardens (Supporting Information Table S3).

Key fitness proxies

Vegetative vigor is commonly used as a proxy for survival, although trade‐offs with stress tolerance may occur, particularly under drought conditions (Fririon et al., 2023), and young individuals with limited capacity for recovery may be more prone to mortality (Waring, 1987). Reproductive traits are also key indicators of fitness, including bud burst, flowering time, fruit set, crop load, maturation and seed yield. In wild populations, seed germination and seedling vigor further inform reproductive success (Feurtey et al., 2017; Postma & Ågren, 2022).

Plasticity and climate sensitivity

Many fitness‐related traits are plastic and shaped by G × E interactions (Blumstein, 2024). Phenological traits, such as bud burst and flowering time, are especially climate‐sensitive and play a key role in synchronizing with pollinators or avoiding abiotic stress (e.g. late frosts) (Legave et al., 2013; Kudo & Cooper, 2019; Medda et al., 2022; Mura et al., 2022). Their expression reflects interactions among genetic, physiological, and epigenetic processes (Miryeganeh & Armitage, 2025).

Context dependency and standardization

Trait relevance varies across developmental stages, from juvenile growth to reproductive maturity (Petit et al., 2011), and may be influenced by management practices such as irrigation, pruning, and rootstock use (Table 1; Box 3). Standardized experimental designs are therefore essential to ensure comparability across sites and environments.

Toward integrative metrics

Fitness proxies can be complemented by eco‐climatic indicators – a subset of agroclimatic metrics computed over the crop cycle – that integrate environmental and phenological data to quantify key constraints, such as frost risk during budburst and flowering, thermal accumulation, and drought exposure (Caubel et al., 2015; Vanalli et al., 2021; Le Roux et al., 2024; Roussos, 2024). More broadly, resilience – defined as the capacity to resist and recover from stress – provides an integrative framework that links short‐term responses to long‐term fitness and improves the interpretation of genomic offset predictions (Serrie et al., 2024).

Agronomic practices introduce another layer of decoupling. Modern cultivars are often selected for yield stability across broad environments rather than for local adaptation, while irrigation, fertilization, pruning, and pest control can buffer environmental stress and weaken climate‐driven genomic signals (Campbell et al., 2025). As a result, GEA signals in cultivated systems may reflect a mixture of biological adaptation, breeding history, and reliance on external inputs. Careful sampling design, management‐related covariates, and validation in common gardens are therefore essential.

Technical limitations further constrain inference. While high‐quality reference genomes and dense single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) datasets exist for several cultivated fruit tree species, comparable resources remain limited for many wild relatives. In addition, structural variants – including transposable element insertions (Box 1) – are likely contributors to adaptation in long‐lived species but remain undercharacterized in most fruit tree systems (Mérot et al., 2020; Zunino et al., 2024).

Climate data resolution and ecological realism

A second major challenge concerns the environmental predictors used to train GEA models and generate genomic offset predictions. Widely used datasets such as WorldClim (Fick & Hijmans, 2017), CHELSA (Karger et al., 2017), and Copernicus (for Europe, https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets) provide broad spatial coverage but often rely on multi‐decadal averages that obscure short‐term climatic extremes such as late frosts, droughts, and heat waves (Dauphin et al., 2023). In fruit trees, such events can have delayed and cumulative effects on growth, reproduction, and yield across multiple years. Higher resolution climate data and eco‐climatic indicators can improve biological realism, but they require dedicated expertise and careful matching with phenological stages (Le Roux et al., 2024).

Historical and anthropogenic processes further complicate interpretation (Lotterhos, 2024). Centuries of human selection and long‐distance movement of cultivars have decoupled many genotypes from their original environments, making it difficult to reconstruct their historical climatic niches (De Aquino et al., 2022; McLaughlin et al., 2025). Unlike many annual crops, fruit trees often lack clear geographic centers of origin or strictly defined traditional varieties. Grafting and human‐mediated movement of cultivars allow genotypes to persist in climates far removed from their ancestral or selective environments. This suggests that adaptation in fruit tree systems may rely less on in situ genetic change than on cultivar turnover, crop substitution or relocation of production areas, as observed in viticulture, where varieties are increasingly moved to track their optimal climatic niche (Morales‐Castilla et al., 2020). Consequently, current distributions may reflect agronomic practices, market history, or management capacity rather than historical adaptation, complicating genotype–environment modeling and weakening the ecological interpretation of genomic offset predictions. One practical approach is to approximate the realized climatic niche of genotypes using current orchard distributions, cultivar‐specific production zones, or production‐weighted climatic barycenters, as proposed for perennial crops such as coffee (Tournebize et al., 2022).

Phenotypic validation: the missing layer

A key strength of genomic offset is its ability to generate predictions without long‐term field trials. However, without empirical validation, these predictions remain speculative, particularly for long‐lived species. Because offset estimates are intended to approximate maladaptation, they should be tested against fitness‐related traits measured across environments.

Common garden orchards offer a robust experimental framework for this purpose. By growing genotypes under replicated conditions across climatic gradients, it becomes possible to test how well offset estimates correlate with key traits such as survival, growth, flowering time, or fruit yield (Box 3). Where long‐term field trials are not feasible, early‐stage phenotyping under controlled conditions (e.g. nursery or glasshouse) can provide faster, albeit partial, insights into adaptive divergence (Bradley St Clair & Howe, 2007). The main bottleneck is now less the generation of genomic data than the acquisition of robust, standardized phenotypic data for the same genotypes across multiple environments. Coupling genomic offset with trait‐based performance will therefore be essential for improving predictive accuracy and making genomic forecasts useful for breeding and conservation.

Challenges and solutions for establishing common garden orchards in fruit trees

Long‐term, replicated common garden orchards are essential for quantifying adaptive variation, validating genomic offset predictions, and assessing climate resilience in both cultivated and wild fruit trees. However, their implementation across multiple sites presents substantial logistical, legal, and scientific challenges (Fig. 3; Table S4).

Legal and logistical constraints

Establishing international networks requires compliance with regulations such as the Rio Convention, the Nagoya Protocol, and the International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture (FAO, 2001; United Nations, 1992; 2010). Securing Material Transfer Agreements (MTAs) and Data Transfer Agreements (DTAs) is essential to ensure traceability and compliance. Although standardized legal frameworks exist (Smith et al., 2017; Cowell et al., 2022; Duval et al., 2022), their implementation often requires dedicated expertise.

Experimental design and biological complexity

Robust experimental design must account for management practices, propagation methods, and rootstock effects. In cultivated systems, grafting onto specialized rootstocks can strongly influence growth, yield, and stress tolerance (Saucet et al., 2016; Marini & Fazio, 2018; Savoi et al., 2020; Plavcová et al., 2022). Orchard management practices, including pruning, irrigation, and training systems, further shape tree architecture and microclimate, affecting phenotypic outcomes (Anthony et al., 2020; Campbell & Kalcsits, 2024; Pereira et al., 2024; Sideman et al., 2025).

Comparability between wild and cultivated material

Seed‐derived offspring of cultivated trees are not genetically identical to parental cultivars, complicating comparisons with wild populations. Experimental designs should therefore combine grafted cultivars with seed‐propagated wild individuals, ideally including grafted wild references as controls. Early‐stage protocols (e.g. glasshouse pregrowth) must also be standardized to avoid bias in phenotypic assessments.

Data standardization and integration

Harmonized phenotyping protocols are critical for cross‐site comparability. Adoption of FAIR data principles (Wilkinson et al., 2016; Arend et al., 2022) and standardized frameworks such as MIAPPE (Papoutsoglou et al., 2020) facilitates data reuse and integration. Relational databases further support the storage and harmonization of large, multi‐site datasets, enabling reproducible analyses and robust validation of genomic predictions.

Genomic offset models capture only part of the genetic basis of climate adaptation

Genomic offset analyses are fundamentally derived from GEA models, which estimate relationships between allele frequencies and environmental gradients and use these fitted relationships to predict genomic mismatch under future climates. In this framework, genomic offset should not be viewed as independent from GEA, but rather as a class of predictions derived from GEA models (Gain et al., 2023). Different implementations correspond to different ways of quantifying predicted genomic change or maladaptation from the fitted genotype–environment relationships. These predictions may rely on different statistical formulations, including geometric, regression‐based, machine learning, or risk‐based approaches, which partly explains the diversity of genomic offset metrics currently used in the literature (Gain et al., 2023).

However, in species where adaptation is highly polygenic – as suggested in forest trees and potentially in fruit trees – some GEA implementations may have limited power to capture diffuse adaptive responses distributed across many loci. Genomic offset may also overlook adaptive processes that do not generate consistent allele‐frequency shifts, including phenotypic plasticity, genotype‐by‐environment interactions, and epigenetically mediated responses. Plasticity, which may arise from both genetic and epigenetic processes, can itself be adaptive, yet remains rarely incorporated into current genomic offset frameworks (Box 1). Epigenetic mechanisms, including environmentally induced changes in DNA methylation, may reflect or mediate responses to environmental stress (Kelly, 2019), but are rarely incorporated into genomic offset models (Dodd & Douhovnikoff, 2016; Anastasiadi et al., 2021; Rodríguez‐Calcerrada et al., 2024). Clarifying the stability, reversibility, and inheritance of such epigenetic variation is essential for disentangling the relative contributions of genetic adaptation, phenotypic plasticity, and epigenetically mediated responses to climate resilience in fruit trees. Recent developments offer promising directions: Incorporating genetic load can improve forecasts (Jiang et al., 2025), flexible statistical models may better capture polygenic adaptation (Matz & Black, 2024), and GEA frameworks can now be extended to epigenetic data, such as DNA methylation profiles (Caye et al., 2019). These strategies remain largely unexplored in fruit trees but could help disentangle the relative contributions of genetic adaptation, plasticity, and epigenetically mediated responses.

Finally, genomic offset models rely on biological assumptions that require explicit testing. A central assumption is that contemporary environments are informative proxies for past selective pressures. Importantly, genomic offset predictions do not strictly require populations to be at demographic or migration–selection equilibrium. Relative differences among populations can remain informative under non‐equilibrium conditions, provided that stochastic components such as demographic variance are not strongly spatially structured. However, in species shaped by complex demographic histories, past climatic shifts, or introgression, present‐day genetic composition may reflect legacy adaptation rather than current conditions (Chen et al., 2023). Testing multiple climatic baselines, such as the Last Glacial Maximum, the Holocene, and present‐day climates, could help distinguish legacy adaptation from contemporary selection. A second assumption is that present genotype–environment relationships will remain stable under future climates. Yet novel selective pressures, including emerging pathogens and extreme climatic events, may alter these relationships (Singh et al., 2023). Expanding validation beyond phenology to include disease susceptibility, drought tolerance, and reproductive performance will therefore be critical for robust genomic forecasting.

Conclusion: unlocking the predictive power of genomic offset for climate‐resilient fruit trees

Fruit trees combine a distinctive set of biological and agronomic features – including long generation times, vegetative maintenance, grafting, and intensive management – that shape their responses to rapid climate change. These characteristics, together with complex domestication histories, crop–wild gene flow, and the partial decoupling of genotype–environment relationships in cultivated systems, create major challenges for predicting adaptation and maladaptation in perennial crops. Although genomic offset approaches provide a promising framework to estimate genomic mismatch under future climates, their application to fruit trees remains in its infancy.

Several key challenges must now be addressed, including robust phenotypic validation, improved sampling strategies integrating both cultivated and wild diversity, more ecologically realistic climate predictors, and a better understanding of the genetic and epigenetic architecture of adaptation. Together with the priorities outlined in Box 4, these challenges define a broader research agenda for advancing genomic forecasting in perennial systems.

Despite these limitations, rapid methodological progress is expanding the scope of predictive approaches. Advances in environmental modeling, multi‐omics, machine learning, and landscape genomics, combined with coordinated networks of common garden orchards, now provide unprecedented opportunities to test genomic predictions under realistic environmental conditions. Complementary approaches such as transfer functions and reaction norms – widely used in forestry and quantitative genetics – could further strengthen genomic forecasting by linking predicted genomic mismatch with realized phenotypic performance across climatic gradients (Rehfeldt et al., 1999; Broadhurst et al., 2023). More broadly, these challenges parallel those encountered in genomic selection and genotype‐by‐environment prediction, underscoring the need for tighter integration between predictive genomic approaches and phenotypic data across environmental gradients (Juliana et al., 2018; Papin et al., 2024).

Looking ahead, one of the most promising directions will be the integration of genomic offset with mechanistic, ecophysiological, and demographic models. By explicitly linking genomic variation to plant function – through traits such as phenology, water‐use efficiency, stress tolerance, or reproductive success – these approaches may help bridge the gap between statistical predictions and biological processes. This integration is particularly relevant for fruit trees, whose responses are strongly influenced by climatic extremes, long life cycles, and human management.

More broadly, fruit trees represent an exceptional yet underused system for developing and testing predictive frameworks of climate adaptation in long‐lived organisms. Their extensive genomic resources, replicated common garden networks, documented breeding histories, and strong economic importance offer unique opportunities to connect evolutionary theory with applied breeding and conservation. In this context, genomic offset should not be viewed as a stand‐alone predictor but rather as one component of an integrated framework that combines genomic, phenotypic, climatic, and experimental data. When coupled with empirical validation and process‐based understanding, genomic forecasting could evolve from a largely conceptual approach into an operational tool for breeding, conservation, and climate adaptation in perennial crops.
