Necrotizing enterocolitis in a patient with Microvillous Inclusion Disease: A case report

Introduction

Microvillous Inclusion Disease (MVID) is a rare congenital disorder within Congenital Diarrheas and Enteropathies (CODE), characterized by intractable diarrhea in early infancy [1], typically requiring long-term parenteral nutrition (PN) support. Electron microscopy reveals villous atrophy and subapical cytoplasmic inclusions of brush border microvilli [2]. MVID is caused by mutations in the MYO5B gene, defects in STX3 and STXBP2 have also been linked [3].

Myosin Vb, an actin-based motor protein, regulates vesicle trafficking through the plasma membrane recycling system in polarized cells by interacting with Rab11a proteins [4], [5]. Mutations in Myosin Vb disrupt brush border protein trafficking, leading to the loss of epithelial cell polarity [3], [6].

This case report was approved by the hospital ethics committee. Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient’s parents.

Case report

We present a case of a 5-month-old male with MVID. The patient was born prematurely at 32 weeks of gestation via cesarean section, with an Apgar score of 10, 10, and a birth weight of 2460 g. During the first days of life, he developed protracted diarrhea with streaks of mucus, worsening as breast milk feedings increased to 20 ml per feed (60 ml/kg/day). Initial labs revealed metabolic acidosis with normal lactate, an abdominal X-ray showed thick content in the colon with numerous small air bubbles. The patient was treated conservatively for suspected necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) with intravenous (IV) antibiotics for a week and kept nil per os (NPO) with total PN support for 10 days, gradually improving.

However, his condition deteriorated again with the reintroduction of enteral feeds, leading to metabolic acidosis, acute kidney injury and weight loss, necessitating the PN re-initiation. He was then transferred to our tertiary care center. Since birth, the patient exhibited low serum gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) cholestasis, with direct bilirubin levels rising from 0.71 to a peak of 10.12 mg/dL.

Genetic testing confirmed a compound heterozygous mutation in MYO5B. The patient remained fully PN dependent, receiving only trophic enteral feeds of breast milk, yet he suffered from daily diarrhea with losses of approximately 100 ml/kg.

At 5 months, the patient switched to polymeric infant formula at the family's request, worsening his diarrhea to 20 episodes daily. Amino acid-based formula was introduced, but his clinical condition deteriorated, presenting with dehydration and mild metabolic acidosis (pH 7.3, bicarbonate 20).

IV fluid supplementation was initiated alongside his PN and amino acid-based formula, but the patient’s clinical condition failed to improve. He remained clinically dehydrated despite adequate fluid replacement. Blood cultures taken at admission were negative. Initially acholic, his stools had turned dark with a reddish hue, and his abdomen became increasingly tender. An abdominal ultrasound revealed pneumatosis intestinalis, gas in the portal system, and significant ascites. The pneumatosis intestinalis was confirmed by an abdominal X-ray (Fig. 1). NEC was diagnosed using Vermont Oxford Network (VON) criteria [7].

(A) Abdominal X-ray displaying pneumatosis intestinalis in the right abdomen (arrow). (B) Transverse ultrasound of the liver showing multiple punctate hyperechoic foci indicative of portal venous gas (arrows), which was not clearly visible on the X-ray. (C) Transverse ultrasound of the bowel with color Doppler, highlighting air in the bowel wall consistent with pneumatosis intestinalis (arrow).

The patient was once again treated for NEC conservatively with IV antibiotics, NPO, abdominal decompression, and fluid management, showing clinical and radiologic improvement. He remained afebrile, with negative stool bacterial PCR. Amino acid-based feeds were reintroduced afterward, with no signs of abdominal pain or recurrence of bloody stools. While stools returned to their baseline mucous texture, volume remained high (121 ml/kg/day). Transitioning to donor breast milk significantly reduced stool output (90 ml/kg/day).

Discussion

Patients with MYO5B mutations have been reported to experience complications beyond those related to their PN dependence, with cholestasis being the most common [2]. Pulmonary complications have also been observed in MVID, including bronchopulmonary dysplasia, severe neonatal respiratory distress [8], and interstitial lung disease complicated by P. Jirovecii infection [9]. Additionally, renal Fanconi syndrome has been reported in two patients with MVID [5].

NEC is not typically associated with MVID, with only two reported cases of NEC occurring in MVID patients [10], [11]. Sayar et al. described a premature infant with MVID who developed NEC on the 7th day of life [10]. A patient with MVID reported by Ruemmele et al. experienced four NEC episodes at 2 days, 4, 7, and 11 weeks of age, each presenting with fever, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, elevated CRP, and intestinal pneumatosis. All episodes were managed conservatively with a two-week IV antibiotic course and NPO, with no surgical intervention required [11]. Although our patient received NEC treatment in the early neonatal period, imaging did not confirm the diagnosis. Ruemmele et al. also noted isolated episodes of bloody diarrhea without pneumatosis [11]. Similarly, our patient had a bloody stool episode at 8 months, accompanied by mild restlessness, but no elevated inflammatory markers or imaging abnormalities.

Some key risk factors for NEC in preterm infants include low flow and poor tissue perfusion due to perinatal complications, such as placental abruption, leading to ischemic events [12], [13]. Ischemic intestinal necrosis may also occur in cases of congenital heart disease, where the underlying pathophysiology similarly relates to impaired blood flow [14]. In MVID, severe and often life-threatening diarrhea can lead to hypovolemia, which in turn may cause temporary ischemia [1]. Intestinal ischemia may also trigger enterocolitis.

The enteral feeds in our patient were switched from breast milk to standard formula, which is another potential risk factor for NEC [12], [13], though its exact role in NEC pathogenesis remains unclear. Feeding increases intestinal oxygen consumption [15]. A recent study in mice showed that early postnatal intestinal circulation responds poorly to feeding, with less pronounced postprandial intestinal hyperemia [16]. Younger pups, with their immature microvasculature, exhibited poorer responses to formula feeding, resulting in intestinal hypoxia. The authors suggested that feeding in preterm infants can cause critical imbalance between oxygen supply and demand, leading to intestinal hypoxia and NEC.

Although our patient was 5 months old at the time of the NEC episode, with a presumably mature gut, the same study indicated that hyperosmolar formula (but not breast milk) induced intestinal mucosal hypoxia in experimental NEC. It's possible that the shift from breast milk to formula triggered ischemia in our patient's already compromised bowel.

Interestingly, after reintroducing oral feeds, our patient tolerated the amino acid-based formula better than polymeric formula, despite their similar osmolality (360 vs. 370 mOsm/kg, respectively). Stamm et al. demonstrated that intact protein formula was associated with eosinophilic intestinal inflammation in patients with pediatric intestinal failure (PIF) [17], although only one of 105 biopsied cases involved MVID. There is also evidence that intestinal failure patients tend to react negatively to cow’s milk-based formulas [18].

Regarding the association between NEC and cow’s milk protein allergy (CMPA), Narumoto et al. described a 10-month-old patient who developed NEC following a liver transplant, attributing it to CMPA [19]. Symptoms appeared immediately after the introduction of cow’s milk, and eosinophil infiltration was found in the resected bowel. Diarrhea reoccurred after cow's milk was reintroduced a month later. Intact protein formulas may increase intestinal oxygen consumption more than hydrolyzed formulas, potentially lowering the threshold for ischemia. Additionally, high lactose content in both bovine milk and intact protein formulas can exacerbate diarrhea due to osmotic effect, especially in a bowel that may have suffered from transient ischemia during major surgery. In the two reported cases of NEC in patients with MVID, Ruemmele et al. noted that the infant was fed breast milk prior to the first NEC episode, no additional nutritional details were provided [11]. Sayar's report did not address nutritional factors [10].

Food protein-induced enterocolitis syndrome (FPIES) can sometimes mimic NEC, presenting with symptoms like abdominal distension, bloody stools and flank discoloration [14], and could be considered in the differential diagnosis of our patient. However, lack of improvement with the hydrolyzed formula, makes FPIES a less likely cause.

NEC predominantly affects preterm infants, whose susceptibility to intestinal injury is heightened by underdeveloped gastrointestinal defense mechanisms. These include reduced gastric acid, digestive enzymes, peristalsis, polymeric immunoglobulin A (IgA), and mucus production [12]. In patients with MVID, small intestinal biopsies have revealed fewer goblet cells and reduced mucus secretion [20], [21]. While these findings come from case reports, it is plausible that MVID patients may experience damage to the protective mucus layer due to abnormal goblet cell secretion.

The benefits of feeding in MVID remain unclear, as oral intake often worsens symptoms and increases diarrhea volume. Most patients cannot tolerate enteral feeding, and evidence on this topic is limited. Ruemmele et al. and Goulet et al. reported that adequate oral caloric intake is nearly impossible due to severe intestinal malabsorption [1], [22]. Avitzur et al. concluded that nutritional management relies primarily on exclusive PN and IV fluids [23].

Despite these recommendations, many caregivers find it emotionally challenging to withhold food from their child. Eating is more than a necessity - it holds cultural, social, and emotional significance. In select cases, allowing minimal oral intake may support social interaction, and enhance emotional well-being for both child and parents.

In the case of our patient, several factors may have contributed to the development of NEC. We hypothesize that intestinal ischemia, triggered by diarrhea following the transition from breast milk to polymeric formula, played a significant role. Other contributing factors, such as damage to the mucus layer, may have further exacerbated the condition. In conclusion, there should be a high index of suspicion for NEC in patients with MVID, given their susceptibility to hypoperfusion and compromised mucosal integrity.
