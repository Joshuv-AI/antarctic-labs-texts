Engineered TIMP2 with narrow MMP-9 specificity is an effective inhibitor of invasion and proliferation of triple-negative breast cancer cells
Authors: Naama Rotenberg, Mark Feldman, Jason Shirian, Alexandra Hockla, Evette S Radisky, Julia M Shifman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMatrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are a family of endopeptidases that degrade extracellular matrix proteins, functioning in various physiological processes such as tissue remodeling, embryogenesis, and morphogenesis. Dysregulation of these enzymes is linked to multiple diseases. Specific inhibition of particular MMPs is crucial for anti-MMP drug development as some MMPs have shown antidisease properties. In this study, we aimed to design a highly specific inhibitor of MMP-9, that plays a crucial role in cell invasion and metastasis, using tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 2 (TIMP2s), an endogenous broad-family MMP inhibitor, as a prototype. In our earlier work, we were able to narrow down the specificity of the N-terminal domain of TIMP2 (N-TIMP2) toward MMP-9, yet at the expense of lowering its affinity to MMP-9. In this study, a library of N-TIMP2 mutants based on previous design with randomized additional positions was sorted for binding to MMP-9 using yeast surface display. Two selected N-TIMP2 mutants were expressed, purified, and their inhibitory activity against a panel of MMPs was measured. The best engineered N-TIMP2 mutant (REY) exhibited a 2-fold higher affinity to MMP-9 than that of the WT N-TIMP2, and 6- to 1.1 x 104-fold increase in binding specificity toward MMP-9 compared to five alternative MMPs. Moreover, REY demonstrated a significant increase in inhibition of cell invasion and proliferation compared to the WT N-TIMP2 in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells. Therefore, our engineered N-TIMP2 mutant emerges as a promising candidate for future therapeutic development, offering precise targeting of MMP-9 in MMP-9–driven diseases.Keywords: MMP inhibitor, TIMP2, protein engineering, therapeutic protein, protein–protein interactions

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are a family of zinc-dependent, calcium-containing, endopeptidases that degrade extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins as well as regulate the activity of other proteinases, growth factors, cytokines, chemokines, and cell receptors (1). In humans, the MMP family comprises 23 different enzymes. MMPs are commonly classified based on their structure and substrate preferences into five subgroups: collagenases, gelatinases, matrilysins, stromelysins, and membrane-type MMPs. Generally, MMPs are multidomain proteins with similar, yet not identical structures. All MMPs are expressed with a prodomain, which keeps the MMP inactive until it is cleaved by another protease. Additionally, all MMPs share a highly conserved catalytic domain with a zinc ion in the active site. Additional domains are present only in some MMPs, including a transmembrane domain in membrane-type MMPs, a hemopexin-like domain in some MMPs, and a fibronectin domain in gelatinases; these domains are important for substrate recognition (2, 3).

MMPs mediate various functions of cell signaling and tissue homeostasis and are important for tissue remodeling, wound healing, embryogenesis, morphogenesis, and other physiological processes. However, as MMPs are capable of degrading ECM proteins, their synthesis and activity are kept under tight control. Dysregulation of MMPs contributes to several pathological conditions including tissue destruction, fibrosis, cancer progression, and metastasis (4, 5). In cancer, MMP overexpression is well documented in various cancer types and high levels of some MMPs are correlated with tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis (6, 7, 8). While many MMPs exhibit cancer-promoting effects, including, for example, MMP-9 and MMP-14, it has become apparent that some MMPs such as MMP-8 (8) possess antitumorigenic effect.

In the past years, many MMP inhibitors (MMPIs) have been developed, yet despite promising preclinical data, most MMPIs failed clinical trials, and to date there is only one Food and Drug Administration-approved MMPI, Periostat, for periodontal disease treatment (9). The failure of the MMPIs in clinical trials owing to several reasons, one of which is serious side effects due to unselective inhibition. Initial strategies for MMPI design focused on targeting the catalytic zinc, which is necessary for MMP activity by small molecules with strong Zn+2 chelating properties. The poor selectivity of these broad-spectrum inhibitors resulted in serious side effects such as musculoskeletal syndrome and gastrointestinal disorders that led to discontinuing and failing clinical trials (9, 10). Therefore, it is now clear that MMP-specific inhibition is crucial for MMPI development. Moreover, for anticancer drug design, specific inhibition of a particular MMP family member is crucial due to different effects of each MMP in cancer.

The high sequence and structural homology within the MMP catalytic domain make the development of selective inhibitors extremely challenging. Nevertheless, recently several strategies were implemented to overcome this difficulty. One strategy involves the development of specific inhibitory anti-MMP antibodies. In this strategy, the antibody is designed to have an epitope that is specific to a single MMP isoform and thereby, bind and inhibit a particular MMP (11, 12). Another strategy for MMPI design is based on engineering of tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs). TIMPs are natural endogenous metalloproteinases inhibitors of the MMP family and are tight regulators of ECM remodeling. The TIMP family consists of four members (TIMP1-TIMP4) that inhibit MMPs in 1:1 stoichiometry by binding to their active site and the neighboring areas utilizing the TIMP N-terminal residues and two adjacent loops. TIMPs are made up of two domains, an N-terminal domain of 126 residues and a smaller C-terminal domain. The N-terminal domain folds if produced independently and is capable of inhibiting MMPs with an inhibitory constant (Ki) in the lower nanomolar range (13, 14). All TIMPs bind to MMPs nonspecifically, inhibiting most family members with similar inhibitory constants. Recently, TIMP2 was shown to exert an inhibitory effect on tumor growth and metastasis in animal models when administered exogenously (15). Several groups including ours utilized TIMPs to engineer MMPI with enhanced binding specificity to a particular MMP, including MMP-14, MMP-3, and MMP-9 (16, 17). In particular, our group used a combination of computational design and yeast surface display (YSD) with negative selection to design two N-TIMP2 variants, 9D_2 and 9D_6, that showed up to 2000-fold enhanced selectivity toward MMP-9 compared to a panel of alternative MMPs. Yet, affinity of these engineered N-TIMP2 mutants to MMP-9 was similar to or slightly worse than that of WT N-TIMP2 (18).

In this study, we aimed to further optimize our previously engineered N-TIMP2 mutant to achieve higher affinity and specificity for MMP-9. For this purpose, we constructed a combinatorial library based on the 9D_2 mutant, with three additional N-TIMP2 positions randomized to all 20 amino acids, and then sorted the yeast-displayed library to select variants that possess high affinity to MMP-9. Two engineered mutants, with eight mutations with respect to WT N-TIMP2, were expressed and purified and evaluated for their inhibitory activity against MMP-9 and five alternative MMPs. The two engineered N-TIMP2 mutants exhibited a 2-fold improvement in affinity to MMP-9 compared to that of WT N-TIMP2 and between 6-fold and 1.1 × 104-fold enhanced binding specificity toward MMP-9 relative to five alternative MMPs, demonstrating superior results compared to the previously engineered mutants. Moreover, cellular assays done with triple negative breast cancer MDA-MB-231 cells demonstrated that treatment with nanomolar concentrations of our best mutant results in >90% inhibition of cell invasion and cell proliferation, however does not cause cytotoxicity. Therefore, our N-TIMP2 variant presents an attractive candidate for therapeutic development in various MMP-9–driven diseases.

Results

N-TIMP2 library design and construction

In a previous study, we employed a combination of computational and in vitro evolution approaches to engineer N-TIMP2 variants with enhanced binding specificity toward individual MMPs, MMP-9 and MMP-14 (18). One resulting N-TIMP2 mutant, 9D-2, with five mutations, exhibited up to 1000-fold higher affinity to MMP-9 relative to MMP-14. However, its affinity to the target MMP, MMP-9, was not increased relative to that of WT N-TIMP2. In the present study, we aimed to further improve MMP-9 affinity and specificity of the 9D-2 mutant to MMP-9 by randomizing three additional positions on the N-TIMP2 surface that have not been previously randomized. We chose positions 14, 69, and 70 for randomization since these positions have shown high potential for affinity improvement for MMP-9 by computational saturation mutagenesis (19, 20). Randomization of these three positions to all twenty amino acids produced a small combinatorial library of 8 × 103 N-TIMP2 variants, all containing the five mutations of the 9D-2 mutant (Fig. 1). The N-TIMP2 library was constructed and subcloned into the pCHA plasmid compatible with the YSD technology. In this construct, the N-TIMP2 gene was followed by a Myc tag, while the N terminus of the protein was kept free to allow for interaction with MMP-9 (Fig. 2A).

Selection of N-TIMP2 mutants using YSD

The N-TIMP2 library was transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae EBY100 yeast cells and sorted for binding to MMP-9 using fluorescently activated cell sorting (FACS). During FACS experiments, an inactive MMP-9E402Q variant was used to avoid enzyme autocleavage that occurs rapidly in active MMP-9 at concentrations used in the experiment (21). Protein expression of N-TIMP2 variants was measured using a phycoerythrin-conjugated Myc tag antibody, while MMP-9E402Q binding to N-TIMP2 variants was measured by monitoring fluorescence of Dylight-488 fluorophore conjugated to MMP-9E402Q (Fig. 2A).

An initial presorting round S0 was performed to collect the top 35% N-TIMP2 variant–expressing cells, followed by three additional rounds of sorting (Fig. 2B). In each round, the concentration of MMP-9E402Q was decreased relative to the previous round to select high-affinity binders. After three rounds of selection, 15 individual clones were sequenced and analyzed for amino acid preferences at the three randomized positions (Fig. 2C and Table S1). At position 14, arginine, lysine, or asparagine occurred, with arginine being the most favored. At position 69, serine, glutamic acid, or threonine were observed with the WT serine being the most favored. At position 70, tryptophan, alanine, phenylalanine, and tyrosine were observed, with tryptophan being the most favored. Out of all sequenced N-TIMP2 variants, two mutants, RTW and REY, were chosen for further characterization since these mutants contained all the mutations that were observed in other selected variants.

N-TIMP2 variant expression, purification, and characterization

WT N-TIMP2, 9D-2, and the two engineered mutants, REY and RTW, were expressed in Pichia pastoris as described previously (18) and purified first by Ni-affinity chromatography and then by size-exclusion chromatography to collect the monomeric N-TIMP2 variant species (Figs. S1, S2 and Table S2).

Next, we determined the ability of the three N-TIMP2 variants to inhibit MMP-9 using an enzyme activity assay that measures the cleavage of a fluorogenic peptide substrate, resulting in fluorescence at 395 nm (22). The fraction of active MMP-9 was measured in the presence of various concentrations of the N-TIMP2 variants and the data were fit to Equation 1 to determine the apparent MMP-9 inhibitory activity (Kiapp) for each N-TIMP2 variant (Fig. 3). Our data revealed that WT N-TIMP2 and the 9D-2 mutant possess very similar inhibitory activity against MMP-9 with Kiapp values of 0.34 ± 0.05 nM and 0.31 ± 0.03 nM, respectively (Fig. 3 and Table 1). In contrast, the two engineered variants (REY and RTW) demonstrated an about 2- to 3-fold enhancement in Kiapp (0.10 ± 0.01 nM and 0.15 ± 0.02 nM for RTW and REY, respectively).

To determine the binding specificity of our N-TIMP2 mutants to MMP-9, we repeated the enzymatic inhibition assay using five alternative MMPs that represent several MMP subgroups: collagenases (MMP-1 and MMP-8), stromelysins (MMP-3 and MMP-10), and membrane-bound MMPs (MMP-14) (Fig. 4 and Table 1). Our data reveal that unlike WT N-TIMP2, the two engineered N-TIMP2 mutants possess very high discrimination against MMP-14, MMP-10, and MMP-3. Moderate (6- to 7-fold) discrimination was found against MMP-8, while a trend suggesting minimal discrimination was shown against MMP-1. Binding specificity of the two engineered mutants was equal or improved compared to that shown by the previously reported 9D-2 mutant, emphasizing the capability of mutations at positions 14, 69, and 70 to tailor interactions with MMPs. Overall, our results demonstrate that the two engineered N-TIMP2 mutants are more potent and specific toward MMP-9 compared to WT N-TIMP2 and the previously engineered 9D-2 mutant.

Evaluating N-TIMP2 mutant activity in cellar assays

Among human MMPs, MMP-9 is known to be a predominant contributor to tumor growth (23), cell migration, and invasion (24, 25). In breast cancer, we previously found that MMP-9 is most highly expressed in the aggressive and poor prognosis basal-like triple negative subtype, and we demonstrated that MMP-9 expression by triple-negative breast cancer cells is crucial for their invasion in culture models and metastasis in mice (26). We hence next set to evaluate the inhibitory activity of our engineered N-TIMP2 mutants on several procancer cellular processes, using MDA-MB-231 cells as an accepted model of human basal-like triple-negative breast cancer (27, 28). It is common for inhibition of cellular phenotypes to require substantially higher N-TIMP2 concentrations than the Kis measured with purified enzymes, and we anticipated that biological effects on MDA-MB-231 cells might require mid to high nanomolar concentrations of N-TIMP2 variants based on our previous work (29). Therefore, for these assays, we proceeded with only one engineered mutant, REY, since the second mutant, RTW, showed some predisposition to aggregation at concentrations higher than 100 nM.

First, we evaluated the inhibitory effect of WT N-TIMP2 and REY on the ability of cancer cells to survive and proliferate. Different concentrations of N-TIMP2 variants were added to MDA-MB-231 cells and then proliferation under each N-TIMP2 treatment was measured (Fig. 5). Our data show that both WT N-TIMP2 and REY inhibited cancer cell proliferation, with the inhibition effect increasing with the inhibitor concentration and reaching almost total inhibition at the highest tested concentration of 600 nM. However, REY demonstrated a more pronounced inhibitory effect compared to WT N-TIMP2, with the highly significant difference shown at the lowest, 100 nM concentration (with 88% and 47% inhibition for REY and WT N-TIMP2, respectively). These results demonstrate that treatment with low, 100 nM concentration of the REY mutant is sufficient for ∼90% inhibition of proliferation while higher concentrations are necessary to achieve the same level of inhibition for WT N-TIMP2.

MMP-9 degrades matrix proteins and thereby allows cancer cells to invade and migrate through the ECM, and we have previously found that MMP-9 activity is critical for invasion of triple-negative breast cancer cells (26). We hence evaluated the inhibitory effect of WT N-TIMP2 and REY mutant on MDA-MB-231 cell invasion using Matrigel transwell invasion assays that measure the ability of cancer cells to cross an artificial basement membrane (Fig. 6). The invasion assays were performed in the absence or presence of three different concentrations of the WT N-TIMP2 or the REY mutant (Fig. 6). Our data shows that both WT N-TIMP2 and REY inhibited cancer cell invasion even at the lowest tested concentration of 100 nM. The inhibition efficiency increased as more N-TIMP2 variant was added to the cells, for both the WT N-TIMP2 and the REY mutant. In all cases, REY produced significantly higher inhibition of cell invasion at all protein concentrations tested with the inhibition reaching almost 100% at highest tested concentration of 600 nM, proving that the REY mutant is an efficient inhibitor of cancer cell invasion.

We next evaluated whether addition of WT N-TIMP2 or REY affects cell viability using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. We performed this experiment using either the MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cell line or Vero cell line isolated from kidney epithelial monkey cells, as a model for normal cells (Figs. S4 and S5). Our data show that both WT N-TIMP2 and REY did not affect cell viability at concentrations of up to 600 nM in both cell lines, demonstrating that our N-TIMP2 variants do not cause cytotoxicity at the concentrations used in this study.

Discussion

In this study, we engineered two high-affinity and high-specificity N-TIMP2 inhibitors of MMP-9 that each contains eight mutations with respect to WT N-TIMP2 at MMP-9 binding interface positions. While five mutations were carried over from the previously reported mutant 9D-2 (S4P, I35V, A68N, V71I, and H97R), three additional positions (14, 69, and 70) were engineered in this study. The three mutations at these positions resulted in a 2-fold enhancement in affinity to MMP-9 and further enhancement in binding specificity toward MMP-9 relative to alternative MMPs.

To explain the improvement in affinity of the two engineered N-TIMP2 variants, we analyzed the contribution of each of the mutations at positions 14, 69, and 70 to the protein interaction with MMP-9. For this purpose, we introduced the selected mutations in N-TIMP2 using the Rosetta suite (https://rosettacommons.org/software/) (30) and analyzed each newly introduced interaction. The modeled structure predicts that arginine at position 14, present in both mutants, forms four new hydrogen bonds with MMP-9: two with the backbone of Lys184, one with the side chain of the Asp182, and one with the backbone of the same position (Fig. 7A). At position 70, A70W and A70Y mutations of the RTW and RWY variants, respectively, are predicted to improve packing and hydrophobic interactions with MMP-9. In addition, Trp70 introduces a new potential H-bond with the backbone of His230 on MMP-9, while Tyr70 may form a new hydrogen bond with a side chain of Asp235 (Fig. 7B). At position 69, serine of WT N-TIMP2 is predicted to form a hydrogen bond with the side chain of Asp235 and the same hydrogen bond is predicted for a threonine in the RTW mutant, while Glu69 of the REY mutant is predicted to form a hydrogen bond with the side chain of His236 (Fig. 7B). Thus, no new interactions with MMP-9 are introduced by mutations at the 69 position, yet these mutations might be beneficial for MMP-9 affinity on the background of mutations at position 70.

In addition to improved affinity for MMP-9, our two engineered mutants showed better selectivity toward MMP-9 relative to four out of five alternative MMPs (MMP-3, MMP-8, MMP-10, and MMP-14), than WT N-TIMP2 (Table 1). Moreover, selectivity was also improved compared to the previously reported 9D-2 mutant for almost all MMP targets. To understand the reason for improved selectivity, we modeled the interaction of the engineered mutants with five alternative MMPs by superimposing the crystal structure of each MMP for MMP-9 in our modeled structure of MMP-9 in complex with RTW and REY. Examination of the binding interfaces of the two engineered N-TIMP2 variants and six MMPs suggested that selectivity of the two N-TIMP2 mutants toward MMP-9 is likely to be due largely to the amino acid at position 70. At position 70, an Ala does not contact the MMP surface, presenting a cold-spot cavity (31). Mutations of an alanine to large aromatics, tryptophan in the RTW mutant or a tyrosine in the REY mutant, fills the cavity when the N-TIMP2 variant is interacting with MMP-9 (Fig. 8). However, the same mutations are predicted to generate steric clashes with MMP-3, MMP-10, and MMP-14, which might explain the significant decrease in affinities for these MMPs. By contrast, no steric hindrance was predicted with MMP-1 or MMP-8 upon these mutations in the two N-TIMP2 mutants. Moreover, for MMP-1, Ser235 instead of Asp235 in MMP-9 could form the same hydrogen bond with either Thr69 of the RTW mutant or Tyr70 of REY (Fig. 8). In MMP-8, position 235 is occupied by an alanine, which could not form a hydrogen bond with the N-TIMP2 variant, likely resulting in a slight specificity improvement toward MMP-9 relative to MMP-8 demonstrated by the two engineered proteins.

Finally, we were able to show that our engineered MMP-9–specific mutant (REY) inhibits procancer processes in the triple-negative breast cancer MDA-MB-231 cells, demonstrating the importance of MMP-9 inhibition for therapeutic strategies. In spite of the modest 2-fold increase in affinity to MMP-9, the REY mutant was a significantly more potent inhibitor as compared to WT N-TIMP2 at the lowest tested concentration of 100 nM in both invasion and proliferation assays while exhibiting no cytotoxicity. These results suggest that lower concentrations of our mutants may be sufficient for a strong inhibition of cancer progression, as our mutants are highly selective toward MMP-9 and hence bind mostly to MMP-9 while exhibiting small cross-reactivity with alternative MMPs.

Our N-TIMP2 variant has several distinct advantages over the potent specific anti-MMP-9 antibody andecaliximab that showed initial promise in treating ulcerative colitis and gastric cancer but later failed in clinical trials (32). Andecaliximab like most MMP-targeted antibodies, recognizes pro-MMP-9 as well as active MMP-9, and in fact binds 1000-fold more tightly to pro-MMP-9 (33) than to the intended target, active MMP-9. Because the majority of MMP-9 in the body is present as latent proMMP-9, there is a large sink to absorb the drug, requiring high doses. Second, andecaliximab is a noncompetitive inhibitor of MMP-9 that reduces its enzymatic activity by ∼50% but does not quench it completely (33). Our N-TIMP2 variants interact with only active MMP-9, exhibit 30-fold better Kiapp for active MMP-9 than andecaliximab and result in 100% inhibition of MMP-9. Hence, our N-TIMP2 variants present attractive candidates for further testing and therapeutic development in cancer research.

Experimental procedures

MMP-9E402Q expression, purification, and labeling

The gene for the human MMP-9 catalytic domain, residues 107 to 215 and 391 to 443 inside the pET28a vector (under Lac operon and with N-terminal 6× His-tag) was used as a template for mutagenesis. Forward and reverse primers were designed, where the reverse primer contained the E402Q mutation. The following primers were used:

Forward primer:

5′-CCAGACCTTTGAAGGCGACTTGAAGTGGCATCACCATAATATTACC-3’

Reverse primer (E402Q mutation in bold):

5′-CCAGTGCATGCCCAAACTGATGTGCGGCAACCAAAAAC-3’

The MMP-9 E402Q (MMP-9E402Q) mutant was generated using the transfer-PCR (TPCR) technique (34) with the above primers and using the WT MMP-9 gene as a template. TPCR products were transformed into Escherichia coli TOP10 cells, plasmids were purified using plasmid purification kit (Presto Mini Plasmid Kit, Cat. No. PDH100), sent for sequencing, and MMP-9E402Q sequence was confirmed.

MMP expression and purification

Both the active WT MMP-9 and inactive MMP-9E402Q proteins were expressed using the same protocol. The MMP-9 gene constructs were transformed into E. coli Bl21(DE3)pLysS for expression. Bl21(DE3)pLysS transformants were grown in LB medium with kanamycin (50 μg/ml) at 37 °C until an absorbance at 600 nm of 0.6 to 0.8 was reached. Then, MMP-9 expression was induced by 1 mM IPTG and cultures were grown overnight postinduction for 18 h. MMP-9 was overexpressed in inclusion bodies. One liter of induced Bl21(DE3)pLysS bacterial suspension was pelleted and cells were incubated with 30 ml lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.5–8.0, 0.5 M NaCl, 0.5% v/v Triton, lysozyme (100 μg/ml)), overnight at 37 oC. The lysed cells were further disrupted by four rounds of sonication (60% amplitude, 5 s on/off for 2 min) coupled with pelleting and resuspension of the pellet in 30 ml buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.5–8.0, 0.5 M NaCl, and 0.5% v/v Triton). In the fourth round, soluble denatured MMP-9 was extracted from the inclusion bodies by sonicating the pellet with denaturing buffer (8 M urea, 25 mM Tris pH 8). The denatured MMP-9 was incubated with 5 ml of pre-equilibrated nickel resin (INDIGO Ni-Agarose resin, Pure Cube, Cat. No. 75105), overnight at room temperature. Then, the protein was purified with affinity chromatography, where the washing step was done with 10 column volumes of buffer (8 M urea, 25 mM Tris pH 7.5–8.0, 30 mM NaCl, and 30 mM imidazole), and elution step with at least three column volumes of buffer (8 M urea, 25 mM Tris pH 7.5–8.0, 30 mM NaCl, and 200 mM imidazole). The eluted protein was diluted in the buffer (6 M urea, 25 mM Tris pH 7.5–8.0, and 30 mM NaCl) to a final concentration of 50 μg/ml and incubated with pre-equilibrated nickel resin, overnight at 4 oC. Then, the protein-bound resin was collected and transferred into 45 ml of the refolding buffer (50 mM Tris, 30 mM NaCl, 10 mM CaCl2, 20 μM ZnCl2 pH 8, 0.2% Zwittergent and 0.1% Brij). Protein was incubated in the refolding buffer overnight at 4 oC, then collected on a gravity column and eluted with elution buffer (50 mM Tris, 30 mM NaCl, 10 mM CaCl2, 20 μM ZnCl2 pH 8, 0.2% Zwittergent and 0.1% Brij, 200 mM imidazole). Finally, the refolded protein was purified using ion-exchange chromatography (AIEX) with HiTrapQ HP AIEX 1 ml column on AKTA Pure chromatography system, at 4 oC, using buffer A (20 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl, and 5 mM CaCl2, pH 8) and buffer B (20 mM Tris, 1 M NaCl, and 5 mM CaCl2, pH 8). MMP-9E402Q monomers were eluted in 35% buffer B (350 mM NaCl).

The catalytic domain of MMP-14 was expressed and purified as previously described (17, 35). Constructs containing the prodomain and catalytic domain of human MMP-1, MMP-3, and MMP-10 were expressed in E. coli, extracted from inclusion bodies, purified by ion-exchange chromatography under denaturing conditions, refolded, and activated as previously described (36, 37), to generate the active catalytic domains. A construct for expression of the catalytic domain only of human MMP-8 (residues 100–262) was generated by subcloning into the pET3a expression vector. Recombinant protein was expressed in E. coli strain BL21(DE3) grown in LB medium at 37 oC. Cultures were grown to an A600 of 0.4 to 0.5, induced by addition of 0.4 mM IPTG, and grown for four more hours before harvesting cell pellets and extracting inclusion bodies. Inclusion bodies were solubilized in 6 M urea, 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 100 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and recombinant MMP-8 was purified under denaturing conditions on a Q-sepharose column eluted with a linear gradient to 0.5 M NaCl. The purified protein was then refolded by dialysis versus 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2, and 0.5 mM ZnCl2, as previously described (38). Refolded active MMP-8 was subsequently clarified by centrifugation and further purified to homogeneity on a Superdex-75 column.

MMP-9E402Q labeling

First, MMP-9E402Q was dialyzed against buffer (0.05 M sodium borate, 150 mM NaCl pH 8.6) using a dialysis cassette with 10 k molecular weight cut-off (slide-A-lyzer dialysis cassette, cat. no. 66380). Then, the MMP-9E402Q was labeled with DyLight488 amine reactive dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat. no. 46402) according to manufacturer’s protocol and stored in aliquots at −80 oC.

N-TIMP2 library construction

An N-TIMP2 library was designed based on the N-TIMP2 9D-2 mutant that was obtained in a previous study by our lab (18). The library was constructed by randomizing three positions (14, 69, and 70) in the N-TIMP2 9D-2 gene. PCRs were performed with the following primers to create the library (mutated codons in bold):

Forward 1:

5′-CCGTCGGTTCCGCTGCAGAAGGCTCTTTGGACAAGAGATGCAGCTGCCCACCGGTG-3’

Forward 2:

5′-CCGGTGCACCCGCAACAGGCGTTTTGCNNKGCAGATGTAGTGATCAGGGCCAAAGCGGTC-3’

Reverse 1:

5′-GTCTTCTTCGGAGATAAGCTTTTGTTCTGCACGCGTGGATCCCTCGCAGCCCATCTGGTACC-3’

Reverse 2:

5′-CCTCCAACGTCCAGCGAGACCCCACAGATMNNMNNGTTGGGGGCCGTGTAGATAAACTCTATATCC-3’

The first PCR was performed with forward 1 and reverse 2 primers, while the second PCR was performed with forward 2 and reverse 1 primers. The conditions for both PCR reactions were as following: 30 cycles of denaturing at 95 oC for 30 s, annealing at 60 oC for 1 min and elongation at 72 oC for 1 min, and finally, incubation at 72 oC for 7 min. A sample from each PCR was run on a 1% agarose gel to indicate the desired products, and then 15 μl of both PCR1 and PCR2 products were mixed for a third PCR producing the full N-TIMP2 library. The third PCR reaction was performed with the same conditions as mentioned above, yet for 15 cycles.

Finally, pCHA plasmids were linearized with BamHI and NheI restriction enzymes, and then the products of the third PCR were transformed with the linearized plasmids to S. cerevisiae EBY100 yeast cells by electroporation. Transformants were randomly sampled by colony PCR to confirm the presence of the N-TIMP2 gene insert in the pCHA plasmid. The obtained clones were sequenced to validate that positions 14, 69, and 70 were mutated.

YSD and FACS analysis and sorting

WT N-TIMP2 gene and N-TIMP2 library were subcloned into the pCHA vector and were expressed in S. cerevisiae EBY100 yeast strain (39).

EBY100 yeasts containing the gene for N-TIMP2 (WT or library) were grown in SDCAA media (2% dextrose, 0.67% Difco yeast nitrogen base, 0.5% Bacto casamino acids, 0.54% Na2HPO4 and 0.86% NaH2PO4∗H2O), overnight at 30 oC, until an A600 of 7 to 8 was reached. Then, N-TIMP2 library expression was induced with galactose by growing a sample of the SDCAA-suspension in the inductive SGCAA media (2% galactose, 0.67% Difco yeast nitrogen base, 0.5% Bacto casamino acids, 0.54% Na2HPO4, and 0.86% NaH2PO4∗H2O), overnight at 30 oC, until an A600 of 3 to 4 was reached. After induction, cells were incubated with anti c-Myc antibody conjugated to phycoerythrin (anti c-Myc antibody. Santa Cruz cat. sc-40) for 45 to 60 min at 4 oC. Thereafter, labeled MMP-9E402Q (MMP-9E402Q-DyLight488) was added and the cells were incubated for another 60 min at 4 oC. Finally, cells were washed three times with buffer (50 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2, pH 8.0, 1% bovine serum albumin) and then analyzed by FACS.

Four sorting rounds were performed with our library (S0-S3) using the FACS sorter. The initial round (S0) was a presorting round, where high expressing cells were collected. In the following sorting rounds, cells were sorted for binding, where high binders were collected—in each round MMP-9E402Q concentration was decreased—and the collected cells continued to the next sorting round. The first round of sorting for binding (S1) was performed with 500 nM MMP-9E402Q-DyLight488, the second round (S2) was performed with 300 nM MMP-9E402Q-DyLight488, and the third and final round (S3) was performed with 150 nM MMP-9E402Q-DyLight488. The cells collected in the S3 round and named S3 library were then plated for sequencing.

Library sequencing and logo construction

S3 library was plated on SDCAA agar plates (2% dextrose, 0.67% Difco yeast nitrogen base, 0.5% Bacto casamino acids, 0.54% Na2HPO4, 0.86% NaH2PO4∗H2O, 18.2% sorbitol, and 1.5% agar) and grown for 3 days at 30 oC. Several different colonies were sampled by colony PCR using the following primers:

Forward: 5′- CCGGTTATTTCTACTACCGTCGGTTC-3’

Reverse: 5′- GTCAGTTCCTGCAAGTCTTCTTCG-3’

Colony PCR products were run on 1% agarose gel and 15 samples that indicated N-TIMP2 gene were sent for sequencing. Sequences were analyzed and amino acid sequence logo was created with WebLogo (https://weblogo.berkeley.edu/logo.cgi). N-TIMP2 mutants that we considered as representative of all the mutations obtained were selected for expression.

Protein expression and purification

pPICZαA plasmids containing our desired mutants were amplified and purified from TOP10 bacteria using a DNA extraction kit (Maxi plasmid DNA kit, Geneaid Cat. No. PM010). Then, plasmids were linearized with SacI restriction enzyme and transformed into P. pastoris X-33 yeast strain by electroporation. P. pastoris transformants were plated on YPDS agar plates (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 2% D-glucose, 1 M sorbitol, and 2% agar) with Zeocin (100 μg/ml) and grown for 3 days at 30 °C. Several colonies of each mutant were sampled by colony PCR using the following primers:

AOX1 forward primer: 5′-GGTATCTCTCGAGAAAAGATGCAGCTGCTCCCCG-3′

AOX1 reverse primer: 5′-GCTGGCGGCCGCCTCGCAGCCCATCTGGTA-3’

Colony PCR products were run on a 1% agarose gel and samples that indicated the N-TIMP2 gene were sent for sequencing.

P. pastoris clones containing our mutants or WT N-TIMP2 genes were grown in 50 ml Buffered Glycerol-complex medium (2% peptone, 1% yeast extract, 0.23% K2H(PO4), 1.18% KH2(PO4), 1.34% yeast nitrogen base, 4 × 10–5% biotin, and 1% glycerol) until an A600 of ten was reached. Then, the cultures were transferred into 500 ml inductive BMMY medium (2% peptone, 1% yeast extract, 0.23% K2H(PO4), 1.18% KH2 (PO4), 1.34% yeast nitrogen base, 4 × 10–5% biotin, and 0.5% methanol) and induction was continued for 72 h at 30 °C, with the addition of 0.5% methanol every 22 to 26 h. After 72 h of induction, cells were pelleted at 817g for 5 min, supernatant was collected, the pH of the solution was raised to 8, and the solution was incubated at 4 oC for 1 h. Then, imidazole was added to a final concentration of 10 mM, and the solution was filtered with 0.22 μm filter.

Nickel resin (1 ml; INDIGO Ni-Agarose, Pure Cube, Cat. No. 75105) was added to the solution, and the solution was incubated for 1 to 2 h at room temperature. Then, N-TIMP2 proteins were purified by affinity chromatography where the washing step was done with 10 column volumes of washing buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 300 mM NaCl, pH 7.5, and 10 mM imidazole), and the elution step was done with 10 column volumes of elution buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 300 mM NaCl, pH 7.5, and 250 mM imidazole). The N-TIMP2 elution fraction was concentrated to 1 ml or less, using a 3 kDa molecular weight cut-off centrifugal concentrator (Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter, Cat. No. UFC9003), and then purified with size-exclusion chromatography using a Superdex75, 120 ml column with buffer (50 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl pH 7.5). N-TIMP2 monomers were collected, and concentration was determined with the Bradford assay (Pierce Bradford Protein Assay Kit, Cat. No. 23200), using the manufacturer’s protocol.

Enzymatic inhibition assay and Kiapp determination

The apparent Ki of MMP inhibition by our N-TIMP2 mutants, as well as by the WT N-TIMP2 and 9D_2 mutant, were determined using an MMP enzymatic activity assay with decreasing concentration of the N-TIMP2 inhibitors. N-TIMP2 variants were incubated with either 0.1 nM MMP-9, 0.1 nM MMP-8, 0.2 nM MMP-14, 0.5 nM MMP-10, 4 nM MMP-3 or 0.5 nM MMP-1, for 1 h at 37 °C in buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 0.15 M NaCl, 10 mM CaCl2, and 0.02% Brij 35 pH 7.5). After incubation, 50 μl of fluorogenic MMP substrate MCA-Pro-Leu-Gly-Leu-Dpa-Ala-Arg-NH2·TFA (Sigma-Aldrich, MMP substrate, Cat. No. SCP0193) were added to the N-TIMP2:MMP solution in a final concentration of 7.5 μM. Then, fluorescence at 395 nm was immediately measured at 37 °C, at least every 30 s for at least 30 min, with irradiation at 325 nm, on a Biotek Synergy H1 plate reader (BioTek). Uninhibited enzyme activity (MMP without N-TIMP2) and basal fluorescence (buffer without N-TIMP2 or MMP) were performed as controls. At least three assays were performed for each N-TIMP2 protein.

Mean velocities (Vm) of the enzymatic cleavage of the fluorogenic substrate were derived from the fluorescence generated by this cleavage. Fractions of residual MMP activity were determined by dividing Vm by the uninhibited velocity (V0) and were plotted against the corresponding concentration of N-TIMP2. From this plot the apparent Ki (Kiapp) value is calculated based on the following equation (40) using MATLAB (https://www.mathworks.com/):

f = fraction of activity, Vm/V0.

Kiapp values are an average of the Kiapp values determined from a single inhibition assay for each N-TIMP-2 protein.

Binding specificity was determined according to the following formula:

Specificity = Kiapp of MMP-X/Kiapp of MMP-9.

Colony formation assay

Triple-negative breast cancer MDA-MB-231 cells were treated with different concentrations of either WT N-TIMP2 or REY N-TIMP2 mutant. Untreated cells were set as control. The cells were seeded on 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells and cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium supplemented with 10% of fetal bovine serum (FBS) for 3 days in standard conditions (37 oC and 5% CO2). The growing medium was replaced once after 48 h and WT N-TIMP2 or REY mutant were readded to the medium in the appropriate concentrations. After 3 days of incubation, the resulting colonies were fixed with 100% methanol for 20 min at −20 oC, stained with 0.4% crystal violet for 30 min at RT, and finally washed with double-distilled water to remove the excessive stain. Each sample was imaged using a Nikon microscope at a magnification of 10×, and the colonies of each sample were counted using ImageJ (https://imagej.net/ij/index.html).

Cell invasion assay

Cell invasion was assayed using Corning Matrigel invasion chamber 24-well plate.

Matrigel (CLS356234 Corning(R) Matrigel(R) Basement membrane) was diluted in cold PBS in 1:4 ratio. The upper compartment of the transwell chamber was coated with 100 μl Matrigel and incubated overnight at 37 °C until gelled. 2 × 105 of confluent TNBC MDA-MB-231 cells were resuspended in serum-free DMEM in the absence or presence of N-TIMP2 variants at different concentrations and then transferred into the upper chamber of each transwell. Lower chambers contained fresh DMEM supplemented with 20% of FBS. After culturing for 24 h, the cells at the upper surface of the membrane were removed using a swab, while the cells that migrated to the lower membrane surface were fixed with 100% cold methanol for 30 min and stained with 0.4% crystal violet for 1 h. The cells were imaged using a Nikon microscope at a magnification of 10×. Images were processed and cells were counted with ImageJ.

MTT assay

The N-TIMP2 variants were tested for in vitro cytotoxicity, using MDA-MB-231 (Breast cancer) and Vero (Normal, African green monkey kidney) cells by conventional MTT assay. Briefly, the above cell lines were incubated at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates for 24 h in 100 μl of DMEM with 10% FBS, at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Following seeding, the culture supernatant was removed and serum-free DMEM medium containing various concentrations of N-TIMP2 variants, REY and WT, were added and the cells were incubated for 24 h. Wells without N-TIMP2 variants served as controls. MTT dye (10 μl and 5 mg/ml) was added to each well and the plate was incubated for an additional 4 h. The medium together with MTT were aspirated off the wells, dimethylsulfoxide (100 μl) was added and the plates shaken for 5 min. The absorbance of each well was measured at 540 nm in a micro plate reader and the percentage of cell viability was calculated using the formula: Cell viability = average absorbance of six replicates containing N-TIMP2 variant/average absorbance of six replicates of control wells × 100%.

Modeling of N-TIMP2/MMP complexes

The crystal structure of WT N-TIMP2 in complex with MMP-14 (PDB 1BUV) and the crystal structure of MMP-9 catalytic domain (PDB 6ESM) were used to generate N-TIMP2/MMP-9 complex by the structural alignment of MMP-9 and MMP-14 and subsequent removal of MMP-14. The N-TIMP2/MMP-9 modeled structure was then minimized and the mutations corresponding to the mutants REY and RTW were introducing using the Rosetta flexbb_design protocol. The complexes of alternative MMPs with N-TIMP2 variants were modeled by the structural alignment and substitution of MMP-9 by alternative MMP in the N-TIMP2 variant/MMP-9 complex using the crystal structures of MMP-1 catalytic domain (PDB 3SHI), MMP-3 catalytic domain (PDB 4G9L), MMP-8 catalytic domain (PDB 3DPE), MMP-10 catalytic domain (PDB 1Q3A), and MMP-14 catalytic domain (PDB 1BUV).
