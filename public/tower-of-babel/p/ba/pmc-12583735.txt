Astragali Radix-Carthami Flos alleviate brain-heart injury after cerebral ischemia/reperfusion via regulating TSPO signaling
Authors: Qiang Zeng, Li Yu, Chang Li, Jianzhen Chen, Haitong Wan, Yu He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ischemic stroke is often accompanied by secondary cardiac dysfunction, yet its underlying pathogenesis remains under investigation. Astragali Radix-Carthami Flos (ARCF) is a drug pair with medicine food homology, commonly used for cerebrovascular and cardiovascular diseases. This study found that in the middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion rat model, ARCF management mitigated CI/R-induced brain-heart injury, as evidenced by improved neurological function, reduced cerebral and cardiac damage, restored serum biomarker levels, and enhanced overall cardiac function. Transcriptomic analysis, combined with network pharmacology and molecular docking, identified oxidative phosphorylation process and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production as key pathogenic mechanisms, with TSPO as a central target. Mechanistic studies revealed that the ARCF maintained mitochondrial homeostasis by inhibiting the TSPO/VDAC1/MCU pathway in the brain and the TSPO-VDAC1 axis in the heart. These findings highlight the potential of ARCF as a functional food-derived intervention for mitigating stroke-induced brain-heart injury, offering new insight into the food-based management of neurocardiac complications.

Introduction

In both health and disease states, the heart and brain are anatomically and functionally interconnected. Patients with heart disease often experience cognitive decline and cerebral microvascular damage, and the electrocardiographic changes, abnormal ventricular wall motion, reduced left ventricular blood flow and elevated serum cardiac enzymes occur in patients with stroke1. Cardiac diseases cause approximately 20–40% of all stroke2,3. At the same time, stroke also induce cardiovascular complications in up to 70% of patients4. Despite significant advancements in understanding brain-heart interactions following stroke5–7, the causative mechanisms are still under-investigated, and the identification of therapeutic targets remains challenging8. Further research is needed to explore the pathogenesis and therapeutic targets of stroke-related cardiac dysfunction.

Transcriptomics involves the analysis of the entire collection of RNA transcripts generated by the genome under particular conditions9. This field provides a comprehensive view of gene expression, enabling researchers to insight the molecular mechanisms underlying various diseases. One of the key advantages of transcriptomics is its ability to capture dynamic changes in gene activity, offering insights into disease progression and response to treatment10,11. By analyzing RNA expression patterns, scientists identify potential biomarkers, therapeutic targets, and the interplay between different biological pathways12. Moreover, transcriptomics is particularly valuable in studying complex diseases that involve multiple organs or systems, as it reveals the molecular crosstalk and regulatory networks that drive pathophysiology13. Therefore, transcriptomics has increasingly been used to investigate the molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets of multi-organ injuries in recent years. In a study of myocardial infarction, it was found through transcriptomics that mineralocorticoid receptor acts as a crucial hub in transmitting injury information between the liver and heart during myocardial infarction14. A potential crosstalk between the liver and extrahepatic organs, including the lungs, kidneys, and brain, was revealed in various acute liver injury models by analyzing transcriptome data15. Similarly, transcriptomics has been employed to preliminarily uncover the correlation between the brain and liver in patients with Parkinson’s disease16. The mechanisms of brain and heart injury after cerebral ischemia/reperfusion (CI/R) are sophisticated, and the overlapping phenotypic features and signaling pathways underlying the injury of both brain and heart after CI/R at the transcriptional level remain enigmatic. The analysis of transcriptome data related to brain and heart after CI/R offers the possibility to understand the complex mechanism of brain-heart injury following CI/R.

The prevention and treatment of CI/R induced brain-heart injury is a significant concern for the general public. It is essential to find and develop a safer and more accessible method based on the inhibition of cross-talk mediators and common pathological mechanisms for treating brain-heart injury after CI/R. Traditional Chinese medicines, particularly those with both medicinal and food properties, are intriguing contenders that are gaining increasing popularity because of their effectiveness in curing diseases with few side effects17. The dried root of Leguminosae plant Astragalus membranaceus (Fisch.) Bge. var. mongholicus (Bge.) Hsiao or A. membranaceus (Fisch.) Bge., known as Astragali Radix (AR), is recognized as both medicine and food in Medicine-Food Directory issued by National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China18. It exhibits various pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammation and antioxidation, and is widely used to boost the immune system, improve overall health and well-being19,20. Carthami Flos (CF) is the dried flowers of Compositae plant Carthamus tinctorius L., which is widely utilized in both food and medicine for its natural coloring, flavoring, and potential health benefits21,22. In food, it serves as a natural colorant, flavor enhancer, and functional ingredient in teas, soups, and health products. Medicinally, it is valued for its ability to promote blood circulation and protect cardiovascular system. As a classic herb pair with medicine food homology, Astragali Radix-Carthami Flos (ARCF) is the core of many Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) formulas for managing cardiovascular disease, such as Buyanghuanwu Decoction and Naoxintong capsule23,24. The excellent therapeutic effect of ARCF on CI/R injury is a consequence of its rich active ingredients, including calycosin, astragaloside IV, hydroxysafflor yellow A, and carthamin. These components have multiple benefits for the management of cerebrovascular and cardiovascular diseases, such as preventing oxidative stress, alleviating inflammation, inhibiting apoptosis and autophagy25. Nevertheless, the mechanisms of ARCF in alleviating brain and heart injury after CI/R are not yet fully understood. In the era of big data and artificial intelligence, leveraging innovative tools is crucial for addressing challenges that traditional methods cannot easily resolve. Network pharmacology is an integrative approach that encompasses pharmacology, information networks, computer science, and related fields26. With the advancement of computer technology and ongoing developments in systems biology, network pharmacology is gaining increasing recognition. Particularly, network pharmacology has been frequently utilized in TCM to research the TCM pharmacodynamic substance, and to reveal the multi-target mechanisms of active components27. As a computational technique, molecular docking is used to forecast the optimal orientation and binding affinity of a ligand to a target receptor. This technique helps to elucidate the molecular-level interactions between TCM active ingredients and disease target28. The integration of network pharmacology, molecular docking and experimental verification can comprehensively identify and interpret the possible mechanisms of brain and heart injury after CI/R and the key targets of ARCF inhibiting CI/R-induced brain-heart injury from multiple perspectives.

This research analyzed transcriptomic data of brain and heart after CI/R obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database to investigate the common potential therapeutic targets and mechanisms of brain-heart injury after CI/R. Subsequently, a comprehensive analysis, which incorporated network pharmacology and molecular docking methods, was conducted to reveal the biological function and core target of ARCF in alleviating brain-heart injury after CI/R. Finally, the efficacy, potential therapeutic targets and mechanisms of ARCF in alleviating CI/R-induced brain-heart damage were evaluated and validated using in vivo experiment. This study provides new perspectives into potential targets for the intervention of brain-heart injury after CI/R.

Results

Potential common targets identification of brain and heart injury after CI/R

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) is a powerful approach used to identify clusters of highly related genes, or gene modules, that share similar functions. By analyzing the co-expression patterns of thousands of genes, WGCNA allows researchers to explore complex gene networks and their associations with specific phenotypes, ultimately aiming to pinpoint potential hub genes. For GSE166162, the mean connectivity of similarity matrix improved upon reaching a threshold power of 14 (scale-free R2 = 0.85). 35 gene modules were recognized using average linkage hierarchical clustering (Fig. 1). Among these modules, the white module (r2 = 0.92, p = 0.009), brown module (r2 = 0.82, p = 0.04), and tan module (r2 = 0.94, p = 0.004) were identified as being highly correlated with the progression of CI/R induced brain injury, encompassing 955 co-expressed genes within these modules. For GSE137482, the mean connectivity of similarity matrix improved upon reaching a threshold power of 12 (scale-free R2 = 0.85) (Fig. 2). A total of 6 modules were recognized. Among them, the turquoise module (r2 = 0.81, p = 0.002), brown module (r2 = 0.90, p = 7e-05), yellow module (r2 = 0.62, p = 0.03), and green module (r2 = 0.85, p = 5e-04) were identified as being highly correlated with the progression of CI/R induced brain injury, encompassing 2841 co-expressed genes within these modules. For GSE102558, the mean connectivity of similarity matrix improved upon reaching a threshold power of 10 (scale-free R2 = 0.85) to determine the hub modules and gene profiles linked to heart injury caused by CI/R (Fig. 3). A total of 18 modules were recognized. Among them, the tan module (r2 = 0.73, p = 0.007), turquoise module (r2 = 0.90, p = 7e-05), greenyellow module (r2 = 0.84, p = 6e-04), red module (r2 = 0.75, p = 0.005), blue module (r2 = 0.93, p = 1e-05), and yellow module (r2 = 0.74, p = 0.006) were identified as being highly correlated with the progression of CI/R induced heart injury, encompassing 3173 co-expressed genes within these modules. Next, 32 potential common targets of brain-heart injury after CI/R were recognized through overlapping the target genes from these three databases (Fig. 4A). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks construction and analysis

A PPI network of common targets was further plotted to examine the interactions among the 32 potential common targets. The PPI network diagram was comprised of 53 edges and 32 nodes, where edges representing protein interactions and nodes denoting protein name. As shown in Fig. 4B, a darker color for a node signified a higher degree value. Top 10 targets with node degree values were listed as follows: Ptpn11 (7), Bmpr2 (7), Dbi (7), Ppa1(7), Rnf7 (6), Vwf (6), Imp3 (5), Ndufb10 (5), TSPO (4), Ssr2 (4).

Pathway enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were executed to investigate the enriched pathways and functions of 32 common targets involved in brain and heart injury following CI/R. The top 10 items from biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) were visualized in a bar diagram. The color and length of bar indicated the degree of enrichment and number of genes, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4C, the potential targets were primarily enriched in ATP metabolic process (GO:0046034), mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I (GO:0005747), and NADH dehydrogenase (ubiquinone) activity (GO:0008137), respectively. Subsequently, the representative pathways associated with core targets were explored using KEGG analysis. The top 20 pathways closely associated with brain-heart injury after CI/R were displayed in Fig. 4D. The analysis revealed that the top 2 pathways with highest gene counts were primarily involved in oxidative phosphorylation and chemical carcinogenesis-reactive oxygen species. This finding suggested that oxidative phosphorylation and ROS played critical role in brain-heart injury after CI/R, which was worthy of further exploration.

Identification of TSPO as a hub gene of ARCF against brain-heart injury after CI/R

A database of constituents absorbed into blood and metabolites of traditional Chinese medicine (DCABM-TCM) was used to seek the blood components of ARCF, and 51 blood components were obtained. The 513 targets of 51 blood compounds were detected from the Bioinformatics Analysis Tool for Molecular mechANism of Traditional Chinese Medicine (BATMAN-TCM), SwissTargetPrediction (STP), and Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP) databases. By overlapping the 513 targets of ARCF and the 32 common targets of brain-heart injury after CI/R, the key potential target of ARCF for anti-CI/R induced brain-heart injury was identified as TSPO. Subsequently, the binding affinity prediction between TSPO and 12 main blood components of ARCF was performed using AutoDock. The molecular structures of 12 main blood components of ARCF (formononetin, calycosin, ononin, calycosin 7-O-glucoside, formononetin 7-O-glucuronide, astragaloside IV, caffeic acid, astragaloside III, astragaloside II, hydroxysafflor yellow A, kaempferol-3-glucuronide, anhydrosafflor yellow B) were retrieved from PubChem Compound. The 3D coordinate of TSPO (PDB ID, 4UC1; resolution, 1.8 Å) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Specific binding patterns of target protein and 12 main blood components were optimized using PyMoL 2.6.0. The optimal docking images of receptor and ligands after visualization were exhibited in Fig. 5. The binding energies between target protein and 12 main blood components were −11.1 (formononetin), −11.3 (calycosin), −11.9 (ononin), −12.3 (calycosin 7-O-glucoside), −12.1 (formononetin 7-O-glucuronide), −13.2 (astragaloside IV), −7.4 (caffeic acid), −14.0 (astragaloside III), −14.9 (astragaloside II), −12.7 (hydroxysafflor yellow A), −11.2 (kaempferol-3-glucuronide), −13.6 (anhydrosafflor yellow B) kcal/mol, respectively. The results of molecular docking revealed that these components docked well with TSPO. Collectively, we suggest that ARCF may alleviate the brain and heart injury after CI/R by modulating TSPO and its related signaling pathways.Fig. 5Visualization of molecular docking of the 12 major components and the TSPO protein.

ARCF treatment attenuated brain injury and cardiac dysfunction in middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) rats

A MCAO/R rat model was constructed to validate the alleviating effect of ARCF on brain-heart injury after CI/R. ARCF with different doses were administered to observe neurobehavioral damage, brain infarct volume, S100B levels in serum, and pathological changes of brain tissue in MCAO/R rats after 7 days. Notably, CI/R led to severe neurological deficits and cerebral infarction. From Fig. 6C, there were no neurobehavioral defect in sham group rats. In MCAO/R group rats, the serious behavioral defects were observed. Edaravone (EDA) is a potent free-radical scavenger. PK11195, a classical TSPO ligand, has an inhibitory effect on TSPO activity. The neurological deficit scores of MCAO/R rats were obviously decreased after administration of ARCF, EDA and PK11195 (p < 0.05). The infarct volume percentage of each group was displayed in Fig. 6A, B. There was no brain injury in sham group rats, so infarct size was not observed. The coronal brain sections from the MCAO/R group rats had remarkable infarct areas, consistent with their having the highest neurological deficit score among the groups, demonstrating that the MCAO/R model has been successfully prepared. Compared with the model group, different administration groups significantly reduced the infarct volume. The percentages of infarct volume in MCAO/R, ARCF(L), ARCF(M), ARCF(H), EDA, PK11195, and PK11195 + ARCF(M) groups were 34.82 ± 2.87%, 24.82 ± 3.71%, 21.49 ± 2.71%, 16.83 ± 1.64%, 14.02 ± 1.96%, 11.34 ± 3.27%, 10.71 ± 3.24%, respectively. Moreover, the results of S100B ELISA assay and H&E staining confirmed that ARCF(L), ARCF(M), ARCF(H), EDA and PK11195 obviously reduced brain damage in rats with MCAO/R (Fig. 6D, E). Effect of ARCF on mitochondrial function in brain and heart after CI/R

Oxidative stress predominantly contributes to pathological conditions during CI/R. The abnormal production of ROS driven by reverse electron transfer reaction after CI/R is one of the important reasons for inducing oxidative stress. The levels of ROS in rat brain mitochondria, tissue with brain and heart, and serum significantly increased after CI/R, while ARCF management significantly reduced the levels of ROS in serum, tissue with brain and heart, and brain mitochondria (Fig. 8A–D). Mitochondrial permeability transition is a major component of oxidative injury-induced cell death during I/R. We then investigated whether the reduction of CI/R-induced brain-heart injury by ARCF was due to the blocking of mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening. Calcein-AM experiment was used to detect mPTP opening status. Calcein fluorescence was localized in mitochondria, and the opening status of mPTP was determined by a decrease in mitochondrial calcein fluorescence. The results showed that CI/R-induced brain-heart injury significantly induce loss of calcein fluorescence in brain and heart mitochondria compared to the sham group. The opening of mPTP was noticeably reduced after administration of PK11195 and ARCF, respectively (Fig. 9C, D, G, H). The opening of mPTP was associated with a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). JC-1 is widely used to detect MMP during induction of cell death. The JC-1 experiment results demonstrated that the model group had a significant loss of MMP compared with that in sham group. The loss of MMP in brain and heart mitochondria was reduced to varying degrees by administration of PK11195 and ARCF (Fig. 9A, B, E, F). ARCF alleviated CI/R-induced brain-heart injury by down-regulating TSPO, VDAC1, MCU, and Cytc expression

The protein and mRNA expression of TSPO were evaluated by western blotting and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) to reveal the potential therapeutic target of ARCF in mitigating brain-heart injury after CI/R. As demonstrated in Fig. 10, the expression levels of TSPO were noticeably upregulated in the brain and heart tissues of the model group, whereas treatment with ARCF and PK11195 markedly attenuated the protein and mRNA expression levels of TSPO. Meanwhile, the protein and mRNA expression levels of VDAC1, MUC and Cytc were abnormally increased in the brain tissue after CI/R. After treatment with PK11195 and ARCF, their expression levels were significantly decreased. The results suggested that increased TSPO expression induced abnormal expression of VDAC1, MUC, and Cytc. ARCF might interfere with the abnormal expression of VDAC1, MUC, and Cytc, leading to decreased ROS production, improved mitochondrial function, and reduced cell death by inhibiting the upregulation of TSPO after CI/R. In the heart tissue after CI/R, the protein and mRNA expression of VDAC1 and Cytc were also abnormally increased. Discussion

Multi-organ damage caused by single-organ disease is a widespread medical problem that greatly affects the health of patients, such as CI/R not only causes brain damage, but also leads to cardiac dysfunction4. Although the association between brain damage and cardiac dysfunction in ischemic stroke has been established, further investigation is required to gain a more comprehensive understanding of their cross-talk pathogenic mechanisms. Previous studies have demonstrated that ARCF holds promise as a potential therapeutic option for brain injury after CI/R or heart injury after myocardial ischemia29,30. In view of the enormous public health and economic burden, the research aimed to seek the key targets of brain-heart injury after CI/R, and to assess the therapeutic potential and possible mechanism of ARCF on brain-heart injury after CI/R, thus providing the optimal treatment strategy for this condition.

In contrast to the widely studied studies of single organ injury after ischemia/reperfusion, the pathological mechanism between brain and heart damage after CI/R remains poorly understood. In this research, new avenue be opened for investigating the underlying common mechanism of inter-organ damage by studying the overlapping phenotypic features of brain and heart after CI/R at the transcriptional level. Using advanced analytical approaches including WGCNA and Venn diagram, 32 genes were identified as potential target genes related to brain and heart injury after CI/R. GO and KEGG functional analysis identified several biological processes and pathways to determine possible action mechanisms. GO analysis outcomes showed that genetic makeup was mainly involved in ATP metabolism, mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I, and NADH dehydrogenase (panquinone) activity in terms of BP, MF, and CC, respectively. These genes showed significant enrichment in chemical carcinogenesis-reactive oxygen species and oxidative phosphorylation pathways. Our findings suggest that the oxidative phosphorylation process in mitochondria may be disturbed after CI/R, leading to the massive production of ROS, and ultimately resulting in cell death. Thus, it is crucial to tackle the harmful effects of mitochondrial dysfunction and abnormally elevated ROS levels on brain and heart, and to seek the potential of targeting these genetic determinants as a promising therapeutic strategy for individuals suffering from brain-heart injury after CI/R.

In this research, we investigated the pharmacodynamic effect of ARCF on brain-heart injury after CI/R. After ARCF was given to MCAO/R rats, the cerebral infarction volume and neurological deficit score were reduced, the heart weight/tibia length ratio was increased, the cardiac function were improved, and the levels of S100B and Tn-1 in serum were decreased, suggested that ARCF effectively alleviated brain and heart injury after CI/R. Network pharmacology and molecular docking methods were utilized to forecast the target of ARCF in alleviating brain and heart injury after CI/R. The results indicated that TSPO was a key therapeutic target of ARCF for treating brain and heart injury after CI/R. TSPO is a membrane protein with molecular weight of 18 kDa, containing five α-helical transmembrane domains, initially described as peripheral benzodiazepine receptor31,32. TSPO is located on the outer membrane of mitochondria, and participates in various mitochondrial functions, including oxidative stress regulation, energy metabolism homeostasis, and cholesterol transport33. It was reported that after CI/R, TSPO was highly expressed in the ischemic site, and the mPTP opening increased, leading to MMP lost, promoted the transfer of Cytc to the cytoplasm, and ultimately caused cell death34. These effects were believed to be related to the interaction between VDAC1 and activated TSPO. The interaction between VDAC1 and TSPO has been shown to reduce mitochondrial coupling and promote excessive production of ROS35,36. Recent research has found that the abnormal production of ROS after I/R was related to the reverse electron transport chain37. After I/R occurs, the NAD + /NADH ratio was unbalanced and the mitochondrial complex I activity was increased, resulting in a sharp increase in mitochondrial ROS levels38. The results demonstrated that the levels of ROS in brain mitochondria increased sharply, while the NAD + /NADH ratio decreased and the activity of mitochondrial complex I increased after CI/R. These results were consistent with those reported in the literature39. Abnormal ROS levels, NAD + /NADH ratios, and mitochondrial complex I activity were modulated after administration of ARCF and TSPO inhibitors (PK11195), respectively. In addition, the expression of MCU in brain tissue increased after CI/R occurred, and decreased after administration of ARCF and PK11195, respectively. It was reported that MCU activation enhanced chemical reduction of NAD+ to NADH and electron transport chain activity40. Our results suggested that the expression levels of TSPO and VDAC1 increased after CI/R, and their interaction promoted MCU activity on the inner membrane of mitochondria. Activated MCU promoted the chemical reduction of NAD+ to NADH, resulting in mitochondrial complex I activity increased abnormally, which leads to a reverse electron transport reaction driving the production of ROS. ARCF and PK11195 inhibited the expression of TSPO and reduced the interaction between TSPO and VDAC1, thereby decreased the MCU activation, increased the NAD + /NADH ratio, reduced the mitochondrial complex I abnormal activity and ROS levels, and ultimately maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis.

In the heart tissue following CI/R, we also observed an increase in the expression of TSPO and VDAC1, accompanied by an increase with mPTP opening and MMP loss. Validation experiments showed that ROS levels in serum, tissue with brain and heart, and brain mitochondria were significantly increased, and the expressions of TSPO, VDAC1 and Cytc in heart tissue were also significantly rised, as well as mPTP opening and MMP loss were increased. After administration of PK11195, the ROS levels in serum, brain and heart tissues, and brain mitochondria were significantly decreased, the expression of TSPO, VDAC1 and Cytc was visibly reduced, the mPTP opening and MMP loss were significantly decreased. After administration of ARCF, the regulatory trend of ARCF on the ROS levels, TSPO, VDAC1 and Cytc expression, MMP loss, and mPTP opening after CI/R were similar to that of PK11195. CI/R led to greater myocardial vulnerability1. The high levels of ROS in blood may induce oxidative stress in the heart, thereby activating TSPO in myocardial mitochondria, resulting in the destruction of mitochondrial homeostasis and the occurrence of cardiac injury. Considering recent findings on TSPO as an oxidative stress sensor in ischemia/reperfusion injury, ROS accumulation in brain may act as damage signaling molecules to trigger TSPO activation in the distal organ (heart). In summary, the transcriptomic data combined with WGCNA analysis was conducted to identify that mitochondrial dysfunction and abnormal ROS production were closely associated with the pathological mechanism of brain and heart injury after CI/R. ARCF management decreased neurological deficit score and cerebral infarction volume, reduced Tn-1 and S100B levels, improved cardiac function indicators after. This might be related to the fact that ARCF reduced ROS levels and mPTP opening via regulating the TSPO-VDAC1 axis after CI/R. ARCF was a potential drug for CI/R-induced brain-heart injury treatment by suppressing the ROS production and mitochondrial dysfunction, which was further verified in experiment. This study confirmed the protective effect of ARCF against brain-heart injury following CI/R and identified key therapeutic targets for ARCF in combating CI/R-induced brain-heart damage. These findings lay the groundwork for additional investigation and health management application of ARCF.

Methods

Transcriptome data collection of brain and heart injury induced by CI/R

One transcriptomic dataset for heart injury (GSE102558) and two transcriptomic datasets for brain injury (GSE166162 and GSE137482) post-CI/R were collected from the GEO database to analyze the common transcriptional changes between the brain and heart following CI/R. GSE102558 provided a dataset with gene expression profiles of 6 normal heart tissues and 6 heart tissues of rats afflicted with MCAO/R. Additionally, GSE166162 (comprising transcriptional information of rat brain tissues from 3 sham-operation samples and 3 samples with CI/R) and GSE137482 (comprising transcriptional information of rat brain tissues from 6 sham-operation samples and 6 samples with CI/R) provided transcriptome data from control and MCAO/R rats.

Screening of hub modules and identification of common genes from CI/R-induced brain-heart injury

The datasets of GSE102558, GSE166162 and GSE137482 were analyzed using WGCNA to identify hub modules linked to brain and heart injury after CI/R. The samples were assessed for missing data points before clustering. The characteristic gene network was visualized by computing module membership, gene significance, and modules related to disease phenotypes. The P-value and Pearson correlation coefficient were utilized to pinpoint pivotal modules linked to brain and heart injury after CI/R. The specific analysis process is referred to the previously reported literature41. Ultimately, the significant module genes from three datasets were combined to obtain common genes of brain-heart injury using Venn diagram.

Enrichment analysis of common genes from CI/R-induced brain-heart injury

GO and KEGG analyses were conducted utilizing the packages of “enrichplot”, “clusterProfiler”, “ggplot2”, and “org.Hs.eg.db” in R software to explore the associated functions and enriched pathways of common genes involved in brain-heart injury after CI/R. Then, common genes-related BP, MF, and CC were identified by GO analysis, and enriched signaling pathways suggested by common targets were determined by KEGG analysis. The significance of the results was assessed using a criterion of P < 0.05. The visualizations of these results were depicted through a bar chart.

PPI network construction of common genes

A PPI analysis was performed using STRING, with a combined score of ≥ 0.70, to further explore the interconnections among the identified common genes. The PPI network was then constructed and visualized using Cytoscape 3.9.1, and potential core targets were identified using the CytoNCA plugin.

Collection of ARCF blood components and target prediction

Compared to TCM components typically detected in vitro, those verified to enter the bloodstream are more likely to be the truly effective compounds responsible. The disclosure of blood components of TCMs is crucial for understanding the material basis and molecular mechanisms underlying their efficacy. DCABM-TCM (http://bionet.ncpsb.org.cn/dcabm-tcm/) is the pioneering database that systematically collects blood components from TCM prescriptions and herbs. The blood components of ARCF were collected using this database. All targets related to the blood components of ARCF were identified and extracted from BATMAN-TCM (http://bionet.ncpsb.org.cn/batman-tcm/), STP (http://www.swi sstargetprediction.ch/), and TCMSP (https://tcmsp-e.com/). These targets were intersected with the brain-heart injury related targets after CI/R to pinpoint the potential therapeutic targets of ARCF against brain-heart injury.

Molecular docking validation of ARCF main components with the core target

The 3D structural receptor (core target) and ligands (main components of ARCF) were downloaded from RSCB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/) and PubChem database, respectively. Before docking, AutoDockTools was used to perform hydrogenation, dehydration, and other pretreatment on proteins and ligands. The PyMol was employed for visualizing the docking conformations.

Experimental materials

AR and CF were obtained from Hangzhou Huadong Herbal Pieces Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). The PK11195 and edaravone were acquired from GlpBio Technology (California, USA) and MedChemExpress (N. J., USA), respectively. The primers (TSPO, VDAC1, MCU, and Cytc) were provided by Sangon Biotech. (Shanghai, China). The mitochondrial isolation kit (BL153A) and NAD + /NADH assay kit (BL1431A) were obtained from Labgic Technology (Beijing, China). The mitochondrial complex I assay kit (G0845W48) was purchased from Suzhou Grace Biotechnology (Suzhou, China). All-In-One RT MasterMix (G592) were obtained from Applied Biological Materials (Vancouver, Canada).

Animal grouping and dosage administration

Zhejiang Chinese Medical University Laboratory Animal Research Center provided the Sprague-Dawley (SD) male rats (280–320 g). All rats were housed under SPF laboratory conditions with an unrestricted diet and acclimated to the environment for at least one week before experimental use. The research was approved by Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (Approval No. 20240401-13), and rigorously performed followed the relevant guidelines.

Prior to the trial, the rats fasted for 12 h. The MCAO technique as previously reported was used to cause transitory focal CI/R19. Ninety-six SD rats were randomly divided into eight groups (n = 12 in each group): sham group, MCAO/R model group, positive group (EDA), ARCF(L) group, ARCF(M) group, ARCF(H) group, inhibitor group (PK11195), and inhibitor+ARCF(M) group. For ARCF treatment, the MCAO/R rats were intragastrically administered at three dosage levels once a day for 7 days. With reference to the 1 (AR, 2.7 g/kg; CF, 0.9 g/kg), 2 (AR, 5.4 g/kg; CF, 1.8 g/kg), and 4 (AR, 10.8 g/kg; CF, 3.6 g/kg) times the clinically recommended dosage of SMR in Chinese Pharmacopoeia, the three dosage levels (L, M, and H) of ARCF were calculated. EDA and PK11195 were administered by intraperitoneal injection at a dosage of 3 mg/kg. EDA was administered through daily injections for a duration of 7 days. PK11195 was injected once on the same day after MCAO/R, and then intraperitoneally every three days for a total of three times. Rats from the MCAO/R and sham groups received equivalent doses of normal saline.

Evaluation of the neurobehavioral deficits

After 12 h from the last administration, neurological deficit score was assessed in a single-blinded manner in accordance with modified neurological severity score (mNSS) system. The mNSS assesses four parameters: movement, sensation, balance, and reflexes, where higher scores reflect more severe dysfunction. The scoring criteria were based on established protocols from previous studies42.

Echocardiography evaluation

After completion of the neurological deficit scoring, rats were anesthetized using 3–4% isoflurane for induction in an enclosed chamber with 1 L/min 100% oxygen, followed by maintenance at 1.5–2% via a nose cone during echocardiographic imaging. Body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a heated platform, and anesthesia depth was monitored by assessing the loss of righting and pedal withdrawal reflexes. Transthoracic echocardiography was carried out using using Vevo1100 instrument (VisualSonics, Canada). After thorough skin preparation of the left anterior chest area, the animals were positioned supine. Once a regular heart rhythm was observed, echocardiographic assessment commenced. A probe with high-frequency was used to facilitate positioning, and echocardiography with M-mode was employed to observe cardiac motion in the left ventricular long axis section. Measurements including left ventricular EF and FS were obtained using the left ventricular long-axis view.

Examination of cerebral infarct volume

After echocardiography evaluation, rats were euthanized by gradual-fill carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines. Upon loss of consciousness, decapitation was performed to ensure death and to allow for immediate brain harvesting for TTC staining. The brains were removed, frozen, and cut into six thick coronal sections (2 mm). The TTC staining was operated and the infarct volume of each slice was quantified according to the reported method20.

Measurement of Tn-1 and S100B

Blood samples were collected from the sacrificed animals and centrifuged to obtain serum at 4000×g for 10 min. Based on the ELISA kit manual, the OD values of Tn-1 and S100B in serum samples were detected.

Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) staining

The pathological changes of brain and heart after CI/R were observed using HE staining. The brain and heart tissues of sacrificed rats were taken out and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 12 h at 4 °C. The brains and hearts were embedded, sectioned, and stained with HE as reported. Then, the morphology was observed using a microscope at 400× magnification after sealing.

Cardiac histological analyses

Cardiomyocyte size was assessed by measuring the cross-sectional area of cardiac muscle cells on HE stained sections. Images were captured from randomly selected fields, and the cross-sectional area of at least 20 cardiomyocytes per group was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). The measurements were then averaged for each group. The data were normalized to the mean cardiomyocyte size of the control group2. The control group consisted of sham-operated animals that underwent the same surgical procedures without induction of MCAO/R injury.

Measurement of NAD + /NADH and mitochondrial complex I activity

After euthanizing the rats, fresh brain tissue was immediately collected and rinsed with pre-cooled PBS. Approximately 30 mg of tissue was weighed for the detection of NAD+ and NADH contents, while around 100 mg of tissue was used for the assessment of mitochondrial complex I activity. The levels of NADH and NAD+ were quantified using an NAD + /NADH assay kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. A mitochondrial complex I assay kit was utilized for the assessment of mitochondrial complex I activity. The assay involved the catalysis of NADH dehydrogenation by complex I to produce NAD + , with the activity determined by measuring the NADH oxidation rate at 340 nm.

Measurement of ROS

Fresh brain or heart tissues (approximately 100 mg) were collected and mitochondrial isolation was performed using the mitochondrial isolation kit according to its operating manual. For mitochondrial ROS detection in brain tissue, the isolated brain mitochondria were broken by ultrasonic processor (JY92-IIN, LCKC, China), and the ROS content in mitochondrial supernatant was detected by ROS assay kit. Similarly, the serum and the tissue homogenate of brain and heart were detected by ROS assay kit to visualize ROS levels.

MMP and mPTP opening assays

The mitochondria with brain and heart were incubated with JC-1 and calcein-cobalt dye, respectively. JC-1 exists as a monomer with green fluorescence (emission at 530 nm and excitation at 485 nm), but when it interacts with anions in the mitochondrial matrix, it forms aggregates, emitting red fluorescence (emission at 590 nm and excitation at 485 nm). The red/green fluorescence ratio of JC-1 serves as an indicator of MMP value. A reduction in this ratio suggests depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane. The mPTP assay reagent is a fluorescent dye that uses calcein-cobalt technology to analyze and observe the activity of mPTP. The mPTP activity was evaluated by detecting the change of green fluorescence (emission at 505 nm and excitation at 488 nm) in mitochondria.

RNA quantitation by RT-qPCR

The total RNA from the brain tissue around ischemia and heart tissue was extracted by total RNA extraction reagent. And then, 5X All-In-One RT MasterMix was applied to transcript RNA as cDNA. RNA purity was detected using a spectrophotometer, and only high-quality samples (A260/280 > 1.6) were used for cDNA synthesis. The qPCR was performed using a ChamQ SYBR qPCR Mixture kit.

The mRNA expression of TSPO, VDAC1, MCU and Cytc in brain tissue and TSPO, VDAC1, Cytc in heart tissue were detected using PCR System (ABI 7500, USA). Relative quantification (2−ΔΔCt) was applied to estimate the mRNA expression levels of target genes. The housekeeping gene GAPDH was used for normalization. Western blotting

Rats were euthanized after administration, and the brain tissue around ischemia and heart tissue were collected. The brain tissue and heart tissue were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer. The BCA kit was utilized to quantify total proteins, in which TSPO, VDAC1 and Cytc proteins were quantified in heart tissue, and TSPO, VDAC1, MCU, and Cytc were quantified in brain tissue. The proteins in each sample were separated and transferred to the polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Following a blocking step, the membranes were incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies were as follows: VDAC1 (1:1000, GB111939), Cytc (1:1000, GB12080), TSPO (1:1000, FNab10473), MCU (1:1000, PAB39961), and GAPDH (1:2000, GB11002). The TBST-washed membranes were incubated with goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (GB23303, 1:3000) at room temperature for 1 h. After the reaction with enhanced chemiluminescence solution, the membranes were put into the chemiluminescence instrument for protein band visualization, and the gray values were analyzed by AIWBwellTM software (Servicebio, China).

Statistical analysis

The levels of statistical significance (p < 0.05) were determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0. All data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation.
