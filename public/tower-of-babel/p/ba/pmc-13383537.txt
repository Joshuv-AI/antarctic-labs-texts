Mapping the psychological efficacy boundaries of ideological guidance in cyberspace: a social identity theory perspective
Authors: Dan Wu, Chuchu Wang, Chenchao Zhang, Yong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe expanding role of digital platforms in ideological communication demands a rigorous understanding of when and why such efforts succeed or fail. This study investigates the psychological efficacy boundaries of ideological guidance in cyberspace through the lens of social identity theory and self-categorization theory. A three-layer structural equation model—comprising an identification input layer, an efficacy transmission layer, and a boundary response layer—was constructed and tested using a multi-method quantitative design that integrated cross-sectional survey data from 1,260 respondents across three social media platforms with a 2 × 3 between-subjects online experiment manipulating identity salience and message intensity. Results reveal an inverted U-shaped relationship between group identification strength and psychological efficacy, with moderate identification yielding optimal receptivity. Piecewise regression identified dimension-specific inflection points: behavioral efficacy boundaries emerged earliest (at approximately 4.5 on a 7-point scale), followed by affective (4.8) and cognitive efficacy (5.2). Structural path analysis confirmed that information frame matching and group norm salience mediate the identification–efficacy link, while identity threat perception and group polarization intensity function as boundary-triggering moderators. Within the post-threshold decay zone, psychological reactance and cognitive dissonance intensified substantially, operating as parallel mechanisms that accelerate efficacy deterioration. These findings suggest that the relationship between group identification and persuasive outcomes is more conditional than commonly assumed, and offer preliminary empirical indicators for understanding the limits of digital ideological communication strategies.Keywords: Social identity theory, Cyberspace ideological guidance, Psychological efficacy boundaries, Group identification, Psychological reactance, Piecewise regression

Introduction

The rapid proliferation of digital platforms has fundamentally reshaped how individuals encounter, absorb, and respond to ideological messaging. As societies undergo large-scale digital transformation, cyberspace has become far more than a medium of communication—it now functions as a primary arena where collective beliefs are negotiated, contested, and reinforced [1]. Governments, institutions, and various political actors increasingly rely on online channels to project ideological narratives, yet the psychological mechanisms that determine whether such efforts succeed or falter remain insufficiently understood. This gap is particularly consequential given that online populations are not passive recipients; they actively filter, reinterpret, and sometimes reject ideological content based on deeply rooted group affiliations and identity commitments [2]. Against this backdrop, a rigorous examination of the psychological efficacy boundaries governing ideological guidance in cyberspace is both timely and necessary.

Social identity theory, originally articulated by Tajfel through minimal group experiments in the 1970s, offered a powerful explanation for intergroup discrimination by demonstrating that mere categorization into arbitrary groups suffices to trigger in-group favoritism [3]. Turner later extended this foundation with self-categorization theory, shifting analytical focus from intergroup relations toward the cognitive process by which individuals define themselves at varying levels of abstraction—personal, social, and superordinate [4]. This theoretical evolution proved consequential: it provided a mechanism through which situational salience of particular social categories could predict attitude alignment, normative conformity, and susceptibility to persuasion within groups [5]. Over the past two decades, scholars have adapted these frameworks to digital environments, recognizing that online communities generate novel forms of categorical belonging that do not map neatly onto offline group structures [6]. Virtual anonymity, algorithmic curation, and the fluidity of digital group boundaries introduce complications that the classical paradigm did not anticipate, demanding both theoretical refinement and fresh empirical investigation.

A growing body of research has examined specific facets of this broader problem. Studies on online group identification have revealed that perceived prototypicality of message sources significantly moderates persuasion outcomes in forum-based and social media settings [7]. Work on ideological dissemination in digital spaces has documented how echo chambers and filter bubbles amplify in-group consensus while attenuating exposure to counter-attitudinal information, thereby altering the conditions under which ideological guidance can penetrate resistant audiences [8]. Meanwhile, scholars investigating psychological efficacy in digital contexts have proposed that efficacy operates not as a binary outcome but along a gradient shaped by identity threat, cognitive load, and platform affordances [9]. Cross-cultural comparative work, though still limited, suggests that collectivist cultural orientations may buffer against efficacy decay in certain online persuasion scenarios, whereas individualist contexts exhibit sharper threshold effects [10].

Despite these advances, several critical shortcomings persist. First, the quantitative measurement of psychological efficacy boundaries—the precise conditions under which ideological guidance transitions from effective to ineffective—lacks standardized operationalization. Most existing studies rely on attitudinal self-report scales that capture directional change but fail to locate inflection points or saturation thresholds with adequate precision. Second, the causal pathways linking identity mechanisms to guidance outcomes remain unclear. While correlational evidence abounds, few studies have employed experimental or quasi-experimental designs capable of disentangling whether social identification drives receptivity to ideological messages or whether successful ideological exposure instead strengthens pre-existing identification [11]. This chicken-and-egg ambiguity undermines both theoretical clarity and practical application. Third, the patterns of efficacy attenuation across different cultural contexts have received remarkably little empirical scrutiny; the handful of cross-cultural studies available tend to compare only two national samples, rendering their conclusions difficult to generalize [12].

It is precisely these unresolved tensions that motivate the present study. We argue that social identity theory, particularly when integrated with self-categorization principles, provides a uniquely suitable lens for mapping the boundaries of psychological efficacy in online ideological guidance. The theory’s emphasis on categorical salience, prototypicality, and depersonalization offers conceptual tools that align naturally with the fluid, identity-rich dynamics of cyberspace. From a practical standpoint, identifying where and why efficacy diminishes carries direct implications for the design of digital communication strategies, platform governance, and the cultivation of constructive online discourse.

Methodologically, this paper adopts a multi-method quantitative research design. We first construct a theoretical model of efficacy boundaries grounded in the social identity framework, specifying hypothesized threshold conditions and moderating variables. This model is then tested through a large-scale survey administered across multiple online platforms, supplemented by controlled experimental manipulations that isolate the effects of identity salience and source prototypicality on message acceptance. The combination of breadth from survey data and causal precision from experimental data allows for triangulation that neither approach could achieve independently. Structural equation modeling and moderated mediation analysis serve as the primary analytical techniques for quantifying boundary conditions and mapping causal pathways.

The contributions of this study are threefold. At the level of theoretical modeling, we bring social identity theory into closer dialogue with online ideological guidance research by proposing a preliminary boundary-condition framework that explores when identity-based persuasion mechanisms may activate, saturate, or reverse. At the level of boundary identification, we offer tentative empirical indicators—derived from both correlational and experimental evidence—that approximate efficacy transition zones across varying degrees of identity salience. At the level of intervention strategy, we translate these findings into actionable principles for practitioners seeking to enhance the resonance of ideological messaging while respecting the psychological autonomy of online audiences. Taken together, these contributions aim to bridge the gap between abstract identity theory and the concrete challenges of ideological communication in an increasingly digitized world.

Core constructs of social identity theory and their applicability to cyberspace

Social identity theory rests on three interlocking processes. Social categorization partitions the perceptual field into in-group and out-group, supplying cognitive shortcuts that simplify an otherwise overwhelming social environment [13]. Social comparison then evaluates the relative standing of one’s own group against relevant out-groups, generating either positive distinctiveness or identity threat depending on the outcome [3]. Social identification, the third process, describes the degree to which individuals internalize group membership as part of their self-concept, and this internalization regulates both attitudinal conformity and behavioral commitment [14].

Turner’s self-categorization theory refined this architecture by introducing the meta-contrast ratio, a principle that formalizes how perceivers determine category boundaries. The ratio holds that a collection of stimuli is more likely to be categorized as a single group when the average differences between that collection and a comparison set exceed the average differences within the collection itself [4]. Cyberspace complicates these mechanisms in consequential ways. Anonymity and visual deindividuation strip away personal identity cues, paradoxically amplifying social categorical salience rather than diminishing it—a dynamic captured by the Social Identity model of Deindividuation Effects [16]. Simultaneously, algorithmic filtering creates information cocoons that artificially inflate perceived in-group consensus, thereby raising while suppressing visible intra-group disagreement [8]. These platform-specific affordances do not invalidate classical social identity processes; they restructure the informational inputs on which categorization and comparison operate [17]. More recent developments in digital identity research have extended the SIDE model to account for algorithmic self-presentation, platform-mediated identity curation, and the increasing porosity of online group boundaries—factors that the original SIDE formulation did not fully anticipate but that are consistent with its core logic [6].

We contend that the theory remains applicable to virtual group contexts provided two conditions hold: salient category cues exist within the platform ecology, and users perceive meaningful intergroup differentiation. Building on this reasoning, we operationally define online social identity strength as the composite of cognitive centrality, in-group affect, and perceived in-group ties, measured through adapted multi-item scales validated for digital interaction settings [18].

It bears noting that digital environments are not monolithic. Public-facing platforms such as open social media forums differ substantially from semi-private messaging groups in terms of visibility, norm enforcement, and the salience of identity cues. Similarly, text-heavy discussion boards place different demands on categorization processes compared with image- or video-centric platforms where visual identity markers dominate. Institutional broadcasting of ideological content operates under different relational dynamics than peer-to-peer communication within networked communities. These distinctions matter because the conditions under which categorization becomes salient, and the speed at which identification can intensify, likely vary across platform architectures. Although the present study does not model these distinctions comprehensively, we flag them here as theoretically important boundary conditions that future work should address more granularly [6].

Defining the psychological efficacy of ideological guidance in cyberspace

Ideological guidance in cyberspace refers to the purposive shaping of collective beliefs, value orientations, and normative commitments through digitally mediated discourse [19]. To avoid conceptual slippage, we explicitly distinguish this phenomenon from several adjacent constructs. Unlike propaganda, which operates through unidirectional message saturation and typically lacks interactive feedback loops, ideological guidance presupposes interactive engagement and relies on identity-congruent framing to achieve persuasive traction [20]. It also diverges from routine opinion management, which primarily targets short-term sentiment regulation rather than durable shifts in cognitive schema [21]. Finally, it should not be conflated with general political persuasion, which need not involve group identity activation, or with digital governance communication, which carries institutional authority not assumed in peer-mediated contexts. We operationalize psychological efficacy not as a single higher-order latent construct but rather as a family of related yet conceptually distinct outcomes, each with potentially different antecedents and threshold dynamics. This framing follows the tradition in persuasion research that treats belief change, emotional alignment, and behavioral compliance as related but non-equivalent domains [22]. Three dimensions compose this family. Cognitive assimilation captures the extent to which target audiences incorporate guided content into their existing belief structures. Affective resonance measures the emotional alignment between the ideological message and recipients’ group-based sentiments. Behavioral conversion indexes observable actions—sharing, endorsing, or mobilizing—that follow from internalized ideological positions [22]. The concept of an efficacy boundary, central to this study, denotes the critical threshold interval at which ideological guidance transitions from positive facilitation to diminishing returns—or, in more extreme cases, provokes reactance and counter-attitudinal backlash. We propose that this boundary is not a fixed point but a dynamic zone conditioned by at least three factors: situational context, including platform type and topic salience; identity strength, as defined in Core Constructs of Social Identity Theory and Their Applicability to Cyberspace; and individual difference variables such as prior ideological commitment and critical media literacy [24]. Recognizing this dynamism is essential, because models that treat efficacy as monotonically increasing with exposure intensity fail to account for the well-documented boomerang effects observed when audiences perceive ideological messaging as coercive or identity-threatening [9].

Analytical framework of social identity-driven efficacy boundaries for ideological guidance

Integrating social identity theory with persuasion communication models yields a framework in which group identification strength serves as the core moderating variable governing ideological guidance outcomes. Persuasion research has long recognized that message acceptance depends not merely on content quality but on the perceived relationship between the source and the recipient’s salient group membership [25]. We build on this insight by positioning two mediating pathways: information frame matching, which captures the congruence between message framing and group-endorsed interpretive schemas, and group norm salience, which reflects the degree to which collective norms are activated during message processing [26].

The boundary-triggering mechanisms in our framework draw on psychological reactance theory and cognitive dissonance theory. These two mechanisms, while often invoked together, rest on distinct theoretical foundations and should not be conflated. Reactance theory [27] centers on perceived threats to personal autonomy: when individuals sense that a message constrains their freedom to hold or express their own views, they respond with motivational arousal directed toward restoring that freedom, often by rejecting the message or moving in the opposite direction. Cognitive dissonance theory [28], by contrast, concerns the aversive state arising from inconsistency between simultaneously held cognitions: when a guided message contradicts a deeply internalized group belief, the resulting psychological discomfort motivates defensive rejection or selective reinterpretation to restore cognitive consistency. Whether these mechanisms operate in parallel, sequentially, or interactively remains an empirical question that our data can only partially address. We provisionally treat them as concurrent boundary-triggering processes while acknowledging that future research should employ process-tracing designs to determine their temporal ordering. We hasten to add that these mechanisms are not inevitable consequences of high identification per se. Social identity theory does not imply that strong identification uniformly produces rigidity. Much depends on the normative content of the group identity, the prototypicality of the message source, and whether the guided message is experienced as affirming or threatening to the group’s self-conception [29]. A highly identified member of a normatively open and pluralistic group may exhibit greater receptivity, not less, to messages that invite ideological expansion. Our framework, therefore, should be understood as specifying conditions under which boundary effects are most likely to emerge, rather than as a deterministic account of identification-driven resistance. Beyond this central hypothesis, we derive two supplementary propositions. The identity threat perception threshold hypothesis posits that efficacy tends to diminish substantially once perceived threat to group distinctiveness moves beyond a critical range [14]. The group polarization spillover hypothesis contends that high-identification online communities amplify extreme positions through iterative social comparison, eventually pushing collective attitudes beyond the range amenable to guided moderation [31]. Importantly, we do not assume that high identification uniformly produces rigidity or exclusionary thinking. Recent work by Borinca, Guerra, and Uka [32] demonstrates that reactions to inclusion in a superordinate common ingroup are shaped by the interplay of ethnic identification and the need to belong, indicating that highly identified individuals may respond with openness rather than resistance when the message satisfies belonging-related motives. This nuance implies that the boundary effects we hypothesize should be understood as conditional on the relationship between message framing and identity-relevant motives, rather than as deterministic consequences of identification strength per se. Construction of the theoretical model for psychological efficacy boundaries

Drawing on the analytical framework established in Chapter II, we construct a structural equation model that organizes the efficacy boundary system into three functionally distinct modules. The identification input layer encompasses three exogenous variables: online group identification strength, information content characteristics, and platform contextual factors [34]. These variables jointly feed into the efficacy transmission layer, where mediating processes—frame matching and norm salience, as hypothesized earlier—channel identity-based inputs toward three endogenous efficacy outcomes: cognitive efficacy, affective efficacy, and behavioral efficacy [35]. The boundary response layer, positioned at the terminal end of the model, captures the threshold values at which each efficacy dimension ceases to increase and begins to plateau or reverse. Research methods and data collection procedures

This study employs a multi-method quantitative design combining a cross-sectional survey with an online scenario experiment. We avoid the term mixed-methods because the present design does not include a qualitative component; instead, it integrates two quantitative approaches that differ in their inferential strengths. The survey component captures naturally occurring variation in group identification, platform usage patterns, and efficacy perceptions, while the experimental component isolates causal effects through controlled manipulation [39]. Figure 2 depicts the complete workflow from instrument development through final analysis.

Data collection and analysis workflow

As shown in Fig. 2, the research proceeded in four sequential phases: instrument preparation, pilot testing, formal data collection, and data screening. Scale items were selected from validated instruments identified in Table 3 and adapted to online ideological contexts through iterative expert review involving five communication scholars and three social psychologists. A pilot test with 120 respondents confirmed acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s for all constructs) and satisfactory construct validity via confirmatory factor analysis [40].

The online scenario experiment adopted a 2 (identity salience: high vs. low) 3 (guidance message intensity: mild, moderate, strong) between-subjects design, yielding six experimental conditions. Identity salience was manipulated through priming tasks that either highlighted or downplayed participants’ membership in their primary online community. Message intensity was operationalized as a composite manipulation varying argument density, directive language, and repetition frequency simultaneously [41]. We acknowledge that this composite manipulation does not isolate the individual effects of each component, and future studies should disentangle these factors through factorial designs. Manipulation checks confirmed the intended effects: participants in the high-salience condition reported significantly greater group identification accessibility than those in the low-salience condition (Mhigh = 5.34, SD = 1.12 vs. Mlow = 3.21, SD = 1.28; t(418) = 8.72, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.74). Message intensity conditions also differed as expected, with the strong condition perceived as significantly more forceful than both moderate and mild conditions (F(2, 417) = 42.16, p < .001, partial η² = 0.17). Full experimental stimuli are provided in Appendix A. Participants were recruited from three social media platforms—Weibo, Zhihu, and Bilibili—to ensure ecological diversity across different platform architectures (microblogging, question-and-answer, and video-based communities respectively). Initial collection yielded 1,487 responses; after removing cases with response times below three minutes, invariant response patterns, or missing data exceeding 10%, the final valid sample comprised 1,260 respondents [42]. Variable measurement and analytical strategy

Online group identification strength was assessed using a modified version of the Multidimensional Social Identity Scale, adapted to capture digital community belonging across cognitive centrality, in-group affect, and in-group ties [43]. The tripartite psychological efficacy construct—cognitive, affective, and behavioral dimensions—was measured with a purpose-built scale developed through the procedures described in Research Methods and Data Collection Procedures, with all subscales achieving Cronbach’s values between 0.81 and 0.89. Psychological reactance was gauged through a revised edition of Hong’s Psychological Reactance Scale tailored to online messaging contexts [44]. Cognitive dissonance drew on an adapted version of the Elliot and Devine thermometer paradigm, reformulated as Likert-type items measuring discomfort arising from belief-message incongruence [45]. Confirmatory factor analysis across all constructs yielded acceptable fit indices (CFI , RMSEA , SRMR ), supporting the discriminant and convergent validity of the measurement model.

Common method bias was addressed through both procedural and statistical remedies. Procedurally, we introduced a two-week temporal separation between the measurement of predictor variables (identification strength, platform factors) and outcome variables (efficacy dimensions, reactance, dissonance), administered through separate online sessions linked by anonymized participant codes. Item formats were also varied across constructs, alternating between Likert-type, semantic differential, and scenario-based response formats to reduce method-driven covariance [46]. Statistically, beyond the conventional Harman’s single-factor test (which showed no single factor exceeding 29.4% of total variance), we implemented an unmeasured latent method factor approach in the CFA model. When a common method factor was added to the measurement model, the average change in factor loadings was 0.02, and no loading changed by more than 0.05, suggesting that common method variance does not substantively bias the parameter estimates.

The analytical strategy operates on three levels. First, covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM) tests the full path model specified in Construction of the Theoretical Model for Psychological Efficacy Boundaries. We initially considered partial least squares SEM given the model complexity; however, because all indicators in the final measurement model are reflective and the sample size is sufficiently large (N = 1,260), CB-SEM using maximum likelihood estimation was deemed more appropriate for its superior parameter estimation properties and its provision of global fit indices [47]. Second, to test the inverted U-shaped hypothesis (H1), we first estimate quadratic polynomial regression models by including a squared identification term, where a significant negative quadratic coefficient provides primary evidence for the curvilinear relationship. As presented in Table 5, a noteworthy pattern emerges: identification strength correlates positively with all three efficacy dimensions, yet psychological reactance and cognitive dissonance also rise alongside identification, hinting at the nonlinear dynamic hypothesized earlier.

To probe this further, we divided participants into tertiles—low (n = 408), moderate (n = 437), and high (n = 415) identification groups—and conducted one-way ANOVA across the three efficacy dimensions. All omnibus F-tests reached significance: cognitive efficacy, F(2, 1257) = 47.83, p < .001; affective efficacy, F(2, 1257) = 39.26, p < .001; behavioral efficacy, F(2, 1257) = 31.54, p < .001. We note that dividing a continuous predictor into tertiles is statistically inefficient and may create artificial group differences; these ANOVA results should therefore be regarded as descriptive illustrations rather than formal tests of the curvilinear hypothesis. Tukey post hoc comparisons revealed that the moderate identification group scored significantly higher than both the low and high groups on all three dimensions (p < .01 for each pairwise contrast), while the difference between low and high groups was non-significant for cognitive efficacy and only marginally significant for behavioral efficacy [50]. This pattern—moderate identification outperforming both extremes—offers preliminary descriptive support for the inverted U-shaped relationship posited in H1. To provide a more rigorous test, we estimated quadratic polynomial regression models with identification strength and its squared term as predictors for each efficacy dimension. The quadratic term was significant and negative in all three models: for cognitive efficacy, B = − 0.14, SE = 0.03, t = − 4.67, p < .001; for affective efficacy, B = − 0.19, SE = 0.03, t = − 6.33, p < .001; for behavioral efficacy, B = − 0.23, SE = 0.04, t = − 5.75, p < .001. The inclusion of the squared term significantly improved model fit over the linear-only specification in each case (cognitive: ΔR² = 0.017, p < .001; affective: ΔR² = 0.031, p < .001; behavioral: ΔR² = 0.026, p < .001). These results confirm that the curvilinear relationship is not an artifact of tertile categorization but holds under continuous modeling. Figure 3 presents these group differences visually.

What deserves particular attention is the interaction term between identity threat perception and identification strength. The negative coefficient ( = -0.28, p < .001) indicates that when individuals high in group identification simultaneously perceive their group’s distinctiveness as threatened by the guided message, efficacy does not merely plateau—it actively deteriorates. This moderation effect was more pronounced than the polarization interaction ( = -0.21), suggesting that identity threat constitutes a more potent boundary trigger than group polarization spillover in our sample [52]. Collectively, these results provide support for all six hypotheses and suggest a structural architecture through which identification both facilitates and ultimately constrains ideological guidance efficacy. We note that while the experimental component permits causal inference regarding identity salience and message intensity effects, the survey-based path estimates remain correlational and should be interpreted accordingly.

Efficacy boundary inflection point identification and threshold estimation

Applying the piecewise regression model specified in Eq. (4), we estimated breakpoints separately for each efficacy dimension. The cognitive efficacy boundary emerged at an identification strength score of approximately 5.2 on the 7-point scale. Below this threshold, the ascending segment showed a significant positive slope ( = 0.39, p < .001); above it, the slope turned negative but non-significant ( = -0.08, p = .21), indicating insufficient evidence for a significant decline in this sample, though the directional trend is consistent with plateau-like attenuation. Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals around this breakpoint estimate yielded a range of [4.87, 5.53], indicating non-trivial uncertainty. Affective efficacy, by contrast, reached its approximate transition zone earlier—at roughly 4.8—and the post-threshold slope was both negative and significant ( = -0.17, p < .01), suggesting that emotional alignment with guided messages deteriorates more readily than cognitive acceptance once identification becomes excessive [53]. The affective breakpoint confidence interval spanned [4.48, 5.12]. The most notable finding concerned behavioral efficacy, whose approximate transition zone appeared at roughly 4.5, with a pronounced downward trajectory beyond that value ( = -0.26, p < .001). The behavioral breakpoint confidence interval was [4.16, 4.84]. Model comparison tests indicated that the piecewise specification provided marginally better fit than the quadratic model for behavioral efficacy (ΔAIC = − 3.7) but not for cognitive efficacy (ΔAIC = 1.2), suggesting that the quadratic and segmented models are roughly equivalent descriptors of the underlying pattern. This ordering—behavioral transition zones emerging first, affective second, cognitive last—is consistent with theoretical expectations: action commitments demand greater personal investment and may therefore be more sensitive to motivational shifts than passive belief adjustment. We note, however, that behavioral measures often show lower endorsement because they imply cost, visibility, and social commitment, and these features may operate independently of reactance. The ordering we report is therefore suggestive of differential vulnerability, but the underlying mechanism requires more targeted experimental work to isolate.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, the three curves share an ascending left segment but diverge sharply in their post-threshold behavior. The cognitive efficacy curve plateaus gently, the affective curve bends downward at a moderate gradient, and the behavioral curve drops steeply—a visual pattern that underscores how differently these efficacy facets respond once identification crosses their respective boundaries.

Examining what happens psychologically within the decay zone proved revealing. Among respondents scoring above their dimension-specific breakpoint, psychological reactance scores averaged 4.61 (compared to 3.24 in the pre-threshold range), representing a 42% increase. Cognitive dissonance showed a similar but somewhat less dramatic elevation of 33% [54]. These activation patterns confirm that the boundary mechanisms postulated in H4 and H6 are not merely theoretical constructs but measurable psychological states that intensify in tandem with efficacy decline.

Demographic heterogeneity in boundary location warrants brief comment. Subgroup analyses revealed that younger participants (aged 18–25) exhibited lower behavioral efficacy thresholds than older cohorts, possibly reflecting heightened sensitivity to perceived ideological imposition. Education moderated threshold placement as well: respondents with postgraduate training showed cognitive efficacy boundaries approximately 0.4 scale points higher than those with high school education, suggesting that advanced critical literacy delays—but does not prevent—the onset of efficacy stagnation [55]. These heterogeneity patterns reinforce the argument advanced in Defining the Psychological Efficacy of Ideological Guidance in Cyberspace that efficacy boundaries are not universal constants but context- and person-dependent zones whose precise location shifts with audience characteristics.

Discussion

The indentification strength and psychological efficacy, supported by converging evidence from polynomial regression, ANOVA contrasts, and piecewise regression, warrants a careful—if necessarily tentative—mechanistic interpretation. Why should stronger identification—ostensibly a resource for persuasion—eventually undermine the very guidance it initially facilitates? The answer, we believe, lies in the dual nature of intense group attachment. At moderate levels, identification primes individuals to attend to in-group sources, accept group-endorsed frames, and experience emotional solidarity with collective narratives. Yet once identification intensifies beyond a critical range, it triggers exclusionary cognition: the group’s boundaries harden, tolerance for ideological nuance contracts, and any message perceived as externally imposed—even when substantively aligned with group values—gets flagged as a potential threat to group autonomy. This is not mere stubbornness. It reflects a deep-seated protective reflex whereby highly identified members prioritize group distinctiveness preservation over informational openness. The result appears paradoxical: the individuals most psychologically invested in their group may become less receptive to guided ideological adjustment, interpreting such guidance as encroachment rather than enrichment. An important caveat is warranted here: the present data cannot fully distinguish whether high identification per se drives this resistance, or whether the observed decline instead reflects a message-identity misfit—that is, highly identified individuals may simply be more discerning, and messages that lack fine-grained calibration to the specific norms and values of the group are experienced as misaligned or intrusive regardless of identification level. This distinction has substantive implications: if the mechanism is identification-driven rigidity, then there is a hard ceiling on persuasion; if it is message misfit, then better-calibrated messages might preserve efficacy even at high identification levels. Our design does not conclusively adjudicate between these interpretations, and future research should manipulate message-identity congruence orthogonally to identification strength to separate these processes.

The staggered ordering of efficacy boundary thresholds across the three dimensions—behavioral at 4.5, affective at 4.8, cognitive at 5.2—reveals something important about the architecture of psychological resistance. Behavioral conversion sits at the apex of personal commitment; sharing a post, signing a petition, or mobilizing others requires not just agreement but willingness to bear social costs and invest personal resources. Several alternative explanations for the observed boundary effects deserve explicit consideration. Ceiling effects on the identification scale could artificially compress variance at the upper end, making it harder to detect positive associations. Response fatigue may disproportionately affect later-measured constructs. Differences in perceived message credibility across the experimental conditions could contribute to differential efficacy decline. Generalized aversion to strong directive language, independent of identity processes, could explain part of the behavioral decline. We cannot rule out these possibilities entirely, though the differentiated threshold ordering across dimensions (behavioral first, cognitive last) would be difficult to explain by ceiling effects or fatigue alone, as these artifacts would affect all dimensions similarly. With these caveats noted, it stands to reason that the behavioral dimension would show sensitivity earliest, since the gap between privately acknowledging a message and publicly acting on it widens as autonomy threat intensifies. Affective efficacy occupies an intermediate position: emotional resonance can persist even when behavioral willingness erodes, but sustained emotional alignment eventually falters if the individual senses manipulative intent behind the affective appeal. Cognitive efficacy proves most resilient, likely because belief integration operates at a more abstract, less personally costly level—one can mentally accommodate new information without feeling that one’s agency has been compromised. This hierarchical vulnerability pattern carries a practical implication worth stating plainly: practitioners who rely solely on attitudinal or belief-level indicators may risk overestimating the effectiveness of their guidance efforts if behavioral and emotional commitment have already begun to weaken. However, we note that lower endorsement of behavioral items could also reflect the higher social desirability costs of endorsing action, response fatigue, or ceiling effects on the identification scale, rather than a qualitatively distinct boundary process. The ordering we observe is suggestive rather than definitive, and the underlying mechanism warrants more targeted experimental investigation.

Engaging with the classical propositions of social identity theory in its Western formulations raises a productive tension. Tajfel and Turner’s original framework was developed primarily through face-to-face intergroup encounters where physical co-presence, visual cues, and embodied interaction anchored the categorization process. Cyberspace strips away many of these anchoring features. The disembodied nature of online interaction might, at first glance, seem to weaken identification—after all, what holds a group together without shared physical space? Our findings point in a different direction, but with an important qualification. Online environments do not weaken identification so much as restructure its informational basis. Algorithmic curation and echo chamber dynamics artificially concentrate in-group cues while filtering out cross-cutting perspectives, creating what might be called hyper-categorical environments where identification intensifies rapidly but on a narrower foundation. This narrowness, we suspect, is part of why efficacy boundaries appear at relatively moderate identification levels in our data: the identification that digital platforms foster may be intense yet brittle, lacking the relational depth that sustains receptivity to challenging messages in offline communities.

The de-individuated character of online interaction adds another layer. When personal identity recedes and social identity becomes the dominant self-construct—a process the SIDE model describes well—individuals become especially sensitive to perceived norm violations. Ideological guidance that inadvertently transgresses group norms, even slightly, risks triggering disproportionate backlash because there is no personal relationship buffer to absorb the friction. In face-to-face settings, an individual might tolerate ideological discomfort out of interpersonal loyalty or politeness; online, where the interlocutor is an abstract category representative rather than a known person, such tolerance diminishes sharply.

These findings carry several practical implications for those responsible for ideological guidance in digital spaces. First, and most fundamentally, the data suggest caution against maximizing group identification as a strategy. The temptation to cultivate intense in-group solidarity as a vehicle for message acceptance is understandable, but our results demonstrate that it is self-defeating beyond a threshold. A more effective approach would involve moderate activation of group identity—enough to establish source credibility and normative relevance, but not so much that exclusionary cognition takes hold. Second, practitioners would benefit from real-time monitoring of boundary signals: rising reactance indicators, declining engagement metrics, and emergent counter-argumentation patterns can serve as early warnings that the efficacy boundary is approaching. Acting on such signals—by softening message intensity, introducing pluralistic framing, or temporarily shifting to less identity-salient content—might delay the onset of diminishing returns. We stress, however, that the specific threshold values reported here (4.5, 4.8, 5.2) should not be treated as operational benchmarks; they are sample-specific estimates with non-trivial confidence intervals, and their ecological validity in real-world communication campaigns remains to be established. Third, the demographic heterogeneity documented in Efficacy Boundary Inflection Point Identification and Threshold Estimation argues forcefully against one-size-fits-all strategies. Younger audiences and those with lower formal education reach behavioral efficacy boundaries sooner, suggesting that guidance targeted at these groups requires gentler calibration, more dialogic framing, and greater attention to perceived autonomy preservation. Conversely, highly educated audiences tolerate somewhat stronger cognitive engagement but remain equally vulnerable at the affective and behavioral levels, meaning that intellectual sophistication should not be mistaken for comprehensive resilience to reactance effects.

Before summarizing these practical considerations, an explicit ethical reflection is warranted. Because the present study investigates conditions under which ideological messages achieve maximal psychological impact, there is an inherent risk that the findings could be read instrumentally—as a manual for optimizing ideological influence. We wish to state clearly that our intent is analytic rather than prescriptive: understanding the psychological mechanisms through which ideological guidance operates, including its failure modes, serves the broader goal of informed public discourse and transparent communication practice. Researchers and practitioners alike should attend to the ethical dimensions of calibrating identity-salient messaging, particularly regarding informed consent, transparency of persuasive intent, and respect for the psychological autonomy of message recipients. Taken together, these observations suggest that effective ideological guidance in cyberspace is less about persuasive force and more about navigating a narrow corridor between under-stimulation and over-stimulation of group identity—a corridor whose width varies across individuals, platforms, and cultural moments.

Conclusion

This study set out to map the psychological efficacy boundaries of ideological guidance in cyberspace through the lens of social identity theory. By constructing a three-layer structural equation model and testing it with a multi-method quantitative design combining survey data from 1,260 respondents and a 2 × 3 between-subjects online experiment, we have arrived at three sets of preliminary findings that, pending replication, may contribute to both theoretical understanding and practical awareness.

First, group identification strength and psychological efficacy follow an inverted U-shaped trajectory. Moderate identification creates the optimal conditions for message receptivity—sufficient in-group trust to grant source credibility, yet not so intense as to trigger defensive exclusion. This curvilinear pattern complicates the implicit assumption in some applied communication work that stronger group bonds invariably translate into greater persuasive reach, although we note that prior social identity research has already suggested this possibility under certain conditions. They may not. Beyond approximate transition zones, the solidarity that facilitated openness appears, in our data, to shift toward reduced receptivity—though the extent to which this reflects identification strength per se versus message-identity misfit remains an open question.

Second, the three efficacy dimensions do not share a single boundary. Behavioral efficacy erodes first (at approximately 4.5 on the identification scale), followed by affective efficacy (4.8) and cognitive efficacy (5.2). This gradient reflects the differential psychological costs associated with each dimension: acting on a guided message demands more personal investment than feeling moved by it, which in turn requires more than merely comprehending it. The practical consequence is that surface-level cognitive acceptance can persist long after behavioral and emotional commitment have already collapsed—a discrepancy that conventional attitude surveys are poorly equipped to detect.

Third, the boundary region is not a passive zone of diminishing returns but an active psychological space where two distinct mechanisms converge. Psychological reactance intensifies as individuals perceive autonomy infringement, while cognitive dissonance escalates when guided messages clash with entrenched group beliefs. These dual triggers operate in parallel rather than sequentially, and their combined activation accelerates efficacy deterioration more sharply than either mechanism alone would predict.

The theoretical contributions of this work, understood as preliminary and in need of replication, span three levels. Conceptually, we framed efficacy boundaries as dynamic, person- and context-dependent transition zones rather than fixed cutoff values—a framing that synthesizes rather than fundamentally revises existing insights from social identity and persuasion research. Methodologically, the application of piecewise regression to identify dimension-specific inflection points offers a replicable approach for future boundary research. Analytically, the integration of social identity mechanisms with reactance and dissonance frameworks yields a more complete causal architecture than prior models that treated identification as a unidirectional facilitator.

For practitioners engaged in digital governance and online ideological communication, the findings argue for calibrated rather than maximized identity activation, continuous monitoring of boundary-approach signals, and audience-differentiated messaging strategies that account for demographic variation in threshold placement.

Several limitations temper these conclusions and must be acknowledged with full candor. The sample drew exclusively from three social media platforms, raising questions about generalizability to other digital ecosystems. The cross-sectional survey component captures a snapshot rather than the temporal evolution of efficacy boundaries, which likely shift as platform norms, political climates, and user demographics change. Moreover, the controlled experimental scenarios, while valuable for causal inference, inevitably sacrifice some ecological validity relative to the messy, multi-stimulus reality of actual online ideological encounters. Additional limFitations warrant explicit mention. The experimental manipulation of guidance intensity conflated argument density, directive tone, and repetition frequency, preventing us from isolating which component drives the observed effects. The sample skews toward young, highly educated respondents, which may systematically bias the observed transition zones, particularly given the established moderating effects of education and media literacy on persuasion resistance. Our measurement model, while achieving acceptable global fit indices and AVE values, would benefit from independent replication with alternative item pools to confirm that the efficacy dimensions are truly distinct from neighboring constructs such as general attitude change, engagement, and compliance. The survey-based path estimates remain correlational, and the causal language we occasionally employ should be understood as applicable only to the experimental component. The breakpoint estimates carry non-trivial confidence intervals and should be interpreted as approximate indicators rather than precise psychological constants. Platform-level variation (Weibo, Zhihu, Bilibili) was not formally modeled as a moderator, although supplementary multi-group analyses showed no significant differences in path coefficients across platforms. Finally, the study was conducted in a single cultural context, limiting the generalizability of both the transition zone values and the gradient ordering to other societies.

Future research should address these gaps through longitudinal panel designs capable of tracking how efficacy boundaries migrate over time, cross-cultural comparative studies that test whether the transition zone patterns and gradient ordering observed here replicate across societies with different collectivist-individualist orientations—although we recognize that such extrapolation from a single-culture sample must remain highly tentative, and computational social science approaches—particularly agent-based modeling and large-scale digital trace analysis—that can capture boundary dynamics at population scale with temporal granularity that survey methods cannot achieve.
