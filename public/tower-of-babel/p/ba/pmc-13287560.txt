Free-breathing Three-dimensional MR Cholangiopancreatography Using Centric k-space Encoding Improved Gallbladder Visualization: A Prospective Study
Authors: Junya Tsuzaki, Taiki Nozaki, Daiki Ito, Tetsushi Habe, Sayaka Miyazaki, Manabu Arai, Manabu Hase, Hiroki Sakata, Ryo Tsukada, Yoshitake Yamada, Masahiro Jinzaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMR cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) often suffers from signal loss because of concentrated bile. Gradient-and-spin-echo (GRASE) MRCP reduces this effect but is limited by breath-holding. We developed MRCP with centric order encoding (MRCP-CORE), a novel free-breathing sequence with shorter TE using centric k-space trajectory, T2 preparation, and velocity encoding, and evaluated its ability to visualize gallbladder.MethodsIn this prospective, single-center study, we enrolled 100 consecutive patients who underwent 3 MRCP sequences (MRCP-CORE, conventional MRCP, and GRASE MRCP) on a 3T system. Two blinded radiologists independently assessed the visibility of anatomical structures, artifacts, and overall image quality. SNR, contrast-to-noise ratio, and contrast ratio of the periductal tissue and gallbladder were measured. The Friedman test was used for comparisons; Jonckheere–Terpstra analysis tested the association between the gallbladder visibility and quantitative metrics.ResultsNinety-nine patients (mean age, 68.5 ± 10.9 years; 52 men, 47 women) were evaluated. MRCP-CORE showed significantly higher SNR than conventional and GRASE MRCP (20.9 vs. 17.3 vs. 15.8; P < 0.001). Gallbladder visibility was significantly higher with MRCP-CORE than via conventional MRCP (P = 0.02) and comparable to that via GRASE MRCP (P = 1.00). No significant differences in artifact and overall image quality were observed among sequences. Trend analysis revealed gallbladder visibility was significantly influenced by bile concentration in conventional MRCP (P < 0.001), but not in MRCP-CORE (P = 0.12) or GRASE MRCP (P = 0.43).ConclusionMRCP-CORE enhanced gallbladder depiction compared with conventional MRCP, offering a robust free-breathing alternative to breath-hold GRASE MRCP.Keywords: biliary imaging, concentrated bile, gallbladder, k-space trajectory, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography

Introduction

MR cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is pivotal in the non-invasive diagnosis of biliary and pancreatic diseases, enabling accurate detection of gallstones, strictures, and anatomical anomalies.1,2 Poor image quality risks misdiagnosis, leading to unfavorable clinical outcomes.3–7 Conventional MRCP using turbo spin-echo (TSE) sequences with a long TE produces T2-weighted images (T2WIs) to highlight bile and pancreatic fluid. However, concentrated bile, present in 49%–85% of gallbladder (GB) examinations, reduces signal because of T2 shortening, posing diagnostic challenges.8–11 This high prevalence underscores the clinical need for imaging techniques that preserve biliary conspicuity under these common circumstances, as signal loss can lead to diagnostic errors and adversely affect clinical management and surgical planning.8,12,13

One way to address this issue is to use the gradient-and-spin-echo (GRASE) sequence. As a hybrid technique that merges spin echo and gradient echo,14 GRASE MRCP enables a much shorter TE than does conventional TSE-based MRCP, thereby preserving strong T2 contrast, even in cases of concentrated bile.15 Studies have suggested that GRASE MRCP and breath-hold TSE-based MRCP with compressed sensing provide superior image quality compared to that via respiratory-triggered MRCP in patients with irregular breathing patterns.16–18 However, the main drawback of GRASE MRCP is its reliance on breath-holding, which limits applicability in patients with restricted breath-holding ability, such as pediatric or sedated patients.

To solve this problem, we proposed a new TSE-based MRCP sequence called MRCP with centric ordered encoding (MRCP-CORE), which utilizes a centric order k-space trajectory and minimizes TE to enhance bile visualization during free breathing, and compared its performance with that of conventional free-breathing 3D TSE MRCP and breath-hold GRASE MRCP.

Materials and Methods

This prospective, single-center study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Keio University School of Medicine, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations concerning research involving human subjects.

Patients and examination protocol

We prospectively enrolled 100 consecutive patients who underwent MRCP via a 3.0T MRI scanner (Ingenia Elition X 3.0T; Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands) for various clinical indications at Keio University Hospital between November 2024 and January 2025. No exclusion criteria were applied, and all patients were included in the study.

The examination protocol was as follows: after acquiring localizer images and single-shot T2WI in both the axial and coronal planes, each MRCP sequence (MRCP-CORE, conventional MRCP, and GRASE MRCP) was performed sequentially. To account for potential fatigue-related effects on breathing, the acquisition order of the sequences was randomized for each patient. The detailed imaging parameters for each sequence are listed in Table 1, and the sequence chart and k-space trajectory of MRCP-CORE are shown in the Supplementary Figure. Conventional MRCP is the sequence routinely used in daily clinical practice in our institute. To enhance T2 contrast in this sequence and MRCP-CORE, an improved motion sensitized driven equilibrium (iMSDE) pulse is employed as a T2 preparation pulse, along with an additional driven equilibrium pulse. In MRCP-CORE, the shorter TE tends to preserve signals from flowing blood; therefore, the velocity encoding (VENC) of the iMSDE pulse was set to 5 cm/sec to induce phase dispersion of the vessel signal based on a previous study.19

Qualitative evaluation

Two board-certified radiologists (J.T. and M.A., with 9 and 13 years of experience in abdominal MRI, respectively) independently evaluated all MRCP images while being blinded to the clinical information. The visibility of the right hepatic duct, left hepatic duct, common hepatic duct, common bile duct (CBD), main pancreatic duct (MPD), GB, artifact, and overall image quality were assessed using a 4-point Likert scale. The scoring criteria for anatomical structure and overall image quality were as follows: 1 = poor, 2 = moderate, 3 = good, and 4 = excellent. For artifact, the scoring was as follows: 1 = severe, 2 = mild, 3 = trivial, and 4 = no artifact. For each item, the average of the 2 readers’ scores was used for analysis.

Quantitative evaluation

One reader (J.T.) measured the SNR of the CBD, contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), contrast ratio (CR) between the CBD and periductal tissue, and CR between the CBD and bile within the GB. Based on a previous report,20 circular or elliptical ROIs were placed in homogeneous, artifact-free areas of the CBD, GB, and periductal tissue. The signal intensity (SI) and standard deviation (SD) of each region were measured. The ROI sizes were set to at least 5 mm2 for the CBD and 20 mm2 for the other regions.

The measurement formulas were defined as follows:

SNR = SICBD/SDCBD, CNR = (SICBD − SIperiductal tissue)/SDCBD, CRperiductal tissue = (SIperiductal tissue − SICBD)/(SIperiductal tissue + SICBD), and CRGB = (SIGB − SICBD)/(SIGB + SICBD).

In these formulas, CRperiductal tissue indicates the strength of background suppression, with lower values reflecting better suppression. Conversely, the CRGB represents the relative signal of concentrated bile within the GB, where a lower value indicates lower GB visualization on MRCP.

Visibility evaluation of GB with concentrated bile

Patients were stratified into 4 groups according to the CRGB measured on conventional MRCP, using cut-off values of –0.3, –0.5, and –0.7. For each MRCP sequence, trend analysis was performed to assess the association between the degree of bile concentration and GB visibility scores.

Statistical analysis

The Friedman test was used to compare the 3 MRCP sequences in both qualitative and quantitative evaluations. The Jonckheere–Terpstra test was conducted to evaluate the trend between the degree of bile concentration and GB visibility for each MRCP sequence. Statistical significance was set at a 2-sided P-value < 0.05, and Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons. For analysis using Bonferroni correction, adjusted P-values are reported. Inter-observer agreement for qualitative score between 2 readers and intra-observer agreement for qualitative scoring by 1 reader were assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ) coefficient. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA).

Patients

Of the 100 enrolled patients, 1 withdrew consent after the examination, leaving 99 patients available for analysis. The mean age of the included patients was 68.5 ± 10.9 years, with a male-to-female ratio of 52:47. The clinical characteristics and acquisition order of the MRCP sequences are shown in Fig. 1.

The GB was evaluated in 87 patients, excluding 12 patients who had undergone a previous cholecystectomy. All the other anatomical structures, artifacts, and overall image quality were evaluated in all cases. Although all sequences generally yielded good image quality, the GB visibility scores were significantly higher for MRCP-CORE and GRASE MRCP than for conventional MRCP (P = 0.02 and 0.04, respectively) (a representative case is shown in Fig. 2). For MPD visualization, MRCP-CORE showed a slightly lower score than did conventional MRCP, although the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.21) (Table 2). Discussion

Our prospective intra-individual study of 99 patients demonstrated that the newly proposed MRCP-CORE sequence outperformed both routine free-breathing 3D TSE MRCP and breath-hold GRASE MRCP. Owing to its markedly shorter effective TE (52 msec), MRCP-CORE provided the highest SNR and achieved GB visibility scores that were significantly superior to those of conventional MRCP while remaining statistically equivalent to those of GRASE MRCP. Trend analysis using CRGB-based subgroups demonstrated that GB visibility significantly declined with increasing bile concentration in conventional MRCP. In contrast, MRCP-CORE maintained visibility even under high bile concentration, indicating that it is less susceptible to signal loss from concentrated bile. Importantly, these gains were realized without compromising the depiction of other anatomical structures.

Bile stored in the GB becomes concentrated, resulting in substantial T2 shortening. As MRCP relies on heavy T2-weighting to depict ductal structure and cystic lesions while suppressing background signals, this leads to marked signal loss of bile. To address this limitation, sequences with shortened TE, such as GRASE MRCP, have been developed and are widely used to improve visualization in the presence of concentrated bile. The GRASE technique, first introduced by Oshio and Feinberg,21 achieves a shorter TE by combining gradient echo and spin-echo sequences, resulting in a shorter TE compared to that using a pure spin-echo sequence.22 When applied to abdominal imaging, several studies have demonstrated that GRASE MRCP yields high-quality 3D MRCP images within a short acquisition time, and a substantially shorter TE helps minimize signal loss caused by concentrated bile, resulting in improved image quality.14,15 However, a significant drawback of GRASE MRCP is its incompatibility with free-breathing acquisition. Recently, Wang et al.18 reported that respiratory patterns affect the image quality in both respiratory-triggered and breath-hold MRCP. They suggested that monitoring the breathing curve may aid in selecting the most appropriate acquisition method. Consequently, GRASE MRCP carries an inherent risk of suboptimal imaging in patients with irregular or unreliable breath-holding capability. In addition, GRASE MRCP has several other limitations. First, owing to its hybrid nature, GRASE is more susceptible to magnetic susceptibility artifact than spin-echo sequences.22 Consequently, intraluminal gas or metallic devices retained after surgery or endoscopy may more readily degrade the image quality with GRASE MRCP than with TSE-based MRCP. Second, the short TR and TE used in GRASE MRCP increase its sensitivity to T1 contrast effects. In standard MRCP, oral negative contrast agents are typically administered to suppress the high signal from the gastrointestinal fluid and enhance the visibility of fine tubular structures, such as the bile and main pancreatic ducts. Although the T2 shortening effect of these agents efficiently attenuates bowel signal on TSE-based MRCP, they also exert a T1 shortening effect.23 Consequently, their negative contrast performance may be insufficient or even paradoxically produce a hyperintense signal on GRASE MRCP. While the intrinsic T1-weighting of GRASE MRCP is advantageous for visualizing concentrated bile,15 clinicians should be aware that the presence of oral contrast agents, hematoma, or other T1 breath-hold shortening substances can compromise the examination quality. Thus, although GRASE MRCP enables satisfactory biliary evaluation, even in the presence of concentrated bile, it is not universally ideal. These GRASE-specific drawbacks underscore the need for TSE MRCP techniques that can achieve a shorter TE.

Two prior studies have addressed this issue. Itatani et al.24 employed a variable-flip-angle short-TE Cube sequence (TE = 80 msec) and improved ductal conspicuity, whereas Ihara et al.25 introduced a T2-preparation pulse combined with a navigator-triggered TSE MRCP, lowering the effective TE to 141 msec and enhancing image quality. Our MRCP-CORE shared Ihara’s concept of incorporating a preparatory technique; however, the most notable difference lies in the use of a centric k-space trajectory. The contrast in MRI images is heavily influenced by the low-frequency components located at the center of the k-space.26,27 Therefore, the k-space trajectory directly affects both the effective TE and artifact formation, making it a crucial factor in determining the image contrast. MRCP-CORE employs centric ordering, which prioritizes the acquisition of these low-frequency components and thereby shortens the effective TE. Using this approach, MRCP-CORE achieved the shortest TE among the sequences compared. Additionally, in Ihara’s method, the T2-preparation time (100 msec) and TE were considerably longer than those in MRCP-CORE, providing excellent background suppression but at the cost of greater T2 relaxation. Because MRCP-CORE shortened both the T2-preparation time and the TE, its CRperiductal tissue was worse than that of conventional MRCP, yet the limited T2 relaxation minimized signal loss from highly concentrated bile. A shorter TE, however, also diminished flow-void effects, allowing slow venous blood to remain hyperintense. To offset this drawback, we appended VENC gradients to the iMSDE pulse, introducing phase dispersion that selectively suppresses vascular signal while preserving the bile duct hyperintensity. If VENC was set excessively high, vascular suppression would be incomplete; if set too low, slow bile flow would dephase as long as the vessels. Furthermore, when setting VENC excessively low, large gradient moments would provoke eddy-current artifact and unwarranted signal loss in stationary tissues. Nakayama et al.19 demonstrated that, in MSDE-augmented balanced steady state free precession MRCP, VENC values of 3–5 cm/sec optimized flow suppression and image quality. Based on these findings, we selected a VENC of 5 cm/sec in MRCP-CORE balancing vascular dephasing and artifact. Moreover, another distinguishing feature of MRCP-CORE is the use of a driven equilibrium pulse, which enhances T2 contrast without prolonging TE. Taken together, MRCP-CORE extends the preparatory-pulse strategy pioneered by Ihara et al. by combining a shorter TE with targeted vascular suppression, thereby providing superior conspicuity of the GB even in the presence of concentrated bile compared to conventional MRCP.

Theoretical concerns with long-echo-train 3D-TSE using centric sampling are twofold: (1) cumulative T2 decay in late-train echoes at the k-space periphery attenuates high-spatial-frequency content that determines edge sharpness; and (2) unstable early-train signal can populate the low-spatial-frequency content, increasing line-to-line variability in low-frequency weighting. Either mechanism can present as image blurring. In our implementation, however, MRCP-CORE had the shortest echo space (2.9 msec) among the 3 sequences and a shot duration (288 msec) that was substantially shorter than that of conventional MRCP (586 msec). These timing characteristics reduce the range of TEs across the echo train and thereby limit the degree of T2-decay-related modulation of k-space. We also inserted a start-up echo segment before sampling the k-space center to tune the effective TE and damp early-train transients at low-spatial-frequencies, thereby reducing line-to-line k-space variability. In addition, centric k-space ordering in MRCP-CORE acquires the low-spatial-frequency, contrast-defining data at a short effective TE, helping to preserve duct conspicuity even when the echo train is relatively long. Taken together, the shorter echo spacing and shot duration, combined with centric ordering, likely mitigated any blurring that might otherwise occur, which is consistent with our findings that overall image quality and duct visibility did not deteriorate, whereas GB conspicuity improved in the presence of concentrated bile.

Also, from a workflow standpoint, MRCP-CORE modifies only the k-space view ordering within a standard 3D-TSE sequence; accordingly, it imposes no reconstruction-time overhead and leaves routine clinical workflow essentially unchanged.

Our study has several limitations. The primary limitation is its single-institution design, which may have restricted the broader generalizability of our findings. Although the patient cohort was relatively large and included individuals with diverse clinical indications, it is possible that different patient populations and MRI systems would yield somewhat different outcomes. Moreover, because the TSE-based MRCP sequences compared in this study were routinely used in clinical practice, they differed in several acquisition parameters in addition to the k-space trajectory; therefore, the present comparison does not isolate the effect of the k-space trajectory alone. We also did not investigate alternative schemes or variations in the acquisition methods; therefore, further sequence optimization may be possible. Further studies, including multi-institutional trials and sequence modifications, are necessary to validate and refine our approach.

Conclusion

MRCP-CORE provides a free-breathing, TSE-based MRCP technique that effectively shortens TE through centric k-space ordering. It preserves signal from concentrated bile and maintains high ductal visibility, outperforming conventional MRCP and matching GRASE MRCP, without the latter’s breath-hold dependency. These results suggest its potential utility in routine clinical practice, especially for patients unable to sustain breath-holding.
