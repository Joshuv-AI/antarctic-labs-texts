Function and defense mechanisms of wheat 14-3-3 protein TaGF14a in stripe rust resistance
Authors: Beibei Shi, Xiaolu Huang, Zhenqing Bai, Jiawen Wu, Qinggui Lian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs critical regulatory elements in plant signaling transduction, 14-3-3 proteins orchestrate defense responses against biotic and abiotic stresses. This study investigates the functional role of TaGF14a in wheat’s immune response to Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (Pst), providing novel genetic insights for stripe rust resistance breeding.ResultsPhylogenetic analysis revealed a close evolutionary relationship between TaGF14a and rice OsGF14a. Subcellular localization studies demonstrated that the protein was located in the in the nucleus, cytoplasm, and plasma membrane. Pathogen challenge experiments showed significant induction of TaGF14a expression following Pst inoculation. Virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) technology was used to silence TaGF14a, which resulted in expanded necrotic lesions and compromised fungal containment during incompatible interactions. Background

The 14-3-3 proteins, a conserved eukaryotic scaffold family, function as molecular rheostats by binding phosphorylated serine/threonine motifs on client proteins, thereby modulating their stability, localization, and activity [1, 2]. These dynamic interactions enable 14-3-3 proteins to integrate diverse signaling pathways, including growth regulation, stress adaptation, and immune responses [3]. Structurally, 14-3-3 s operate as obligate dimers, forming phospho-dependent interaction hubs that coordinate cellular networks through combinatorial associations with transcription factors (e.g., MYC/MYB families) [4] and stress-responsive kinases [5].

Emerging evidence positions 14-3-3 proteins as pivotal regulators of plant-pathogen interplay. Pathogen invasion triggers species- and context-dependent modulation of 14-3-3 expression. For example, Verticillium dahliae suppresses tomato 14-3-3 transcripts [6], while soybean cyst nematode infection upregulates homologs in soybean roots [7]. By binding phosphorylated residues in proteins, 14-3-3 proteins exert defense functions through immune regulation [3]. Mechanistically, 14-3-3 s amplify immune outputs by stabilizing resistance (R) proteins. In Arabidopsis, 14-3-3λ has been shown to interact with the resistance protein RPW8.2. Overexpression of 14-3-3λ triggers cell death and enhances powdery mildew resistance via a salicylic acid (SA)-dependent signaling pathway [8]. Both 14-3-3λ and κ are essential for phosphorylating MAPKKK and activating downstream MAPK cascades. These proteins directly interact with receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase (RLCK) PBL19 and MAPKKK5 to promote MAPK activation, thereby enhancing the plant’s immune response against pathogens [9]. Moreover, pathogen effector proteins have evolved to target 14-3-3 proteins for immune suppression [10]. For instance, the coat protein of necroviruses interacts with Nicotiana benthamiana 14-3-3α, reducing MAPKKKα activity. This mechanism inhibits host cell death initiation and facilitates viral replication [11]. Additionally, Phytophthora palmivora-secreted FIRE protein employs a type I recognition motif to interact with host 14-3-3 proteins, promoting successful infection [12]. These studies collectively highlight the multifaceted roles of 14-3-3 proteins in plant-pathogen interactions. Despite these advances, the functional landscape of 14-3-3 proteins in cereal-pathogen systems, particularly wheat, remains underexplored.

Wheat stripe rust, caused by Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (Pst), represents a persistent threat to global wheat production. While durable resistance breeding offers sustainable control, the molecular mechanisms underpinning wheat-Pst interactions remain fragmented [13]. Herein, we identify TaGF14a, a wheat 14-3-3 isoform transcriptionally induced during Pst challenge, as a critical immune coordinator. Through integrated molecular genetics and protein interactome analysis, we demonstrate a critical role for TaGF14a in mediating stripe rust resistance. Our findings expand our understanding of the multifunctional roles of plant 14-3-3 proteins, and also establish a novel framework for dissecting the genetic architecture underlying wheat resistance to stripe rust.

Results

Identification and sequence analysis of wheat TaGF14a gene

After cloning and obtaining this wheat 14-3-3 protein, a 786-bp DNA sequence was obtained, with an open reading frame (CDS) encoding a 262-amino acid protein. Phylogenetic analysis with 8 rice [14] and 12 Arabidopsis 14-3-3 proteins [15] revealed that the acquired 14-3-3 protein exhibited the closest evolutionary relationship with rice OsGF14a (Os08t0480800). Following the rice naming system, this protein was named TaGF14a (Fig. 1a). MODELLER prediction of the TaGF14a three-dimensional structure showed it composed of nine anti-parallel α-helices (Fig. 1b). Based on the SAVES evaluating, the fourth model is the optimal one (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/?job=318236). And Ramachandran plot of TaGF14a 3D structure was showed in Fig. 1c, with total number of residues being 261 and residues in most favored regions being 94.6%, which was reasonable.

Functional analysis of wheat TaGF14a. a Phylogenetic analysis of wheat TaGF14a. b The predicted of TaGF14a 3D structure. c The Ramachandran plot of TaGF14a 3D structure. d The localization of TaGF14a in Nicotiana benthamiana. Bars, 20 µm

For subcellular localization, TaGF14a was fused with GFP and expressed in tobacco cells. As shown in Fig. 1d, high GFP expression was observed in tobacco cells. Under green fluorescence, both the control (GFP alone) and GFP-tagged TaGF14a were detected in the nucleus, cell membrane, and cytoplasm, indicating broad subcellular distribution of TaGF14a.

TaGF14a is upregulated after stripe rust infection

Previous studies have established that 14-3-3 proteins play central roles in plant immunity [3]. In this study, to investigate the function of TaGF14a in wheat-stripe rust interaction, we analyzed its expression by qRT-PCR following inoculation with Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (Pst) (Fig. 2). The TaGF14a was induced in both incompatible- and compatible-interactions, with the highest relative expression reaching 12-fold compared with control in the incompatible interaction (wheat Su11 and Pst race 23) at 120 hpi, and 7.9-fold in the compatible interaction (wheat Su11 and Pst race 31) at the same time point. Notably, expression levels in the incompatible interaction were 2.1-fold (at 96 hpi) and 1.5-fold (at 120 hpi) higher than in the compatible interaction, indicating TaGF14a involvement in wheat resistance to stripe rust.

Silencing of TaGF14a reduces wheat resistance to stripe rust

Based on the increased TaGF14a expression during stripe rust infection, especially in the incompatible interaction, Barley stripe mosaic virus (BSMV) induced gene silence (VIGS) was used to knockdown TaGF14a. A 107-bp fragment starting at position 733 of the TaGF14a was cloned into the BSMV:γ vector. The target sequence for TaGF14a VIGS started from the non-coding 733 region and included 107-bp fragment of the TaGF14a open reading frame. FES buffer (Mock), BSMV:γ (negative control), and BSMV:γ-TaPDS (positive control) were inoculated onto the second leaves of Su11. Ten days after virus inoculation, the negative control and BSMV:γ-TaGF14a showed mild chlorotic mosaic symptoms, while the positive control yielded photobleaching, and Mock showed no symptoms, confirming the virus were successful inoculation (Fig. 3a). The fourth leaves of BSMV:γ-TaGF14a, BSMV:γ, and Mock plants were subsequently inoculated with CYR23 (incompatible) or CYR31 (compatible) races. At 15 dpi, Mock and BSMV:γ plants in the incompatible interaction displayed obvious necrotic area, and the necrotic areas in TaGF14a-silenced plants was more pronounced than in control plants. In the compatible interaction, all groups had uredia phenotypes, with TaGF14a-silenced plants producing more uredia (Fig. 3b). Quantitative analysis of TaGF14a expression at 0, 24, 48, and 120 hpi confirmed silencing efficiencies of 57–60% (incompatible interaction with CYR23) and 56–68% (compatible interaction with CYR31) relative to BSMV:γ controls (Fig. 3c).

To characterize stripe rust expansion in silenced plants, we quantified colony areas at 120 hpi with CYR23. Substomatal vesicles (SVs), a structure for successful infection of stripe rust, were observed in both TaGF14a-silenced and control plants (Fig. 3d). Calculation of hyphal expansion across 30 infection sites revealed an average area of 2395 µm2 in controls versus 4425 µm2 in silenced plants, demonstrating significantly enhanced fungal growth in the absence of TaGF14a (Fig. 3e).

Transcript analysis of resistance-related genes (TaPR1, TaPR3) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging genes (TaCAT, TaSOD) further validated the phenotype. The TaPR1 expression was significantly downregulated at 24, 48, and 120 hpi, while TaPR3 was significantly reduced at 24 hpi during the interaction between TaGF14a-silenced plants and CYR23 compared with control (Fig. 4a). Conversely, TaCAT and TaSOD transcription was significantly induced at 24 and 120 hpi in silenced plants (Fig. 4b), suggesting that TaGF14a silencing impairs wheat resistance to stripe rust by disrupting both defense signaling and ROS homeostasis.

Transient overexpression of TaGF14a does not induce cell death or suppress BAX-induced necrosis

To validate the role of TaGF14a in disease resistance signaling, a PVX-based transient overexpression system in tobacco leaves was used to assess TaGF14a function in cell death elicitation. At 5 dpi, the cell death was observed at sites 1, 2, and 6 infiltrated with Bax, PVX + Bax, or TaGF14a + Bax, whereas no cell necrosis was detected at sites 3, 4, and 5 infiltrated with PVX vector, buffer, or TaGF14a alone (additional file 1: Fig. S1). These results indicated that TaGF14a overexpression in tobacco neither induced cell death nor inhibited Bax-triggered cell death.

TaGF14a expression level in response to hormone treatments and abiotic stresses

To investigate the signaling pathways involving TaGF14a, wheat Su11 seedlings were treated with salicylic acid (SA), methyl jasmonate (MeJA), ethylene (ET), and abscisic acid (ABA). qRT-PCR technology analysis detected TaGF14a transcript levels at 0, 0.5, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h post-treatment (hpt) with different exogenous hormones (Fig. 5a). TaGF14a were significantly reduced at 3 and 12 hpt under SA treatment. After MeJA treatment, the expression level of TaGF14a was the highest at 3 hpt, which was 3.3-fold expression peak than control. While ET treatment downregulated TaGF14a expression at 3 hpt. And ABA treatment had no significant effect on TaGF14a expression. These results indicated that TaGF14a responds to the positive regulation induced by the exogenous hormone MeJA.

The transcript levels of TaGF14a. a The transcription of TaGF14a after exogenous hormones treatment. b The transcription of TaGF14a after abiotic stresses treatment. Bars indicate the mean ± SD of three independent replicates. The asterisk (*) indicates a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05)

Four abiotic stress treatments, including salt (NaCl), drought (PEG 6000), low temperature (Cool, 4 °C), and high temperature (Hot, 37 °C), TaGF14a exhibited distinct expression patterns (Fig. 5b). PEG 6000, NaCl, and high temperature treatments downregulated TaGF14a at multiple time points, with significant reductions at 1, 3, and 12 hpt under PEG 6000, at 3 hpt under NaCl, and at 1, 3, 12, and 24 hpt under high temperature. In contrast, low temperature stress significantly upregulated TaGF14a expression at 6 and 12 hpt, reaching 3-fold and 2-fold higher than the control, respectively. These findings indicated that TaGF14a is involved in environmental stress responses and exhibits positive regulation under cool stress.

To clarify the signaling pathways of TaGF14a, TaGF14a was as a bait protein to screen interacting proteins via yeast two-hybrid. We identified 26 candidate proteins interacting with TaGF14a (Table 1). Among them, we identified and confirmed interaction between TaGF14a and xylanase inhibitor, cysteine proteinase 1, related to plant defense response; serine/threonine-protein phosphatase, LOG-type lectin S-receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase, receptor like protein kinase S.2, E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase XBOS36 and RGLG2 related to cell signaling; chlorophyll a-b binding protein, photosystem II protein D1, photosystem I reaction center subunit XI and ultraviolet-B receptor UVR8 related to photosynthesis.

Discussion

TaGF14a participates in stresses response through multiple pathways

The 14-3-3 proteins typically function as homo- or heterodimers, binding to phosphorylated target proteins via a conserved groove formed by nine α-helices in each monomer. This groove accommodates target proteins through specific amino acid residue interactions at its core [2]. In plants, 14-3-3 proteins exhibit functional and nomenclatural diversity; for example, they are termed general regulatory factors (GRFs) in Arabidopsis [15] and G-box factor 14-3-3 (GF14) in rice [14]. Previous study identified 17 putative 14-3-3 family members in wheat cultivar Chinese Spring [16]. In this study, we screened a wheat 14-3-3 protein, TaGF14a, which revealed the closest evolutionary relationship with rice OsGF14a. The subcellular localization results indicate that TaGF14a is located in the cell membrane, and cytoplasm, consistent with the localization of TaGF14b [17], suggesting conserved subcellular functions among wheat 14-3-3 family members.

Accumulating evidence highlights the critical role of 14-3-3 proteins in mediating plant responses to abiotic stresses across species, including rice, Arabidopsis thaliana, banana, and Brachypodium distachyon [1, 3, 18, 19]. These proteins modulate stomatal movement, root growth, hormone signaling, and metabolic pathways to counteract drought, salt, cold, and heavy metal stresses [20]. Our study demonstrated that TaGF14a expression was upregulated under low-temperature stress, aligning with findings in rice and grapevine where cold stress induced OsGF14b/c/d/e/f and VviGRF15/17/like2, respectively [14, 21]. This conservation suggests a conserved role for 14-3-3 proteins in cold stress adaptation across monocotyledonous species.

14-3-3 proteins act as key nodes in plant hormone signaling, including gibberellins [22], brassinosteroids [23], abscisic acid (ABA) [24], ethylene [25], jasmonic acid (JA), and salicylic acid (SA) [26, 27]. Studies have demonstrated that overexpression OsGF14b in rice enhanced plant resistance to panicle blast by upregulating JA biosynthesis genes while suppressing the expression of genes related to SA synthesis, which indicated that OsGF14b-mediated resistance is associated with both JA- and SA-dependent pathways [27]. Conversely, the expression of OsGF14f had been observed to positively regulate resistance to rice leaf blast and bacterial wilt through the SA-dependent signaling pathway. Moreover, the expression levels of SA pathway-related genes (PAL1, NH1, PR10a, and PR14) in OsGF1f overexpression plants were higher compared to control plants. Furthermore, following infection with the rice leaf blight pathogen, the endogenous SA level in OsGF14f overexpression plants was higher [26]. In this study, TaGF14a responds to the positive regulation induced by the exogenous hormone MeJA, and TaGF14a was significantly reduced under SA treatment, which TaGF14a-mediated resistance is associated with both JA- and SA-dependent pathways.

TaGF14a positively regulates disease resistance by directly or indirectly regulating the expression of oxidative stress response genes

The 14-3-3 proteins have been demonstrated to be intimately associated with plant disease resistance [28]. Evidence proved the significance of 14-3-3 proteins in PAMP-triggered oxidative bursts and stomatal immunity. The disruption of the interaction between 14-3-3 with virulence effectors like Pseudomonas syringae HopM1 compromises plant immunity by restoring pathogen virulence [29]. Similarly, in effector-triggered immunity (ETI), tomato 14-3-3 proteins TFT1 and TFT3 played central roles in Prf/PTO effector-mediated ETI, interacted with the NLR complex and MAPKKKα [28]. While pepper CaHDZ27 was regulated by the 14-3-3 protein CaTFT7, which functioned as a positive regulator in pepper immunity against Ralstonia solanacearum. In addition, CaTFT7 interacted with CaHDZ27 to promote the stability of CaHDZ27 and activate pepper immunity [4]. In summary, these studies have revealed the evolutionary significance of 14-3-3 proteins as central regulators of plant-pathogen interactions. Our study extends this paradigm to wheat, demonstrating that TaGF14a is indispensable for stripe rust resistance.

Hypersensitive response (HR), characterized by localized cell death and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, constitutes a hallmark of incompatible plant-pathogen interactions [30]. While transient expression assays in tobacco revealed that TaGF14a does not directly induce cell death, its functional necessity became evident through ROS homeostasis regulation. The dual role of 14-3-3 proteins in ROS regulation, promoting oxidative bursts during PAMP recognition while preventing excessive ROS accumulation, highlights their contextual specificity [29]. The Phytophthora infestans RxLR effector protein PITG06478 hijacked tobacco 14-3-3 proteins to inhibit plasma membrane H-ATPase activity and induce cell death [31]. Tomato 14-3-3 protein 7 (TFT7) interacted with MAPKKKa to positively regulate programmed cell death (PCD) [32]. Notably, the transcription levels of TaCAT and TaSOD increased in TaGF14a-silenced plants after inoculation stripe rust, correlating with accelerated ROS scavenging and compromised disease resistance. This finding suggests that TaGF14a directly or indirectly regulating the expression of oxidative stress response genes to affect the accumulation of ROS following pathogen infection, which are important in the process of plant resistance to pathogen infection.

TaGF14a synergistically regulates downstream genes expression through interactions with multiple proteins

As molecular scaffolds, 14-3-3 proteins integrate stress signals by modulating the stability, localization, and activity of phosphorylated client proteins [3]. In rice, OsGF14c positively regulates Magnaporthe oryzae resistance by maintaining the balance of the GRAS transcription factor OsSCL7 [33]. In order to investigate the way of TaGF14a participates in regulating cellular redox homeostasis and improving plant disease resistance, we identified 26 putative TaGF14a interactors using a yeast hybrid system. Proteins related to plant defense response and cell signaling were received our attention, including xylanase inhibitor, LOG-type lectin S-receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase, serine/threonine-protein phosphatase, receptor like protein kinase S.2, and E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase RGLG2 and XBOS36. And the expression patterns of these interactors, including xylanase inhibitor, LOG-type lectin S-receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase, receptor like protein kinase S.2, and E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase RGLG2 and XBOS36 were induced differently after stripe rust inoculation, with RGLG2 showing inverse transcriptional dynamics to TaGF14a, suggesting antagonistic regulation in immune homeostasis.

Ubiquitin, a post-translational modified protein, added to the target protein through a series of enzymatic reactions to regulate its function, which specifically attached to the target protein for degradation and regulate the abundance of the target protein. E3 ubiquitin ligase plays a crucial role in the ubiquitination process. For example, the binding of 14-3-3 to the components of E3 ubiquitin ligase ETO1/EOL, promoted degradation of 14-3-3 [34]. ETO1-like (EOL) proteins target ACS, a rate-limiting enzyme in the ethylene synthesis pathway, isoforms for degradation [35]. 14-3-3 ω interacted with AtACS7 on the cell membrane and in vitro, thereby enhancing enzyme stability and affecting ethylene synthesis [22]. Additionally, associated with the transcription factor WRI1, 14-3-3 prevents its degradation by shielding the binding site for E3 ubiquitin ligase [34]. The interaction of E3 ubiquitin ligase RGLG2 and TaGF14a, suggested that ubiquitin proteins affected the immunity function of TaGF14a through a similar mechanism, but the mechanism still needs further investigation. E3 ubiquitin ligase TaGW2 mediated the ubiquitination and degradation of both TaSnRK1γ and TaVPS24 to enhance stripe rust susceptibility in wheat [36]. The wheat RING-finger E3 ubiquitin ligase TaPIR1 negatively regulated Pst resistance by specifically ubiquitinating the TaHRP1 to regulate its stability. TaHRP1 overexpression plants improved disease resistance. TaPIR1 expression inhibited the downstream activation of TaHRP1 and TaHRP1-induced ROS accumulation [37].

During the invasion of pathogenic fungi, these fungi secrete xylanases, which break down xylans, destroys the integrity of wheat cell walls, and promote further fungal invasion and spread [38]. The xylanase inhibitors in wheat serve the critical function of neutralizing and inhibiting these exogenous xylanases [39]. Our findings revealed that the expression of wheat xylanase inhibitor was induced during early Pst infection, suggesting its involvement in the wheat Pst resistance response. In summary, TaGF14a may interact with different proteins, exert multiple biological functions, and participate in regulating wheat response to diseases resistance.

Conclusions

In summary, we successfully screened a 14-3-3 family gene TaGF14a, which is located in the nucleus, cell membrane, and cytoplasm. TaGF14a is upregulated under Pst infection and induced by MeJA and cool stress. Silencing TaGF14a significantly reduces wheat resistance to stripe rust, resulting in induction of the TaCAT and TaSOD genes involved in ROS scavenging. Meanwhile, 26 potential interacting proteins of TaGF14a were screened, which xylanase inhibitor and E3 ubiquitin ligase RGLG2 of these interacting proteins may synergistically function with TaGF14a in the wheat resistance to stripe rust signaling pathway.

Methods

Materials and treatments

The wheat (Triticum aestivum) cv. Suwon 11 (Su11) and Mingxian 169 were used and cultivated under 23 °C and a 14 h/10 h light/dark cycle. Su11, having the YrSu gene, is resistant to CYR23 which constitute an incompatible interaction, and reaction type is “0”); but highly susceptible to CYR31 which constitute a compatible interaction, and reaction type is “4”) [40]. Pst races CYR23 and CYR31 were grown on Mingxian 169, and fresh uredospores were collected for inoculation. The wheat plants inoculated with stripe rust uredospores were placed in a dark incubator at 10 °C and 90% humidity for 24 h, then transferred to a growth chamber set at 16 °C under a 16 h/8 h light/dark cycle. Nicotiana benthamiana plants were cultivated under 25 °C and a 14 h/10 h light/dark cycle [41].

Su11 leaves were inoculated with urediniospores of CYR23 and CYR31, and harvested at 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h post-inoculation (hpi) based on previous microscopic observations of the interactions between wheat and Pst [42, 43]. For hormones treatment, Su11 were sprayed with 100 mM ethylene (ET), 100 mM abscisic acid (ABA), 100 mM methyl-jasmonate (MeJA) and 2 mM salicylic acid (SA) and were sampled at 0, 0.5, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h post treatment (hpt). For abiotic stress treatments, wheat leaves were harvested at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h post treatment (hpt) [44, 45]. Each sample has three independent biological and technical replicates for qRT-PCR. After isolating total RNA and synthesizing first-strand cDNA, the qRT-PCR reaction system and protocol were performed using the TaKaRa TB Green Premix Ex TaqII kit (TaKaRa, Code: RR820A, Beijing, China). The TaEF-1a gene (accession number Q0303033) was as the house keeping gene in qRT-PCR analysis. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 with Duncan’s multiple range test (P < 0.05).

Gene cloning and bioinformatics analysis of TaGF14a

The TaGF14a gene sequence (accession number: TraesCS7D02G292400) was identified in the Ensembl Plants database (https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html). Full-length primers (TaGF14a-C-F/TaGF14a-C-R; additional file 1: Table S1) were designed with Primer Premier 6.0 and used for PCR amplification with wheat cDNA as the template. For phylogenetic analysis, 14-3-3 full-length protein sequences of Oryza sativa (accession numbers: Os03t0710800, Os08t0430500, Os02t0580300, Os04t0462500, Os08t0480800, Os01t0209200, Os11t0609600), Arabidopsis thaliana (accession numbers: AT1G22300.1, AT1G26480.1, AT1G34760.1, AT1G35160.2, AT1G78300.1, AT2G42590.3, AT3G02520.1, AT4G09000.2, AT5G10450.4, AT5G16050.1, AT5G38480.1, AT5G65430.3) and wheat TaGRF were aligned by ClustalW, and the maximum likelihood method in MEGA software (v.12.0, http://www.megasoftware.net/) was used to construct a phylogenetic tree. The MODELLER software (https://salilab.org/modeller/), which was installed based on Anaconda, and the installation code was “conda config – add channels salilab conda install modeler,” was used to predict the TaGF14a 3D structure [46]. Furthermore, to evaluate the stereochemistry of MODELLER, the SAVES (v6.0, https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/) [47] was used to evaluate the dihedral rationality of the models. And Ramachandran plot of TaGF14a 3D structure was also detected on the SAVES [48].

Subcellular localization of TaGF14a

The full-length ORF of TaGF14a (without the stop codon) was cloned into pCaMV35S::GFP vector via BamHI digestion and ligation. After successful detection, empty vector plasmid pCaMV35S::GFP and recombinant plasmid pCaMV35S:: TaGF14a-GFP were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. The recombinant bacteria were cultured to an OD600 of 0.6, resuspended in tobacco infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl2, pH 5.6), and supplemented with 200 μM acetosyringone. And the suspension was infiltrated into 4-week-old N. benthamiana leaves. After 48–72 h, GFP fluorescence were detected with a confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with a 488 nm filter (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) [4].

BSMV-mediated gene silencing

A silencing fragment of TaGF14a was cloned into the BSMV:γ vector. The recombinant plasmids (BSMV:γ-TaGF14a), the negative control (BSMV:γ), the positive control (BSMV:γ-TaPDS, carrying a fragment of wheat phytoene desaturase gene), and the other BSMV components (α and β) were linearized and performed using the RiboMAX™ Large Scale RNA Production Systems-T7. After diluted, 100 ng of each RNA (BSMV: α, β, and γ-gene) was mixed with 10.5 μL FES buffer for inoculation onto the second leaf of wheat seedlings, and total 72 wheat seedlings were inoculated with BSMV:γ-TaGF14a, 20 wheat seedlings were inoculated with FES buffer as the Mock blank control, 72 wheat seedlings were inoculated with BSMV: γ, and 10 wheat seedlings were inoculated with BSMV:γ-TaPDS [49].

Ten days post inoculation, when photobleaching appeared on leaves of the BSMV:γ-TaPDS, the fourth leaves of Mock, BSMV:γ, and BSMV:γ-TaGF14a plants were inoculated with CYR23 or CYR31. The method used for inoculation with CYR23 or CYR31 followed the protocols described by Shi et al. [41]. Leaf samples were collected at 0, 24, 48, and 120 h post inoculation with CYR23 or CYR31 for extracting RNA to detect the silencing efficiency of TaGF14a gene and analyze the expression levels of genes in plant resistance, such as pathogenesis-related genes (TaPR1: AJ007348.1, TaPR3: DQ090946) and reactive oxygen species scavenging genes (TaCAT: AF475100.1, TaSOD: AK453889.1). In addition, samples were taken and stained with WGA AF488 at 120 hpi for histological observation, and infection sites (n = 30) were imaged under a fluorescence microscope to quantify colony areas. Phenotypes were observed and recorded 15 days post inoculation (dpi). The experiments were repeated three times [41].

Transient overexpression of TaGF14a in tobacco

The full-length ORF of TaGF14a was cloned into the PVX106 vector, and the recombinant plasmid was transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. Recombinant bacteria were infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. Phenotypes were observed 5 dpi. Bax (a mouse cell death inducer) site was infiltrated Bax at 0 hpi and was as positive control for PVX106:TaGF14a; PVX106 was as blank control and buffer was as negative control. At 24 hpi, the same infiltration site was challenged with BAX at PVX106 + Bax, which was the positive control for PVX106:TaGF14a + Bax to assess TaGF14a-mediated cell death regulation [49]. The experiments were repeated three times.

Yeast two-hybrid analysis

The full-length ORF of TaGF14a was cloned into the pGBKT7 vector and transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae Y2H Gold using the Matchmaker™ Gold Yeast Two-Hybrid System User Manual (Takara, Dalian, China). The Nuclear Yeast Two-Hybrid Library Construction Kit (Genecreate, Wuhan, China) was used to construct a cDNA library derived from Pst-infected wheat, which screened for interacting proteins. After selection, PCR was carried out on the positive clones, and the products of PCR reaction were sent to Sangon Biotech to obtain DNA sequences, which were analyzed and compared using EnsemblPlants BLAST tool.
