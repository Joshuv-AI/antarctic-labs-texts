Interaction of genes and hormones associated with germination in onion seeds under accelerated aging conditions
Authors: Reza Kamaei, Mohammad Kafi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDue to their limited storage longevity, onion seeds are more prone to deterioration compared to many other vegetable seeds. Understanding the mechanisms that enhance seed viability and delay deterioration is essential. This study investigated the effects of aging on germination, along with biochemical, physiological, and molecular attributes, in three Iranian onion seed populations: Horand, Kazerun, and Zargan. The seeds were exposed to different aging conditions, including a control (non-aged seeds), three and six days of accelerated aging, and one year of natural aging.MethodologyA factorial experiment (3 × 4) was conducted in a completely randomized design with four replications to evaluate the effects of seed aging (control, 3-day and 6-day accelerated aging, and 1-year natural aging) on one onion cultivar (Zargan) and two landraces (Horand and Kazerun) collected from different climatic regions of Iran. Accelerated aging was performed at 40 °C and ~ 100% humidity. Germination traits, electrical conductivity, malondialdehyde (MDA), DPPH radical scavenging activity, total phenolic and flavonoid content, and endogenous levels of ABA and GA1 were measured. Gene expression (WRKY20, LIG4, GAI1, LOX2, NCED1, NCED5) was quantified using qRT-PCR. Data were analyzed by ANOVA and Duncan’s test using SAS and R software.ResultsThe findings revealed that membrane damage, malondialdehyde (MDA), phenol, and abscisic acid (ABA) levels increased in all three populations (Horand, Kazerun, and Zargan) with the highest increases observed in the Kazerun lot. In Kazerun, MDA increased by up to 225% and ABA by up to 192% under 6-day aging compared to the control. Electrolyte leakage also rose sharply in Kazerun, reaching a 99% increase. In terms of seed viability, the germination percentage in Kazerun decreased significantly—by 28%, 54%, and 41% under 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year aging, respectively—while the reduction in Horand was more moderate, with only 12% under 1-year aging. DPPH radical scavenging activity decreased by 36% and flavonoid content by 49% in Kazerun after 6-day aging, which were more pronounced reductions than in Horand and Zargan. Aging significantly reduced GA1 levels in all seed populations, with the sharpest decrease observed in Kazerun (up to 55% after 6 days). Gene expression analysis showed that the expression of WRKY20, LOX2, NCED1, and NCED5 in Kazerun increased by up to 2.34-, 7.33-, 4.85-fold, and 164%, respectively, compared to the control. These increases were higher than those in Horand and Zargan.ConclusionsThis study highlights the adverse effects of seed aging on onion seed quality, emphasizing the importance of both physiological traits and gene expression patterns in determining seed viability. The findings suggest that specific molecular markers and stress-responsive genes may serve as key indicators of seed deterioration. A better understanding of these mechanisms could contribute to the development of strategies for improving seed storage and extending seed longevity in onion and other crops. Further integrative research combining physiological, biochemical, and molecular approaches is recommended to elucidate the complex regulatory networks involved in seed aging.Keywords: Abscisic acid, Accelerated aging, Antioxidant capacity, Gene expression, Onion seed, WRKY20

Background

Seeds serve as the cornerstone of food security, genetic resource preservation, and the starting point of production in all plant species, representing a prerequisite for achieving high yields across diverse crops [1]. The role of seeds in food security extends beyond being a source of crop production; they are also vital tools for genetic resource sustainability and reducing dependence on agricultural imports. Seed deterioration is a major challenge in agriculture, particularly in less developed countries and geographical regions where seeds are exposed to high temperatures and relative humidity during ripening and storage [2]. For instance, in tropical regions where storage conditions are more challenging, seed losses significantly impact crop productivity. Even under optimal environmental conditions, seed deterioration can occur, posing a significant barrier to agriculture and germplasm conservation [3]. Factors such as natural oxidation and the degradation of seed reserves can exacerbate this process, even under favorable conditions.

Accelerated aging is recognized as a precise, cost-effective, and reliable test for evaluating seed storability. This test plays a pivotal role in understanding the cellular and biochemical mechanisms underlying seed deterioration and aging [4]. Seed deterioration impacts numerous cellular and metabolic processes, ultimately disrupting seed viability and vigor. These disruptions can adversely affect germination percentage and rate, seedling growth, and field performance [5]. A key factor initiating these events is oxidative stress and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [6]. Free radicals have the potential to damage essential structures, including cellular membranes, DNA, and proteins. Such processes can lead to chromosomal alterations in radicle cells, DNA damage in both dry and imbibed seeds and membrane degradation due to spontaneous or enzymatic lipid peroxidation [7].

Nevertheless, seeds, once dried, are equipped with various enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant compounds that protect them against reactive oxygen species (ROS) [8]. These antioxidants scavenge ROS generated during seed deterioration and regulate their levels during seedling development, thereby supporting germination and the early stages of seedling growth [6]. During seed deterioration, a decline in antioxidant activity is observed, which is attributed to reduced regulatory activities of related genes and protein metabolism [9]. This reduction in activity ultimately leads to the loss of seed viability and germination capacity [10]. In addition to antioxidants, plant hormones play a pivotal role in regulating seed aging and viability. Abscisic acid (ABA) generally increases during seed deterioration, promoting dormancy and inhibiting germination [11], while GA1 tends to decline, reducing the seed’s ability to break dormancy and initiate growth [12]. The balance between ABA and GA, along with their interaction with ROS and antioxidant systems, is critical in determining seed longevity and germination success [13].

The genetic characteristics of different plant cultivars can also influence the rate of reactions occurring during seed deterioration. The nutritional reserves acquired by seeds during development on the mother plant, play a significant role in this process [14]. Certain cultivars exhibit lower levels of deterioration compared to others [15].

Several genes have been implicated in regulating seed viability and the oxidative stress response during aging. WRKY20, a member of the WRKY transcription factor family, is involved in plant stress responses and regulation of defense-related genes, including those associated with antioxidant systems [16]. LIG4 (DNA ligase IV) is essential for the non-homologous end-joining pathway of DNA double-strand break repair, a mechanism particularly important during seed aging due to accumulated genomic damage [17]. GAI1, a DELLA protein, acts as a negative regulator in gibberellin (GA) signaling, influencing seed germination and dormancy release [18]. LOX2 (lipoxygenase 2) is associated with lipid peroxidation, a key indicator of membrane deterioration under oxidative stress conditions [19]. NCED1 and NCED5 encode 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenases, key enzymes in abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis, and play significant roles in seed dormancy, stress signaling, and the inhibition of germination under unfavorable conditions [20]. These genes were selected to explore their expression patterns under accelerated aging conditions and to elucidate their potential roles in seed deterioration processes.

For instance, in a study examining the effects of accelerated aging on three onion cultivars, antioxidant activity in the Kazerun landrace was significantly lower than in the Zargan cultivar and the Horand landrace, while the levels of free radicals were reported to be higher in Kazerun [21]. Additionally, in a study on natural seed deterioration in oat seeds stored for 6 and 12 months at 4 °C, germination and antioxidant activity significantly declined, while the rate of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and malondialdehyde content increased [22].

Onion seeds are known to exhibit relatively short storage longevity compared to other vegetable crops. Under ambient storage conditions (25–30 °C and 50–60% RH), their viability can decline sharply within 6–12 months, depending on cultivar and seed quality. Even under optimal conditions (5–10 °C and 30–40% RH), longevity rarely exceeds 1.5 to 2 years. Natural deterioration is often accelerated by high temperature, relative humidity, and seed moisture content, leading to membrane degradation, oxidative stress, and hormonal imbalance. The optimal growing conditions for onion include well-drained loamy soil, full sunlight, and cool to moderate temperatures during early growth stages (13–25 °C). Understanding the interaction between genetics and storage stress is therefore crucial for maintaining seed vigor and ensuring successful crop establishment [23].

The storage of onion seeds poses significant challenges due to their sensitivity to temperature and humidity. Therefore, identifying mechanisms that enhance seed resistance to deterioration is essential. The objectives of this study are as follows: (1) To determine the effect of accelerated aging on the levels of plant hormones in onion seeds, (2) To investigate the expression of key genes associated with germination under accelerated aging conditions and (3) To analyze the relationships among physiological traits, hormonal levels, and gene expression in onion seeds.

Results

Germination percentage

The results indicated that the germination percentage was significantly affected by the increase in aging duration, seed lot, and their interaction (p ≤ 0.001). Germination percentage decreased with increasing aging time, with the most significant reduction observed in seeds subjected to 6-day accelerated aging (Fig. 1). For the Kazerun lot, the germination percentage in seeds subjected to 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging decreased by 28%, 54%, and 41%, respectively, compared to the control treatment. In the Horand lot, the germination percentage decreased by 16%, 22%, and 12% under 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging, respectively, compared to the control. In contrast, for the Zargan cultivar, the germination percentage decreased by 18%, 40%, and 6% under 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging, respectively, compared to the control.

Germination percentage of HOR (genotypes), ZAR (cultivar) and KAZ (genotypes) onion landraces under different levels of aging. [AA 3 d, AA6 d and A natural refer to three-days aging, six-days aging, and one-year natural aging, respectively]

Electrolyte leakage

The data related to electrolyte leakage were analyzed using analysis of variance, which revealed significant effects of seed aging, seed populations, and their interaction (p ≤ 0.001). Electrolyte leakage increased at all levels of aging, with the most significant increase observed in the Kazerun lot. Specifically, in Kazerun, electrolyte leakage increased by 33%, 99%, and 9% in the 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging treatments, respectively, compared to the control. Similarly, in the Zargan cultivar, electrolyte leakage increased by 31%, 71%, and 2% in the 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging treatments, respectively, compared to the control. However, there was no significant difference in electrolyte leakage across aging treatments in the Horand lot. These results indicate that electrolyte leakage is influenced by both aging and seed lot, with Kazerun being the most sensitive to aging-induced changes in electrolyte leakage (Fig. 2). In this study, a negative correlation between electrolyte leakage and germination percentage was observed (Fig. 15).

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content

The results indicated that seed aging, seed populations, and their interaction had a significant effect on the MDA content in the seeds. MDA levels increased with the aging treatments, and this increase was more pronounced in the Kazerun lot compared to the Zargan and Horand populations. Specifically, in Kazerun, MDA levels increased by 67%, 225%, and 93% in the 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging treatments, respectively, compared to the control. In contrast, in Horand, MDA levels increased by 30%, 140%, and 50% compared to the control. These findings suggest that the Horand seed lot may be more resilient to age-induced changes in MDA content compared to the other two seed populations (Fig. 3).

MDA of HOR (genotypes), ZAR (cultivar) and KAZ (genotypes) onion landraces under different aging levels [AA 3d, AA6 d and A natural refer to three-days aging, six-days aging, and one-year natural aging, respectively]

DPPH

The effects of aging, seed populations, and their interaction (p ≤ 0.001) on the DPPH levels in the seeds were significant. The DPPH levels decreased across all aging treatments. In Kazerun, the DPPH levels decreased by 17%, 36%, and 11% in the 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging treatments, respectively, compared to the control. In Horand, DPPH levels decreased by 12.5%, 26.4%, and 8% in the 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging treatments, respectively, while in Zargan, the reductions were 15%, 31%, and 7%, respectively (Fig. 4). In this study, the DPPH levels showed a positive correlation with the germination percentage (Fig. 15).

Phenolic content

The phenolic levels in the seeds were significantly influenced by the aging treatments, seed populations, and their interaction (p ≤ 0.001). As the aging period increased, the phenol content in the seeds also increased, with the most noticeable effect observed in the Kazerun lot. In Horand, the phenol content increased by 5% under natural aging and by 4% and 15% under the 3-day and 6-day accelerated aging treatments, respectively, compared to the control seeds. In Zargan, the increase in phenol levels was 6%, 15%, and 4% for the 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year aging treatments, respectively. In contrast, Kazerun seeds had 12%, 19%, and 9% higher phenol content compared to the control seeds under the corresponding aging conditions (Fig. 5).

Flavonoids

The analysis of variance results showed that aging, seed populations, and their interaction significantly affected the flavonoid content. The application of different aging treatments led to a decrease in flavonoid levels. Horand had higher flavonoid content than Zargan and Kazerun at all aging levels. In Kazerun, flavonoid content decreased by 31%, 49%, and 33% for the 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging treatments, respectively, compared to the control. However, in Horand, flavonoid levels decreased by 23%, 35%, and 17%, and in Zargan, the decreases were 13%, 41%, and 23% for the 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging treatments, respectively (Fig. 6). A positive correlation was observed between flavonoid content and germination percentage (Fig. 15).

ABA

The levels of Abscisic Acid hormone in the seeds were significantly influenced by aging, seed populations, and their interaction (p ≤ 0.001). The ABA content in the seeds increased with the aging process in all seed populations, but Kazerun showed a greater increase compared to Zargan and Horand. The Horand seed lot exhibited a 90%, 161%, and 42% increase in ABA under 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging conditions, respectively. On the other hand, Zargan showed increases of 100%, 171%, and 31% compared to the control for the same aging treatments. The Kazerun seed lot also showed a significant increase in ABA, with increases of 135%, 192%, and 178% compared to the control (Fig. 7). A positive correlation was observed between seed ABA content and electrolyte leakage, while a negative correlation was found between ABA and germination percentage (Fig. 15).

Gibberellin A1 (GA1)

The effects of aging, seed populations, and their interaction (p ≤ 0.001) on the levels of GA1 in the seeds were significant. The GA1 content in the seeds decreased under different aging conditions. In Kazerun, the GA1 level under 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging conditions decreased by 36%, 55%, and 30%, respectively, compared to the control. In Horand, the GA1 level decreased by 11%, 37%, and 5%, while in Zargan, it decreased by 27%, 47%, and 20% under the same aging treatments (Fig. 8). The GA1 levels in this study had a positive correlation with the germination percentage (Fig. 15).

The expression of WRKY20 in Horand increased by 1.75, 2.46, and 1.29 times, and in Zargan, it increased by 1.88, 5.76, and 1.74 times, under 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging conditions, respectively, compared to the control. In Kazerun, the expression of WRKY20 increased by 2.01, 2.34, and 1.83 times under 3-day, 6-day, and 1-year natural aging conditions, respectively, compared to the control (Fig. 9).

Schematic overview of molecular and physiological pathways involved in seed deterioration under accelerated aging conditions

The schematic model illustrates the molecular and physiological mechanisms underlying seed deterioration during accelerated aging. Aging stress initiates oxidative stress through excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which in turn triggers lipid peroxidation and membrane damage, leading to increased levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and electrolyte leakage. This process is further exacerbated by upregulated expression of LOX2, a gene involved in catalyzing lipid peroxidation. Concurrently, a significant reduction in antioxidant capacity—including diminished DPPH scavenging activity, phenolic compounds, and flavonoids—is associated with downregulation of WRKY20, a key transcription factor in antioxidant defense pathways. Hormonal regulation is also disrupted: the biosynthesis of abscisic acid (ABA) is enhanced via increased expression of NCED1 and NCED5, which contributes to inhibition of germination by suppressing hydrolytic enzyme activity. Simultaneously, a decline in gibberellin (GA1) levels—potentially linked to the activity of GAI, a repressor of GA signaling—further impairs seed germination and vigor. Additionally, reduced expression of LIG4, a gene essential for DNA repair, indicates the accumulation of genotoxic stress. Collectively, these changes culminate in a marked decrease in seed germination and viability (Fig. 16).

Discussion

Seed storability is a critical agronomic and physiological trait in crop production. It has been demonstrated that seed storability is regulated by genetic and environmental factors; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear [24]. High-vigor seeds typically exhibit significant advantages in germination and subsequent seedling growth. Various studies have reported that aging treatments significantly reduce seed germination percentage, germination potential, germination index, and vigor index [25, 26]. The findings revealed that accelerated aging conditions had pronounced adverse effects on germination traits, such as the germination percentage of onion seeds. These alterations are likely attributable to structural damage to membranes and the degradation of seed energy reserves caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS) [6]. Furthermore, the reduction in germination percentage was closely associated with increased electrical conductivity and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, indicating that elevated levels of these two parameters lead to diminished membrane integrity and consequently reduced germination rates. Both electrical conductivity and malondialdehyde concentrations were significantly elevated in seeds subjected to accelerated aging. These indicators reflect structural and functional membrane damage and can serve as key markers of seed deterioration under adverse conditions [27, 28]. The results suggest that membrane damage induced by lipid peroxidation and electrolyte leakage could be a major factor contributing to reduced germination capacity. These findings on onion seed storability are consistent with similar studies in model plants such as Arabidopsis and important crops like rice, where seed longevity is also influenced by both genetic and environmental factors. However, species-specific differences in seed composition and physiology may lead to variations in the underlying molecular mechanisms regulating seed aging.

The increase in electrical conductivity reflects ion leakage caused by cellular membrane damage. Lipid peroxidation, triggered by reactive oxygen species (ROS), leads to membrane degradation and enhanced electrolyte leakage [29]. In this study, we analyzed the expression of the LOX2 gene in onion and found that its expression is significantly associated with seed longevity. Lipid oxidation has long been proposed as a major factor contributing to seed deterioration. Supporting this hypothesis, changes in membrane lipids and stored fats, along with increased oxidation products, have been reported in Arabidopsis during both natural and accelerated aging. With aging, oil content, as well as the total amount of fatty acids and phospholipids in Arabidopsis seeds, decreases, whereas levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), lipid peroxides, and oxidized triacylglycerols increase [30]. Moreover, the increase in MDA, a primary product of lipid peroxidation, serves as an indicator of oxidative stress and membrane damage [31]. This finding suggests that under seed accelerated aging conditions, ROS production increases while the seed’s antioxidant system becomes inefficient. Our results demonstrated that in the Kazerun landrace, MDA levels increased by 67%, 225%, and 93% compared to the control after three days, six days, and one year of natural aging, respectively. Similarly, Małecka et al. [32] reported that elevated MDA levels and electrical conductivity in aged beech seeds directly correlate with reduced seed quality. Similar patterns of LOX2 upregulation and lipid peroxidation have been reported in Arabidopsis and rice seeds under aging conditions [30, 33]. Nevertheless, the extent of lipid peroxidation and its impact on seed viability can vary among species, likely due to differences in seed oil content and antioxidant capacity, highlighting both conserved and species-specific aspects of seed deterioration mechanisms.

DPPH activity level is a critical indicator for evaluating the antioxidant capacity of seeds, particularly under aging conditions. Seed aging leads to increased production of free radicals and oxidative stress, which reduce germination vigor. The DPPH activity, representing the seed’s ability to neutralize free radicals, significantly decreased in seeds subjected to accelerated aging. This decline in DPPH activity may result from the heightened production of ROS, which rapidly depletes antioxidant reserves and exacerbates oxidative stress. Moreover, the reduction in DPPH activity is directly correlated with increased MDA levels and electrical conductivity. These changes signify the degradation of antioxidant metabolites and intensification of membrane damage [34]. On the other hand, phenols and flavonoids act as key antioxidants in mitigating oxidative stress. In this experiment, a significant reduction in total phenol and flavonoid content was observed in aged seeds. This decline could be attributed to their structural degradation or rapid consumption in neutralizing ROS. A study by Önder et al. [4] demonstrated that the reduction of flavonoids in safflower (Carthamus tinctorius L.) seeds under accelerated aging was associated with increased ROS production and diminished antioxidant activity.

Abscisic acid (ABA) acts as a negative regulator by inhibiting germination-related processes, whereas gibberellins (GAs) play a pivotal role in activating hydrolytic enzymes that break down the seed coat and initiate embryo growth [35, 36]. The results of this study revealed that accelerated aging led to increased ABA levels and decreased GA1 levels in onion seeds. These hormonal changes may be associated with the upregulation of genes involved in ABA biosynthesis. Conversely, a potential downregulation of genes that inhibit the gibberellin pathway might hinder the positive effects of GA1 on germination. These findings align with the study by Chen et al. [37], which reported that elevated ABA levels in seeds subjected to accelerated aging inhibit the activity of hydrolytic enzymes. Additionally, the results of Wang et al. [38] demonstrated that reduced GA1 levels under stress conditions lead to a lower germination percentage, further corroborating our data. Comparable hormonal responses involving elevated ABA levels and reduced GA1 concentrations during seed aging have been documented in Arabidopsis and various cereal crops [37, 38]. While the core hormonal regulatory pathways appear conserved, differences in seed sensitivity to these hormones suggest species-specific adaptations in germination regulation under stress.

The NCED1 and NCED5 genes are key regulators in the biosynthesis pathway of abscisic acid (ABA) [39]. These genes encode the enzyme 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase, which is the rate-limiting step in ABA synthesis [40]. Under accelerated aging conditions, the increased expression of these genes leads to elevated ABA levels, which act as a negative regulator of the germination process. ABA inhibits the activity of hydrolytic enzymes (such as alpha amylase), preventing seed coat rupture [41]. Results of our study found that, as aging levels increased, the expression of NCED1 and NCED5 significantly increased. Moreover, the elevated expression of NCED1 and NCED5 in this study was clearly associated with a reduction in germination percentage. These changes may also lead to reduced energy metabolism and increased oxidative damage, which can be observed through electrical conductivity and MDA levels. A study by Ruan et al. [42] showed that overexpression of NCED5 in Arabidopsis resulted in increased ABA levels, which could reduce germination capacity.

The GAI1 gene is a negative regulator in the gibberellin (GA) signaling pathway [43]. This gene belongs to the DELLA family and inhibits processes such as the activation of hydrolytic enzymes and embryo growth by preventing GA1 activity [18]. Under accelerated aging conditions, the potential increase in GAI1 expression could block the positive effects of GA1 on germination, leading to a delay in the germination process [44]. The results of this experiment showed that the expression level of the GAI1 gene was higher in the control treatment compared to the various aging levels. Research by Shu et al. [45] indicated that the overexpression of DELLA genes under salt stress suppresses GA1 activity, leading to a decrease in soybean seed germination. Additionally, the LOX2 gene encodes lipoxygenase, an enzyme crucial in the lipid peroxidation pathway [46]. This process leads to the production of oxidative products such as malondialdehyde (MDA), which is an indicator of membrane damage [47]. A study by Djian et al. [33] also emphasized that LOX2 is directly involved in membrane lipid degradation and the increase of MDA, which aligns with our findings showing that as aging levels increased, the expression of LOX2 was significantly elevated. The upregulation of LOX2 during accelerated aging is associated with increased lipid peroxidation. LOX2 catalyzes the dioxygenation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), leading to hydroperoxides that degrade into MDA. Our data showed a strong positive correlation (p < 0.001) between LOX2 transcript levels and MDA accumulation, supporting the hypothesis that LOX2 activity is a key contributor to oxidative membrane damage during seed aging.

The WRKY20 gene belongs to the WRKY transcription factor family, which plays a crucial role in regulating responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses, including oxidative stress [48]. This gene specifically contributes to the regulation of antioxidant pathways and the production of secondary metabolites, such as flavonoids and phenols [49]. The potential reduction in WRKY20 expression under aging conditions could explain the decreased antioxidant activity (DPPH) and the degradation of phenolic and flavonoid compounds. This results in reduced seed resistance to oxidative stress, thereby diminishing seed quality. A study by Luo et al. [50] demonstrated that WRKY20 expression significantly increased antioxidant capacity in Arabidopsis plants subjected to oxidative stress, helping to mitigate ROS-induced damage, which aligns with our findings. Although functional validation of WRKY20 through knockdown or overexpression was not conducted in this study, its increased expression was accompanied by significant changes in antioxidant enzyme activities (e.g., SOD, CAT). Previous studies have shown that WRKY transcription factors, including WRKY20, act as positive regulators of stress-responsive genes in various species (e.g., Arabidopsis, rice) [51, 52]. Therefore, we hypothesize that WRKY20 may play a similar regulatory role in onions, possibly by activating antioxidant defense pathways during seed aging. Additionally, the LIG4 gene encodes DNA ligase, an enzyme involved in repairing double-strand breaks in DNA [53]. LIG4 activity could play a critical role in reducing the negative effects of accelerated aging [54]. In the present study, the reduction in LIG4 expression under aging conditions was consistent with decreased onion seed germination, likely due to the accumulation of DNA damage. A study by Waterworth et al. [55] emphasized that reduced LIG4 activity in seeds under stress leads to diminished germination vigor, as DNA damage remains unrepaired.

Genotypic differences in response to accelerated aging were clearly evident in this study. Among the three onion genotypes, Horand demonstrated the highest resilience to aging, as evidenced by its relatively stable germination percentage, lower membrane damage, and higher antioxidant capacity (e.g., DPPH and flavonoid content) under stress. In contrast, Kazerun exhibited the most significant deterioration across all measured traits, suggesting a higher sensitivity to oxidative damage and hormonal imbalance. Zargan showed intermediate behavior. The expression levels of stress-responsive genes such as WRKY20, LOX2, NCED1, and NCED5 were also markedly higher in Kazerun, potentially reflecting an overactive stress signaling pathway leading to germination inhibition. These findings highlight the existence of genotype-dependent mechanisms regulating seed longevity, which may stem from differences in genetic background, seed developmental history, or basal antioxidant systems.

The findings of this study provide valuable insights for practical applications in seed storage management and onion breeding programs. The identified molecular markers—such as LOX2, WRKY20, NCED1, NCED5, GAI1, and LIG4—can be used as potential indicators to monitor seed viability during storage. For example, stable expression of WRKY20 and LIG4, alongside lower expression of LOX2 and ABA-related genes, may indicate better seed storability. These markers can aid in early detection of seed deterioration and help optimize storage conditions. Moreover, breeding programs may utilize this molecular knowledge to select or engineer genotypes with enhanced antioxidant capacity, hormonal balance, and DNA repair potential, thereby improving seed longevity and vigor under aging or suboptimal storage conditions.

Conclusion

Given the global importance of onion, conducting screening to identify varieties and populations resistant to seed decay, as well as understanding the mechanisms by which seeds can withstand specific storage conditions such as high temperature and humidity, is of great significance. Our study revealed that onion seed storage conditions resulted in a decrease in DPPH, flavonoids, GA1, and the expression of the GAI1 gene, while the levels of electrolyte leakage, MDA, phenol, abscisic acid, and the expression of the WRKY20, LIG4, LOX2, NCED1, and NCED5 genes were increased. The indigenous onion varieties of Iran exhibited varying responses to different levels of aging, which were reflected in molecular, biochemical, and physiological changes, such as antioxidant enzymes, oxidative stress markers, free radicals, hormone concentrations, and electrolyte leakage. However, our findings suggest that the Kazerun population is more sensitive to storage conditions than the Horand population and the Zargan cultivar, primarily due to the increased expression of the WRKY20, LIG4, LOX2, NCED1, and NCED5 genes. This leads to a decrease in GA1 levels and an increase in abscisic acid. Therefore, the Horand and Zargan populations demonstrate greater resistance to storage conditions than Kazerun. Molecular processes related to onion seed aging under different storage conditions have not yet been fully identified. To address these uncertainties, conducting comprehensive genomic studies is recommended. Additionally, investigating the role of priming treatments in mitigating the effects of seed aging and their impact on the regulation of related gene expression could be a valuable subject for future research.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and experimental design

This experiment was conducted in the Plant Physiology Laboratory at Ferdowsi University of Mashhad. A factorial experiment (3 × 4) was carried out in a completely randomized design (CRD) with four replications to investigate the effects of different aging levels (control, 3-day accelerated aging, 6-day accelerated aging, and 1-year natural aging) on the Zargan cultivar and the Horand and Kazerun landraces of onion. The selection of the Zargan cultivar alongside the Horand and Kazerun landraces for this study is grounded in their distinct genetic backgrounds and differential responses to seed aging and storage conditions. Zargan, a widely cultivated commercial cultivar, is recognized for its relatively greater seed longevity and vigor under typical storage environments. Conversely, the Horand and Kazerun landraces represent local germplasm adapted to unique agroclimatic zones, exhibiting notable variation in antioxidant capacity and susceptibility to oxidative stress during seed aging. Previous research has indicated that the Kazerun landrace, in particular, accumulates higher levels of reactive oxygen species and shows reduced antioxidant defenses compared to Zargan and Horand, rendering these genotypes valuable models for exploring the molecular and physiological mechanisms underlying seed deterioration [21]. Including these diverse genotypes enables a comprehensive assessment of genetic and biochemical factors influencing onion seed viability under accelerated aging conditions. As a released cultivar, Zargan was obtained from Falat Seed Company that has the license of onion seed production, while Horand and Kazerun landraces were kindly offered by the seed bank of the Plant Sciences Research Institute, Ferdowsi University of Mashhad, Iran, with codes MOC101 and MOC102, respectively. The onion landraces used in this study were collected from two geographically distinct regions of Iran with contrasting climatic conditions. The Horand landrace was sourced from Horand, located in northwestern Iran (38.8631° N, 47.3667° E), characterized by a semi-arid, moderately temperate climate with a 30-year average annual temperature of approximately 14.5 °C and an average annual precipitation of around 290 mm. In contrast, the Kazerun landrace was collected from Kazerun in southern Iran (29.6190° N, 51.6540° E), which experiences a warmer and relatively more humid climate, with an average annual temperature of 21.8 °C and precipitation of approximately 448 mm.

Seed aging conditions

To simulate seed deterioration under stress, accelerated aging treatments were applied using a saturated humidity method. For each treatment, seeds were placed in a single layer on a fine mesh tray, suspended above 40 mL of distilled water inside a sealed plastic container (to maintain ~ 100% relative humidity). The containers were incubated at 40 °C in a temperature-controlled chamber for either 3 or 6 days depending on the treatment. The moisture content was 100% immediately after the aging treatment. After the aging period, seeds were removed and air-dried at room temperature (25 °C) for 24 h to restore moisture content to approximately 6% before further analysis [44]. The control treatment consisted of freshly harvested seeds whose germination was assessed immediately after harvest. Until germination testing, the seeds were stored under optimal conditions (6% seed moisture content and 5 °C) to preserve viability. In contrast, the naturally aged seeds were stored for one year under ambient room temperature (approximately 25 °C) with the same moisture content (6%). For each treatment, 25 seeds were placed in 9 cm Petri dishes on Whatman No. 42 filter paper, moistened with 5 mL of distilled water. Subsequently, a standard germination test was performed in accordance with the rules of the International Seed Testing Association (ISTA), conducted in a germinator set at 20 °C, 60% relative humidity, and dark conditions for 12 days [56]. Germination was defined as the emergence of a radicle at least 2 mm in length.

Trait measurements

Germination characteristics

The total germination percentage was calculated using the following formula:

Ni = Number of germinated seeds and N = Total number of seeds in the Petri dish.

Electrical conductivity

The electrical conductivity was measured according to the guidelines of the International Seed Testing Association [56], and the electrical conductivity per gram of seed for each sample was calculated using the following formula:

W = Weight of the seed sample in grams, ECs = Electrical conductivity of the seed sample in microsiemens per centimeter, ECc = Electrical conductivity of the water (control sample) in microsiemens per centimeter and EC = Electrical conductivity obtained per gram of seed in microsiemens per centimeter per gram.

Physiological traits

To investigate the physiological traits involved in germination, seeds at the physiological germination stage (prior to radicle emergence) were used.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content

Malondialdehyde was measured using the method of Stewart and Bewley [57]. The assay is based on the reaction of malondialdehyde produced with thiobarbituric acid (TBA), forming a colored MDA-TBA complex, and its concentration was measured using a spectrophotometer.

DPPH measurement

DPPH radical scavenging activity was determined using the method of Abe et al. [58] with a standard ascorbic acid solution. The reaction mixture included 25 µL of 90% ethanol extract and 1 mM DPPH solution. Absorbance at 517 nm was read after 30 min of incubation in the dark, and DPPH radical scavenging was reported in milligrams per gram dry weight.

Total phenolic content

Total phenolic content (TPC) was measured using the Folin-Ciocalteu method [59] with a standard gallic acid solution. In this method, 25 µL of 96% methanolic extract was reacted with Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (FC), 20% Na2CO3, and ddH2O in the dark for 30 min. Absorbance was recorded at 765 nm, and TPC was calculated in milligrams per gram dry weight. Total flavonoid content (TFC) was measured using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method [60], with quercetin (QR) as the standard solution. In this method, 70% ethanol extract of seeds, methanol (MeOH), 2% AlCl3, 1 M potassium acetate (CH3CO2K), and ddH2O were reacted at room temperature for one hour. Absorbance was read at 415 nm, and TFC was calculated in milligrams per gram dry weight.

Quantification of endogenous ABA and GA1 levels

ABA and GA1 were extracted and purified from onion seeds that reached the physiological germination stage. This process was carried out using the methods described by Son et al. [61] and Izydorczyk et al. [62]. For each landrace and cultivar, four biological replicates were considered.

Purification and quantification of ABA and GA1 levels was performed using a Liquid Chromatography-Electrospray Ionization Mass Spectrometry (LC-ESI-MS/MS) system (Agilent 1260–6430, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA). The protocol for this step was based on the method outlined by Yoshimoto et al. [63].

Gene expression

RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from seed samples using the protocol described by Chang et al. [64] with minor modifications. Approximately 100 mg of finely ground seed tissue was homogenized in 1 mL of preheated extraction buffer containing 2% CTAB, 2% PVP, 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 25 mM EDTA, 2 M NaCl, and 2% β-mercaptoethanol. The homogenate was incubated at 65 °C for 10 min, then extracted twice with an equal volume of chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (24:1). The aqueous phase was collected and RNA was precipitated by adding 0.25 volume of 10 M LiCl, incubated overnight at 4 °C. The RNA pellet was obtained by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 20 min, washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and finally dissolved in RNase-free water. RNA purity and concentration were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometry.

To synthesize cDNA, 2 µg of RNA was placed into a tube, and 1 µL of Oligo DT (5 mM) and 2 µL of dNTP (5 mM) were added, bringing the total volume to 13 µL. The tubes were incubated for 5 min at 65 °C, followed by 1 min on ice. After centrifuging for 5 s, 4 µL of FSB buffer1, 1 µL of each of DTT2 (0.1 mM), RNase Out (1 mM), and SSIII3 (1 mM) were added. The reaction was carried out at 50 °C for 60 min, followed by 15 min at 70 °C. Then, 0.5 µL of RNase H was added to the tubes, and the reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Finally, MQ H2O4 was added to reach a final volume of 100 µL, completing the cDNA synthesis.

Q-RT PCR analysis

For qRT-PCR, the cDNA synthesized in the previous step was diluted 1:10. The PCR reaction mixture consisted of 10 µL of SYBR Green qRT-PCR Master Mix (Invitrogen), 10 µL of diluted cDNA, and 1 µL of primers. Primers were designed using Primer 3 and Oligo Analyzer software, and their sequences are listed in Table 1. The Q-RT PCR was performed using the Rotor-Gene Q (Qiagen) device. The Q-RT PCR protocol included an initial 4-minute denaturation at 94 °C, followed by 50 cycles of 10 s at 95 °C, 15 s at 60 °C, and 20 s at 72 °C. After each cycle, a melt curve program (ranging from 72 °C to 95 °C, with a 5-second pause at each temperature) was performed, and fluorescence data was recorded at 72 °C during the melt curve. Gene expression levels were normalized to the reference gene and analyzed using comparative quantitative analysis with Rotor-Gene Q software. A control treatment, where water was used instead of the sample, was included to ensure that primer dimerization did not affect the results. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method, and StepOne Software v2.3 (Applied Biosystems) was used for initial Ct value analysis. Statistical analysis of gene expression data was performed using GraphPad Prism 9. The data presented are the averages of four biological replicates, and the standard error was used to compare differences.
