IGF1R deficiency mitigates acute lung injury by promoting anti-inflammatory transcriptional profiles
Authors: Alfredo Urtubia, Sergio Piñeiro-Hermida, Elvira Alfaro-Arnedo, Judith Beni-Ledesma, Marta Canalejo, María de Toro, José García Pichel, Icíar P López
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute lung injury (ALI), acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and COVID-19 are characterized by hyperinflammation, commonly referred to as “cytokine storm”. The insulin-like growth factor (IGF) pathway, particularly the type 1 receptor (IGF1R), plays a critical role in lung homeostasis and has been implicated in the pathogenesis of pulmonary inflammatory diseases. In mice, widespread Igf1r deficiency attenuates lung inflammation and alveolar damage in bleomycin (BLM)-induced ALI.MethodsWe analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing datasets from lung tissue of COVID-19 cases and control donors as well as mouse lungs to determine Igf1r and IGF family expression across pulmonary cell types. Furthermore, we conducted bulk RNA sequencing on lungs from Igf1r-deficient mice three days after BLM or saline instillation, followed by differential expression and functional enrichment analyses. Findings were further tested through protein detection, assessment of DNA damage and methylation in lung tissues, and functional assays using Igf1r-deficient primary mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs).ResultsIGF1R was broadly expressed across multiple cell types in both human and mouse lungs under normal and pathological conditions. Other IGF family members showed cell-type-specific expression, which was modulated by lung injury. Transcriptomic profiling revealed differentially expressed genes between BLM-challenged and control mouse lungs, detecting biological processes and signaling pathways involved in ALI pathobiology. Igf1r deficiency in BLM-challenged mice reversed a large fraction of the transcriptional changes triggered by BLM, including “cytokine storm”-related gene expression. Functional enrichment analysis additionally revealed significant modulation of pathways related to DNA damage, metabolic reprogramming, mitochondrial homeostasis, and epigenetic regulation. In vitro, Igf1r-deficient MEFs exhibited decreased mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis, protection against BLM-induced nuclear damage and mitochondrial accumulation, and decreased histone H3 acetylation. Moreover, Igf1r-deficient mouse lungs displayed increased global DNA methylation following BLM challenge.ConclusionsIGF1R is a key modulator of the inflammatory and molecular response to ALI pathogenesis. IGF1R deficiency dampens the “cytokine storm”, modifies transcriptional and epigenetic profiles and promotes protective cellular responses. These findings highlight IGF1R signaling as a potential therapeutic target in ARDS and related lung injuries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03339-x.Keywords: Acute lung injury, IGF1R, Bleomycin mouse model, Cytokine storm, Epigenetics

Background

Inflammation is a critical factor in many respiratory diseases including acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and coronavirus disease-19 (COVID-19) [1, 2]. ARDS is primarily caused by acute and diffuse inflammatory damage of the alveolar-capillary barrier, which leads to pulmonary edema and reduced lung compliance, thus compromising gas exchange and causing refractory hypoxemia [3]. ARDS has been strongly associated with severe COVID-19 pneumonia cases, especially those that require mechanical ventilation [4]. In this regard, it has been proposed that a prominent factor related to COVID-19 severity and mortality is the development of a “cytokine storm” following severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection, where overproduction of proinflammatory cytokines exacerbates ARDS and can lead to extensive tissue damage, multiple organ failure, and death [5].

Acute lung injury (ALI) has been experimentally studied as a model of ARDS using the bleomycin (BLM) mouse model [6, 7]. BLM treatment induces reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, oxidative stress and subsequent DNA damage in the lung [8]. In mice, BLM causes alveolar damage and inflammation, leading to ALI within a week [9, 10]. In the early inflammatory phase, activation of both alveolar macrophages and neutrophils promotes the release of proinflammatory mediators and ROS, leading to apoptosis or necrosis of alveolar type 1 cells as well as increased permeability of the alveolar-capillary barrier, pulmonary edema, and reduction of surfactant production [11, 12]. In rodents, BLM induces key features of ALI three days after treatment, including increased expression of multiple inflammatory mediators [13, 14]. Thus, exploring the molecular basis of the cytokine storm in the BLM mouse model could be of relevance to explore novel therapies for the clinical “cytokine storm”.

The insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R) is a ubiquitously expressed trans-membrane tyrosine kinase that is activated by its ligands, IGF1 and IGF2. IGF1R signaling regulates multiple cellular functions such as growth, proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, adhesion and migration. IGF signaling is tightly regulated by several high-affinity IGF binding proteins (IGFBPs), IGFBP specific proteases (i.e. pappalysins) and their inhibitors (i.e. stanniocalcins) [15, 16]. IGF signaling has been widely reported to play a role in multiple lung diseases with an inflammatory component [17]. More specifically, there is substantial evidence for the relevance of IGFs in ARDS [18, 19]. More recently, proteins of the IGF system were postulated as components of the ‘cytokine release syndrome’ and intermediaries in long COVID-19 patients [20–22]. In addition, IGF1R was identified as an outcome biomarker in critical COVID-19 patients to predict mortality [23].

By using the IGF1R-deficient conditional mutant mice UBC-CreERT2; Igf1rfl/fl (CreERT2) [24], we previously investigated the involvement of IGF1R in the ALI process mediated by BLM. IGF1R widespread deficiency conferred resistance to BLM-mediated ALI, improving survival within a week. Three days after injury, IGF1R-deficient mice presented attenuated lung inflammation and protection against alveolar damage [25]. In order to gain new insights into the molecular mechanisms involved in IGF modulation of acute murine lung inflammation as a model of the pulmonary cytokine storm observed in patients, we analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) expression profiles from human COVID-19 and control lungs, normal mouse lungs, and a murine model of ALI at day 3 post-BLM challenge, and we performed high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in lung tissue of CreERT2 and Igf1rfl/fl control mice three days after BLM or saline (SAL) intra-tracheal instillation. Retrieved data revealed widespread IGF1R expression in human and mouse lungs, barely modified by the pathological conditions, while a more cell type-specific expression was observed for other IGF system genes. Transcriptomic analysis of acute injured lungs revealed IGF1R as a promoter of proinflammatory “cytokine storm”-related gene profiles with actions on DNA damage response, metabolism, mitochondrial homeostasis and epigenetics, which were further tested at the protein or functional level. In summary, our results provide new insights into the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying acute lung inflammation and the associated “cytokine storm” mediated by IGF1R function.

Materials and methods

Single-cell transcriptomics data mining and visualization

scRNA-seq datasets were analyzed using the Scanpy package in Python. Human lung scRNA-seq data from COVID-19 autopsies and control samples (GSE171524; Melms et al., 2021 [26]), as well as mouse lung data from the Tabula Muris project (GSE109774; Tabula Muris Consortium, 2018 [27]) were obtained as preprocessed .h5ad files from the CELLxGENE portal. The whole-lung scRNA-seq dataset from a mouse model of BLM-induced lung injury (GSE141259; Strunz et al., 2020 [14]) was downloaded from GitHub as a Seurat .RData file and converted to .h5ad format using the SeuratDisk package. High-resolution cell type annotations provided by the authors were used in all datasets, with some fine-grained populations grouped to enable consistent clustering in the context of this study. Analyses were restricted to defined data subsets and/or grouped by conditions as appropriate for each dataset. Custom dot plot visualizations were then generated from normalized, log-transformed expression matrices (Fig. 1). Mean expression values were computed only from cells with detected expression for each gene, and scaled by gene. For additional details, see Additional file 1.

Generation of IGF1R-deficient mice, establishment of the BLM-induced acute lung injury murine model and tissue collection

UBC-CreERT2; Igf1rfl/fl mice were crossed with Igf1rfl/fl mice to directly generate descendants in equal proportions in the same litter. Tamoxifen (TMX) (Sigma) was daily administered for five consecutive days to four-week-old mice of both genotypes to induce a postnatal Igf1r gene conditional deletion in UBC-CreERT2; Igf1rfl/fl mice in the so-called CreERT2 mutants [24]. Six-week-old Igf1rfl/fl (control) and CreERT2 (Igf1r-deficient) female mice were used for SAL or BLM treatment, as previously reported [25], to generate the four experimental groups: Igf1rfl/fl-SAL, CreERT2-SAL, Igf1rfl/fl-BLM and CreERT2-BLM. Left lungs were used for histopathological evaluation or immunohistochemistry, and right lung lobes for RNA, DNA and protein extraction (Fig. 2A). For additional details, see Additional file 1.

Lung RNA isolation, cDNA library Preparation and high-throughput sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from right lung lobes and assessed for quantity and quality. Sequencing libraries were prepared by following the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina Inc.). Then libraries were assessed for concentration and size, and run in a HiSeq 1500 PE100 lane to obtain high-throughput sequencing data. For additional details, see Additional file 1.

Bioinformatic analyses

After quality assessment, trimming and filtering, RNA-seq reads were aligned to the Mus musculus GRCm38.p6 reference genome. Resulting alignment files were then quality-assessed, sorted and indexed. Reads on gene features were counted and three differential expression comparisons were established between experimental groups (Fig. 2B). Next, read count normalization, quantitative differential expression analysis, principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering were performed for each comparison. To control the false discovery rate (FDR), p-values were amended by Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) multiple testing correction and those features with FDR adjusted p-values below a 0.05 threshold and fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5/≤ 0.67 (│log2 FC│ ≥ 0.58) were considered as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Reactome gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was then performed. Raw reads and read counts were submitted to Gene Expression Omnibus (accession number GSE274184). For additional details, see Additional file 1.

Histology, immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed for histopathological evaluation. Club cell 10 kDa secretory protein (CC10) (1:100; clone E-11; cat. no. sc-365992; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX), Precursor form of pulmonary-associated surfactant protein C (Pro-SPC) (1:100; clone M-20; cat. no. sc-7706; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and Acetylated histone H3 at lysine 9 (H3K9ac) (1:200; clone C5B11; cat.no. 9649; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) antibodies were used for fluorescence immunostaining on lung sections. For 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) immunohistochemical analyses (Novolink Polymer Detection System; Novocastra Laboratories Ltd), Cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1 (Cdkn1a/p21) (1:100; clone M19; cat. no. sc-471; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and Microtubule-associated protein 1a/1b light chain 3 (LC3) A/B (1:150; cat. no. 4108; Cell Signaling Technology) antibodies were used. Lung injury score was calculated as described by Matute-Bello et al. [28]. For additional details, see Additional file 1.

Generation of Igf1r KO mice and isolation and culture of mouse embryonic fibroblasts

Heterozygous Igf1rfl/− mice, originated from TMX-treated UBC-CreERT2; Igf1rfl/fl matings, were inter-crossed to generate embryos for isolation of primary mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs). At day 16 of gestation, mouse embryos were dissected and homozygous control Igf1rfl/fl and Igf1r KO (Igf1r−/−) embryo littermates were used to establish primary cultures of MEFs. For additional information, see Additional file 1.

MEFs treatments, sample collection and immunofluorescence staining

For experiments assessing BLM-induced cytokine mRNA expression, DNA lesion frequency, and histone H3 acetylation, Igf1rfl/fl and Igf1r−/− MEFs were treated with 10 µg/mL BLM in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) or DMEM for 24 h and used for RNA, DNA, or histone extraction. For immunofluorescence analyses of BLM-induced DNA damage and mitochondrial homeostasis disruption, MEFs were treated with 20 µg/mL BLM in DMEM or DMEM for 2 h, washed, and cultured for 48 h. Cells were then fixed and stained with Translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (TOM20) (1:1000; Cat. No. 11802-1-AP; Proteintech) or phospho-Histone H2AX (γH2AX) (1:100; Cat. No. 05-636; Sigma) primary antibodies, Alexa Fluor™ secondary antibodies (1:500; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and fluorochrome-conjugated Phalloidin DyLight™ 488 (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). For longer-term experiments of induced senescence, MEFs were treated similarly, cultured for 7 days, and then used for protein extraction and β-Gal staining (Cell Signaling Technology kit #9860). For additional details, see Additional file 1.

MEFs RNA isolation, reverse transcription and qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from TRIzol® homogenates of cultured MEFs and reverse-transcribed to cDNA. cDNA samples were amplified by real-time qPCR in triplicate reactions for each primer pair assayed. Results were normalized using the Hprt1 gene as endogenous control. For qPCR primers information and additional details, see Additional file 1.

DNA lesion frequency and mitochondrial copy number assessment

DNA lesion frequency was assessed by long-amplicon quantitative PCR (LA-qPCR), targeting a 6.6-kb nuclear fragment and a 10-kb mitochondrial fragment, following the protocols established by Furda et al. [29] and González-Hunt et al. [30] with minor modifications. For mitochondrial DNA assays, samples were linearized prior to LA-qPCR to enable efficient amplification. Relative mitochondrial to nuclear DNA content was determined by real-time qPCR using short amplicons and analyzed by the comparative Ct method. Mitochondrial DNA copy number and lesion frequency calculations were performed as described by González-Hunt et al. [30].

Mitochondrial morphology and network connectivity analysis

Igf1rfl/fl and Igf1r−/− MEFs stained for TOM20, actin/phalloidin and DAPI were imaged on a confocal microscope (Stellaris 8, Leica Microsystems). The ImageJ Mitochondria Analyzer plugin developed by Chaundhry et al. was then used for 3D mitochondrial object segmentation and morphological and network connectivity analysis [31]. Maximal projections of z-stack images were used for illustration purposes. For additional details, see Additional file 1.

Measurement of mitochondrial respiration and glycolytic activity

Igf1rfl/fl and Igf1r−l− MEFs were seeded on XF24 plates coated with poly-D-lysine and grown for 24 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Prior to each assay, culture medium was washed and replaced by pre-warmed Seahorse XF-DMEM medium (pH 7.4) containing 10 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine, 2% FBS and with or without 1 mM sodium pyruvate, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 45 min in a non-CO2 incubator. Then, the Seahorse XF24 Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) was used to repeatedly measure oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) under basal conditions and after the injection of the corresponding inhibitors. After each assay, 1 µg/mL Hoechst was injected to stain nuclei, which were imaged immediately after assay completion and counted for normalization. For the mitochondrial respiration assay, sequential injections consisted of 1.5 µM oligomycin, 2 µM FCCP, and a mixture of 0.5 µM antimycin A and 0.5 µM rotenone (all from Sigma-Aldrich). Normalized OCR data were used to calculate basal respiration, proton leak, ATP production-coupled respiration, maximal respiration, coupling efficiency, spare respiratory capacity, and non-mitochondrial OCR. For glycolytic function assessment, the Seahorse XF Glycolytic Rate Assay Kit (Agilent Technologies) was used. After baseline measurements, 0.5 µM rotenone/antimycin A and 50 mM 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) were sequentially injected. Glycolysis-associated proton efflux rate (GlycoPER) was calculated from ECAR and OCR data, and used to determine basal and compensatory glycolysis. For additional details, see Additional file 1.

γH2AX foci and nuclear morphology analysis

Igf1rfl/fl and Igf1r−/− MEFs stained for γH2AX and DAPI were imaged on a confocal microscope (Stellaris 8, Leica Microsystems). The ImageJ software was then used for image processing, γH2AX foci counting and nuclear morphology analysis. Finally, nuclear atypia classification into “normal”, “mis-shapen”, “micronuclei” and “multinucleated” categories was performed as previously described [32]. For additional details, see Additional file 1.

Measurement of reactive oxygen species

ROS measurement on cultured MEFs was performed using the ROS-sensitive dye 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) (Invitrogen). Briefly, Igf1r−/− and Igf1rfl/fl MEFs were cultured in a 96-well plate for 24 h, washed and incubated in HBSS-H2DCFDA (10 µM) with increasing concentrations of H2O2. Finally, fluorescence intensity was monitored at 485/535 nm (excitation/emission) in a microplate reader. The experiment was repeated three times with five replicates per condition. For additional details, see Additional file 1.

Western blotting

Superior mouse lung lobes were solubilized in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Protein samples were then separated in 4–20% Bis-Tris gels (GenScript Biotech) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad). Following blocking, membranes were incubated overnight with Cdkn1a/p21 (1:500; clone M19; cat. no. sc-471; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), TOM20 (1:2000; cat. no. 11802-1-AP; Proteintech), Acetyl-CoA synthetase short chain synthetase 1 (ACSS1) (1:1000; Cat. No. 17138-1-AP; Proteintech), pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase isoform 2 (PDK2) (1:500; Cat. No. ab68164; Abcam), PDHA1 pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 subunit alpha 1 (PDHA1) (1:1000; Cat. No ab1103330; Abcam) and phosphorylated PDHA1 (pPDHA1) (Phospho S293; 1:1000; Cat. No. ab177461) primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibodies and incubated in ECL substrate. Finally, chemiluminescence signals were detected in a ChemiDoc Imager (Bio-Rad), quantified and normalized to total protein signals. For additional details, see Additional file 1.

Histone H3 acetylation assessment

Histone H3 acetylation levels were quantified using the Histone H3 Acetylation Assay Kit (Cat. No. ab115102; Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom), following the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 0.75 µg of histone extract per well was loaded into the 96-well assay plate in technical triplicates for each sample. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured in a microplate reader (POLARstar Omega; BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany) and acetylation levels were determined by interpolating background-subtracted absorbance values from the positive control standard curve. Results are expressed as ratios of Histone H3 acetylation relative to the mean of the control group (untreated Igf1rfl/fl MEFs).

Lung genomic DNA extraction and global DNA methylation assay

Lung genomic DNA was extracted from the accessory lung lobes. Global DNA methylation levels were obtained with an ELISA commercial kit (5-mC DNA ELISA Kit, Zymo Research; D5325) as previously described [33]. According to manufacturer’s protocol, absorbance was measured at 450 nm in a plate reader (POLARstar Omega) and quantification of global DNA methylation was obtained by calculating the amount of methylated cytosines in the samples (5-mC) relative to a standard curve, including negative and positive controls. For additional details, see Additional file 1.

Statistical analysis

Following a Shapiro-Wilk normality test, statistical significance was determined using the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for pairwise comparisons, or two-way ANOVA/complex model for two-variable analyses. Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc.). For all analyses, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Expression of IGF signaling genes in human ARDS and COVID-19 patients and in animal models of ALI

To investigate the involvement of IGF signaling in ALI, we conducted a comprehensive literature review and data mining of NCBI databases. Human studies supporting the involvement of IGF signaling in ALI-related conditions —particularly ARDS and COVID-19— were examined alongside experimental studies using animal models of ALI. The search yielded 27 references pertaining to human patient studies and 16 reports on rodent models, the findings of which are summarized in Table S1. In general, elevated IGF1R activity, expression, or serum levels were associated with greater disease severity. Conversely, Igf1r deficiency or blockade demonstrated a protective effect against ALI in rodents. Furthermore, increased levels of IGF2 receptor (IGF2R), IGF1, and several IGFBPs were reported in ARDS/COVID-19 and in murine ALI models (see details in Table S1).

Additionally, we screened the NCBI GEO and CELLxGENE databases for publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets containing gene expression profiles from lung tissue. This search identified 14 human and 18 mouse datasets relevant to ARDS/COVID-19 or BLM-induced ALI, which are provided in Table S2. To explore IGF system gene expression across lung cell types in the context of ARDS/COVID-19, we mined scRNA-seq data from lung tissue of control donors and lethal COVID-19 cases (GSE171524 dataset from Melms et al. study [26]; Table S2), which are comparatively displayed in Fig. 1A. Consistent with prior knowledge, IGF1R exhibited widespread expression in the lung. Thus, most epithelial, stromal and endothelial cell subtypes showed a substantial proportion of cells with detectable IGF1R expression in both conditions. Among these, airway ciliated cells displayed the highest frequency of expressing cells. By contrast, most immune cell populations featured in this dataset showed limited IGF1R transcript detection, especially lymphocytes. IGF2R and insulin receptor (INSR) were also broadly expressed. Other IGF genes exhibited more restricted expression patterns. Remarkably, IGF1 showed enriched expression in vascular smooth muscle cells and adventitial fibroblasts from COVID-19 samples. Furthermore, IGFBP4 expression was evident in several stromal and endothelial cell populations, with a general tendency toward increased detection frequency and expression levels in the COVID-19 subgroups. It is also noteworthy that Pappalysin-1 (PAPPA) expression was predominantly observed in fibroblasts (Fig. 1A).

Likewise, scRNA-seq expression data of IGF system genes in normal young mouse lung cell types were retrieved from the Tabula Muris study (GSE132042 dataset [27]; Table S2) and displayed in Fig. 1B. In notable agreement with human data, Igf1r gene expression was also found widespread in mouse lungs, albeit with lower overall detection frequency compared to the human lung dataset. Interestingly, Igf1r expression was also prominent in basophils and neutrophils. Igf1 expression very much resembled human lung data in fibroblasts, with the highest detection frequency and relative mean expression found in myofibroblasts. In addition, Igf1 expression was enriched in interstitial macrophages and almost undetectable in other cell types. Igf2 presence was residual, as in humans. Remarkably, Igfbp genes showed abundant and diverse expression across mouse lung cell types. Thus, Igfbp2 expression was almost exclusively restricted to ciliated epithelial cells, while Igfbp3 was predominantly detected in myofibroblasts and alveolar fibroblasts, with minimal expression in other cell types. Igfbp4 showed a broader expression profile, with generally high frequencies of expressing cells across fibroblast, smooth muscle, and endothelial cell subtypes and higher mean expression in adventitial fibroblasts and artery endothelial cells, partially mirroring the human lung profile. Igfbp5 expression was highly enriched in ciliated epithelial cells, myofibroblasts, and smooth muscle cells. Finally, Igfbp6 expression was observed at high frequency in adventitial and alveolar fibroblasts, as well as in smooth muscle cells and alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells at lower levels (Fig. 1B).

Whole transcriptome differential expression and functional enrichment analyses in lungs of BLM-challenged and IGF1R-deficient mice

To determine the impact of IGF1R deficiency in combination with BLM injury on global lung gene expression, RNA-seq was performed on lungs of the four experimental groups of mice indicated in Fig. 2A-B, and differential expression was analyzed in three comparisons: CreERT2-SAL vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL, Igf1rfl/fl-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL and CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM (Fig. 2B). A threshold of false discovery rate adjusted p-value (FDR adj. p-value) < 0.05 and fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5/≤ 0.67 (│log2 FC│ ≥ 0.58) was set to consider differentially expressed genes (DEGs). DEGs are represented in volcano plots in Fig. 2C-E, and differential expression data and description for all DEGs is shown in Tables S3-S5. In the CreERT2-SAL vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL comparison, down-regulated genes (378) tended to show higher differential expression than up-regulated genes (203) (Fig. 2C and Table S3). In Igf1rfl/fl-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL lungs, 989 up- and 811 down-regulated DEGs were detected, with greater changes predominating in up-regulated genes (Fig. 2D and Table S4. Finally, in CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM lungs, 1005 up- and 1550 down-regulated DEGs were identified, the latter showing much greater expression changes overall. Of note, the number of DEGs elicited by IGF1R deficiency under BLM challenge is 1.4 times greater than those caused by BLM (Fig. 2E and Table S5). A Venn diagram indicating the overlap of DEGs between the three comparisons is shown in Fig. 2F. Interestingly, 952 genes were counter-regulated between the CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM and Igf1rfl/fl-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL comparisons, accounting for more than half of the 1800 genes differentially expressed as a consequence of BLM challenge. Among them, 74 genes were also differentially expressed in CreERT2-SAL mice in basal conditions (CreERT2-SAL vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL). Moreover, it should be noted the very consistent overlap observed in genes shared by the CreERT2-SAL vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL and CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM comparisons while not differentially expressed in the Igf1rfl/fl-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL comparison. Thus, 179 down-regulated and 50 up-regulated genes match, whereas only 3 genes appeared counter-regulated. Principal component analysis (PCA) graphs and hierarchical clustering heatmaps of RNA-seq groups are shown and described in Fig. S1. Altogether, these data highlight not only the dependence on IGF1R signaling to mediate BLM-induced acute lung damage but also to maintain basal lung homeostasis.

On the whole, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GO, KEGG and REACTOME databases. Regarding the effect of IGF1R deficiency in basal conditions, functional enrichment of CreERT2-SAL vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL differential gene expression identified down-regulated pathways and processes mainly involved in cell cycle regulation, extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, and DNA damage response. Interestingly, the “Regulation of IGF transport and uptake by IGF Binding Proteins” REACTOME term was also observed. All pathways and processes spotted regarding IGF1R deficiency in naïve lungs showed a down-regulation trend (Table S6). As expected, the functional clusters with the most significant transcriptional changes in the Igf1rfl/fl-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL comparison included up-regulated pathways/processes involved in the pathobiology of BLM-induced ALI, including mainly inflammation but also ECM, vascular homeostasis, DNA damage response and cellular senescence/cell cycle arrest. Remarkably, the “Regulation of IGF transport and uptake by IGF Binding Proteins” REACTOME term was also detected in this comparison, as well as several signaling pathways acting downstream of IGF signaling (Table S7). Conversely, the majority of these biological processes and pathways appeared as significant and in the opposite direction (down-regulated) in the CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM comparison. In addition, GSEA detected down-regulated processes/pathways involved in cell cycle regulation, not identified in the Igf1rfl/fl-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL comparison but in notable agreement with the CreERT2-SAL vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL comparison. Furthermore, several metabolic processes were significantly dysregulated, mostly down-regulated (ROS, nitric oxide, alpha-amino acid, arginine and proline, alcohol, pyrimidine) but with three up-regulated, namely “Regulation of lipid metabolic process”, “Acetyl-CoA metabolic process” and “Cellular ketone metabolic process” (Table S8).

Transcriptomic analysis of lungs from BLM-challenged IGF1R-deficient mice corroborates previous findings and uncovers novel biological functions of IGF1R

In order to gain knowledge on the altered molecular mechanisms mediated by IGF1R deficiency in lungs from BLM challenged mice, intensive data mining based on functional annotations in databases (MGI, GO, KEGG, REACTOME, GeneCards and UniProt) was performed in the RNA-seq CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM comparison. DEGs in CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM lungs were functionally grouped and represented as up- and down-regulated genes in the reported functional categories. The most relevant functional categories, ordered according to the number of genes involved from highest to lowest were: inflammation, metabolism, cell cycle, mitochondrial homeostasis, ECM organization, vascular homeostasis, DNA damage response, autophagy, IGF signaling and epigenetics. All of them, with the exception of epigenetics comprised genes mostly down-regulated (Fig. 3). Expression data and description of the genes featured in each functional group are shown in Tables S9-S18, respectively. In sum, these results reinforce the concept that IGF1R deficiency causes a generalized inhibition of transcription in the lung in the context of a BLM challenge, affecting multiple biological functions.

Here it is particularly striking that several of the genes counter-regulated in the Igf1rfl/fl-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL and CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM comparisons (Cxcl10, Oas1a, Oas1g, Oas3, Oasl2, Ifit1, Ifit3, Ifi44, Ifi204, Isg15, Mx1, Irf7, Zbp1,Timp1, Serpina3n, Mmp12, Lgals3bp) were also down-regulated in CreERT2-SAL vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL lungs, without the effect of any insult other than SAL instillation. Transcriptional changes of genes with proinflammatory function including Tnf, Il1b, Il6, Cxcl1 and Adgre1, as well as anti-inflammatory Cd209 were previously validated by qPCR, and in case of TNF also by ELISA [25].

The counteraction of the BLM-induced “cytokine storm” transcriptional profile by IGF1R depletion is further reflected in the high number of inflammation-related genes with altered expression in the full set of CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM DEGs (604 out of 2555 total DEGs; 80% downregulated) (Fig. 3 and Table S9). This anti-inflammatory effect was additionally supported by GSEA, which identified a large set of downregulated inflammation-related pathways and processes. To better illustrate this enrichment profile, the number of genes and the statistical significance of each GO Biological Process term and KEGG pathway within the “inflammation” functional category (Table S8) are shown in Fig. 4E. Notable GO terms include “T cell activation” (126 genes), along with interleukin (IL-6, -1, -10, -8, -12, -2, and -13) and chemokines production, and responses to IFN-γ. Additional processes comprise adaptive and innate immune responses, acute inflammation, and responses to IFN-α and IL-1, as well as migration, proliferation, or activation of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and macrophages. Among the KEGG pathways, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction was the most enriched (84 genes), followed by the IL17, TNF, NF-κB, and chemokine signaling pathways. The leukocyte transendothelial migration, Th17 cell differentiation, and PD-L1 expression and PD-1 checkpoint pathways were also present, although with lower gene counts and significance (Fig. 4E).

Given the critical role of lung fibroblasts in ARDS, COVID-19, and profibrotic activity in BLM-challenged mice [26, 34, 35] we examined Igf1r protein levels in cultured primary mouse fibroblasts (Fig S2). Both MEFs and adult lung-derived fibroblasts—isolated from Igf1rfl/fl embryos and Igf1rfl/fl mice, respectively—exhibited robust Igf1r protein expression, whereas MEFs isolated from Igf1r−/− embryos did not show detectable expression (Fig. S2A). Based on these findings, we used primary MEFs to assess the effect of IGF1R deficiency on inflammatory cytokine responses to BLM treatment. In Igf1rfl/fl MEFs, BLM exposure substantially increased Cxcl1, Cxcl2 and Cxcl10 mRNA expression. In contrast, Igf1r−l− MEFs failed to significantly upregulate Cxcl1 and Cxcl2 in response to BLM, although Cxcl10 remained elevated. The expression levels of Ccl2, Il6 and Tnfa were unaffected by either BLM treatment or Igf1r genotype (Fig. S2B).

According to previous data [25], lung inflammation was highly reduced in CreERT2-BLM compared to Igf1rfl/fl-BLM lungs (Fig. S3A-D) and mRNA expression of the surfactant protein C precursor gene (Sftpc) was significantly reduced in Igf1rfl/fl-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL lungs, while increased in CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM lungs. Furthermore, a similar expression profile was found for the marker of bronchiolar club cells Scgb1a1 (Tables S4 and S5). These results were further investigated by fluorescence immunohistochemistry (see details in Fig. S3E-P). Interestingly, while pro-SPC staining was rare in the bronchioles of Igf1rfl/fl-SAL and CreERT2-SAL lungs (Fig. S3I-J), it was greatly increased in Igf1rfl/fl-BLM bronchioles (Fig. S3K), with pro-SPC+ staining in a subpopulation of bronchiolar cells which were not CC10+ cells (Fig. S3O). Cells of this type were recently identified as alveolar type 2 (AT2) proliferating precursors in BLM-challenged lungs [36]. Of note, expression of pro-SPC in bronchioles of CreERT2-BLM mice was restored to basal levels (Fig. S3L). Assessment of the lung injury score allowed us to determine BLM-mediated inflammation and damage to the alveolar tract. As expected, the lung injury score was significantly increased in both groups of BLM-exposed mice. Notably, CreERT2-BLM mice showed a significant reduction in acute lung injury score compared to Igf1rfl/fl-BLM mice (Fig. S3Q). Taken together, these results demonstrate that IGF1R deficiency very efficiently protects the lungs against BLM-mediated injury in both the bronchiolar and alveolar compartments.

Screening for specific DEGs of the IGF/Insulin family revealed significant changes of different components on the three comparisons examined (Fig. 5). In the absence of BLM challenge (CreERT2-SAL vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL comparison), CreERT2-SAL mice showed notable down-regulation of Igf1 and Stc2. On the contrary, Igfbp6 and Irs2 were up-regulated. (Fig. 5A). Remarkably, several IGF/insulin signaling components were dysregulated upon BLM challenge (Igf1rfl/fl-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL comparison). Thus, four of the six IGFBP genes were differentially expressed, with Igfbp3, Igfbp5 and Igfbp6 down-regulated and Igfbp4 up-regulated. Interestingly, the Pappalysin 1 gene (Pappa), a metalloproteinase with IGFBP proteolytic activity, exhibited a high increase in expression, whereas the Stanniocalcin 1 gene (Sct1), a PAPPA 1 and 2 inhibitor, was down-regulated (Fig. 5B).

To contrast these data and improve interpretability, we additionally mined the cell-type specific expression profiles of IGF system genes from the only currently available scRNA-seq dataset that includes data from murine lung tissue at day 3 post-bleomycin injury (GSE141259; Strunz et al., 2020 [14]; Table S2). Filtered expression data from PBS-treated controls and day 3 BLM-challenged mice are shown in Fig. S4. This dataset, while providing valuable single-cell resolution in a matched experimental setting, presented somewhat low per-cell transcriptomic complexity in the samples of interest, with low to moderate unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts and gene features across most cell types. In these cases, a substantial dropout rate of genes with low to medium expression may occur, a well-known feature of scRNA-seq pipelines [37, 38] that likely contributed to the sparse expression patterns observed for some IGF system genes, as opposed to data on Fig. 1B. This is evident in the expression pattern of Igf1r, displaying residual detection in most cell types. Nevertheless, other IGF system genes did show expression enrichment in several cell types. It is worth noting the higher proportion of fibroblasts from BLM-challenged mice expressing Igf1, in agreement with COVID-19 data from Fig. 1A. In addition, Igfbp3 is present only in a subset of fibroblasts from control PBS-treated mice; Igfbp4, Igfbp5, and Igfbp6 show a mixed expression profile; and a small proportion of fibroblasts from BLM-treated mice present Pappa expression (Fig. S4), in partial agreement with BLM-induced differential expression in Igf1rfl/fl-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL mice (Fig. 5B).

Finally, IGF1R deficiency in CreERT2-BLM mice (CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM comparison) counter-regulated most of the IGF system genes that had been changed by BLM action in control mice (Igf1rfl/fl-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL comparison) (Fig. 5B), exhibiting up-regulation of Igfbp3, Igfbp5, Igfbp6, Insr and Irs1 and down-regulation of Igf1, Igfbp4, Ig2fbp2, Pappa2, Igf2r and Ide. (Fig. 5C).

It should be noted that differential expression analysis did not show a significant decrease in Igf1r normalized counts in either CreERT2-SAL vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL or CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM lungs. However, assessment of read coverage along the Igf1r transcribed region and exon-specific FPKM quantification showed that both CreERT2 samples presented similar levels of reads throughout the entire Igf1r mRNA sequence compared to both Igf1rfl/fl samples, but a very diminished amount of reads mapping to exon 3, corresponding to the floxed Igf1r gene region where TMX-induced Cre-mediated deletion occurs (Fig. S5A-B). Inspection of the exon junctions detected by the same analysis corroborated that in CreERT2 mice an effective alternative splicing occurs between exon 2 and exon 4 of the Igf1r consensus mRNA, generating “full length” mRNAs with an efficient deletion of exon 3 (Fig. S5A). However, translation of these mutant mRNAs in CreERT2 mice could only generate a putative non-functional truncated α chain polypeptide (see details in Fig. S6). Besides, a large reduction in IGF1R protein expression was previously confirmed in the lungs of CreERT2-SAL and CreERT2-BLM mice [25, 39].

IGF1R depletion alters expression of DNA damage-related genes and protects against BLM-mediated cell cycle arrest, nuclear impairment and ROS

Since BLM induces DNA damage in the lung and GSEA identified DNA damage response-related pathways and processes in the three RNA-seq comparisons (Tables S6-S8), we decided to explore the effect of IGF1R deficiency on DNA damage-related functions in depth. Overall, data mining analysis in CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM lungs showed 138 DEGs with DNA damage-related functions (Fig. 3 and Table S15). Among them stand several genes with key functions in DNA damage responses, including DNA repair, genomic stability maintenance and cell cycle arrest (Fig. 6A). Remarkably, the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1 (commonly known as p21) gene (Cdkn1a), a well-recognized marker of cell cycle arrest [40], showed greatly reduced expression (6.0 times) in CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM lungs. In view of these data, we decided to evaluate p21 protein expression by Western blot and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Thus, Western blot analysis on lung extracts showed a significant increase of p21 protein expression after BLM injury both in Igf1rfl/fl-BLM and CreERT2-BLM lungs compared to controls. Although there was not a statistically significant difference between genotypes, a tendency to decreased p21 protein was observed in CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM lungs (Fig. 6B-C). IHC for p21 in bronchiolar epithelial cells showed a similar profile. Quantification of p21+ bronchiolar cells revealed an increase in both genotypes of BLM-treated mouse lungs compared to controls, and, in addition, a significant decrease in CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM lungs. However, although p21+ alveolar cell counts were lower in CreERT2-SAL mice, no significant changes were observed in BLM-treated mice of either genotype (Fig. 6D-E).To evaluate the effect of BLM treatment on DNA damage and ROS detoxification in the context of IGF1R deficiency, primary MEFs were used. With this goal, Igf1rfl/fl and Igf1r−/− MEFs treated with 20 µg/mL BLM for 2 h were immunostained against Phospho-H2AX (γ-H2AX) as a marker of DNA damage after 48 h (Fig. 6F). Quantification of γH2AX+ foci per nucleus did not show significant differences. However, nuclear morphology assessment showed lower nuclear perimeter as well as higher nuclear circularity in Igf1r−/− MEFs (Fig. 6G). In addition, frequencies of nuclei with distinct nuclear abnormalities changed in Igf1r−/− MEFS (Fig. 6H). Next, DNA damage was assessed in mouse lungs and MEFs treated with BLM by long amplicon qPCR (LA-qPCR), a method that measures DNA lesion frequency by differential amplification of long DNA fragments [41] (Fig. S7). In mice, nuclear DNA lesion frequency was found increased in the lungs of both genotypes of BLM-challenged mice. However, although a trend toward fewer DNA lesions was apparent in CreERT2-BLM compared to Igf1rfl/fl-BLM mice, no statistically significant differences were found. Mitochondrial DNA lesions, although also showing a tendency to increase in the lungs of Igf1rfl/fl-BLM mice and to decrease in CreERT2-BLM mice (p = 0.07), results did not reached statistical significance (Fig. S7A). No differences in BLM-mediated nuclear or mitochondrial DNA damage were neither found in MEFs by this technique (Fig S7B). Notably, in both mice lungs and MEFs, BLM treatment increased mitochondrial DNA content in both genotypes (Fig S7A-B). Since p21 also contributes to the induction of cellular senescence in the long term, we determined p21 expression levels in untreated or BLM-treated MEFs by Western blot 7 days post-treatment. Although non-treated MEFs did not show differences between genotypes, BLM induced a significant increase in p21 content in Igf1rfl/fl, an increase that was not observed in Igf1r−/− MEFs (Fig. 6I-J). However, when we looked for a reduction in senescence-associated beta-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining in BLM-treatedIgf1r−/− relative to Igf1rfl/flMEFs, we did not find differences (data not shown). Subsequently, ROS detoxification capacity was assessed by treating primary MEFs with increasing concentrations of H2O2 using a ROS-sensitive probe as an indicator. Of note, ROS levels were found significantly reduced in Igf1r−/− MEFs when compared to Igf1rfl/fl controls (Fig. S8). Taken together, these data suggest that IGF1R deficiency could protect MEFs from BLM-mediated DNA damage, nuclear impairment, cell cycle arrest and ROS.

IGF1R deficiency impacts expression of mitochondrial homeostasis-related genes and prevents mitochondrial accumulation after BLM challenge

Extensive data mining identified 228 DEGs with functions in the mitochondria in CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM lungs (Fig. 3 and Table S12), including a particularly relevant group of DEGs with key roles in modulating mitochondrial function and dynamics (Fig. 8A). Among them, three up-regulated genes with central roles in mitochondrial quality control and mitophagy highlight: PTEN Induced Kinase 1 (Pink1), E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase PARKIN (Park2) and BCL2 interacting protein 3 (Bnip3). In addition, the uncoupling protein 4 gene (Slc25a27) and another solute carrier with a putative role in electron transport chain uncoupling (Slc25a47) were also up-regulated. Furthermore, three genes encoding cytochrome C oxidase structural subunits (Cox6a2, Cox7a1 and Cox8b) were up-regulated, as well as the mitochondrially encoded cytochrome oxidase II (mt-Co2) and ATP synthase 6 (mt-Atp6). However, it should be noted that the cells themselves differed in size, with BLM-treated Igf1rfl/fl MEFs being generally more enlarged and with more aberrant morphology than Igf1r−/− MEFs (Fig. 8D). Transcriptomic analysis reveals a mixed autophagy-related gene expression pattern in lungs of BLM-challenged IGF1R-deficient mice

Since multiple studies have shown that IGF1R regulates autophagy in various tissues and conditions [42, 43], we explored the expression profile of genes involved in the regulation and activity of autophagy in the lungs of IGF1R-deficient mice. A considerable number of autophagy-related genes were detected in CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM mouse lungs (Fig. 3 and Table S16). However, an unclear, mixed differential expression pattern occurs, as genes with central roles in both positive and negative regulation of autophagy were up- and down-regulated (Fig. S11A). IHC for LC3 did not show conclusive evidence of LC3 puncta, with mainly diffuse and unchanged staining in all four groups (Fig. S11B).

IGF1R deficiency impacts epigenetic regulation in BLM-challenged mice

In-depth screening of differential expression data revealed 22 genes with important roles in histone-related epigenetic regulation and DNA accessibility (Fig. 9A) as part of the abovementioned epigenetics functional gene cluster (Fig. 3 and Table S18). Thus, some prominent class II (Hdac5, Hdac10) and class IV (Hdac11) histone deacetylases (HDACs) were up-regulated. It is also worth noting the up-regulation of Akap8 and Akap8l, which encode two paralog proteins (A-kinase anchoring protein 8 and A-kinase anchoring protein 8-like) which recruit HDAC3 and possibly other HDACs to the vicinity of chromatin. Furthermore, some genes encoding histone methylases were up-regulated, including Ezh1, Smyd1 and Prdm6. In addition, two H2 histone genes were up-regulated (H2afv and Hist1h2be), both replication-dependent histones. Among the downregulated genes stand the P-element-induced wimpy testis genes Piwil2 and Piwil4, which play an important role in RNA interference and transposon silencing.

Based on these data of IGF1R involvement in epigenetic regulation and given the increased transcriptional levels of genes involved in histone deacetylation, we decided to study the levels of acetylated histone H3 (H3K9ac) in lung sections. Immunofluorescence staining for H3K9ac in Igf1rfl/fl-BLM and CreERT2-BLM mice showed a heterogeneous, non-uniform patchy pattern throughout the lungs of both genotypes, with accumulation of cells presenting high H3K9ac fluorescence intensity on certain regions, particularly surrounding blood vessels (Fig. S12A). However, quantification of fluorescence intensity did not show significant differences between genotypes either when measured in the whole lung or only in alveolar areas (Fig. S12B-C). On the other hand, determination of total histone H3 acetylation levels in MEFs revealed a significant reduction in Igf1rfl/fl MEFs upon BLM treatment. Interestingly, Igf1r−/− MEFs still showed a further significant decrease, with acetylation levels reduced to nearly half those measured in BLM-treated Igf1rfl/fl MEFs (Fig. 9B). Finally, we evaluated lung genomic DNA methylation levels in BLM-challenged mice. Of note, we found a significant increase in percentage (20%) of 5-methylcytosine (5-mC) in genomic lung DNA of CreERT2-BLM mice relative to Igf1rfl/fl-BLM mice (Fig. 9C). Taken together, these results suggest a role of IGF1R in regulating epigenetic events in the context of ALI.

Discussion

Here we report pulmonary cell type-specific mRNA expression of IGF system genes retrieved from scRNA-seq datasets, including lethal COVID-19 and control donor samples, normal mouse lungs, and a murine model of ALI at day 3 post-BLM challenge. Overall, the data revealed generalized IGF1R gene expression in human and mouse lungs, with little variation under pathological conditions. In contrast, other IGF system genes showed more localized and context-dependent expression. We also performed bulk RNA-seq on lungs from IGF1R-deficient and control mice three days after BLM or saline intra-tracheal instillation, to explore the molecular mechanisms mediated by IGF/IGF1R signaling in pulmonary homeostasis and BLM-induced acute inflammation. As expected, and consistent with our previous findings [25], IGF1R deficiency led to widespread downregulation of gene expression in the lung, counteracting the hyperinflammatory profile and conferring protection against ALI. Functional enrichment analysis further revealed significant modulation of pathways related to DNA damage response, metabolic reprogramming, mitochondrial homeostasis, and epigenetic regulation.

Lung transcriptome data showed that down-regulated genes in the CreERT2-SAL vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL lungs were higher in number and tended to show greater differential expression differences than up-regulated genes. This trend was previously noted in transcriptomic studies of lungs from both CreERT2 mice (using a different analytical pipeline) and prenatal Igf1-deficient mice [25, 44]. Notably, an even higher transcriptional blockade was observed in CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM lungs after BLM challenge. These findings clearly indicate that deficiency of IGF/IGF1R signaling results in a generalized transcriptional inhibition in the lung. IGFs are well known to regulate diverse cellular responses in multiple cell types and in both healthy and diseased tissues [45]. In the murine lung, the relevance of IGF1R signaling was demonstrated by the fact that it shows the highest activation levels of all organs tested after IGF1 exposure [46]. Therefore, it is not surprising that lack of IGF1R causes generalized transcriptional repression in CreERT2-BLM lungs, and specifically in all reported biological functions except epigenetics.

Data retrieved from scRNA-seq datasets revealed a fairly widespread and stable mRNA expression of IGF receptors (IGF1R, IGF2R, and INSR) across pulmonary cell types in both normal and pathological lungs of human and mice. In agreement with this, Igf1r was not differentially expressed in Igf1rfl/fl-BLM mice compared to unchallenged controls. However, increased levels of IGF1R protein immunoreactivity reported in fibroproliferative ARDS and in blood of ARDS and critically ill COVID-19 patients [19, 23, 47] could indicate post-transcriptional regulation. In contrast, IGF1, IGFBPs, and IGFBP regulators exhibited more restricted and disease-specific expression patterns, particularly in fibroblast, smooth muscle, and endothelial subtypes, as well as certain epithelial and immune cell subsets, suggesting regulatory roles for these genes in lung homeostasis and injury responses. Accordingly, changes in protein levels of IGF1 and IGFBP-2, −3, −4, −5, and −6 have been reported in lungs and blood of ARDS and COVID-19 cases, and in mouse models of ALI. Indeed, in the mouse lung, the IGF1/IGF1R axis often promotes inflammation and tissue damage. Conversely, IGF1R deficiency can be protective against hyperinflammatory responses (see Table S1 and references therein).

Interestingly, GSEA identified the “IGF signaling” gene cluster as common to the three differential expression comparisons. Thus, Igf1rfl/fl-BLM mouse lungs showed reduced expression of Igfbp6, Igfbp5, and Igfbp3, and upregulation of Igfbp4. The IGFBP protease Pappa was also induced, while its inhibitor Stc1 [16] was downregulated, supporting an overall activation of the IGF/IGF1R axis in response to BLM. Interestingly, most of these changes were reversed in IGF1R-deficient CreERT2-BLM mice. Of note, Igfbp3, Insr, Igfbp5, and Igf1 DEGs in this comparison were previously validated by qPCR [25]. In particular, Igfbp6 was not only downregulated after BLM challenge and counter-regulated by IGF1R deficiency but also showed reduced basal expression in the absence of IGF1R, suggesting a strong dependence on IGF1R signaling in the lung. Taken together, these findings indicate that IGF system components are highly counterbalanced in the BLM-injured mouse lung, forming a tightly regulated, complex network. Changes in several IGFBPs and other IGF signaling mediators were previously reported in BLM-challenged rodents, although at later stages during the establishment of lung fibrosis [48–50]. Furthermore, transcriptomic alterations in IGF-related genes observed in our murine model are consistent with previous reports in ARDS patients [18, 19, 51]. Plasma signatures of IGF proteins have also been detected in the context of COVID-19 cytokine release syndrome [20], and levels of IGF1, IGF1R, and IGFBP2 have been associated with disease severity and mortality risk [22, 23, 52]. These reports suggest that IGF/IGF1R signaling is also implicated in pulmonary diseases involving a “cytokine storm”, and that IGF proteins should be further tested as candidate biomarkers in respiratory diseases with this context.

Our results further support the anti-inflammatory effect of IGF1R deficiency in the context of ALI, by largely counteracting the “cytokine storm” resulting from BLM-induced injury. In addition to reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines and their signaling pathways, gene enrichment analysis revealed that IGF1R depletion in ALI has a prominent effect on the expression of genes related to T-cell activation, which is consistent with the demonstrated role of these immune cells in this context [53]. Of note, Cxcl10, one of the most counter-regulated genes in BLM-challenged lungs, while also basally down-regulated by IGF1R deficiency, has been regarded as one of the key cytokines mediating the COVID-19 “cytokine storm” [54]. Remarkably, interferon response pathways were markedly down-regulated in BLM-challenged IGF1R-deficient mice, and several interferon response counter-regulated genes were also repressed in IGF1R-deficient mice under basal conditions. This aligns with evidence that excessive or imbalanced interferon responses drive hyperinflammation and severe lung pathology in COVID-19 patients and in mouse models of ARDS/COVID-19 [55–58]. Another gene that stands out is Lcn2 (Lipocalin2, also known as Neutrophil Gelatinase-Associated Lipocalin), which was the most strongly counter-regulated gene by IGF1R deficiency under BLM challenge. Lcn2 plays a multifaceted role in lung inflammation and is expressed by activated AT2 cells during ALI [14, 59].

Importantly, Igf1r was widely detected in fibroblasts of both mice and human lung scRNA-seq data. In addition, other IGF system genes, primarily Igf1 and Igfbp4, were more selectively expressed by various subtypes of lung fibroblasts, and their expression pattern showed changes in pathological conditions. Of note, fibroblasts control local immune responses, as well as factors that regulate their inflammatory or suppressive profiles. Moreover, lung fibroblasts are involved as effectors in COVID-19 complications, and in BLM-induced lung fibrosis [26, 34, 35, 60]. In this regard, primary fibroblasts seem to be suitable models for studying certain molecular mechanisms involved in ALI, as shown herein. Indeed, BLM treatment induced Cxcl1, Cxcl2, and Cxcl10 mRNA expression in cultured MEFs, chemokines involved in recruiting immune cells, primarily neutrophils, to sites of inflammation or injury [61]. However, this induction was not as significant in Igf1r−/− MEFs. In this regard, it is worth noting that BLM-induced lung neutrophil infiltration was previously found to be greatly reduced in IGF1R-deficient mice [25].

The reduction of alveolar damage in CreERT2-BLM lungs was evidenced by the significant reduction of their lung injury score. Along with acute interstitial and intra-alveolar injury, BLM also causes bronchiolar damage [8, 62], which we also found counteracted in CreERT2-BLM mice. The role of IGF1/IGF1R signaling abnormalities in inflammatory lung diseases has been previously reviewed [17]. In fact, mice with compromised signaling of IGF1R were reported to be protected against inflammation in the lung tumor microenvironment, as well as upon allergic responses and ALI in different respiratory models [39, 63–68]. These data indicate that IGF1R is an important player in promoting ALI and blocking its action could blunt many of ALI fatal consequences in lung diseases with an inflammatory component.

Mechanistically, BLM causes cell damage by generating ROS and single- and double-stranded DNA breaks [8, 69, 70]. Our GSEA on the Igf1rfl/fl-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL comparison identified a “DNA damage response” functional cluster with DEGs mainly upregulated. Remarkably, IGF1R deficiency reversed the expression of most of these genes in the CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM comparison. Furthermore, DNA-damage response pathways were also found in the CreERT2-SAL vs. Igf1rfl/fl-SAL comparison, including the REACTOME pathways “DNA repair” and “DNA damage bypass”, with all genes down-regulated. This suggests that IGF1R is involved in positively regulating genes that promote DNA repair and other responses to DNA damage after lung injury but also in basal conditions. In this sense, IGF1R has been reported to be located in the nucleus and involved in DNA repair and DNA damage tolerance in various cell lines [71–75]. Nevertheless, down-regulation of DNA damage response genes could also be explained by reduced upstream DNA damage. In this regard, it was recently reported that IGF1R deficiency in AT2 cells protects against radiation-induced lung injury in mice [76]. However, DNA damage assessment did not show statistically significant reductions in BLM-treated IGF1R-deficient mouse lungs or Igf1r−/− MEFs. In addition, data obtained from primary IGF1R KO MEFs show a reduction in ROS levels following H2O2 treatment, indicating an enhanced ability to alleviate oxidative stress and supporting a positive effect of the lack of IGF1R on cellular redox status as another possible mechanism that attenuates lung damage. To clarify this thoroughly, further investigations are needed to better understand the role of IGF1R in relation to DNA damage and ROS buffering in the injured lung.

Transcriptome data would suggest that IGF1R deficiency elicits a metabolic shift from glycolysis to fatty acid-derived energy source in the BLM-challenged lung. Thus, we found a remarkable pattern of DEGs implicated in differential substrate usage, including the up-regulation of Pdk1 and Pdk2 in IGF1R-deficient lungs. These two PDH kinases play a pivotal role in the regulation of glucose and fatty acid metabolism by inhibiting the formation of Acetyl-CoA from pyruvate and down-regulating aerobic respiration [77, 78]. These transcriptomic changes resemble those of a caloric restriction/ketogenic diet characterized by a downregulated glucose metabolic pathway and an upregulated ketogenic pathway to render acetyl-CoA [79–81]. To further explore this concept in MEFs, we analyzed mitochondrial respiration with the Seahorse mito-stress test, both in standard conditions and without pyruvate in the media, as well as glycolytic activity with the glycolytic rate test. Real-time OCR showed that Igf1r−/− MEFs had decreased basal and ATP-linked mitochondrial respiration compared to Igf1rfl/fl MEFs. Moreover, basal and compensatory glycolysis were also found reduced in Igf1r−/− MEFs. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting a stimulatory effect of IGF signaling on energy production in different cell types including primary human lymphoblasts, rat neurons, primary mouse astrocytes and muscle cells [82–85]. Interestingly, pyruvate absence significantly decreased mitochondrial respiration in Igf1r−/− MEFs but not in control Igf1rfl/fl MEFs. Of note, these results are in line with a recent study with human and rat cardiomyocytes where stimulation with IGF1 increased mitochondrial respiration along with a reduction in PDH phosphorylation [86]. Disruption of IGF1R function was previously associated with several beneficial metabolic alterations [79–81]. Moreover, we have recently reported that CreERT2 middle aged mice are able to counteract the negative effects of a high fat diet in glucose metabolism, adiposity and inflammation [87]. Transcriptomic changes altering the metabolic profile could also be due to the accumulation of proinflammatory immune cells in the lungs of Igf1rfl/fl-BLM mice since they are known to preferentially use glycolysis and high lactate production pathways [88, 89]. Further studies on IGF1R- dependent differential substrate use and energy production should be carried out to further explore these mechanisms.

In our transcriptomic data, several key genes involved in the positive regulation of mitophagy and mitochondrial quality control were found to be up-regulated (Pink1, Parkin and Bnip3). Depending on the severity of mitochondrial dysfunction, PINK1 mediates the decision between promoting mitophagy or preventing apoptosis through PARKIN phosphorylation [90, 91] and BCL2 interacting protein 3 (BNIP3) has also been described to be highly involved in mitochondrial quality control and suppresses PINK1 proteolytic cleavage to promote mitophagy [92]. Thus, upregulation of Pink1, Parkin and Bnip3 would suggest a positive regulation of mitophagy in CreERT2-BLM lungs. In contrast, most of the literature refers IGF/IGF1R signaling as protector of mitochondria, improving mitophagy, mitochondrial function and biogenesis [93–95]. Mitochondrial DNA content in mouse lungs and MEFs, although found increased after BLM challenge, were not significantly affected by IGF1R depletion. Similarly, the study on mitochondrial mass, morphology and network connectivity in BLM-treated MEFs did not show significant differences in mitochondrial morphology or fragmentation. However, these MEFs did show a higher mitochondrial volume paired with senescence-like cell enlargement in BLM-treated Igf1rfl/fl MEFs that was partially reversed in Igf1r−/− MEFs. Thus, Igf1r−/− MEFs presented a milder senescence-like morphology in response to BLM challenge, which includes abnormal accumulation of mitochondria. Longer-term assessment of cellular senescence markers after BLM treatment revealed a moderate reduction in p21 protein levels Igf1r−/− MEFs compared to Igf1rfl/fl MEFs, but without demonstrating a reduction in SA-β-gal staining.

In-depth exploration of DEGs involved in autophagy did not reveal a clear effect of IGF/IGF1R signaling on autophagy-related functions in the injured lung but rather contradictory or bidirectional roles, in the same way that is profusely reflected in the literature [96–99]. These contradictory results could indicate that IGF1R effects on autophagy are tissue-, cell type- or even cell status-dependent. The different cellular composition present in the injured lungs could also explain the complexity of DEGs related to autophagy in the CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM comparison.

Importantly, we also identified predominantly up-regulated DEGs involved in epigenetic mechanisms in CreERT2-BLM vs. Igf1rfl/fl-BLM lungs, including histones, histone methylases and HDACs. Of note, many of them are involved in mediating gene repression by chromatin condensation [100–106]. Moreover, the fact that CreERT2-BLM mice show increased 5-methylcytosine (5-mC) in genomic lung DNA and that Igf1r−/− MEFs exhibit reduced histone H3 acetylation support the massive gene transcription repression in BLM-challenged IGF1R-deficient lungs since DNA methylation and decreased histone acetylation increase chromatin condensation, favoring repression of gene expression [107, 108]. There are some evidences that IGF1/IGF1R signaling regulates expression of histone deacetylases and alters gene activation by histone modifications [109, 110]. Moreover, it has been shown that IGF1R phosphorylates H3 to induce gene expression and chromatin remodeling [111]. Furthermore, loss of IGF1R and insulin receptor (INSR) in absence of their ligands was demonstrated to induce a major decrease in the expression of multiple imprinted genes and miRNAs concomitant with an increase in DNA methylation, which was not associated with changes in expression of DNA methylases, suggesting a non-canonical IGF1R/IR-mediated mechanism [112]. In this sense, IGF1R would be acting as an unblocker of the epigenetic load after the aggressive damage of BLM to the lungs. Altogether, these data suggest that IGF1R could be a key component that regulates epigenetics in ARDS.

This study presents several limitations. First, the use of bulk RNA sequencing, while offering broader transcriptomic coverage and greater sensibility than scRNA-seq, lacks the resolution to identify specific pulmonary cell subtypes involved in the observed lung phenotype allowing to delineate cell-type-specific responses. Second, the use of primary MEFs as an in vitro model may not fully recapitulate the biology of adult lung fibroblasts, potentially affecting the translatability of mechanistic findings. Lastly, although the investigation of multiple IGF1R-mediated effects in BLM-induced lung injury provides a broader perspective, it may have limited the depth of mechanistic insight into individual pathways. Ongoing studies are being conducted to overcome these limitations and to further clarify the role of IGF1R signaling in ALI.

Conclusions

Herein we analyzed RNA expression profiles from both human COVID-19 lungs and murine BLM-induced ALI and investigated the transcriptomic impact of IGF1R depletion in BLM-challenged mouse lungs. Our results support a complex regulatory network involving IGF system components in maintaining lung homeostasis under physiological conditions and following ALI. Notably, IGF1R deficiency led to significant alterations in gene expression across multiple functional categories, including metabolism, cell cycle, mitochondrial homeostasis, DNA damage, and epigenetic regulation. These results offer new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying ALI and the “cytokine storm,” implicating IGF1R as a major modulator. Overall, our data expand the current understanding of IGF1R-mediated transcriptomic regulation in the lung and underscore its potential as a therapeutic target in ALI.
