The Impact of Hiatus Hernia Size on Dysplasia Progression in Patients With Barrett's Esophagus
Authors: E. Young, S. Hoile, A. Singh, H. Philpott, R. Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and Study AimsBarrett's esophagus (BE) is the only known precursor to esophageal adenocarcinoma, a condition with high morbidity and mortality and rapidly rising incidence in the Western population. Hiatus hernias increase exposure to acid and bile in the esophagus, driving epithelial damage that eventually leads to BE and dysplasia. Evidence is limited regarding the influence of hiatus hernias on dysplasia progression in BE, particularly in the modern era of endoscopy.Patients and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained database, including patients undergoing surveillance endoscopy for BE at a single Australian tertiary center from 1999 to 2025. Endoscopic and histological data were recorded from all surveillance endoscopies and stratified according to the presence and size of hiatus hernia.ResultsA total of 547 patients with BE were included, of whom 72.8% were male. 46.6% (n = 255) had a hiatus hernia measuring ≥ 3 cm. Progression of dysplasia during the study period was more common in those with a ≥ 3 cm hiatus hernia (13.7% vs. 3.8%; HR 3.12, p = 0.001), as was progression to at least high‐grade dysplasia (10.2% vs. 1.6%; HR 2.65, p = 0.012). There was a corresponding reduction in progression‐free survival in the ≥ 3 cm hiatus hernia group (128 months vs. 189, p < 0.001).ConclusionHiatus hernias, particularly those measuring ≥ 3 cm, significantly increase the likelihood of dysplasia progression in BE. Patients with ≥ 3 cm hiatus hernias should be considered high risk when undertaking endoscopic treatment and surveillance. Further research is required to establish the role of antireflux surgery in parallel with resection and eradication therapy.

Introduction

Barrett's esophagus (BE) represents a pivotal stage in the progression from benign gastroesophageal reflux disease to life‐threatening esophageal adenocarcinoma. It is characterized by a gradual change from the resilient squamous lining of the distal esophagus to a metaplastic intestinal epithelium prone to dysplastic transformation, in response to chronic gastroesophageal reflux [1]. While this metaplasia is an adaptive change to protect from ongoing injury, it is widely accepted as being the only precursor lesion to esophageal adenocarcinoma, a condition with a rapidly rising incidence in Western populations [2, 3, 4, 5]. Despite decades of research, the precise factors that drive progression from benign metaplasia to dysplasia and eventually adenocarcinoma remain incompletely understood.

The presence of a hiatus hernia results in disruption of physiologic antireflux mechanisms, with the stomach herniating above the diaphragm and a subsequent loss of synchrony between the lower esophageal sphincter and the diaphragmatic narrowing [6]. This anatomical insult leads to prolonged acid exposure, impaired mucosal clearance, and a theoretically more hostile microenvironment for the development and progression of BE and dysplasia [7]. It is therefore not surprising that hiatus hernias have been demonstrated to increase the risk of BE, particularly long‐segment BE (> 3 cm) [8]. Despite this, evidence for the impact of a hiatus hernia on dysplasia progression remains limited, particularly in the modern era of endoscopic BE management. We sought to address this question by analyzing more than 20 years of BE surveillance data in our center.

Patients/Materials and Methods

We conducted a retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained database including patients undergoing surveillance endoscopy for BE at a single Australian tertiary center from 1999 to 2025. Baseline patient characteristics were documented, including age, sex, BE length (according to the Prague classification), and the presence and size of hiatus hernia. The date of each procedure, as well as histological results from biopsies taken, were documented for all surveillance endoscopies.

All procedures were performed by consultant gastroenterologists or gastroenterology advanced trainees with direct consultant supervision. Procedural sedation was at the discretion of the endoscopist and/or the anesthetist. BE was defined according to the 2011 American Gastroenterological Association Position Statement as being any extent of metaplastic columnar epithelium that predisposes to cancer development, replacing the stratified squamous epithelium of the distal esophagus [9]. We defined dysplasia progression as dysplasia that developed more than 3 months after an initially non‐dysplastic endoscopy. Dysplasia detected before this interval was considered missed at initial endoscopy. All cases with dysplastic BE were confirmed by two gastrointestinal pathologists. Surveillance was performed at the discretion of the endoscopists but was in line with ACG guidelines for non‐dysplastic BE (3‐yearly surveillance for ≥ 3 cm BE segment, 5‐yearly for < 3 cm).

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 29. Confidence intervals for proportions were calculated using the Agresti–Coull Method. Fisher's exact tests were used to calculate significance for dichotomous variables and unpaired t‐tests for continuous variables. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to represent progression‐free survival, with Cox regression analyses to test statistical significance. The proportional hazards assumption was checked and no violations were detected, supporting the validity of the model. This study was approved by the Central Adelaide Local Health Network Human Research Ethics Committee (Reference Number: 19244). A waiver of consent was obtained, given the retrospective nature of the study.

Results

A total of 547 patients with BE were included in the study, of which 72.8% were male. 63.4% (n = 347) of patients had a hiatus hernia, with 46.6% (n = 255) having a hiatus hernia measuring 3 cm or more (Table 1). The mean age at most advanced histology or the end of the study period was 61.4, with a mean hiatus size of 2.6 ± 0.11 cm and a maximum hiatus size of 13 cm. The mean length of BE was 2.8 ± 0.15 cm circumferentially and 3.9 ± 0.14 cm maximally. This study included a total of 1388 patient‐years follow‐up, with a mean follow‐up per patient of 28.3 months (±42.3). There was no difference in duration of follow‐up between patients with a ≥ 3 cm hiatus hernia and those without (30.22 vs. 26.61 months, p = 0.32). 12.8% (n = 70) of cases had at least low‐grade dysplasia (LGD) during the study period, with 9.3% (n = 51) having high‐grade dysplasia (HGD) and 13.3% (n = 73) adenocarcinoma (Table 2). 8.4% (n = 46) of patients had progression of dysplasia during the study period, at a median of 34 months after their initial endoscopy at our center. 6.4% (n = 35) of patients progressed to at least HGD and 3.7% (n = 20) to adenocarcinoma. Of those who progressed to adenocarcinoma the majority were early stage, including 55% (n = 11) T1a, 25% (n = 5) T1b, and 20% (n = 4) T2 adenocarcinomas.

Given 63.4% of patients in this study had a hiatus hernia of any size, ROC curves were generated (Figure 1) to establish the optimal cut‐off point for hiatus hernia size for the prediction of dysplasia progression, in order to improve specificity. When analyzed for both dysplasia progression of any degree, as well as for progression to at least HGD, the optimal cut‐off represented by the Youden's Index was the presence of a hiatus hernia ≥ 3 cm, hence this was then used for subsequent analysis. Out of the 255 patients with a hiatus hernia of 3 cm or more, 13.7% (n = 35) of patients had dysplasia progression during the study period (HR 3.12, p = 0.001), with 10.2% (n = 26) progressing to at least HGD (HR 2.65, p = 0.012) and 5.1% (n = 13) progressing to adenocarcinoma (HR 1.7, p = 0.25).

Predictors of dysplasia progression were then analyzed using a multiple variable Cox regression model, including hiatus hernia ≥ 3 cm as well as potential confounders of sex, age at worst histology, and BE length according to the Prague classification (Table 4). While male sex (HR 3.46), age at worst histology (HR 1.05), and ≥ 3 cm hiatus hernia (HR 2.3) all remained statistically significant, the circumferential and maximal length of the Barrett's segment had no significant impact (HR 1.0 and 1.02, respectively).

Discussion

This study adds to the evidence implicating the presence and size of a hiatus hernia as an important risk factor in the development and progression of dysplasia in BE. As early as 2002, Avidan et al. demonstrated that hiatus hernias were over‐represented in a population of patients with BE and HGD or adenocarcinoma [10]. More recently, a large 2021 US registry study demonstrated that those with a hiatus hernia as well as BE were more likely to be diagnosed with LGD (aOR 34.5) and HGD (aOR 14.7) compared to those without [11]. However, this is not without controversy, as Sikkema et al. published a prospective cohort study in 2011 including 713 patients with non‐dysplastic BE or LGD, and found no impact of hiatus hernia size on progression (OR 0.9, p = 0.8) [12]. Nevertheless, only 16% of patients in the study by Sikkema et al. had LGD at baseline, with 84% having non‐dysplastic BE. Of this non‐dysplastic group, only 1.9% of patients had progressive dysplasia during the study period; hence, the study was unlikely to be adequately powered to demonstrate the impact of hiatus hernia presence and size. Our cohort included more patients with baseline dysplasia and therefore, unsurprisingly, more patients with dysplasia progression during the study period—allowing further delineation of these key risk factors. As a result, our study has added to the evidence supporting the role of hiatus hernias in dysplasia progression, demonstrating that even in the context of a BE surveillance program, those with a hiatus hernia (in particular those with a hiatus hernia ≥ 3 cm) are at increased risk of dysplasia progression over time, including progression to HGD and adenocarcinoma.

The previously mentioned large registry study by Kwon et al. provided strong evidence for the association between hiatus hernias and dysplasia in BE due to the size of the study (including almost 120 000 patients), however there are limitations associated with the use of ICD codes to define variables [11]. One such limitation is the inability to quantify the size of the hiatus hernia in these patients. Consequently, the impact on clinical practice is limited to the awareness of this high‐risk population rather than identifying specific candidates for surgical intervention, as it is not feasible to recommend hiatus hernia repair in all patients with BE. In our study, we demonstrated through analysis of ROC curves the optimal hiatus size cut‐off for predicting risk of dysplasia progression as being ≥ 3 cm. Not only does our data influence risk stratification for patients with large hiatus hernias, it also lays the foundation for further research into the role of antireflux surgery.

Current evidence regarding the impact of antireflux surgery on dysplasia in BE is conflicting and has several limitations. The most recent meta‐analysis favored fundoplication with a reduction in progression to dysplasia or adenocarcinoma (OR 0.34, p = 0.04) compared to medical therapy [13]. However, the only RCT included in this analysis from Parrilla et al. in 2003 found no difference between medical and surgical therapy, with only two patients in each group (3% and 5%, respectively) developing HGD [14]. More recent evidence, including a 2024 multinational cohort study of almost 34 000 patients with BE, found no difference in the risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma over 10–32 years of follow‐up [15]. Nevertheless, many of these studies may be hampered by a lack of targeted selection criteria for the surgical arm, including all patients with BE irrespective of hiatus hernia presence or size. Additionally, these studies frequently exclude patients with any prior history of BE dysplasia prior to surgery, limiting the study population to those at lower risk of dysplasia progression [16]. Our study raises the question as to whether those with a hiatus hernia ≥ 3 cm and/or a prior history of dysplasia should be considered for antireflux and hernia repair surgery. These patients are at a greater risk of dysplasia progression and may be more refractory to endoscopic eradication therapy such as RFA [17]. Further research with more targeted selection of surgical candidates should therefore be considered in this area.

Our results add further to the controversy regarding the impact of BE length on the risk of dysplasia. According to our multiple variable Cox regression model, a hiatus hernia ≥ 3 cm in size, male sex and increasing age were all associated with increased risk of dysplasia progression, while the length of circumferential or maximal BE was not. While multiple studies have shown higher risk of dysplasia with increasing BE length, others including a large registry study from 2011 have found no impact of BE length when controlled for additional confounders [18, 19, 20, 21]. Of note, in our study those with a hiatus hernia ≥ 3 cm had a significantly longer mean circumferential (mean difference 1.6 cm, p < 0.001) and maximal (mean difference 1.5 cm, p < 0.001) BE length, a finding which has been similarly supported elsewhere [8]. In the studies mentioned above, the size of hiatus hernia has not been included in multivariate analyses as a potential confounder. Where it has been considered, it has included only the presence or absence of a hiatus hernia, a notoriously unreliable endoscopic finding in terms of interpretation and reporting [22]. It is therefore possible that those studies demonstrating higher dysplasia risk in longer BE segments are confounded by the impact of large hiatus hernias increasing cumulative acid and bile exposure, thereby increasing the ongoing mucosal damage driving dysplasia development. This would be supported by a 2020 study from Barrie et al. which found the proximal extent of early adenocarcinoma to be < 3 cm from the gastroesophageal junction in 40% of cases rather than in the proximal end of long BE segments [23]. Additionally, these predominantly retrospective studies would be confounded by bias related to an increased likelihood of surveillance in longer‐segment BE.

This study is the first to demonstrate the role of hiatus hernia presence and size on the risk of dysplasia progression in BE. However, there are limitations. Although this is a prospectively maintained database, the data were analyzed retrospectively. Consequently, some datapoints were not included in our database that may have made a meaningful addition to the multivariable analysis, including proton pump inhibitor use and adherence (though their role in prevention of dysplasia progression in BE remains controversial), reflux symptom severity, and obesity [24]. Additionally, objective quantification of acid and nonacid reflux was not included in our study, which represents an important unmeasured confounder as the association between hiatus hernia size and dysplasia likely represents persistent uncontrolled reflux. Nevertheless, this is of doubtful clinical significance as our results would still support consideration of antireflux surgery and hiatus hernia repair in these patients. A further limitation is that the documentation of hiatus hernia size was based on subjective endoscopic assessment. The alternative of performing cross‐sectional imaging or barium swallow in all patients would not only have subjected them to additional investigation and radiation but would also have limited generalisability, as these studies are rarely performed in clinical practice for the measurement of hiatus size. Utilizing a “cut‐off” point of ≥ 3 cm hiatus hernia size may reduce the subjectivity in the documentation of hiatus hernia presence at endoscopy, although the inter‐observer reliability of this measurement has not been proven.

In summary, patients with a ≥ 3 cm hiatus hernia are at increased risk of dysplasia in BE and are more likely to have progressive dysplasia during surveillance, with an associated reduction in progression‐free survival. Further prospective studies are needed to determine whether patients with larger hiatus hernias and BE, with or without dysplasia, should undergo antireflux surgery instead of routine medical management. Meanwhile, clinicians should recognize the increased risk in these patients during endoscopic surveillance and when determining future surveillance intervals.
