Robotic sleeve right upper lobectomy with thymic/pericardial fat flap buttress
Authors: Brenden Sheridan, Jane Zhao, Calvin Jung, Angel Doño, Erin Koffman, Ganpat Valaulikar, Thomas Ng
Published: 2024

Robotic anastomosis after sleeve right upper lobectomy.

Central Message

When feasible, sleeve lobectomy is preferred over pneumonectomy for lung cancer. We describe the surgical technique for robotic sleeve right upper lobectomy with a thymic/pericardial fat flap buttress.

When feasible, sleeve lobectomy is preferred over pneumonectomy for resection of central lung cancers, as it decreases morbidity while maintaining equivalent disease-free survival.1,2 For the complex procedure of sleeve lobectomy, the robotic approach has been shown to be feasible, safe, and oncologically effective.3 In this report, we provide a detailed technical description of robotic sleeve right upper lobectomy in a patient with endobronchial tumor (Figure 1), with pathology showing a 2.5-cm typical carcinoid tumor, T2aN0 (main bronchus involvement). Institutional Review Board approval was not required; informed written consent for publication was provided.

B, Dotatate positron emission tomography scan showing right upper lobe collapse (star) from an endobronchial avid lesion (arrow) but without evidence of metastatic disease.

Surgical Technique

Airway Management and Positioning

Bronchoscopy is performed to ensure that sleeve lobectomy is feasible. Lung isolation is by left-sided double-lumen endotracheal tube. The patient is placed in the left lateral decubitus position, and the bed is flexed (Figure 2, A).

A, The patient is placed in the lateral decubitus positioned with the operating table flexed to allow for the intercostal spaces to widen and the hip to drop, thereby optimizing the mobility of the robotic arms. B, Robotic ports are place into 2 interspaces, the seventh and eighth. Robotic arm 1 is placed through the r port for retraction, robotic arm 2 is placed through the L port for left-handed instruments, robotic arm 3 is placed through the C port for the 30-degree camera, robotic arm 4 is placed through the R port for right-handed instruments, and port A is used by the bedside assistant.

Port Placement

Four robotic ports are placed in the lower chest into 2 intercostal spaces (approximately the seventh and eighth; Figure 2, B). The 8-mm camera port is placed in the mid-axillary line eight interspace. For the right-handed instruments, a 12-mm port is placed as anteriorly as possible in the seventh interspace. All stapling is performed through this port. For the left-handed instruments, a posterior 8-mm port is placed in the eighth interspace. The 8-mm retraction port is placed further posteriorly in the seventh interspace. A bedside 8-mm assist port is placed in the eighth interspace between the camera and the anteriormost port. CO2 (8 mm Hg pressure, 20 L/min flow rate) is delivered via a noncamera port to avoid fogging. The Da Vinci Xi robot (Intuitive) is docked.

Instruments

We use a 30-degree camera, because this optimizes the ability to “look around” structures during dissection. For the working arms (right hand and left hand), we prefer long bipolar and fenestrated bipolar graspers. For retraction, we use a tip-up fenestrated grasper. The bedside assistant uses the assist port for suctioning and extraction of lymph node specimens.

Inferior and Posterior Dissection

The pulmonary ligament is divided, and station 8 and 9 nodes are resected as available (Video 1, A). Dissection continues posterior to the hilum, removing hilar/station 10 nodes, followed by subcarinal/station 7 lymph node dissection. The posterior bifurcation between the right upper lobe bronchus and bronchus intermedius is then dissected, where the sump/station 11 node is resected to expose the underlying pulmonary artery.

Cephalad and Anterior Dissection

With the upper lobe retracted inferiorly, paratracheal lymph node dissection is performed, resecting stations 4R and 2R nodes (Video 1, B). Hilar/station 10 nodes adjacent to the right mainstem bronchus are resected. The lung is then retracted posteriorly for anterior hilar dissection. The superior pulmonary vein is dissected and divided with a robotic white 45-mm vascular stapler, sparing the vein to the middle lobe. Behind the divided vein, interlobar/station 11 nodes are resected to expose arteries to the upper lobe, the anterior trunk, and posterior ascending aorta, both of which are divided with a robotic 45-mm vascular stapler. Right upper lobe nodes/station 12 are then resected. Dissection of the anterior aspect of the bifurcation between the upper lobe bronchus and bronchus intermedius then meets up with the earlier posterior dissection of this same region, creating an open path to visualize the posterior chest wall. This allows for fissure division with several robotic 45-mm blue staplers from anterior to posterior, minor fissure first and then the major fissure between the right upper and lower lobes. This “fissure-less” technique avoids direct fissure dissection, which minimizes air leaks when incomplete fissures are encountered.

Bronchial Sleeve Anastomosis

Intraoperative bronchoscopy aids in determining the location of the airway incisions (Video 1, C). Bronchus intermedius and right mainstem bronchus incisions are made just distal and proximal to the tumor, respectively, using robotic rounded-tip scissors. Margins of the right mainstem bronchus and bronchus intermedius are sent for frozen analysis to ensure that they are free of microscopic tumor. We anastomose the bronchus intermedius to the right mainstem bronchus using 2 running absorbable barbed 3-0 Stratafix (Ethicon) sutures on an RB-1 needle, along with the robotic Mega Suture-Cut needle driver, which has suture cutting blades at its proximal aspect, thus reducing the number of instrument exchanges. The first suture is used to complete the half of the anastomosis farther away from the camera, beginning from mid-cartilaginous to mid-membranous aspect (Figure 3, A). The second suture is then used to complete the remaining half of the anastomosis (ie, the half closer to the camera). Often there is a size mismatch, which requires a conscious effort to travel more on the mainstem bronchus during suturing; this will result in expected telescoping of the anastomosis. Flexible bronchoscopy is performed to confirm a widely patent and healthy anastomosis and to clear the airways of any blood or secretions.

A, Robotic anastomosis of the bronchus intermedius (BI) with the right mainstem bronchus (RM) after sleeve right upper lobectomy, with suture 1 completing the half farther away from the camera and suture 2 completing the half closer to the camera. B, Buttress using a thymic/pericardial fat flap (F) to completely wrap the anastomosis (A).

Thymic/Pericardial Fat Flap for Buttress

Although there are no comparative studies to inform superiority, we prefer to use a thymic/pericardial fat flap over an intercostal muscle flap owing to the ease of harvest and the ability to provide a 360-degree wrap (Video 1, D). A 360-degree intercostal muscle wrap may result in the formation of a constricting ring of bone from residual periosteum. This flap might not be available in patients with prior anterior mediastinal surgery, such as sternotomy for cardiac procedures or in patients with minimal tissue within the anterior mediastinum. Pedicled thymic pericardial fat is harvested from below, beginning at the diaphragm and carried to the cephalad, with a pedicled blood supply and drainage superiorly. Pitfalls include phrenic nerve injury if dissection comes too close; entry into the pericardium; which is minimized by using bipolar energy during dissection; and entry into the left pleura, which is rarely problematic, as the CO2 is readily absorbed. The flap is then passed around the anastomosis for a complete 360-degree wrap and maintained in place with sutures (Figure 3, B). In theory, this flap protects against any air leaks caused by gaps in the suture line, anastomotic dehiscence, or fistulization between the airway and pulmonary artery.

Final Steps

The specimen is removed via an endoscopic bag after widening either the anterior or assist port. A 28 Fr chest tube is placed through the camera port, the lung is expanded, and a leak test is performed. To avoid positive pressure on the anastomosis, the patient should be extubated.

Conclusions

Here we describe in detail our technique for robotic sleeve right upper lobectomy with a thymic/pericardial fat flap buttress. Our patient had a typical carcinoid tumor, but this technique can be applied to any endobronchial non–small cell lung cancer involving the origin of the upper lobe bronchus that, after negative margin resection, allows for some remaining bronchus intermedius and right main bronchus for anastomosis. The robotic platform facilitates the completion of this complex thoracic procedure by a minimally invasive technique.
