Immediate page kidney during transplantation: A case report and literature review
Authors: Indrawarman Soerohardjo, Tanaya Ghinorawa, Ahmad Shafwan Natsir, Toni Febriyanto, Patria Putrapratama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPage kidney is a rare but critical post-transplant complication caused by subcapsular hematoma, leading to graft hypoperfusion and dysfunction. We report a 26-year-old male who developed immediate Page kidney intraoperatively, presenting with elevated resistive indices and reversed diastolic flow. Timely diagnosis using Doppler ultrasound enabled urgent re-exploration and hematoma evacuation, successfully restoring graft function. A literature review of 40 cases highlights that early identification and prompt surgical intervention are critical to favorable outcomes. These findings reinforce the need for heightened clinical vigilance, prompt imaging, and timely surgical management to preserve graft viability and patient outcomes.Keywords: Kidney transplantation, Page kidney, Subcapsular hematoma, Allograft dysfunction, Post-transplant complications

Background

Immediate Page kidney (PK) in the post-transplantation setting is a rare yet serious complication, most commonly resulting from subcapsular hematoma formation secondary to biopsy, trauma, or interventional procedures such as angioplasty.1,2 It typically presents with abrupt allograft dysfunction and severe hypertension, and may clinically mimic acute rejection—a phenomenon referred to as “pseudo-rejection”.3, 4, 5 This resemblance can complicate diagnosis, underscoring the importance of distinguishing PK from true rejection to ensure appropriate therapeutic intervention.

Early recognition and timely management are essential to prevent irreversible graft damage. The condition emphasizes the need for vigilant postoperative surveillance and the routine use of diagnostic imaging modalities, particularly Doppler ultrasonography, to identify hemodynamic alterations suggestive of PK.3,5 Accurate diagnosis and rapid decompression of the compressive lesion, when indicated, are key to preserving graft viability and improving patient outcomes.

A comparative analysis of Page kidney (PK) cases was conducted through a structured literature review using PubMed, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar databases. Search terms included “Page Kidney,” “Page Renal,” “Page Phenomenon,” “Renal Transplantation,” “Kidney Transplantation,” “Renal Allograft,” and “Kidney Allograft.” Inclusion criteria encompassed full-text case reports or case series specifically addressing PK in the post-transplant setting. Studies outside this scope, such as analytical research articles or literature reviews, were excluded. The initial search yielded 278 articles; following the removal of eight duplicates and the exclusion of 152 studies due to irrelevance or lack of full-text access, 118 articles remained. After full-text screening, 26 studies comprising 40 individual PK cases were included in the final analysis. This study aims to summarize the clinical characteristics, diagnostic approaches, and therapeutic interventions reported in post-transplantation PK cases to enhance early recognition, guide appropriate management, and mitigate future occurrences through early diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old male with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), maintained on hemodialysis, was admitted for living donor renal transplantation. His past medical history was notable for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with ejection fraction of 40 % and latent cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection. After a thorough preoperative evaluation, including successful immunologic crossmatching and compatibility assessment, the donor kidney was procured from a biologically related living donor.

The transplantation was performed in the right iliac fossa via a standard extraperitoneal approach. The renal artery and vein were anastomosed end-to-side to the external iliac artery and vein, respectively, without intraoperative complications. Total ischemia time was 1 hour 7 minutes and 23 seconds, including a first warm ischemic time I of 3 minutes 8 seconds, cold ischemic time of 22 minutes 30 seconds, and a second warm ischemic time of 38 minutes 52 seconds. Urinary continuity was re-established 3 minutes post-reperfusion. No bleeding was observed at the vascular anastomoses.

Following fascial and skin closure, intraoperative Doppler ultrasonography identified a hypoechoic lesion measuring 10.7 x 3.5 × 1.8 cm surrounding the graft, suggestive of a subcapsular hematoma. Simultaneously, a rise in resistive indices (RI) from 0.59 to 0.70 (pre-fascial closure) to 1.08 (post-fascial closure) with reversed diastolic flow in the interlobar, interlobular, and segmental arteries indicated significant impairment of renal perfusion (See Fig. 1).

Color Doppler ultrasound of the renal artery of the transplanted kidney. (a) Pre-evacuation: subcapsular hematoma (red arrow) with increased resistive index (RI) of 1.08 and reversed diastolic flow post-fascial closure. (b) Post-evacuation: RI normalized to 0.62, indicating restored graft function.

The surgical team proceeded immediate re-exploration with capsulotomy technique and evacuation of the subcapsular hematoma (See Fig. 2). Following hematoma evacuation, the RI of the mid-interlobar artery normalized to 0.62, with a peak systolic velocity of 30.23 cm/s, consistent with restored graft perfusion.

Subcapsular hematoma measuring 10.7 x 3.5 × 1.8 cm during re-exploration.

Postoperatively, the patient had a stable clinical course. Urine output was 0.96 mL/kg/h, and there were no signs of delayed graft function. He was discharged on postoperative day seven with good graft function. At follow-up, the patient reported satisfactory recovery with stable renal function and no recurrence of complications after the subcapsular hematoma was successfully addressed through re-exploration.

Discussion

Page Kidney (PK) is a rare but serious cause of acute allograft dysfunction post-transplant, typically due to subcapsular hematoma compressing the renal parenchyma and impairing perfusion.3,6 Though often presenting with hypertension and rising creatinine, PK mimics acute rejection but differs in etiology—mechanical rather than immunologic. Triggers include biopsy, trauma, lymphocele, or surgical manipulation.2,7,8,8, 8, 9, 10 In transplant recipients, the critical issue is not systemic hypertension but compromised graft perfusion. Early recognition is essential, with Doppler ultrasound serving as the key diagnostic tool, showing elevated resistive indices or reversed diastolic flow.3 Prompt surgical intervention, such as hematoma evacuation or capsulotomy, is vital to restore perfusion and preserve graft function.11

A comprehensive review of 40 reported cases revealed that PK most frequently affects males (82.5 %) across a broad age range (16–72 years), with over half presenting within the first postoperative week (Table 1). Clinical manifestations included oliguria or anuria, flank or abdominal pain, hematuria, and hypertensive episodes.8,12,13 Triggers varied, with renal biopsy (25 %), surgical manipulation (20 %), trauma (15 %), and vascular interventions such as angioplasty (12.5 %) being notable contributors; spontaneous hematomas accounted for 27.5 % of cases.9, 10, 11,14, 15, 16, 17 These findings emphasize that PK can occur across the full spectrum of the perioperative and post-transplant period, necessitating sustained clinical vigilance.

Doppler ultrasonography served as the primary diagnostic modality in nearly all cases, with hallmark findings including elevated resistive indices (>0.9), reversed or absent diastolic flow, and evidence of perinephric or subcapsular hematoma.3 Some hematomas exceeded 10 cm in diameter, further emphasizing their potential hemodynamic impact.8 Ultrasound also played a central role in guiding treatment decisions and monitoring therapeutic response. Color duplex imaging, including evaluation of peak systolic velocity and waveform morphology, added functional insights into graft perfusion.3

Therapeutically, approximately 75 % of cases underwent surgical intervention, primarily in the form of hematoma evacuation or capsulotomy.11 A smaller proportion received percutaneous drainage or were managed conservatively. Approximately one-third of patients required temporary hemodialysis, reflecting the acute severity of renal compromise. Despite the seriousness of the condition, most patients experienced functional recovery within 1–2 weeks post-intervention, and long-term graft outcomes were favorable when diagnosis and management were timely.5,11

These findings highlight several critical themes in PK management. First, early recognition remains paramount and hinges on a high index of suspicion—particularly in the setting of abrupt graft dysfunction.3,11 Second, Doppler ultrasonography should be promptly utilized to confirm diagnosis and guide intervention.5 Third, timely surgical decompression is often necessary to preserve graft viability. Importantly, all reviewed cases survived, further underscoring the treatable nature of PK when managed appropriately.11

Conclusions

In conclusion, PK should be considered a transplant emergency with a well-defined, albeit rare, etiology. Early imaging, multidisciplinary coordination, and prompt decompressive intervention are essential to prevent irreversible graft loss. Increased awareness and standardization of diagnostic and therapeutic protocols can further improve outcomes in this critical subset of transplant recipients.
