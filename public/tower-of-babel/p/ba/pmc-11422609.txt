An improved analytical solution on viscous dissipation effect in extended Stokes’ second problem in microchannel with isothermal boundaries
Authors: C.H. Hor, C.P. Tso, G.M. Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this analytical study, the fluid motion within a microchannel is induced by the oscillation of one surface parallel to the other stationary surface, termed the extended Stokes’ problem. The novelty and research gap are acquiring the thermal effect of such motion due to the viscous dissipation or fluid friction, subject to symmetric isothermal boundary conditions. The study may shed light on the role of viscous dissipation in temperature rise in the synovial fluid of an artificial hip joint, or in the fluid layer of a mechanical bearing. The full exact analytical temperature field, until now, has been unsolved, as it involves unsteady flow with manipulation of a complicated velocity field. The assumptions in the model are one-dimensional, incompressible, laminar, Newtonian flow with constant properties in a microchannel. Through the methodology of partial differential equation analysis, the temperature field is obtained in terms of Brinkman number, Prandtl number and a dimensionless angular frequency, and results are verified with a reported numerical solution, for specified range of the variables. Results complement recent approximate solutions which are valid only for the limited condition of the dimensionless angular frequency being less than or equal to unity, whereby suggesting a new Stokes number.

Introduction

The Stokes’ second problem is a classical fluid dynamics problem [1], referring to fluid motion induced by an oscillating plane surface tangent upon an infinite fluid mass initially at rest, is extended to the case of the fluid being contained within a channel with a stationary plane. Such fluid motion had been solved analytically by Shrag [2], by Bohme and Stenger [3], and by Ding et al. [4]. With the advent of broad interest in microchannel flow, with one definition of it being flow in a channel with gap size within the range 10μm≤Dh≤200μm [5,6], where Dh is the hydraulic diameter of the channel, there was awareness that the viscous dissipation or fluid friction effect could contribute significantly to the rise in fluid temperature [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Hence the literature has been abound with viscous dissipation effect studies, for examples, Xu et al. [11], Aydin [12,13], Han & Lee [14], Nonino et al. [15], Buonomo et al. [16], Altunkaya et al. [17], Uwaezuoke [18] and Boujelbene et al. [19]. However, analytical solutions to this specific case involving an oscillating surface are rather lacking, perhaps because of the complexity; and not because of the lack of applications, since the problem is basic in fluid dynamics, and also basic to some design of mechanical bearings, and to the synovial fluid gap in the artificial hip joint [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]].

Following fluid mechanics methodology, after the velocity field has been obtained by solving the momentum equation, the velocity is inserted to the energy equation to solve for the temperature field. Ding et al. [4] used a full velocity expression and solved for the temperature field, approximately, by using a time-averaged technique. The assumptions of the considered problem are the fluid is incompressible and Newtonian with constant properties, and the flow is unidimensional, laminar and unsteady due to the oscillation. The thermal boundary conditions are isothermal conditions. Thus, that time-averaged method [4] reduced the solution to that of a steady flow with no time dependence, only dependence on position and the Brinkman number Br.

Hor et al. [25,26] and Tso et al. [27] derived another velocity field expression, different in form from those of Shrag, Bohme & Stenger, or Ding et al., but it was verified to result in the same numeric values. However, they found that the new expression derived can be greatly simplified under the condition that a dimensionless frequency parameter “beta” is less than or equal to unity. With this simplified form of the velocity, it was then possible to solve the energy equation more satisfactorily, albeit subject to having the correct beta values. The result was a new temperature profile that is time-dependent and also contains the dependency on two pertinent dimensionless numbers, the Brinkman number, as well as the Prandtl number Pr. We refer to these results as the Previous Study.

The present paper reports a significant progress in the study by using the full form of the velocity field without simplification, in solving for the temperature. The results are more complicated but fully display the dependency on all values of beta, as well as on the Prandtl number and the Brinkman number. The validity is verified with numerical analysis solutions, which had been reported earlier [[28], [29], [30]], at certain random numerical values of the parameters.

Validation of results with numerical solutions

Since the analytical results are complex and experiment data are lacking in the literature, one way is to validate some results with numerical solutions of the same set of differential equations, although numerical solutions are not generic, and the execution process is less challenging. The selected ranges in the present study are 1≤Br≤10,1≤β≤10,1≤Pr≤5 and 1≤t∗≤5. Fig. 2(a), (c), (e), (g) and (i) on the left-hand side of Fig. 2 displays graphically the analytical temperature solution in Eq. (3.43). On the right-hand side of Fig. 2(b), (d), (f), (h), (j) are results generated from our reported numerical analysis using the Matlab software for the same problem [28]. The numerical scheme convergence criteria have been discussed and validated in our earlier study [28]. The value of the dimensionless variables should be non-zero, as zero value represents no fluid motion. In addition, the range for the Br and the β values is set at 10. This is because for fluid properties such as that for fluid water in microchannel flow, these values could be considered as extreme cases.

The analytical solution is plotted using the sum from 1 to 20 for each of the series in n,p and q in Eq. (3.43), respectively. The rationale is that the average fluctuation of temperature field is less than 0.01 % if we take more than the sum of 20 for each of the series in n,p and q. In other words, the solution can be considered to have converged at this sum. For the numerical study, the time step is taken to be Δt∗=0.02. The analytical results are seen in Fig. 2 to have good agreement with the numerical results at the same conditions. Parametric studies on the effects of Br,Pr, and β at selected t∗

The first parameter Br contains the viscous dissipation effect, and by comparing the effect of Br at a constant β and Pr, as in Fig. 2(a) and (c), it is clear that the Br has significantly affected the fluid temperature. The Dirichlet thermal boundary allows the frictional heat generated to diffuse out of the microchannel, leading to the highest temperature occurring around the middle of the microchannel, and only at low β. Also, by comparing Fig. 2(a) and (c), the temperature doubles with the Br, implying that the temperature field is directly proportional to the Br.

The second parameter Pr denotes the ratio of the momentum diffusivity to the heat diffusivity, implying that the temperature field will be lower when the Pr increases, because high Pr means the momentum diffusion is dominant over the heat diffusion, and the fluid has less opportunity to be heated up. And vice versa for low Pr. The effect of Pr can be observed by comparing Fig. 2(a) with 2(i). At lower Pr, it shows a significant increase in the overall of temperature, and the opposite is the case for higher Pr. For example, when Pr=5, in Fig. 2(i), the temperature field is significantly lower as compared to that in Fig. 2(a), specifically at time t∗=1,3 and 4, and slightly lower at t∗=2 and 5.

By tuning the third parameter, β, Fig. 2(e) and (g) show that the temperature profile had slight increases when β is doubled, at constant Br and Pr. It is notable that the gradient is steeper and the peak temperature for each time occurs closer to the lower oscillating plate for higher values of β. As the lower plate oscillates at higher frequency, it will increase the velocity gradient for fluid nearer to the lower plate and hence induces more viscous dissipation there, due to the increase in friction among the fluid layers closer to the oscillating plate than to the stationary plate. The dimensionless angular frequency β is a significant dimensionless number that may deserve being a named group, in this class of fluid motion generated purely by an oscillatory plate, since when β≤ 1, the generally complicated motion can be described by a simple sinusoidal motion everywhere within the channel. This is true for all channels and not just for microchannels. Perhaps, an appropriate name for β would be Stokes no. 3, a ratio of viscous diffusion time scale to the oscillation time scale, for this class of oscillatory motion. (Note: Stokes no.1 is for vibration time in particle dynamics, while Stokes no. 2 is for calibration of rotameters [35].)

It is noteworthy that in the present analysis and analyses in the literature, the convective term in the energy equation has been tacitly neglected. It is of the form Re∙(u∗du∗dx∗), where Re is the Reynolds number. Since a microchannel refers to a minute characteristic length W, this implies a low value of Re and justifies the term to be neglected. Coincidentally, an analytical solution may be difficult to be realized otherwise.

Conclusion

(1)A new time-dependent analytical solution is obtained for the viscous dissipation effect in an extended Stokes' second problem, in a microchannel with isothermal boundaries. A full velocity solution is used to derive the temperature solution, which is the core of the novelty.(2)This solution improves greatly upon the classic solution of Ding et al. [4], which employed a full velocity solution to obtain a time-averaged temperature result. In particular, our temperature field extends the usefulness of the results by showing clear relationship with the Brinkman number, the Prandtl number, and a dimensionless angular frequency β.(3)Present temperature result also complements our recent reported temperature results based on using a simplified velocity solution, by taking only the first term in the velocity solution. Those earlier results are only valid for cases where the β is less than or equal to the critical value of unity. This also accentuates the importance of this dimensionless variable β in this class of problems, and we suggest to call β the Stokes number 3.(4)As the temperature expressions are complicated, all analytical results are verified with Matlab numerical results, over a defined set of variables, and which were detailed in the earlier study. The temperature profiles have been described and the physical phenomena explained through the key parameters.(5)The present work paves the way for future studies in this class of problems at other basic thermal boundary conditions, after which we may be able to relate better the relationship between thermal boundaries and the fluid motion behaviour. Perhaps an experimental verification should be attempted, too.
