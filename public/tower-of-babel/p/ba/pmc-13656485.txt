Reassessing the health–agriculture nexus: evidence from super-efficiency DEA and panel models in OECD countries
Authors: Fuad Selamzade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAgricultural productivity and health expenditures are closely interconnected, influencing both economic development and social welfare. Despite growing interest in the health–agriculture nexus, empirical evidence on their bidirectional relationship across Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries remains limited.MethodsThis study investigates the relationship between health expenditures and agricultural productivity using an dataset covering 38 OECD countries from 2010 to 2021. In the first stage, agricultural productivity was measured using a Super-Efficiency Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) model, allowing for the ranking of efficient countries beyond the conventional efficiency frontier. In the second stage, panel data models were employed to examine the effects of health expenditure indicators on agricultural productivity and the reverse effects of agricultural productivity on health expenditures.ResultsThe findings indicate substantial heterogeneity in agricultural productivity across OECD countries. Panel estimations reveal that public health expenditures have a positive and statistically significant effect on agricultural productivity, whereas private and out-of-pocket health expenditures exhibit limited or insignificant effects. Furthermore, higher agricultural productivity is associated with increases in per capita and public health expenditures, suggesting a reciprocal relationship between agricultural performance and health investment. The results highlight the importance of health financing in strengthening labor productivity and supporting sustainable agricultural development.ConclusionsHealth expenditures contribute not only to improved population health but also to enhanced agricultural efficiency and economic performance. Policies aimed at expanding public healthcare investments, particularly in rural areas, may generate productivity gains in agriculture while promoting broader sustainable development objectives. By integrating Super-Efficiency DEA with panel econometric techniques, this study provides novel empirical evidence on the health–agriculture nexus in OECD countries and offers relevant policy implications for both health and agricultural sectors.

Introduction

Agricultural productivity is considered a critical factor for countries' economic growth and food security. Especially in developed economies such as Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries, the sustainability and efficiency of agricultural production are directly linked to inputs from different sectors. In this context, the interaction between the health sector and agriculture should be regarded as a key component of economic and social development. Health expenditures have a direct impact on labor productivity, enhancing the physical and mental capacities of individuals working in agricultural production and thereby shaping productivity. Recent studies emphasize that health expenditures, as a component of human capital, improve labor productivity by reducing morbidity and mortality, thereby enhancing agricultural output [12, 14].

Agricultural productivity is a fundamental indicator of efficiency that expresses the relationship between the quantity of agricultural inputs used and the output obtained within a specific period. This concept is generally evaluated using total factor productivity or partial productivity measures. An increase in agricultural productivity allows for greater output per unit input, facilitating more efficient use of resources. This not only enhances the competitiveness of agricultural enterprises but also plays a critical role in ensuring food security. Within the framework of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), agricultural productivity is directly linked to poverty reduction, food security, and environmental sustainability [41, 42].

The focus on OECD countries is motivated by their heterogeneous composition, encompassing both advanced and emerging economies. OECD members are characterized by relatively high levels of public and private health financing, alongside technologically intensive agricultural production systems that simultaneously pursue sustainability objectives [41, 42]. This duality makes OECD countries an ideal setting for investigating the interplay between health investments and agricultural productivity. Furthermore, the OECD’s policy framework explicitly integrates agriculture and health within the broader agenda of sustainable development, thereby enhancing the relevance and applicability of the study’s findings for policymakers.

The historical development of agricultural productivity is closely associated with innovations in agricultural technologies. Factors such as mechanization, fertilizer use, irrigation infrastructure, high-yield seed varieties, and agricultural education have been decisive in improving productivity. Particularly, the "Green Revolution" of the second half of the twentieth century brought about significant productivity increases in developing countries and contributed to the fight against hunger. However, in the current era, new determinants of agricultural productivity include the development of sustainable production techniques, addressing climate change, and conserving natural resources. Moreover, human capital theory highlights that education and health investments are crucial for adopting modern agricultural technologies, underscoring the role of health expenditures in sustaining productivity growth [47].

From an economic perspective, agricultural productivity growth supports both producer welfare at the micro level and economic growth at the macro level. It facilitates the transfer of labor from agriculture to other sectors, thereby accelerating structural transformation. Furthermore, a productive agricultural sector helps stabilize food prices, reduce inflationary pressures, and positively contribute to the trade balance. Hence, agricultural productivity holds a central role not only in agricultural policies but also in broader development strategies. For OECD countries, this linkage is particularly important given their dual challenge of maintaining competitiveness while ensuring sustainability in line with climate and trade policies [40].

The sustainable development framework further situates agricultural productivity within global policy agendas. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) explicitly link food security (SDG 2) with health (SDG 3), recognizing their mutual reinforcement. OECD analyses highlight that agricultural productivity contributes to poverty reduction, stabilizes food prices, and supports environmental sustainability, while health investments ensure a capable workforce to sustain these outcomes [41, 42]. This dual perspective underscores the importance of examining the bidirectional relationship between health financing and agricultural outcomes.

Agricultural productivity has long been analyzed within the framework of production theory, where efficiency is defined as the optimal transformation of inputs into outputs. Classical models such as the Cobb–Douglas production function and Total Factor Productivity (TFP) measures emphasize the role of technological progress, capital, and labor in driving agricultural growth [40]. Within this framework, Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) provides a non-parametric approach to measuring efficiency, allowing for cross-country comparisons without imposing restrictive functional forms [16]. Recent methodological advances, including Dynamic Network DEA, have further enabled the decomposition of efficiency into interconnected sub-sectors, such as public health and medical care, thereby offering a more nuanced understanding of sectoral linkages [22].

This study’s novelty lies in its empirical examination of the bidirectional relationship between health expenditures and agricultural productivity across OECD countries. While most existing research has focused either on the efficiency of health systems [22] or on agricultural productivity and sustainability in isolation [26, 40], comprehensive analyses that integrate both dimensions remain scarce. By employing the Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) model developed by Charnes, Cooper, and Rhodes [16] and extending it with the Super-Efficiency approach, this study provides new methodological diversity and robust empirical evidence. In doing so, it contributes to the literature by demonstrating how health financing influences agricultural outcomes and how agricultural productivity, in turn, affects health expenditures, thereby enriching the discourse on sustainable development and human capital.

The main question of the research is whether public and private health expenditures have statistically significant effects on agricultural productivity in OECD countries? The contribution of the research to the literature is; the inclusion of DEA method in econometric research of OECD countries including developed and developing countries, and the determination of empirical evidence on the bidirectional effects between health financing and agricultural outcomes. This research makes a significant contribution to the literature by analyzing how health expenditures influence agricultural productivity. However, further empirical studies are needed to identify which components of health investments most affect agricultural performance. Future studies can provide clearer insights by conducting more detailed country-level analyses. Sect. " Literature review" presents the theoretical framework, Sect. Literature review

A review of the literature reveals that the effects of agricultural productivity on health expenditures are mostly estimated using parametric methods such as stochastic frontier analysis. There are relatively few studies employing non-parametric Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA), with most using the Malmquist Index Total Factor Productivity approach. Additionally, existing studies are generally country- or region-specific, and similar analyses for OECD countries comprising both developed and developing economies remain limited.

Gupta and Verhoeven [27] evaluated the efficiency of government spending on education and health in 37 African countries between 1984 and 1995 using Free Disposal Hull (FDH) analysis. Benin et al. [11] aimed to estimate agricultural productivity at the public and household levels in Ghana for the period 2001–2006. McNamara et al. [36] examined micro- and macro-level studies to assess the impact of public health on agricultural growth in Sub-Saharan Africa between 1960 and 2006. Allen and Qaim [4] used Stochastic Frontier Analysis and Structural Equation Modeling to explore the effects of social services on agricultural productivity in Sub-Saharan African countries during 1961–2010. Akinwale and Ayodele [2] analyzed the relationship between government expenditure components and agricultural productivity in Nigeria using the Autoregressive Distributed Lag (ARDL) technique for 1981–2017. Aboaba et al. [1] examined the effect of out-of-pocket health spending on technical efficiency in rice production in Southwestern Nigeria. Onya et al. [43] studied the impact of public health expenditures and government effectiveness on labor productivity in West Africa between 1980 and 2022. Sogah et al. [54] used the ARDL method to analyze the effect of government expenditures on agricultural productivity in Ghana for the period 2000Q1–2022Q4. Ukpe and Mohamadou [56] investigated the relationship between out-of-pocket health expenditures and agricultural production in Cameroon using annual time series data from 2000 to 2023.

Sevim and Bali [52] used the Malmquist Total Factor Productivity Index to examine agricultural efficiency in the European Union (EU) and candidate countries for 1961–2002. Mollavelioğlu and Ceylan [38] analyzed changes in agricultural sector efficiency for Türkiye and 17 EU countries between 1962 and 2006 using the Malmquist Index and convergence tests. Vlontzos, Niavis, and Manos [59] assessed the energy and environmental efficiency of the primary sector in EU countries for 2001–2008 using DEA. Allen and Ulimwengu [5] applied structural equation modeling to analyze the impact of social expenditures on health outcomes in nine Sub-Saharan countries for 1990–2002. Yeni and Teoman [62] used the Malmquist index to investigate the sustainability of the agricultural sectors in the EU and Türkiye during 2008–2019. Göksu and Demirtaş [23] examined the contribution of Regulatory Impact Analysis (RIA) to sustainable development in OECD countries using the RIA method and found that most countries had low efficiency scores, while the United Kingdom achieved its highest efficiency score due to progress made since the 1990s. While this study focuses on the policy aspect of RIA, your work adds methodological and thematic diversity to the literature by identifying the two-way interaction between health spending and agricultural productivity using RIA super efficiency analysis and panel data analysis.

Allen et al. [3] explored the relationship between health expenditures, health outcomes, and labor productivity in rural Tanzania’s 113 regions for 2008. Bilişik [13] analyzed total factor productivity in the agricultural sector for BRICS and MINT countries from 2010 to 2013. Moreno‐Enguix and Lorente Bayona [39] calculated the relationship between public sector performance and expenditure efficiency using FDH and DEA for 35 countries in 2012. Atici, Ulucan, and Bayar [6] measured agricultural productivity changes in 34 OECD countries for 1990–2014 using the Malmquist Index. Gong [24] estimated the effects of public expenditures and trade on agricultural productivity in China for 2004–2015 using Cobb–Douglas Stochastic Frontier Analysis (SFA) and Malmquist Index. Liu, Xia, and Hou [34] evaluated both static and dynamic health expenditure efficiency in China’s rural regions from 2007 to 2016 using the Super Slack-Based Measure (SBM) and Malmquist Index. Bala, Singh, and Janor [8] examined the elasticity of health expenditures on agricultural productivity growth in Southeast Asia for 2000–2016 using panel data analysis. Jakovljevic et al. [30] analyzed the efficiency of health expenditures in nine Asian economies, both OECD and non-OECD members, for 1996–2017. Erdinç and Aydınbaş [20] assessed the effectiveness of agricultural value-added and its determinants using panel data analysis in 20 selected countries for 2000–2018. Aydınbaş [7] studied factors related to agricultural productivity in Türkiye and BRICS countries using panel data methodology.

In the literature, the relationship between health care expenditures and agricultural productivity has been primarily examined using parametric methods. Studies focusing on Africa, Asia, and developing countries have used stochastic efficient frontier analysis, ARDL, and Cobb–Douglas models to estimate the impact of health care expenditures on agricultural productivity in a unilateral manner [2, 4, 24]. However, the use of nonparametric methods remains limited, with country- or regional-level analyses typically employing DEA and Malmquist TFP approaches [6, 52, 59]. The lack of a comprehensive and comparative analysis for OECD countries is a significant gap in the literature. Furthermore, most existing studies examine the impact of health care expenditures on agricultural productivity in a unilateral manner, ignoring the interactions between factors.

In this context, our study makes three original contributions to the literature. First, in contrast to the classic Malmquist Approach to Total Factor Productivity (MI TFP), we discriminated against countries that achieved maximum productivity performance using the de facto efficiency analysis (DEA) method. Second, both developed and developing countries were assessed within the same model using data for 2010–21, covering 38 OECD countries, which goes beyond the scope of ad hoc studies focused on individual countries or regions. Third, we used panel data analysis to test the bidirectional causality between health care spending and agricultural productivity. The results show that public spending on health care increases agricultural productivity, and agricultural productivity, in turn, has an impact on reducing infant mortality and under-five mortality, among other things. In this regard, the study adds a new dimension to the literature by identifying the mutual interactions between health and agriculture and providing compelling empirical evidence for OECD countries.

Methodology

The primary objective of this study is to examine the impact of health expenditures on agricultural productivity across OECD countries. The study used 2010–2021 data obtained from the data pages of 38 OECD member countries. To achieve this goal, super-efficiency scores of the countries were first calculated using widely employed agricultural input and output variables found in the literature, [1, 3, 4, 8, 12, 14, 34, 52, 59]. In the second phase, the relationship between health expenditures and other health status indicators with agricultural productivity was analyzed using the panel data analysis method.

Super-efficiency

In Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA), the Super-Efficiency (SE) model is used in cases where the Decision-Making Unit (DMU) receives an efficiency score greater than one. This occurs because each DMU does not allow itself to be part of the reference set [21, 49].

To calculate the super-efficiency score for DMU “j,” its data are excluded from the X (NxI) and Y (MxI) matrices, resulting in Nx(I-1) and Mx(I-1) matrices. When this modified linear program is run, the j-th DMU cannot be a part of its own reference set and, therefore, its efficiency score will exceed one if it was originally on the efficient frontier. This model is solved individually for each DMU using the reduced data set [18, 51].

The super-efficiency model serves as an alternative to the constant returns to scale CCR model by Charnes, Cooper, and Rhodes [16] and the variable returns to scale BCC model by Banker, Charnes, and Cooper [10]. This method can also be used in sensitivity analysis to identify outliers or in second-stage regressions using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) instead of Tobit regression to avoid truncation bias [50]. A known drawback is the infeasibility issue in some linear programs. For a discussion and resolution of this issue, see Lovell and Rouse’s [35] work titled “Equivalent Standard DEA Models to Provide Super-Efficiency Scores.”

The assumption of Constant Returns to Scale (CRS) was adopted to reflect the relatively homogeneous technological environment of OECD countries, where advanced agricultural systems limit the presence of scale economies. This assumption is consistent with prior OECD-focused DEA studies and provides a reasonable approximation of production conditions [46].

Finally, the choice of an output-oriented CCR model was deliberate. The central research question concerns the maximization of agricultural productivity given available resources. An output orientation is therefore appropriate, as it evaluates how effectively countries transform inputs into agricultural outputs. This orientation also aligns with policy objectives that emphasize increasing food production and efficiency rather than minimizing input use.

By explicitly justifying the selection of variables, the assumptions of isotonicity, multicollinearity, and constant returns to scale, and the rationale for adopting an output-oriented CCR model, the DEA component of the study is theoretically coherent and empirically robust.

Panel data analysis

In the second stage of the analysis, super-efficiency DEA scores were employed as dependent variables in panel regressions to investigate the impact of health expenditures on agricultural productivity. We acknowledge that DEA scores are generated regressors and may contain measurement error, which could potentially bias subsequent estimations. Nevertheless, the two-stage DEA framework remains a widely adopted methodology in efficiency analysis, particularly in cross-country studies, as it allows for the integration of non-parametric efficiency measures with econometric modeling [53].

To mitigate concerns regarding bias, robustness checks were conducted by varying model specifications and input–output selections, and the results remained consistent across these alternatives. This stability suggests that the findings are not unduly sensitive to measurement error in DEA scores. While bootstrap DEA methods provide formal bias correction and confidence intervals, the super-efficiency approach was deliberately chosen for its ability to discriminate among efficient decision-making units and to generate continuous scores suitable for regression analysis [15].

We explicitly recognize this limitation and emphasize that future research could adopt bootstrap DEA or other bias-correction techniques to strengthen inference. At the same time, the consistency of our results across alternative specifications provides confidence in the robustness of the current approach.

Economic studies employ both parametric and non-parametric methods. Parametric models require econometric data structured as cross-sections, time series, or panel data. The main advantage of using panel data in this study lies in its ability to analyze data across both dimensions simultaneously [25]. However, panel data models come with limitations, such as measurement errors, design flaws, short time series, nonresponse, attrition, and cross-sectional dependency [9, 28, 32, 64].

Panel data analysis explores causal relationships between economic variables by integrating cross-sectional and time-series data. As such, it allows for multiple observations of the same units over time [25].

Here, μi is the unobserved individual effect, and νit is the idiosyncratic error in E(uit) ~ N(0, σ2). If each time period applies uniformly to all cross-sections, the panel is balanced; otherwise, it is unbalanced [33, 63].

Two main approaches are used in panel regressions: Fixed Effects (FE) and Random Effects (RE). The choice between them depends on the nature of the data and research objectives [33]. The FE model assumes time-invariant individual effects and estimates coefficients using dummy variables and OLS. It restricts variation across entities to changes in intercepts [37]. The RE model, on the other hand, treats individual effects as part of the error term and assumes no correlation with the regressors. It uses Generalized Least Squares (GLS) for estimation.

If the p-value is below 0.05, the difference is systematic, favoring the Fixed Effects model; otherwise, the Random Effects model is preferred [33]. Based on Hausman test results, the assumption of independence between regressors and individual effects is either validated or rejected. Neither model is universally perfect—RE may overestimate, while FE may underestimate the true effect [31].

Before formulating the panel regression models, we conducted diagnostic tests to ensure the robustness of the econometric framework. Following descriptive statistics and pairwise correlations, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was calculated to detect potential multicollinearity among explanatory variables. The results confirmed that VIF values were below the conventional threshold, indicating that multicollinearity was not a serious concern. This procedure is consistent with the methodological approach suggested by Panda and Mahalik [45], who emphasize the importance of VIF testing in econometric studies of energy and governance.

In addition, advanced panel diagnostics were applied to account for the specific characteristics of multi-country data. Cross-sectional dependence was tested using Pesaran's Cross-Sectional Dependence test, slope heterogeneity was examined through the Delta test, and unit root properties were assessed using second-generation panel unit root tests. Furthermore, cointegration analysis was performed to verify the existence of long-run equilibrium relationships between health expenditures and agricultural productivity. These procedures follow the framework outlined by Panda, Mahalik, and Rather [44], ensuring that the empirical results are not biased by unobserved dependencies or spurious correlations.

Data and variables

The data used in the study were obtained from the data pages of the World Bank, the International Labor Organization, the World Health Organization and the Food and Agriculture Organization internet portal. The study used 2010–2021 data obtained from the data pages of 38 OECD member countries.

The dataset is structured as an unbalanced panel due to missing observations in certain years and countries. Efficiency scores in the DEA stage were calculated using complete input–output data, while panel regressions were estimated using econometric techniques suitable for unbalanced panels [9]. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the exclusion of incomplete observations did not materially affect the results.

The selection of inputs and outputs in the DEA model was guided by both theoretical foundations and established empirical practice. Agricultural employment, land use, fertilizer consumption, and freshwater withdrawals, presented in Table 1, were chosen as inputs because they represent the primary resources that directly determine agricultural production capacity. The outputs presented in Table 1 — including crop, food, and livestock production indices, cereal yields, and agricultural value added—capture the multidimensional outcomes of agricultural activity and are widely employed in cross-country efficiency studies [6, 59]. Results

Super efficiency results

Table 3 presents the super-efficiency results calculated using data from 2010 to 2021 for agricultural productivity in OECD countries. An examination of the results reveals that 12 out of 38 OECD countries—namely Belgium, Canada, Estonia, Finland, Iceland, Israel, Latvia, Luxembourg, Norway, Sweden, Türkiye, and the United States—were efficient across all years. In contrast, 13 countries—including Chile, Colombia, Denmark, France, Greece, Ireland, Japan, Lithuania, Poland, Portugal, South Korea, Spain, and Switzerland—were found to be consistently inefficient throughout the period. According to the super-efficiency scores, the top three OECD countries were Estonia, Iceland, and Norway, respectively. Estonia achieved a record-breaking super-efficiency score of 10.088 in 2018, the highest among all countries and years. Although a decline in Estonia’s super-efficiency scores was observed in 2020 and 2021, the country maintained high productivity levels in other years. Iceland exhibited a dramatic increase in its super-efficiency values beginning in 2012, rising from 5.319 in 2012 to 7.695 in 2013 and reaching 8.132 in 2021, thereby demonstrating substantial improvements in productivity. Norway and Sweden were found to have consistently high and stable levels of agricultural productivity. In Table 3, when examining countries with unstable or fluctuating performance, it is observed that Luxembourg demonstrated good performance with super-efficiency scores above 1 from 2010 to 2016. The United Kingdom’s agricultural productivity was super-efficient from 2010 to 2016, but fell below the threshold of 1 during the 2017–2021 period. A similar pattern was observed in the Netherlands, which was super-efficient between 2011 and 2018, but did not achieve efficiency in 2010 and from 2019 to 2021.

The super-efficiency scores reveal considerable heterogeneity among OECD countries. Nations with higher scores typically combine advanced technological adoption with sustainability-oriented agricultural practices. For instance, countries at the frontier have implemented precision agriculture technologies, including digital monitoring systems, satellite-based irrigation management, and sensor-driven fertilizer application. These innovations reduce input waste and enhance output efficiency. In addition, sustainable production methods such as crop rotation, integrated pest management, and organic soil enrichment contribute to long-term productivity gains while minimizing environmental externalities.

By contrast, countries with lower efficiency scores often rely on traditional labor-intensive methods, excessive fertilizer use, or unsustainable freshwater withdrawals. These practices lead to diminishing returns and environmental degradation, which are reflected in lower DEA scores. The results suggest that efficiency is not simply a function of input intensity but of strategic policy frameworks that integrate health, education, and technology transfer. High-performing countries have also benefited from coordinated policies between ministries of agriculture and health, ensuring that rural labor forces remain healthy and capable of adopting modern techniques.

For countries with lower scores, the implication is clear: adopting technology transfer programs, promoting health literacy, and investing in sustainable agricultural strategies are essential to closing the efficiency gap. International evidence from OECD-FAO reports highlights that localized adaptation of best practices—such as smart irrigation, renewable energy use in farming, and rural healthcare integration—can significantly improve both agricultural productivity and health outcomes.

The relatively high value of σₑ, which reflects the variance of the error term across countries, indicates that a substantial portion of the unexplained variation in the model stems from random fluctuations over time and country-specific unobserved heterogeneity.

In Model 8, agricultural productivity has a positive and statistically significant effect at the 1% level on government health expenditures (GHEx). These findings demonstrate the complex interplay between health expenditures and macroeconomic/agricultural indicators, suggesting that different types of health spending exert varying effects. Notably, the positive effect of private health expenditures on agricultural productivity underscores an important consideration for future health policy planning.

The estimation results of Model 12 provide a nuanced picture of the health–agriculture nexus in OECD countries. The constant term (7.639, p < 0.01) reflects a strong baseline productivity level. Current health expenditure (% of GDP): −1.347, the negative and highly significant coefficient indicates that aggregate health spending relative to GDP may reduce agricultural efficiency. This reflects macroeconomic crowding-out effects, were disproportionate health allocations limit resources available for agricultural investment. Domestic general government health expenditure (% of GDP): 0.732, public health financing exerts a positive and significant effect, underscoring the role of government-led investments in improving labor health and productivity, which in turn enhances agricultural efficiency.

Agricultural inputs: Employment in agriculture (% of total employment): −0.071, although insignificant in this specification, the negative sign is consistent with structural transformation theory, suggesting that excessive reliance on agricultural labor reduces efficiency. Agricultural land (% of land area): −0.524, the negative and significant coefficient implies that larger shares of agricultural land do not necessarily translate into higher productivity. This may reflect diminishing returns to land expansion and inefficiencies in land use management. Annual freshwater withdrawals (% of total freshwater withdrawal): −0.163, the negative effect highlights inefficiencies in water use. Excessive withdrawals may indicate unsustainable practices that undermine long-term productivity. Fertilizer consumption (kg per hectare of arable land): −0.461, the negative and significant coefficient suggests that higher fertilizer intensity is associated with declining productivity, possibly due to overuse, soil degradation, or environmental externalities.

These results emphasize that agricultural productivity in OECD countries is not simply a function of increasing inputs. Instead, input quality, efficiency, and sustainability are critical. Over-reliance on land, water, and fertilizer without technological modernization or sustainable practices leads to inefficiency. This complements the DEA findings, which showed that productivity gains depend on balanced input use and technological innovation. At the same time, the dual role of health expenditures is evident: while aggregate spending relative to GDP may crowd out resources, targeted government health investments enhance labor productivity and agricultural outcomes.

Discussion

The findings of this study are largely consistent with the existing literature that explores the impact of health expenditures on agricultural productivity. Gupta and Verhoeven [27] demonstrated that African countries underperform in terms of the efficiency of health and education expenditures compared to countries in Asia and the Western Hemisphere. Similarly, our analysis suggests that not only the amount but also the efficient utilization of health expenditures is a significant determinant of agricultural productivity. These conclusions align with the findings of Allen and Qaim [4], as well as Allen et al. [3], who emphasized the positive effect of health expenditures on labor productivity in rural Tanzania, while also noting that the magnitude of this impact varies by disease type and expenditure category.

On the other hand, divergent views in the literature suggest that the relationship between agricultural productivity and health outcomes is neither direct nor stable. McNamara et al. [36] found that low-cost health investments can enhance labor productivity, yet macro-level analyses often reveal a more complex and ambiguous relationship. Similarly, Vlontzos, Niavis, and Manos [59] discovered that countries with higher environmental standards tend to show lower performance in energy and environmental efficiency, implying that in developed nations, the relationship between efficient health spending and agricultural productivity may be more intricate.

Studies conducted on European Union (EU) countries, including Turkey, emphasize the significance of health and public spending in improving agricultural productivity. Sevim and Bali [52] found that Türkiye, despite having vast agricultural land, lagged behind EU countries in terms of agricultural efficiency. Likewise, Mollavelioğlu and Ceylan [38] reported that while Türkiye has not converged with EU countries in terms of agricultural productivity, it has been moving toward long-term equilibrium with certain member states. Our findings corroborate these results, indicating that Türkiye must focus more on the efficiency of its health expenditures to boost agricultural productivity.

Important insights on the evaluation of health expenditures’ impact on agricultural output can be drawn from studies by Allen and Ulimwengu [5] and Liu, Xia, and Hou [34]. In particular, Liu et al. [34] showed that health expenditures in China’s rural areas have displayed unstable trends over time, which has caused fluctuations in agricultural output in the long run. This supports our findings, suggesting that health expenditures should be evaluated not only in terms of short-term but also long-term impacts.

Finally, recent studies by Aydınbaş [7] and Onya et al. [43] have examined the impact of public expenditures on agricultural and labor productivity, generally reporting positive effects. However, there are also instances where these effects were found to be statistically insignificant. In developing countries, weak government performance may hinder the transformation of health expenditures into agricultural gains. In contrast, the study by Yeni and Teoman [62] found that total factor productivity in Türkiye and EU countries has increased particularly due to technological advancements. In this context, our findings suggest that the impact of health expenditures on agricultural productivity may vary across countries and that efficiency depends not only on the amount spent but also on how policies are implemented.

The empirical findings reveal a negative relationship between health expenditures as a share of GDP (HEx/GDP) and agricultural productivity, while per capita health expenditures (HExCap) demonstrate a positive effect. At first glance, these results may appear contradictory; however, they reflect distinct mechanisms operating at different levels of economic analysis.

From a macroeconomic perspective, an increase in the ratio of health expenditures to GDP may indicate inefficiencies in resource allocation. Excessive aggregate spending can crowd out investments in other productive sectors, including agriculture, thereby reducing sectoral efficiency. In particular, higher private health expenditures may divert household resources away from agricultural inputs, limiting productivity gains.

By contrast, the micro-level dimension captured by per capita health expenditures highlights improvements in the quality and accessibility of healthcare services. Greater per capita spending enhances individual health outcomes, strengthens labor capacity, and improves both physical and cognitive performance. These improvements translate into higher agricultural efficiency, as healthier workers are more capable of adopting modern technologies and sustaining productivity growth.

Thus, the dual findings are not contradictory but rather complementary: while aggregate expenditure ratios reflect macro-level resource allocation dynamics, per capita expenditures capture micro-level service quality and labor productivity effects. This distinction aligns with human capital theory, which emphasizes the role of targeted health investments in enhancing workforce productivity, and with macroeconomic resource allocation frameworks that caution against disproportionate sectoral spending.

By explicitly situating the results within these theoretical perspectives, the study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of the health–agriculture nexus. It demonstrates that the impact of health financing on agricultural productivity depends not only on the scale of expenditure but also on its distribution and effectiveness at the individual level.

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, the analysis is limited to OECD countries during the 2010–2021 period because data were obtained from the World Bank and relevant country institutions' websites, and data for some countries were not available from these sources. This limits the generalizability of the findings to non-OECD and low-income economies with different agricultural and health structures. Second, due to data availability limitations, the study focused on aggregate indicators of health expenditures and agricultural inputs, without distinguishing between specific health service categories or agricultural technologies. Finally, potential external factors such as climate change, extreme weather events, institutional quality, and technological innovation were not explicitly included in the econometric models due to insufficient data availability. Future research could address these limitations by using bootstrap DEA techniques, broader country samples, disaggregated expenditure data, and additional control variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the health-agriculture relationship or other intersectoral relationships.

Conclusion

This study aims to evaluate the effects of agricultural productivity on health expenditures and health indicators in OECD countries between 2010 and 2021. For this purpose, a non-parametric input-oriented Super-Efficiency Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) model and a parametric Stochastic Frontier Analysis (SFA) with panel data were employed to estimate agricultural productivity.

According to the super-efficiency scores, countries such as Estonia, Iceland, Norway, and Sweden consistently demonstrated high levels of agricultural performance. Estonia, in particular, reached a record efficiency score of 10.088 in 2018. Iceland also showed a remarkable upward trend after 2012. Conversely, countries like Türkiye, Luxembourg, and Ireland displayed fluctuating performance across the years. Japan, South Korea, Colombia, Poland, and Portugal had consistently low super-efficiency scores, indicating lower agricultural productivity.

Panel data analysis revealed that public health expenditures have a statistically significant positive effect on agricultural productivity, whereas the share of total health expenditures in GDP and out-of-pocket expenditures showed no significant impact. This suggests that publicly funded healthcare may enhance agricultural labor productivity, especially in rural areas. However, the relatively low explanatory power of the models highlights the need to incorporate additional variables and conduct more comprehensive analyses.

The results also demonstrate that the effect of agricultural productivity on health expenditures varies depending on the type of spending. Agricultural productivity positively influences per capita and public health expenditures but may reduce the share of health spending in GDP. These findings underscore the direct and indirect effects of agricultural development on the health sector. Yet, the limited explanatory power of the models suggests that the relationship between agricultural productivity and health expenditures should be examined using more detailed variables and extended models.

Based on the findings of this study, a set of multidimensional and evidence-based policy recommendations are proposed, covering both the agricultural and healthcare sectors.

The panel data analysis demonstrated that public health expenditures exert a statistically significant and positive effect on agricultural productivity. This finding underscores the importance of strengthening public healthcare infrastructure in rural areas to enhance the physical capacity and labor productivity of the agricultural workforce. Expanding primary healthcare services through mobile health units and family health centers, alongside tailored health insurance packages for agricultural workers, would promote preventive healthcare and sustain productivity gains. Moreover, agricultural support programs should not only aim to increase production but also integrate measures that improve rural living conditions, such as access to healthcare services, hygiene, and nutrition. These recommendations are directly linked to the empirical evidence showing that healthier workers contribute to higher agricultural efficiency and improved health outcomes, including reductions in infant and under-five mortality.

At the same time, the negative effect of total health expenditures as a share of GDP raises concerns about resource allocation efficiency. Health investments should therefore be strategically directed toward interventions that directly improve productivity, such as infectious disease control and nutrition support programs. A performance-based management approach in health expenditure administration, coupled with monitoring systems, would ensure efficiency. For countries with low efficiency levels, localized adoption of sustainable agricultural techniques, technology transfer, and educational policies from high-performing countries is recommended. Efforts to improve health literacy and agricultural technology education among rural populations would further strengthen the nexus between health and agriculture, ensuring that policy interventions are both evidence-based and operationally feasible.

These policy recommendations aim not only to enhance agricultural productivity but also to improve health-related living conditions in rural areas. The integrated approach suggested by the study can enhance the effectiveness of agricultural sector policies while maximizing the impact of rural health reforms on public health outcomes.
