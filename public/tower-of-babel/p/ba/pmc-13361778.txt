Effect of water temperature on life history traits of Radix natalensis, the intermediate host of Fasciola gigantica
Authors: Agrippa Dube, Chester Kalinda, Tawanda Manyangadze, Moses John Chimbari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadix natalensis, a gastropod, serves as an intermediate host of Fasciola gigantica in tropical and subtropical regions, causing fascioliasis, a disease of public health and veterinary importance worldwide. Predicting disease burden requires understanding how climate change influences its life-history traits. This study investigated the effects of temperature on the life-history traits of R. natalensis. Snails were exposed to six temperature treatments (15.5 ℃, 19.5 ℃, 20.3 ℃ (control), 24.6 ℃, 29.8 ℃, and 35.1 ℃). For each treatment, 10 jars served as replicates, each containing two snails. Observations were conducted over two follow-up periods (2 weeks and 9 weeks). Data analysis was performed using repeated-measures ANOVA, the Kruskal -Wallis test, and the log-rank test.ResultsIn the first two weeks of the study, shell height was highest among snails exposed to 24.6 °C (4.14 ± 0.56 mm) and least among those at 35.1 °C (3.90 ± 0.50 mm. A significant increase in shell height was observed to be associated with the interaction between temperature and time (RM Anova F6,72 = 4.51, p < 0.001). In contrast, the effect of temperature alone was not statistically significant (RM Anova F5,72 = 1.95, p = 0.092). For the duration of the experiment, snails maintained at 19.5℃ attained a higher mean shell height (5.43 ± 1.04 mm) compared to those at 15.5 °C (4.85 ± 0.89 mm); however, the overall effect of temperature on shell height was not statistically significant (F3,512 = 0.07; p = 0.978). On the other hand, there were notable differences in reproductive production between temperature treatments. Egg masses were substantially higher in snails kept at 19.5 °C: 22 (11) than in those kept at 15.5 °C: 6 (8); (z = − 2.762; p = 0.003). Survival probability declined with increasing temperature, particularly at 29.8 °C and 35.5 °C. Overall, survival rates differed significantly across the temperature treatments (Log-rank test: p < 0.0001).ConclusionRadix natalensis’s survival and reproductive performance are temperature-dependent, whereas snail growth is influenced by the combination of incubation conditions and time. These findings imply that temperature fluctuations will play a critical role in shaping the future transmission dynamics of fascioliasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05550-4.

Introduction

Climate change influences both the development free-living stages of Fasciola spp. and the lymnaeid intermediate host snails. Interactions between rainfall and temperature influence fascioliasis transmission dynamics [1]. Radix natalensis is a freshwater lymnaeid snail widely distributed in the subtropical and tropical regions of the world [2, 3]. This gastropod snail is a major intermediate host snail (IHS) of the digenean trematode Fasciola gigantica, the causative agent for fascioliasis in domestic, wild ruminants, and humans [4]. Climate change is expected to have short and long-term effects on ecological processes, influencing the life history traits of IHS and altering the timing of disease transmission. These shifts may alter the transmission dynamics of fascioliasis, thereby modifying the disease’s geographical distribution [5, 6]. Temperature is a critical climatic factor that influences the physiological processes and life history traits of many organisms. The metabolic rate of ectothermic organisms such as freshwater snails is regulated by ambient temperature, and that may impact their development, survival, and reproduction.

Climate change-induced temperature changes have both short and long-term effects on R. natalensis’ biology and ecology. This may result in a new temperature regime with higher average temperatures, thereby influencing disease transmission patterns. Higher temperatures accelerate metabolic rates, resulting in faster snail development and reproduction [7], but may decrease survival rate, increase sensitivity to environmental stress, and compromise their immune systems [7]. In contrast, lower temperatures may impede these processes, thus influencing population dynamics, reproductive outcomes, and overall transmission dynamics of fascioliasis [8].

Radix natalensis’s biology and ecology, especially their role in the life cycle and spread of F. gigantica, have been extensively studied [3, 9, 10]. Mostly, the temperature influences the interaction between F. gigantica and R. natalensis, particularly with respect to the development of intramolluscan larval stages and the subsequent emergence of cercariae [10–13]. However, there is limited information on the effects of temperature on the life-history traits of R. natalensis, the major IHS of F. gigantica in Southern Africa. There is only one study by Prinsloo [14], who studied the effect of temperature on the distribution of R. natalensis in the then Transvaal province in South Africa. Therefore, in this study, we explored how temperature affects the life-history traits of this species under controlled laboratory conditions since the presence and distribution of this snail species in a particular area may be associated with fascioliasis risk, a disease of global veterinary importance.

Materials and methods

The study designs for snail breeding and temperature experiments were published in the Mendeley Data repository: 10.17632/hzyrrktjs2.

Snail collection

Field collected R. natalensis snails were obtained from Kwafik’suthe Dam (-29.672, 29.876) in Impendle Municipality and from the Plains Farm (-29.149, 30.008) in Mpofana Municipality, both located in the uMgungundlovu District, KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. Snails were collected using scooping and hand-picking methods as described by Appleton [15]. The field-collected snails were morphologically identified using snail identification keys described by Brown [15, 16].

Breeding

Two hundred and fifty F1 snails were successfully bred from forty field-collected adult snails using a modified protocol developed by Dube et al. [17]. Using the developed protocol, three snails were randomly placed into 2 L transparent containers, filled with dechlorinated water, and fed the snails with algae wafers and trout pellets. A white polystyrene substratum was placed in each container. The snails were fed three times per week, and the room temperature was maintained at 25 °C ± 1 °C. Snails in the transparent plastic breeding containers started laying eggs after 2 weeks. Eggs hatched in about two weeks, and the new snails were reared using the same protocol mentioned above. Two hundred and fifty snails were successfully bred.

Temperature experiment

Design

The experimental design followed a hierarchical structure of 60 L aquarium (housing unit) → 2 L jar (replicate unit) → individual snails. For statistical analysis, the jar was treated as the experimental unit, while individual snails were considered subsamples within each replicate.

A total of 120 R. natalensis F1 snails with a shell height of 3.81 ± 1 mm were randomly assigned to six temperature treatments: 15.5 °C, 19.5 °C, 24.6 °C, 29.8 °C, and 35.1 °C, and a control group at ambient temperature 20.3 °C. The temperature treatments were selected to represent a range of environmentally relevant temperatures that Radix natalensis may experience in natural freshwater habitats. The lower temperature (15.5 °C) represents cooler environmental conditions, such as winter temperatures or high-altitude water bodies. The moderate temperatures (19.5 °C and 24.6 °C) are optimal for freshwater habitats and are considered favorable for snail growth and reproduction. Higher temperatures (29.8 °C and 35.1 °C) represent temperatures that may occur in shallow water bodies during hot seasons or under warm environmental conditions. These temperature treatments, therefore, allowed the assessment of snail life-history responses across a biologically and ecologically relevant temperature gradient.

Temperatures were maintained using a 100 W aquarium heater (seBO HS-100) and a seBO submersible pump (WP-3200) for water circulation, which were used to maintain a constant temperature and temperature readings in each honey jar were taken at 7 AM, 12 PM, and 6 PM (three times a day), and the heaters were adjusted to maintain a relatively desired experimental temperature. To maintain a low temperature of 15 ℃, four kilograms (4 kg) of ice blocks were added at 8 AM and 6 PM daily to the aquaria set for the experimental temperature. For each temperature treatment, two 60 L aquaria were used, each containing five 2 L honey jars, for a total of 10 jars (replicates) per treatment. Each jar was filled with dechlorinated water, and two juvenile snails were randomly allocated to each jar. Thus, each temperature treatment comprised 20 snails in total. Across all temperature treatments, a total of 12 aquaria and 60 jars were used, with 120 snails at the start of the experiment. The snails were fed on algal wafers and trout pellets three times a week. Furthermore, the water in the honey jars was changed at feeding time to reduce waste accumulation. In each container, we added a white polystyrene substratum for egg laying. Egg masses and snail mortalities were recorded daily. Shell heights were measured weekly using a digital vernier caliper. A snail was confirmed dead if it showed no reaction when pricked with a needle [7]. Artificial lighting was provided in the laboratory room using a 4 × 100-watt fluorescent tube. The breeding room maintained a 12-hour day-night cycle. No artificial aeration was done in the breeding containers. Furthermore, water pH was maintained between 7.40 and 9 and checked with a Hanna (HI9829) multiparameter water test meter.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using StataNow/SE 18.5 [18]. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize shell-height measurements and the number of egg masses laid at different temperature treatments. For normally distributed data, confirmed through the Shapiro-Wilk tests [19]. Data were summarized as means and standard deviations for normally distributed data and as medians and interquartile ranges for non-normally distributed data. Repeated measures ANOVA was used to evaluate the effect of temperature on the growth of R. natalensis. Snails maintained at 29.8 °C and 35.1 °C survived for only two weeks; therefore, the effect of temperature on snail shell height was assessed over the first two weeks for all snails. Thereafter, we assessed the effect of temperature on snail height for the eight weeks for snails that survived beyond the two weeks. Data on the number of eggs laid were not normally distributed; thus, a nonparametric Anova, the Kruskal-Wallis test, was used to determine whether differences in egg numbers across temperature treatments existed. Furthermore, the Dunn’s test, a pairwise multiple-comparison approach, was used to determine outcome differences for the treatments following the Kruskal-Wallis test [20]. We determined the effect of temperature on snail longevity using survival analysis. This was done initially for the first two weeks and later for the snails that survived until the end of the experiments. The differences in survival time across temperatures were compared using the Log Rank (Mantel-Cox) test.

Results

Effect of temperature on growth

The average temperatures attained during the experiments were 15.5 °C, 19.5 °C, 24.6 °C, 29.8 °C, 35.1 °C, and 20.3 °C in the control experiment. Overall, shell height increased across all temperature treatments. In the first two weeks, shell-height was highest among snails in the 24.6 °C (4.14 ± 0.56 mm) and least among those at 35.1 °C (3.90 ± 0.50 mm) (Fig. 1A). The results showed a positive interaction between temperature and time on snail growth (RM ANOVA, F (6, 72) = 4.51, p < 0.001). In contrast, the effect of temperature alone on snail growth was not statistically significant (RM Anova F5,72 = 1.95, p = 0.092), although it appeared to influence snail growth in small increments. By the end of the study, the snails maintained at 19.5 had the shell height of 5.43 ± 1.04 mm while those at 15.5 °C had the smallest shell height of 4.85 ± 0.89 mm (Fig. 1B). Temperature had no effect on snail shell height (F3,512=0.07; p = 0.978).

Effect of temperature on reproduction

Overall, 561 egg masses were collected across all temperature treatments (Supplementary file S1). The median (IQR) of egg masses laid across the different temperatures was 13 (17). By the second week, a total of 137 (24.4%) egg masses had been laid across all treatments (Fig. 2). The least number of egg masses laid by the second week was in the snails maintained at 35.1 °C (total: 3 egg masses; 1.5 (1) egg masses). By the end of the study, snails maintained at 19.5℃ had laid 35% (n = 197 egg masses) of the total egg masses, while the least was among those maintained at 15.5℃ (13.7%, n = 77 egg masses) (Fig. 2).

Temperature influenced snail reproduction across the different temperature treatments during the experimental period. Additionally, a statistical difference (KWallis: X2 = 9.641, p = 0.023) was observed in the effect of temperature on the total number of eggs laid. The snails kept at 19.5℃ laid significantly more egg masses than those at 15.5 °C (15.5℃: 6 (8) vs. 19.5℃: 22 (11) egg masses; z = -2.762; p = 0.003). Furthermore, significant differences in the median number of egg masses laid were observed between snails maintained at 15.5℃and 20.3 ℃ (15.5 °C: 6 (8) egg masses vs. 20.3 °C: 17 (11) egg masses; z = -2.141; p = 0.016) and between those maintained at 19.5 °C and 24.6℃ (19.5℃: 22 (11) egg masses vs. 24.6℃: 11 (17) egg masses; z = 2.023; p = 0.022).

Dunn’s Pairwise Comparison of egg masses by temperature revealed that the total number of egg masses laid at 19.8℃ and 35.1℃ treatments was significantly different, with a P-value of 0.0425 < 0.05 (P < 0.05). Furthermore, it was observed that the number of egg masses laid at low (15.5℃) and high temperatures (24.6℃-35.1℃) was lower than that laid between 19.5℃-24.6℃. Therefore, we can conclude that changes influenced the number of eggs laid across the temperature treatments.

Snail survival was highest at 19.5 °C, with a mean survival time of 8.200 weeks (95% CI: 6.378–10.022), indicating optimal conditions. Survival declined at both lower and higher temperatures. At 15.5 °C, mean survival was 6.296 weeks (95% CI: 4.272–8.320), showing moderate survival but greater variability. At 24.6 °C, survival decreased further to 4.750 weeks (95% CI: 2.897–6.603. Discussion

According to our findings, water temperature influences the life-history traits of R. natalensis, the main intermediate host of F. gigantica in the subtropical and tropical regions of Africa and Asia. Temperature is a key climatic factor driving the physiological and ecological dynamics of freshwater snails, and the current study provides insights into its role in shaping the biology of R. natalensis [21]. The optimal growth, fecundity, and survival of the R. natalensis strains were favoured by a temperature of 19.5℃ in the current study; this might be due to their adaptability to survive in subtropical environments. On the other hand, these life history traits were negatively impacted by temperatures above and below this optimal temperature. Therefore, we might say temperature has both positive and negative effects on the biology and ecology of poikilothermic freshwater mollusks, influencing their development, reproduction, and survival [2, 22, 23].

Effect of temperature on snail growth

We observed a positive interaction between time and temperature. Snails exposed to the following temperatures, 19.5℃ and 24.6℃, exhibited accelerated growth rates during their first two weeks of exposure, which may be associated with increased metabolic activity typically observed in ectothermic organisms at moderate higher temperatures [24]. However, there is a decline in snail growth to snails kept at temperatures beyond 24.6℃, most likely due to thermal stress and resource limitation [25]. Comparable temperature-growth patterns have been reported in other freshwater snails, including Radix acuminata [26].

Within the first two weeks, the highest shell height was observed in snails exposed at 24.6℃, and the least among those exposed at higher temperatures (29.8℃ and 30.1℃). This observation may suggest that higher temperatures may negatively affect growth in ectotherms under certain conditions. Since reduced growth is often associated with reduced reproductive output, this may affect snail population dynamics [27]. The smallest shell height was observed at 15.5 °C, implying that low temperatures impede the growth of R. natalensis [28, 29]. For the first 2 weeks of the experiment, our findings partially agree with those of [10, 13, 14], suggesting that 24.5℃ is the optimal temperature for growth. Higher temperatures above 24.5℃ expedite metabolic processes, redirecting energy and resources towards survival, leading to stress in the experimental snails and reduced growth [30]. At the end of the study, optimal growth of R. natalensis was observed in snails assigned to the 19.5℃ treatment, whereas snails kept at 24.6℃ died in week 8, before the end of the experiment. This may indicate that the snail can endure temperatures considered suboptimal in Kenya and Nigeria [9, 13]. This may also indicate that these snails can tolerate relatively low temperatures, which may influence their survival and distribution under cooler environmental conditions. The optimal growth temperature of 19.5 °C for R. natalensis in our study differs from those found by other researchers [9, 12, 13]. This might be due to differences in climate conditions and geographical distribution of the snails [31]. Overall, we concluded that temperature, along with incubation time, significantly affected snail growth.

Effect of temperature on reproduction

Generally, warmer temperatures are associated with higher snail reproduction, while extremely low and high temperatures are associated with reduced reproduction [1]. The highest fecundity was observed at 19.5℃, suggesting that R. natalensis exhibits enhanced reproductive performance at this temperature under laboratory conditions. Overall, reproduction was optimal at 19.5℃ and declined at temperatures below and above this temperature. These observations may reflect reduced metabolic activity at lower temperatures and physiological stress at higher temperatures, which can influence enzymatic and metabolic processes in ectothermic organisms. The findings agree with those of an earlier study by Prinsloo and van Eeden [14], who suggested that the R. natalensis snail found in the KwaZulu-Natal province reproduced best at 18℃ − 25℃. Therefore, under laboratory conditions, 19.5℃ may represent the temperature for R. natalensis reproduction, which may influence the transmission risk of fascioliasis through effects on intermediate host abundance and seasonal population dynamics. These findings may suggest that temperature changes could influence snail reproductive patterns and population dynamics.

Effect of temperature on the survival

The survival and longevity of R. natalensis were inversely related to increasing temperatures. At elevated temperatures, snails exhibited reduced life spans, which might be due to accelerated metabolic rates and increased energy demands that outpaced resource availability [28]. This finding is important for understanding the ecology of R. natalensis in natural habitats, as temperature extremes may contribute to population bottlenecks. Such changes in host abundance may indirectly affect disease transmission risk by altering the availability of intermediate hosts. Our study showed that snail mortality increased linearly with temperature, with snails maintained at 29.8℃ and 35.1℃ unable to survive beyond two weeks of the 9-week experimental period. These mortalities might be due to thermal stress. Therefore, reduced survival of R. natalensis at these temperatures may reduce its potential contribution to fascioliasis transmission risk by decreasing the intermediate host population unless the parasite switches to alternative intermediate hosts that can withstand these extreme thermal effects. This might be the case observed in the study area, where an alternative host of fascioliasis, Pseudosuccinea columella, was predominantly distributed across all 39 sampled sites, compared to only 3 sites for R. natalensis [32]. In contrast, high survival rates were observed in snails exposed to suboptimal temperatures (15.5℃ and 20.3℃), suggesting that these temperatures may favour increases in R. natalensis populations, which, in turn, may influence the transmission risk of fascioliasis. Low temperatures prolong survival compared to high temperatures. The survival analysis suggests that 19.5℃ may represent an optimal temperature for survival of the experimental snails under the conditions of this study. Changes in temperature associated with climate change may influence temperature-dependent life-history traits of R. natalensis. Warmer temperatures may enhance snail population growth and parasite development rates, potentially increasing the risk of disease transmission [33]. However, extreme temperatures may reduce snail survival, thereby reducing the population and distribution of the intermediate hosts.

Limitations and future considerations

Although this work offers valuable insights into how temperature affects R. natalensis, a number of limitations should be noted. The statistical power and generalisability of the results may have been limited by small sample size and limited number of replicates. Future studies should therefore incorporate larger sample sizes and increased replication to enhance the robustness of the results.

Furthermore, the complexity of natural habitats, where a variety of ecological factors, such as competition, predation, and resource availability, interact with temperature to affect snail biology, might not be adequately represented by controlled laboratory conditions. To better comprehend these dynamics in natural settings, field-based research is therefore advised.

Future studies should focus on identifying the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying thermal adaptation in R. natalensis. Furthermore, a more thorough knowledge of the species’ ecological plasticity would result from examining the cumulative effects of temperature with other abiotic variables, such as water pH and salinity.

Finally, there is a need for integrated studies that assess the impact of temperature across all developmental stages of the snail parasite system, including the survival and persistence of intermediate host snails over time. Such work will be essential for improving predictions of fascioliasis transmission under changing climatic conditions.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that temperature influences the growth, survival, and reproduction of R. natalensis. A low temperature of 15.5℃ enhances survival but reduces reproduction and growth. The optimal temperature range of 19.5℃-24.6℃ stimulates growth and reproduction, whereas temperatures above 29.8℃-35.1℃ are lethal, negatively affecting survival, growth, and reproduction. The considerable impact of temperature on the life history traits of R. natalensis may underscore its potential influence on the transmission dynamics of fascioliasis. By elucidating the thermal sensitivities of R. natalensis, this study may provide the information and knowledge needed for effective disease control and environmental management.
