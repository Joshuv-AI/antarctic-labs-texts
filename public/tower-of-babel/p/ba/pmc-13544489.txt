Emotional Burden of Olfactory Dysfunction: A Psychological Cluster Analysis from a Tertiary Smell and Taste Clinic
Authors: Tom Fischer, Keren Avirame, Idan Peled, Ella Hazan, Elinoy Herstain, Hen Chaushu, Nidal Muhanna, Avraham Abergel, Narin N. Carmel Neiderman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points The emotional burden of olfactory dysfunction bears distinct psychological profiles.Objective olfactory dysfunction findings do not reliably reflect patients' emotional burden.Olfactory‐related quality of life assessment may identify patient risk of emotional distress.

Introduction

Olfactory dysfunction (OD) is associated with neuropsychiatric comorbidity and reduced quality of life (QOL) [1]. OD may also co‐occur with depressive disorders [2, 3], although findings from large population‐based studies have been inconsistent [4]. Emotional responses to olfactory loss vary widely, ranging from profound distress to minimal impact. Notably, subjective olfactory‐related QOL was found to be a stronger predictor of well‐being than objective performance in psychophysical tests [5]. Machine‐learning approaches applied to large olfactory datasets have recently identified distinct patient characteristics not captured by conventional analyses [6]. Here, we applied a data‐driven clustering approach to subjective and affective outcomes with the aim of identifying distinct psychological responses to OD and examined their relationship with psychophysical olfactory performance [5].

Methods

The study patients were recruited from the Smell and Taste Clinic at a tertiary urban medical center between 2024 and 2025. The healthy controls were volunteers without known conditions affecting olfactory function. The exclusion criteria were pregnancy, recent upper respiratory infection, and corticosteroid use within 4 weeks. Psychophysical olfactory function was assessed by the validated Hebrew Sniffin’ Sticks Threshold‐Discrimination‐Identification (TDI) test [7, 8]. Participants completed the He‐WHO‐5 Well‐Being Index, Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (He‐PANAS), Questionnaire of Olfactory Disorders (He‐QOD), Nasal Obstruction Symptom Evaluation (He‐NOSE) scale, and Sino‐Nasal Outcome Test‐22 (He‐SNOT‐22), and subjective taste impairment was assessed by a dichotomous scale.

Group comparisons were performed by independent‐samples t‐tests, χ 2 tests, or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. K‐means clustering was performed on standardized PANAS, WHO‐5, and QOD scores, with the SNOT‐22 emotional and sleep subdomains additionally included based on preliminary regression analyses examining predictors of well‐being. The optimal number of clusters was determined by the Gap Statistic (k = 3). TDI scores were compared across clusters by one‐way analysis of variance with Tukey post hoc testing. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and size effects are reported. Analyses were conducted using Jamovi (v2.3) and Python (v3.11).

Results

OD Patients Versus Controls

In total, 80 OD patients (mean age 44.7 ± 16.6 years; 57.5% male) and 48 controls (mean age 40.8 ± 14.3 years; 45.8% male) were enrolled. The OD patients had significantly lower He‐TDI scores (t(117) = 10.1, p < 0.001, d = 1.89) and a higher sinonasal symptom burden on both He‐SNOT‐22 (U = 747.5, p < 0.001, r = 0.56) and He‐NOSE (U = 1073.5, p < 0.001, r = 0.39), as well as a higher burden of olfactory‐related QOL (He‐QOD) (U = 116.0, p < 0.001, r = 0.92). In contrast, general well‐being and affective measures showed mixed results: the patients reported significantly reduced positive affect (U = 1255, p = 0.013, r = 0.27), but there were no significant group differences for negative affect (U = 1705, p = 0.957, r = 0.01) or well‐being (U = 1560, p = 0.258, r = −.012) (Table S1).

Clustering Based Upon Subjective and Affective Measures

K‐means clustering (BSS/TSS = 49.5%; silhouette = 0.25) identified three profiles. Cluster 1 (high distress; n = 26) had elevated He‐SNOT‐22 sleep/emotional scores, high He‐QOD impairment, low He‐WHO‐5 scores, and high negative affect. Cluster 2 (intermediate distress; n = 29) showed moderate impairment and a mild effect on well‐being. Cluster 3 (low distress; n = 44) showed minimal impairment and high well‐being scores, comprising mostly controls (91%) (Table S3). The three clusters were well‐separated in principal component analysis space (PC1: 54.0%; PC2: 18.4%; combined: 72.4%), supporting the distinctiveness of the derived profiles (Figure 1). Cluster membership was strongly associated with group assignment (χ 2(2) = 73.4, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.86) (Figure S1).

Intermediate‐Distress Versus High‐Distress Profiles

Group comparisons between the high‐distress (Cluster 1) and intermediate‐distress (Cluster 2) profiles revealed no significant difference in age or etiology, although there was a significantly higher proportion of females in the high‐distress profile (χ 2(1) = 5.26, p = 0.022, Φ = 0.31). Interestingly, subjective taste impairment was more prevalent in the high‐distress profile (χ 2(1) = 5.59, p = 0.018, Φ = 0.33) (Table 1). Analysis of the psychophysical olfactory performance revealed that the profiles differed significantly on the TDI total score (t(47) = 2.54, p = 0.015) with a higher odor threshold (U = 158, p = 0.006, r = 0.45) and a higher odor discrimination (t(47) = 2.82, p = 0.007, d = 0.81) in the high‐distress cluster (Figure S2).

Discussion

Unlike prior studies that have focused primarily on psychophysical testing, the current study clusters participants based on their subjective responses to validated olfactory‐related QOL assessments. We identified three distinct psychological response profiles, demonstrating that emotional burden is not determined by the severity of sensory loss according to TDI, but rather by the perceived deprivation of olfactory capability. Notably, the intermediate‐distress cluster exhibited the poorest psychophysical olfactory performance yet better psychological functioning than the high‐distress cluster. This dissociation supports prior evidence that subjective olfactory‐related QOL, rather than psychophysical olfactory performance, more strongly influences emotional burden outcomes [5, 6, 9].

The higher prevalence of subjective taste impairment in the high‐distress cluster suggests that perceived multisensory disruption may amplify the psychosocial burden [4]. Furthermore, differences in the TDI threshold and discrimination, rather than decreased identification scores, point toward a potential role for early perceptual processing in shaping emotional experience in this setting. These findings support the routine assessment of olfactory‐related QOL and affective state alongside psychophysical testing to better identify patients at risk for emotional repercussions of sensory loss. Targeted interventions incorporating olfactory training, hedonic re‐learning, and psychoeducation may be particularly beneficial for high‐distress patients [10].

This study has several limitations, including its cross‐sectional design, recruitment from a single tertiary referral center, and reliance upon self‐report measures. Longitudinal studies incorporating cognitive and neurobiological measures are needed to further elucidate causal mechanisms. Overall, the identification of distinct psychological response profiles conceptualizes OD as a multidimensional condition requiring integrated sensory and affective assessment.
