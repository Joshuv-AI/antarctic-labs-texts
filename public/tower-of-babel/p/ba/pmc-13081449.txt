Virulence, cytotoxicity potential and biofilm production ability of Listeria spp. isolated from raw fish in Poland
Authors: Marta Sołtysiuk, Paulina Przyborowska, Agnieszka Wiszniewska-Łaszczych, Dawid Tobolski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe presence of Listeria spp. in raw fish poses a significant food safety risk, primarily due to the pathogenic potential of Listeria monocytogenes. However, comprehensive data on the virulence characteristics of Listeria populations, including non-monocytogenes species, isolated from raw fish in Poland are limited. This study aimed to characterize a collection of Listeria spp. isolates by assessing their virulence gene profiles, biofilm-forming capabilities, and cytotoxic potential to better understand the public health risk associated with their presence in the aquatic food chain.ResultsA total of 104 Listeria spp. isolates (L. monocytogenes n = 24, L. seeligeri n = 36, L. welshimeri n = 30, L. innocua n = 14) were analyzed. A clear demarcation in pathogenic potential was observed: L. monocytogenes isolates universally harbored key virulence determinants, with 95.8% carrying a complete Listeria Pathogenicity Island 1 (LIPI-1), and all belonging to the clinically relevant serogroup 1/2a-3c. This genetic potential was confirmed phenotypically, as cytotoxic activity against four eukaryotic cell lines was exclusively observed in this species. In contrast, other Listeria species were non-cytotoxic, although they carried isolated virulence genes (plcA, mpl), suggesting a role as an environmental reservoir. Biofilm formation was a common trait across all species, with 69.2% of isolates demonstrating this ability in a quantitative assay. However, this phenotype was not significantly associated with the presence of the luxS gene (p > 0.05).ConclusionsRaw fish sold in Poland are a probable vehicle for highly virulent L. monocytogenes strains equipped with a full genetic potential for causing human disease. The widespread ability of all Listeria species to form biofilms highlights a critical mechanism for persistence in food processing environments, where non-pathogenic species may shelter the primary pathogen. These findings underscore the necessity for extensive surveillance and control strategies targeting the entire Listeria genus to mitigate the public health risk associated with raw fish consumption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05323-z.Keywords: Listeria monocytogenes, Foodborne pathogen, Food of animal origin, Aquaculture, Virulent factors, Cytotoxic activity

Background

Comprising 28 recognized species, the genus Listeria (L.) is composed of Gram-positive, facultatively anaerobic bacteria [1]. Within Listeria genus, L. monocytogenes and L. ivanovii are well-documented zoonotic pathogens, responsible for severe diseases in both human and animal populations [2]. Historically, other species like L. seeligeri, L. welshimeri, and L. innocua were classified as non-pathogenic. However, recent findings have associated these species with sporadic instances of human listeriosis, suggesting that the pathogenic potential within the Listeria genus may be more extensive than previously understood [3, 4].

Of particular concern is L. monocytogenes, a foodborne pathogen responsible for listeriosis, a disease posing a substantial threat to public health. The clinical spectrum of this infection is broad and includes severe conditions such as septicemia and meningitis. The risk of contracting listeriosis is high in immuno-compromised persons, the elderly, neonates and pregnant women where it can lead to grave outcomes including fetal loss and premature delivery [5]. Surveillance data from Europe indicates a concerning upward trend in listeriosis cases. A report from the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) identified listeriosis as the fifth most common zoonotic disease in 2023. The notification rate for cases rose by 5.8% from the preceding year, reaching the highest level documented since 2007 [6]. Moreover, among all reported zoonoses, listeriosis is associated with the most severe outcomes, demonstrating the highest rates of both hospitalization and mortality, with a case-fatality rate of 18.1% recorded in 2022 [7]. Recent outbreaks highlight the importance of developing effective prevention and control strategies to mitigate L. monocytogenes contamination in aquatic food products. Between 2022 and 2023, seventeen cases of listeriosis, resulting in two deaths, were linked to contaminated fish products [6]. In Germany, 22 outbreaks associated with the consumption of salmon products caused 228 cases and 17 confirmed deaths [8]. Additionally, the European Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed (RASFF) portal has recorded 114 notifications related to L. monocytogenes in raw fish and fish products over the last five years [9].

The ubiquitous nature of L. monocytogenes is well-established; it is frequently isolated from a wide range of environmental sources, including soil, water, and decaying vegetation, as well as from human and animal carriers [1, 10]. Food-producing animals, such as cattle, sheep, and poultry, often serve as asymptomatic reservoirs, shedding the bacterium in their feces and contributing to its prevalence in agricultural settings [1, 11, 12]. Although L. monocytogenes is not indigenous to marine or freshwater ecosystems, it readily contaminates these environments. Surface runoff from agricultural lands, carrying contaminated soil and animal waste, introduces the pathogen into rivers, lakes, and coastal waters [13]. Once in the aquatic environment, it can colonize fish and seafood. Consequently, the primary vector for human infection is the consumption of contaminated food. The “farm-to-fork” continuum provides numerous opportunities for contamination, from initial processing to final preparation. Raw or inadequately processed foods, which lack a terminal heat treatment step to eliminate the pathogen, represent the most significant sources of exposure for humans. Based on variations in somatic (O) and flagellar (H) antigens, L. monocytogenes is classified into 13 recognized serovars [14]. Despite this diversity, the vast majority of human listeriosis cases worldwide are attributed to just a few specific serovars. In particular, serovars 1/2a, 1/2b, and 4b are responsible for majority of all reported human infections, with serovar 4b often being associated with major outbreaks and more severe clinical outcomes. Therefore, epidemiological surveillance is crucial not only for detecting the presence of the pathogen but also for identifying and characterizing clinically significant serovars. This practice is essential for tracking contamination sources, understanding transmission routes, and implementing targeted control measures to protect public health [15].

The clinical relevance of specific serovars is directly linked to their pathogenic potential, which is determined by a complex set of virulence factors. The ability of Listeria spp. to function as an intracellular pathogen depends on a multi-stage infection process that includes overcoming host barriers, invading and replicating within host cells, utilizing cellular machinery, and evading the host’s immune response [16, 17]. The infection cycle is controlled by a group of virulence genes, located in both the core and accessory genomes. The process begins with the invasion of host cells, particularly non-phagocytic intestinal epithelial cells, which is mediated by surface proteins called internalins. The genes inlA and inlB encode the primary internalins that bind to host cell receptors, initiating bacterial uptake [17]. After internalization, the bacterium must escape from the phagosome into the cytoplasm to survive. This crucial step is facilitated by listeriolysin O (LLO), a pore-forming toxin encoded by the hlyA gene. Once in the cytoplasm, the ActA protein (encoded by actA) induces polymerization of host actin, forming an actin tail that generates force for movement through the cytoplasm and into adjacent cells. The spread is completed by two phospholipases, PlcA and PlcB, which dissolve the double-membrane vacuole that forms around the bacterium as it enters a neighboring cell [18]. Many of these key virulence genes (hlyA, actA, plcA, plcB), along with mpl, are organized into a well-defined cluster known as Listeria Pathogenicity Island 1 (LIPI-1), considered the most critical genetic locus for virulence. The entire island is under the control of the prfA gene, which functions as the primary transcriptional regulator, coordinating the expression of these virulence factors and also influencing intrinsic antimicorbial resistance [19].

However, the pathogenic process of L. monocytogenes is not solely dependent on its virulence factors. A crucial aspect of its success as a foodborne pathogen is its virulence gene repertoire. The remarkable stress resistance of L. monocytogenes is an important factor contributing to persistence and contamination of food, which in turn constitutes a source for human listeriosis. The psychrotrophic nature of L. monocytogenes enables its persistence in various food processing environments [2, 15]. Its ability to grow at refrigeration temperatures presents a significant risk, particularly for raw and ready-to-eat (RTE) fish products that do not undergo heat treatment before consumption [20]. The European Union (EU) has implemented a food safety criterion for L. monocytogenes established by European Commission Regulation (EC) No. 2073/2005. The main criterion for L. monocytogenes depends on the food’s ability to support its growth, with the general rule being that it must not be detected in a 25 g sample for RTE foods. However, for foods that support growth, a manufacturer can prove to a competent authority that the level will not exceed 100 CFU/g throughout the shelf life [21]. Certain RTE foods can be a particular problem if they have characteristics that support the survival and growth of L. monocytogenes [22]. Fish products can become contaminated with L. monocytogenes at any stage of the production, processing, and distribution chain [23–25], with a particular risk for fish intended for raw consumption [9]. Controlling L. monocytogenes in food-processing facilities is challenging due to its capacity to form biofilm on various surfaces and to survive and multiply under extreme environmental conditions, including low temperature, wide pH range, and high salt concentration [15, 18, 24, 26, 27]. Also interaction between L. monocytogenes and resident background microbiota could affect their ability to survive, grow and persist in microhabitats and niches in the food industry. Research findings investigated the competitive ability of L. monocytogenes strains grown together in separate culture mixes with other L. monocytogenes, L. innocua and Gram-negative bacteria in biofilms on stainless steel indicated complex patterns of bacterial interactions. L. monocytogenes inhibition in the multibacteria cultures only partially depends on cell contact and likely involve various antagonistic and bacterial tolerance mechanisms [28].

Numerous studies have focused on the molecular characterization of L. monocytogenes isolates from various food sources [29, 30]. In Poland, however, research has predominantly characterized isolates from meat products, including RTE items [31]. Despite existing data from serotyping, genotyping, and virulotyping of Polish Listeria spp. isolates, information regarding the genomic characteristics of strains from raw fish remains scarce. As long as raw fish may by a potential vehicle for Listeria contamination of processing plant and may be intended for consumption as RTE food as a direct risk of listeriosis in human, the investigation of what risk the raw material may pose for both situations seems reasonable. The present study aims to address this knowledge gap by providing a detailed analysis of Listeria spp. isolated from raw fish, focusing on the characterization of their virulence genes, cytotoxicity, and biofilm formation ability.

Material and methods

Samples collection

The bacterial isolates investigated in this study originated from a cross-sectional survey conducted over a three-year period, from January 2020 to December 2022, across various locations in northern Poland. A total of 750 raw fish samples encompassing various species, were collected and screened for the presence of Listeria spp. The samples were collected as final product after gutting, intended to use for cooking. From the initial screening, a total of 104 isolates were identified and selected for further characterization, comprising 24 L. monocytogenes, 14 L. innocua, 36 L. seeligeri, and 30 L. welshimeri strains. A detailed account of the sampling strategy, procedures for bacterial isolation, and initial species identification is provided in a prior publication [32]. For the experiments described herein, all isolates were subcultured and maintained on Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) medium (Difco, Sparks, MD, USA) to ensure viability and purity.

Molecular characterization: virulence genes and genoserogrouping

Genomic DNA was isolated from each Listeria strain using a 1 mL sample from an overnight culture grown in BHI broth. The culture was transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf-type tubes (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), and bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation (MiniSpin, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), with the supernatant subsequently discarded. To facilitate the degradation of the Gram-positive cell wall, the resulting bacterial pellet was resuspended in a lysis buffer containing lysozyme (A&A Biotechnology, Gdynia, Poland) and mutanolysin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), followed by incubation to ensure complete enzymatic activity.

Following the lysis step, total genomic DNA was purified using a commercial extraction kit (A&A Biotechnology, Gdynia, Poland), in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The purified DNA was eluted in nuclease-free water and stored at −20 °C pending analysis. The quality and concentration of the extracted DNA were then assessed. A BioSpectrometer (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) was used to measure absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm. The readings were utilized to determine the DNA concentration and to calculate the A260/A280 ratio, which served as an indicator of purity and the absence of significant protein contamination. Prior to its use in subsequent polymerase chain reaction (PCR) applications, each DNA sample was normalized with nuclease-free water to a final working concentration of 5 ng/µL.

The molecular screening of isolates was conducted using conventional PCR to detect a panel of specific genetic markers. This panel included nine virulence-associated genes (inlA, iap, plcA, hlyA, prfA, actA, inlJ, inlC, mpl) and one gene linked to biofilm formation (luxS). For L. monocytogenes isolates, multiplex PCR was used for genoserogrouping by targeting the lmo0737 and ORF2819 markers. All oligonucleotide primers used for amplification are listed in Table 1.

PCR reactions were prepared in a final volume of 25 µL. Each reaction consisted of 12.5 µL of PCR Mix Plus Red (A&A Biotechnology, Gdańsk, Poland), which contains Taq polymerase, dNTPs, and MgCl₂, 10 pmol of the respective forward and reverse primers, and 10 ng of purified genomic DNA (added in a 2 µL volume) as the template. Thermal cycling was carried out in a Mastercycler Vapo.protect pro. (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). The cycling protocol was programmed as follows: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, primer annealing at a gene-specific temperature for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s. A final extension step was conducted at 72 °C for 5 min to ensure complete amplification of the target fragments. To validate the results, each PCR run included positive controls with DNA from reference strains (L. monocytogenes ATCC 13932, L. ivanovii ATCC 19119, L. innocua ATCC 33090, L. welshimeri ATCC 35897, and L. seeligeri ATCC 35967; ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and a non-template negative control.

The resulting PCR amplicons were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. A 8 µL volume of each reaction product was loaded onto a 1.5% (w/v) agarose gel (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) prepared in 1x TAE buffer and stained with SimplySafe intercalating dye (EURx, Gdańsk, Poland). Electrophoretic separation was performed at 70 V for 60 min. The size of the amplified DNA fragments was estimated by comparison to a 100–1000 bp DNA ladder (A&A Biotechnology, Gdynia, Poland) run in parallel. The separated DNA fragments were visualized under UV light, and the gel images were captured for documentation and analysis using a GelDoc-IT System (UVP, Warsaw, Poland).

Biofilm formation assays

The capacity of each Listeria spp. isolate to form biofilms was evaluated using two distinct methods: a qualitative plate-based assay and a quantitative spectrophotometric assay.

The biofilm-forming potential of each Listeria spp. isolate was initially screened using a qualitative assay on Congo Red Agar (CRA) (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK), following methodologies adapted from Freeman et al. (1989), Salman & Shamsudin (2011), and Arciola et al. (2002) [39–41]. The assay evaluates the production of the extracellular polysaccharide matrix, a key component of biofilms. For the procedure, a sterile loop was used to streak each bacterial isolate onto the surface of a freshly prepared CRA plate. The inoculated plates were then subjected to a dual-phase incubation: first, an aerobic incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, followed by a subsequent incubation period at ambient room temperature overnight. The secondary incubation step was critical for allowing complete development of the colony coloration. Following incubation, a visual assessment of the colonies was performed by a laboratory technician with extensive experience in microbiological analysis. To ensure consistent and objective classification, a six-color reference scale, known as the Arciol scale, was utilized for interpretation [41]. According to the scale, isolates that produced very black or black colonies were categorized as positive biofilm producers (P). Colonies exhibiting a dark, almost black appearance were classified as having an indicative or intermediate biofilm-forming ability (I). Conversely, isolates that formed colonies with very red to bordeaux coloration were designated as negative (N), signifying an inability to produce a significant extracellular matrix under the tested conditions.

A quantitative assessment of biofilm biomass was subsequently performed for all isolates using a crystal violet staining method in 96-well flat-bottom microtiter plates, based on the protocol described by Ebrahimi et al. (2013) [42]. The first step involved the preparation of a standardized bacterial inoculum. Starter cultures for each isolate were grown in Tryptone Soy Broth (TSB) (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK), for 16 h at 37 °C. The bacterial cells were then harvested from the broth by centrifugation at 6000 × g for 10 min (MiniSpin, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). After carefully decanting the supernatant, the resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of fresh TSB. The optical density of the bacterial suspension was spectrophotometrically adjusted to a value of 1.0 at a wavelength of 595 nm to ensure a uniform starting concentration for all isolates. The standardized suspension was then diluted at a 1:40 ratio in fresh TSB. For biofilm formation, 200 µL aliquots of the diluted bacterial suspension were dispensed into eight replicate wells of a 96-well microtiter plate (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). A set of wells containing 200 µL of sterile TSB only was included in each plate to serve as a negative control.

The microtiter plates were incubated under static conditions for 24 h at 37 °C to allow for biofilm development. After the incubation period, the culture medium was aspirated from each well, and non-adherent planktonic cells were removed by washing the plates three times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The plates were then inverted and allowed to air-dry at room temperature for one hour to fix the adherent biofilm. For staining, 200 µL of a 0.2% aqueous crystal violet solution (CHEMPUR, Piekary Sląskie, Poland) was added to every well, and the plates were incubated for 15 min. Following staining, the excess crystal violet solution was removed, and the plates were washed again with PBS to eliminate unbound dye. The crystal violet retained by the biofilm was solubilized by adding 200 µL of an 80:20 (v/v) mixture of ethyl alcohol and acetone to each well. The absorbance of the solubilized dye, which is proportional to the biofilm biomass, was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm using an ASYS UVM340 microplate reader (Biogenet, Jozefow, Poland). To ensure the reliability of the data, all tests for a single isolate were performed in three columns with eight technical replicates in each strain.

For data analysis and classification, the criteria established by Stepanović et al. (2007) were applied [43]. An optical density cut-off value (ODc) was determined by calculating the mean absorbance of the negative control wells and adding three times the standard deviation of the negative control. The biofilm-forming capability of each isolate was then categorized based on the measured optical density (OD) relative to the ODc. Isolates were classified as non-producers if the OD value was less than or equal to the ODc (OD ≤ 0.188). An isolate was considered a weak producer if its OD was greater than the ODc but less than or equal to twice the ODc (0.188 < OD ≤ 0.375). An OD value greater than twice the ODc but less than or equal to four times the ODc (0.375 < OD ≤ 0.752) corresponded to a moderate producer. Finally, isolates exhibiting an OD value greater than four times the ODc (OD > 0.752) were classified as strong biofilm producers.

Cell line and culture condition

Four cell lines obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were used for cytotoxicity assays: Caco-2 [Human epithelial colorectal adenocarcinoma (ATCC HTB-37)], Vero [African Green Monkey kidney, (ATCC CCL-81)], HT-29 [Caucasian colon adenocarcinoma grade II, (ATCC HTB-38)], and Hep-G2 [Human caucasian hepatocyte carcinoma, (ATCC HB-8065)]. All cell lines were cultured in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxic potential of secreted and cell-bound factors from each Listeria spp. isolate was evaluated in vitro using four distinct mammalian cell lines: Caco-2, HT-29, HepG2, and Vero. The procedure was divided into the preparation of bacterial extracts and the subsequent measurement of cell viability.

For the preparation of cytotoxic extracts, each Listeria spp. strain was first cultivated on Tryptone Soy Yeast Extract Agar (TSYEA) (Biomaxima, Lublin, Poland) under aerobic conditions at 37 °C for 24 h. Bacterial biomass was then harvested and suspended in sterile Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The bacterial suspension was standardized by McFarland Densitometer DEN-1 (BioSan, Riga, Latvia) to a concentration of approximately 1 × 109 colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL). To induce the release of both secreted and cell-bound cytotoxic components, vancomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added to the standardized suspension to a final concentration of 15% (w/v). The mixture was then incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Following incubation, the suspension was subjected to centrifugation at 2500 × g for 20 min to pellet the bacterial cells. The resulting supernatant, containing the bacterial factors, was carefully collected and sterilized by passing it through a 0.45 μm syringe filter (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) to produce a cell-free extract for immediate use in the assay.

For the assay, eukaryotic cell lines were harvested using a trypsin-EDTA solution (0.05% w/v trypsin, 0.02% w/v EDTA) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and cell aggregates were dissociated by gentle pipetting. A suspension of freshly prepared cells was seeded into 96-well flat-bottom plates (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After allowing the cells to adhere to the plate surface, the culture medium was replaced with the previously prepared cell-free bacterial supernatants.

The viability of the cell monolayers following exposure to the bacterial extracts was quantified using a colorimetric WST-1 cell proliferation assay (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). A 10 µL volume of the WST-1 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for a period ranging from 0.5 to 2 h. The absorbance, which correlates with the number of viable, metabolically active cells, was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a Sunrise microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). Each assay plate included a negative control (cells treated with sterile medium only, representing 100% viability) and a positive control (cells treated with 1% Triton X-100 (ThermoFisher SCIENTIFIC, Waltham, USA) to induce 100% cell death). The entire cytotoxicity experiment for each isolate was performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

All statistical computations were performed using Python (version 3.9) and the associated scientific libraries, including pandas, SciPy, statsmodels, and scikit-learn. A p-value below 0.05 was the criterion for statistical significance.

The prevalence of virulence-associated genes was calculated for the entire collection of isolates and also for each Listeria spp. separately. The Chi-square (χ2) test of independence was employed to assess associations between the presence of specific genes and the species of the isolate. For significant associations, pairwise post-hoc comparisons between species were performed using Fisher’s exact test. To control the false discovery rate from multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Cramér’s V was calculated to quantify the strength of association in the Chi-square tests.

Biofilm production was quantified by measuring optical density and subsequently categorized based on the criteria outlined by Stepanović et al. (2007) [43]. Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ) was used to evaluate the level of agreement between the quantitative optical density method and the qualitative CRA assay. The relationship between the presence of individual genes and the continuous variables of biofilm OD and cytotoxicity was examined using Spearman’s rank correlation.

Results

A total of 104 Listeria spp. isolates were analyzed in this study. The collection was composed of four species, with L. seeligeri being the most prevalent, accounting for 34.6% (n = 36) of the total isolates. The other species identified were L. welshimeri (28.8%; n = 30), L. monocytogenes (23.1%; n = 24), and L. innocua (13.5%; n = 14).

Detection of virulence and biofilm associated genes in Listeria spp. isolates

Molecular screening was conducted on all 104 Listeria spp. isolates to determine the presence of ten virulence-associated genes. The prevalence of selected genes was found to be significantly dependent on the bacterial species for nine virulence-associated genes (inlA, iap, plcA, hlyA, prfA, actA, inlJ, inlC, mpl, Chi-squared test, P < 0.001) except the biofilm-associated gene luxS (Chi-squared test, p = 0.745) (Supplementary Table S1). The pathogenic species L. monocytogenes consistently harbored virulence-associated genes at the highest frequencies.

Specifically, the iap gene was detected in 91.7% of L. monocytogenes isolates, a prevalence significantly higher than in L. seeligeri (11.1%) and L. welshimeri (3.3%) (p < 0.001 for both comparisons). This gene was not detected in any L. innocua isolates. The plcA gene was ubiquitous in L. monocytogenes (100%), which was significantly more frequent than in L. innocua (78.6%; p = 0.043), L. welshimeri (43.3%; p < 0.001), and L. seeligeri (11.1%; p < 0.001). Similarly, the mpl gene, present in 100% of L. monocytogenes isolates, was found at significantly lower frequencies in L. innocua (28.6%), L. seeligeri (8.3%), and L. welshimeri (3.3%) (p < 0.001 for all pairwise comparisons).

In a stark contrast, the core virulence genes prfA, actA, hlyA, and the internalins (inlA, inlC, inlJ) were exclusively found in L. monocytogenes isolates. A focused analysis of the 24 L. monocytogenes isolates revealed that all (100%) belonged to the serogroup 1/2a, 1/2c, 3a, 3c. This group exhibited a high prevalence and extensive profile of virulence determinants. Within this species, several key virulence genes, including plcA, hlyA, mpl, and the internalin genes inlA, inlC, and inlJ, were ubiquitous, being detected in 100% of the isolates. Other important virulence factors were present at slightly lower frequencies: the regulatory gene prfA and the actin-polymerizing protein gene actA were each identified in 95.8% (23/24) of isolates, while the invasion-associated gene iap was detected in 91.7% (22/24) of isolates. Consequently, a complete LIPI-1, defined by the presence of hlyA, mpl, actA, plcA, and prfA, was identified in the 23 isolates (95.8%) that possessed all five of these genes.

The biofilm-associated gene luxS was the only gene whose prevalence did not show a statistically significant difference among the species (p = 0.745). It was detected in a total of 16.3% (17/104) of the isolates. The distribution across species was relatively uniform: the gene was identified in 20.8% (5/24) of L. monocytogenes isolates, 28.6% (4/14) of L. innocua isolates, 13.3% (4/30) of L. welshimeri isolates, and 11.1% (4/36) of L. seeligeri isolates.

Biofilm production profile of Listeria spp

The biofilm-forming ability of the 104 Listeria spp. isolates was assessed using both qualitative and quantitative methods. Statistical analysis revealed that the distribution of biofilm formation phenotypes differed significantly among the species in the qualitative CRA assay (Chi-squared test, p = 0.014). Post-hoc analysis indicated this difference was primarily driven by L. seeligeri, which exhibited a significantly lower frequency of negative biofilm producers compared to both L. monocytogenes (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.002) and L. welshimeri (p = 0.011). In contrast, no significant differences were found in the quantitative spectrophotometric assay, both when comparing biofilm categories (Chi-squared test, p = 0.231) and median biomass values (Kruskal-Wallis test, p = 0.082).

In the qualitative CRA assay, 18 isolates (17.3%) were classified as positive biofilm producers. L. seeligeri showed the highest proportion of positive producers at 25.0% (9/36), followed by L. innocua (21.4%; 3/14), L. monocytogenes (12.5%; 3/24), and L. welshimeri (10.0%; 3/30). A larger group of 52 isolates (50.0%) demonstrated an indicative (intermediate) phenotype, which was most prevalent among L. seeligeri (63.9%; 23/36) and L. welshimeri (60.0%; 18/30). The remaining 34 isolates (32.7%) were classified as negative for biofilm production. This negative phenotype was most common in L. monocytogenes (54.2%; 13/24) and L. inocua (57.2%; 8/14) (Supplementary Table S2).

The quantitative spectrophotometric assay provided a more detailed categorization of biofilm biomass. Overall, 11 isolates (10.6%) were identified as strong, 15 (14.4%) as moderate, 46 (44.2%) as weak, and 32 (30.8%) as non-producers. Among L. monocytogenes (n = 24), 3 isolates (12.5%) were strong producers, 2 (8.3%) were moderate, 10 (41.7%) were weak, and 9 (37.5%) were non-producers. For L. seeligeri (n = 36), 4 isolates (11.1%) were strong, 5 (13.9%) were moderate, 20 (55.6%) were weak, and 7 (19.4%) were non-producers. In the L. welshimeri group (n = 30), 2 isolates (6.7%) were strong, 8 (26.7%) were moderate, 9 (30.0%) were weak, and 11 (36.7%) were non-producers. Finally, for L. innocua isolates (n = 14), 2 (14.3%) were strong, 0 (0%) were moderate, 7 (50.0%) were weak, and 5 (35.7.3%) were non-producers (Supplementary Table S2).

To assess the consistency between the two methodologies, κ was calculated. The analysis revealed a moderate level of agreement between the two methods (Cohen’s κ = 0.51, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the relationship between the presence of the biofilm-associated gene luxS and the observed biofilm-forming phenotypes was investigated. No significant association was found between the presence of the luxS gene and the biofilm production categories determined by the qualitative CRA method (Chi-squared test, p = 0.583). Similarly, a comparison of biofilm biomass revealed no significant difference in the median OD values between isolates carrying the luxS gene and those without it (Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.814).

Cytotoxicity Profile of Listeria spp. Isolates

The cytotoxic potential of cell-free supernatants from all 104 Listeria spp. isolates was evaluated against four eukaryotic cell lines, revealing distinct species-specific differences. No measurable cytotoxic effect was observed for any of the isolates belonging to L. innocua, L. welshimeri, or L. seeligeri (n = 80 isolates, 76.9% of the total).

Cytotoxic activity was almost exclusively associated with the L. monocytogenes species. Of the 24 L. monocytogenes isolates, 22 (91.7%) demonstrated measurable cytotoxicity against at least one cell line, while only 2 (8.3%) were non-cytotoxic. The intensity of the cytotoxic effect varied considerably among the isolates and was dependent on the target cell line. The most pronounced effects were observed against Hep-G2 cells, where 79.2% of isolates showed activity, and HT-29 cells (66.7%). Caco-2 cells were less susceptible (37.5%), while Vero cells exhibited the lowest activity, with only one isolate (4.2%) inducing a cytotoxic response.

Detailed analysis of the cytotoxic effect of L. monocytogenes isolates, summarized in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S3, revealed specific patterns for each cell line. For the Caco-2 cell line, 9 isolates were active, resulting in a mean cell death of 10.9% (± 3.8% SEM) across the entire L. monocytogenes group. For the Vero cells, the L. monocytogenes isolates were less active, with only one isolate yieldinga medium level of cytotoxicity, leading to a low overall mean cell death of 1.5% (± 1.5% SEM). Greater cytotoxic activity was observed against the HT-29 cell line, where 16 isolates were active, yielding a mean cell death of 33.4% (± 6.1% SEM). The most pronounced effects of cytotoxicity were observed with the Hep-G2 cell line, where 19 isolates were active, corresponding to the highest mean cell death of 50.5% (± 6.6% SEM).

A significant association was found between the absence of the iap, prfA, and actA genes and a lack of cytotoxic activity. Specifically, the absence of the iap gene was significantly associated with non-cytotoxicity across the Caco-2, HT-29, and Hep-G2 cell lines (Mann-Whitney U test, p < 0.05).

Discussion

The contamination of raw fish with Listeria spp. represents a significant and ongoing challenge for food safety, particularly given the well-established pathogenic potential of L. monocytogenes. This study provides a comprehensive characterization of 104 Listeria spp. isolates recovered from raw fish in Poland, where the predominant species were L. seeligeri (34.6%) and L. welshimeri (28.8%), followed by the primary pathogen L. monocytogenes (23.1%). Our results confirmed a clear demarcation in virulence potential between L. monocytogenes and the other co-isolated species established before [2], however, the investigation also uncovers noteworthy traits in species typically considered non-pathogenic, such as a widespread capacity for biofilm formation. Our findings, therefore, contribute to a deeper understanding of the distribution of virulence factors, biofilm-forming capacity, and cytotoxicity of Listeria populations circulating in the aquatic food chain, providing valuable data for regional risk assessment.

An intriguing aspect of our findings is the detection of certain virulence-associated genes, notably plcA, mpl, and iap, in species generally considered non-pathogenic, such as L. innocua and L. welshimeri. While our statistical analysis confirmed that the prevalence of these genes was significantly lower than in L. monocytogenes, their presence is noteworthy. For example, plcA was identified in a substantial proportion of L. innocua (78.6%) and L. welshimeri (43.3%) isolates. Our findings in atypical Listeria spp. isolates stays in conjunction with previous findings. It was reported that the L. innocua may contains all the members of the PrfA-regulated virulence gene cluster (LIPI 1) of L. monocytogenes [45]. Moreover, the atypical L. innocua strains, positive for hlyA and plcA were isolated from food of animal origin [46, 47]. Our finding, supported by sporadic reports in literature [48–50], suggests that the broader Listeria genus may function as a reservoir for virulence factors, with the potential for horizontal gene transfer. The presence of these genes alone is insufficient to confer a pathogenic phenotype, a conclusion that is strongly supported by the complete lack of cytotoxicity observed for these species in our in vitro assays. This finding nevertheless highlights the genetic plasticity within the genus and underscores the importance of continued surveillance of all Listeria spp/in the food supply chain, as they may contribute to the evolution of pathogenicity in the broader microbial community. However, it is wort to highlight that Listeria genome was reported as highly stable [51], thus different models considering evolution of the core genome in relation to virulence and adaptation to different environments based on recombination and positive selection, and the phylogenetic relationship between of L. monocytogenes and between them and other Listeria spp. may happened trough a long evolution process and selection pressure [52]. It has been reported that recombination does not happen evenly in all the Listeria genome, but it can represent a rapid means to introduce novel genetic material into the bacterial genome. It was noted that approximately 50.0% of the core genes of L. monocytogenes showed evidence for recombination. The short length of recombining fragments (i.e. few hundred base pairs), indicated the horizontal gene transfer, occurred through transformation. Recombination followed by selective pressure allowed L. monocytogenes strains to successfully competed and survived in a broad range of different environments. Together with recombination, also point mutations contributed to bacterial evolution. It was showed that inlA mutations leading to premature stop codons were common events in L. monocytogenes, and it leaded to the generation of truncated secreted proteins [52].

In stark contrast to the other species, the L. monocytogenes isolates from this study exhibited a remarkably consistent and comprehensive virulence profile indicating a high pathogenic potential, nevertheless, raw fish samples represented different type of fish species and originated from different producers. A key finding was the near-universal presence of a complete virulence cassette; the essential genes plcA, hlyA, mpl, and the internalins inlA, inlC, and inlJ were detected in 100% of the 24 isolates. Furthermore, the core components of the LIPI-1 were highly conserved, with prfA and actA presented in 95.8% (23/24) of isolates and iap in 91.7% (22/24). Consequently, a complete LIPI-1, essential for intracellular survival and cell-to-cell spread, was identified in almost all (95.8%) of the strains. This high prevalence of a full genetic “toolkit” for pathogenesis aligns with previous reports on L. monocytogenes from raw fish internationally. For instance, a study in Iran by Jamali (2015) [25] found that 100% of isolates carried actA, hlyA, and iap, while over 95% carried plcA and prfA. Similarly, research in Turkey by Karakaya et al. (2025) [30] and in Egypt by Basha et al. (2019) [53] documented the presence of a wide array of virulence genes, including internalins and LIPI-1 components, in all tested isolates from fish. The consistent detection of this robust genetic profile in Polish isolates suggests that strains circulating in the regional raw fish supply are not environmentally attenuated but are fully equipped for infection, posing a significant and direct risk to public health.

A critical finding of our study is that all 24 L. monocytogenes isolates (100%) belonged to the serogroup 1/2a, 1/2c, 3a, 3c. This is of particular public health significance, as this group includes serotype 1/2a, which, along with 1/2b and 4b, is responsible for the vast majority of human listeriosis cases worldwide [15, 31]. Our results are consistent with other studies on fish isolates in Poland; for example, Wieczorek and Osek (2017) reported that serogroup 1/2a-3a was the most common (64.3%) among 28 isolates from fresh fish [54]. The complete dominance of a single, clinically relevant serogroup in our collection, combined with the near-universal presence of a full LIPI-1 and key internalin genes, provides compelling evidence of a high-risk population of L. monocytogenes in the examined raw fish. This dual finding strongly suggests that these isolates are not only genetically equipped to cause disease but also belonged to a lineage frequently implicated in human infections (Linae II – 1/2a-3a), underscoring their considerable pathogenic potential.

Beyond direct virulence, the ability of Listeria to persist in food processing environments is a critical factor in its transmission, with biofilm formation being a key survival strategy. A significant finding of this study was that the majority of all Listeria isolates (69.2%) demonstrated the ability to produce biofilm in vitro, irrespective of species. Interestingly, our analysis showed that the distribution of biofilm phenotypes, as assessed by the qualitative CRA method, was significantly different among species (p = 0.014), a difference not observed in the quantitative assay. This suggests that while the capacity to produce biofilm biomass may be widespread, the regulation or composition of the biofilm matrix may differ between species. Furthermore, we observed no significant correlation between the presence of the luxS gene associated with quorum sensing and biofilm regulation and the biofilm-forming phenotype. This lack of a direct genotype-phenotype link is consistent with other studies, which have shown that biofilm formation is a complex, multifactorial process not dependent on a single gene like pgpH or agrA, agrB, agrC and agrD [16, 55]. The widespread ability of both pathogenic and non-pathogenic Listeria to form biofilm, as demonstrated in our study, highlights a broader environmental adaptation that facilitates persistence in food processing plants. This finding reinforces the notion that control strategies must target the entire Listeria genus, as non-pathogenic species forming biofilm can create protective niches that may also shelter L. monocytogenes.

The ultimate confirmation of pathogenic potential lies in the ability of an isolate to inflict damage on host cells. Our study unequivocally demonstrated that this capacity was exclusive to L. monocytogenes. While all other species were non-cytotoxic, 91.7% of L. monocytogenes isolates exhibited measurable cytotoxic activity against at least one cell line. This finding functionally validates the genetic data, confirming that only L. monocytogenes possessed a complete and active virulence apparatus. A novel insight from our work is the strong statistical association between the absence of the iap, prfA, and actA genes and a complete lack of cytotoxic activity (p < 0.05). While the pore-forming toxin LLO, encoded by the universally present hlyA gene, is the direct mediator of cytotoxicity [14], our results suggest that a fully functional regulatory and invasion-associated genetic framework is essential for the expression of this phenotype. The two non-cytotoxic L. monocytogenes isolates in our collection lacked these specific genes (iap, prfA and actA), providing a clear genetic basis for their attenuated virulence. This link between a specific, albeit minor, genotypic variation and a complete loss of a key phenotypic trait underscores the intricate regulation of Listeria pathogenesis.

It is important to acknowledge several limitations of the present study. Firstly, the isolates were collected from a specific geographical region in northern Poland, and therefore, the findings may not be fully representative of the Listeria spp. populations circulating in other parts of the country or in different types of aquatic environments. Secondly, our molecular analysis focused on the presence or absence of specific virulence genes. This approach does not provide information on the level of gene expression, which can significantly influence the pathogenic phenotype. Furthermore, the in vitro models used to assess biofilm formation and cytotoxicity, while standardized, may not fully recapitulate the complex conditions encountered within a host or a food processing environment. Specifically, the quantitative biofilm assays were performed at 37 °C to maintain experimental consistency with the cytotoxicity protocols; however, we acknowledge that this temperature does not reflect the refrigeration conditions typical of fish processing environments, where biofilm formation dynamics may differ. The use of cell-free supernatants for cytotoxicity testing, in particular, assesses the effect of secreted toxins but does not capture the dynamic interactions of intracellular infection. Moreover, although technical replicates were included to ensure precision, the study did not incorporate independent biological replicates involving separate cell passages or experiments conducted at different time points, which limits the evaluation of inter-experimental reproducibility. Finally, our investigation was limited to a predefined set of virulence and biofilm-associated genes, and other genetic factors not included in our screening could also contribute to the observed phenotypes. Despite these limitations, this study provides a valuable and detailed characterization of Listeria spp. from raw fish, offering important insights into their pathogenic potential.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that raw fish in Poland are a reservoir for highly virulent Listeria monocytogenes strains. These isolates possess a complete virulence gene profile, including LIPI-1 and key internalins, and their pathogenic potential was functionally validated by their exclusive cytotoxic activity in vitro. In contrast, other co-isolated species (L. innocua, L. welshimeri, and L. seeligeri), while phenotypically non-pathogenic, carry isolated virulence genes, suggesting they may act as an environmental reservoir for these determinants. Crucially, the widespread ability of all tested species to form biofilm—a trait independent of luxS gene presence—represents a significant persistence risk in food processing environments, potentially sheltering the primary pathogen. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive monitoring of the entire Listeria genus within the food chain to effectively mitigate the public health risk posed by L. monocytogenes.
