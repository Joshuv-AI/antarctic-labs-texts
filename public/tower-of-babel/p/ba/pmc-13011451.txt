Comparing mental health professionals’ willingness to use digital therapeutics: a cross-national survey in South Korea and Germany
Authors: Daa Un Moon, Shinjae Park, Min-Ji Kim, Stefan Gutwinski, Felix Bermpohl, Malek Bajbouj, Hong Jin Jeon, Alva Lütt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital therapeutics (DTx) are software-based medical interventions used to prevent, manage, or treat health conditions. As mental health DTx become more prevalent, understanding clinicians’ willingness to use them and the systemic and individual factors that shape that willingness is critical for effective implementation. This study aimed to compare perceptions of DTx among mental health professionals in South Korea and Germany.MethodsA cross-sectional, web-based survey was conducted between February and May 2024 among mental health professionals in South Korea and Germany. The survey assessed participant characteristics, digital technology use, willingness to use DTx, perceived applicability, motivations, and related concerns. Group differences between countries were analyzed, and logistic regression was used to identify predictors of willingness to use DTx. Exploratory analyses examined differences across professional groups.ResultsA total of 237 participants completed the survey (South Korea: n = 133; Germany: n = 104). Overall, 63.7% (n = 151) reported willingness to use DTx, with a significantly higher proportion in Germany than in South Korea (71.2% vs 57.9%; p = 0.035). Significant differences were observed in expected DTx functionalities and perceived applicability across mental health conditions. South Korean clinicians reported higher willingness to use AI-based chatbots, wearable-supported monitoring, and digital psychotherapy, whereas German clinicians reported higher willingness to use relaxation-based interventions. Perceived applicability also differed by country: South Korean participants more frequently considered DTx suitable for stress-related and childhood/adolescent-onset disorders, while German participants more frequently considered them suitable for substance use and schizophrenia spectrum disorders. In multivariable analysis, higher technological readiness (OR = 4.92, 95% CI 2.19–11.09; p < 0.001), residence in Germany (OR = 3.37, 95% CI 1.53–7.42; p = 0.003), and being a medical doctor (OR = 2.78, 95% CI 1.23–6.25; p = 0.027) were associated with greater willingness to use DTx.ConclusionsClinicians in both countries reported willingness to use DTx, although levels varied by country, profession, and level of digital engagement. Background

Digital therapeutics (DTx) are evidence-based medical interventions delivered via software to prevent, manage, or treat diseases and disorders [1, 2]. Unlike general wellness applications, DTx are designed to produce clinically validated outcomes and are subject to regulatory oversight similar to pharmaceuticals and medical devices [2–5]. In the context of mental health, DTx may offer scalable and accessible treatments, such as internet-based cognitive behavioral therapy, real-time symptom tracking, or AI-guided behavioral interventions, integrated into routine care [2–4, 6].

The development of DTx is increasing globally, but its clinical adoption remains uneven [7–10]. Regulatory frameworks, reimbursement mechanisms, and provider readiness vary considerably across countries, influencing how these technologies are implemented in mental health care [9, 11]. Germany and South Korea represent two countries with advanced digital infrastructure and strong public engagement with health technologies; however, they differ significantly in terms of regulatory maturity and the integration of DTx into clinical practice. Germany introduced the Digital Healthcare Act (DVG) in 2019, enabling physicians and psychotherapists to prescribe certified digital health applications (DiGA), which are reimbursed by statutory health insurance [12, 13]. These applications are evaluated and approved by the Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices (BfArM), which maintains the official DiGA directory [14]. As of early 2024, over 50 DiGAs had been approved, with more than half targeting psychiatric conditions such as depression, anxiety, or insomnia [14].

In contrast, South Korea initiated its regulatory pathway for DTx in 2020, with the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) approving the first products in 2023 [15–18]. Although South Korea has a high smartphone penetration rate and has rapidly integrated digital innovations, the use of DTx remains limited [17]. As of 2024, four DTx products had received market approval, of which two target mental health conditions [18]. While Germany has established reimbursement mechanisms and clinical workflows for DTx, South Korea’s integration remains shaped by evolving policies and emerging reimbursement models [11, 16].

Cultural and technological factors further differentiate these national contexts. In South Korea, a high level of digital literacy and widespread use of mobile technologies support early engagement with digital health tools [17, 18]. In Germany, adoption has been described as cautious, with clinicians citing concerns about data privacy, patient suitability, and the therapeutic value of digital interventions [19, 20]. The digital divide, particularly among older patients, remains a further barrier to widespread uptake [21]. These contrasting environments may shape not only how clinicians evaluate DTx but also their willingness to use it in clinical practice.

Mental health professionals play a critical role in the successful implementation of DTx. As gatekeepers, their perceptions, clinical confidence, and digital readiness influence whether DTx are integrated into mental health systems [22]. While DTx can support clinical decision-making, treatment standardization, and patient self-management, clinicians face challenges such as insufficient training, uncertainty surrounding clinical evidence, and a lack of fit with existing workflows [23, 24]. Addressing these concerns is essential to realizing the potential of DTx in real-world practice [25]. Individual factors, including personality characteristics, may also influence engagement with digital health technologies. Prior studies have shown that psychological dispositions shape attitudes toward AI and digital tools [26] and are linked to the use of mobile health applications and wearable technologies [27, 28]. Research in digital mental health similarly indicates that individual differences can affect intention to use or persist with such interventions [29, 30].

Despite increasing attention to DTx development, few studies have explored how health professionals across different health systems and cultures perceive their use, especially in psychiatry. Germany and South Korea offer valuable comparative insight, given their differing levels of regulatory maturity and cultural approaches to digital health. This study aimed to compare perceptions, willingness to use, and perceived barriers to DTx among mental health professionals in both countries. By identifying shared and context-specific challenges, the study seeks to inform tailored implementation strategies and contribute to a broader understanding of how regulatory, technological, and cultural factors influence DTx integration in mental healthcare.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a web-based, cross-sectional online survey to explore the perceptions, willingness to use, and barriers to using DTx among mental health care professionals in South Korea and Germany. Data collection was conducted between February 15 and May 21, 2024. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Charité Universitätsmedizin Berlin, Germany (No. EA1/271/23), and Samsung Medical Center, South Korea (No. 2023–08-181–003). Participation was voluntary, and all participants provided electronic informed consent prior to survey initiation.

Participants and recruitment

Eligible participants were adult mental health professionals (psychiatrists, psychologists, psychotherapists, and psychiatric nurses) currently practicing in either South Korea or Germany. Participants were required to have an active clinical role, defined as providing direct patient care and having access to the internet. Recruitment was conducted through professional networks and institutions in both countries. These included academic psychiatric departments, professional associations, hospital-based networks, and digital health interest groups. A publicly accessible website was developed to host the survey link and provide information about the study. Recruitment emails were distributed in both countries, and similar channels were used to promote equivalent visibility. Participants reviewed an online consent form outlining the study’s purpose, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, anonymity, and data protection protocols. Completion of the survey implied consent. Participation was anonymous, and no personally identifiable information was collected. Participants could withdraw at any time.

Survey

An initial survey draft was developed by DM and AL after conducting a literature review [31–36], with all authors contributing feedback and approving the final version. The survey, available in Korean and German, was designed to be completed in approximately 15 minutes. Before launch, it was pilot-tested with a small sample of mental health professionals in both countries to ensure clarity and cultural appropriateness.

Measures

Participants provided sociodemographic and professional information, including age group, gender, years of education, country of residence, relationship status, profession, clinical experience, work environment, and primary patient population. To explore their use of digital technology, participants were asked about their personal and professional use of devices, including computers, smartphones, and wearables, as well as their average screen time.

The survey incorporated validated scales to assess participants’ attitudes toward technology and personality traits. The Technology Readiness Index (TRI 2.0) measured participants’ predisposition to embrace or resist new technologies. This scale evaluates four dimensions: optimism, innovativeness, discomfort, and insecurity [37]. The Affinity for Technology Interaction (ATI) assessed participants’ ease and preference for engaging with technology in their daily lives, offering insights into their comfort level with using digital tools [38]. The Big Five Inventory-10 (BFI-10), a brief and widely used measure of personality traits, was included to capture key aspects of personality across five dimensions: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and emotional stability [39].

Participants were introduced to DTx through a standardized definition: software programs (web or mobile applications) developed to deliver evidence-based interventions for preventing, managing, or treating medical disorders or diseases, including those related to mental health [1]. Based on this definition, participants were asked about their willingness to use DTx, preferred functionalities, reasons for use, and concerns regarding their implementation. The survey concluded with an open-ended question, allowing participants to share additional comments or suggestions regarding digital health interventions.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and their responses. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (± SD) or medians with interquartile ranges [IQR], depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons between participants in South Korea and Germany were conducted using independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

To identify factors associated with willingness to use DTx, logistic regression analyses were performed. Univariable models first explored potential predictors, including demographic, professional, and technological variables. For categorical variables with multiple levels (e.g., age, profession), Bonferroni correction was applied. Variables found to be significant at p < 0.05 in univariable models were considered for inclusion in a multivariable logistic regression using forward stepwise selection (P for entry and removal = 0.05). Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. No survey weights or quotas were applied. Missing data were minimal, and complete case analysis was used.

In addition, exploratory analyses were conducted to examine profession-specific differences in motivations for using DTx, expected DTx functionalities, and perceived applicability across diagnostic categories. Likert-scaled items were compared using Kruskal-Wallis tests, and binary survey items were compared using chi-square tests.

All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic, professional, and technological characteristics

A total of 284 mental health professionals responded to the survey. Results are reported in the order South Korea followed by Germany for consistency. After excluding 47 incomplete responses (13 from South Korea, 34 from Germany), 237 complete responses (133 from South Korea, 104 from Germany) were included in the final analysis (Table 1). The majority of participants in both countries identified as female (South Korea: 75.9%; Germany: 72.1%, p = 0.322). Participants in South Korea were older than those in Germany, with a lower proportion aged 30–39 years (32.3% vs. 53.9%) and a higher proportion aged 50 years and older (26.3% vs. 9.6%; p < 0.001). Median years of professional experience were similarly higher in South Korea (6 [5–6] vs. 5 [3–5.5] years; p < 0.001). Professional backgrounds differed significantly by country (p < 0.001). In South Korea, psychologists (38.4%) and nurses (37.6%) were more prevalent than in Germany (22.1% and 24.0%, respectively), whereas medical doctors were more common in Germany (21.1% vs. 48.1%).

A comparison of digital device use and technology-related behaviors between participants in South Korea and Germany is presented in Supplementary Table S1. Regarding the use of digital devices, smartphone ownership was nearly universal in both groups (South Korea: 100%; Germany: 98.1%; p = 0.192). Participants in South Korea reported higher daily screen time (3 [2–5] vs. 2 [2–3] hours; p < 0.001), higher smartwatch ownership (44.4% vs. 20.2%; p < 0.001), and more daily computer use at work (6 [4–8] vs. 4 [3–6] hours; p < 0.001). Supplementary Table S2 presents the results comparing attitudes toward technology and personality traits. Participants in South Korea demonstrated higher technological readiness with higher TRI 2.0 (mean 3.43 ± 0.42 vs. 3.18 ± 0.49; p < 0.001) and ATI scores (median 3.78 [3.33–4.22] vs. 2.89 [2.39–3.56]; p < 0.001). South Korean participants demonstrated significantly higher willingness to use AI-based chatbots (2 [2–3] vs. 1 [1–3]; p < 0.001), wearable-based health monitoring (3 [2–3] vs. 2 [1–3]; p < 0.001), (social media) based mental health support communities (3 [2–3] vs. 2 [1–3]; p = 0.003) and digital psychotherapy (3 [2–3] vs. 2 [1–3]; p = 0.001). German participants reported higher willingness to use relaxation techniques (3 [2–3] vs. 3 [3–4]; p = 0.013). Participants in both countries reported similar levels of willingness to use DTx for disease monitoring, biofeedback/neurofeedback, gamified mental health interventions, integrated clinical decision support tools, and teleconsultation, with no statistically significant differences between South Korea and Germany.

Perceived barriers also varied by country (Supplementary Table S3). South Korean participants more frequently reported concerns about high costs (3 [2–3] vs. 1 [1–2]; p < 0.001), lack of research and evidence (3 [2–3] vs. 2 [1–3]; p < 0.001), and data privacy and security (3 [3–4] vs. 3 [2–3]; p < 0.001). They also reported greater concern regarding technical challenges and uncertain reliability (3 [2–3] vs. 2 [1.5–3]; p = 0.008) and perceived DTx as more time-consuming (2 [2–3] vs. 1 [1–2]; p < 0.001). German participants, in contrast, more often expressed concerns about a lack of personal connection with the patient (2 [1–3] vs. 3 [2–4]; p < 0.001), that DTx may not be suitable for all patients (3 [3–3] vs. 3 [3–4]; p = 0.001), and that its use may be limited for certain patient groups (3 [3–3] vs. 3 [3–4]; p = 0.005). Concerns about a lack of knowledge and training were common in both countries, with no significant difference (3 [3–3] vs. 3 [2–3], p = 0.070).

Perceived applicability to mental health conditions

Both groups perceived DTx as applicable to a range of mental health conditions (Table 2). South Korean participants reported higher applicability for neurotic and stress-related disorders (69.2% vs. 49.0%; p = 0.002), behavioral syndromes related to physiological disturbances and physical factors (48.9% vs. 24.0%; p < 0.001), and childhood and adolescent-onset disorders (42.1% vs. 17.3%; p < 0.001). German participants more frequently reported DTx applicability for substance use disorders (30.1% vs. 51.0%; p = 0.001) and schizophrenia spectrum disorders (11.3% vs. 31.7%; p < 0.001). Both groups reported similar high applicability for mood disorders (63.9% vs. 68.3%; p = 0.483) and stress management and prevention (74.4% vs. 65.4%, p = 0.130), with no significant differences.

South Korean participants more frequently reported convenience and accessibility for patients (85.0% vs. 71.2%; p = 0.010). German participants were more likely to report the potential to improve patient outcomes (51.1% vs. 82.7%; p < 0.001) and interest in researching innovative therapeutic approaches (29.3% vs. 48.1%; p = 0.003). Other motivational factors, including positive feedback from patients and perceived cost efficiency, did not differ significantly between countries.

Predictors of willingness to use DTx

Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with willingness to use DTx. Univariable logistic regression results are presented in Fig. 1. Participants living in Germany were more likely to report willingness than those in South Korea (OR = 1.79; 95% CI: 1.04–3.10; p = 0.036). Professional background was associated with willingness, with medical doctors reporting higher willingness compared with psychologists (OR = 2.73; 95% CI: 1.35–5.55; p = 0.011) and nurses (OR = 2.53; 95% CI: 1.25–5.13; p = 0.007). Several technology-related variables were also significant. Higher TRI 2.0 (OR = 2.71; 95% CI: 1.47–5.02; p = 0.001) and higher ATI scores (OR = 1.52; 95% CI: 1.12–2.07; p = 0.008) were both associated with willingness, as was increased work-related computer use (OR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.11–1.40; p < 0.001) and social media use (OR = 2.08; 95% CI: 1.21–3.57; p = 0.008). Motivational variables were also important. Participants who reported interest in researching innovative therapeutic approaches (OR = 2.54; 95% CI: 1.41–4.56; p = 0.002), perceived potential to enhance patient outcomes (OR = 2.02; 95% CI: 1.16–3.50; p = 0.013), cost efficiency (OR = 2.07; 95% CI: 1.08–4.00; p = 0.029) were more willing to use DTx. Years of professional experience were inversely associated with willingness (OR = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.64–0.95; p = 0.013). Age, gender, providing psychotherapy, and personality traits were not significantly associated with willingness to use DTx (all p > 0.05)

Profession-specific differences in DTx perceptions

Exploratory analyses examined whether motivations for using DTx, expected DTx functionalities, and perceived applicability across mental health conditions differed by professional group (Supplementary Table S4). For expected DTx functionalities, several significant differences were observed. Nurses reported higher ratings for gamified mental health interventions (3 [2–3] vs. 2 [1–3] among medical doctors and 2 [2–3] among psychologists; p = 0.003), AI-based chatbot support (3 [2–4] vs. 2 [1–3] and 2 [1–2]; p < 0.001), wearable-based health monitoring (3 [2–4] vs. 2 [1–3] and 2 [2–3]; p < 0.001), and monitoring patients’ healthcare service utilization (3 [3–4] vs. 3 [2–3] and 3 [2–3]; p = 0.012). For reasons for willingness to use DTx, only “positive feedback or inquiries from patients” differed significantly across groups (p = 0.005), reported by nurses (32.0%) compared with psychologists (18.9%) and medical doctors (25.6%). Perceived applicability across mental health conditions was generally similar across professional groups. Significant differences were found for schizophrenia-spectrum disorders (p = 0.013), with higher applicability reported by nurses (25.3%) and medical doctors (20.5%) compared with psychologists (10.8%). Mood [affective] disorders also differed (p = 0.014), with medical doctors reporting higher applicability (76.9%) than psychologists (59.5%) or nurses (57.3%).

Discussion

This study explored the perceptions, willingness to use, and observed barriers towards DTx among mental health professionals in South Korea and Germany. The findings underscore both shared and country-specific factors that influence clinician readiness to integrate DTx into mental health care. Overall, while professionals in both countries expressed moderate-to-high willingness to use DTx, significant differences emerged regarding perceived applicability, motivations for use, and concerns.

Participants from both countries demonstrated a high overall willingness to use DTx. However, willingness was significantly higher among German participants. This difference likely reflects disparities in systemic exposure and institutional support. Germany’s well-established framework for DTx (DiGA) [12, 19, 40] provides a structured regulatory and reimbursement pathway. DiGAs are fully reimbursed by statutory health insurance (GKV), which facilitates accessibility and incentivizes use. As of September 2023, approximately 374,000 DiGA prescriptions had been activated, demonstrating the widespread integration of DTx into German health care and clinicians’ exposure to and confidence in prescribing these tools [41]. In contrast, South Korea’s regulatory and reimbursement environment remains emergent. Despite the approval of four DTx products by April 2024, fewer than ten prescriptions had been recorded, suggesting limited clinical integration to date [11, 18].

Technology readiness emerged as a critical factor influencing willingness to use DTx. However, while Korean participants reported higher technology readiness, clinicians in Germany showed greater willingness to use DTx, likely reflecting the more established systemic support for DTx use in Germany, including clearer regulatory frameworks and reimbursement pathways. This suggests that environmental and structural enablers may play a more decisive role in influencing clinical willingness than individual digital affinity alone [3].

Personality traits were not significantly associated with willingness to use DTx in this study. While prior work has identified associations between personality characteristics and attitudes toward digital health or AI-based technologies, these effects have generally been small [26, 29]. Other studies similarly emphasize that structural and technological factors, such as perceived usefulness, workflow integration, and system-level support, tend to play a more prominent role in digital health adoption than individual personality differences [27, 28, 30].

Professional background was another significant predictor. Medical doctors were more likely to express willingness compared to psychologists and nurses. This difference may relate to clinical authority and decision-making roles. In Germany, both physicians and psychological psychotherapists can prescribe DiGA [42, 43], while in South Korea, only medical doctors currently hold prescribing authority [44]. This difference in clinical decision-making roles may influence perceptions and familiarity with DTx. However, uptake among psychotherapists in Germany remains low in practice, accounting for only 5% of DiGA prescriptions, highlighting that regulatory permission does not guarantee practical use and suggesting that other factors, such as limited training, awareness, or perceived patient fit, may be more influential [45]. Exploratory analyses also suggested variation across professions in how DTx functionalities and clinical applications were perceived. Nurses reported higher ratings for certain functionalities, such as gamified interventions, AI-based chatbot support, and wearable-based monitoring, while medical doctors more often viewed DTx as applicable for mood disorders. Although exploratory, these differences may reflect profession-specific roles and workflows and highlight the value of tailoring implementation efforts to diverse professional groups.

Barriers to DTx use reflected both shared and context-specific concerns. Across both countries, clinicians reported limited knowledge and insufficient training as key obstacles, findings consistent with previous literature [23, 46, 47]. Korean participants more frequently cited concerns about high costs, data security, and insufficient clinical evidence, reflecting system-level constraints and limited exposure to validated tools. Conversely, German clinicians more frequently emphasized concerns about maintaining therapeutic relationships and patient suitability, highlighting differing clinical priorities across contexts.

Prior studies examining patients’ and service users’ experiences with digital mental health interventions reported comparable patterns of perceived benefits and barriers. Systematic reviews note that digital interventions are generally viewed positively by users, but concerns such as usability difficulties, limited personalization, data privacy, and reduced personal connection with clinicians remain common [48, 49]. Other studies highlight the importance of perceived usefulness, trust, and ease of integration into daily routines for sustained engagement with digital mental health tools [50, 51]. These findings suggest that successful implementation of DTx requires alignment of both clinician and patient perspectives, particularly regarding clinical utility, usability, and data protection.

Despite these challenges, both groups recognized clear benefits of DTx, including improved patient outcomes, increased accessibility, and support for evidence-based practice. These motivating factors suggest actionable pathways to build clinician confidence. In South Korea, strengthening reimbursement structures and integrating DTx into national guidelines may catalyze broader acceptance [52]. In Germany, targeted training and workflow integration support may further enhance adoption. Overall, collaboration among stakeholders, including policymakers, clinicians, and industry leaders, seems critical for a successful implementation.

This study is among the first to systematically compare clinician perspectives on DTx across culturally and systemically distinct healthcare settings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and only captures attitudes at a single time point. Second, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce reporting biases, including social desirability or recall bias. Third, the use of an online, convenience-based recruitment strategy may have led to sampling bias, potentially excluding clinicians with limited digital access or lower technology literacy, thereby overestimating overall technological readiness and willingness to use DTx [53]. In addition, willingness to use DTx, as measured in this study, may not directly translate into actual clinical adoption. Unmeasured contextual variables, such as institutional policies, organizational culture, or access to digital infrastructure, may also influence responses. Moreover, differences in primary working environments and treatment populations between the two country samples may limit the interpretation of cross-country comparisons. German participants more frequently worked in hospital-based settings and reported a higher focus on schizophrenia spectrum and substance use disorders, whereas South Korean participants more often worked in outpatient settings and reported a greater focus on stress-related and neurotic disorders. These contextual differences may have influenced clinicians’ perceptions of DTx, particularly regarding applicability and perceived barriers. Lastly, given the rapid evolution of the DTx landscape, these findings may require periodic reassessment to remain current. Future research should employ longitudinal and mixed-method designs, engage diverse stakeholder perspectives, including patients, and explore implementation across a broader range of countries and care settings.

Conclusions

The integration of DTx into mental health care will depend not only on regulatory frameworks and technological availability but also on clinician readiness and perceptions. By identifying the factors that influence willingness across two distinct systems, this study underscores the need for coordinated implementation strategies that address both systemic and individual-level barriers. Ongoing support, education, and context-sensitive deployment will be critical to realizing the full potential of DTx in mental health practice.
