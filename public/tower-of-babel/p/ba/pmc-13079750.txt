Impact of dietary replacement of soybean meal with high-protein wheat on gut microbial metabolism in chickens
Authors: Dorota Miśta, Jarosław Król, Ewa Pecka-Kiełb, Joanna Kamińska, Dorota Pelan, Anna Szuba-Trznadel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The study examined the potential use of high-protein wheat (AW) as an alternative to soybean meal (SBM)-based diet for poultry. One-day-old broiler chickens were divided into 4 groups: control (C), fed a standard mixture, and three experimental groups (W50, W75 and W100) in which 50, 75 and 100%, respectively, of SBM was replaced by AW. Performance and caecal parameters (body weight, caecal morphometry, short-chain fatty acids – SCFAs) of the birds were measured at days 14, 21, 35 and 42 of age. Selected groups of microorganisms in the ileum and caecum were assessed quantitatively. Microbial metabolites (SCFAs and methane) were analysed in vitro, using caecal inocula collected from another group of control chickens, incubated with substrates containing SBM and AW in the proportions corresponding to those in the feeding protocols. The W100 chickens had a distinctly lower body weight than control animals. The development of caecal parameters was also slower, though physiologically correct, in this group. The microbial colonization of the caeca (especially by lactic acid bacteria) and changes in their activity took a longer time in chickens receiving the highest dose of AW. The W50 group was most similar to the control group with respect to performance and caecal parameters, as well as microbial metabolites. However, in the in vitro experiment, no major differences in the SCFA profile or methanogenesis were observed. Based on the obtained results, it can be recommended to replace 50% of soybean meal with high-protein wheat in the diet of broiler chickens, preferably from day 21 of age.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42098-7.

Introduction

Composing an efficient wheat-based diet for poultry has been a noteworthy challenge and course of action in many European countries in recent decades, as it would help curb dependence on imported soybean meal (SBM), thus contributing to the sustainable development of the meat industry. Given that nearly all varieties of SBM are genetically modified and considering the prospect of introducing a ban on using such feed products, finding an alternative to SBM appears to be a vital problem in the production of poultry. Because the growth of broiler chickens requires intake of sufficient amounts of high-quality digestible protein1, the full replacement of SBM may necessitate the supplementation of crystalline amino acids (AAs). This could also have a number of additional effects, such as reduced emission of ammonia2.

The intensive breeding work on new plant cultivars provides wider possibilities of using feed raw materials as a source of protein in the nutrition of monogastric animals. Wheat cultivars (Triticum aestivum L.) with high protein levels can also be a valuable protein source in poultry nutrition as an alternative to SBM, especially in countries where wheat is grown on a large scale. The qualitative and quantitative composition of nutrients in a plant is mediated genetically, therefore, much effort is made to cultivate varieties with the most favourable chemical profile. A higher protein content in feed grains usually indicates a better feed quality. However, in many cultivars of winter wheat, a higher protein content (2–3% higher than the average) is not always reflected by a high nutritional value – the increase in total nitrogen may not be accompanied by increased levels of AAs3. In contrast, winter wheat of the Activus (AW) cultivar is characterized by a high protein content with a large share of essential AAs in grain, and its additional advantage is the high adaptability to different soil moisture3. Thanks to these properties, the grain of this plant, with appropriate supplementation with crystalline AAs, could be a valuable alternative to SBM component in poultry nutrition.

The nutrition of broilers affects the physiology of their digestive tract, which should be monitored continuously as in other meat-producing animal species. Microbial populations of the gastrointestinal tract (GI tract) play an important role in animal health and growth performance and their composition is affected by host inherent features as well as environmental factors including dietary modifications4. Bacteria are the dominant group of chicken GI tract microbiota, which is also made up of protozoa, fungi and viruses. Owing to the production of metabolites – in particular short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) – the intestinal bacteria play crucial role in the poultry health maintenance and regulation of such processes as metabolism, absorption of nutrients, development of the host tissues, maturation and proper function of the immune system, as well as controlling the intestinal environment and protection from colonization of the gut by harmful bacteria5,6. The presence of microbiota protects the caeca from colonization by pathogenic bacteria through various mechanisms, such as the production of bacteriocins, activation of the intestinal immune system, modifying the receptors used by pathogenic bacteria, as well as the production of SCFAs. The latter limits pathogen growth directly or indirectly by lowering pH, as well as by favoring the proper barrier function of gastrointestinal epithelium. These are products of microbial metabolism of nutrients not digested in the small intestine, such as non-starch polysaccharides, resistant starch, oligosaccharides, fermentable dietary fibre, as well as proteins and amino acids. SCFAs contribute to maintenance of microbial balance via acid stress and regulation of virulence genes in common enteric pathogens7. Moreover, they are important sources of energy for metabolic processes, lipogenesis and body growth. An impact of SCFAs, mainly butyrate and propionate, on fat deposition was also documented in chickens4. These microbial metabolites are considered a kind of communication between the microbiota and the host, they may act as the signal of changes in microbial activity and also as leucocyte activators8,9. Thus, the concentration and profile of SCFAs can be considered as an crucial indicator of hindgut health and may help control intestinal environment in poultry.

Considering the above, this study was carried out to assess the essential metabolic processes in the caecum and performance of gut microbiota in broiler chickens raised on feed in which SBM was replaced (in various proportions) by high-protein AW. We hypothesized that replacing SBM with high-protein wheat would not negatively affect the activity of the caecal microbiota. Therefore, in vivo and in vitro (using a simulation system of processes occurring in chicken caeca) studies were conducted to analyse the dynamics of SCFA production as well as to determine the number of selected indicator bacteria depending on the applied dietary modification.

Materials and methods

In vivo experiment

The in vivo experiment was performed in accordance with institutional guidelines in compliance with national (Journal of Laws of the Republic of Poland, 2015, item. 266, with the changes from Journal of Laws from January 23rd 2023, item 465) and international law and policies (directive 2010/63/EU with regulation 2019/1010/EU). Additionally, all the experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Welfare Advisory Team at the Faculty of Biology and Animal Science, University of Environmental and Life Sciences in Wrocław (04/2023), and all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Birds’ rearing

One hundred and twelve one-day-old Ross 308 male broiler chicks (purchased from a commercial hatchery), with an initial body weight (BW) of 45.88 ± 1.49 g, were randomly divided into four groups (corresponding to four dietary programs). Each dietary treatment included seven replications (boxes), resulting in a total of 28 boxes with four birds each. During the study, chickens were kept under standard rearing conditions. Temperature, humidity, and lighting were consistent with the adequate rearing program10. The initial temperature in boxes was 32 °C and was gradually decreased by 0.5 °C every day, reaching 24 °C by the second week of the experiment. From the third week onward, the temperature was maintained at 20–21 °C. The relative humidity in the boxes ranged from 60% to 73%. During the first seven days of life, the lighting program consisted of 22 h of light and 2 h of darkness. From the remainder of the experiment, the photoperiod was gradually adjusted to 18 h of light and 6 h of darkness. The room was equipped with a mechanical and automatic ventilation system to ensure proper temperature, humidity, and acceptable level of ammonia and carbon dioxide, in accordance with relevant standards. The animals were under veterinary supervision throughout the entire experimental period. The health and welfare status of the birds was monitored at least three times per day by trained personnel. Observations included general behaviour, feed and water intake, physical condition, and any signs of distress or illness. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional and national animal welfare guidelines (Journal of Laws of the Republic of Poland, 2015, item. 266, with the changes from Journal of Laws from January 23rd 2023, item. 465).

Plant material

Activus wheat (AW), harvested in 2020 and purchased as certified grain material from Saatbau Polska Sp. z o.o. (Środa Śląska, Poland), was used for the preparation of all poultry feed mixtures in this experiment. The chemical composition and nutritional value of AW have been described previously³. Activus wheat is a high-protein wheat cultivar, containing more than 16% crude protein in the grain (according to AOAC analyses). True protein (TP) accounted for approximately 95% of crude protein (CP), with essential amino acids (EAAs) representing about 33% of total amino acids. Glutamic acid was the most abundant amino acid (28.8% of CP), whereas lysine, tryptophan, and methionine were identified as the most limiting essential amino acids. The contents of nitrogen-free extractives, crude fat, and crude fibre - fractions largely determining the energy value of wheat - amounted to 689.3 g/kg, 13.0 g/kg, and 26.7 g/kg, respectively³.

Neutral detergent fibre (NDF), acid detergent fibre (ADF), and acid detergent lignin (ADL) were determined according to the Van Soest method (1991) using an Ankom 200 Fiber Analyzer. The NDF fraction was 6.22%, while ADF and ADL accounted for 3.66% and 1.64%, respectively. Hemicellulose content was calculated as the difference between NDF and ADF, and cellulose content as the difference between ADF and ADL. Based on these calculations, hemicellulose constituted the largest fibre fraction (2.56%), followed by cellulose (2.02%) and lignin (1.64%).

Total dietary fibre (TDF) content in wheat amounted to 11.67% and was estimated using near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), an analytical technique based on the absorption of light in the wavelength range of 800–2500 nm by organic matter. Non-starch polysaccharides (NSP) represented the largest proportion of TDF, accounting for approximately 74%. Arabinoxylans (total arabinose + xylose; A + X) constituted nearly 50% of TDF, whereas lignin (ADL) accounted for approximately 13% of TDF.

The gross energy (GE) content of AW was 18.6 MJ/kg³ and was reflected in the apparent metabolizable energy (AME) values estimated for monogastric animals using NIRS. The AME content of Activus wheat for poultry corresponded to approximately 69% of its GE value.

Nitrogen-free extractives (NFE), representing the readily digestible carbohydrate fraction, were calculated by difference [dry matter (DM) − crude protein (CP) − ether extract (EE) − crude fibre (CF) − crude ash (CA)], with all nutrients determined according to standard AOAC procedures. The NFE content of Activus wheat amounted to 689.3 g/kg. Starch content, estimated by NIRS, was 58.26%. Total arabinoxylans (total A + X) reached 5.67%, with insoluble arabinoxylans accounting for 74% of total A + X. Total NSP content was 8.59%, of which the insoluble fraction represented approximately 78%.

Dietary treatments

After purchase, the broiler chickens were randomly assigned to four experimental groups, which were fed in a three-stage feeding system (starter, grower and finisher) according to the following feeding protocols (Supplementary Tables S1-S3)11:

Control group (C), fed a standard complete mixture containing wheat grain (AW) and post-extraction soybean meal (SBM) in proportions according to the Broiler Nutrition Specifications12 and Poultry Nutrition Standards13;W50 group, fed a standard complete mixture, in which 50% SBM was replaced by AW (w/w);W75 group, fed a standard complete mixture, in which 75% SBM was replaced by AW (w/w);W100 group, fed a standard complete mixture, in which 100% SBM was replaced by AW.

Control group (C), fed a standard complete mixture containing wheat grain (AW) and post-extraction soybean meal (SBM) in proportions according to the Broiler Nutrition Specifications12 and Poultry Nutrition Standards13;

W50 group, fed a standard complete mixture, in which 50% SBM was replaced by AW (w/w);

W50 group, fed a standard complete mixture, in which 50% SBM was replaced by AW (w/w);

W75 group, fed a standard complete mixture, in which 75% SBM was replaced by AW (w/w);

W75 group, fed a standard complete mixture, in which 75% SBM was replaced by AW (w/w);

W100 group, fed a standard complete mixture, in which 100% SBM was replaced by AW.

W100 group, fed a standard complete mixture, in which 100% SBM was replaced by AW.

To make all the mixtures isoenergetic, their energy values were adjusted by adding an appropriate amount of soybean oil. In addition, because CP values in the experimental mixtures were lower than those recommended for poultry (by 5%, 4%, and 3% in starter, grower and finisher feed mixtures, respectively), the demand for AAs was covered by the addition of crystalline AAs (DL-Methionine, L-Arginine, L-Lysine, L-Threonine, L-Tryptophan, L-Valine) in appropriate proportions.

In order to avoid increasing viscosity of feed and moisture of feces, the enzymes xylanase (RONOZYME® WX) and phytase (HiPhos) were also added (0.2 g/kg) to the feed mixtures for all groups. Both enzymes were purchased from DSM Nutritional Products, Kaiseraugst (Switzerland).

Intestinal samples preparing

On days 14, 21, 35, and 42 of the experiment, all birds from each box were individually weighed. Subsequently, one bird was randomly selected from each box, rendered unconscious (via CO2 stunning) and euthanized by decapitation, according to Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council of 22 September 2010. Upon dissection, the ileum and both caeca were separated and caecal length (CL) was tape-measured. Then, the left caecum and a fragment of the ileum from each bird was forwarded for microbiological examination (except the 42 day-old chickens), and the right caecum was weighed and used for analyses of products of microbial activity (SCFAs). After emptying, weight of each right caecum was measured and calculated as the percentage of the final chicken body weight. The variability of the caecal weight (CW) and the percentage of caecal weight in total body weight (CW%) over time was examined to trace the development of the avian caeca during rearing. Moreover, caecal weight to length ratio (CW/CL) was calculated.

Caecal SCFA analysis

Following dissection, a one-gram caecal sample from each chicken was diluted 4-fold with an appropriate buffer solution14, homogenized and centrifuged at 2800 g for 20 min. Next, 0.05 mL formic acid per 1 ml of supernatant was added15. Suspensions prepared in this way were stored at −80 °C for future analysis.

After thawing, the samples were analysed using a 7890 A gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a flame ionisation detector and an Agilent J&W DB-WAX column, using helium as carrier gas. The aim of the analysis was to determine the concentrations of the following SCFAs: acetic, propionic, isobutyric, butyric, isovaleric, valeric, isocaproic and caproic. Standard curves were prepared using Volatile Free Acid Mix (Supelco, Bellefonte, USA), and the total SCFA concentration was calculated as the sum of the SCFA concentrations in the digesta. The chromatograms were analysed using ChemStation software (Agilent Technologies). Based on the results of SCFA analysis, acetate to propionate (A: P) and propionate to butyrate (P: B) ratios, as well as non-glucogenic fatty acids to glucogenic fatty acids ratio (NGR) were calculated. NGR, considered as the SCFA utilization rate, was calculated according to Ørskov16, as modified by Abrahamse et al.17: NGR = (A + 2 B + Bc)/(P + Bc), where A, P and B represent the molar proportions (mol%) of acetic, propionic and butyric acids, respectively, and Bc represents the sum of valerate and branched-chain fatty acid molar proportions in the total SCFA concentration.

Microbiological examination

In order to monitor physiological status of the GI tract, the microbiological examination of gut contents was carried out at the day 14, 21 and 35 of chicken age. The materials from the ileum and caecum (portions of ~ 0.1 g) were collected aseptically, weighed and suspended in a corresponding volume of buffered saline (PBS) to obtain an initial suspension of 1:10 (weight/volume). Next, serial dilutions (1:100 through 1:10,000) in PBS were made. One hundred µl of each dilution was then transferred onto the following media: blood agar (Trypticasein Soy Lab-Agar™ supplemented with 5% sheep blood), Mac Conkey Lab-Agar™, KAA Confirmatory Lab-Agar™ and MRS Lab-Agar™ (all purchased from BioMaxima, Lublin, Poland and prepared on Petri dishes of 90 mm diameter). The media were incubated aerobically for 24 hours at 37°C. The isolated bacteria were tentatively identified based on colony and cell morphology, Gram reaction and catalase activity, and then by sequence analysis of the 16S rRNA gene. For the latter procedure, bacterial DNA was first extracted from agar cultures using a commercial kit (A&A Biotechnology, Gdańsk, Poland). Next, PCRs (using the universal primers 16S-27f [5’-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3’] and 16 S-907r [5’-CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT-3’]) were carried out to obtain ~ 900-bp DNA fragments. All sequencing procedures were performed at Genomed S.A., Warsaw, Poland.

The following groups of intestinal bacteria were taken into consideration in the experimental chickens: (1) Escherichia coli, (2) Gram-positive, catalase-negative cocci (the genera Enterococcus and Streptococcus) and (3) the lactic acid bacteria (Lactobacillus and closely related genera). For each type of medium, the plates containing between 30 and 300 of colony forming units (CFUs) of these microorganisms were counted. Next, considering a corresponding dilution factor and the volume of fluid transferred onto the medium surface (i.e., one-tenth of a ml), numbers of the bacteria were calculated per one gram of the ileal or caecal content.

In vitro experiment

Fermentation substrates

For the assessment of fermentation processes in vitro, the caecal inocula, collected from another group of eighteen 42-day-old Ross 308 male chickens fed with standard complete mixtures in the three-stage feeding system (as the control group described previously), were incubated with various fermentation substrates (C, W1,W2, W3), containing SBM and AW in the proportions corresponding to those in the feeding protocols C, W50, W75 and W100, respectively, used in the in vivo experiment. In particular, the following substrates were examined:

C (control), with the SBM to AW ratio amounting to 1: 2 (33.3% and 66.7%).

W1, with the SBM share of 16.7% and AW of 83.3% (1:5).

W2, with the SBM share of 8.3% and AW of 91.7% (1:11).

W3, consisting of 100% AW.

The ingredients were ground using ZM 200 mill (Retsch, Haan, Germany) into fine particles.

Preparing of inoculum and in vitro fermentation process

Immediately after resection of the caeca, their contents were squeezed out and mixed to obtain pooled inoculum samples (from three birds each). Pooled samples were homogenised and their pH was measured using a CP-401 pH meter (Elmetron, Zabrze, Poland). The incubation was performed using ANKOM RF Gas Production System (ANKOM Technology, Macedon, NY, USA) consisting of a module of 5 glass bottles equipped with temperature and pressure sensors, in a shaking water bath at 39 °C for 24 h. The ANKOM system monitors gas pressure in the headspace of each individual bottle, recording partial (at 10-minute intervals) and cumulative gas pressures during the whole fermentation process. Four 20-gram subsamples from each pooled caecal sample were transferred into the bottles, diluted in 80 ml of pre-warmed buffer solution14, and then prepared for incubation by adding 1 g of one of the substrates C, W1, W2 or W3. Each fermentation substrate was tested in 6 replicates. Prior to incubation, each bottle was thoroughly flushed with CO2 through a Luer port until the internal pressure achieved 8 psi (55.2 kPa) and then hermetically closed. All manipulations with the caecal digesta were performed immediately after collection.

Methane and SCFA analyses

The total gas production from each fermentation substrate was calculated using the Clapeyron equation (ideal gas law) based on the cumulative gas pressure, the headspace volume and the temperature inside the bottle. For analysis of methane, gas samples from each bottle were collected manually through side ports using a 10 mL gas-tight syringe (Agilent Technologies) and immediately tested chromatographically with a 7890 A gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies), equipped with flame ionisation and thermal conductivity detectors, two Supelco columns (Porapak Q and HayeSep Q) and a 5 A molecular sieve, with helium as carrier gas (flow rate 25 ml/min). The gas samples were collected at the end of the experiment, so it did not affect the pressure conditions in the units. The chromatograms were analysed using ChemStation software (Agilent Technologies) based on analyses of methane standards (Linde Group, Kraków, Poland). The molar concentration of methane was calculated from the Clapeyron equation, based on the gas pressure and the percentage of methane in the total gas volume.

The liquid content of each bottle was examined for pH, and then 10-mL samples were subject to SCFA analysis. For this purpose, the samples were centrifuged at 2800 g for 20 min, and then fermentation was stopped by adding 0.05 mL formic acid per 1 ml of supernatant. The samples obtained were stored at −80 °C. The chromatographic analysis and adequate calculations were carried out as in the in vivo experiment.

Statistical analysis

Preprocessing

In the first step, extreme values differing from the mean value by more than three standard deviations (σ) were rejected (the 3σ method). The process was carried out iteratively and the mean and standard deviation were determined again for n-1-element samples. The process was repeated until no case differed from the mean value by more than 3σ.

Analysis

The obtained data were analysed and presented graphically using the R statistical software18 and graphical package ggplot219. To identify the relationship between the body and caecum weight of chickens and the SCFA concentrations, the Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used. In order to identify significant differences concerning parameters of gut development as well as the numbers and activity of microbiota in the broilers, an one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was first performed for each sampling date, followed by a t-test for mean differences to obtain detailed information, each time verifying the assumption of variance homogeneity. Descriptive statistics for measured variables were presented in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5. Changes of the analysed traits in the broiler digestive tract over time were assessed using linear regression, each time determining the regression coefficient and testing its statistical significance. In addition, the 95% confidence interval of the simple regression was marked on the graphs, taking into account the statistical uncertainty of inference for the entire population.

Development of the chicken body and caeca

Feeding with experimental mixtures containing Activus wheat resulted in a lower, as compared to the C group, BW in chickens (Fig. 1A). This was particularly evident in birds of the W100 group (with the highest content of AW in feed) which had about 50% lower BW over the whole experiment. However, for the two remaining experimental groups (W75 and W50) these differences in BW diminished with chickens’ age. Animals of the W75 group had BW lower by 23% at day 35 and by only 12% at day 42. Correspondingly, BW of the W50 chickens differed by 16% at day 35 but at the end of the study no differences were observed between this group and control one. Not surprisingly, chickens of the W100 group had also a distinctly lower caecal weight (CW), observed as early as at day 14 of rearing (Fig. 1B). The linear coefficient of weight gain per day of life indicated much lower BW- and CW gains in chickens of the W100 group compared to the others. In contrast, CW in chickens of the groups W50 and W75 differed from that of control one at day 14 only. At the remaining sampling times, the caecal weights were similar in groups C, W50 and W75, with even higher caecal weights in the W50 group compared to the control one at days 35 and 42.

CW% values decreased in all chicken groups over the rearing period (Fig. 1C). This decrease was statistically significant in groups C and W100 with linear coefficients of −0.004 and − 0.003, respectively, for each day of the chick’s life. Interestingly, CW% was relatively high in the W100 group compared to others, especially at the last two sampling timepoints. Since this parameter physiologically decreases with the age of birds, high CW% with low BW in the W100 group (with normal health parameters) may indicate a slower biological development of chickens from this group. In the W75 group, the CW% was higher than in the C group at day 35, but no statistical differences were observed at day 42. For the W50 group, no statistical differences were detected compared to the control group at days 21, 35, and 42. This observation was confirmed by the measurements of caecal length, which was lower in W100 than in other chicken groups between day 14 and 35, but at day 42 no differences were observed among the groups (Table 1). However, no significant differences were found in caecal length compared to the control group in the W50 and W75 groups throughout the study. The CW/CL ratio increased with the chickens’ age and with the caecal development, with the fastest increase noted in the W50 group and the slowest in the W100 group (Fig. 1D).

Caecal SCFA concentration

Chromatographic analysis revealed total SCFA concentration at an average level of 106.7 mmol/kg of caecal content (Fig. 2A, Supplementary material Table S4). A positive correlation was found between the total SCFA concentration in the caecum and BW of chickens and between the total SCFA concentration and CW (Table 2). Calculated per 100 moles of total SCFA concentration (mol%), the most abundant SCFA appeared acetate (71.3 mol%), followed by butyrate (17.0 mol%), propionate (6.8 mol%), and others (4.1 mol% in total). The percentage of acetate decreased with age in all groups of chickens and was highest in group W100, which differed significantly from group C (Fig. 2B).The remaining groups did not differ significantly from the control group in this parameter. In contrast, propionate molar proportion increased along with the birds’ development, achieving the highest level in the C group (significantly different from the groups W100 and W75) (Fig. 2C). Significantly positive correlations were found between the molar proportion of propionate and BW of chickens and between the molar proportion of propionate and CW (Table 2). In the case of butyrate, no general trends in differences among groups were observed; the only statistical difference was found between group W75, in which the molar proportion was highest, and the control group (Fig. 2D). However, a positive correlation was found between butyrate (mol%) and CW of chickens (Table 2). No differences or statistical trends were noted for acids present in minor concentrations in the caecal content, i.e. isobutyrate, isovalerate, valerate or caproate (Table 1). Due to the lack of detectable amounts of isocaproate and heptanoate in most samples, no statistical analysis was performed for these SCFAs.

Microbiological examination

In the ileum of chickens of all age groups, the most abundant microbial population were lactic acid bacteria (Lactobacillus johnsonii and Ligilactobacillus salivarius, Table 3). However, numbers of these microorganisms fluctuated considerably in individuals within a given experimental group. The average numbers of lactic acid bacteria slightly increased with birds’ age, amounting to 107 CFUs at days 21 and 35. The two remaining groups of microorganisms studied, i.e., E. coli and Gram-positive cocci (the latter being represented mainly by Enterococcus gallinarum, E. faecium, E. hirae and Streptococcus alactolyticus), were detected at much lower numbers (102 − 103 of each per one gram of the ileal content) and their populations remained relatively stable throughout the experiment. For all the bacteria examined in the ileum, there were no significant differences between chickens of the control group and the experimental groups W50 through W100.

Gas production and methane emission

The production of gas during incubation was similar in each experimental group (94.2 mmol/kg of caecal sample on average), which may indicate similar intensity of microbial fermentation processes. Correspondingly, methanogenesis also showed no differences among groups and averaged 19.1 mmol/kg.

Discussion

Various feed products have been proposed for chickens as alternatives to SBM, such as rapeseed meal20, sunflower meal21, fly larvae meal22 and many other, mostly locally produced materials. Compared to SBM, these alternatives often have a significantly lower protein content and, particularly in plant-based products, higher fibre content23. Lower concentrations of digestible protein can be counterbalanced to some extent by the supplementation of crystalline AAs. However, the large amounts of fibre, composed mainly of non-starch polysaccharides (NSP), have been shown to decrease digestibility of many nutrients, thus making the energy yield significantly lower24. For these reasons, the total SBM replacement has often failed to achieve the expected results23.

In the present study, we examined the feasibility of using the Activus cultivar of wheat as the partial or total substitution for SBM in broiler chickens diet. The results of diet modification were assessed by the overall performance of the experimental birds, development of the caeca, detection of fermentation products (both in vivo and in vitro), as well as by the characterization of selected microbial populations. The increased crude protein content in the Activus wheat (as compared to other wheat cultivars), along with significantly low crude fibre concentration (2.67%), could make this cultivar a potential, large-scale alternative to SBM. However, the chickens fed mixtures in which SBM was completely replaced by AW had significantly less body weight compared to the control group in our study. This may have been attributed to some factors concerning wheat characteristics and the diet composition, such as fibre structure. For example, Sanchez et al.25 observed a lower, as compared with corn-based diet, final BW and BW gain of broilers fed a diet rich in wheat, which could be attributed to higher concentration of anti-nutritive soluble fibre in the latter feed. There are also some reports indicating that wheat-based diet, as containing high levels of soluble NSP present in wheat cell walls, increased viscosity of digesta and slowed down its passage in the GI tract, decreased activities of digestive enzymes and digestibility of nutrients, thus compromising broiler growth performance2,26. This effect can be counteracted by adding enzymes degrading NSP and reducing viscosity of digesta. Although such supplementation was accomplished in the present study, and an increased viscosity of digesta was not detected (unpublished data), such negative impact of fibre could not be excluded. Fibre fractions contained in Activus wheat used in this study could ensure the stability and development of caecal microbiota. NSP (8.59%), including arabinoxylans (5.67%), is the main substrate for fermentation in the poultry caecum, and such content appears to be sufficient to support microbial activity. The relatively low level of lignin (1.64%) seems to be also favourable for microbiota. However, the high proportion of insoluble fibre fractions (74% arabinoxylans and 78% NSP) might result in slower fermentation and slower colonization of the caeca by microbiota in young chickens, which was confirmed in our present study. The slower fermentation and slower production of SCFAs may have resulted in a slower growth of the chicks. Nevertheless, adverse effects should be reduced by the added xylanase. It is therefore possible that with such a high proportion of wheat in diets, the xylanase supplementation should be higher.

In addition to lower BW, chickens of the W100 group showed a weaker development of the caeca when compared to those fed SBM. The avian caeca play an important role in microbial fermentation of nutrients, mainly carbohydrates (to SCFAs), as well as in water balance, recycling of nitrogen, microbial synthesis of vitamins, absorption of minerals and gut immune homeostasis27,28. The development of the caeca in birds is genetically determined and depends largely on phylogeny and the nutritional preferences of a given species. In general, the caeca were evolutionally much better developed in herbivorous than in carnivorous birds and are relatively large in Galliformes and Anseriformes29. Moreover, granivorous species and those consuming large amounts of vegetal fibre were found to have the most developed caeca among 166 species of birds examined by Poppema30. Although the development of the cecum in birds is phylogenetically determined, there may be differences within the species due to dietary variation. Studies on wild and domestic avian species indicated that the caeca were highly adaptive to diet and may change their size under influence of different dietary components31. It seems that the type of fermentable carbohydrate, mainly dietary fibre, plays a key role here, but this relationship between caecal development and diet is not absolute in all birds32. A previous study on chickens indicated that both caecal length and weight were positively correlated with the increase in fibre level in diet, independently on fibre source24. It could partly explain less developed caeca in chickens receiving more AW in our experiment, as the mixtures intended for feeding birds from group W100 were characterised by a lower level of fibre per kg of the mixture in each feeding phase (the fibre content was lower by ca. 11%, 10% and 7.6% in the starter, grower and finisher mixtures, respectively; analysis according toAOAC11. The slower caecal development could be due to the weaker stimulation of the microbiota caused by the high proportion of insoluble fibre fractions, as mentioned above. Furthermore, the relatively low NDF levels in AW indicated a lower content of substrate for microbial activity, which might result in slower and less intense fermentation.

In addition, relative caecal weight as a percentage of body weight, illustrating the allometric growth of this organ, did not differ significantly among groups in our study, so the lower absolute weights and lengths of the cecum in group W100 were only due to the lower body weights of the birds in this group. However, this relative caecal weight in general decreased with age of birds in our study, but the mean values were highest in W100, which might reflect slower biological development of the chicken body. Interestingly, differences among groups in the caecal length no longer occurred at day 42 of chicken life, and in the groups C and W50 the caeca had similar lengths at days 35 and 42. This indicates that caecal development might be proper, but only slower in the groups receiving more AW. The length of the caecum in our 35-old and 42-old chickens was about 20 cm, which was in agreement with previous studies on modern commercial broilers29,33. The slower development of the caeca in group W100 was also evidenced by a slower increase in the cecum weight to length ratio with bird age. This could be explained by the fact that the insoluble fibre fractions in wheat delayed the colonization of the caecum and reduced the stimulation of microbiota development in young chicks, but after day 21 of age, when caeca were well developed, the microbiota was already stable and can ferment the insoluble fibre fractions, high-protein wheat can better replace soybean meal and no longer adversely affects the development of caeca of chickens, especially in W50 and W75 groups.

The development of the caeca is strictly linked to its colonization by species-specific microbiota. The microbial community of the GI tract has a vital significance in digestion and absorption of nutrients as well as for a proper function of the immune system, gut tissue development and maintaining good health. In chickens, the composition of bacterial communities varies greatly, depending on age, diet and environmental factors34. In the ileum, the most abundant group of bacteria appear to be members of the phylum Firmicutes, and in particular, Lactobacillus35,36. In comparison to the ileal bacterial community, the caecal microbiota becomes significantly more diverse and numerous; however their composition, detected by various authors, varied depending on study design, chicken’s diet and the method of identification. Firmicutes have also been often reported as the most predominant phylum36,37, although another phylum, the Bacteroidetes, may also take a large part in the caecal microbiota35 or even outnumber the former38.

Caecal microbiota in chickens play an important role in the digestion of structural carbohydrates and in recycling of nitrogen, involving production of essential AAs39. The fermentation processes of dietary fibre contribute to cover energy requirements for animal hosts, mainly via production of SCFAs as energy source. However, microbial populations in the GI tract can vary significantly in their metabolic activities. For example, acetic acid is reported to be the main metabolic end product of Bacteroidetes, the production of propionic acid is accomplished by both Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, while butyric acid is mainly produced by Firmicutes40,41. The microbial digestion and absorption of metabolites in the caeca are allowed by 12–20 h transit time of digesta, which is much longer than in upper GI tract segments (about 2.5 h)40. The fermentative activity of the caecal microbiota influences the development of caeca in birds, similarly as in case of other fermentation chambers in animals. Its metabolites – SCFAs – stimulate enterocyte division in the small and large intestines and are crucial to maintain the proper level of their proliferation8.

In our study, a group of well-cultivable, non-fastidious bacteria was selected to monitor the overall health status and physiological conditions in the ileum and caecum of the experimental chickens. Lactic acid bacteria (in the present work represented by Lactobacillus johnsonii and Ligilactobacillus salivarius), members of the Firmicutes, occur usually in large numbers in distal portions of the GI tract and their activity is essential for fermentation processes in the caecum. In turn, Escherichia coli is a well-established potential pathogen, responsible for various local and systemic infections in chickens of all age- and technological groups, referred to as colibacillosis42. An increased number of E. coli in the GI tract could be not only associated with a higher risk of the disease but also account for a heavier contamination of the chicken’s premises. Similarly, the genera Streptococcus and Enterococcus can also cause some health problems (e.g. peritonitis and pericarditis)42. Enterococci, as a stable component of the GI tract microbiota, may also serve as an additional marker of the functional status of the alimentary tract. Similarly, the quantitative assessment of the genus Streptococcus could be a predictor of chicken’s performance as this microorganism was shown to have a significant negative correlation with body weight35.

Quantitative analysis of the microbial population in the present study revealed a distinctly lower numbers of lactic acid bacteria in the 14-day-old chickens fed exclusively with wheat-based feed (W100). This impaired development of the microbiota responsible for fermentative processes could result in or be associated with the observed slowdown in the BW gain and CW in this experimental group. As the birds progressed in age (at days 21 and 35), the numbers of lactic acid bacteria increased to the values detected in the control group. For the two remaining groups of bacteria examined within the experiment, i.e., E. coli and Gram-positive cocci, no clear differences in cell numbers were found among the four chicken groups. This fact may confirm the overall good health status of all experimental birds and low risk of bacterial disturbances in the GI tract.

The development of the caecal microbiota, observed through our study, was thus correlated with the caecal development, and both the length and weight of the caecum showed a positive correlation with the total concentration of microbiota metabolites ‒ SCFAs. In the in vivo experiment, the most abundant SCFA in caecal digesta was acetate (71% on average), followed by butyrate (17%) and propionate (7%), which was in agreement with previous studies on broiler chickens43–46, and this general trend was not affected by dietary treatments in our study. While all of the main SCFAs are involved in energy metabolism, some of them may play important role in specific processes, for example acetate takes part in lipogenesis, cholesterogenesis and ketogenesis and propionate in gluconeogenesis, glycogenesis and the production of long-chain fatty acids47. The majority of butyrate is metabolized in colonocytes providing them energy for primary metabolism, as well as promoting their growth and functions48. All SCFAs, after being absorbed, are involved in regulations of many metabolic processes5.

In the present study, the SCFA profile changed during experiment, which was reflected in the calculated caecal indices, such as NGR and A: P, decreasing with bird age. The decline in these parameters was fastest in group C, which was probably related to the faster development of the microbiota in this group and confirms our observations about the slower caecal development of chickens receiving more wheat, caused probably by the composition of fibre in wheat. These decreases in NGR and A: P in our study resulted mainly from an increase in the molar proportion of propionate with chickens’ age, which confirmed observation of Liu et al.48 on broiler chickens. We found a positive correlation between caecum development parameters (weight and length) and molar proportion of propionate and butyrate, and negative one for acetate. The highest level of acetate and the lowest level of propionate and, thus, the slowest shift towards increasing propionate molar proportion (a process occurring normally with chicken age) were observed in the W100 group. This effect could be due to the high proportion of insoluble fibre fractions in wheat, which could lead to a shift in the fermentation profile towards acetate at the expense of propionate. Since propionate is an effective glucogenic SCFA, its growing concentration may contribute to increase in chicken body weight15.

The in vitro gut simulation models are considered to be valuable tools to evaluate an impact of feed components on the GI tract environment and microbial activity, so can be used for optimization of poultry’s feed formulations49. Used simultaneously with the in vivo method, they can have a beneficial contribution to research due to their repeatability and the elimination of individual physiological differences in animals. The general lack of significant changes in the SCFA profile (except for isovalerate) under the influence of the added substrates in the in vitro experiment, where an uniform inoculum from the caecal digesta of adult 42-day old chickens was used, seems to confirm the hypothesis that changes in the SCFA profile in the W100 group in our in vivo experiment resulted from the slower growth and development of the chicken body rather than from the influence of diets rich in wheat on the caecal microbiota. Isovalerate, similarly to other minor acids such as isobutyrate and valerate, originates from microbial processes of degradation of protein and AAs50, and achieves a concentration range of 0.3–1.6 mol% in the caeca of 42-day-old broilers51,52. Such molar proportions of isovalerate were exceeded in all our samples, which might indicate proper proteolytic activity of caecal microbiota, although they were lower in W3 samples compared to the control. Interestingly, the total SCFA concentrations were higher after in vitro incubation of samples with AW than SBM substrates. Since the same inocula were used for each kind of sample we may conclude that substrates with wheat created a more suitable environment for microbiota development, resulting in its larger activity.

In addition, methanogenic microbial activity was also observed in the caeca of chickens. Since reducing greenhouse gases emission is an important current trend in animal breeding, some attempts have been undertaken also in poultry production53. For this reason, studies on the influence of poultry feed ingredients and supplements on the caecal microbiota should also focus on methanogenic activity. According to Marounek and Rada54, methane production in chicken caeca increases up to 5 months of age, however, broiler chickens are usually slaughtered at 1–2 months of age. Saengkerdsub et al.55 detected methane production by chicken microbiota at the level of 11–59 mmol/kg of caecal content during 24 h incubation, depending on the feedstuff added. In our in vitro study we observed moderately intensive methanogenesis of 18–20 mmol/kg, without any significant differences irrespective of the kind of fermentation substrates. As methanogenic Archaea are very sensitive to environmental changes in GI tract, the lack of differences in methane production under influence of dietary modifications may be an indicator of the absence of negative impacts and the stability of microbiota.

Conclusions

The introduction of AW to the diet of broiler chickens as a replacement for SBM resulted in lower body weight compared to the control. However, the differences decreased with age, especially in the W50 and W75 groups. The slower growth of chickens receiving more AW also resulted in relatively lower morphometric parameters of the caeca, such as absolute caecal weight and length, but a higher caecal weight to body weight ratio (%). It is worth noting, however, that in groups W50 and W75 the differences concerned only CW at day 14. With the chicken’s age, an increase in the total concentration of SCFAs was observed as well as the shift in their profile towards an increasing molar proportion of propionate at the expense of acetate; however, these changes occurred slowest in the W100 group. The W50 group was most similar to group C with respect to microbiological metabolites. At day 14, we also noted differences between the W100 group and the others in the microbial population in relation to lactic acid bacteria and Gram-positive cocci, but these differences levelled out at later time points. However, no such differences were observed for the W50 and W75 groups compared to the control group. Taken together, the slower growth in W100 chickens fed AW accompanied by slower caecal development in relation to morphometry and microbial activity was most clearly noticeable at day 14, but these differences diminished with birds’ age. The cause might be related rather to some dietary factors, e.g. fibre composition in wheat, during the period of the most intensive chicken growth, than to the impact of AW on caecal microbiota. The lack of negative influence of AW on the metabolic activity of microbiota in the in vitro experiment using uniform caecal inocula from adult birds seems to confirm this assumption. Microbiological parameters in the W50 and W75 groups were similar to those in the C group. The results show that partial replacement of SBM with AW (up to 75%) in chicken nutrition does not have adverse effects, whereas full replacement may be associated with a slower body growth and microbial colonization rate. Based on the results obtained, it can be recommended to replace soybean meal with 50% high-protein wheat, preferably from the 21 st day of the chickens’ life. Further research could contribute to an improved balance of the chicken diet based on high-protein wheat.
