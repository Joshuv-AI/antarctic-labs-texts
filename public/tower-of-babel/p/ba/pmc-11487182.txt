Hierarchical exsolution in vertically aligned heterostructures
Authors: Javier Zamudio-García, Francesco Chiabrera, Armando Morin-Martínez, Ivano E. Castelli, Enrique R. Losilla, David Marrero-López, Vincenzo Esposito
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Metal nanoparticle exsolution from metal oxide hosts has recently garnered great attention to improve the performance of energy conversion and storage devices. In this study, the nickel exsolution mechanisms in a vertically aligned nanostructure (VAN) thin film of heteroepitaxial (Sr0.9Pr0.1)0.9Ti0.9Ni0.1O3−δ-Ce0.9Gd0.1O1.95 with a columnar architecture was investigated for the first time. Experimental results and Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations reveal that the multiple vertical interphases in a VAN with a hierarchical arrangement provide faster and more selective Ni diffusion pathways to the surface than traditional bulk diffusion in epitaxial films. Kinetic studies conducted at different temperatures and times indicate that the nucleation process of the exsolved metal nanoparticles primarily takes place at the surface through the phase boundaries of the columns. The vertical strain is crucial in preserving the film’s microstructure, yielding a robust heteroepitaxial architecture after reduction. This innovative heteromaterial opens up new possibilities for designing efficient devices through advanced structural engineering to achieve controlled nanoparticle formation.

Introduction

The exsolution of metal nanoparticles from a parent oxide support has emerged as a promising strategy to boost the electrochemical properties of materials for energy conversion and storage devices1–5. The formation of nanoparticle catalysts from a host lattice has demonstrated enhanced nanoparticle dispersion, smaller particle size and improved thermal and chemical stability compared to the traditional infiltration approach, attributed to the stronger metal-oxide support interaction6,7. In perovskite-type materials with A-site deficiency (A1-xBB′O3-δ), where B′ represents an easily reducible cation, an annealing treatment under reducing conditions leads to the homogeneous distribution of metal nanoparticles on the surface6,8–11. Exsolution is a partial decomposition of a parent perovskite structure governed by thermodynamics and kinetics12–14. Different nano-engineering strategies have been proposed to control and tailor the driving forces, thereby reducing the energy barriers for achieving highly dispersed metal nanoparticles.

Recently, significant efforts have been made to promote the exsolution of metal nanoparticles, including lattice strain control15, the impact of surface orientation16, oxygen release control17, charge-balanced doping18, phase transition19, and electrochemical poling20. However, partial degradation of the host lattice or preferential nanoparticle segregation within the bulk was observed after the exsolution process due to the sluggish diffusion of B-site cations15,16,21–24. In particular, the mass diffusion process that facilitates nanoparticle formation at the surface is hampered as exsolved particles can be trapped in the host perovskite oxide12,16. As a result, the complete exsolution of these particles to the surface may be limited. Weber et al. observed that space charge regions and surface chemistry of perovskite oxides play a critical role in the Ni exsolution kinetics and particle migration25. Several studies have also revealed that structural defects such as dislocations, grain boundaries and anti-phase boundaries are preferable nucleation sites, providing favourable cation diffusion pathways to the host surface8,26,27. However, most of the research efforts have been focused on investigating the exsolution process in bulk materials and thin films. At the same time, functional oxides with advanced architectures have not been explored yet.

Heteroepitaxial vertically aligned nanostructures (VAN) are formed through spontaneous ordering of two immiscible phases, offering promising properties that can be applied in multiple energy applications28–30. VAN films have rendered excellent results as magnetic materials, ferroelectrics, ionic conductors or fuel cells due to their unique microstructure that enables both in-plane and vertical strain engineering31–35. Compared to conventional thin films, the large vertical interface area in VAN films could further trigger the exsolution process by facilitating multiple pathways for cation mobility. This unique feature is expected to enhance the exsolution kinetics and overcome the bottleneck of nanoparticle migration to the surface. The exsolution in heteroepitaxial films is particularly relevant for designing oxide-based devices with synergetic functionalities, such as fast ionic-electronic conductivity and electrocatalytic properties. Additionally, the improved transport properties demonstrated by VAN films12,16,36–38 could avoid employing high reducing temperatures (≥800 °C) to achieve metal nanoparticle exsolution, which could induce partial degradation under long-term operation.

In this work, we report the first evidence of an enhanced exsolution process in a VAN film at intermediate temperatures. The growth process leads to self-assembled hierarchical nanocomposite materials suitable for energy applications. As a benchmark for the concept, we propose electrochemically active (Sr0.9Pr0.1)0.9Ti0.9Ni0.1O3−δ-Ce0.9Gd0.1O1.95 (SPTNO-CGO) VAN films. Pr-doping in A-site was implemented for better electrochemical performance, which is attributed to the Pr4+/Pr3+ redox couple39,40. Additionally, a A-site cation deficiency of 10 mol.% was employed to facilitate the exsolution of Ni nanoparticles. The VAN films were grown using pulsed laser deposition (PLD) on SrTiO3 (STO) and (LaAlO3)0.3(Sr2TaAlO6)0.7 (LSAT) (001) single crystals to obtain a highly coherent structure. The epitaxy and structural coherency reveal the atomic diffusive mechanisms in the self-assembling process.

Results

Microstructural and structural characterisation of VAN films

The microstructure ordering, film thickness and long-range columnar architecture of the SPTNO-CGO VAN film deposited onto STO (001) were thoroughly investigated using HAADF-STEM and HRTEM on a thin cross-sectional lamella (Fig. 1). The HAADF-STEM image at lower magnification reveals a homogeneous film thickness of approximately 200 nm. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and HRTEM analyses were performed to elucidate the composition and local structure of the columns. The EDS colour maps of Sr and Ti (in red) and Ce (in green) cations evidence a modulation pattern closely matching the HAADF-STEM contrast (Figs. 1b and S1, Supplementary information), indicating a phase separation between the SPTNO and CGO parent compounds through the whole film thickness. High-magnification HRTEM images provide further confirmation of the phase separation within the film, where the formation of homogeneously distributed 5 nm width crystalline nanocolumns of fluorite CGO and perovskite SPTNO phases was observed (Fig. S2). These results confirm the spontaneous ordering of both phases on the STO (001) substrate. Regarding VAN growth, various microstructures have been reported in the literature, including nanopillars-in-matrix or nano-checkerboard, as described by Sun et al. and Chen et al.28,41. The SPTNO-CGO VAN (50 wt.%) studied in this work exhibits a long-range column width of approximately 5 nm for both SPTNO and CGO components. These findings suggest the formation of a nano-checkerboard structure rather than a columnar enclosure of one phase surrounded by the parent compound, which would likely require a considerably higher weight percentage of the parent compound or exhibit significantly different column width for each phase, contrary to what we observed in this work. The morphology of the as-deposited samples was investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. S3) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Fig. S4a), confirming the dense microstructure at a roughness level of ∼1 nm.

The VAN thin films were reduced under 5% H2–Ar at 650 °C to study the exsolution of Ni nanoparticles. As shown in Fig. 1c, the reduction treatment successfully promoted nanoparticle formation on the surface of the layer. The EDS analysis not only confirmed the composition of the Ni nanoparticles located on the surface of the VAN but also revealed the absence of any secondary phases resulting from Ni exsolution from the lattice (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, the homogeneous cation distribution in the film was verified by the Ti and Ce EDS mappings (Fig. S5). This analysis depicts the chemical and morphological effect of the exsolution process on the columnar microstructure, providing a comparison to the pristine state (Fig. 1a). Notably, the long-range columnar ordering remained unaltered after the reduction process and the subsequent cation migration to the surface. Although the long-term annealing may promote a certain degree of cationic interdiffusion, the VAN thin film maintains a clear separation between CGO and SPTNO columns.

Interestingly, the VAN film remained fully dense, showing no visible porosity or degradation after the Ni-exsolution process. In contrast, previous reports on La0.2Sr0.7Ti0.9Ni0.1O3−δ films deposited onto STO (001) have shown that a significant amount of Ni nanoparticles were trapped within the host perovskite oxide, even at reduction temperatures as high as 800 °C for long times15,16. This phenomenon also led to partial film degradation. Similar findings were found after Ni exsolution from other perovskite-based materials deposited by PLD onto STO (001), such as La0.2Ba0.7Sn0.9Ni0.1O3−δ37 or BaZr0.9Y0.1O3−δ with 1 wt.% Ni42.

Recently, Weber et al. observed the spontaneous formation of embedded NiOx within the host oxide of the as-deposited material12, using a stoichiometric target of SrTi0.9Nb0.05Ni0.05O3 and controlling the film properties by varying the deposition conditions. However, our study employed a different perovskite composition, (Sr0.9Pr0.1)0.9Ti0.9Ni0.1O3−δ, featuring A-site deficiency, similar to other perovskites reported in the literature8,15,16, which has been shown not to induce phase separation between NiOx and the host perovskite oxide in as-deposited material.

Figure 1e shows a schematic representation of the heteroepitaxial growth of the VAN. The in-plain lattice matching relationships SPTNO (001) || CGO (110) || STO (001) and out-of-plane SPTNO (001) || CGO (001) || STO (001) were obtained, consistent with previous findings on the STO-Sm0.2Ce0.8O1.9 VAN deposited onto STO (001)31. Interestingly, it was observed that the CGO fluorite phase undergoes a 45° in-plane rotation to minimise the lattice mismatch with STO (001) substrate31,43,44. The d-spacing obtained for both phases confirmed the out-of-plane coherent relationship (Fig. 1f, g). Although thick epitaxial films usually suffer from structure relaxation45, the nanoscale contact between vertically aligned columns of two immiscible phases enables vertical interfacial strain, thereby forming a well-ordered heterostructure28,30,31,45.

Extensive X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis supports the long-range crystalline order and structure observed by TEM analysis before and after reduction (see Figs. S6 and 7). The θ−2θ XRD scans of the as-deposited VAN layer deposited on STO (001) reveal a series of peaks corresponding to a preferential (00l) orientation of both the CGO and SPTNO phases, along with a minor SPTNO (111) orientation (Fig. S6). Similar results were obtained for the single SPTNO films on STO (001) (Fig. S6a, c) and the SPTNO-CGO VAN film deposited on LSAT (001) substrates (Fig. S7). In the case of the as-reduced SPTNO, the out-of-plane lattice parameter slightly decreased from 3.962 Å for the as-deposited film to 3.930 Å after reduction. This behaviour is similar to those previously observed for other Ni-doped SrTiO3-based epitaxial films8,10,16. Although the partial reduction of Ti4+ to Ti3+ induces a lattice cell expansion, the concomitant exsolution of NiO from the lattice to form Ni0 nanoparticles on the electrode surface results in a larger cell contraction.

On the other hand, the SPTNO-CGO VAN showed a minor decrease in the out-of-plane lattice parameter for the SPTNO phase after reduction, from 3.988 to 3.981 Å, while the out-of-plane lattice spacing of the CGO phase remained practically unchanged. This behaviour is presumably attributed to the unique microstructure of the VAN film, where both in-plane and vertical strain are present due to the coexistence of columns composed of two different materials, hindering significant changes in the cell volume. Notably, both films retained crystallinity after chemical reduction without appreciable crystallographic misorientation. A minor reflection is observed at around 2θ = 44° in both the as-prepared and reduced sample (Fig. S6d). This effect may be attributed to a minor, unidentified impurity formed during self-assembling. It is important to note that the intensity of this impurity is three orders of magnitude lower than the SPTNO peak, as shown in Fig. S6d, with a logarithmic y-axis scale confirming its minor presence. Under reducing conditions, this small reflection shifts slightly to higher angles and does not change in intensity, much like the strained SPTNO. This result suggests that the impurity may be a perovskite-type phase similar to SPTNO but with a slightly larger lattice parameter.

The in-plain conductivity of the SPTNO and SPTNO-CGO VAN films, deposited onto LSAT (001), was determined using the four-probe Van der Pauw method under both air and 5% H2-Ar atmospheres on cooling (Fig. 1h). SPTNO exhibited conductivity values in air and 5% H2-Ar of 0.077 and 2.87 S cm−1, respectively, at 650 °C. The higher conductivity values in reducing conditions are attributed to an increase in the charge carrier concentration due to the partial reduction of Ti4+ to Ti3+. These conductivity values are higher than those observed for dense (Sr0.7Pr0.3)0.95TiO3±δ polycrystalline pellets at the same temperature in air (5·10−5 S cm−1) and 5% H2-Ar (0.84 S cm−1), as well as other films such as SrTi0.9Fe0.1O3-δ (∼10−5 S cm−1) or SrTi0.996Ni0.004O3-δ (∼10−6 S cm−1) deposited by PLD49,50. The conductivity in air of the VAN is slightly higher than the blank SPTNO film, ∼0.27 S cm−1 at 650 °C, despite the lower ionic conductivity of the CGO bulk (0.02 S cm−1 at 650 °C). In reducing conditions, the conductivity of the VAN slightly decreases at high temperatures to 0.1 S cm−1 at 650 °C. Such a decrease in conductivity is expected due to the large number of in-plane interfaces in the VAN structure35. It is worth noting that the SPTNO-CGO VAN exhibits very fast reduction kinetics, reaching stable conductivity values within 30 min after feeding with 5% H2-Ar. The limited increase in conductivity for VANs under reducing conditions can be attributed to the particular nickel exsolution process, as the conductivity of the SPTNO and CGO typically increases under such conditions. Microstructural characterisation of the samples was carried out after the electrochemical measurements to check the stability of the films after successive oxidation/reduction cycles (Fig. S11). In the particular case of the SPTNO film, the particle size is larger, and the population density is lower when compared to the VAN, as will be elaborated in the subsequent sections.

Additionally, small cracks and degradation of the sample surface can be observed for the SPTNO film. In contrast, the VAN film exhibited no signs of degradation. Some particles were surrounded by the secondary phase, previously attributed to Ni ions7,51,52, given that the cooling down was conducted in a reducing atmosphere.

Surface Ni migration and coalescence

A kinetic analysis was also conducted to gain further insight into the nanoparticle nucleation mechanism. The Ni nanoparticle size in the VAN decreases as the exsolution temperature decreases, leading to a higher particle density (Fig. 2a–c). Interestingly, the sample reduced at 600 °C for displays rounded nickel nanoparticles and a rectangular shape exsolved positioned 90° apart, indicating a preferred nucleation orientation (Fig. 2b). The VAN was reduced at 650 °C for only 1 min and then cooled down fast to room temperature (Q, quenching) to obtain information about the initial stages of the exsolution process, which occur before particle nucleation. This sample showed small Ni-nanoparticle size of ∼17 nm and a high population density (∼80 particles µm−2) (Fig. 2d). The proximity of the Ni nanoparticles facilitates an initial fast particle growth, which is subsequently reduced at later stages. Yet, EDS analysis reveals that some Ni is still migrating towards the surface while the long-range columnar ordering of the VAN is maintained (Fig. S5). This behaviour can be attributed to the short reduction time of just 1 min, followed by rapid cooling.

After reducing the VAN at 650 °C for 1 h, the population density of Ni nanoparticles decreased significantly to 5 particles per µm−2. In contrast, their particle size increased to ∼46 nm (Fig. 2e). This increase in particle size can be attributed to Ni migration and coalescence. In addition, the nanoparticles show an irregular shape, surrounded by a secondary phase, most likely attributed to Ni ions (diffusion species) merging to form Ni particles, as reported previously8,15,16. Similar particle shapes have been observed in other reports on Ni-exsolved nanoparticles in thin films, such as La0.8Ce0.1Ni0.4Ti0.6O3−δ7 and La0.2Sr0.7Ti0.9Ni0.1O3−δ53 as well as Fe–Co bimetallic exsolution in La0.3Sr0.7Cr0.3Fe0.6Co0.1O3−δ51. EDS analysis was performed to study this intermediate stage in the nucleation process (Fig. S12). The EDS mappings suggest that the secondary phase may be composed of Ni atoms, and no other elements have been detected.

When the reducing treatment was extended to 6 h, nanoparticles with a cubic shape were discernible, suggesting that the intermediate material is nucleating to form the Ni nanoparticles with an average size of ∼65 nm (Fig. 2c). After reduction for 12 h, the average particle size remained similar ∼67 nm, but the particles exhibited a faceted shape. Interestingly, there is no presence of the intermediate phase, indicating that the nucleation process has been completed at this temperature (Fig. 2f). Low magnification SEM images for samples treated at different reducing times demonstrated the homogenous distribution of Ni nanoparticles on the surface (Figs. 2g and S13). Figure 2h, i shows the population density and particle size distribution as a function of the reduction time and temperature.

The surface chemistry of the as-prepared and reduced SPTNO-CGO VAN film was examined using XPS. The spectra of O, Sr, Ti, Ni and Ce are provided in Fig. S14. After the annealing in 5% H2-Ar at 650 °C for 12 h, a partial reduction of the oxidation states of Ti (2p), Ni (2p) and Ce (3d) is discernible. The Ti 2p3/2 core level in the as-deposited sample only shows a band at 457.9 eV, characteristic of Ti4+54. However, a minor band attributed to Ti3+ at lower binding energy is discernible after reduction. The Ni 2p region shows a band centred at 854.8 eV, corresponding to the presence of Ni2+ in the perovskite lattice55. After the reduction treatment, a significant portion of Ni2+ is reduced to Ni0 (852.4 eV), confirming the presence of metallic Ni on the surface. The Ce 3d core level shows Ce 3d5/2 and Ce 3d3/2 splitting characteristics. The Ce4+ state is the main valence state in the as-deposited film. A certain amount of Ce3+ is also present, findings that were previously associated with forming oxygen vacancies in CeO2-doped compounds56,57. Furthermore, thermal annealing further promotes the reduction of Ce4+ to Ce3+.

Figure 3a, b compares low-magnification SEM images of the surface of an SPTNO thin film and a VAN layer after the reduction treatment at 650 °C for 12 h. As can be observed, Ni nanoparticle size, shape and population density are considerably different in both samples. The average Ni nanoparticle size in the SPTNO epitaxial film is approximately 120 nm, significantly larger than that of the VAN ~ 67 nm. In addition, the population density of Ni nanoparticles in the VAN is higher than that in the SPTNO, which is desirable for energy applications due to the increased number of active sites for the electrochemical reactions, potentially leading to improved performance. The SPTNO parent compound was reduced at different temperatures and times for further comparison with the VAN (Fig. S15). After a reduction at 550 °C for 6 h (Fig. S15b), nanoparticle exsolution in the SPTNO is not homogenous, likely due to sluggish Ni migration at lower temperatures and shorter times. This result contrasts with the more uniform exsolution observed in the VAN, as shown in Fig. 2a and discussed earlier. Additionally, the SPTNO sample reduced at 650 °C for 1 min exhibits a particle density of approximately of 33 µm−2, which is 2.5 times lower than the density observed in the VAN under the same conditions (∼80 particles µm−2), with a slightly higher particle size. It is worth mentioning that the particle size obtained in this work is relatively larger when compared to other reports in the literature. However, several factors, such as preparation conditions, film orientation and lattice strain, have been found to play a significant role in determining particle size after the exsolution process. For instance, Kim et al.16 observed considerably higher particle size (40 ± 17 nm) for (001) oriented films compared to (111) films (20 ± 7 nm). Han et al.15 demonstrated that compressive-strained films lead to the formation of considerably smaller nanoparticles compared to tensile-strained films. These findings help to explain the slightly larger particle size obtained in this work. Additionally, related compositions in the literature, such as (Sr0.7Pr0.3)0.9Ti0.9Ni0.1O3-δ have shown similar particle sizes of 50-100 nm58,59.

EDS characterisation of the sample surface confirmed the formation of Ni nanoparticles exsolved from both the epitaxial SPTNO (Fig. S16) and VAN (Fig. S17). Furthermore, AFM characterisation was performed on both samples before and after reduction, revealing a dense microstructure with comparable roughness below 1 nm (Fig. S4). After reduction, Ni nanoparticles were discernible on the surface, showing the SPTNO-CGO VAN sample a higher nanoparticle density and smaller size, as observed by SEM.

The shape of the Ni nanoparticles is also different for the two samples. The nanoparticles in the SPTNO epitaxial film show a hemispherical shape, indicating signs of isotropic growth. Conversely, the VAN layer exhibited faceted square-shaped nanoparticles, suggesting anisotropic growth. The HRTEM image of the Ni nanoparticle shows a d-spacing of 3.6 Å, consistent with the expected values for the Ni-metal (Fig. S18). This finding indicates that the microstructure of the VAN film may promote the formation of oriented Ni nanoparticles in the (001) direction, as observed in Fig. S18. Typically, weak particle-support interactions results in the formation of spherical shapes with a lower surface-area-to-volume ratio to decrease surface energy, similar to traditional nanoparticles prepared by infiltration techniques60. On the contrary, when there is a strong interaction between the metal and the oxide support, the resulting nanoparticle shape tends to be hemispherical or faceted, as observed for SPTNO film and VAN, respectively.

Cross-sectional HAADF-STEM of the epitaxial SPTNO film revealed the presence of segregated Ni nanoparticles within the bulk film (Figs. 3c, d and S19). This phenomenon can be attributed to the hindered bulk mass diffusion, resulting in what can be described as “trapped” nanoparticles within the host perovskite oxide. Many studies have consistently led to similar observations of Ni nanoparticles trapped within the host perovskite in conventional films8,15,16,61. Contrary to what is observed in traditional films, in this work and in the literature, the VAN exhibits a clean columnar microstructure with no visible Ni particles trapped within the film (Fig. 3e, f), as confirmed by HAADF-STEM and EDS analysis. These findings confirm the enhanced Ni diffusion kinetics to the surface in the VAN system. These findings provide evidence that the nanoparticle nucleation in the VAN predominantly occurs at the surface. The experimental results confirm a different nucleation process for Ni nanoparticles in a VAN compared to traditional epitaxial film, where the nanoparticles on the surface preferably nucleate from small nanoparticles emerging from the bulk film15,16,62 (Fig. 3g, h). The proposed mechanism for the initial Ni diffusion and particle growth in the VAN is illustrated in Fig. 3h. However, the final particle size is larger due to the coalescence process. This hypothesis aligns with the particle size being significantly larger than the column width; thus, all the Ni does not come from the volume beneath. Since random directions are not observed during the nanoparticle growth process, it suggests that the superficial phase boundaries between the nanocolumns serve as the preferred diffusion pathways for particle-particle nucleation in the VAN.

Different mechanisms for nanoparticle growth have been proposed in the literature, including particle migration and coalescence, as well as Ostwald ripening63. Particle migration involves the Brownian motion of nanoparticles, which can lead to coalescence when they come into close proximity. In contrast, Ostwald ripening involves the growth of larger nanoparticles by incorporating material from smaller neighbouring particles. During this process, the mobile species migrate through the oxide support from the smaller particles, eventually merging into the larger particles51,64.

While both mechanisms can occur simultaneously, the proximity between Ni nanoparticles during the initial exsolution stages in the VAN, may favour Ostwald ripening, followed by particle migration at later stages. Nevertheless, in situ investigations are crucial for studying the complex particle growth mechanisms of exsolved nanoparticles in oxide supports, as these processes indeed occur simultaneously.

Weber et al. recently observed that surface defects can play a significant role in particle migration and coarsening25,65. The presence of oxygen vacancies and defects on the surface of the VAN, particularly at the phase boundaries, can create a positive space charge region that promotes particle migration during the early stages of the exsolution process. Additionally, grain-boundary interfaces and defects have been identified as preferred sites for nanoparticle nucleation66. However, it is essential to note that the short reduction times investigated in this work (650 °C for 1 min) are less commonly studied in the literature, so the very initial stages of the particle growth are typically not explored. At longer reduction times (650 °C for 1 h), particle growth is significantly reduced, with particle size similar to those after 12 h.

Further insights into the influence of vertical interfaces on Ni migration were investigated through DFT calculations (Figs. 4 and S20). In the case of bulk diffusion, which represents the conventional mechanism for Ni migration, the energy barrier is 3.68 eV. On the other hand, the energy barrier for the Ni migration across the interface is significantly reduced to 1.40 eV. This finding aligns with reports on improved cation migration through grain boundaries in different materials42,66,67 and strongly support the hypothesis that the columnar architecture of the VAN promotes faster channels for Ni migration, as observed in the experimental results. The high density of large vertical interfaces in the nanoengineered structure of the VAN, featuring nanocolumns of approximately 5 nm, provides multiple coherent pathways for cation diffusion. This architecture effectively mitigates the bottleneck of Ni migration to the surface, as confirmed by HAADF-STEM/EDS analysis. In contrast, the conventional film presents a high energy barrier for Ni transport, resulting in sluggish exsolution kinetics and the trapping of Ni nanoparticles within the host oxide.

The redox stability of the Ni nanoparticles was investigated after calcining the VAN in air atmosphere at 600 °C during different annealing times (Fig. S21). The Ni-exsolved nanoparticles remain stable after thermal treatment in air at 600 °C for 1 h. They are not redissolved into the SPTNO perovskite lattice within the VAN; however, their morphology changes to flat crystals due to partial oxidation to NiO51,52. A prolonged oxidation treatment in air at 600 °C for 6 h resulted in a flat material close to the Ni nanoparticles. Hence, this intermediate phase is attributed to the formation of NiO, as previously observed for other metal nanoparticles after oxidation in air7,51,52. Subsequently, a second reduction treatment was performed at 650 °C for 1 min, resulting in additional Ni nanoparticles with smaller grain sizes (∼5 nm, indicated with orange arrows). Similar findings were previously observed by Lai et al.51, who demonstrated that metal oxide particles with irregular shapes, formed under oxidation conditions, can disintegrate into several smaller nanoparticles when subjected to reducing conditions. Weber et al. also observed minimal re-dissolution of Ni nanoparticles into the perovskite host lattice under oxidising conditions in epitaxial films68. The fact that NiO is not reincorporated into the VAN suggests that the heteroepitaxial architecture, characterised by a limited column width, hinders this process. As a result, stable Ni nanoparticles are obtained, which is desirable for reversible energy conversion devices. Furthermore, a low-magnification SEM image confirms the homogeneous distribution of Ni nanoparticles on the film surface without any signs of Ni agglomeration (Fig. S22).

The microstructural and redox stability of the Ni-exsolution in the VAN can be attributed to several factors. In conventional epitaxial films, the exsolution process often induces a significant cell contraction, negatively affecting the film’s integrity. Since bulk diffusion is dominant, the absence of preferential diffusion pathways towards the sample surface hinders the cation mobility, thus promoting the formation of Ni metal clusters inside the film. However, in the case of the VAN, CGO columns induce vertical strain in the SPTNO phase, minimising cell contraction and avoiding large structural changes in the film. This factor contributes to maintaining the columnar microstructure and prevents film degradation.

Additionally, the tensile-strained SPTNO plays a crucial role in selectively promoting nickel exsolution, facilitating efficient diffusion pathways to the surface through phase boundaries of the CGO columns. Indeed, the high concentration of vertical interfaces in the VAN enhances the exsolution of metal nanoparticles by providing faster diffusion pathways for NiO towards the surface, subsequently undergoing reduction to Ni-metal: Ni2+ + 2e− →Ni0. Moreover, the high density of diffusion pathways, induced by the narrow column width of 5 nm, likely impedes the formation of clusters within the VAN film. These findings demonstrate the significant influence of film architecture on diffusion and nucleation processes, providing the means to control and tailor the nanoparticle size and population. However, it is worth noting that the unique microstructural architecture of VAN, characterised by vertical columns, does not seem to favour the socketing of these nanoparticles. This phenomenon can be attributed to the heterostructure architecture of the VAN, in contrast to the conventional single-phase films. Future research that systematically varies the density of interfaces in the VAN could provide valuable insights into how to tailor the density and size of nanoparticles precisely.

Discussion

This work provides an alternative approach to promote efficient nanoparticle exsolution from self-assembled functional oxides at intermediate temperatures. DFT calculations and experimental data have revealed that the unique architecture of the vertically aligned nanocomposites, characterized by numerous vertical interphases, provides favourable diffusion paths to the sample surface. This result contrasts with traditional epitaxial films, where bulk diffusion prevails. Vertical strain also plays a critical role in maintaining the microstructure integrity of the film during the exsolution process, preserving the columnar microstructure of the heteroepitaxial film and offering ideal support for nanoparticle formation with improved redox stability. Moreover, the exsolved nanoparticles exhibit good stability after oxidation in air and do not redissolve into the film, which is crucial for energy applications to prevent performance degradation. This transformative advance in tailoring the exsolution process of metal nanoparticles in vertically aligned nanocomposites opens the possibility to explore new directions in nanomaterials research. It enables precise control over the population, size and stability of exsolved nanoparticles at intermediate temperatures, potentially facilitating their application in solid oxide fuel cells, electrolysers, hydrocarbon reforming and heterogeneous catalysis.

Methods

Target and film preparation

(Sr0.9Pr0.1)0.9Ti0.9Ni0.1O3−δ (SPTNO) and (Sr0.9Pr0.1)0.9Ti0.9Ni0.1O3−δ-Ce0.9Gd0.1O1.95 (SPTNO-CGO) VAN films were deposited onto SrTiO3 (STO) and (LaAlO3)0.3(Sr2TaAlO6)0.7 (LSAT) (001) single crystals (Crystec GmbH) at 650 °C using a large-area PLD chamber (Surface PLD). During the deposition process, the oxygen pressure was maintained at 0.0066 mbar with a target-substrate distance of 70 mm. The target was ablated by a 248 nm KrF excimer laser (Coherent Lambda Physik GmbH) with a laser fluence of 1.2 J cm−2 and a laser repetition rate of 10 Hz.

The SPTNO target was prepared by the solid-state reaction method from the corresponding oxides or carbonates, SrCO3, Pr6O11, TiO2 and NiO (Merck, purity >99.5%), which were previously calcined separately at 1100 °C for 1 h. All the powders were mixed in an agate mortar for 15 min and then calcined at 1200 °C for 1 h. After that, the powders were grounded in a planetary ball mill (Pulverisette 7) in absolute ethanol media. The resulting dry powders were uniaxially pressed into disks of 25 cm in diameter and sintered at 1400 °C for 5 h with a heating and cooling ramp of 5 °C/min. A similar procedure was used for the preparation of SPTNO-CGO target (50 wt.%), where the Ce0.9Gd0.1O1.95 commercial powders (Rhodia) were mixed with the SPTNO powders (previously calcined at 1200 °C). In both cases, the relative density of the targets was higher than 98%.

Structural and microstructural characterisation

The films were characterised by XRD performing θ−2θ and RSM in a Rigaku Smartlab diffractometer with a rotating Cu anode. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), High-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) imaging and EDS were performed in an FEI Talos F200X, both before and after the reduction treatments. SEM images and the electron transparent lamellas for TEM analysis were obtained using an FEI Helios Nanoslab 650, equipped with a focused ion beam. For the preparation of the lamella, Ir was sputtered at room temperature to provide high electronic conductivity to the surface (Fig. S23). The particle size and distribution were calculated from different areas of the sample surface using ImageJ software. AFM characterisation was performed in a Dimension Icon from Bruker. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out on a Physical Electronics PHI-5700 using an Mg Kα source (1253.6 eV). Each spectral region was scanned several times to obtain a good signal-to-noise ratio. A Multipak-V9.3 software package was used for data analysis. Binding energies were determined for the position of the adventitious C 1s peak at 285.0 eV (accurate ± 0.1 eV). Ni 2p core level was selected instead of 3p due to the significant overlapping of Ti4+ 3s with Ni0 3p in the binding energy range 62–67 eV. A Shirley-type background was subtracted from the signals, and the recorded spectra were consistently fitted using Gaussian−Lorentzian curves to determine the binding energy of the different element core levels more accurately. For data comparison, we maintained fixed values for the Full Width at Half Maximum and the spacing between peak splitting for both as-deposited and reduced samples.

Electrical characterisation

In-plane conductivity of the different films in air and 5% H2-Ar, ranging from room temperature and 650 °C upon cooling, was determined using the four-probe Van der Paw method. Gold ink and Au wires were used for the electrical connections to the electrochemical setup. Conductivity measurements were carried out in two cycles of Air/5% H2-Ar to confirm the reproducibility of the data and redox stability of the samples. The cooling down was performed in a reducing atmosphere. The current and voltage in the sample were measured using a Keithley 2700 multimeter, and a Yokogawa 7651 source was employed to apply the voltage.

DFT calculations

Our calculations utilised the DFT framework implemented in VASP (Vienna ab initio simulation package). Exchange-correlation effects were addressed using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional revised for solids (PBE-sol) and projector-augmented waves based atomic pseudopotentials. We applied a 600 eV energy cutoff to ensure the convergence of plane waves. The energy convergence criterion between consecutive images was set to 10−6 eV, and the interatomic force criterion was adjusted so that the norm of all forces remained below 0.05 eV/Å. Structural optimisations were performed using the conjugate gradient method, with Gaussian smearing (0.05 eV width) for partial occupancies of electronic states. Hubbard U corrections of 4.7 eV and 5.8 eV were also applied for Ti and Ni d orbitals, respectively, along with a 4.5 eV f orbital correction for Ce.

Regarding the bulk structure, Ni mobilisation required an oxygen vacancy, a B-site cation vacancy, and a B-site cation substitution. The bulk structure, composed of 78 atoms arranged in a (4, 4, 2) supercell of SrTiO3, exhibited periodicity in all dimensions. Brillouin zone sampling was conducted using a Gamma-centered mesh of (2 × 4 × 4) k-points. In the interface structure, an additional defect, an oxygen vacancy in the ceria slab near the Ni, was introduced compared to the bulk. The interface, periodic only on the (110) plane, consisted of 150 atoms arranged in a (4, 4, 2) SrTiO3 supercell paired with a CeO2 (4, 2, 3) supercell rotated at 45°. Reciprocal space was sampled using a (2 × 4 × 1) k-points mesh. Structural relaxation occurred separately initially, followed by matching them together and allowing further relaxation of positions. Migration barriers were evaluated using the Nudged Elastic Band method, employing a maximum force criterion of 0.05 eV/Å with three internal images for convergence.
