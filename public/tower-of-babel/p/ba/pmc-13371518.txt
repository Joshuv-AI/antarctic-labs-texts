Interferon-induced protein 44-like promoter Hypomethylation as an epigenetic hallmark of systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Xiaohui Zhang, Yong Fan, Lanlan Ji, Dai Gao, Yan Geng, Zhuoli Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) lacks sensitive biomarkers for early detection and longitudinal monitoring. Despite the diagnostic potential of IFI44L promoter hypomethylation, its dynamics across the clinical spectrum, ranging from pre-clinical stages to treatment response, remain poorly characterized. This study investigated IFI44L methylation across the SLE continuum to evaluate its performance in early diagnosis, disease activity assessment, and monitoring therapeutic response.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study incorporating a longitudinal component, IFI44L promoter methylation was quantified by methylation-sensitive high-resolution melting analysis in 566 participants: healthy controls (HC, n = 106), first-degree relatives of SLE patients (at-risk, n = 27), incomplete lupus erythematosus (ILE, n = 68), classified SLE (active, n = 124; stable, n = 62), and an independent validation cohort of patients with other connective tissue diseases (CTDs, n = 179). Differences between groups were analyzed using non-parametric tests. ROC curves, Spearman’s correlation, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were employed to assess diagnostic performance, clinical associations, and treatment response, respectively.ResultsIFI44L promoter methylation exhibited a stepwise decline across the SLE continuum, with the highest levels in HCs and the lowest in active SLE. Hypomethylation (defined as <0.25) was present in 18.5% of at-risk individuals, 47.1% of ILE, and 90.5% of treatment-naïve SLE patients. It effectively distinguished SLE from HCs (AUC 0.892; sensitivity 78.5%, specificity 100%) and other CTDs (AUC 0.856; sensitivity 78.5%, specificity 92.7%), outperforming anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm antibodies in ILE. Among classified SLE patients, IFI44L promoter methylation levels were significantly lower in active versus stable SLE (p < 0.001). Lower methylation correlated with specific organ involvement (mucocutaneous, serositis, vasculitis, severe nephritis), high disease activity (SLEDAI-2K: r = −0.406, p < 0.001), and specific autoantibody profiles. Longitudinal analyses demonstrated significant increases in IFI44L methylation following induction therapy (p = 0.018).ConclusionsHypomethylation of the IFI44L promoter represents a dynamic, disease-specific epigenetic alteration across the SLE continuum. It demonstrates strong diagnostic accuracy for both definitive SLE and incomplete disease, correlates with disease activity, and is modifiable with treatment. These findings support its potential as a versatile biomarker to enhance early detection and monitoring.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08282-z.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disease characterized by heterogeneous clinical manifestations and an unpredictable course [1]. The global prevalence of SLE is estimated to be 43.7 (15.87 to 108.92) per 100,000 persons [2], with nearly 1 million cases reported in China alone [3]. Despite advances in diagnosis and management, significant challenges remain in early detection, monitoring disease activity, and predicting relapse using currently available biomarkers. These unmet clinical needs necessitate the development of sensitive biomarkers that reflect immune dysregulation and dynamic clinical shifts.

DNA methylation has emerged as a key epigenetic mechanism linking environmental exposures to immune activation in SLE [4–6]. The type I interferon (IFN) pathway plays a central role in SLE pathogenesis, and hypomethylation of IFN-stimulated genes has been consistently observed in patients with SLE [7–9]. Among these genes, interferon-induced protein 44-like (IFI44L) serves as a prototypical IFN-stimulated gene. Early epigenome-wide studies demonstrated widespread hypomethylation in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from SLE patients compared with healthy controls, particularly at IFI44L and other IFN-related gene loci [10]. Importantly, IFI44L promoter hypomethylation has shown promise as a biomarker with high sensitivity and specificity for distinguishing SLE from healthy individuals and other autoimmune diseases.[11–13].

However, most prior studies have focused on patients with established SLE, leaving several critical questions unresolved. It remains unclear whether IFI44L promoter methylation alterations occur during the preclinical or early disease stages, whether the changes correlate with disease activity or treatment response, and whether they can facilitate patient stratification. Moreover, the diagnostic performance of IFI44L methylation in broader cohorts of patients with connective tissue diseases (CTDs) has not been fully elucidated.

To address these gaps, we systematically investigated IFI44L promoter methylation across the SLE disease spectrum, from individuals at genetic risk and those with early-stage disease to patients with active or stable SLE. This study aims to clarify whether IFI44L promoter methylation can serve as a robust biomarker for disease susceptibility, early detection, activity monitoring, and differential diagnosis in clinical practice.

Methods

Study population

This study employed a primarily cross-sectional design, with an embedded longitudinal sub-study. Participants were categorized into five distinct groups: patients with classified SLE (active, n = 124; stable, n = 62), individuals with incomplete lupus erythematosus (ILE) (n = 68), first-degree relatives of SLE patients (at-risk group) (n = 27), healthy controls (n = 106), and a validation cohort comprising patients with other CTDs (n = 179).

Established SLE patients fulfilled either the 2012 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) classification criteria or the 2019 European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) classification criteria. Active and stable SLE required Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K) >4 or ≤4, respectively, with concordant confirmation from two independent rheumatologists.

ILE required fulfillment of all of the following conditions:

Presence of one or more clinical features suggestive of SLE (e.g., malar/discoid rash, arthralgia/arthritis, oral ulcers, or unexplained alopecia).

Presence of one or more clinical features suggestive of SLE (e.g., malar/discoid rash, arthralgia/arthritis, oral ulcers, or unexplained alopecia).

Presence of at least one immunologic abnormality, defined as positivity for antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA), specific extractable nuclear antigen antibodies, or documented hypocomplementemia (low C3 and/or C4).

Presence of at least one immunologic abnormality, defined as positivity for antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA), specific extractable nuclear antigen antibodies, or documented hypocomplementemia (low C3 and/or C4).

Clinical suspicion of SLE, without meeting SLE classification criteria.

No prior exposure to systemic immunosuppressive or immunomodulatory therapy.

At-risk group included biologically related parents, siblings, or children (age ≥ 14 years) of patients with a confirmed SLE diagnosis. All of them were free of clinically apparent autoimmune disease and met the same general exclusion criteria applied to healthy controls.

Healthy controls were defined as individuals with no personal history or family history of systemic autoimmune disorders. They had no evidence of active infection or malignancy, and were not on any immunomodulatory medications at the time of enrollment.

The validation cohort consisted of patients diagnosed with other CTDs who met established classification criteria for their respective conditions: primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS), dermatomyositis (DM), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic sclerosis (SSc), ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV), adult-onset Still’s disease (AOSD), and psoriatic disease (PsD).

Additionally, a longitudinal subgroup of 16 SLE patients provided paired peripheral blood samples obtained at baseline (prior to induction therapy) and at a median of three months after treatment, allowing analysis of dynamic changes in IFI44L promoter methylation to therapeutic intervention.

IFI44L promoter methylation measurement

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using a commercial Whole Blood Genomic Nucleic Acid Extraction and Purification Kit (Shenzhen Sciarray Biotech Co., Ltd., China). Purified DNA was subjected to bisulfite conversion using the IFI44L Gene Methylation Detection Kit (Shenzhen Sciarray Biotech Co., Ltd., China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The bisulfite-treated DNA was subsequently used as the template for PCR amplification of the IFI44L promoter region. Methylation-sensitive high-resolution melting (MS-HRM) analysis was performed on a LightCycler 480 II Real-Time PCR System (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland) to quantify methylation levels.

The target fragment was amplified using the following primers: forward 5’-TGTGGATAGT GATAATTTGTTATAAAGTAA-3’ and reverse 5’-AACCTCATCCAATCTTAAAACACTTATA −3’, with the reverse primer biotinylated at the 5’ end. Methylation levels were determined by HRMTool software (Shenzhen Sciarray Biotech Co., Ltd., China) based on the melting curve profiles. Two CpG sites within the IFI44L promoter (Chr1:79,085,222 and cg06872964 at Chr1:79,085,250) were assessed, and their mean methylation value was used for subsequent analyses. In line with previously published reports [13], IFI44L promoter methylation levels below 0.25 (25%) were defined as hypomethylation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.1.3). Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as number (percentage). Between-group differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal-Wallis test for multiple groups, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction, respectively. Associations between IFI44L promoter methylation levels and clinical parameters were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Multiple linear regression analysis was employed to evaluate the independent association between IFI44L methylation and disease activity, adjusting for absolute lymphocyte counts as a potential confounder.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were conducted to evaluate the diagnostic performance of IFI44L promoter hypomethylation, including its ability to distinguish SLE from healthy controls or patients with other CTDs, as well as to identify active SLE. The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated accordingly. The optimal diagnostic cut-off points were determined by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). To ensure the generalizability and clinical utility of the assay, the empirically adopted threshold of 0.25 was further validated against these mathematically optimal cut-offs within the current cohort. For the longitudinal subgroup, changes in methylation levels between paired pre- and post-treatment samples were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Overall trend of IFI44L methylation across the SLE spectrum

IFI44L promoter methylation exhibited a clear, stepwise decline along the SLE disease spectrum (Fig. 1). Healthy controls showed the highest methylation levels [0.44 (0.39–0.48)]. Individuals at at-risk for SLE demonstrated a modest, non-significant reduction compared to healthy controls [0.38 (0.35–0.44), p = 0.129]. Patients with active SLE displayed the most pronounced hypomethylation [0.08 (0.03–0.16)], which significantly differed from all other groups (all p < 0.01). Methylation levels showed partial recovery in patients with stable SLE [0.17 (0.08–0.33)], but remained significantly lower than in healthy controls, high-risk individuals, and patients with ILE (Table 1, Fig. 1). Collectively, these data delineate the potential clinical utility of IFI44L methylation as a multifaceted biomarker, effectively mirroring the disease continuum, from health and high-risk group (risk stratification), through early detection of ILE, to disease activity monitoring and treatment response assessment.

Demographic characteristics and IFI44L methylation levels across the SLE disease spectrum

IFI44L methylation in individuals at high-risk for SLE

Although the overall methylation difference between the at-risk group and healthy controls was not statistically significant, heterogeneity was observed within the high-risk cohort. 5 (18.5%) individuals exhibited IFI44L promoter hypomethylation (range: 0.18–0.25). All 5 subjects were ANA positive (titers 1:100–1:1000). One was weakly positive for anti-dsDNA antibody (IIF titer 1:10) and positive for anti-SSA, another was positive for both anti-SSA and anti-SSB antibodies. Complement levels were within normal limits, and none presented clinical manifestations of SLE at the time of sampling. This finding suggests that IFI44L hypomethylation may serve as a subclinical immune activation marker, predating the onset of full-blown SLE, and could potentially identify a subgroup of at-risk individuals who merit closer clinical surveillance.

IFI44L methylation and clinical features in incomplete LE

Among 68 individuals with ILE, 32 (47.1%) exhibited IFI44L promoter hypomethylation. Common clinical manifestations included rash (30.9%), arthralgia (14.7%), alopecia (13.2%), mucosal ulcers (7.4%), and fever (7.4%). Laboratory findings included leukopenia (8.8%), anemia (2.9%), thrombocytopenia (1.5%), microscopic hematuria (7.4%), and proteinuria (0.52 g/24 h in 1 patient). All patients were ANA-positive (titers 1:100–1:100000). Autoantibody profiles included anti-dsDNA (11.8%), anti-SSA (35.3%), anti-SSB (16.2%), anti-nRNP (23.5%), anti-Sm (13.2%), anti-rRNP (8.8%), and anti-histone (5.9%). Among patients tested for antiphospholipid antibodies, positivity rates were: lupus anticoagulant 20.6%, anticardiolipin 4.8%, and anti-β2-glycoprotein I 28.6%. Hypocomplementemia was observed in 13.2% of patients.

Patients with hypomethylation had significantly lower lymphocyte counts [1.2 (0.9–1.6) vs 1.7 (1.4–2.1) x109/L, p < 0.001] and platelet counts [206 (185–240) vs 264 (212–294) x109/L, p = 0.002], along with higher immunoglobulin levels [IgG: 16.0 (14.0–20.2) vs 13.4 (12.2–14.6) g/L, p = 0.002; IgA: 2.6 (2.2–3.2) vs 2.1 (1.8–2.9) g/L, p = 0.040] and higher SLEDAI-2K scores [2 (2-4) vs 2 (0–2), p = 0.045] compared to those with normal methylation.

Active SLE patients were younger and exhibited lower hemoglobin, higher proteinuria, and more frequent hematuria, pyuria, and urinary casts. IFI44L promoter methylation was significantly lower in active SLE than in stable SLE [0.08 (0.03–0.16) vs 0.17 (0.08–0.33), p = 0.007].

Association of IFI44L promoter methylation with specific clinical manifestations

Subgroup analyses indicated lower IFI44L methylation levels in SLE patients presenting with rash, mucosal ulcers, serositis, or vasculitis. Although patients with lupus nephritis showed a trend toward lower methylation, this did not reach statistical significance. Notably, patients with hematuria or urinary protein > 3.0 g/24 h had significantly lower methylation levels, suggesting an association between hypomethylation and more severe renal involvement (Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.05; Fig. 2).

Methylation levels were inversely associated with ANA titers and anti-dsDNA antibodies, and positively associated with lymphocyte counts, hemoglobin, and complement levels. Correlation of IFI44L promoter methylation with autoantibodies

ANA titers were stratified as low (1: 100), moderate (1:320-1:1000), and high (>1:1000). IFI44L methylation seemed to decrease stepwise as ANA titers increased. Patients with cytoplasmic ANA patterns exhibited lower IFI44L methylation than those without. Moreover, positivity for anti-Sm, anti-nRNP, anti-rRNP, and anti-histone antibodies was associated with significantly lower IFI44L methylation (Fig. 2). For all multi-group comparisons depicted in Fig. 2, p-values were adjusted using Dunn’s post-hoc Bonferroni correction to ensure statistical stringency.

Among 42 treatment-naïve SLE patients, 38 (90.5%) exhibited IFI44L hypomethylation, compared to 34 (81.0%) who were anti-dsDNA positive, 17 (40.5%) anti-Sm positive, and 32 (76.1%) with hypocomplementemia.

Correlation of IFI44L promoter methylation with laboratory markers and disease activity

We further explored the correlations between IFI44L methylation and parameters of SLE disease activity. IFI44L methylation showed a moderate inverse correlation with SLEDAI-2K scores (r = −0.406, p < 0.001) and modest positive correlations with complement levels (C3: r = 0.345, p < 0.001; C4: r = 0.310, p < 0.001). A weaker but significant inverse association was also observed with anti-dsDNA autoantibody titers (r = −0.252, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Among routine laboratory indices, IFI44L methylation was positively correlated with absolute lymphocyte counts (r = 0.477, p < 0.001). In contrast, associations with other hematological markers were statistically significant but substantially weaker, including hemoglobin levels (r = 0.280, p < 0.001), white blood cell counts (r = 0.186, p = 0.0115), and serum IgG levels (r = −0.159, p = 0.031). Furthermore, although no linear correlation was found with total 24-hour urinary protein excretion across the entire cohort, stratified analysis revealed that patients with massive proteinuria (>3.0 g/24 h) exhibited markedly lower methylation levels compared to those with milder renal involvement (Bonferroni-corrected; Fig. 2).

To exclude the potential confounding effect of cell-type heterogeneity, particularly lymphopenia, we performed multiple linear regression analysis. The results confirmed that both SLEDAI-2K scores (β = −0.008, p < 0.0001; Adjusted R2 = 0.209) and active disease status (β = −0.068, p = 0.0005; Adjusted R2 = 0.169) remained significant and independent predictors of IFI44L hypomethylation after adjusting for absolute lymphocyte counts.

Among 62 stable SLE patients, 37 (59.7%) exhibited IFI44L hypomethylation. Compared to 25 patients with normal methylation, hypomethylation patients had higher anti-dsDNA levels [0 (0, 162) vs 0 (0, 0), p = 0.009] and higher SLEDAI-2K scores [2 (0, 4) vs 0 (0, 2), p = 0.023], elevated IgG and IgA levels [IgG: 13.6 (12.2, 17.1) vs 10.6 (8.7, 13.0) g/L, p = 0.011; IgA: 2.8 (2.0, 3.3) vs 1.9 (1.5, 2.5) g/L, p = 0.025], lower white blood cell and lymphocyte counts [WBC: 5.0 (4.3, 5.7) vs 5.8 (5.2, 7.6) x109/L, p = 0.013; LY: 1.2 (1.0, 1.6) vs 1.7 (1.2, 1.9) x109/L, p = 0.049]. These findings suggest that persistent IFI44L hypomethylation in stable SLE may indicate subclinical activity and potentially higher relapse risk, warranting longitudinal investigation.

Longitudinal changes of IFI44L promoter methylation following treatment

In 16 active SLE patients with paired samples before and 3 months after initiation of induction therapy (Supplementary Table 1), SLEDAI-2K score decreased from 12 (6-15) to 8 (5-10) (p = 0.008). Concurrently, IFI44L methylation increased substantially from 0.04 (0.03–0.07) to 0.13 (0.08–0.23) (p = 0.018) (Fig. 3), supporting its dynamic response to treatment and potential utility in monitoring disease activity.

Diagnostic performance for active SLE

Using a cutoff of <0.25, IFI44L hypomethylation demonstrated a sensitivity of 87.9%, specificity of 40.3%, and AUC of 0.641, 95% CI: 0.573–0.709 for identifying active SLE. Its sensitivity was higher than that of anti-dsDNA positivity (77.4%) and low complement levels (61.8%), although specificity was slightly lower.

Diagnostic performance of IFI44L methylation for SLE and incomplete lupus

Diagnostic performance for established SLE

The diagnostic performance of the 0.25 threshold was further validated by ROC analysis. While the mathematically optimal cut-off points determined by the maximum Youden index were 0.279 (SLE vs. HC) in our cohort, we adhered to the 0.25 threshold to prioritize high clinical specificity and ensure methodological consistency with prior multicenter studies (Supplementary Fig. S1). This choice was justified by the fact that the 0.25 threshold maintains peak diagnostic specificity with only a marginal impact on sensitivity compared to the mathematical optima. Compared with healthy controls, IFI44L hypomethylation demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for SLE, with a sensitivity of 78.5%, specificity of 100%, and AUC of 0.892, 95% CI: 0.863–0.922.

Compared with the independent cohort of 179 patients with other CTDs, comprising DM, RA, pSS, PsD, AOSD, AAV, and SSc, IFI44L methylation in SLE was significantly lower than in all other CTD groups (all p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 2), with no significant differences among the non-SLE CTD subgroups (Fig. 4). For distinguishing SLE from other CTDs, IFI44L hypomethylation yielded a sensitivity of 78.5%, specificity of 92.7%, and AUC of 0.856, 95% CI: 0.821–0.891. Even in the challenging distinction between SLE and DM, IFI44L hypomethylation retained a sensitivity of 78.5%, specificity of 83.3%, and AUC of 0.809, 95% CI: 0.748–0.870.

Diagnostic performance for ILE

Compared to healthy controls, IFI44L hypomethylation diagnosed ILE with a sensitivity of 47.1% and specificity of 100% and AUC of 0.735, 95% CI: 0.676–0.795. Against other CTDs, sensitivity was 47.1%, specificity 92.1%, and AUC 0.696, 95% CI: 0.633–0.759. In contrast, conventional markers such as anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm antibodies, while highly specific, showed very low sensitivity (11.9% and 13.6%, respectively) and low AUC values (0.560 and 0.568). Hypocomplementemia also performed poorly for ILE (sensitivity 13.2%, specificity 94.1%, AUC = 0.537, 95% CI: 0.466–0.607).

Discussion

While prior seminal studies have established IFI44L promoter hypomethylation as a highly sensitive and specific diagnostic marker for established SLE [11, 13–15], our study extends its potential utility earlier in the disease continuum. We demonstrate its presence in a subset of asymptomatic at-risk individuals and its graded reduction in ILE, suggesting a role in risk prediction and early detection. Furthermore, our longitudinal data solidifies its dynamic correlation with treatment response, supporting its potential use for monitoring. We demonstrate that IFI44L hypomethylation is a sensitive, disease-specific epigenetic alteration that mirrors SLE development and progression, which argues for a broader application across the clinical management spectrum of SLE.

A key finding of this study is the progressive emergence of IFI44L promoter hypomethylation along the SLE continuum. Notably, while IFI44L hypomethylation was entirely absent in healthy controls, it was detectable in 18.5% of at-risk individuals, increased to 47.1% in ILE, and reached 90.5% in treatment-naïve patients with classified SLE. This stepwise pattern suggests that IFI44L hypomethylation is an early epigenetic event that precedes or accompanies the transition from preclinical autoimmunity to overt disease. This observation is biologically plausible, given that persistent type I IFN pathways activation is a central driver of SLE immunopathology and occurs early in disease development [16–18]. IFI44L, as a prototypical IFN-stimulated gene, becomes more transcriptionally accessible upon hypomethylation, thereby amplifying IFN signaling [11, 19, 20]. Our data align with previous reports of elevated IFN signatures in at-risk individuals and those with ILE [21–24], indicating that IFI44L hypomethylation can detect early upstream immune perturbations before overt clinical manifestations appear, potentially providing a window for early intervention.

Furthermore, we found that IFI44L hypomethylation was closely associated with specific clinical phenotypes and disease activity. Patients with rash, mucosal ulcers, serositis, or vasculitis exhibited significantly lower methylation levels. Although the association with lupus nephritis did not reach statistical significance, patients with severe renal manifestations had markedly reduced methylation, suggesting a link to more severe renal inflammation. Whether lupus nephritis is consistently associated with lower hypomethylation remains controversial [11, 14]. These findings are consistent with previous reports that associate type I IFN–mediated immune activation with tissue injury and organ involvement in SLE.[11, 25].

Current serologic markers for SLE have well-recognized limitations: ANA exhibits high sensitivity but poor specificity, while anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm antibodies offer high specificity at the cost of low sensitivity. In this context, IFI44L promoter hypomethylation demonstrates a more balanced profile. It maintained high sensitivity (78.5%) and specificity (100%) for distinguishing SLE from healthy controls (AUC = 0.892) and showed robust performance in differentiating SLE from other CTDs (sensitivity 78.5%, specificity 92.7%, AUC 0.856). For ILE, it offered superior sensitivity (47.1%) compared to anti-dsDNA (11.9%) and anti-Sm (13.6%), while retaining high specificity. These findings support its potential as a complementary biomarker for early identification and stratification across the lupus spectrum.

Correlation analyses further supported the clinical relevance of IFI44L hypomethylation, revealing a significant inverse association with SLEDAI-2K scores and modest positive correlations with serum C3 and C4 levels. These findings suggest the marker’s potential in reflecting systemic disease activity. In contrast, while associations with other routine laboratory indices achieved statistical significance, their relatively low correlation strengths indicate that their clinical link to IFI44L epigenetic status is less substantial compared to the core metrics of SLE activity. In detecting active SLE, IFI44L hypomethylation showed higher sensitivity (87.9%) than anti-dsDNA positivity (77.4%) and hypocomplementemia (61.8%), though with slightly lower specificity. This positions IFI44L methylation as a composite biomarker integrating serologic and clinical dimensions of SLE activity.

Our longitudinal data from 16 patients with active SLE demonstrated a significant increase in IFI44L methylation following effective induction therapy, concurrent with reduced disease activity. This dynamic reversibility strengthens its potential utility as a biomarker for monitoring treatment response. While pharmacological agents such as corticosteroids and mycophenolate mofetil may modulate DNA methylation profiles, the synchronized recovery of IFI44L methylation alongside clinical improvement suggests that these changes reflect the resolution of the interferon-driven state rather than a non-specific drug effect. This is further supported by the observation that the vast majority of treatment-naïve patients exhibited profound hypomethylation prior to any medication, reinforcing this epigenetic signature as an intrinsic marker of disease activity. Notably, among patients with clinically stable SLE, those with persistent IFI44L hypomethylation exhibited higher anti-dsDNA titers, higher SLEDAI-2K scores, and lymphopenia, all of which are recognized risk factors for SLE flare [26–30]. This suggests that IFI44L methylation may help identify a subgroup of “stable” patients with subclinical activity and higher relapse risk, warranting prospective validation.

While IFI44L hypomethylation effectively distinguished SLE from other rheumatic diseases in our cohort, approximately 16.7% of DM patients also exhibited IFI44L hypomethylation. This is biologically plausible given the shared centrality of type I IFN pathways in DM pathogenesis [31–33]. Robust IFN signatures have been documented in muscle, skin, and peripheral blood, where they correlate strongly with disease activity [34, 35]. While previous studies have demonstrated the high sensitivity and specificity of IFI44L promoter hypomethylation for distinguishing SLE from RA and pSS, its discriminative performance for DM will require validation in larger, disease-specific cohorts. Therefore, while IFI44L methylation holds high discriminative value, its interpretation should consider the clinical context, particularly when DM is a differential diagnosis.

This study has several limitations. First, the longitudinal treatment cohort was small (n = 16), larger cohorts are needed to confirm its utility for monitoring disease activity. Second, autoantibody testing in first-degree relatives was not performed comprehensively but was restricted to those with IFI44L hypomethylation, which may introduce selection bias. Third, long-term, prospective studies are essential to determine whether IFI44L hypomethylation in at-risk individuals or those with ILE predicts progression to classified SLE, and whether persistent hypomethylation in stable disease predicts future flares. Fourth, our single-center design necessitates validation in diverse, multicenter cohorts. Fifth, detailed medication history for the non-SLE CTD control groups was not systematically recorded. Although the high prevalence of IFI44L hypomethylation in treatment-naïve SLE patients (90.5%) suggests that this is a primary pathogenic feature rather than a drug-induced effect, the potential impact of pharmacological interventions on the methylation profiles of other CTDs remains to be fully elucidated. Finally, the 100% specificity against healthy controls in our study may be influenced by the relatively small control group and requires cautious interpretation.

Conclusion

This study provides comprehensive evidence that IFI44L promoter hypomethylation is a sensitive, dynamic, and highly disease-specific epigenetic biomarker across the SLE spectrum. It captures early immune dysregulation, reflects disease activity, responds to therapeutic intervention, and offers superior diagnostic value in ILE. These findings support the integration of IFI44L methylation as a valuable tool to enhance early detection, activity monitoring, and therapeutic guidance in SLE.
