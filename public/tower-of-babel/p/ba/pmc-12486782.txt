Continuous passive motion for prevention of ankle contracture and muscle loss in mechanically ventilated ICU patients
Authors: Chen-Chun Lin, Yi-Jia Lin, Hsien-Chun Wang, Wei-Chun Hsu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis prospective, within-patient controlled study aimed to evaluate the effects of continuous passive motion therapy on ankle mobility and muscle morphology in mechanically ventilated ICU patients—a population vulnerable to ICU-acquired weakness and joint contractures.ResultsTwelve ventilated patients completed the protocol. The ankle treated with continuous passive motion therapy showed a significant improvement in dorsiflexion passive range of motion, increasing from 9.90° [IQR: 6.20–13.15°] to 12.99° [IQR: 9.07–20.20°] (p = 0.004), while the control ankle showed no significant change (p = 0.388). The increase in passive range of motion was significantly greater in the continuous passive motion treated ankle compared with the control (4.18° vs. 0.42°, p = 0.028). No significant between-limb differences were observed in tibialis anterior muscle thickness, cross-sectional area, or pennation angle. Echointensity increased in both limbs without group differences. These findings suggest that continuous passive motion can be applied as an early intervention to preserve ankle mobility in ICU patients. Although short-term muscle morphology changes were not significant, passive range of motion gains suggest continuous passive motion may help prevent contractures and support early rehabilitation during critical illness.Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06944431, registered on April 25, 2025. Introduction

Critically ill patients commonly experience neuromuscular complications, notably intensive care unit-acquired weakness (ICU-AW) and joint contractures. ICU-AW occurs in approximately 40% of patients requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation, with reported incidence ranging from 32% using clinical assessment to 47% using electrophysiological techniques [1]and is associated with prolonged hospitalization, delayed functional recovery, and increased mortality [2–4]. Concurrently, immobility during ICU stays contributes significantly to joint contractures, affecting approximately one-third of patients experiencing extended ICU admissions [5–7]. Among these complications, ankle joint plantar-flexion contractures (foot drop) are particularly debilitating, severely impacting patients’ balance, gait retraining, and overall functional recovery after ICU discharge [5].

Although early mobilization is widely recognized as beneficial, leading to reduced ICU stays, lower incidence of delirium, preservation of muscle integrity, and enhanced quality of life post-discharge [8, 9]its implementation remains challenging. Many critically ill patients are unable to participate in early active mobilization due to sedation, hemodynamic instability, or other medical contraindications. Furthermore, evidence suggests that early mobilization might carry increased risks of adverse events for certain patient populations [10]. Thus, alternative strategies are essential for patients unsuitable for early active rehabilitation, emphasizing preservation of joint mobility and muscle integrity to facilitate future rehabilitation opportunities.

Continuous passive motion (CPM) therapy has emerged as a viable intervention designed initially for postoperative orthopedic rehabilitation. CPM passively moves joints through a controlled range, potentially maintaining flexibility by inhibiting periarticular collagen cross-linking, preserving soft tissue extensibility, and maintaining muscle-tendon compliance [11]. However, its effectiveness in critical illness settings remains uncertain, with limited evidence suggesting potential benefits in reducing muscle atrophy, such as that of the tibialis anterior muscle, in ICU patients unable to actively mobilize [12]while other studies have reported minimal effects [13]. Thus, rigorous evaluation of CPM’s impact on joint mobility preservation and muscle atrophy prevention is warranted.

This study aimed to evaluate whether CPM therapy effectively mitigates passive range of motion (PROM) loss at the ankle joint in sedated, mechanically ventilated ICU patients. Additionally, muscle structural changes were assessed via ultrasound imaging to determine if CPM influences muscle morphology compared to limbs receiving standard care alone. Ultrasound parameters, including muscle thickness (MT), cross-sectional area (CSA), echointensity (EI), and pennation angle (PA), were used to comprehensively monitor morphological changes and provide insights into the functional implications of observed alterations.

Methods

This prospective, within-patient controlled pilot study was conducted in a tertiary medical ICU setting and was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06944431 on April 25, 2025. Eligible participants were adults with acute respiratory failure expected to require mechanical ventilation for > 5 days. This determination was made by board-certified intensivists based on clinical judgment, taking into account disease severity, PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio, APACHE II scores, and chest radiographic findings. Exclusion criteria included neuromuscular disorders, recent lower limb surgery or trauma, critical limb ischemia, amputation, deep vein thrombosis, significant leg wounds, or pregnancy.

The left ankle received CPM using a motorized device (KINETEC® Breva, USA), programmed to cycle between 30° dorsiflexion and 60° plantarflexion with 10-second holds at each end range. Sessions lasted 30 min, twice daily on weekdays, for up to 7 days or until ICU discharge. The right ankle received standard care but no structured passive motion.

Outcome assessments were conducted by a trained physiotherapist who was not formally blinded to the intervention, as he visited the ICU daily and could potentially observe patient care. During assessment, both ankles were measured in the same manner without emphasis on the intervention side. The primary outcome was ankle dorsiflexion PROM, measured by a trained physiotherapist using a goniometer at baseline and post-intervention with the knee at 30° flexion. Each measurement was repeated three times and averaged. Secondary outcomes included changes in tibialis anterior muscle morphology—specifically MT, CSA, PA, and EI—assessed via bedside ultrasound using a 5–12 MHz linear transducer (ClearVue 350, Philips, USA), following previously published study protocols [14–16]. EI was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD) with a fixed region of interest. Ultrasound assessments were conducted before the first CPM session and after the seven-day intervention or at ICU discharge. Adverse events such as skin injury, joint pain, muscle spasms, and device-related issues were closely monitored to evaluate CPM safety. Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1. Assuming a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.7), α = 0.05, and power = 0.80 for a two-tailed paired analysis, a minimum of 10 participants would be required.

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were expressed as median [25th-75th percentile], while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages.

The primary outcome was compared between the CPM-treated and control ankles within patients using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Secondary outcomes were analyzed as within-patient comparisons using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Between-limb differences post-intervention were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Twelve patients met inclusion criteria and completed the CPM intervention, with a median age of 80 [68.5–88] years, median Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II score was 20 [18.5–28], and median body mass index was 23.6 [18.4-27.75]. Ankle dorsiflexion PROM

At baseline, there were no significant differences in ankle dorsiflexion PROM or ultrasound-derived muscle parameters between the CPM-treated and control limbs. After the 7-day intervention, ankle PROM in dorsiflexion improved in the CPM-treated limbs, (9.90° [6.20–13.15°] to 12.99° [9.07–20.20°], p = 0.004), whereas no significant change was observed in the control limb (9.75° [8.20–17.15°] to 11.00° [6.88–20.37°], p = 0.388) (Table 2). A Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirmed a significantly greater improvement in dorsiflexion PROM in the CPM-treated limb compared to the control limb (ΔPROM, defined as the median of individual changes from Day 1 to the final assessment in PROM, 4.18° [0.89–5.80°] vs. 0.42° [-0.90–1.93°], p = 0.028) (Table 3).

Ultrasound muscle assessments

From baseline to the final assessment, a significant increase in EI was observed in both limbs (control limb: 52.80 [46.49–59.99] to 64.07 [61.09–69.63], p = 0.02; CPM-treated limb: 55.13 [47.29–60.79] to 64.66 [62.41–68.37], p = 0.002). Other ultrasound parameters, including MT, CSA, and PA, did not exhibit significant baseline differences between groups (p > 0.05) (Table 3). Comparison of changes in ultrasound measures between the CPM-treated and control limbs revealed no statistically significant differences in ∆MT (p = 0.291), ∆CSA (p = 0.843), ∆PA (p = 0.291), and ∆EI (p = 0.932) (Table 3). No adverse outcomes related to the intervention were observed during the study period.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that CPM modestly improved ankle joint mobility in mechanically ventilated patients, whereas the control limb receiving standard care showed no change. The approximately 3° gain in dorsiflexion, although small, may be clinically meaningful. Even mild plantar-flexion contractures can affect gait, delay rehabilitation, and contribute to long-term functional limitations [5, 17]. Therefore, maintaining ROM may support earlier mobilization and improve long-term recovery. Our findings align with the theoretical benefits of passive movement in preventing stiffness and maintaining joint flexibility. By delivering sustained, repetitive stretch without requiring patient effort, mechanical CPM may help preserve dorsiflexion and facilitate future rehabilitation. However, our results differ from those of a recent randomized trial by Stiller et al., which reported no significant improvements in ankle ROM or stiffness following routine passive limb exercises in ICU patients [13]. This discrepancy may stem from differences in intervention delivery. Manual exercises are hard to sustain at consistent intensity and frequency for extended periods—such as 30 min—due to caregiver fatigue and workload. In contrast, our protocol used an automated CPM device that delivered uniform, rhythmic mobilization without operator fatigue. This consistency may partly explain the more favorable outcomes in joint mobility and muscle preservation.

In contrast to the to the joint mobility improvements, our ultrasound assessments did not show a significant benefit of CPM in preserving muscle mass or architecture of the tibialis anterior. Both limbs exhibited similar trajectories, with no evidence of CPM-related muscle preservation. Several factors may explain this. First, the short study duration and small sample size may have limited our ability to detect subtle atrophy in the tibialis anterior. Muscle atrophy in critical illness is a gradual process, and a one-week window might be too short to register substantial losses in a distal muscle like the tibialis anterior. In critical illness, early muscle wasting tends to be more pronounced in large, proximal muscles (such as the quadriceps) than in smaller, more distal muscles. Annetta et al. observed in young trauma ICU patients that after three weeks of critical illness, the rectus femoris CSA decreased by about 45%, whereas the tibialis anterior CSA showed only a 22% non-significant reduction [14]. This suggests that ankle dorsiflexors may be relatively resistant to early atrophy or require a longer duration of disuse to reveal measurable wasting. Additionally, our older, possibly more metabolically compromised cohort may have had a blunted anabolic response, further masking any potential effect of CPM. Second, measurable variability, particularly in PA assessments of a small muscle, could have obscured minor architectural shifts. Although no significant PA changes were detected, prior studies have shown that PA can fluctuate during critical illness and may hold prognostic value. For instance, low quadriceps PA at ICU admission or early declines over the first week have been linked to worse clinical outcomes and ICU-acquired weakness [16, 18].

A key observation was the significant bilateral increase in muscle EI, with no between-limb differences. EI reflects compositional alterations—such as fat infiltration, fibrosis, and edema—and may precede overt muscle atrophy [14, 19]. Prior studies have shown that EI increases early during critical illness, even when CSA remains stable [14]. Our findings suggest that systemic catabolic factors, such as inflammation, or denervation, may drive early muscle quality deterioration that passive motion alone cannot prevent.

Beyond the specifics of joint and muscle changes, our study should be viewed in the context of the challenges of rehabilitating critically ill patients. Early active mobilization has well-documented benefits for ICU survivors in terms of functional outcomes and strength [20]. However, this approach is often not feasible in deeply sedated or hemodynamically unstable patients. Moreover, not all early rehabilitation strategies yield clear benefits. Excessive or premature activity may contribute to “overwork weakness,” particularly in muscles recovering from disuse or injury [21]. Several recent clinical trials have reported neutral or even adverse outcomes. For example, a multinational RCT involving 741 mechanically ventilated patients found that early, high-intensity mobilization did not improve long-term physical outcomes and was associated with more adverse events, including cardiovascular instability and device dislodgement [10]. Similarly, Moss et al. showed that intensifying standard physiotherapy sessions for patients with acute respiratory failure did not enhance physical recovery [22]. In contrast to active mobilization, passive strategies like neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) are also used clinically. NMES can reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-alpha [23]; however, its effectiveness is often limited in critically ill patients due to frequent muscle cell membrane dysfunction, which may impair contraction [24]. Evidence indicates NMES may help preserve muscle mass in milder cases or during the post-acute phase, but its benefit appears reduced in more severe illness [25–27]. These findings caution against a one-size-fits-all approach and underscore the need for individualized, titrated rehabilitation strategies based on each patient’s capacity and clinical stability.

In light of these considerations, CPM appears to be a promising adjunct for ICU patients unable to participate in active therapy. Our pilot data suggest that CPM can safely help preserve joint mobility when patients are not yet capable of moving independently. This may have important clinical implications. Incorporating CPM into the care of deeply sedated or immobilized patients may help maintain musculoskeletal integrity and improve readiness for rehabilitation once they awaken or stabilize. As an early preventive measure, CPM could protect joints and muscle–tendon units when conventional physiotherapy is not feasible. Preserving joint range of motion and tissue extensibility may facilitate faster and more effective mobilization during recovery. Future studies should aim to incorporate electroneuromyography (ENMG) and other neurophysiological tools to more precisely characterize the underlying mechanisms of ICU-acquired weakness (ICU-AW), particularly in differentiating between critical illness polyneuropathy and myopathy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although the a priori sample size calculation indicated adequate power for the primary outcome, the single-center design and relatively small cohort may limit the generalizability of the findings and the ability to detect smaller effects in secondary outcomes. Second, the advanced age of participants (median age 80) may have reduced muscle responsiveness to CPM therapy, influencing muscle plasticity. Third, the short observation period (7–14 days) may have been insufficient to detect significant morphological muscle changes, and long-term functional outcomes post-ICU discharge were not assessed. Fourth, outcome assessors were not blinded to intervention allocation. Given that the observed differences in ankle dorsiflexion ROM were relatively small, the lack of blinding may have introduced measurement bias. Finally, although the within-patient control design reduced inter-patient variability, consistently using the left limb instead of randomly assigning sides may have introduced bias from preexisting differences such as foot dominance. These limitations warrant cautious interpretation. Future studies should incorporate blinded assessment strategies, larger and more diverse samples, randomized controlled designs, and long-term follow-up to strengthen the evidence base.

Conclusions

This study suggests that CPM can be applied as a safe and potentially beneficial early intervention to preserve ankle mobility in mechanically ventilated ICU patients who are unable to engage in active movement. CPM was associated with improved dorsiflexion PROM, which may help prevent joint contractures and support early rehabilitation. Although no short-term changes were observed in tibialis anterior muscle morphology, maintaining joint range may still enhance rehabilitation readiness. These preliminary findings warrant larger, longer-term studies to validate CPM’s impact on functional outcomes such as mobilization, ambulation, and discharge readiness. If confirmed, CPM may serve as a valuable early physiotherapy tool, particularly for deeply sedated or immobilized patients.
