Quaternary ammonium carbon dots for membrane perforation-immunity synergistic therapy against drug-resistant cancer metastasis
Authors: Qi Wu, Shanshan Wang, Jingchun Wang, Siran Jin, Yikai Ma, Xiaodan Wu, Jing Liu, Hui Zhang, Guanghui Tan, Yingxue Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multidrug resistance and invasive metastasis constitute pivotal clinical bottlenecks that severely compromise curative outcomes of malignant tumors. Conventional chemotherapy and immunotherapy frequently fail to achieve satisfactory efficacy due to drug resistance barriers and tumor immune escape. Herein, a hyaluronic acid‑cinnamaldehyde Schiff base micelle nanoplatform loading quaternary ammonium‑modified carbon dots (HACA@QASCDs) is rationally constructed, which achieves targeted killing of drug‑resistant tumor cells, remodeling of immunosuppressive microenvironments, and inhibition of distant metastasis via a sequential cascade of irreversible membrane perforation, mitochondria‑dependent apoptosis, and immunogenic cell death (ICD). HACA@QASCDs actively accumulate in drug‑resistant CT26 (DR‑CT26) cells through HA‑CD44 recognition and enable pH‑triggered QASCDs release in acidic tumor microenvironments. The liberated QASCDs elicit irreversible membrane perforation, leading to lactate dehydrogenase leakage, disrupted calcium homeostasis, mitochondrial depolarization, and subsequent intrinsic apoptosis. Such membrane damage simultaneously ignites ICD, and the released damage‑associated molecular patterns effectively drive dendritic cell maturation and M2‑to‑M1 macrophage polarization. In vivo evaluations in bilateral syngeneic tumor models reveal that HACA@QASCDs alone yields 54.2% primary tumor inhibition and 28.6% distant tumor inhibition. Upon combination with αPD‑L1, the distant tumor inhibition rate is markedly elevated to 68.7%. By integrating membrane perforation‑mediated direct cytotoxicity and ICD‑evoked immune activation, HACA@QASCDs offers a highly potent and clinically translatable synergistic strategy to surmount tumor multidrug resistance and block invasive metastasis.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04815-2.

Introduction

Multidrug resistance and invasive metastasis represent two core bottlenecks curbing radical clinical therapy for malignant tumors, which also act as primary contributors to high recurrence rates and poor survival outcomes among late-stage patients [1–5]. Conventional clinical interventions including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, surgical resection and immune checkpoint blockade fail to sustain durable antitumor efficacy, largely due to adaptive phenotypic evolution of tumor cells [6, 7]. Tumor resistance arises from coordinated dynamic regulation rather than isolated molecular events, involving overexpressed drug efflux pumps, mutated therapeutic targets, hyperactivated DNA damage repair and reprogrammed cellular metabolism alongside immunosuppressive microenvironments [8, 9]. Elevated migratory and invasive capacities further accelerate distant tumor colonization, creating a vicious cycle of resistance, recurrence and metastasis. Novel integrated therapeutic strategies are therefore urgently required to simultaneously reverse drug resistance, suppress metastatic progression and relieve tumor-associated immune suppression.

Physical cytotoxicity enabled by plasma membrane perforation establishes an innovative paradigm to bypass resistance limitations inherent to target-dependent chemotherapeutics [10, 11]. Unlike small-molecule agents that exert toxicity through intracellular target binding, membrane-disruptive modalities directly compromise the integrity of phospholipid bilayers. Such effects circumvent canonical resistance pathways driven by efflux transporter upregulation and target mutation [12, 13]. Irreversible membrane rupture sequentially triggers intracellular calcium overload and mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately initiating mitochondria-mediated apoptosis. Damaged tumor cells concurrently secrete damage-associated molecular patterns including calreticulin, adenosine triphosphate and high-mobility group box 1 protein, which trigger immunogenic cell death and lay the foundation for remodeling immunosuppressive niches [14–16]. Quaternary ammonium salts exhibit robust membrane-disruptive activity owing to their cationic amphipathic architecture, yet their in vivo translation is severely hampered [17, 18]. Free small-molecule quaternary ammonium groups bind cell membranes via weak monodentate electrostatic interactions and readily dissociate. Lacking tumor selectivity, these molecules non-specifically damage normal tissue and erythrocyte membranes, triggering hemolysis and systemic inflammatory side effects. Stimuli-responsive nanocarriers provide a viable strategy to mitigate off-target toxicity by enhancing tumor-specific accumulation of bioactive agents [19]. Hyaluronic acid-cinnamaldehyde Schiff base micelles integrate active tumor targeting and pH-triggered cargo release [20, 21]. Hyaluronic acid chains selectively recognize CD44 receptors overexpressed on tumor cell surfaces to facilitate lesion-specific nanoparticle enrichment. Schiff base linkages embedded within the micellar backbone undergo selective hydrolysis under mild acidic conditions corresponding to endosomes and lysosomes (pH ≈ 5.5), enabling carrier disassembly and localized payload liberation to minimize off-target damage. Nevertheless, encapsulation of free quaternary ammonium molecules cannot resolve weak membrane adhesion and insufficient perforation efficiency, necessitating a tailored nanostructured scaffold to amplify membrane-damaging potency. Carbon dots possess outstanding biocompatibility and stable fluorescence signals, permitting real-time visual tracking of nanomedicine distribution [22]. More importantly, their rigid spherical architecture contains abundant surface sites for high-density covalent grafting of quaternary ammonium alkyl chains to fabricate multi-site cationic anchoring units termed QASCDs. QASCDs firmly anchor cell membranes through multivalent electrostatic attraction and generate concentrated cytotoxic domains on membrane surfaces. The rigid carbon core mechanically expands ordered phospholipid assemblies and elevates membrane tension. Steric hindrance imposed by carbon dot frameworks further impedes lipid rearrangement, converting superficial reversible lesions induced by free quaternary ammonium into permanent membrane perforations and substantially boosting tumoricidal potency. Combining intrinsic fluorescence tracing capability with structural advantages as functional scaffolds, carbon dots serve as ideal core materials for delivering membrane-disruptive quaternary ammonium moieties.

Inspired by the above advances and unresolved drawbacks, this work fabricates pH-responsive targeted HACA@QASCDs nanomicelles with QASCDs encapsulated within the hydrophobic HACA core. This unified nanoplatform executes irreversible membrane perforation as its primary tumoricidal pathway to evade multiple chemoresistant mechanisms. Direct membrane disruption triggers mitochondria-dependent apoptosis in drug-resistant tumor cells. Concurrent immunogenic cell death releases damage-associated molecular patterns, which drive dendritic cell maturation and reprogram tumor-associated macrophages to reconstruct systemic antitumor immunity. Coupled with HA-CD44-mediated active targeting and acid-responsive cargo release, HACA@QASCDs achieve concurrent eradication of resistant lesions, suppression of invasive metastasis and reversal of immune suppression, forming a synergistic therapeutic cascade spanning tumor accumulation, pH-triggered payload release, physical membrane damage and systemic immune activation. Experimental section

Preparation of CDs

2 g of citric acid and 6 g of urea were ultrasonically dispersed in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and the mixture was then poured into a reaction kettle. The reaction kettle was placed in an oven heated to 160 °C for 6 h of heating. After cooling, the reaction solution was poured into 60 mL of absolute ethanol, followed by centrifugation for 15 min at a speed of 10,000 revolutions per minute (rpm). The precipitate was washed with cold methanol three times and then freeze-dried to obtain CDs in the form of a black powder.

Preparation of QAS

Add dodecyldimethyl tertiary amine (DMTA) (2 mmol) into a 25 mL three-necked flask, then add 10 mL of acetone as the solvent and 1 mL of triethylamine (98%). Under stirring, add 1,5-diiodopentane (2 mmol) dropwise, and heat under reflux for 12 h. Remove the acetone solvent, purify the residue by silica gel column chromatography (with d as the mobile phase), and obtain dark brown paste with a yield of 83%. NMR and Low-resolution mass spectrometry data were availablede in the Supporting Information.

Preparation of QASCDs

10 mg of QAS, 10 mg of CDs, and 1 mL of triethylamine were added into 5 mL of acetone, and the mixture was refluxed for 5 h. After cooling, the reaction solution was centrifuged for 10 min at 10,000 rpm. The precipitate was rinsed with dichloromethane three times and then freeze-dried to obtain the product.

Preparation of HACA@QASCDs

15 mg HA was dispersed in 6 mL water/DMF mixed solvent (V/V = 1:2), followed by sequential addition of 15.5 mg 1‑ethyl‑3‑(3‑dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) and 11.5 mg N‑hydroxysuccinimide (NHS). The mixture was magnetically stirred at 50 °C for 6 h. Afterwards, 2.5 mL ethylenediamine (98%) was slowly added dropwise to the reaction system, and stirring was maintained overnight. After naturally cooling down to room temperature, the crude solution was dialyzed against deionized water for 3 d using a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 1 kDa. The dialyzed HA solution was transferred to a 25 mL round-bottom flask, and 2.5 mg CA was added dropwise, with continuous stirring for 6 h. Separately, 5 mg QASCDs were dispersed in 5 mL methanol and subjected to ice water-bath sonication for 10 min. The resulting QASCDs dispersion was slowly dropped into the aforementioned HACA reaction solution, followed by homogenization at ambient temperature for 10 min by ultrasound. Subsequently, all solvents were completely removed via rotary evaporation to form an organic film on the inner wall of the flask. 10 mL deionized water was then supplemented, and the mixture was vigorously stirred for 1 h. The obtained solution was further dialyzed against deionized water for 24 h using a 1 kDa MWCO dialysis membrane, with the external dialysate renewed every 6 h. The purified dialysate was slowly added dropwise into 10 mL methanol, and the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min. The collected precipitate was freeze-dried to afford HACA@QASCDs nanomicelles.

Scanning electron microscopy (SET) analysis of cell membrane perforation

DR-CT26 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at 1 × 10⁵ cells per well with 2 mL complete RPMI-1640 medium (10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin) and cultured for 24 h to reach 50%–60% confluence. Cells were assigned to blank control, free QAS and HACA@QASCDs groups. The HACA@QASCDs group received 100 µg·mL⁻¹ formulations, and free QAS was supplemented at an equivalent dose according to the drug loading of micelles. After 24 h incubation, culture medium was discarded, and cell monolayers were gently rinsed twice with prewarmed PBS (5 mL per well). Cells were fixed in 1 mL 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature in stationary plates to avoid cell loss. After one PBS rinse, gradient ethanol dehydration was conducted sequentially with 30%, 50%, 70%, 90% and 95% ethanol (2 mL per well, 5 min for each step), followed by two rounds of absolute ethanol dehydration (5 min each). Samples were air-dried naturally, mounted on conductive stubs and sputtered with 5–10 nm gold film. Cell membrane morphology and perforation structures were imaged by SEM at 5–15 kV accelerating voltage. At least three random fields per group were recorded, and pore morphology, size and distribution were statistically quantified to assess membrane damage.

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assay for membrane fluidity detection

DR-CT26 cells were seeded on coverslip-containing 6-well plates and cultured to ~ 70% confluence. After PBS washing, cells were incubated with 200 µg·mL⁻¹ HACA@QASCDs for 2 h. Meanwhile, DiL stock was diluted to 5 µmol·L⁻¹ with serum-free medium for membrane labeling. Treated cells were rinsed thoroughly and stained with DiL working solution at 37 °C for 30 min in dark. After final PBS washes, coverslips were transferred cell-side down into confocal dishes filled with live-cell imaging buffer without air bubbles. Dishes were stabilized on a 37 °C live-cell stage for 5 min before observation. Only intact, evenly fluorescent non-overlapping cells were selected for testing. A 2–3 μm circular ROI was randomly drawn on cell membranes. The ROI was scanned three times at low laser power to record baseline fluorescence, then photobleached with high laser power for 8–10 s to capture post-bleaching signal. Time-lapse scanning at 5 s intervals was performed for 180 s under low laser power to record dynamic fluorescence recovery.

Dil membrane labeling for nanomembrane co-localization observation

DiL stock was diluted to 5 µmol·L⁻¹ with serum-free RPMI 1640. DR-CT26 cells were seeded on coverslip-equipped 6-well plates and cultured to ~ 70% confluence. After PBS rinsing, cells were incubated with mixed DiL working solution and 200 µg·mL⁻¹ samples (CDs, free QAS, QASCDs, HACA@QASCDs) for 30 min in dark. After incubation, cells were thoroughly washed with prewarmed PBS, and fresh complete medium was replenished. Fluorescence microscopy was applied to image and analyze co-localization between nanomaterials and cell membranes.

Phalloidin staining for F-actin cytoskeleton observation

Phalloidin stock was diluted with serum-free RPMI 1640 to 100 nM working solution. DR-CT26 cells were seeded on coverslip-containing 6-well plates and cultured to ~ 70% confluence. Cells were divided into blank control and four treatment groups (CDs, free QAS, QASCDs, HACA@QASCDs) with a uniform concentration of 200 µg·mL⁻¹ for 4 h incubation. All subsequent procedures were carried out in the dark. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, washed with PBS, and permeabilized using 0.5% Triton X-100 (10 min). Coverslips were mounted with anti-fade medium without air bubbles, and confocal laser scanning microscopy was used to image F-actin cytoskeleton.

JC-1 assay for mitochondrial membrane potential

DR-CT26 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 1 × 10⁵ cells/well and cultured for 24 h to reach proper confluence. Cells were randomly divided into three groups (n = 3 biological replicates) and treated with equivalent doses of free QAS, QASCDs and HACA@QASCDs for 24 h. After treatment, cells were washed three times with prewarmed PBS, then incubated with freshly prepared JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. Excess stain was washed off with prewarmed PBS, and fresh PBS was added for protection. Fluorescence images were acquired immediately under a fluorescence microscope. The excitation/emission wavelengths were set at 525/590 nm for JC-1 aggregates and 490/525 nm for JC-1 monomers.

Fluo-4 AM staining for intracellular calcium detection

DR-CT26 cells were seeded at 1 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h to reach 50%–60% confluence. Cells were randomly divided into four groups (n = 3): blank control, free QAS, QASCDs and HACA@QASCDs. HACA@QASCDs were administered at 100 µg·mL⁻¹, and equivalent doses of QAS and QASCDs were supplemented according to the micelle drug loading, followed by 24 h incubation. After treatment, cells were washed three times with prewarmed calcium-free PBS to eliminate extracellular calcium interference. Unbound dye was washed twice with calcium-free PBS, and 1 mL calcium-free PBS was added for immediate imaging. Fluorescence was captured under consistent microscope settings at Ex/Em = 494/516 nm, with three random fields recorded per group. Immunofluorescence detection of cleaved caspase-3

Cell seeding, drug treatment, fixation, permeabilization and blocking steps followed Sect. 2.13 (F-actin staining). Western blot (WB) analysis of apoptosis-related proteins including caspase family and mitochondrial cytochrome C

Establishment of bilateral syngeneic tumor model and in vivo combination therapy evaluation

Seven-week-old female Balb/C mice were used to establish a bilateral syngeneic tumor model. Briefly, 1 × 10⁶ DR-CT26 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right dorsal flank to generate primary tumors. Three days later, the same number of cells were inoculated into the left dorsal flank to construct contralateral tumors. All treatments were only applied to primary tumors, while contralateral tumors remained untreated to evaluate systemic anti-tumor immune responses. When primary tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm³, mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 5): saline control, αPD-L1 monotherapy, QASCDs monotherapy, HACA@QASCDs monotherapy and HACA@QASCDs/αPD-L1 combination therapy. For local treatment, HACA@QASCDs (5 mg·kg⁻¹) was intratumorally injected on days 0, 5 and 10. For systemic immunotherapy, αPD-L1 antibody (6 mg·kg⁻¹) was intraperitoneally administered on days 1, 6 and 11. During the 14-day treatment period, mice body weight and tumor volume were routinely monitored. Tumor volume was calculated as: V = (length × width²)/2. On day 14, peripheral blood was collected for serum cytokine detection. All mice were euthanized, and major visceral organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) as well as bilateral tumor tissues were harvested for histological staining and tumor-infiltrating immune cell analysis.

Statistical analysis

All experimental assays were independently performed at least three times, and all quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences among multiple groups were analyzed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. A p value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. The significance levels were denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of HACA@QASCDs

HACA@QASCDs were fabricated via a multi-step self-assembly strategy, as illustrated in Scheme 1. Briefly, amine-rich CDs were first synthesized via a solvothermal method using citric acid and urea as precursors and DMSO as the solvent. The obtained CDs were then covalently modified with N, N-dimethyl-1-iodopentyl-dodecyl quaternary ammonium salt (QAS) through an alkylation reaction to afford QASCDs. The chemical structure of QAS was confirmed by ¹H NMR, ¹³C NMR, and high-resolution mass spectrometry (Figures S1–S3). HA was aminated with ethylenediamine after carboxyl activation by EDC/NHS to yield amino-modified HA (HA-NH₂). HA-NH₂ was further reacted with cinnamaldehyde via condensation to form HACA. Finally, QASCDs were encapsulated into the hydrophobic core of HACA micelles through a film hydration method to construct the pH-responsive nanoplatform HACA@QASCDs. To optimize the colloidal stability, systematic screening was performed within the carrier/drug mass ratio range of 2:1–5:1 as previously reported [23]. Taking particle size uniformity, polydispersity index (PDI), and colloidal stability as key indicators, the optimal mass ratio was determined to be 3.5:1, at which the drug loading content of HACA@QASCDs reached 12.5% (Table S1 in the Supporting Information). The critical concentration of the colloid is 0.11 mg·mL⁻¹.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that pristine CDs exhibited uniform spherical morphology with a diameter of 12–22 nm (Fig. 1a). High-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) displayed distinct lattice fringes of 0.21 nm, corresponding to the (100) crystal plane of graphitic carbon (Fig. 1b) [24]. Raman spectroscopy showed an ID/IG ratio of 0.87, indicating a relatively high graphitization degree. X-ray diffraction (XRD) exhibited a broad characteristic peak at 2θ = 26.1°, assigned to the (002) plane of carbon materials (Fig. 1c) [24]. After QAS modification, QASCDs showed irregular morphology with a size range of 40–50 nm (Figures S4a–c), while Raman and XRD patterns remained almost identical to those of CDs. After HACA encapsulation, HACA@QASCDs displayed uniform spherical nanostructures with a diameter of 130–150 nm (Fig. 1d).

The Raman spectrum (ID/IG = 0.87) and XRD pattern of HACA@QASCDs were consistent with those of QASCDs, verifying that the carbon core structure was well preserved during assembly (Fig. 1e and Figure S5a). However, no obvious lattice fringes were observed in the HR-TEM image of HACA@QASCDs, which could be attributed to the shielding effect of the thick polymeric micelle shell. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy showed a broad band at 3100–3650 cm⁻¹ corresponding to O − H and N − H stretching vibrations. The peak at 2800–2900 cm⁻¹ was assigned to alkyl C − H stretching, and the characteristic peak at 1696 cm⁻¹ originated from the overlapped stretching of Schiff base C = N and amide C = O bonds, confirming the successful formation of Schiff base and amide linkages (Figure S5b) [25, 26]. UV-Vis absorption spectra showed that QAS exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at 365 nm, and CDs showed an absorption peak at 341 nm with a broad absorption band at 500–900 nm due to the conjugated system in the carbon core (Fig. 1f) [27, 28]. The HACA polymer exhibits an obvious characteristic absorption peak at 365 nm, which originates from the chromophoric Schiff base structure derived from cinnamaldehyde. After the formation of HACA@QASCDs nanomicelles, the Schiff base chromophores are confined within the interior microenvironment of micelles. The conformational restriction disrupts molecular conjugation and markedly suppresses π–π* electronic transitions. Consequently, the characteristic peak of HACA is drastically attenuated. In contrast, the intrinsic absorption peak of QASCDs carbon dots remains unchanged. CDs, QASCDs and HACA@QASCDs emit red fluorescence under green light excitation (Figure S7). Under excitation at 530 nm, all three samples exhibit fluorescence emission peaks centered near 600 nm, demonstrating that quaternary ammonium modification and HACA micelle encapsulation do not alter the intrinsic energy levels of carbon dots, while the fluorescence intensity decreases in a gradient manner. Pristine CDs display the strongest fluorescence emission, as electron-hole recombination proceeds predominantly via radiative transitions without interference from surface functional groups. After surface functionalization with quaternary ammonium cations to yield QASCDs, the fluorescence intensity declines noticeably. Grafted quaternary ammonium groups trigger intramolecular charge transfer, reducing radiative photon emission and resulting in mild fluorescence quenching. Severe fluorescence quenching is observed for HACA@QASCDs, which originates from aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ) of QASCDs confined within the micellar interior. This stepwise fluorescence quenching indirectly verifies the successful fabrication of the nanocarrier. Moreover, such spectral properties lay a spectroscopic foundation for subsequent intracellular fluorescence tracking and tumor microenvironment-responsive imaging applications.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) verifies that HACA@QASCDs only contain characteristic signals of carbon, nitrogen and oxygen (Fig. 1g). Such elemental composition fully matches the chemical constituents of three synthetic precursors, namely carbon dots, quaternary ammonium modifiers and hyaluronic acid-cinnamaldehyde. No characteristic peaks of exogenous impurities are detected in the survey spectrum. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements showed that the hydrodynamic sizes of CDs, QASCDs and HACA@QASCDs were 44.13 ± 10 nm, 74.53 ± 8 nm, and 170 ± 15 nm, with polydispersity index (PDI) values of 0.15, 0.17 and 0.21, respectively, indicating favorable dispersity (Fig. 1h). Zeta potentials of CDs, QASCDs, and HACA@QASCDs were + 6.5 mV, + 12.6 mV and − 18.4 mV, respectively (Fig. 1i) [29], further verifying successful QAS modification and HACA coating. DLS measurements reveal a gradual increase in hydrodynamic diameters from CDs to QASCDs and HACA@QASCDs, consistent with the stepwise assembly process. Bare CDs possess the smallest particle size. Covalent grafting of quaternary ammonium moieties enlarges particle dimensions to form QASCDs. Subsequent hydrophobic encapsulation of QASCDs inside hyaluronic acid-cinnamaldehyde micelles introduces a hydrophilic HA outer shell, which further raises the overall hydrodynamic size to 170 ± 15 nm. This sequential size variation directly verifies successful encapsulation of QASCDs within micellar carriers. CDs carry weak positive surface charges at + 6.5 mV due to intrinsic surface amino groups. Modification with abundant cationic quaternary groups elevates the zeta potential of QASCDs to + 12.6 mV. Coating with anionic HA chains reverses the surface potential to − 18.4 mV. Such dynamic shifts in surface charge confirm successful conjugation of the HA shell.

HACA@QASCDs remained structurally stable at physiological pH 7.4 but underwent obvious dissociation under acidic pH 5.5 conditions (Figure S8a). Fluorescence responses showed that HACA@QASCDs were quenched at pH 7.4 but recovered fluorescence under acidic conditions (Figure S8b), indicating typical pH-responsive disassembly and drug release behaviors. In vitro release assays demonstrated that the cumulative release of QASCDs reached 62% within 12 h at pH 5.5, whereas only 4% was released at pH 7.4 (Figure S9). Long-term stability evaluation showed that the particle size and zeta potential of HACA@QASCDs remained nearly unchanged in PBS, saline, and cell culture medium over 21 days (Figure S10), revealing excellent physiological stability. To evaluate the applicability of the nanomicelles in blood circulation, we incubated HACA@QASCDs in serum for 48 h and continuously monitored changes in particle size, polydispersity index (PDI) and zeta potential. As shown in Table S2, the hydrodynamic diameter only slightly increased from 166 ± 12 nm to 170 ± 8 nm, the PDI remained below 0.2, and the zeta potential stayed nearly unchanged without obvious particle aggregation. These results demonstrate that the micelles possess excellent colloidal stability in serum and effectively resist protein adsorption, which makes them suitable for intravenous administration in vivo.

HA-CD44-mediated tumor targeting and multidrug resistance reversal

To systematically evaluate the active tumor-targeting capacity of HACA@QASCDs, CD44-highly-expressed drug-resistant colorectal cancer cells (DR‑CT26) and CD44-lowly-expressed normal fibroblasts (L929) were employed as comparative cell models. The cellular internalization profiles were investigated via fluorescence imaging and quantitative analysis based on the intrinsic fluorescence of carbon dots. Both HACA@QASCDs and QASCDs exhibited fluorescence emission in the red channel under green light excitation, and the intracellular fluorescence intensity directly reflected the accumulation level of nanomaterials.

Under identical drug concentration and incubation duration, HACA@QASCDs exhibited significantly enhanced red fluorescence in DR‑CT26 cells compared with QASCDs (Fig. 2a). Semi-quantitative analysis revealed that the intracellular accumulation of HACA@QASCDs was approximately 1.95‑fold higher than that of QASCDs (Fig. 2b). In sharp contrast, no significant difference in fluorescence intensity was observed between the two groups in CD44‑negative L929 normal fibroblasts (Figs. 2c, d). Furthermore, competitive inhibition assay using free HA markedly reduced the cellular uptake of HACA@QASCDs in DR‑CT26 cells (Figure S11), verifying that the internalization was predominantly mediated by the specific interaction between HA and CD44 receptors [30].Cellular uptake results reveal slight non-specific internalization of HACA@QASCDs in CD44-low L929 normal cells, which implies mild off-target effects and potential cellular and in vivo toxicity, representing a limitation of the present system. Nevertheless, benefiting from HA-CD44 targeted recognition, the micelles exhibit abundant accumulation in CD44-overexpressing drug-resistant tumor cells compared with free QAS and bare QASCDs, while their uptake by normal cells is remarkably reduced. Such design effectively alleviates off-target damage and improves the biosafety profile of the nanoplatform.

Subsequently, the in vitro antitumor efficacy and multidrug resistance reversal activity of HACA@QASCDs were systematically evaluated using wild‑type CT26 and drug‑resistant DR‑CT26 cell lines via MTT assay. Cells were treated with HACA@QASCDs at a concentration gradient of 25–200 µg·mL⁻¹, QASCDs at an equivalent drug dosage, and doxorubicin (DOX) as a positive control. In wild‑type CT26 cells, HACA@QASCDs, QASCDs, and DOX all displayed remarkable dose‑dependent cytotoxicity (Fig. 2e). In drug‑resistant DR‑CT26 cells, the cytotoxicity of DOX was drastically diminished, verifying the typical multidrug resistance phenotype. In contrast, HACA@QASCDs and QASCDs still maintained potent killing activity comparable to that in wild‑type cells (Fig. 2f). These results demonstrate that HACA@QASCDs can effectively circumvent conventional chemotherapeutic drug‑associated multidrug resistance via a target‑independent physical cytotoxic mechanism.

HACA@QASCDs-mediated cell membrane perforation and molecular mechanism

To elucidate the interaction pattern between HACA@QASCDs and the cell membrane, DiI membrane fluorescence probe was employed to label the membrane of DR‑CT26 cells [31], and the intrinsic red fluorescence of carbon dots was used to visualize the localization and distribution of nanomaterials on the cell membrane (Figure S13). The fluorescence signals of bare CDs showed negligible co‑localization with the cell membrane, whereas those of QASCDs and HACA@QASCDs overlapped strongly with the cell membrane outline, confirming that both formulations could specifically accumulate on the cell membrane.

SEM images revealed that cell membranes in the blank control group remained intact and smooth with no observable pores. (Fig. 3a, Table S2). QAS treatment induced only sparse and shallow pores, with approximately 5–8 pores per cell and a pore size of 50–80 nm at 12 h. The perforation effect was significantly enhanced in the QASCDs group, with 20–25 pores (100–150 nm) at 12 h and 30–35 pores (150–200 nm) at 24 h, indicating that the nanoscale size of carbon dots potentiates membrane damage. In the HACA@QASCDs group, membrane perforation was most drastic, reaching 35–40 pores (150–200 nm) at 12 h and 45–50 pores (200–300 nm) at 24 h, eventually leading to irreversible structural disruption. These results confirm that HACA@QASCDs enables efficient accumulation and rapid release of QASCDs on tumor cell membranes via CD44‑mediated active targeting and acidic pH‑responsive drug release, thereby triggering robust and irreversible membrane perforation. Free quaternary ammonium salts are small monocationic molecules that weakly attach to cell membranes through electrostatic interactions and readily diffuse away. These compounds exert limited disruption on phospholipid bilayers. In contrast, QASCDs are fabricated with carbon dot backbones covalently grafted with abundant quaternary moieties to form a multi-site electrostatic anchoring architecture. Such structures stably bind to cell membranes. The rigid carbon cores expand gaps between phospholipid molecules and allow multiple hydrophobic alkyl chains to simultaneously disrupt ordered lipid packing. A high local concentration of quaternary groups is sustained at membrane surfaces to generate abundant irreversible pores with enlarged sizes, rendering QASCDs far more potent in triggering membrane perforation than free quaternary ammonium salts. HACA@QASCDs carry negative surface charges derived from hyaluronic acid shells and naturally undergo electrostatic repulsion against anionic tumour cell membranes, yet they display superior membrane damaging capacity relative to free cationic quaternary ammonium salts and bare QASCDs. The outer hyaluronic acid chains specifically bind overexpressed CD44 receptors on DR-CT26 cells. Ligand-receptor recognition counteracts electrostatic repulsion and markedly elevates nanomaterial accumulation on drug resistant tumour cells. After cellular internalisation within acidic tumour microenvironments, Schiff base linkages undergo hydrolytic cleavage to shed hyaluronic acid shells and release concentrated cationic QASCDs. Highly active damaging agents accumulate locally at cell membranes and induce severe structural breakdown. Neither free quaternary ammonium salts nor bare QASCDs possess CD44 targeting capability or acid responsive release behaviour, which leads to inadequate accumulation within tumour lesions. The enhanced damaging effects enabled by targeted accumulation and microenvironment triggered cargo release fully offset the initial electrostatic repulsion, enabling HACA@QASCDs to achieve the strongest membrane perforation activity among all tested formulations.

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assays showed that the fluorescence recovery ratio reached approximately 90% within 180 s in the control group but was only 45% in the HACA@QASCDs group, indicating markedly inhibited lateral mobility of membrane lipids [32]. To further quantify the impairment of membrane barrier integrity, FITC penetration and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assays were performed [33].

FITC fluorescence intensity positively correlated with membrane permeability. The control group exhibited only weak background fluorescence; the QAS group showed mild fluorescence; the QASCDs group displayed significantly enhanced fluorescence; and the HACA@QASCDs group exhibited the strongest fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3c, d), indicating the most severe membrane permeabilization. Meanwhile, LDH release in the HACA@QASCDs group was significantly higher than in all other groups, further verifying irreversible membrane perforation and loss of structural integrity.

Phalloidin F‑actin staining (Fig. 4a) showed intact cytoskeleton and regularly arranged stress fibers in the control and CDs groups, with normal cell morphology. The QAS group exhibited slight cytoskeletal disorganization and locally loose microfilament arrangement. The QASCDs group showed obvious cytoskeletal damage, reduced stress fibers, fractured microfilament networks, and cellular shrinkage. In the HACA@QASCDs group, cytoskeletal collapse was most severe, with nearly complete disappearance of stress fibers, extensive fragmentation of the microfilament network, and remarkable cellular shrinkage, consistent with typical membrane perforation‑induced cytoskeletal disruption [17]. These results demonstrate that HACA@QASCDs‑induced membrane perforation further triggers cytoskeletal depolymerization, which is consistent with SEM, FITC penetration, and LDH release results, collectively verifying QASCDs‑mediated cascade membrane damage.

Collectively, the molecular mechanism of HACA@QASCDs‑mediated membrane perforation is illustrated in Fig. 4b. After CD44‑mediated targeting and accumulation on the DR‑CT26 cell membrane followed by internalization, HACA@QASCDs disassemble in response to the acidic intracellular environment and rapidly release QASCDs. The cationic quaternary ammonium groups on QASCDs exert strong electrostatic interactions with negatively charged phospholipid headgroups, anchoring the nanoparticles stably on the membrane. The long alkyl chains of QAS insert into the hydrophobic core of the lipid bilayer, disturbing the ordered arrangement of lipids, reducing membrane fluidity, and increasing membrane tension. The nanoscale size of carbon dots further widens the gaps between lipid layers and exacerbates structural defects. Driven synergistically by electrostatic anchoring and hydrophobic insertion, the cell membrane undergoes irreversible rupture and forms stable pores. Unlike conventional reversible membrane pores, QASCDs‑induced pores cannot be repaired by lipid rearrangement. The strong anchoring effect of QAS and steric hindrance of carbon dots jointly block membrane reconstruction, ultimately leading to irreversible destruction of the membrane barrier, Ca²⁺ overload, massive LDH release, and cytoskeletal collapse, forming a cascade‑amplified lethal membrane damage effect.

HACA@QASCDs induces mitochondria-dependent apoptotic pathway

To identify the cell death phenotype induced by membrane perforation, apoptosis of DR‑CT26 cells was quantitatively analyzed using Annexin V‑FITC/PI staining and flow cytometry (Fig. 5a). QAS, QASCDs and HACA@QASCDs all significantly promoted cellular apoptosis. Among them, the QASCDs group exhibited an early apoptosis rate of 21.2%, a late apoptosis rate of 4.69%, and a total apoptosis rate of 25.89%. The HACA@QASCDs group showed an early apoptosis rate of 17.5%, a late apoptosis rate of 7.62%, and a total apoptosis rate of 25.12%. At the same incubation duration, the percentage of early apoptotic cells in the free QASCDs group was higher than that in the HACA@QASCDs group, and no significant difference was detected in the total apoptosis level between the two groups. This observation suggests that the HACA polymeric shell can regulate the cellular apoptotic cascade and retard the accumulation of early apoptotic cells. Only static endpoint flow cytometry was utilized in this study, which fails to fully capture the dynamic temporal progression of apoptosis. The above phenomenon only reflects the measurement result at this single endpoint. Follow-up multi-time-point gradient experiments could be performed to further explore the kinetic discrepancies in apoptosis initiation.

To elucidate the molecular pathway underlying HACA@QASCDs‑mediated apoptosis, the ultrastructural changes of mitochondria were observed using transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 5b). In the control group, mitochondria exhibited regular morphology with dense and continuous cristae. After treatment with HACA@QASCDs, mitochondria displayed severe structural damage, characterized by swelling, distorted and broken cristae, and outer membrane collapse, verifying that the nanomaterial directly disrupts mitochondrial structural integrity.

JC‑1 probe analysis showed that the control and CDs groups exhibited predominantly red fluorescence (JC‑1 aggregates), indicating normal mitochondrial membrane potential. In contrast, the proportion of green fluorescence (JC‑1 monomers) was significantly increased after QASCDs and HACA@QASCDs treatment, demonstrating remarkable mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization [34] (Fig. 5c). Fluo‑4 staining revealed that cytosolic free Ca²⁺ levels were significantly elevated in all treatment groups, especially in the HACA@QASCDs group, indicating severe disruption of calcium homeostasis (Fig. 5d). Immunofluorescence assays showed that Cyt C was mainly localized in mitochondria in the control group. After treatment with HACA@QASCDs, Cyt C was massively released into the cytoplasm, accompanied by significantly enhanced fluorescence signals of cleaved‑Caspase‑3 (Fig. 5e, Figure S14). Corresponding quantitative analyses are displayed in Figs. 5g–i. Western blot results were consistent with the immunofluorescence observations. The expression of Cyt C was markedly decreased in the mitochondrial fraction but increased in the cytoplasmic fraction in treatment groups. Meanwhile, the expression levels of Cleaved‑Caspase‑9 and Cleaved‑Caspase‑3 were significantly upregulated, particularly in the HACA@QASCDs group (Fig. 5f). These results are highly consistent with the regulatory pattern of the classical mitochondria‑dependent apoptotic pathway [35], confirming that HACA@QASCDs induces mitochondrial structural damage and dysfunction, thereby triggering membrane potential collapse, calcium overload [36], Cyt C release, and sequential activation of Caspase‑9/‑3, ultimately initiating the mitochondria‑dependent intrinsic apoptotic program.

Collectively, these observations establish a solid mechanistic cascade. HACA@QASCDs disrupt mitochondrial ultrastructure and trigger mitochondrial swelling and cristae rupture. This structural damage further induces mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization and disturbs intracellular calcium homeostasis. These events facilitate the efflux of cytochrome C from mitochondria and sequentially activate the cleavage of caspase-9 and caspase-3, which ultimately initiates the intrinsic mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. Compared with bare QASCDs, HACA-coated nanomicelles exhibit enhanced tumor targeting capacity and inflict more severe mitochondrial damage, thereby achieving a stronger pro-apoptotic effect.

It should be noted that western blot quantification in the current study mainly focuses on the reduction of mitochondrial cytochrome C protein. Immunofluorescence imaging directly visualizes the translocation of cytochrome C from mitochondria to the cytoplasm and supports the occurrence of protein release. Independent quantitative measurement of cytochrome C in cytoplasmic fractions has not been performed, which represents a limitation of the present experiments.

HACA@QASCDs induces ICD in DR-CT26

To systematically investigate whether HACA@QASCDs‑mediated cell death is immunogenic, the hallmark damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) of ICD were detected, including cell‑surface CRT exposure, extracellular ATP release, and extracellular excretion of HMGB1 [37]. Immunofluorescence results showed that the fluorescence intensity of cell‑surface CRT in DR‑CT26 cells was significantly enhanced after treatment with QASCDs and HACA@QASCDs compared with the control group (Fig. 6a). Quantitative analysis revealed that the relative fluorescence intensity of CRT in the HACA@QASCDs group was increased by approximately 33% compared with the QASCDs group (Fig. 6b), which was closely associated with the enhanced tumor‑targeting internalization mediated by HA. Meanwhile, the nuclear fluorescence intensity of HMGB1 was significantly reduced (Fig. 6c and d), indicating massive extracellular release of nuclear HMGB1 [38].

Colorimetric analysis further confirmed that extracellular ATP levels in the culture supernatant were significantly upregulated in all treatment groups (Fig. 6e). Collectively, these results fully demonstrate that HACA@QASCDs can effectively induce CRT exposure, ATP release, and HMGB1 excretion in DR‑CT26 cells, successfully triggering immunogenic cell death in vitro.

HACA@QASCDs remodels the tumor immune microenvironment

DAMPs released during ICD serve as pivotal upstream signals initiating innate and adaptive anti-tumor immune responses, which can systematically reshape the tumor immune microenvironment by regulating the maturation of antigen-presenting cells and the polarization of immune cells [39–41]. In this study, a Transwell non-contact co-culture system was employed to investigate the regulatory effects of DAMPs released from DR‑CT26 cells induced by HACA@QASCDs on DCs maturation and macrophage polarization (Figure S16).

Morphological observations showed that immature DCs in the control group remained round or oval in a resting state. With increased treatment intensity, the cells gradually extended typical dendritic pseudopodia, presenting an irregular activated morphology characteristic of mature DCs, which was most significant in the HACA@QASCDs group (Fig. 7a). Immunofluorescence results revealed that the fluorescence intensities of the co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86 on DCs were upregulated in a treatment-dependent manner, especially in the HACA@QASCDs group (Fig. 7b and c). Quantitative statistical results were consistent with fluorescence observations, showing that the mean fluorescence intensities of CD80 and CD86 increased significantly with treatment concentration (Fig. 7d and e). Flow cytometric analysis further confirmed that the proportion of mature CD80⁺CD86⁺ DCs increased gradually from 6.87% in the control group to 38.4% in the HACA@QASCDs group, showing a remarkable concentration-dependent effect (Fig. 7f). ELISA results demonstrated that the secretion levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL‑6 and TNF‑α were significantly elevated in a concentration-dependent manner, while the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL‑10 was gradually decreased (Fig. 7 g–i). These results confirm that HACA@QASCDs can effectively promote the maturation and activation of DCs by inducing ICD in drug-resistant tumor cells.

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are critical cellular components in the tumor immune microenvironment. M2‑type macrophages dominate the immunosuppressive phenotype, while M1‑type macrophages mediate anti-tumor immune responses, with high plasticity between the two phenotypes [42, 43]. Using the same co-culture model, we further explored the regulatory role of HACA@QASCDs‑mediated ICD in macrophage polarization. Morphological observations showed that RAW264.7 macrophages in the control group exhibited round or spindle‑like resting morphology. After treatment with HACA@QASCDs, the cells displayed irregularly stretched and significantly activated morphology (Fig. 8a). Immunofluorescence results showed that the expression of Arg‑1, a characteristic marker of M2‑type macrophages, was downregulated in a treatment‑dependent manner, most notably in the HACA@QASCDs group (Fig. 8b and c). Flow cytometric analysis revealed that the proportion of M1‑type CD86⁺CD206⁻ macrophages increased markedly from 6.77% in the control group to 55.9%, verifying macrophage polarization toward the anti-tumor M1 phenotype (Fig. 8d). ELISA results showed significantly upregulated pro‑inflammatory cytokines IL‑6 and TNF‑α, alongside decreased anti‑inflammatory IL‑10 (Fig. 8e–g). These results confirm that HACA@QASCDs effectively reverses the immunosuppressive phenotype of M2 macrophages and drives their polarization toward the M1 phenotype.

In summary, HACA@QASCDs induces ICD in drug-resistant tumor cells and releases DAMPs including CRT, ATP, and HMGB1. These signals effectively promote the maturation and activation of DCs for antigen presentation, and reverse the immunosuppressive phenotype of TAMs to facilitate M1 polarization. Thereby, HACA@QASCDs synergistically remodels the tumor immune microenvironment and lays a critical foundation for initiating and amplifying systemic anti-tumor immune responses.

HACA@QASCDs inhibits migration and invasion of drug-resistant DR-CT26 Cells

Tumor invasion and distant metastasis remain the leading causes of clinical therapeutic failure and poor prognosis, while enhanced migration capability constitutes a critical initial step in the metastatic cascade [44, 45]. To systematically elucidate the regulatory effect of HACA@QASCDs on the metastatic potential of drug-resistant tumor cells, the wound-healing assay and Transwell chamber migration assay were performed to evaluate the impact of HACA@QASCDs on the migratory behavior of DR‑CT26 cells.

Wound-healing results showed that the scratch closure rate reached 64.0% within 24 h in the control group. The DOX positive control group exhibited a closure rate of 58.0%, showing no significant difference compared with the control group, indicating that conventional chemotherapeutics hardly inhibit the migration of drug-resistant tumor cells. In contrast, the closure rates were markedly reduced to 34.3% and 22.6% in the QASCDs and HACA@QASCDs groups, respectively, demonstrating that both formulations significantly suppress the lateral migration of DR‑CT26 cells, with HACA@QASCDs showing a more potent inhibitory effect (Figures S4a, 9b). Transwell migration assays further validated these observations. Compared with the control group, the number of cells penetrating the matrix membrane was significantly reduced in both QASCDs and HACA@QASCDs groups, and the inhibitory effect of HACA@QASCDs was more pronounced (Figures S14c–e). Collectively, these results consistently confirm that HACA@QASCDs effectively inhibits the migration of drug-resistant DR‑CT26 cells, exhibiting promising potential for antimetastatic therapy against refractory cancers.

Biosafety and in vivo pharmacokinetic profiles of HACA@QASCDs

Biosafety and in vivo delivery efficiency are essential prerequisites for the in vivo antitumor evaluation of nanomedicines. Herein, the hemocompatibility of HACA@QASCDs was first evaluated via hemolysis assay. Within the concentration range of 0–200 µg·mL− 1, the hemolysis rate of HACA@QASCDs remained below the 5% biosafety threshold, and only 4.3% hemolysis was observed at the highest concentration (200 µg·mL− 1) (Figure S18), indicating no obvious erythrocyte damage. These results confirmed the favorable hemocompatibility of HACA@QASCDs, meeting the safety requirements for subsequent in vivo experiments.

Furthermore, in vivo fluorescence imaging was performed to investigate the tumor-targeting distribution and metabolic behavior of HACA@QASCDs in DR‑CT26 tumor‑bearing mice. Following tail vein injection at a concentration of 10 mg·mL⁻¹, distinct fluorescence signals of HACA@QASCDs were captured at tumour sites as early as 4 h post-administration (Figure S19). The fluorescence intensity peaked sharply at 8 h and remained tightly confined within tumour lesions. Strong fluorescence accumulation persisted in tumours from 16 h to 24 h, and measurable signals were still detectable at 48 h. By contrast, fluorescence faded rapidly in peripheral organs with negligible off-target retention. In vivo imaging of major organs (Figure S20) reinforced this observation. At 8 h after injection, fluorescence predominantly accumulated in tumour tissue, with only minor deposition in the liver and kidneys. Hardly any signal was found in the heart, spleen or lung. Free QASCDs behaved quite differently. Their fluorescence spread diffusely throughout the abdominal cavity and soft tissues after injection. No obvious distinction in signal intensity could be distinguished between tumours and adjacent healthy tissues within 4–16 h. Even at 48 h, most nanoparticles remained trapped in normal organs rather than accumulating preferentially in tumours. Whole-body fluorescence imaging clearly demonstrated the divergent in vivo biodistribution profiles of these two carbon dot formulations. Without targeting ligands, free QASCDs circulated passively in the bloodstream and spread non-selectively across the body. This widespread dispersion lowered the effective drug concentration at tumour foci and inevitably induced undesired damage to healthy tissues. After hyaluronic acid modification, HACA@QASCDs acquired CD44 receptor-mediated active tumour targeting. The nanoparticles specifically anchored to tumour lesions and reached maximum tumour accumulation at 8 h. They were retained sustainably within the tumour microenvironment to maintain high local concentrations, while being rapidly cleared from non-target organs, thereby achieving prominent tumour selectivity. In short, hyaluronic acid functionalisation successfully eliminated the non-specific distribution of bare carbon dots and endowed the nanomaterial with robust tumour-homing capacity. These in vivo distribution data lay a solid foundation for precise anti-tumour therapy with reduced systemic side effects. Continuous monitoring for 72 h confirmed gradual metabolism and clearance of the nanomaterials without obvious long‑term retention (Figure S21), indicating favorable in vivo safety. Further quantitative pharmacokinetic and biodistribution analyses revealed that serum concentrations of HACA@QASCDs peaked at 8–12 h and then declined slowly, demonstrating desirable long‑circulation properties (Figure S22). Collectively, HACA@QASCDs exhibits excellent hemocompatibility, ideal long circulation, efficient tumor targeting, and rapid metabolic clearance with low systemic toxicity, providing a reliable biological basis for subsequent in vivo antitumor therapy.

In vivo antitumor and antimetastatic effects of HACA@QASCDs alone and in combination with αPD-L1

Based on the in vitro apoptotic and antimigratory effects of HACA@QASCDs against DR‑CT26 cells, a bilateral syngeneic drug‑resistant tumor model was established to evaluate the systemic antitumor efficacy in vivo (Fig. 9a) [46]. Seven‑week‑old female BALB/c mice were subcutaneously inoculated with DR‑CT26 cells (1 × 10⁶ cells/100 µL) into the right dorsal flank to establish primary tumors. After 3 days, the same number of cells was injected into the contralateral flank to establish distant tumors. Tumor volume was calculated as V = (L × W²) / 2. When primary tumor volumes reached 100 ± 20 mm³, mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 5): saline, αPD‑L1 monotherapy, QASCDs, HACA@QASCDs, and HACA@QASCDs combined with αPD‑L1. To eliminate direct killing interference caused by nanomaterial diffusion on distant tumors and accurately evaluate systemic immune‑mediated abscopal antitumor effects, local injection was adopted for primary tumors: HACA@QASCDs and QASCDs were administered intratumorally on days 0, 5 and 10 (5 mg·kg− 1); αPD‑L1 was injected intraperitoneally on days 1, 6 and 11 (6 mg·kg− 1). Distant tumors received no direct treatment. Body weight and tumor volume were monitored during treatment, and mice were sacrificed on day 14 for tissue collection.

Therapeutic results showed (Figs. 9b–e) that both primary and distant tumors grew rapidly in the saline group, showing malignant progression typical of drug‑resistant tumors. αPD‑L1 monotherapy exhibited little inhibitory effect, indicating that drug‑resistant tumors were characterized by an immunosuppressive “cold” tumor microenvironment resistant to single immune checkpoint blockade. The QASCDs group showed 37.5% inhibition in primary tumors and 26.3% inhibition in distant tumors. HACA@QASCDs achieved 54.2% primary tumor inhibition and 28.6% distant tumor inhibition, indicating that both formulations suppress distant tumor growth by activating systemic immunity. The combination group showed the most significant antitumor effect: primary tumor growth was almost completely inhibited, and distant tumor inhibition rate reached 68.7%. Mouse body weight remained stable without obvious loss (Fig. 9f). H&E staining of the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney showed no obvious pathological damage (Figure S23), confirming the favorable in vivo biosafety of HACA@QASCDs. The targeting property effectively reduced off‑target toxicity to normal tissues. To further evaluate antimetastatic efficacy, a tail vein pulmonary metastasis model was established. After 14 days of treatment, the number of pulmonary metastatic nodules was 42 and 39 in the saline and αPD‑L1 groups, respectively, with no significant difference. The number decreased to 25 in the QASCDs group, 17 in the HACA@QASCDs group, and further to 7 in the combination group (Fig. 9g). To further evaluate the magnitude of immune responses, we quantified the levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines in peripheral blood samples from each group via ELISA (Fig. 9h–j). Compared with monotherapy groups, the combination treatment with HACA@QASCDs plus αPD-L1 markedly elevated serum concentrations of IFN-γ, TNF-α and IL-6. Robust secretion of these cytokines verified that the combinatorial regimen potently triggered systemic anti-tumor immunity and amplified the immune response elicited by immunogenic cell death. This serves as a critical immunological mechanism underlying the robust suppression of distant tumor progression and pulmonary metastasis.

Collectively, HACA@QASCDs triggers systemic antitumor and abscopal effects by remodeling the immune microenvironment. When combined with αPD‑L1, it significantly enhances immunotherapeutic responses and achieves potent synergistic inhibition of primary tumors, distant tumors and pulmonary metastasis.

HACA@QASCDs combined with αPD-L1 systematically remodels the immune microenvironment of distant tumors

To verify that HACA@QASCDs triggers systemic antitumor immune responses, flow cytometry and ELISA were performed to quantitatively analyze immune cell subsets and cytokine profiles in distant tumor tissues [47, 48]. Mice were divided into five groups: saline control, αPD-L1 monotherapy, QASCDs, HACA@QASCDs, and HACA@QASCDs plus αPD-L1 combination therapy.

Flow cytometric results showed that compared with the control and αPD-L1 groups, the proportions of tumor-infiltrating CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ effector T cells in distant tumors were significantly increased in the QASCDs and HACA@QASCDs groups (Fig. 10a–d). Notably, the percentage of CD8⁺T cells reached the maximum in the combination group, indicating that HACA@QASCDs effectively reverses the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and promotes the infiltration of effector T cells into distant tumors, which can be further amplified by combining with immune checkpoint blockade. Meanwhile, the proportion of immunosuppressive regulatory T (Treg) cells remained at high levels in the control and αPD-L1 groups, decreased in the QASCDs and HACA@QASCDs groups, and was most significantly reduced in the combination group, indicating that the treatment effectively relieves tumor immunosuppression. Further classified tumor-infiltrating T cell subsets according to the expression profiles of CD44 and CD62L. The CD44⁺CD62L⁻ population was defined as effector memory T cells (Tem), which can rapidly exert cytotoxic effects in situ within tumor lesions and serve as the primary effector cells mediating local anti-tumor immune responses. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that the frequencies of Tem cells were only 9.00% and 11.0% in Group I and Group II, with no significant difference between the two groups. With the progressive optimization of therapeutic regimens, the proportion of Tem cells increased to 14.4% in Group III. The percentage of CD44⁺CD62L⁻ effector memory T cells rose sharply to 24.4% in the combination treatment Group IV, whereas this value decreased moderately to 20.5% in Group V with the optimal administration schedule. Quantitative analysis in the bar graph validated the trend observed in dot plots. Compared with the untreated control and monotherapy groups, the nanomedicine combination treatment significantly facilitated the recruitment of effector memory T cells into tumor tissues. The abundant accumulation of immediately activable memory T cells greatly strengthened the cytotoxic immunity within the tumor microenvironment and established long-term anti-tumor immune memory. Notably, Group V achieved the most potent tumor suppression with remarkably reduced tumor volume. The limited lesion space restricted the capacity for infiltrating T cells, leading to a slight decline in the percentage of effector memory T cells. Nevertheless, the absolute number of total memory T cells (central memory T cells plus effector memory T cells) in Group V remained higher than that in Group IV.

Cytokine detection further confirmed the systematic remodeling of the immune microenvironment (Fig. 10e–h): pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-2, IL-6, and TNF-α were significantly upregulated and reached the highest levels in the combination group, while the immunosuppressive cytokine IL-10 showed the opposite trend and dropped to the lowest level in the combination group.

In conclusion, HACA@QASCDs combined with αPD-L1 systematically remodels the immune microenvironment of distant tumors by enhancing effector T-cell infiltration, reducing Treg proportion, inducing immune memory formation, and reshaping the pro-inflammatory cytokine profile, thereby synergistically activating systemic antitumor immunity. These effects constitute the core immunological mechanism underlying the inhibition of distant tumor growth and control of tumor recurrence.

Conclusion

Collectively, this work establishes a versatile and pH‑responsive nanotherapeutic system HACA@QASCDs, composed of hyaluronic acid‑cinnamaldehyde Schiff base micelles encapsulating quaternary ammonium‑functionalized carbon dots. HACA@QASCDs exert robust and target‑independent cytotoxicity toward drug‑resistant colorectal cancer cells via irreversible membrane perforation, further initiating mitochondria‑driven apoptosis and immunogenic cell death, thereby activating systemic antitumor immunity and synergistically suppressing primary tumors, distant lesions, and pulmonary metastasis. This modality circumvents reliance on classical drug targets and endocytic pathways, providing a distinct approach to overcome multidrug resistance and tumor immunosuppression. Mechanistic investigations validate that HACA@QASCDs actively target tumor tissues through HA‑CD44 interactions and achieve stimuli‑responsive QASCDs release in acidic tumor microenvironments. The released QASCDs provoke irreversible membrane disruption via electrostatic interactions, triggering calcium overload, mitochondrial dysfunction, and intrinsic apoptotic signaling. Concurrently, membrane damage‑driven ICD programs promote dendritic cell maturation and macrophage M1 polarization, leading to systematic remodeling of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. In vivo studies using bilateral syngeneic tumor models verify that local treatment of primary tumors elicits a pronounced abscopal antitumor effect. Combination with αPD‑L1 further converts immunologically cold tumors into hot tumors, significantly amplifying antitumor and antimetastatic efficacy. Notwithstanding, the current study is subject to certain limitations, including the use of subcutaneous and experimental metastasis models that incompletely recapitulate clinical pathophysiology, intratumoral injection that differs from standard clinical routes, and relatively short treatment duration that restricts comprehensive assessment of long‑term biosafety and durable anti‑recurrence efficacy. Overall, HACA@QASCDs implement precise therapy against drug‑resistant tumors and distant metastasis via a sequential cascade of membrane perforation, mitochondrial apoptosis, ICD induction, and systemic immune activation. This nanoplatform demonstrates exceptional synergistic therapeutic potential in combination with immune checkpoint blockade. Future investigations will focus on optimizing the delivery system, adopting more clinically relevant tumor models, and performing systematic long‑term safety and anti‑recurrence evaluations, with the ultimate goal of accelerating clinical translation of membrane‑disruptive nanomedicines as safe and effective interventions for drug‑resistant and metastatic malignancies.
