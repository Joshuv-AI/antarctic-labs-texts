Multi-label Contextual Operator Inference in Knowledge Acquisition for Building Generalization: A Multi-modal Learning Approach
Authors: Joris Senn, Zhiyong Zhou, Cheng Fu, Robert Weibel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In traditional map generalization, knowledge acquisition refers to formalizing individual generalization operators and chaining multiple operators into an overall process. It is an essential step towards automating map generalization. To date, machine learning, including advanced deep learning (DL), has been frequently applied to implement individual operators or end-to-end solutions, reducing or even bypassing the intensive effort required to elicit generalization knowledge from expert cartographers. For the example of the generalization of buildings in topographic maps, we focus on the inverse process, that is, how cartographic knowledge acquisition can be performed in DL-based map generalization, a task that has received little attention so far. In particular, we address process recognition and modeling and formulate the decision of whether or not multiple contextual generalization operators, including enlargement, aggregation, typification, and displacement, should be applied to a particular building as a multi-label contextual operator inference task. Instead of using either raster or vector maps to learn map generalization, we propose a multi-modal learning approach to explore the capacity of integrating both map data modalities. We evaluated the approach with national multi-scale map production data in Switzerland, for the transition from 1:25,000 to 1:50,000. The results show that trained DL models achieved F1 scores greater than 0.80 for operator inference except for typification, indicating the promising capacity of DL approaches to acquire procedural knowledge on building generalization. Furthermore, a closer evaluation by operator combinations demonstrates that the multi-modal approach particularly outperforms uni-modal learning in process recognition and modeling involving multiple operators.

Introduction

Map generalization plays a fundamental role in managing a large amount of topographic map data and visualizing it at multiple scales (Duchêne et al. 2014). Since buildings are one of the key object classes represented on topographic maps, the generalization of buildings receives crucial attention within the overall map generalization process. It involves a series of operations, such as simplification, aggregation, and typification, on an individual or groups of buildings to maintain visually meaningful and legible maps when the scale is reduced from a large to a smaller scale (Regnauld and McMaster 2007). Due to the huge volume of data that exist for topographic maps, automated or semi-automated map generalization algorithms, including ones for building generalization, have been, and are being, developed based on knowledge bases (e.g., graphic examples) formalized by expert cartographers (Spiess et al. 2005), constituting one of the most conventional means of cartographic knowledge acquisition (Weibel 1995).

However, knowledge acquisition from cartographic experts is not only labor-intensive but also limited by the vagueness and difficulty of verbal or textual formalization (Weibel et al. 1995). This limitation is particularly magnified in formalizing the generalization process that involves multiple operators (Harrie and Weibel 2007). Machine learning (ML), including advanced deep learning (DL), has great potential to automate knowledge acquisition through the data-driven learning paradigm (Harrie et al. 2024; Kang et al. 2024; Weibel et al. 1995). To date, ML studies related to the explicit automation of knowledge acquisition mainly focus either of functions of structure and pattern recognition (Brassel and Weibel 1988; Sester 2000) preceding the actual generalization steps (Yan et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2022) or on implementations of individual generalization operators, such as selection (Xiao et al. 2024b) and simplification (Du et al. 2022; Yan and Yang 2024; Zhou et al. 2023). However, the generalization process modeling that involves decisions extending to multiple operators has received little attention. Although some studies have introduced end-to-end DL methods to generalize raster maps (Courtial et al. 2023; Feng et al. 2019; Fu et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024), they offer little insight into acquiring knowledge about the generalization process.Fig. 1Graphical illustration of dominant building generalization operators (map data © swisstopo). To overcome the aforementioned limitation, we formalize a new deep learning task for knowledge acquisition in building generalization, termed multi-label contextual operator inference, akin to the process recognition and process modeling steps of the conceptual model of map generalization by Brassel and Weibel (1988). The task aims to infer whether or not particular building generalization operators should be applied to the generalization of a given building based on its surrounding context. As shown in Fig. 1, six operators, including simplification, elimination, enlargement, aggregation, typification, and displacement, are predominantly involved in building generalization. While simplification focuses on the reduction of geometric complexity of individual buildings and elimination typically takes place as a pre-processing step (Spiess et al. 2005, p. 42), the application of the remaining four operators—enlargement, aggregation, typification, and displacement—is commonly guided by contextual relationships of a building with other map features, and they often occur in combination with each other, necessitating the concurrent inference of multiple operators, rather than in isolation. Hence, we deal with these four contextual operators in this article.

In deep learning for map generalization, raster and vector maps have different advantages as input. The former offers a unified way to encode map objects (Feng et al. 2019; Yu and Chen 2022), while the latter is more concise in representing the contextual relationships among map objects and thus more efficient in learning generalization (Cui et al. 2025). Therefore, an intuitive hypothesis is that the integration of these two modalities of map data can further benefit the multi-label contextual operator inference task in knowledge acquisition for building generalization as defined above. The idea of such an integration of more than one data modality for the downstream learning task is typically called multi-modal learning (Baltrušaitis et al. 2018). It is not new to many subdomains of geospatial AI, such as remote sensing and urban computing (Mai et al. 2025), but its capacity for computational cartography, including map generalization, has rarely been explored.

In this article, we propose a multi-modal learning model for multi-label operator inference in knowledge acquisition for building generalization. Given a map depicting street blocks containing buildings and streets that need to be generalized when producing a map of a smaller scale, our approach simultaneously adopts a three-channel raster data model and a graph data model to represent the street block map and inputs them to extract raster and vector map features, respectively. A four-head classification module is designed to infer the four operators for building generalization mentioned above. Related Work

Process Modeling of Map Generalization

Map generalization process modeling refers to the chaining or coordination of multiple elementary generalization operators (e.g., simplification and aggregation) with certain control parameters (Harrie and Weibel 2007). In traditional map generalization, the overall generalization process is seen holistically by simultaneously considering multiple map objects and applying multiple generalization operators to resolve potential conflicts of map legibility (Brassel and Weibel 1988). This requires a higher level of map knowledge acquisition than defining individual operators (Müller 1991; Sarjakoski 2007). Traditional process modeling is explicitly defined by expert cartographers, with support from computer systems (Harrie and Weibel 2007). It mainly follows three paradigms: condition-action modeling, human interaction modeling, and constraint-based modeling.

To date, the constraint-based modeling paradigm has resulted in the most satisfying results with the least human intervention needed for map generalization. In such a paradigm, the map specifications can be formalized in the form of various constraints that must be met to the best possible extent during an iterative generalization process (Beard 1991). This paradigm allows for the evaluation of different strategies that support backtracking should the result not be satisfactory (Harrie and Weibel 2007; Mackaness 1995). The best-performing methodology for constraint-based map generalization is the agent-based approach that models map objects as a set of communicating and interacting agents whose target is to achieve an optimal generalization state for themselves and other map objects (Barrault et al. 2001; Duchêne et al. 2018; Ruas and Duchêne 2007; Sarjakoski 2007). In production environments, often the technically demanding agent-based approach has been replaced by a workflow approach (Stoter et al. 2014). Moreover, other optimization techniques such as genetic algorithms (Wilson et al. 2003), least-squares adjustment (Sester 2005), elastic beams (Bader et al. 2005), and simulated annealing (Ware et al. 2003) have also been applied to constraint-based map generalization.

Machine Learning for Building Generalization

Beyond rules or constraints that are clearly defined by expert cartographers, a lot of generalization knowledge, including process recognition and modeling, is implicit and difficult to describe and parameterize, even though expert cartographers can generalize example cases. This limitation has been called the cartographic knowledge acquisition bottleneck (Sester 2000; Weibel et al. 1995). Thus, machine learning (ML) techniques were applied to existing generalized maps to learn the implicitly encoded knowledge infused by expert cartographers during manual map generalization (Sester 2020). It is essentially reverse engineering the generalization process. Initial generalization approaches based on ML were concerned with the structure characterization of individual map objects (Steiniger et al. 2008), pattern or relation recognition from multiple map objects (Sester 2000; Steiniger et al. 2008), parameter estimations for generalization operators (Lagrange et al. 2000; Plazanet et al. 1998; Weibel et al. 1995), and implementations of individual generalization operators (Cheng et al. 2015, 2013).

The advancement of deep learning has offered a new paradigm for automated map generalization (Touya et al. 2019), as it has a superior capability over traditional machine learning in processing raw map data and requires less feature engineering to achieve satisfactory performance. The existing DL studies for map generalization are mainly aimed at the generalization of topographic features, including buildings and roads. Particularly, these studies predominantly focus on end-to-end generalization for raster building maps (Courtial et al. 2023; Feng et al. 2019; Fu et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024) without considering intermediate procedural knowledge, with the exception of Fu et al. (2024), who made some first steps in this area. For the generalization of vector mode maps, work has focused on implementations of individual building generalization operators, such as aggregation (Yan et al. 2022) and simplification (Du et al. 2022; Jiang et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2023). Moreover, the multiple modalities of maps have commonly been ignored in the research on automated map generalization (Janowicz et al. 2025), although several attempts have fused multiple modalities of other geospatial data, such as remote sensing images and language (Liu et al. 2024; Vivanco Cepeda et al. 2023), and vector geometry and textual descriptions of points of interest (Balsebre et al. 2024).

Research Gaps

In summary, process modeling of map generalization today typically utilizes constraint-based approaches, combined with optimization techniques, or workflow-based approaches, and involves a set of constraints with certain parameters. Deep learning is promising for the acquisition of map generalization knowledge, including building generalization. However, important research gaps remains.

First, apart from rather simple constraints such as those governing map legibility, explicitly modeling constraints is difficult. And modeling the procedural knowledge, that is, the generalization operators, algorithms, and parameter settings resulting from violations of constraints, is even harder. Furthermore, constraints are fixed and thus lack the flexibility to adapt to the varying scenarios of map generalization.

Second, prior studies on DL-based map generalization focused mainly on end-to-end solutions, but rarely on the entire process of building generalization, including multiple operators. This leads to a lack of operator-specific guidance for learning generalization, in particular with respect to procedural knowledge acquisition.

And third, although maps can be represented in multiple data modalities (i.e., raster or vector), existing DL approaches solely learn map generalization in a uni-modal form. It remains unknown to what extent the multi-modal learning paradigm can benefit building generalization, specifically the multi-label operator inference task formulated in this study.

Methodology

Concepts and Terminology

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the operators commonly applied in building generalization mainly include simplification, elimination, enlargement, aggregation, typification, and displacement. In this article, we focus mainly on the latter four so-called context-dependent operators whose application is guided by the surrounding cartographic features of a given building. The simplification operator is excluded from our study because its application modifies the geometries of individual buildings and is therefore not significantly affected by the presence of contextual buildings. We also did not include the elimination operator because it typically occurs before other operators in a sequential generalization process (Spiess et al. 2005, p. 42) and thus is mutually exclusive with the other operators, although it is context-dependent. Another reason for not considering elimination is that this study focuses on the medium-scale transition from 1:25,000 (source scale) to 1:50,000 (target scale), where elimination does not dominate the mix of generalization operators (Cecconi et al. 2002).

The four contextual operators considered here (Fig. 1) can be defined as follows (Regnauld and McMaster 2007; Stanislawski et al. 2014):

Definition 1

(Enlargement) The area of a building is increased to compensate for scale reduction, while maintaining shape clarity. (Aggregation) Two or more buildings of the source-scale map are represented as a new placeholder building in the target-scale map with a similar shape, orientation and aggregate size as the source group of buildings. Additionally, we need to define several concepts that are used in the problem formulation below as well as in the subsequent description of the proposed methodology (for an illustration, see Fig. 2a):

Definition 5

(Street block) An area enclosed by the road network on all sides, denoted by st.blk. The roads bounding the street block, including any dead-end roads lying within the block, are termed context roads, denoted by ct.rds.

Definition 6

(Focal building) Given a street block of buildings, a building that is aimed to be generalized is termed the focal building, denoted by fc.bldg.

Definition 7

(Context buildings) Given a street block of buildings, the remaining ones, except the focal building, are termed the context buildings to characterize the surroundings for generalization of the focal buildingn, denoted by ct.bldgs.

Inspired by the various advantages of raster and vector data formats in representing map information, we introduce the multi-modal deep learning paradigm to extract informative features from different map data and propose a multi-head classifier for contextual operator inference in building generalization. As illustrated in Fig. 3, a three-channel raster data model and a heterogeneous graph data model (built on top of a vector map) are proposed to represent the information of individual map objects and spatial relationships among features in a street block map, respectively. Subsequently, they are input to a convolutional neural network (CNN) and a graph convolutional neural network (GCNN) to extract informative raster features and graph features of the street block map, followed by a concatenation of the features of the two data modalities. Finally, the concatenated features are passed to four classification heads, each of which infers the presence of a building generalization operator with a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) module. Data Models for Multi-modal Maps

Two data modalities of maps are considered for the contextual building generalization operator inference: raster map and vector map. We developed a three-channel raster data model to encode the raster map information and a heterogeneous graph data model to represent the object information given in the vector map.

Three-channel Raster Data Model

Following the layer representation practices used in Courtial et al. (2022) and Fu et al. (2024), a given map of a street block, containing a group of buildings bounded by the road network, is encoded into three channels. As illustrated in Fig. 4 the three channels store the focal building to be generalized, the context buildings, and the context roads, respectively. In practice, the first step is to extract and separate the vector geometries of these map objects. The bounding box (BBOX) of the focal building’s street block is subsequently used to calculate the pixel resolution necessary for the street block to fit into a raster with a side length of 256 pixels according to Eq. 1.1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {resolution} = \frac{\max (\text {BBOX width}, \text {BBOX height})}{256}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Next, the three separate map layers are rasterized using the computed resolution to generate three binary rasters that indicate the presence or absence of the focal building, context buildings, and context roads, respectively, at a given pixel. The created rasters are subsequently padded in the direction of the shorter side of the street block BBOX until they reach a common target shape of 256 x 256 pixels. The extent of all computed rasters corresponds to the extent of the street block. The raster shape of 256 x 256 pixels is empirically chosen given the map scale according to the suggestions provided by Touya et al. (2019), as it offers a good compromise between the sharpness of building boundaries and computational cost. Lastly, the three computed rasters are stacked together according to the proposed three-channel raster data model.Fig. 4Graphical workflow of the proposed three-channel raster data model and its construction

Heterogeneous Graph Data Model

In a vector map, the most important spatial information for contextual building generalization operator inference is the spatial relationships between map objects. Considering the different roles of a focal building, context buildings, and context roads within a street block, a heterogeneous graph data model is proposed. From a conceptual perspective, this is analogous to the outlined three-channel raster data model, as such models allow for the representation of multiple types of nodes and edges (Zhang et al. 2019) and therefore can capture the relationships between buildings and roads within a street block.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, the proposed heterogeneous graph is composed of three types of nodes and four types of edges. Given the map of a street block, the focal building, context building, and road node are assigned to three types of nodes, such that they can be distinguished similarly to the proposed three-channel raster data model. The nodes representing the focal and context buildings are both geometrically extracted from the centroids of the buildings. Then, the Delaunay triangulation (DT) is constructed using the building centroids as seeds, and a proximity graph can be derived from the DT network, similar to the approach in Yan et al. (2019). As a consequence, two types of edges (FC: focal building ↔ context building; CC: context building ↔ context building) are constructed from the proximity graph of the buildings in the street block and added to the heterogeneous graph.Fig. 5Graphical workflow of the proposed heterogeneous graph data model and its construction

Graphical workflow of the proposed heterogeneous graph data model and its construction

To represent the spatial relationships between buildings and roads in a street block, some vertices of the polyline geometries of context roads are sampled. As shown in Step 2 of Fig. 5, the Voronoi polygon based on its polygon vertices is generated, representing its region of influence on the other buildings within the street block (Yan et al. 2019). If the Voronoi polygon generated from the building touches the road network, the intersection between the Voronoi polygon and the road network determines the road segment associated with the respective building. Road nodes are determined by computing the intersection points between the road segment and the major and minor axes of the minimum bounding rectangle (MBR) of the building polygon. Afterward, in Step 3 of Fig. 5, the road nodes are subsequently connected to the building by an edge, ensuring that the orientation of the building is implicitly preserved in the graph structure. Road nodes are not connected to each other, as information associated with roads is only expected to be propagated to buildings during the learning process, rather than among the roads themselves. Such a process leads to two additional types of edges in the heterogeneous graph: FR (focal building ↔ road) edge and CR (context building ↔ road) edge.

Based on the built heterogeneous graph, 10 features are used to characterize different aspects of individual nodes and their contextual relationships, which are commonly used in automated map generalization. As shown in Table 1, the building nodes are described by five properties, including position, size, shape, density, and orientation, and each property is described by two features. Apart from position, the building node features cannot be easily transferred to road segments; therefore only position is chosen to characterize the road nodes. Additionally, the features from the distance property are used as edge weights for all edges in the graph. Multi-modal Map Fusion

Given the great potential of multi-modal deep learning in significantly improving the performance of a wide range of vision and natural language recognition tasks (Huang et al. 2020; Mroueh et al. 2015), this study introduces a multi-modal learning paradigm to use the complementary information provided by the raster and vector map data modalities to make more informed contextual operator inference to generalize a focal building. The key to multi-modal learning is to fuse the information of each data modality. In this study, we implemented it based on a late fusion approach, which involves the integration of the outputs of separate models previously trained on individual modalities to construct an ensemble model that is capable of processing both modalities concurrently (Gadzicki et al. 2020). As illustrated in Fig. 3, the extracted raster features and graph features are directly concatenated into a fused feature. The following sub-sections detail the process used to extract features of each map data modality.

Raster Feature Extraction

In our method, we adopted a classical and significant CNN architecture, AlexNet (Krizhevsky et al. 2017), as a backbone to extract raster map features from the three-channel raster data model. The rationale for using AlexNet is that the network has gained prominence for its exceptional performance in various conventional image classification tasks, while a prior study (Winkler 2023) has empirically demonstrated that a more sophisticated network does not necessarily improve the performance of raster map generalization. As shown in Fig. 6, the adopted CNN architecture is composed of five layers. Each of the first two layers and the last layer contains a convolutional module, an activation module, and a pooling module, while the middle Layers 3 and 4 do not include the pooling module to preserve potentially valuable raster map features.Fig. 6The AlexNet CNN architecture used for raster feature extraction

The AlexNet CNN architecture used for raster feature extraction

Graph Feature Extraction

Given a heterogeneous street block graph, it is natural to learn representations that respect the semantic meaning of each node and edge type through heterogeneous graph neural networks (HGNNs) (Gong et al. 2024; He et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2022). In this study, as shown in Fig. 7, an HGNN composed of three heterogeneous graph convolutional (HeteroConv) layers (Zhang et al. 2019) is employed to extract the features of nodes contained in the heterogeneous street block graph. In each HeteroConv layer, for each node, a certain number of graph convolution modules are defined based on the types of edges connected to it. Then, the features from its neighboring nodes will be passed through these edge-specific graph convolution modules and aggregated to update the node’s features. As shown in the dashed-line box of Fig. 7, given a focal building node connecting a context building node and a road node, the features of the context building node will be processed through an FC SAGEConv module (Zhou et al. 2023), while the features of the road node will be processed through another graph convolutional module, FR SAGEConv. Afterward, the processed node features of the two types will be aggregated to update the focal building node features. Notably, we used the mean aggregation method for this purpose.Fig. 7The three-layer HGNN architecture used for graph feature extraction

The three-layer HGNN architecture used for graph feature extraction

MLP Classification Heads

After removing the classification heads, both the raster CNN and graph HGNN produce fixed-length feature embeddings for each sample: the CNN output is flattened into a one-dimensional vector, and the HGNN output corresponds to the embedding of the target node type. As shown in Fig. 3, the extracted raster features and the graph features are concatenated along the feature dimension, forming a joint representation whose size equals the sum of the raster and graph feature dimensions. This concatenated vector is then passed to four MLP classification heads, each of which will predict whether a specific map generalization operator should be applied to a focal building or not, by integrating complementary information from both modalities. For each classification head, the MLP is a three-layer structure consisting of a fully connected input layer, a ReLU hidden layer, and a fully connected output layer. The optimization of each MLP classification head’s parameters is based on the binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss. Experimental Setup

Data

The building geometries used in this study originate from the DKM25 and DKM50 products of swisstopo (DKM = digitales kartografisches Modell in German, or digital cartographic model), containing vector geometries of all buildings across Switzerland, generalized to 1:25,000 and 1:50,000, respectively. Figure 8 illustrates the buildings at the two scales for a small map section. The scale transition from 1:25,000 to 1:50,000 was chosen since a mixture of contextual operators are predominantly applied in this scale range (Regnauld and McMaster 2007; Roth et al. 2011). By contrast, scale transitions above 1:25,000 are typically characterized by the application of operators displaying independent elements such as simplification or elimination, whereas transitions below 1:50,000 mainly involve typification above all other operators. While the DKM25 data are openly accessible under the product name Swiss Map Vector 25 (SMV25),3 the DKM50 data have not been publicly available and were made available to us by swisstopo upon request. The generalization process to produce these DKM datasets is conducted semi-automatically at swisstopo based on a commercial software system extended by bespoke cartographic tools configured to reach specific generalization objectives. As the tools of the semi-automated production process are applied under the control of expert cartographers, the results of this process are continuously validated visually and can thus be considered representing the approach and outcome of expert cartographers. Overall, the system achieves satisfactory generalization rates for roughly 80% of map features, necessitating manual edits for the remaining 20% (Duchêne et al. 2014). For the case of buildings, human intervention is particularly necessary in dense urban areas, where complex generalization situations may arise, as individual buildings are retained in the transition to DKM50.Fig. 8Buildings generalized to the investigated source and target scales (map data © swisstopo)

Buildings generalized to the investigated source and target scales (map data © swisstopo)

Given the building geometries at the scales of 1:25,000 and 1:50,000, we partitioned the dataset into street blocks. All very large street blocks—mostly located in rural areas—containing more than 75 buildings at 1:25,000 were discarded, leaving 121,422 street blocks with 1,750,468 buildings. This corresponds to roughly 85% of the buildings in the Swiss National Maps at 1:25,000. For these retained buildings, the presence or absence of the four generalization operators in the scale transition from 1:25,000 to 1:50,000 was annotated using a semi-automated workflow, the details of which can be found in (ANONYMIZED_2). The outcome of this workflow was an initial version of a dataset to be used for training and validation of the proposed methodology for multi-modal contextual operator inference.

However, due to the high imbalance in the occurrence of the four individual generalization operators derived from the dataset (see Fig. 9(a)), we applied a label powerset (LP)-based hybrid resampling technique (Charte et al. 2013, 2015) to obtain a balanced dataset (see Fig. 9(b)). As a consequence, this resulted in a sample of 125,000 non-eliminated focal buildings, 80% of which were taken as the training set, while the remaining 20% formed the validation set. Furthermore, a separate, imbalanced test set was derived by randomly drawing a sample of 25,000 non-eliminated buildings from the original dataset that excludes the training and validation sets, which better reflects the model’s ability to classify the operators on a real map, where operators are highly imbalanced.Fig. 9Frequency distribution of contextual generalization operators detected in the training dataset before and after data balancing

Frequency distribution of contextual generalization operators detected in the training dataset before and after data balancing

The proposed multi-modal model was implemented in PyTorch and PyTorch Geometric (PyG). Given the large number of 100,000 training samples, a large batch size of 512 is chosen to train the models, which accelerates processing times. Early stopping was used to determine the appropriate number of epochs for model training by identifying the epoch at which the model starts to exhibit overfitting for the validation dataset (Ying 2019). We adopted the commonly used classification evaluation metrics, including precision, recall, and F1 score, to evaluate the performance of the models for multi-label contextual operator inference in building generalization. Discussion

Advantages of Multi-modal Learning

The global evaluation of individual operators, as elaborated in Section Global Evaluation, demonstrates that all three modalities exhibit similar capabilities for the inference of the four individual operators involved in building generalization, although our proposed multi-modal model achieved slightly higher performance in more metrics compared with the CNN and HGNN models. However, synthesizing the quantitative evaluations on more realistic generalization scenarios with operator sets in Section Operator Combinations and their correspondence with the number of occurrences as mapped in Fig. 11, the advantages of multi-modal learning become clearer. Specifically, the multi-modal model outperforms the other two models for the most frequent operator combination of enlargement and displacement (the dark green group in Fig. 11), and the third most frequent set containing enlargement, aggregation, and displacement (the purple group in Fig. 11). For the second most frequent set involving all four operators (in orange), as well as the other, less frequent sets, the proposed multi-modal model does not show an obvious advantage over the pure raster or the vector models—yet still reaches (close to) the top performance in most cases.Fig. 11Relationship between operator combination prevalence and F\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_1$$\end{document} score

Performance in Different Spatial Configurations

Following the results of the stratified evaluation in Section Stratified Evaluation, all models exhibit an increase in performance with increasing street block area and on buildings in rural street blocks, except the raster model on enlargement. This finding is intuitive, since buildings in rural areas tend to be part of street blocks with much larger areas, as illustrated in Fig. 12. Furthermore, the findings can be considered representative since the training database evidently contains similar numbers of buildings in urban and rural contexts. In Section Evaluation Metrics, it was hypothesized that especially the raster data model may be susceptible to a scale effect, whereby performance would deteriorate with increasing area of street blocks due to the associated decrease in resolution. However, the results presented in Table 5 indicate that the opposite is true, as classification performance improves with increasing street block area.Fig. 12Street block area for buildings in urban and rural street blocks

Street block area for buildings in urban and rural street blocks

Although there is no clear explanation for this inverse scale effect from a technical perspective, it can likely be attributed to insights derived from traditional map generalization practice. First, the higher performance observed for buildings within large street blocks can be linked to the fact that these blocks are predominantly situated in rural areas. Compared to urban contexts, the generalization of buildings in rural areas is generally considered an easier task, as the density of map objects is much lower and thus there is more room available to ‘maneuver’ and the operator combinations required tend to be less complex (Mustière and Moulin 2002; Ruas and Mackaness 1997; Spiess et al. 2005). Second, larger street blocks provide additional cartographic context that can facilitate the decision regarding operators. Transferring these insights to a DL context, it seems logical that the models are more adept at performing the generalization operator prediction for buildings located in rural areas.

Influence of Road Network

As elaborated in Section Influence of Road Network, for all three modalities, the ROC AUC values for enlargement and aggregation are not significantly affected by the removal of road-related features. This is consistent with expectations from traditional generalization practice, as the application of these two operators is not expected to be considerably influenced by the layout of the road network. For the raster and multi-modal models, the removal of road-related features evidently leads to a substantial decrease in ROC AUC for the displacement operator, implying better performance on this operator if the roads are included for training and prediction. Again, this observation is in line with traditional generalization practices, as the enlargement (i.e., widening) of road symbols when transitioning to smaller scales commonly necessitates the displacement of buildings situated along roads. This phenomenon is illustrated in Fig. 13, whereby the amount of available map space dictates the application of displacement. A similar effect can be observed for the vector-based model, which exhibits a slight decrease in performance for typification when roads are excluded (note: typification invariably also includes displacement). Again, this is intuitive, as the layout of the road network constrains the amount of available map space, which is critical for determining building density and therefore also the decision of whether typification should be applied or not (Lee 1996; Regnauld and McMaster 2007).Fig. 13Application of the displacement operator necessitated by the generalization (i.e., enlargement of symbol width) of the roads (map data © swisstopo)

Application of the displacement operator necessitated by the generalization (i.e., enlargement of symbol width) of the roads (map data © swisstopo)

However, the fact that the performance of the graph model does not change for the enlargement and displacement operators when road-related features are excluded suggests that the developed graph construction method may not be the optimal solution to encode structural knowledge associated with roads. Nonetheless, in general, the results obtained demonstrate that including additional cartographic context in the form of the road network during the training process of the different DL models has the potential to increase the quality of building generalization operator inference. Importantly, the findings further suggest that DL models can learn cartographic knowledge that is consistent with traditional generalization practices.

Implications, Limitations, and Future Research

Implications

Overall, the study reported in this article has demonstrated how deep learning models can be used to infer contextual operators for building generalization and thus how DL can be used to learn and acquire cartographic knowledge. We consider this in itself a major contribution, along with the findings regarding the success of multi-operator learning, as well as the superior performance of the multi-modal model compared to the uni-modal models. In a real-world automated map generalization scenario, the model can assist in, or even free cartographers from, defining rules and parameters to decide building generalization operators on a case-by-case basis, which is typically followed by using specific algorithms for inferred operators to carry out the generalization process. This process can not only take full use of existing human-made multi-scale maps, but also improve the level of automation of map generalization with varying building contexts.

Limitations

Nevertheless, there is still room for improvement. The following assessment lists a range of limitations of this present work, none of which we consider a major impediment, however. First, the proposed approach is limited from a conceptual point of view, as it assumes that buildings are generalized individually rather than collectively. Therefore, it cannot account for cartographic conflicts and inconsistencies evoked by interference or knock-on effects between operators. From the perspective of the DL models, the experiments suggest that the multi-modal model does not show a monotonously decreasing trend in the training loss curve and is prone to overfitting (see Fig. 10), which may be mainly caused by the poor capacity in inferring the occurrence of the typification operator while the used BCE loss should sum over all types of contextual operators. Additionally, the imbalanced co-occurrence of contextual operators in the dataset may have imposed further difficulties on the training of the model, although the total volumes of the individual operators had been resampled and balanced. Also, the proposed approach does account for the potential interaction crossing raster and graph modalities, which may impede the upper bound of multi-modal learning (Chen et al. 2019; Wenderoth et al. 2025). Furthermore, the encoding procedure of street blocks as heterogeneous graphs conceived for the vector model is likely too complex to be efficient. Finally, the stratified sampling approach devised to address the imbalance present within the frequency distribution of the generalization operators displays limited effectiveness due to excessive oversampling.

Future Research

To address the identified limitations, future studies should more systematically seek to explore novel DL architectures (e.g., attention-based models, Manzoor et al. 2023; and foundation models, Lu et al. 2025; Zhao et al. 2025) that are capable of simultaneously predicting the presence or absence of individual operators for all buildings in a street block, while this study mainly aimed to identify a new learnable task by DL models for research in map generalization. Additionally, the remaining problems associated with operator imbalance call for the development of tailored resampling and data augmentation approaches, the exploration of alternative loss functions, and the implementation of ensemble methods. The encoding of additional procedural domain knowledge and the application of techniques from the domain of explainable AI have the potential to make meaningful contributions to knowledge acquisition and to the explainability of DL-based map generalization approaches (Fu et al. 2024). Finally, future research should strive to investigate alternative learning frameworks leveraging training datasets with fuzzy labels, such as pseudo-labeling learning (Zhao et al. 2023, 2025), and to develop sophisticated evaluation strategies that better capture the ambiguities intrinsic to the generalization process. All of these developments are paramount to truly ushering in DL as the next paradigm for end-to-end automated map generalization.

Conclusions

This article focused on knowledge acquisition for map generalization using deep learning models, and as such opened a new line of research that was previously untouched in the literature. We formulated a new deep learning task, multi-label contextual operator inference, for process recognition and modeling in building generalization. Given the different advantages of raster and vector maps for generalization, we hypothesized that the integration of the two types of map data modalities could further benefit the multi-label contextual operator inference task. Hence, we proposed a multi-modal learning model to infer four contextual generalization operators commonly used on buildings, including enlargement, aggregation, typification, and displacement, after concatenating the raster and vector features extracted by an AlexNet model and an HGNN model.
