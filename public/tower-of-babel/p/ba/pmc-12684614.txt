Association of Ezetimibe and Statin Combinations With Recurrent Ischemic Stroke: A Nationwide Study With Dual Design
Authors: Da Hoon Lee, Yoon‐A Park, Seo‐A Choi, Jung Sun Kim, Jeong Yee, Hye Sun Gwak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStatin is recommended for patients with ischemic stroke (IS) to lower low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol. Although combining statin with ezetimibe further reduced low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, its impact on IS recurrence has varied by statin type.MethodsWe used 2 study designs—a retrospective cohort and a case–control study—using the Korean Health Insurance Review and Assessment database. Patients hospitalized for IS between July 2017 and July 2021 who were treated with atorvastatin or rosuvastatin were included. The cohort study compared recurrent IS risk between ezetimibe users and nonusers, using 1:1 propensity score matching for baseline characteristics. The case–control study assessed ezetimibe use between recurrent cases with IS and age‐, sex‐, index year‐matched controls. Cox proportional hazards and logistic regression models were used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and adjusted HRs (aHRs) for the cohort study, as well as odds ratios (ORs) and adjusted ORs (aORs) for the case–control study. We conducted several sensitivity analyses.ResultsAmong 26 937 patients with IS (16 215 atorvastatin, 10 722 rosuvastatin), the cohort study showed ezetimibe combined with atorvastatin significantly reduced recurrent IS incidence (aHR, 0.73 [95% CI, 0.55–0.98], P=0.037), whereas rosuvastatin showed no significant difference (aHR, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.80–1.24], P=0.985). The case–control study confirmed ezetimibe's protective effect with atorvastatin (aOR, 0.74 [95% CI, 0.58–0.93], P=0.012) but not with rosuvastatin (aOR, 1.01 [95% CI, 0.83–1.24], P=0.903). Sensitivity analyses supported these findings.ConclusionsEzetimibe combined with atorvastatin was associated with lower recurrent IS rates compared with atorvastatin alone, whereas its addition to rosuvastatin showed no significant association.

Cardiovascular diseases are the leading cause of death globally, with stroke playing a major role in morbidity and frequent hospitalizations. 1 The most common type of stroke, ischemic stroke (IS), is primarily caused by atherothrombotic processes and cardioembolic events. 2 Dyslipidemia, a significant risk factor for atherothrombotic events such as IS, is primarily managed by statins. 3

Statins—particularly atorvastatin and rosuvastatin—are widely recognized for their effectiveness in lowering low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) and decreasing the risk of cardiovascular events, including first‐time and recurrent IS. 4 , 5 , 6 Results from a meta‐analysis showed that statin treatment reduced the risk of recurrent IS by ~20% in patients who have experienced IS. 7 However, approximately 9.1% of statin users encountered challenges in reaching optimal outcomes due to statin intolerance, which hinders the effectiveness of statin monotherapy. 8 Increasing statin dosage to enhance effectiveness may be constrained by potential side effects such as muscle pain and liver toxicity, further restricting the uses of statin monotherapy in managing cardiovascular disease. 9 , 10 An alternative approach involves combining medications.

Ezetimibe—a selective inhibitor of the Niemann‐Pick C1‐like 1 protein in the intestine—has emerged as a complementary approach for managing LDL‐C levels by reducing the absorption of dietary and biliary cholesterol. 11 , 12 When combined with statin therapy, ezetimibe demonstrably achieved an additional 15% reduction in LDL‐C levels as compared with statin monotherapy. 13 In a study involving patients with coronary artery disease whose LDL‐C levels were >70 mg/dL, those who added ezetimibe to their regimen exhibited significantly lower LDL‐C at 12 and 54 weeks than those doubling their statin dose. 14 Additionally, in the Treat Stroke to Target trial, researchers examined the association between the occurrence of major cardiovascular events (MACE) and LDL‐C levels, comparing target LDL‐C levels of <70 mg/dL (lower‐target group) versus 90 to 110 mg/dL (higher‐target group) during lipid‐lowering treatment. 15 The results showed that 65.9% of patients in the lower‐target group and 94.0% in the higher‐target group were treated only with statins; 33.8% and 5.8% of patients, respectively, received a combination of ezetimibe and statins. These findings indicated that achieving LDL levels <70 mg/dL is difficult with statin monotherapy alone and often requires combination therapy with ezetimibe. Based on this study, the 2021 guidelines from the American Heart Association and American Stroke Association recommended maintaining LDL‐C target <70 mg/dL in patients with IS and suggested the addition of ezetimibe if necessary. 16 Similarly, according to the Korean Guidelines for Management of Dyslipidemia, statin therapy is recommended for patients with IS or transient ischemic attack (TIA) with a target LDL‐C level of less than 70 mg/dL or a reduction of more than 50% from baseline levels. If this target is not achieved, ezetimibe can be added as a combination therapy.

Although previous studies have consistently demonstrated that combining statins with ezetimibe effectively reduces LDL‐C levels, which is a surrogate marker, the impact on MACE, an actual clinical outcome, has varied depending on the type of statin used. 17 , 18 , 19 This variation may be due to the intrinsic effects of the statins themselves, or it could be influenced by factors such as patient characteristics or study design. Therefore, it is necessary to examine the effects of ezetimibe combined with different types of statins within a single cohort. Moreover, most previous research has focused on the composite outcome of MACE, with limited studies examining recurrent IS as the primary outcome in patients with IS. 17 , 18 , 20 Therefore, this study aimed to compare the impact of adding ezetimibe to high‐intensity statins—atorvastatin and rosuvastatin—on the incidence of recurrent IS in a large cohort of Korean patients who have previously experienced an IS.

METHODS

Availability of Data and Materials

Because of ethical issues and data protection regulations, data that support the findings of the present study cannot be made publicly available.

Data Source and Study Population

The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Ewha Womans University, which waived the requirement for informed consent (IRB number: ewha‐202304‐0023‐01). We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service database, which covers the entire Korean population and includes patient characteristics, clinical diagnoses, hospital visits, medical procedures, and prescribed medications. 21

We identified all patients diagnosed with IS according to the Health Insurance Review and Assessment data between July 1, 2016 and July 31, 2022, and selected those hospitalized for IS between July 1, 2017 and July 31, 2021. IS was identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) code (I63), which has been validated in several studies with positive predictive values exceeding 82%. 22 In South Korea, stroke is defined as an acute neurological deficit persisting for more than 24 hours or resulting in death. 23 Diagnosis of acute stroke is based on neurological examination and clinical presentation, along with blood test (eg, complete blood count, electrolytes, prothrombin time test, activated partial thromboplastin time) and ECG. If brain imaging (multimodal computerized tomography scan or magnetic resonance imaging) shows no evidence of hemorrhagic lesions, the condition is classified as ischemic stroke (I63). 24 The cohort entry date was defined as the date of the first hospitalization for IS of each patient within the study period, provided that the patient had not been diagnosed with IS within the previous year. We excluded patients <19 years old at cohort entry and those who had experienced an IS within 1 year before the cohort entry date. Patients were further excluded if they (1) were not on statin therapy, (2) were prescribed statins other than atorvastatin or rosuvastatin, (3) were administered both atorvastatin and rosuvastatin, or (4) were prescribed statins for <90 days. Then, we divided them into 2 groups: the atorvastatin and rosuvastatin groups.

Cohort Study

A dual design was applied to enhance the robustness and reproducibility of the findings by integrating both cohort and case–control study designs. For the cohort study, we compared the risk of recurrent IS between ezetimibe users and nonusers. The ezetimibe exposure group included patients who were prescribed ezetimibe at least once during the follow‐up period, and the remaining patients were considered part of the nonexposure group. For enhanced comparability, we conducted 1:1 propensity score matching within the atorvastatin and rosuvastatin groups. Propensity scores were calculated using logistic regression, taking into account variables such as age, sex, index year, comorbidities, and medications at baseline. We applied greedy matching with a caliper of 0.2 times the SMD. 25 , 26 The index date for the cohort study was defined as the cohort entry date.

The outcome measured in this study was the incidence of recurrent IS (ICD‐10: I63) during the follow‐up period. Recurrent IS was defined as rehospitalization for IS that occurred at least 90 days following discharge from a previous IS‐related hospitalization. Patients were censored at the date of statin discontinuation, death, or end of the study period (July 31, 2022).

Case–Control Study

For the case–control study, demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between cases and controls. Recurrent IS cases were defined as patients diagnosed with IS (ICD‐10: I63) at least 90 days after the initial IS date. Patients who did not experience a recurrent IS throughout the study period were identified as potential controls. For cases, the index date was the day of the recurrent IS diagnosis; for controls, the index date was randomly chosen from hospital visit dates. We used exact matching to pair each recurrent IS case with a control, matched 1:1 based on age, sex, and index year. The primary exposure of the study was ezetimibe use. Patients were classified as ezetimibe users if they had at least 1 ezetimibe prescription within 365 days before the index date.

Covariates

We included several covariates in our analysis: sex, age group (per 10‐year interval), index year, comorbidities, and medications. Comorbidities were identified using ICD‐10 codes, which captured diagnoses from hospitalization and outpatient visits (Table S1). Medications were identified through Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical codes in prescription claims data. Comorbidities and medications at baseline were assessed using data from the 365 days preceding the index date in both the cohort and case–control studies. Medications during the follow‐up period were defined as those prescribed after the index date throughout the follow‐up duration in the cohort study.

Statistical Analysis

For the cohort study, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to assess the balance of covariate distribution between ezetimibe users and nonusers. SMD<0.1 indicated a negligible imbalance. To assess the risk of recurrent IS, we calculated the incidence rates of recurrent IS per 1000 person‐years. Cumulative incidence of recurrent IS among patients with atorvastatin or rosuvastatin was assessed using Kaplan–Meier curves and log‐rank tests. We also employed a Cox proportional hazards regression model to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and adjusted HRs (aHRs) with 95% CIs. We tested the proportional hazards assumption of the Cox proportional hazards model using Schoenfeld residuals test. In addition, we calculated the absolute risk reduction to provide a more direct measure of the clinical benefit associated with ezetimibe use. The post hoc power analysis was performed with PROC Power of SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Additionally, we performed a formal interaction analysis to evaluate the potential differential effects of ezetimibe across statin types, using an interaction term (ezetimibe×statin type) after combining atorvastatin cohort and rosuvastatin cohort.

For the case–control study, we compared the characteristics between the cases and controls using the chi‐square test. We then employed a logistic regression model to calculate the odds ratios (ORs) and adjusted ORs (aORs) with 95% CIs. Sequential logistic regressions were conducted to evaluate the association between ezetimibe use and recurrent IS. Model 1 was adjusted for demographic factors, Model 2 was further adjusted for comorbidities, and Model 3 was further adjusted for medications.

Statistical analyses were conducted using the SAS Enterprise Guide ver. 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R studio ver. 3.5.1 (RStudio, Boston, MA, USA). A 2‐tailed P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity Analyses

To ensure the robustness of our findings, we performed several sensitivity analyses. First, we expanded our analysis to include both IS (ICD‐10: I63) and TIA (ICD‐10: G45). TIA was defined as hospitalization or emergency room visits for TIA. Second, we performed the Cox proportional hazards regression analysis adjusted for sex, age, index year, medication during the follow‐up period, and comorbidities to assess the impact of poststroke medication use on the results. Third, we applied robust SEs in the Cox proportional hazards model to account for correlation induced by matching. 27 Fourth, competing risks analysis was conducted to model recurrent IS as the primary event and deaths unrelated to IS as competing events. The Cox model incorporating Fine and Gray weights was used to estimate subdistribution HRs and 95% CIs. 28 Lastly, we conducted inverse probability weighting analyses using stabilized weights to further address potential residual confounding.

RESULTS

We identified all patients diagnosed with IS (ICD‐10: I63) according to the Health Insurance Review and Assessment data between July 1, 2016 and July 31, 2022 (n=1 068 865). From this population, we selected patients who were hospitalized for IS between July 1, 2017 and July 31, 2021 (n=115 226). After excluding 131 patients <19 years and 49 411 who had IS within 1 year before the cohort entry date, the number of eligible patients with IS for the study were 65 684. We further excluded patients not on statin therapy (n=24 382), those prescribed statins other than atorvastatin or rosuvastatin (n=4915), those on both atorvastatin and rosuvastatin (n=5871), and those with a duration of statin treatment <90 days (n=3579). Subsequently, the remaining patients were categorized into 2 groups based on their statin usage: atorvastatin (n=16 215) and rosuvastatin (n=10 722) groups (Figure 1).

Among the 16 215 patients in the atorvastatin group and 10 722 patients in the rosuvastatin group, 2180 and 4024 were ezetimibe users, respectively (Figure 1). The baseline characteristics were generally balanced between ezetimibe users and nonusers in both the atorvastatin and rosuvastatin groups, with the exception of age and index year (Table S2). Figure 2 and Table 2 shows the cumulative incidence of recurrent IS in atorvastatin and rosuvastatin groups. Within the atorvastatin group, ezetimibe users exhibited a decreased incidence of recurrent IS, with rates at 18.47 per 1000 person‐years compared with 24.66 per 1000 person‐years in nonusers (log‐rank P=0.034), corresponding to the absolute risk reduction of 6.19 per 1000 person‐years (95% CI, 4.85–7.53). The post hoc power analysis showed that our study had 76% power to detect an observed median survival difference in the primary outcome (1.75 versus 1.59 years). The aHR stood at 0.73 (95% CI, 0.55–0.98; P=0.037), indicating a 27% reduction in the risk of recurrent IS. Conversely, within the rosuvastatin group, the recurrent IS incidence remained similar regardless of ezetimibe prescription. The incidence rates for ezetimibe users and nonusers were 21.50 per 1000 person‐years and 21.63, respectively (log‐rank P=0.958), with the absolute risk reduction of 0.13 per 1000 person‐years (95% CI, −0.88 to 1.14) and aHR of 1.00 (95% CI, 0.80–1.24; P=0.985). The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for all covariates and the global test, as none of the Schoenfeld residual tests yielded significant P values (all P > 0.05). Meanwhile, when comparing recurrent IS risk between atorvastatin and rosuvastatin monotherapy groups (ezetimibe nonusers), the difference was not statistically significant (log‐rank P=0.1; aHR, 0.81 [95% CI, 0.63–1.03]). In the interaction analysis examining differential effects between statin types, an interaction term (ezetimibe × statin type) was statistically significant in both univariate (HR, 0.73 [95% CI, 0.50–0.97], P=0.046) and multivariable‐adjusted models (aHR, 0.72 [95% CI, 0.50–0.93], P=0.047), indicating a potentially protective effect of ezetimibe when combined with atorvastatin (Table S3).

Case–Control Study

Among 16 215 patients in the atorvastatin group and 10 722 patients in the rosuvastatin group, we identified 1572 and 914 recurrent cases with IS, respectively. As observed in the cohort study, baseline characteristics were balanced between recurrent cases with IS and controls in both the atorvastatin and rosuvastatin groups, except for age distribution and index year (Table S4). Following 1:1 exact matching, 1569 case–control pairs in the atorvastatin group and 900 case–control pairs in the rosuvastatin group were included in the analysis (Figure 3). Matched factors such as sex, age, and index year were well balanced between cases and controls in both groups (Table 3). Recurrent cases with IS in both groups tended to exhibit a higher prevalence of comorbidities. For comedications, the prescription rates were generally comparable with the exception of beta blockers, direct oral anticoagulants, and phosphodiesterase inhibitors in the atorvastatin group and aspirin, beta blockers, and direct oral anticoagulants in the rosuvastatin group.

Sensitivity Analyses

First, the combination of ezetimibe with atorvastatin significantly reduced the incidence of IS or TIA in both the cohort and case–control studies, with an aHR of 0.75 (95% CI, 0.57–0.98; P=0.049) and an aOR of 0.66 (95% CI, 0.53–0.82; P<0.001), respectively, as shown in Tables S5 and S6. In contrast, when ezetimibe was used in combination with rosuvastatin, as with the main results, neither the cohort studies nor the case–control studies demonstrated statistically significant differences. Additional results are presented in Tables S7 through S11 and Figures S3 and S4. Second, when adjusting for sex, age, index year, medication during the follow‐up, and comorbidities, ezetimibe users had a significantly lower risk of recurrent IS compared with nonusers in the atorvastatin group (aHR, 0.74 [95% CI, 0.55–0.99]; P=0.047), whereas no significant difference was observed in the rosuvastatin group (aHR, 1.02 [95% CI, 0.82–1.26], P=0.89). Third, when applying robust SEs, the atorvastatin group showed a significant reduction in recurrent IS risk for ezetimibe users compared with nonusers (aHR, 0.74 [95% CI, 0.55–0.98], P=0.037), and no significant difference was observed in the rosuvastatin group (aHR, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.80–1.24], P=0.99). Fourth, according to the Fine and Gray competing risks analysis, in the atorvastatin group, ezetimibe users were associated with a reduced risk of recurrent IS compared with ezetimibe nonusers (subdistribution HR, 0.74 [95% CI, 0.55–0.99], P==0.041), whereas no significant difference was observed in the rosuvastatin group (subdistribution HR, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.81–1.24], P=0.99). Lastly, in the inverse probability weighting analysis, ezetimibe users in the atorvastatin group had a significantly lower risk of recurrent IS compared with non‐users (aHR, 0.73 [95% CI, 0.54–0.99], P=0.047), whereas no significant difference was observed in the rosuvastatin group (aHR, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.80–1.23], P=0.89). All sensitivity analyses yielded results consistent with the main analysis, indicating the robustness of our findings.

DISCUSSION

The effects of combining ezetimibe with statins to prevent recurrent IS were assessed in this study. Our findings indicated that adding ezetimibe to atorvastatin significantly lowered the incidence of recurrent IS in both cohort and case–control designs. Conversely, combining ezetimibe with rosuvastatin did not significantly associate with recurrent IS rates in either design. The same trend was observed when the analysis was expanded to include patients with TIA. Moreover, similar results were observed even after sensitivity analyses. In the interaction analysis, an interaction term (ezetimibe × statin type) also showed statistical significance, suggesting the protective effect of ezetimibe when combined with atorvastatin; however, it should be interpreted with caution as each statin group was separately propensity score matched and then combined for the analysis.

Previous studies consistently showed that combining ezetimibe with atorvastatin or rosuvastatin resulted in a significantly greater reduction in LDL‐C levels than that of statin monotherapy in patients with hypercholesterolemia or atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. For instance, the combination of atorvastatin 5 mg and ezetimibe 10 mg demonstrated significantly greater reductions in LDL‐C levels than equivalent or doubled doses of atorvastatin monotherapy. 29 This effect remained consistent across different statin intensities; combining ezetimibe 10 mg with atorvastatin 10, 20, 40, or 80 mg led to greater reductions in LDL‐C levels compared with monotherapy with corresponding doses of atorvastatin. 30 Studies further showed that combining ezetimibe with moderate‐intensity atorvastatin significantly reduces LDL‐C levels than that of high‐intensity atorvastatin alone, both at 6 and 12 weeks post treatment. 31 , 32 , 33 In addition, it was reported that treatment with atorvastatin 5 mg and ezetimibe 5 mg showed comparable LDL‐C reductions to atorvastatin 20 mg monotherapy. 34 Similarly, for rosuvastatin, combination therapy with ezetimibe 10 mg significantly reduced LDL‐C levels compared with rosuvastatin monotherapy in both high and moderate‐intensity treatment groups. 35 , 36 Studies also showed that the LDL‐C lowering efficacy of rosuvastatin and ezetimibe 10 mg combination therapy was superior to that of rosuvastatin monotherapy at corresponding doses (5, 10, 20, and 40 mg). 37 , 38

Although numerous studies showed that combining ezetimibe with either atorvastatin or rosuvastatin lowers LDL‐C levels, research on final clinical outcomes, such as MACE, revealed different results between atorvastatin and rosuvastatin. In a randomized controlled trial involving patients with acute coronary syndrome whose LDL‐C levels were >70 mg/dL after being administered atorvastatin 20 mg for 4 weeks, the group treated with atorvastatin and ezetimibe 10 mg exhibited significantly higher event‐free cumulative survival rates at 16 weeks compared with the group with the doubled dose of atorvastatin alone. 20 Additionally, in a nationwide retrospective study of patients who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention, those treated with a combination of atorvastatin 20 mg and ezetimibe 10 mg exhibited a significantly lower incidence of MACE compared with those on 40 mg of atorvastatin monotherapy over a 3‐year study period. 18 Similar to atorvastatin, the beneficial effects of combining simvastatin with ezetimibe were observed in IMPROVE‐IT (ImProved Reduction of Outcomes: Vytorin Efficacy International Trial), as both statins share similar lipophilic properties. This trial demonstrated that adding ezetimibe to simvastatin significantly reduced the incidence of IS in patients with a prior history of IS. 39 For the combination of rosuvastatin and ezetimibe, the RACING (Randomized Comparison of Efficacy and Safety of Lipid Lowering With Statin Monotherapy Versus Statin‐Ezetimibe Combination for High‐Risk Cardiovascular Disease) trial compared patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease treated with a combination of rosuvastatin 10 mg and ezetimibe 10 mg to those treated with rosuvastatin 20 mg monotherapy over 3 years. 17 Unlike the atorvastatin study, the incidence rates of MACE did not show statistically significant differences between the 2 groups (aHR, 0.92 [95% CI, 0.75–1.13]). This outcome remained consistent even after stratifying the results by factors such as diabetes status and age (75 years old). 40 , 41

Research on ezetimibe use for preventing recurrent IS in patients with prior IS is limited. The MESIA (Medium‐Intensity Statin With Ezetimibe Versus High‐Intensity Statin in Acute Ischemic Cerebrovascular Disease) trial examined the effects of ezetimibe in combination with atorvastatin or rosuvastatin among patients with IS or TIA. 42 The primary outcome of the MESIA study was the level and achievement rate of LDL‐C after 3 months of treatment. The findings revealed that only atorvastatin in the moderate‐intensity statin group with ezetimibe 10 mg demonstrated a significantly higher reduction in the primary outcome compared with the high‐intensity atorvastatin‐only group. Although MESIA study also investigated the incidence of recurrent IS or TIA as a secondary outcome, the number of cases was ≤2 due to the small sample size (150 patients) and short period of study duration (90 days), limiting the ability to accurately assess the effectiveness of ezetimibe. Our study, using a large sample size and a follow‐up period of up to 5 years, demonstrated that the addition of ezetimibe to atorvastatin was associated with a reduced risk of recurrent IS. Conversely, the combination of ezetimibe with rosuvastatin did not exhibit any additive effects.

The differential effects of ezetimibe when combined with atorvastatin versus rosuvastatin may be attributed to their distinct pharmacological properties. Rosuvastatin, being hydrophilic, is selectively transported to the liver via organic anion transporting polypeptide 1B1, where it predominantly lowers LDL‐C with minimal extrahepatic distribution. 43 , 44 In contrast, atorvastatin is lipophilic, allowing for wider tissue penetration beyond the liver, which may explain its superior pleiotropic effects compared with hydrophilic rosuvastatin, particularly in enhancing cardiac function, attenuating inflammation, and improving endothelial function. 45 , 46 When atorvastatin is combined with ezetimibe, it could further lower C‐reactive protein and phospholipase A2 levels beyond LDL‐C reduction, leading to greater improvements in atherosclerosis. 47 , 48

In our study, atorvastatin was prescribed to more than half of patients with a history of IS, with rosuvastatin being the second most common statin (approximately 30%). This prescribing pattern aligns with the general statin use trends observed in South Korea. 49 We also observed a notable difference in proportion of ezetimibe use between atorvastatin (13%) and rosuvastatin (38%) cohorts, which likely reflects both clinical factors and market dynamics specific to South Korea. Rosuvastatin‐ezetimibe combination therapy has experienced rapid growth in South Korea due to multiple available combination products and aggressive marketing strategies. 50 These regional prescribing practices for both statins and ezetimibe may differ from those in Western health care systems, suggesting caution when extrapolating our findings to Western populations.

This study had some limitations that require acknowledgment. First, clinical observations and laboratory data were not available in claims data. As a result, we could not evaluate disease severity or common adverse events of statin (eg, gastrointestinal disturbance, myalgia, and elevated liver enzymes), nor could we assess cholesterol levels as a surrogate marker. The lack of LDL‐C data limits our ability to determine how much lipid control was achieved by each group. Second, we did not investigate the indications for ezetimibe such as statin intolerance, which could act as unmeasured confounders. Third, because our focus was on the effects of ezetimibe combined with 2 representative types of statins on recurrent IS, we did not track dosage changes during the follow‐up period. However, the average statin doses were comparable between the 2 groups. Fourth, although a 1‐year washout period from the cohort entry date was implemented, it may be insufficient to confirm the first‐ever IS hospitalization. Fifth, there is a lack of mechanistic explanation for why the effects of ezetimibe differ when combined with these 2 statins. Lastly, as this study was conducted as a retrospective analysis using a single cohort with limited statistical power (76% and 73% power to detect the observed differences in the cohort and case–control studies, respectively), further validation with independent cohorts is necessary to strengthen the generalizability and reliability of these results.

Despite these limitations, including the power slightly below the conventional 80% threshold, our findings are supported by several methodological strengths. First, a key strength of our study lies in its dual design, incorporating both cohort and case–control approaches. This dual design enhanced the reproducibility and robustness of our findings by providing complementary perspectives on the relationship between ezetimibe and statin combination therapy in preventing recurrent IS. As revealed in study results, the comparison between the atorvastatin plus ezetimibe combination group and the atorvastatin alone group yielded similar estimates, with an aHR of 0.73 (95% CI, 0.55–0.98) and an aOR of 0.74 (95% CI, 0.58–0.93). However, the inherent differences between cohort and case–control designs should be carefully considered when interpreting their results. Second, we employed rigorous matching techniques such as propensity score matching for the cohort study and exact matching for the case–control study to effectively minimize confounding effects. Lastly, we performed several sensitivity analyses, including expanding the outcome definition to include both IS and TIA, adjusting for medications during follow‐up, applying robust SEs, conducting competing risk analyses, and performing inverse probability weighting. These analyses further validated the consistency of our primary results and underscored the reliability of our findings. These approaches not only strengthened the internal validity of the study but also ensured that the observed effects were reproducible.

In conclusion, our findings suggested that the combination of ezetimibe with atorvastatin was associated with lower rates of recurrent IS compared with atorvastatin monotherapy. Conversely, we did not observe a significant difference in recurrent IS rates between ezetimibe plus rosuvastatin and rosuvastatin alone in recurrent IS rates. These findings could provide additional evidence for the optimal use of ezetimibe in statin therapy for preventing recurrent IS in patients with a history of IS, although they should be interpreted with caution due to the inherent limitations of observational studies.
