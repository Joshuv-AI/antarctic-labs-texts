Genetic tool for specific tracking of mature neutrophils
Authors: Jiayu Cao, Hui Ping Yaw, Shilin Yi, Yanxi Zhou, Shifan Qin, Yuanyuan Wang, Raul da Costa, Lei Zhang, Dandan Wu, Changbin Chen, Melissa Ng, Immanuel Kwok, Oliver Soehnlein, Xiaoxiang Chen, Jieqing Wan, Lai Guan Ng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tracking mature neutrophils remains challenging due to the lack of reliable cell surface markers. Although CD101 is a promising candidate as a marker for mature neutrophils, its stability under pathological conditions is unclear. Here, we developed a novel CD101-tdTomato reporter mouse model to specifically identify mature neutrophils. CD101 expression, tdTomato fluorescence, and neutrophil physiology were characterized by flow cytometry across peripheral tissues under homeostasis and by a transwell migration assay, in parallel with morphological features assessed by Wright-Giemsa staining. To assess marker stability, CD101-tdTomato and wild-type (WT) mice were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (200 ng, intraperitoneally, 2 h, n = 8), influenza virus (5 × 103 PFU/mL, intratracheally, days 1 and 5, n = 6), or granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) (2.5 μg, intravenously, 2–6 h, n = 5), followed by flow cytometry analyses. CD101-tdTomato mice were crossed with Lysozyme-GFP mice to validate reporter specificity across myeloid subsets. We confirmed that CD101+ and tdTomato+ neutrophils display identical characteristics of mature neutrophils, including poly-segmented nuclei, cell size, and key functional properties under homeostasis. Under pathological challenges, the decrease in CD101 expression was not attributed to shedding or degradation of the protein, supporting its robustness under inflammatory conditions. Using CD101-tdTomato × Lysozyme-GFP mice, we established a dual-reporter system that enables precise distinction of mature neutrophils from total myeloid cells. Collectively, our findings support CD101 as a robust and reliable marker of neutrophil maturity, providing a foundation for its future applications in spatial transcriptomics and lineage tracing studies of the heterogeneity and functions of neutrophils.

Introduction

Neutrophils serve as the frontline defenders of the innate immune system. These cells rapidly migrate to sites of infection or injury, where they engulf pathogens, release antimicrobial molecules, and produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) to neutralize threats [1]. Their quick response is vital for host defense, playing a key role in combating bacterial infections, resolving tissue damage, and modulating immune responses. Despite their critical role in immunity, the heterogeneity of neutrophil populations—particularly the distinction between mature and immature subpopulations—has long posed a challenge to researchers. Neutrophil maturation is a complex process marked by intricate changes in morphology, function, and surface marker expression. These dynamic changes are often heterogeneous, making it difficult to clearly define distinct developmental stages and to track neutrophil behavior under both normal and diseased conditions [2]. The lack of specific, reliable markers complicates efforts to study neutrophil maturation and function, particularly in complex tissue environments [3]. Traditionally, expression levels of markers such as Ly6G and CD11b have been used to assess neutrophil maturation. However, these markers are constitutively expressed across multiple developmental stages, from immature precursors to mature populations [4,5]. Consequently, defining maturation often depends on subjective gating of fluorescence intensity, which lacks the binary precision necessary to resolve cellular heterogeneity, especially in pathological states. Furthermore, C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2) has been used to distinguish between mature and immature subpopulations, but its expression is highly context-dependent and influenced by activation states, making it an imprecise marker for defining neutrophil maturity [6].

CD101, a cell surface glycoprotein, has emerged as a reliable marker of neutrophil maturation, characterized by high expression on mature neutrophils and minimal or undetectable expression on immature and early precursors [7]. This marker thus provides a valuable tool to refine our understanding of neutrophil development and function. However, tracking CD101+ neutrophils in tissues using traditional staining methods is difficult due to technical limitations, such as antibody penetration or tissue fixation issues [8]. The complexity of tissue microenvironments and the limitations of immunohistochemistry often hinder precise identification of mature neutrophils [9].

Another critical question is whether CD101 remains a stable marker under challenged conditions. Neutrophils undergo rapid phenotypic and functional changes during stress, including upregulation of activation markers, release of proteases, and alterations in surface protein expression [10]. Like many surface proteins, CD101 may be subjected to regulation through mechanisms such as proteolytic shedding, internalization, or degradation, potentially compromising its reliability as a marker of neutrophil maturation under inflammatory or pathological conditions [11]. A well-known example is the shedding of surface markers such as CD62L during neutrophil activation, highlighting the need for caution when using surface markers to define cell maturation or activation stages [12]. Similarly, a decrease in CD101+ cells may indicate changes in neutrophil populations, such as their mobilization from the bone marrow to peripheral tissues [13]. Therefore, it is important to validate the reliability of CD101 as a surface marker to distinguish between a true reduction in cell numbers and a stress-induced downregulation of the marker.

To evaluate this further, we developed a CD101-tdTomato reporter mouse model. In this mouse model, the CD101-tdTomato reporting system shows synchronized expression of CD101 and tdTomato, with tdTomato labeling nearly all of the CD101+ mature neutrophils across tissues. We confirmed that CD101+ and tdTomato+ mature neutrophils share identical nuclear morphologies and functional traits. Moreover, tdTomato labeling efficiency remains unchanged under stress conditions. Our results validate CD101 as a reliable cell surface marker and provide a powerful tool for investigating neutrophil heterogeneity, thereby enabling more precise studies of neutrophil function in immunity and disease.

Results

Consistent Expression of CD101 and tdTomato Across Tissues in Homeostasis

CD101 is highly expressed by mature neutrophils [7]. In order to investigate CD101 as a stable cell surface marker, we generated a mouse line in which an exon of CD101 is knocked into an allele encoding Cre recombinase and the fluorescent protein tdTomato separated by a self-splicing T2A peptide (Figure 1A). Gene-targeted animals were verified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (Figure S1A). CD101 expression levels in bone marrow (BM) neutrophils were similar across wild-type (WT), heterozygous, and homozygous CD101-tdTomato mice, indicating that the genetic modification did not alter CD101 gene regulation or protein expression (Figures 1B–1C and S1B, Table S1). Moreover, the homozygous mice showed a high percentage of tdTomato+ cells compared with heterozygous mice, aligning closely with the CD101+ population. This observation extends beyond BM, with similar patterns of CD101 consistency and enriched tdTomato expression observed in the spleen and blood of homozygous mice (Figure 1B–1C). Correlation analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between CD101 and tdTomato expression in BM, blood, and spleen of homozygous mice (Figures 1D and S1C, Table S1). In CD101-tdTomato homozygous mice, tdTomato expression was predominantly restricted to Ly6G+ neutrophils, demonstrating the specificity of the CD101-tdTomato system for labeling neutrophils compared to heterozygous mice (Figures 1E and S1D, Table S2). Furthermore, tdTomato labeled nearly 100% of CD101+ cells in BM, blood, and spleen, indicating high labeling efficiency (Figure 1F, Table S3). CD101+ and tdTomato+ Neutrophils Exhibit Mature Nuclear Morphology Across Tissues

To assess the reliability of tdTomato and CD101 as markers for distinguishing mature from immature neutrophils, we checked the nuclear morphology by Giemsa staining [1] (Figure S2). In the BM of both homozygous CD101-tdTomato and WT mice, tdTomato+ and CD101+ neutrophils showed the characteristic poly-segmented nuclei of mature neutrophils, while tdTomato‒ and CD101‒ cells exhibited a typical donut-shaped nucleus of immature neutrophils. This distinction was consistently observed in blood and spleen, where tdTomato+ and CD101+ cells consistently displayed poly-segmented nuclei (Figure 2A), indicating tdTomato and CD101 reliably identify mature neutrophils across different tissues. Statistical analysis of tdTomato+ cells revealed that nearly all were mature neutrophils, supporting tdTomato as a specific marker for neutrophil maturity across tissues (Figure 2B, Table S4). Functional Characterization of CD101+ and tdTomato+ Mature Neutrophils

To determine whether the genetic modification in the CD101-tdTomato mouse model affects neutrophil physiology, we first compared the viability of neutrophils from CD101-tdTomato mice with that of WT controls. We observed a gradual increase in cell death and apoptosis over time, with identical trends for CD101‒ and tdTomato‒ cells, indicating consistent behavior in the immature population (Figures 3A and S3A–S3B, Table S6). In contrast, mature neutrophils (CD101+/tdTomato+) displayed earlier apoptosis and cell death compared to immature neutrophils, with synchronized changes in CD101+ and tdTomato+ cells (Figures 3A–3B and S3C, Table S6). This highlights distinct survival kinetics between mature and immature neutrophils, with CD101 and tdTomato reliably distinguishing these populations. Furthermore, we examined ROS production, a key effector mechanism critical for neutrophil-mediated host defense [1]. To determine if neutrophils from WT and homozygous CD101-tdTomato mice produce similar ROS levels, we stimulated neutrophils with phorbol myristate acetate (PMA). Neutrophils from bone marrow and spleen between strains showed comparable ROS production, suggesting that the CD101-tdTomato reporter system did not affect this critical function (Figures 3C–3D and S3D, Table S7). Another key biological feature of neutrophils is their migratory capacity. To assess chemotaxis, a vital neutrophil function for their rapid migration to infection sites [2], we isolated CD101+ and tdTomato+ neutrophils using fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) from BM and performed a transwell migration assay. CD101 and tdTomato as Stable Markers in Neutrophil Stress Responses

To further determine whether CD101 is a stable cell surface marker under stress conditions, we evaluated CD101 and tdTomato expression in CD101-tdTomato mice following lipopolysaccharide (LPS) challenge (Figure S4A). After LPS administration, the proportion of CD101+ and tdTomato+ mature neutrophils decreased in the BM of WT and homozygous CD101-tdTomato mice. Both CD101 and tdTomato signals were diminished to a comparable extent, with a high correlation between their expression levels in BM; notably, the efficiency of CD101 antibody labeling was preserved (Figures 4A–4C and S4B–S4C, Tables S9–S10). This observation indicates that CD101 is neither shed nor degraded, but rather that the actual frequency of CD101+ (tdTomato+) neutrophils was reduced. This pattern of decreased CD101+ and tdTomato+ cell proportions with stable labeling was also observed in spleen and blood, suggesting that LPS challenge reduces the mature neutrophil population without compromising CD101 integrity (Figures 4A–4C and S4B–S4C). We further validated this conclusion using ex vivo microscopy of FACS-sorted CD101+ neutrophils from both WT and CD101-tdTomato homozygous mice. We observed that LPS stimulation did not alter the antibody detection of the CD101 surface protein and the tdTomato signal (Figure S4D). To further evaluate the stability of CD101 in a viral infection-induced stress environment, we performed intratracheal injections of influenza A virus (A/Puerto Rico/8/1934, H1N1) into the WT and CD101-tdTomato reporter mice. Following infection, we observed a rapid increase in the infiltration of CD101+ and tdTomato+ mature neutrophils in the lung at 1 d post infection (dpi), which then gradually declined by 5 dpi (Figure 4D left, Table S11). Concurrently, the number of these mature neutrophils in the BM showed a progressive decrease, while their frequency in the peripheral blood steadily rose, reflecting a systematic mobilization from the marrow to the site of infection (Figure 4D middle and right, Table S11). Notably, the dynamic changes in CD101+ cell counts were highly consistent between WT and homozygous CD101-tdTomato reporter mice, suggesting that the genetic insertion does not interfere with the physiological recruitment or maturation of neutrophils. Furthermore, by calculating the labeling efficiency at both 1 and 5 dpi, we found that tdTomato successfully marked CD101+ cells throughout the course of infection in the lung, BM, and blood (Figure 4E, Table S11). These findings reinforce the conclusion that CD101 is a robust and stable marker for mature neutrophils in peripheral organs, even under the intense inflammatory conditions of viral pneumonia.

To investigate the response of CD101-tdTomato reporter mice to emergency granulopoiesis, we intravenously injected 2.5 μg of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), a potent inducer of granulopoiesis, and analyzed neutrophil populations in blood and bone marrow dynamically (Figure 4F and S5A). Two hours after G-CSF injection, bone marrow neutrophils in both WT and homozygous mice sharply declined, followed by partial recovery, reflecting rapid mobilization to peripheral tissues (Figure S5B). Mature neutrophils (CD101+ or tdTomato+) showed a similar sharp decline and slight recovery, with both populations displaying identical trends (Figure 4G, Table S12). The labeling efficiency remained above 90% throughout, confirming synchronized changes in CD101 and tdTomato expression (Figure 4H, Table S12). In contrast, blood neutrophils in WT and homozygous mice rapidly increased after G-CSF injection, remaining elevated above controls at 4 and 6 h, with CD101+ and tdTomato+ mature neutrophils showing synchronized increases (Figures 4I–4J and S5B–S5C, Table S12). These findings demonstrate that the CD101-tdTomato system did not alter emergency granulopoiesis and reliably marks mature neutrophils with stable labeling under G-CSF-induced stress.

Distinguishing Mature Neutrophils From Myeloid Cells

To enable identification of mature neutrophils from the total myeloid cell population, we crossed homozygous CD101-tdTomato mice with Lysozyme-GFP (LysM-GFP) mice, a well-established tool strain for labeling myeloid cells [14]. Flow cytometric analysis of these offspring revealed that the tdTomato+GFP+ population consistently overlapped with the CD101+GFP+ phenotype, indicating that the tdTomato expression faithfully marks mature neutrophils. Importantly, the CD101-tdTomato × LysM-GFP mice exhibited clear separation of tdTomato+ mature neutrophils from the tdTomato‒GFP+ population (comprising immature neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages) across tissues/organs analyzed (Figures 5 and S6). Discussion

Neutrophils play a vital role in immunity, yet distinguishing mature neutrophils from their immature counterparts remains a challenge due to the absence of specific, reliable markers. This difficulty arises because neutrophil maturation involves complex changes in morphology, function, and surface marker expression, which are not always well-defined [3]. Reliable markers are essential for studying neutrophil biology, especially in understanding their roles in health and disease. CD101 has been reported as a promising candidate due to its predominant expression on mature neutrophils, offering greater precision than traditional markers, though its expression under stress remains unclear [7]. Our study introduces a novel CD101-tdTomato reporter mouse model to confirm that CD101+ and tdTomato+ neutrophils share hallmark traits of maturity, such as poly-segmented nuclei, larger cell size (∼15 μm), and enhanced functions like ROS production and chemotactic migration. The model achieves nearly 100% labeling efficiency across bone marrow, blood, and spleen. Moreover, we showed that CD101 reduction under stress reflects population shifts rather than marker shedding or degradation. Furthermore, by crossing CD101-tdTomato mice with LysM-GFP mice—a widely used strain for labeling general myeloid cells [14]—we successfully distinguished mature neutrophils (tdTomato+GFP+) from the general myeloid population (tdTomato‒GFP+). The primary strength of this system lies in the remarkable stability of CD101 as a cell surface marker for mature neutrophils under both homeostatic and pathological conditions. The system preserves CD101 expression and neutrophil function, making it ideal for imaging and functional studies. Unlike antibody-based staining, which struggles with tissue penetration, our fluorescent reporter enables robust visualization of CD101+ neutrophils in complex tissues [8]. The high labeling efficiency of our reporter mouse model makes it a significant improvement over other reporter systems, such as those for Ly6G, which are less specific to mature neutrophils [15].

Compared to prior studies, our work provides a more comprehensive evaluation by testing CD101 under both steady-state and stress conditions, offering a clearer understanding of its behavior [6,11]. Our findings have important implications for the use of CD101 as a marker of neutrophil maturity, particularly in disease settings where neutrophil phenotypes are altered. By establishing the conditions under which CD101 remains a faithful indicator of maturity, we lay the groundwork for its application in cutting-edge technologies like spatial transcriptomics and lineage tracing. Such tools could ultimately reveal the spatial organization and developmental origins of neutrophil subsets, deepening our understanding of their roles in immunity and disease. Moreover, our reporter mouse model offers a versatile platform for future studies of neutrophil biology, bridging the gap between traditional surface marker analysis and modern omic approaches. The CD101-tdTomato model also paves the avenue for high-resolution imaging of mature neutrophils in tissues, overcoming the limitations of immunohistochemistry, which often fails in dense microenvironments [9]. For example, intravital microscopy could leverage tdTomato fluorescence to track CD101+ neutrophils in real-time during infection or tissue injury, offering insights into their migration and effector functions [16].

Despite its strengths, our study has limitations. We focused on stress conditions (LPS, viral infection, and G-CSF), which may not fully represent CD101 behavior in chronic conditions like autoimmune diseases [17]. Additionally, our findings are based on murine systems, and the role of CD101 in human neutrophils requires validation, as species-specific differences in marker expression may exist [18]. Minor variations in tdTomato fluorescence intensity could also affect sensitivity in low-expression settings, potentially limiting detection in certain contexts. Future studies should test CD101 in chronic disease models, such as rheumatoid arthritis or tumor microenvironments, to assess its stability across diverse settings. Validating CD101 expression in human samples and exploring its role in mature neutrophils across diverse human disease models could uncover its potential relevance for disease expression profiling and diagnostic applications. Combining the CD101-tdTomato model with advanced techniques like mass cytometry or spatial transcriptomics could further dissect neutrophil subsets, revealing their functional roles in complex diseases. Pairing CD101 with other markers, such as CXCR4, could enhance specificity for mature neutrophils in inflammatory contexts [19]. These steps would strengthen the utility of CD101 and address its dynamic expression under stress.

In summary, our study shows the CD101-tdTomato model is a powerful tool for tracking mature neutrophils with high specificity. Its future applications in imaging and lineage tracing offer new avenues for studying neutrophil biology. While CD101 shows promise as a reliable marker, its dynamic expression during inflammation calls for further investigation. By providing a platform for precise, in vivo insights, this model sets the stage for deeper explorations of neutrophil roles in health and disease, potentially guiding the development of targeted therapies.

Materials and methods

Mice

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines for the use of experimental animals and reported in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines [20]. The study was reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee in Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (JUMC2023-161-A). For the experiments, mice of both sexes were used. Mice used were between 7 and 10 weeks old. WT (C57BL/6J background), CD101-tdTomato (C57BL/6JCya-Cd101em1(tdTomato-iCre)/Cya), LysM-GFP (B6.129 (Cg)-Lyz 2tm1.1Graf/Mmmh), and CD101-tdTomato × LysM-GFP mice were bred and housed at the animal core facility of the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Shanghai, China). Mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions with a 12/12 h light/dark cycle at 20–24 °C, with continuous access to standard rodent chow and water. Each mouse represented a single experimental unit in all in vivo experiments. Mice were randomly assigned to their respective groups using a computer-generated random number sequence. Daily clinical monitoring was performed. Group sizes were based on our previous experience with neutrophil functional studies and published research, and were considered sufficient to achieve study objectives while minimizing animal use. All animals that completed the experimental protocol were included in the final analysis, and animals that died during treatment were excluded. Detailed exclusion criteria are stated below. To minimize environmental bias, cages were cross-distributed across different shelves, with all treatment and measurements performed in a randomized, rotating order. Mice were deeply anesthetized with ketamine (120 mg/kg) and xylazine (16 mg/kg) at indicated time points. Mice were humanely euthanized by deep inhalation of over-dosed isoflurane. All analyses were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation.

The CD101-tdTomato-iCre knock-in mouse model (C57BL/6JCya-Cd101em1(tdTomato-iCre)/Cya) was generated by Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou) Inc. To generate these mice, the TGA stop codon of Cd101 was replaced with a cassette containing the open reading frame of tdTomato and codon-optimized iCre recombinase separated by a self-splicing P2A and T2A peptide, thereby using the endogenous promoter from Cd101 for the P2A-tdTomato-T2A-iCre expression. A polyadenylation signal (WPRE-BGH pA) was inserted at the 3′-end of the P2A-tdTomato-T2A-iCre sequence in order to terminate transcription. The “P2A-tdTomato-T2A-iCre-WPRE-BGH pA” targeting vector was generated using BAC clones from the C57BL/6J RPCI-23 BAC library. The fertilized eggs were obtained from C57BL/6JCya wild-type mice. The clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats-associated protein 9 (Cas9) protein, guide RNA (gRNA, 5′-GACACTACTGAGGAGCACCTGGG-3′), and the targeting vector were co-injected into fertilized eggs. The injected embryos were cultured in potassium simplex optimized medium (KSOM) overnight, and those that developed to the two-cell stage were transferred into the oviduct of pseudopregnant ICR female mice. The F0 founder mice were identified by PCR and Sanger sequencing, and were bred to wild type mice to test germline transmission and F1 animal generation. The genotype of the F1 mice was also confirmed by PCR.

To generate the dual-reporter mouse system, homozygous CD101-tdTomato knock-in (CD101-tdTomato KI/KI) mice were crossed with LysM-GFP reporter mice. The resulting F1 heterozygous offspring (CD101-tdTomato KI/+ LysM-GFP) were further intercrossed to successfully obtain homozygous double-reporter mice (CD101-tdTomato KI/KI LysM-GFP). WT littermates and LysM-GFP mice were maintained under identical specific pathogen-free conditions and used as controls. For the initial methodological validation, one representative mouse was utilized from each of the following four experimental groups: WT, LysM-GFP, CD101-tdTomato KI/+ LysM-GFP, and CD101-tdTomato KI/KI LysM-GFP. This single-animal-per-group setup was strictly dedicated to qualitative proof-of-concept verification, specifically for establishing precise fluorescence gating boundaries, defining negative/positive populations, and configuring instrument compensation controls. Since the expression of these reporter alleles depends on genetic inheritance rather than biological variability, a single animal per genotype was sufficient to confirm reporter expression and establish gating strategies.

Mouse Genotyping

Tips of mouse tails were incubated at 100 °C for 10 min in 200 μL of 50 mM NaOH solution. After incubation, 50 μL of 1 M Tris-HCl solution was added, and the DNA mixture was vortexed thoroughly. The typical PCR sample consisted of a 20-μL volume containing 1 μL of the primers (10 μM) for the mutant allele (forward: 5′-CTAGTTGCCAGCCATCTGTTGTTT-3′; reverse: 5′-ACTATCTGCTTCTGAAGGATGCAA-3′) or for wildtype allele (forward: 5′-TCTTCTGTGTTTCTCTGGTTGCAG-3′; reverse: 5′-CCAAGGGAATAGATGAGTGGGAGA-3′). Each reaction also contained 4 μL of DNA mixture, 4 μL of ddH2O, and 10 μL of 2× Taq master mix (dye plus) (Vazyme, Jiangsu, China; Cat#P212). The following PCR conditions were applied: 3 min, 94 °C for initial denaturation; 30 s, 94 °C cyclic denaturation; 35 s, 60 °C cyclic annealing; 35 s, 72 °C cyclic elongation for a total of 35 cycles, followed by a 5-min 72 °C elongation step. The PCR result for the mutant allele was 431 bp and for the wildtype allele was 580 bp.

Drug Administrations

LPS challenge

Both WT (C57BL/6J background) and CD101-tdTomato homozygous (total n = 32, n = 8 per group) mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of LPS (Sigma–Aldrich, Missouri, USA) dissolved in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a dose of 200 ng in a final volume of 100 μL. The control group was administered 1× PBS injections at the same volume, route, and timing as the experimental group. Mice were sacrificed and peripheral organs were collected for further experiments 2 h after LPS or 1× PBS injection.

Virus-induced pneumonia model

For the influenza infection model (total n = 34), both WT (C57BL/6J) and CD101-tdTomato homozygous reporter mice were anesthetized by isoflurane and intratracheally administered 5 × 103 PFU/mL in 40 μL of influenza A/Puerto Rico/8/1934, H1N1 acquired from BEI Resources, Virginia, USA (n = 12 per group). The control group received an equal volume of sterile PBS (n = 5 per group). Mice were euthanized at 1 and 5 d post infection, and lung, peripheral blood and BM were then harvested and processed for further analysis. The variation in sample numbers at 5 d in the WT-infection group was due to the exclusion of mice that died after infection.

Emergency granulopoiesis

G-CSF was injected to induce emergency granulopoiesis as previously characterized [1] (total n = 40). The stock solution (in DMSO) was dissolved in PBS and injected intravenously to both WT and CD101-tdTomato mice at a dose of 2.5 μg in a final volume of 100 μL (n = 15 per group). The control group was administered control injections (PBS) at the same volume, route, and timing as for the intervention group (n = 5 per group). Mice were sacrificed at 2, 4, and 6 h after injection, and blood and bone marrow were collected for flow cytometry analysis.

Organ and Tissue Processing

Venous blood was collected via right ventricle puncture into EDTA-coated tubes to prevent coagulation. Red blood cells were lysed with lysing buffer (BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA) for 5 min at room temperature. The cell suspension was washed twice with FACS buffer (PBS, 2% FBS, 0.1% EDTA) by centrifugation at 400 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, discarding the supernatant each time. Cells were resuspended in 200 μL FACS buffer and stored on ice for further experiments. Bone marrow was isolated from mouse femurs using short-time high-speed centrifugation. Red blood cells were lysed with lysis buffer for 30 s at room temperature. Cells were washed once with FACS buffer by centrifugation at 400 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, then the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended in 400 μL FACS buffer and kept on ice for analysis. Spleens were mechanically homogenized through a 70 μm cell strainer to yield a single-cell suspension. Cells were pelleted at 400 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, followed by red blood cell lysis with lysis buffer for 2 min at room temperature. The suspension was washed once with FACS buffer at 400 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, then the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended in 800 μL FACS buffer and stored on ice for subsequent experiments.

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Cell suspensions from BM, blood, and spleen were incubated with flow cytometry antibodies and analyzed using a 5-laser BD Fortessa X-20 (BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA), and data were subsequently analyzed with FlowJo software (v10.8.1, Tree Star, California, USA). Quantification of cell numbers was done using count beads (CountBright; Life Technologies, Massachusetts, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. After exclusion of cell doublets and dead cells with BD Fixable Viability Stain 700 (BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA), immature neutrophils were identified as (Lin/CD115/Siglec-F)‒Gr1+CD11b+CXCR4locKitloLy6G+CD101‒ and mature neutrophils were identified as (Lin/CD115/Siglec-F)‒Gr1+CD11b+CXCR4locKitloLy6G+CD101+. tdTomato labeling efficiency was calculated as: Efficiency = [(the number of tdTomato+ cells)/(the number of CD101+ cells)] × 100%. Representative gating strategies for individual flow cytometry experiments are provided in the Supplementary materials and the antibodies used are shown in Table S13.

Cytospin and Wright-Giemsa Staining

To examine their distinct morphological features, Wright-Giemsa staining was performed as previously described [7]. Briefly, sorted CD101‒, CD101+, tdTomato‒, or tdTomato+ subsets (5 × 104 cells each) were spun onto glass slides using Epredia Cytospin 4 Cytocentrifuge (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) and air-dried. The slide was stained using Wright-Giemsa quick stain kit (Absin, Shanghai, China; Cat#abs9293) following the manufacturer's instructions. 20 μL Giemsa stain reagent A was added directly onto the slide and incubated at room temperature for 2 min. Next, 40 μL Giemsa stain reagent B was added directly onto the slide with reagent A and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Then, the staining reagents were removed under running water and air-dried. Images were acquired by the inverted fluorescence microscope (DM2500, Leica, Hesse, Germany) equipped with a 100× oil immersion objective, and image brightness and cell diameter measurement were processed with ImageJ software (v2.0.0; National Institutes of Health, Maryland, USA).

Oxidative Burst Assay

Oxidative burst activity was measured as a function of their ability to generate superoxide anion and hydrogen peroxide, as detected by oxidation of dihydrorhodamine (DHR) (Invitrogen, California, USA; Cat#D23806) [7]. In brief, sorted Ly6G+ cells (5 × 104 cells each) were incubated with DHR for 30 min at 37 °C in RPMI (supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 1% l-glutamine) and subjected to 100 nM 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (Sigma–Aldrich, Missouri, USA; Cat#P8139) for 30 min at 37 °C. Cells were then washed with 1× PBS followed by immediate data acquisition using flow cytometry.

In Vitro Cell Culture

Sorted cells (5 × 104 for each subset) were plated onto a 24-well plate and cultured in Iscove's modified Dulbecco's medium (Sigma–Aldrich, Missouri, USA) with 25 mM HEPES and l-glutamine containing 10% (v/v) FBS, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Cells were then harvested at 0, 1, 4, 24, 48, and 72 h post culture and analyzed for cell death and apoptosis using the dead cell apoptosis kit (Invitrogen, California, USA; Cat#V35113) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The negative control was treated with only sterilized 1× PBS; the positive control was stressed with 30% (v/v) H2O2 for 30 min. A maximum of 50,000 total events were collected per sample using 5-laser BD Fortessa X-20, and data were subsequently analyzed with FlowJo. The live cells were defined as the SYTOC Green‒Annexin V‒, the apoptotic population was defined as SYTOC Green‒Annexin V+, and the dead cells were defined as SYTOC Green+Annexin V+.

Neutrophil Migration Assay

The neutrophil migration assay was performed using Corning® 6.5 mm Transwell® with 5.0 μm pore polycarbonate membrane insert (Corning, New York, USA; Cat#3421) as described earlier [13]. In brief, sorted cells (1 × 105 for each subset) were applied in triplicate in 200 μL to the surface of membrane insert. Correspondingly, a 500 μL solution of 10−6 M formyl-Met-Leu-Phe-OH (fMLP; Sigma Aldrich, Missouri, USA; Cat#05-22-2500) was added to the lower chamber. As a 100% migration control, 1 × 105 cells for each subset were plated in duplicate in wells without inserts. The migration system was incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 2 h. Cells that migrated to the lower chamber were harvested. Neutrophil migration rate was calculated as: (cell numbers below the insert/100% migration control) × 100%.

Ex Vivo LPS Challenge

CD101+ neutrophils were FACS-sorted from the BM of WT and CD101-tdTomato homozygous mice. Experiments were performed using a total of six mice (three mice per group). Approximately 5 × 104 sorted cells were plated onto round coverslips in 24-well plates and cultured in Iscove's modified Dulbecco's medium with 25 mM HEPES and l-glutamine containing 10% (v/v) FBS, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 2 h to allow for cell recovery and attachment. For activation, cells were treated with LPS at concentrations of 100 ng/mL for 1 h, while the control group received an equivalent volume of sterile PBS. Following stimulation, the cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed with 1% PFA for 15 min at room temperature. After three subsequent washes, the cells were incubated in a blocking and permeabilization buffer containing 1% rat serum and 1% donkey serum for 1 h. To visualize the tdTomato expression, the cells were incubated with a primary rabbit anti-RFP antibody (Rockland, Pennsylvania, USA; Cat#600-401-379) overnight at 4 °C. The following day, after washing, the coverslips were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with a secondary donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 antibody, alongside fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD101 (APC, Moushi101; Invitrogen, California, USA; Cat#17-1011-82) and Ly6G (Alexa Fluor 488, 1A8; BioLegend, California, USA; Cat#127625). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Finally, the coverslips were washed three times, mounted, and imaged using confocal microscopy. Images were acquired by Andor Dragonfly (Oxford Instruments, Northern Ireland, UK, Cat#CR-DFLY-505) and processed with Imaris (v9.9; Oxford Instruments, Zurich, Switzerland).

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v10, California, USA). The groups containing normally distributed independent data were analyzed using a two-tailed Student's t-test (for two groups) or ANOVA (for more than two groups). Normally distributed dependent data were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA. The remaining data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U-test (for two groups) or Kruskal–Wallis test (H test; for more than two groups). Correlation analyses were performed between the percentage of CD101+ cells and tdTomato+ cells within the total neutrophil population (Ly6G+). Bivariate correlation was assessed using Pearson's correlation coefficient. The analysis was performed across various experimental conditions, including steady-state and pathological models (e.g., LPS stimulation), to ensure the consistency of reporter expression. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni correction or Dunn's multiple-comparison tests. P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All data are presented as means ± standard deviations, with replicate numbers specified in the figure legends.
