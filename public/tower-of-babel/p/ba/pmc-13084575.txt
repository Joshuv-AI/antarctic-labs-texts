Stratified Recovery Curves for Patient-Reported Outcomes After Primary Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Joshua P Rainey, Brenna E Blackburn, Claire R Kapron, Christopher E Pelt, Christopher L Peters, Jeremy M Gililland, Lucas A Anderson, Michael J Archibeck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere remains a lack of literature on how patient demographics and modifiable factors influence patient-reported outcome recovery curves following primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA). This study assessed how these factors influence the progression of patient-reported outcomes following primary TKA and to create visual aids to assist in perioperative counseling.MethodsProspective multicenter data from 2196 patients who underwent primary TKA were analyzed using generalized estimating equations to model longitudinal Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (KOOS JR) scores collected preoperatively and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Models were adjusted for repeated measures within patients and included interaction terms between time and demographic or modifiable risk factors to assess differences in recovery trajectories. Stratifications included age, sex, race, ethnicity, body mass index, smoking status, preoperative opioid use, and preoperative activity level. Clinical significance was defined using a minimal clinically important difference of 6.25 points.ResultsPatients who were less than 50 years old, women, black, Hispanic, underweight, or consumed opioids preoperatively all demonstrated significantly lower KOOS JR scores throughout the postoperative recovery curve. However, the rate at which women recovered was significantly greater at each postoperative time point. Black patients recovered at a similar rate compared to white patients, which was also the case for Hispanic vs non-Hispanic patients. While patients who used opioids preoperatively had lower overall KOOS JR scores, they demonstrated significantly quicker recoveries for the first 3 months postoperatively, but these differences became insignificant thereafter.ConclusionsSeveral patient demographics and modifiable risk factors impact patient’s recovery following primary TKA, which can assist in counseling patients regarding recovery following primary TKA.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, KOOS JR, Recovery trajectory, Patient-reported outcomes, Wearable technology, Risk stratification

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains one of the most common procedures performed in the United States and is the definitive surgical treatment for patients who suffer from end-stage osteoarthritis of the knee who have failed conservative management [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Despite the goals to reduce pain and improve quality of life, patient dissatisfaction following primary TKA is estimated to be above 10-15% [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Dissatisfaction following primary TKA is multifactorial, and several studies have evaluated how socioeconomic factors, comorbidities, mental health, and preoperative counseling impact patient satisfaction following primary TKA [[10], [11], [12], [13]].

Patient-reported outcomes are used to assess function and satisfaction following TKA [14,15]. The Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (KOOS JR) is frequently used to assess the postoperative recovery of patients undergoing primary TKA [16,17]. There is literature to suggest that preoperative counseling regarding patient expectations impacts patient satisfaction postoperatively [18,19]. To date, there remains a lack of literature that demonstrates how patient demographics and modifiable risk factors impact the KOOS JR recovery curve for patients who undergo primary TKA.

In our practice, the use of visual aids regarding general TKA recovery curves has been of substantial utility when counseling patients perioperatively [20]. The aim of this study was to assess how demographics and modifiable risk factors influence patients’ KOOS JR recovery curves following primary TKA to generate visual aids to counsel patients perioperatively. We hypothesized that younger age, male sex, non-minority race, normal body mass index (BMI), absence of preoperative opioid use, a nonsmoking status, and higher preoperative activity level would be associated with superior KOOS JR scores and more favorable recovery trajectories following primary TKA.

Material and methods

A secondary analysis was performed on the data obtained from a prospective multicenter trial evaluating if a remote care application and consumer wearables could provide an alternative to typical patient education and physical therapy after primary TKA (mymobility, Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN). Over 20 institutions from the United States, Australia, and the Netherlands participated in the initial prospective multicenter longitudinal cohort study of the mymobility platform (NCT03737149). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients were at least 18 years of age; (2) patients qualified for primary TKA based on physical exam and history; (3) patients owned an iPhone (Apple Inc, Cupertino, CA) capable of pairing to an Apple Watch (Apple Inc, Cupertino, CA) in order to use the mymobility app; (4) written informed consent was obtained; and (5) patients were mobile with no more than a single cane or crutch preoperatively. Exclusion criteria included ongoing alcohol or drug use disorder, member of a protected population (such as a prisoner or someone deemed mentally incompetent), systemic inflammatory arthropathies, participation in any other surgical intervention, physical therapy, or pain management study, or requiring simultaneous or staged bilateral TKAs fewer than 90 days apart. This study was approved by our internal institutional review board (IRB), local IRBs, and the Western IRB-Copernicus Group IRB.

Patient demographic data were obtained, including age, sex, BMI, race, ethnicity, smoking status, and preoperative opioid use. Preoperative daily step count was also collected, and patients were split into high activity based on median step count (daily step count ≥3313 steps) or low activity (daily step count <3313 steps) groups. Patients’ BMIs were classified according to the National Institutes of Health: BMI <18.5 kg/m2 = underweight; BMI ≥18.5-25.0 kg/m2 = normal; BMI >25 kg/m2 but <30 kg/m2 = overweight; BMI ≥30 kg/m2 [21]. These BMI categories were defined according to established National Institutes of Health classifications to ensure comparability with existing arthroplasty literature. Patients’ KOOS JR scores were collected preoperatively and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. The KOOS JR survey evaluates patients who suffer from osteoarthritis of the knee on 7 questions regarding stiffness, pain, and daily activities over the past week. Patients are asked to rate their symptoms as none, mild, moderate, severe, or extreme. Based on a patient’s response, a numerical score is computed. Responses are graded from 0 (none) to 4 (extreme). Based on the responses for the 7 individual questions, the raw summed score is converted to an interval score from 0 (total knee disability) to 100 (perfect knee health) [22,23]. The KOOS JR surveys were self-administered electronically through the mymobility platform. All participating sites utilized standardized electronic data capture protocols embedded within the application, ensuring consistent survey timing and administration across centers.

The KOOS JR recovery curves (change in scores postoperatively) were then stratified to the following scenarios: low vs high preoperative step count, female vs male, age <50 years vs age 50-60 years vs age 60-70 years vs age >70 years, underweight vs normal vs overweight vs obese, never smoker vs ever smoker, no preoperative opioids vs preoperative opioids, Hispanic vs non-Hispanic, and white vs black vs Asian vs other.

A total of 2196 patients were included. The majority of patients were non-Hispanic white women between 60 and 70 years of age. Additionally, most patients were never smokers (66.4%), did not endorse preoperative opioid use (92.4%), had a high preoperative step count of over 3313 daily steps (68.5%), and were obese (52.0%), as summarized in Table 1. Demographic data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Generalized estimating equations were selected to appropriately account for within patient correlation across repeated longitudinal measurements and to generate population-averaged estimates. Interaction terms between time and covariates allowed assessment of whether recovery slopes differed between groups over specific postoperative intervals [24]. Multivariable generalized estimating equation models included all measured demographic and modifiable risk factors simultaneously to account for potential confounding. This allowed estimation of independent associations between each factor and recovery trajectory while adjusting for the others. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for KOOS JR of 6.25 was used to define clinical significance, with one-half standard deviation being considered clinically detectable as previously described [20,25,26]. This threshold represents the smallest change perceptible and meaningful to patients following total joint arthroplasty and excludes smaller differences from being perceived as clinically significant despite potentially being statistically significant. A P value less than 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analyses were performed with Stata 14.2 (College Station, Texas) and SAS 9.4 (Cary, North Carolina).

Results

Demographics—age, sex, and race/ethnicity

In this analysis, differences in overall KOOS JR scores reflect persistent separation between recovery curves across all time points, whereas interaction terms represent differences in slope or rate of change between intervals. Older patients had significantly higher KOOS JR scores overall (all P values ≤.005), Table 2. This was significant for each age grouping of patients, and those who were older had larger improvements in KOOS JR scores overall. For example, patients between 50 and 60 years of age had an overall average KOOS JR score across all time points of 4.6 higher than those under the age of 50 years (P = .005). This difference was age-dependent with patients greater than 70 years overall having an average KOOS JR score of 8.1 points higher than those under the age of 50 years (P < .001), Table 2. Although KOOS JR scores of patients under the age of 50 recovered more quickly than any older group from baseline to 1 month postoperatively (all P values <.05), the rate of recovery was similar among all age groups from 1 month to 1 year postoperatively. The exception to this was patients greater than 70 years of age had a slower recovery rate from the 1 month to 3 months (P = .029) and 6 months to 1 year (P = .013) postoperatively when compared to patients under the age of 50 years.

Men had significantly higher KOOS JR scores throughout the entire postoperative recovery curve (P < .001) by an average of 4.3 points, Table 2. However, KOOS JR scores of women recovered more quickly at all time points (all P values <.001), Table 2.

Black patients had significantly lower KOOS JR scores compared to white patients by 7.6 points on average across all time points (P < .001), Table 2. However, there were no significant differences in rates of recovery at any point postoperatively when comparing black patients to white patients. Asian patients did have slower rates of recovery from preoperatively to 1 month (P = .002) and from 6 months to 1 year postoperatively (P = .032). Otherwise, Asian patients did not have further differences in rates of recovery or significantly different overall KOOS JR scores when compared to white patients. Hispanic patients had significantly lower overall KOOS JR scores by 3.5 points on average (P = .025), but both Hispanic and non-Hispanic patients recovered at equivalent rates throughout the first year following primary TKA, Table 2.

Modifiable risk factors—body mass index, smoking status, preoperative opioid use, and activity level

When using underweight patients (BMI <18.5 kg/m2) as the reference, patients who had normal range BMIs (BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m2) had significantly higher KOOS JR scores by 3.6 points on average (P = .017), Table 3. Similarly, overweight patients (BMI between 25 and 29.9 kg/m2) had significantly higher KOOS JR scores by 2.7 points on average (P = .045) when compared to underweight patients. There was no significant difference between overall KOOS JR scores between underweight and obese (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) patients (P = .322). The overall rates of recovery judged by improvement in KOOS JR scores were similar among all BMI stratifications and time points except for obese patients experiencing significantly greater improvements in KOOS JR scores from 6 to 12 months postoperatively when compared to underweight patients (P = .029), Table 3.

Patients who were ever smokers had no significant differences in overall KOOS JR scores when compared to never smokers, Table 3. Additionally, there were no significant differences in rates of improvement of KOOS JR scores at any point postoperatively for ever smokers vs never smokers, Table 3.

Overall, patients who endorsed preoperative opioid use had significantly lower KOOS JR scores by 5.2 points on average when compared to patients who denied preoperative opioid use (P < .001), Table 3. However, patients who were on preoperative opioids had greater recovery both at the preoperative to 1 month (P = .017) and 1 month to 3 month (P = .003) time intervals. There were no further significant differences in recovery rates beyond these time points, Table 3.

Patients were additionally stratified based on the physical activity metrics. Those with a preoperative daily step count of greater than 3313 steps were categorized as high activity patients, and those with a preoperative daily step count of less than 3313 steps were categorized as low activity patients. Patients who were in the high activity group had significantly higher KOOS JR scores by 2.4 points (P < .001), Table 3. However, low-activity patients recovered at a significantly faster rate compared to high-activity patients at all time points (all P values <.05), Table 3. This study defined 3313 steps as the threshold for high vs low preoperative activity, which was based on the median step count of the cohort. Due to the skewed distribution of preoperative steps, this resulted in an unequal split between groups. While this imbalance should be considered when interpreting these results, the cutoff allowed for consistent stratification.

Discussion

Although the majority of patients tend to be satisfied with their primary TKA, there remains a substantial minority of patients who do not experience expected improvement [9,27,28]. Patient demographics and modifiable risk factors are known to impact patients’ surgical outcomes following primary TKA, but this study is the first to evaluate how particular factors influence the KOOS JR recovery curve following primary TKA, both in terms of overall scores and rates of recovery. Given the available data, we were able to generate visual representations of stratified recovery curves and accomplished the aim of this study, Figure 1, Figure 2.

Older patients had significantly higher KOOS JR scores, and the difference was age-dependent when compared to those under the age of 50 years. However, the difference in overall KOOS JR score only met the MCID threshold when comparing patients who were greater than 70 years old vs those under 50 years old. These findings are in accordance with literature supporting that younger patients may be at risk for higher rates of dissatisfaction following primary TKA. Scott et al. prospectively assessed patients who underwent primary TKA and found that patients who were under 55 years of age had a 24.9% rate of dissatisfaction regardless of Kellgren-Lawrence grade of osteoarthritis [29]. When patients who had Kellgren-Lawrence grades of 1 or 2 underwent TKA, the dissatisfaction rate was 59% [29]. Additionally, Clement et al. also reported lower satisfaction for patients who underwent primary TKA who were also under the age of 55 years, although they noted that depression and poor mental health were significant confounders [30]. Although psychiatric diagnoses were not included in our study, the findings from these age-related recovery curves should give surgeons pause in proceeding with primary TKA in younger patients without thorough preoperative counseling on anticipated recovery.

Sex influenced both overall recovery curve scores and rate of recovery, but these differences did not meet the MCID threshold. Men had significantly higher KOOS JR scores which persisted over time, but they recovered more slowly. Based on available literature, it appears that women tend to have worse symptoms and are more willing to delay surgery despite persistent pain [31,32]. Women are postulated to be more skeptical of surgical outcomes and tend to delay surgery until their symptoms are quite severe [[33], [34], [35]]. The findings of these stratified recovery curves are consistent with prior literature but should be interpreted cautiously. Although there were statistically significant differences between sexes in terms of their recovery curves, these differences do not appear to be clinically significant, and patients should be counseled accordingly.

Regarding race, the only statistically and clinically significant difference in overall KOOS JR scores was found between white and black patients, with the latter having an average of 7.6 points lower KOOS JR scores. In terms of rates of recovery, there were no significant differences between white and black patients. However, Asian patients appeared to plateau in terms of recovery, compared to white patients, at the sixth postoperative month and onward. Additionally, Hispanic patients, on average, had KOOS JR scores 3.5 less than non-Hispanic patients, although this did not reach MCID. Otherwise, the rates of recovery were not significantly different between ethnicities. These findings are consistent with prior literature which has shown black and Hispanic patients to have significantly lower overall PROs [36,37]. Although these differences are multifactorial, there is literature to suggest that the decreased access to and utilization of arthroplasty among racial and ethnic minorities likely play a role in the worse symptoms at preoperative presentation [38]. Importantly, all patients followed similar recovery trajectories from preoperatively to 1 year postoperatively, and patients should be counseled accordingly. The one exception to this was Asian patients, who appeared to recover slower from preoperatively to 1 month postoperatively and 6 months to 1 year postoperatively. Although investigation remains as to the cause of these differences, Asian patients have a documented higher rate of nonhome discharges compared to white patients, which may offer partial explanation to these findings, as least in regard to short-term outcomes [[39], [40], [41]].

Modifiable risk factors, such as smoking, malnourishment, obesity, and preoperative opioid use have been associated with worse outcomes and patient satisfaction following primary TKA [[42], [43], [44], [45]]. Our findings have similarities but also notable differences to prior literature. For example, ever smokers had no significant differences in overall KOOS JR scores and did not recover differently than never smokers. One limitation to the available data is that ever smokers are not further stratified by those who smoke daily, occasionally, or who have quit smoking. Therefore, conclusions must be approached cautiously regarding the available data. Additionally, patients who were more active, denied preoperative opioid use, or had BMIs in the normal or overweight ranges all had significantly higher overall KOOS JR scores, but these failed to achieve MCID as well. Patients in the high physical activity cohort recovered slower than those in the lower physical activity cohort, and this could be related to patient expectations following surgery. However, further research is necessary to validate this claim. The overall hope for these recovery curves is that they will serve as an important visual aid when counseling patients and encourage preoperative optimization, Figure 1, Figure 2. Clinically, these stratified recovery curves may be incorporated into shared decision-making by presenting patients with expected trajectories aligned with their demographic and modifiable characteristics and provide objective data during discussion of preoperative optimization strategies.

Potential limitations

This study is not without limitations. Although this study evaluated the recovery curves of racial and ethnic minorities, the total number of minority patients enrolled was low. The cohort was predominantly non-Hispanic white, with only 4.4% black patients and 3.1% Hispanic patients. Although statistically significant differences were detected, small subgroup sizes limit precision of estimates and reduce generalizability to more diverse populations. Although the data used for this study were from an international, prospective multicenter study, the lack of diversity should be kept in mind when counseling patients and prompt further research efforts. Additionally, these data were derived from a study involving TKA patients utilizing wearable technology in lieu of typical patient education and physical therapy. We are unable to assess if compliance with this wearable technology influenced patients’ recovery curves compared to traditional patient education and physical therapy. We also want to highlight that in order for patients to be included in the original study, possession of an iPhone was mandatory, which limits generalizability of these findings to a broader population. Mandatory iPhone ownership and willingness to engage with wearable technology likely selected for patients with higher digital literacy and potentially higher socioeconomic status. This requirement may exclude patients with limited financial resources or limited access to technology, thereby limiting external validity. Unmeasured variables including socioeconomic status, mental health diagnoses, comorbidity burden, access to rehabilitation resources, and social support likely contributed to recovery heterogeneity. Prior studies have demonstrated that depression and psychological distress influence postoperative satisfaction and functional recovery, and these factors were not available within these data [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Despite these limitations, this study’s primary strength is that the data collected were from over 20 clinical sites, including several international locations, and is the first of its kind to evaluate how demographics and modifiable risk factors influence patients’ recovery following primary TKA.

It should be noted that the findings from this study represent associations rather than causal relationships. The observational nature of this secondary analysis precludes definitive conclusions regarding whether modification of individual risk factors would directly alter recovery trajectories. Future investigations should prioritize inclusion of more racially and socioeconomically diverse populations and incorporate psychological and socioeconomic variables. Additionally, randomized controlled trials targeting modifiable risk factors such as opioid cessation or structured preoperative conditioning could determine whether altering these factors meaningfully changes recovery curves.

Conclusions

There are multiple patient demographics and modifiable risk factors that negatively influence the KOOS JR recovery curve following primary TKA. Using these stratified recovery curves, patients can be counseled regarding their postoperative recovery following primary TKA utilizing data from a large, prospective multicenter trial. These recovery curves also provide further opportunities for investigation and intervention for patients who are at risk for slower recovery after primary TKA.
