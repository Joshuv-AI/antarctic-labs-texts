Improving Patient Sleep before Surgery: The Case for Sleep Prehabilitation
Authors: Daniel Sibley, Marc Sicova, Mandeep Singh, Ian Randall, Daniel Santa Mina
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep health includes quality, quantity, and timing of sleep and is negatively affected by surgery. Poor preoperative sleep health is associated with worse surgical outcomes, including greater morbidity and hospital length of stay.SummaryIn this narrative review, we describe the rationale and supporting evidence for preoperative sleep interventions (i.e., sleep prehabilitation) to improve perioperative outcomes. Our review highlights the link between impaired sleep health and surgical outcomes via several mechanisms including chronic, systemic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, and cognitive function. Instruments and intervention strategies to measure and improve sleep health, respectively, are discussed.Key MessagesWe conclude with sleep prehabilitation recommendations and identify research priorities for the field.

Introduction

Sleep health is a multidimensional pattern of sleep wakefulness that is characterized by appropriate quality, quantity, and timing of sleep that is sufficient for an individual’s optimal physical, mental, and neurobehavioural well-being [1]. Poor sleep health in the preoperative period (i.e., the time between surgery decision-making and the date of surgery) may be associated with worse surgical outcomes including greater pain (total hip arthroplasty) [2], increased risk of surgical complications (in patients with obstructive sleep apnoea [OSA]) [3], and longer hospital length of stay (LOS; fast-track abdominal hysterectomy) [4]. These issues may be intensified by perioperative (i.e., the time between admission for surgery and discharge) sleep disruption through several mechanisms including temperature, melatonin secretion, and the expression of sleep-regulating genes [5, 6]. Two common sleep disorders, insomnia and OSA, have a prevalence as high as 38% and 65%, respectively, in surgical patients awaiting surgery [7–11]. This is similar to rates of insomnia in the general population (16–48%) but much higher than the general population prevalence of OSA (9–24%). Yet, these constitute just 2 of 80 known sleep disorders and do not account for the high and under-recognized prevalence of disrupted sleep health that affects 60%–90% of surgical patients [12–14].

Healthcare providers and researchers have traditionally focused on improving sleep health in the postoperative period [15]; however, this may neglect the high willingness of patients to modify health behaviours prior to surgery [16, 17]. Prehabilitation capitalizes on this time period by instituting health behaviours aimed at creating a physiologic buffer against surgical stress [18]. Prehabilitation has been defined as “a process on the continuum of care that occurs between the time of cancer diagnosis and the beginning of acute treatment, includes physical and psychological assessments that establish a baseline functional level, identifies impairments, and provides targeted interventions that improve a patient’s health to reduce the incidence and the severity of current and future impairments” [19]. Prehabilitation program design varies widely but typically involve a triaged approach of screening, assessing, and (if necessary) intervening in the domains of exercise, nutrition, psychological support, or a combination of these (i.e., multimodal prehabilitation) [20, 21]. A large and growing body of evidence indicates prehabilitation may improve surgical complications, non-home discharge, LOS, and functional recovery [18].

Despite the preliminary association of sleep health and surgical outcomes, sleep health has infrequently been included in preoperative assessments or prehabilitation interventions. In this narrative review, we provide an overview of the epidemiology and aetiology of preoperative sleep health disruption and its impact on surgical outcomes. We also examine the state of the literature on sleep health screening, assessment, and intervention prior to surgery. We conclude with recommendations for sleep prehabilitation design and research priorities.

Epidemiology and Aetiology of Preoperative Sleep Disruption

The epidemiology of preoperative sleep disturbance underscores the rationale for sleep prehabilitation. A meta-analysis by Butris et al. [22] (2023) of 631 patients found a pooled preoperative sleep disturbance prevalence of 60% (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [PSQI] cut-off score >5). Preoperatively, 81% of patients who screen high risk for OSA have not been previously diagnosed [23]. In one study of 24 pulmonary lobectomy patients, 21% had chronic sleep disruption in the preoperative period (i.e., PSQI score >6) [24]. Despite this, 79% experienced postoperative acute sleep disruption, which was associated with functional disability 3 months after surgery. Other studies have noted significant decreases in sleep duration and even a doubling of sleep latency before surgery [25, 26]. Collectively, these data highlight preoperative sleep health disruption as a prevalent concern among preoperative patients and position sleep prehabilitation as potential intervention in the surgical pathway.

The aetiology of preoperative sleep disturbance is likely multifactorial and complex, involving physiological, psychosocial, and environmental components. One study of 39 patients awaiting surgery for breast cancer demonstrated device-measured total sleep time was negatively impacted by intrusive thoughts and emotional well-being [27]. Sleep health may also be negatively impacted by the same factors that can decrease preoperative functional capacity: disease progression, neoadjuvant treatment, psychological stress and anxiety, and lifestyle toxicity (i.e., sedentarism and poor nutritional choices) [28–30]. The prevalence of disrupted preoperative sleep health is also likely to increase owing to society’s growing reliance on light, technology and shift work [31] compounded by an older patient demographic with higher body mass index – established predictors of poor sleep health [32, 33]. Although identifying risk factors for sleep disturbance is essential for identifying at-risk patients, understanding the specific biological pathways linking sleep health to postoperative outcomes is essential for implementing targeted, effective interventions.

Mechanisms of Sleep Health’s Impact on Surgical Outcomes

Sleep health may be implicated in surgical morbidity via several pathophysiological mechanisms (Fig. 1). Insulin resistance and glucose control (pre- and post-op) are modifiable predictors of surgical risk through correlation with lower heart rate variability (HRV), compromised immunity, wound healing, and cognitive decline [33–37]. A study of 20 healthy males demonstrated 1 week of partial sleep deprivation (5 h per night in bed) significantly reduced insulin sensitivity by 20% [35]. These findings have been replicated in other experimental settings [36, 37], consistently demonstrating a 20–30% reduction in insulin sensitivity with just 4-7 nights of disturbed sleep. However, these experiments have not yet been performed within the surgical context. Meta-analytic data support a U-shaped relationship between sleep health and blood glucose in diabetics [38] and healthy adults [39], indicating a possible relationship between sleep and insulin resistance. In addition, just 2 weeks of sleep extension has been shown to improve insulin resistance [40]. Accordingly, insulin resistance is a predictor of surgical morbidity and mortality that is independently associated with sleep [41, 42].

Biological mechanisms linking preoperative sleep health and surgical outcomes.

Sleep also supports autonomic nervous system function where disruptions are clearly observable by adverse changes in blood pressure, heart rate, and sympathovagal balance [43, 44]. HRV, the time variation between consecutive heartbeats, is a marker of autonomic function that is independently related to cardiac mortality [45]. When subjected to partial sleep deprivation for 3 days, recreationally active adults without pre-existing sleep impairments experience statistically significant reductions in HRV (RMSSD baseline vs. day 3 = 33 [16] ms vs. 27 [8] ms, p < 0.05) [46]. As such, acute sleep disruption predisposes patients to impaired function of the autonomic nervous system (i.e., dysautonomia) that is implicated in difficulty with perioperative airway management, haemodynamic instability, and impaired gastrointestinal motility and may increase the risk of perioperative complications [47].

Surgery causes an acute inflammatory response to increase immunity and healing [48]. This adaptive response is blunted by chronic preoperative inflammation but may be amenable to intervention [49]. Interleukin (IL)-6 and C-reactive protein (CRP) are two inflammatory biomarkers associated with cardiovascular disease and perioperative complications [50]. Objectively measured sleep efficiency is significantly associated with both IL-6 and CRP [51], inflammatory markers implicated in surgical mortality and prolonged LOS [50]. Subjects exposed to experimentally induced sleep deprivation experience heightened levels of CRP [52]. Furthermore, one night of partial sleep deprivation has been shown to reduce natural killer cell activity by 28% in healthy males, which returns to baseline following normal sleep on the subsequent evening [53]. Accordingly, sleep disruption may promote weakened preoperative immunity through chronic inflammation, resulting in susceptibility to perioperative infection or delayed wound healing [48]. Innate immunity may also be compromised by insulin resistance through alterations in chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and oxidative burst that reduce the effectiveness of neutrophils and leukocytes [54].

With heightened preoperative inflammation and insulin resistance leading to a weaker peri- and postoperative immune response, patients are more susceptible to wound infection [55]. Very low-certainty evidence in human observational studies demonstrates higher risk of wound infection and dehiscence with poorer sleep quality from OSA [56]. Pre-clinical trials indicate wound healing is dependent on circadian biology and delayed wound healing may be related to the complex interplay between sleep deprivation with inflammatory mediators (e.g., increased TNF-α and IL-6), oxidative damage (e.g., increased 8-OHdG), and endocrine factors (e.g., increased corticosterone and corticotrophin) [57, 58].

IL-6 and von Willebrand factor (vWF) are two inflammatory biomarkers associated with cardiovascular disease and thrombosis implicated in perioperative complications [50]. Sleep health is significantly associated with both IL-6 and vWF [51, 59]. Previous literature has identified IL-6 as a significant predictor of surgical mortality and prolonged LOS [50], while vWF is a prognostic marker for deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism [60].

Metabolic catabolism (i.e., proteolysis and lipolysis) is the breakdown of complex molecules to produce energy, a process that, in situations with inadequate metabolic reserve, can result in loss of overall function and innate immunity [61]. Acute disruptions to sleep health have been shown to alter hormonal profiles in ways that are conducive to metabolic catabolism and hinder muscle protein synthesis (i.e., anabolism). Inadequate lean mass can have several important downstream effects. Rault et al. [62] (2020) found that one night of sleep deprivation resulted in decreased electrical activity of the diaphragm, pre-inspiratory motor amplitude, and tidal volume, resulting in a 50% reduction in diaphragmatic endurance. As such, acute preoperative sleep disruption may result in impaired pulmonary drive and alveolar gas exchange, increasing risk of atelectasis and pneumonia [63]. Accordingly, sleep disruption may favour metabolic catabolism, resulting in loss of function and pulmonary drive.

Postoperative delirium is an acute and reversible disruption to cognitive function caused in part by disruptions to sleep health and related to postoperative non-home discharge and mortality [64, 65]. Postoperative delirium is also highly predictive of cognitive trajectory. In one study, patients without delirium returned to their preoperative level of cognitive function 1 month after surgery, while patients with delirium had still not returned to their preoperative function 1 year after surgery [66]. Disruptions in preoperative sleep may lead to neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity, resulting in increased permeability to the tight junctions that comprise the blood-brain barrier [67]. This increased permeability leaves the cerebral vasculature vulnerable to ion imbalance, toxins, and cytokines that precede delirium [68]. Loss of cognitive function and attention experienced with delirium as a result of sleep disruption may also be related to an imbalance between synapse formation and degradation (i.e., synaptic homoeostasis) [69]. The relationship between the glymphatic system and risk of delirium has been postulated but not well described [70]. Glymphatic clearing of toxins and proteins from the brain occurs at a rate that is 90% greater during sleep than wakefulness [71]. This process clears amyloid-beta and tau, proteins associated with neurodegeneration [72]. As such, disrupted sleep may impede the glymphatic system’s ability to cleanse the brain, resulting in susceptibility to delirium. Outlining the potential mechanisms of sleep health disturbance and surgical complications provides a foundation for experimental research and a rationale for sleep health intervention.

Implementing Sleep Prehabilitation

The heterogenous mechanisms of multimodal prehabilitation to reduce surgical complications have been reviewed previously and involve a complex interplay of intervention effects across multiple organ systems [73]. There is significant overlap between the potential mechanisms of benefit of sleep prehabilitation and that of other prehabilitation modalities. Accordingly, identifying patients that would most benefit from sleep prehabilitation using screening and assessment prior to targeted intervention promotes the most appropriate allocation of prehabilitation resources [74].

Screening

Given the scarcity of resources and the need for triaged patient care, screening all patients for surgical risk as soon as possible following a decision to perform surgery is a recommended component of prehabilitation [75]. Although there are over 80 known sleep disorders [76], the global sleep assessment questionnaire (GSAQ) has been suggested by Klingman et al. [77] as the most suitable screening tool for clinical practice, given its psychometric quality, comprehensiveness, and brevity. The tool is self-administered and probes symptoms that may aid clinicians to uncover underlying disorders or impairment, though further validation in clinical contexts is needed. Predetermined thresholds for further follow-up may be established based on institutional resources for assessment and intervention. To date, clinically important thresholds using the GSAQ have not been established [78]. Once a symptom and corresponding disorder is suspected, a disorder-specific screening tool may be used. For instance, patients who report difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or feeling poorly rested may be screened for insomnia using the Athens Insomnia Scale [79]. Patients who report loud snoring may be screened using the STOP-Bang [80]. Alternatively, the AIS and STOP-Bang may be implemented as standard of care screening tools. These tools have thresholds previously determined to be clinically important for identifying disordered sleep but may increase burden on clinicians and patients and miss other important sleep disorders (e.g., restless leg syndrome). Assessment and Measurement

The growing evidence highlighting the importance of sleep has resulted in calls to include sleep health assessment in routine clinical assessments [84]. The Society of Anesthesia and Sleep Medicine highlights the detrimental influence of disturbed sleep in hospital settings and calls for the inclusion of sleep health intervention within surgical recovery protocols [85]. Routine assessment of sleep health affirms its importance and may improve patient engagement by involving them in their care [86]. Device, self-reported, and objective measures of sleep are often validated using gold-standard polysomnography (PSG), a process whereby several physiological parameters are collected to detect sleep versus wakefulness and sleep architecture [87]. Instruments and sensors including electroencephalogram, electromyogram, and electrooculogram are used to measure sleep stages, limb movement, and body position that, in tandem with other parameters (e.g., oxygen saturation and airflow), can be used to detect sleep, diagnose sleep disorders, and evaluate the response to sleep intervention [88]. The expensive equipment and specialized training required to operate and interpret PSG necessitate its use as a triaged rather than standard element of care. As such, self-reported measures of sleep are commonly used in clinical practice and research.

Sleep diaries are considered the gold-standard self-reported measure of sleep and have been recommended for clinical use for decades [83, 89]. However, sleep diaries may produce results that are significantly different than PSG [90]. Other limitations to sleep diaries include low adherence (67%) [91], lapses in memory, and poor comprehension to questions [92]. Brief and validated measures for sleep health as a “vital sign,” similar to those for physical activity [93] and malnutrition [94], may further aid routine sleep measurement. The RU SATED scale measures several important sleep health domains including satisfaction, alertness, timing, efficiency, and duration (i.e., 5 items) in under 2 min [1]. The tool can be self- or clinician-administered and its preliminary validity and test-retest reliability for measuring sleep health have been established [95]. The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) also has brief sleep measurement tools with strong psychometric properties. The computer adaptive test for sleep disturbance contains 27 items, but 4-, 6-, and 8-item short forms are available [96]. The PROMIS Sleep Disturbance scale (PROMIS SD) demonstrates greater measurement precision than existing sleep measures (e.g., PSQI) and was developed for use in clinical and research settings [82]. The PROMIS SD was found to have high internal and predictive validity in older adults and moderate test-retest reliability in women with fibromyalgia [97, 98]. The PSQI is perhaps the most common self-reported sleep health assessment tool and demonstrates strong validity, reliability, and internal consistency compared to PSG and in heterogeneous clinical populations [99, 100]. However, the optimal time between repeated measurements is unclear, and it is not intended to screen for sleep disorders [101]. Although self-reported sleep measures are most commonly used due to their accessibility, their limitations may necessitate other sleep measures within the prehabilitation context.

Since the pandemic, there has been a growing reliance on remote patient monitoring to alleviate healthcare system burden [102]. Device-measured sleep health presents unique insights into patient sleep patterns while empowering patients to exert greater autonomy over their health. Actigraphy detects movements using an accelerometer to determine sleep versus wake states as well as sleep parameters and has been validated extensively against PSG [103]. Actigraphy has been used to identify sleep impairment up to 1 month after surgery, differentiate between delirious and non-delirious patients, and assess the effect of pharmacological interventions on sleep health [104]. Commercially available activity trackers for the purposes of measuring sleep health are an emerging area in clinical practice [105]. Devices such as the Fitbit Inspire series have been used to assess sleep health in clinical research [106] and demonstrate appropriate validity against PSG [107], indicating their potential value for self-management. Although commercial trackers can measure movement and HRV during sleep [105] and effectively measure sleep vs. wake states (sensitivity ∼85%), the specificity of these tools ranges from 50% to 69% [105]. That is, they correctly identify sleep epochs but are less proficient at identifying wake epochs compared to PSG [105]. They are also limited for measuring sleep architecture (i.e., different stages of sleep) and thus ill equipped for measuring sleep latency, efficiency, and overall sleep quality [81, 108]. A recent scoping review by Kang and Exworthy [109] (2022) highlights the role of wearable technology to facilitate patient behaviour change and increase communication between the patient and the healthcare provider. The authors also found uptake is context specific and may be impeded by perceptions of usage complexity, lack of accuracy, and concerns with privacy [109].

Unfortunately, self-reported and device-assisted measures of sleep often possess weak agreement. For example, in older adults and individuals with insomnia, self-reported and device measures of sleep health may measure distinct constructs, highlighting the need for concomitant use [110, 111]. Ultimately, the choice of sleep measurement needs to be carefully considered alongside contextual factors such as the patient population, available resources, and the sleep measurements being prioritized.

Intervention

Preoperative acute sleep health (i.e., <30 days before surgery) is a predictor of recovery and can be modified over a period of time consistent with the surgical waiting period [112]. Epidemiological and experimental data reveal relationships between sleep and physical fitness, mental health, nutrition, and stress that underscore several strategies that may be used to improve sleep health [113–116]. To date, sleep outcomes in prehabilitation trials have been sparsely investigated. We recently reviewed 47 preoperative pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions that include a perioperative sleep health outcome and found that few were specifically designed to improve sleep and were of modest methodological quality [117]. Notwithstanding these concerns, 13/17 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that measured sleep quality, most commonly measured via the PSQI, significantly improved this outcome and 7/8 trials significantly improved sleep duration.

A retrospective review of 878 gastrointestinal surgery patients demonstrated that prehabilitation comprising exercise, nutrition, and psychological support significantly improved self-reported sleep health versus a usual care control 1 day before surgery (e.g., falling asleep time via PSQI M [SD] 3.4 [0.5] vs. 3.1 [0.4], p < 0.001, d = 0.66) [118]. In contrast, a recent pilot RCT by Atoui et al. [119] (2023) found no effect of home-based multimodal prehabilitation on sleep health compared to usual care among 102 colorectal cancer patients using the PSQI and a wearable tracker. The differences may be a result of variations in sample size, surgical subgroup, and delivery setting. These 2 studies comprise the current prehabilitation literature that evaluates sleep outcomes. As such, the benefit of multimodal prehabilitation interventions for sleep health is unclear owing to limited data. Our systematic review outlines additional strategies that may be used within sleep prehabilitation, summarized in the following section.

Pharmacological Strategies

Pharmacological strategies, such as melatonin, celecoxib, and zolpidem, are commonly used to improve sleep health in clinical settings. At least 10 trials examining the preoperative use of these agents have included measures of sleep health [120–124]. Hansen et al. [122] performed a placebo-controlled RCT 1 week prior to surgery in 54 breast cancer patients. Participants were randomly assigned to 6 mg of oral melatonin or placebo administered 1 h prior to sleep for 13 weeks. The authors observed no differences in sleep quality in those who received melatonin compared to placebo from 2 days preoperatively to 8 days postoperatively, as measured by a visual analogue scale and the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale. Shakya et al. [124] administered 10 mg of zolpidem each night before bed for 7 nights beginning 2 nights prior to surgery among 160 unilateral total hip arthroplasty patients. Sleep quality improved 3 weeks postoperatively in the intervention group compared to placebo as measured by the PSQI. The intervention group also demonstrated a significantly shorter length of hospital stay. Despite improvements in sleep quality and LOS, the administration of pharmacological sleep aids, including zolpidem, has previously been associated with an increased risk of falls and delirium [125, 126]. Mixed findings preclude definitive statements regarding the effectiveness of pharmacological sleep aids initiated preoperatively. Future research should evaluate the cost-benefit of using pharmacological sleep interventions as it relates to safety, efficacy, and availability of non-pharmacological options.

Non-Pharmacological Strategies

The gold-standard non-pharmacological treatment for sleep disturbance is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) [127]. Meta-analyses have established and confirmed CBT-I as an effective treatment for insomnia [128–130]. Our review uncovered 29 trials that used non-pharmacological sleep interventions including education, exercise, nutrition, and psychological intervention [131]. No trial used CBT-I. Yang et al. [26, 132] conducted 2 RCTs of computer-assisted CBT (i.e., not specifically for insomnia) versus usual care and observed mixed results for sleep health. At least 3 RCTs [112, 133, 134] comprising 230 patients delivered preoperative education to coronary artery bypass graft and breast cancer patients and significantly improved sleep health versus control [112, 133, 134]. Malek et al. [112] significantly improved sleep quality the night before coronary artery bypass graft using at least 2 sessions of 45–60 min of a nurse-led face-to-face supportive-educational intervention. The study contained a sample of 160 participants and measured sleep using the Groningen Sleep Quality Scale. The education sessions focused on building rapport, discussing the procedure and correcting patients’ misconceptions, and stress management methods [112]. Although preoperative education sessions have proven effective for improving patient sleep health, they seldom contain sleep-specific education.

Farr et al. [135] (2001) delivered a sleep-specific intervention for 4 preoperative days among 26 males awaiting maxillofacial surgery. The intervention comprised awakening 2 h earlier and retiring 2 h earlier, ingesting caffeine immediately upon awakening and abstaining for the rest of the day, and consuming a high-protein breakfast to minimize circadian rhythm disruption. Despite the sleep-specific nature of this intervention, no differences in circadian temperature rhythm were observed between the intervention and caffeine placebo/usual behaviour group. The 4-day intervention period may not have been long enough to elicit benefit. In 18 short sleepers (i.e., ≤7 h per night average), increasing sleep duration with a time-in-bed extension intervention the week before knee or hip replacement surgery resulted in less pain and morphine use after surgery versus control [136]. Accordingly, non-pharmacological sleep interventions have demonstrated promise for improving preoperative sleep health, though the gold-standard CBT-I intervention remains untested.

Once an intervention is established, it must be implemented in a way that is feasible and effective. An interprofessional approach may be needed with different tiers of care depending on screening and assessment findings. For example, patients screening at high risk for OSA may be treated with continuous positive airway pressure, which has demonstrated lower rates of pulmonary complications and decreased LOS than high-risk patients who did not receive continuous positive airway pressure [137]. Identifying patient risk and delivering appropriate individualized care plans are paramount for intervention feasibility and effectiveness [138].

Following initial screening and assessment, patients may be assigned a Red, Amber, or Green (RAG) rating [138]. Red represents patients with complex sleep health needs resulting in severe impairment or disability and low functioning; they require multimodal treatment from a clinician with specialized sleep training [139]. In addition to OSA and insomnia, patients screening high-risk for other rarer sleep disorders including central sleep apnoea, hypoventilation syndromes, and movement disorders (e.g., restless leg syndrome and periodic limb movement disorder [PLM]) require referral to specialized sleep care. These patients are unlikely to benefit from generalized sleep education and if untreated, these sleep disorders increase surgical risk [140].

Amber represents patients with acute or chronic sleep health disturbance that may not meet the clinical threshold for disorder (i.e., low-moderate risk); they require targeted intervention led by registered and allied health professionals. Green represents patients with mild sleep health impairment, concern for future impairment, or no current sleep health impairment. These patients may receive universal sleep health intervention delivered by any prehabilitation team member with the intention of building healthy sleep habits and reinforcing existing ones. In addition to interdisciplinary care, appropriate sleep prehabilitation requires uptake, support, and commitment from multiple levels of healthcare institutions. With guidance from a recent position paper by the Society of Sleep Anesthesia and Sleep Medicine on sleep during hospitalization, we propose the integration of sleep prehabilitation at the cultural, operational, and policy levels [85]. At the cultural level, sleep health can be recognized as important for both general well-being and successful surgery. This may include staff and patient education, using the epidemiological and mechanistic information provided in this review. At the operational level, developing standardized screening, measurement, and intervention protocols; integrating sleep intervention into existing care pathways; and quality assessment compared to standards developed a priori may improve patient care [139]. At the policy level, the provision of sleep healthcare should be considered an important component of the preoperative period.

The level of uptake will be contingent on the availability of resources including but not limited to time and cost. Prehabilitation guidelines recommend intervening in patients with the highest risk, allocating resources to those in greatest need [75]. As mentioned previously, identifying and treating patients with the greatest sleep disturbance necessitates appropriate screening and referral pathways. Regarding time, the feasibility of implementing sleep prehabilitation is dependent on the “prehabilitation window,” the time between initiating prehabilitation and surgery. The prehabilitation window is mainly contingent on referral pathways (from surgical team to prehabilitation team) and the surgical subgroups being referred. For example, bariatric surgeries are often scheduled to allow sufficient time for preoperative weight loss, which improves surgical outcomes [141]. As such, the average prehabilitation window in this surgical group can be 8.5 months. This window is sufficient to initiate PSG sleep studies, diagnose sleep disorders, and initiate treatment [141]. Other surgical subgroups, including cardiac and oncology surgeries, experience far shorter wait times; the mean prehabilitation window from an evaluation of a clinically integrated prehabilitation service was about 1 month [142]. Accordingly, efficient referral systems and routine sleep screening facilitating appropriate patient selection are priorities for implementing sleep prehabilitation. With resource limitations, preoperative screening may be used to inform the use of peri- and postoperative intervention. Of course, future initiatives to integrate sleep prehabilitation into clinical workflow at the cultural, operational, and policy levels will require data supporting the feasibility, implementation, and cost-effectiveness of sleep prehabilitation.

Future Directions

The negative impact of impaired sleep is unequivocal [143], as is the availability of efficacious treatment [144]. However, both the consequences of impaired preoperative sleep health and the effectiveness of preoperative interventions remain uncertain [117, 145]. Epidemiological data on the benefit of exercise, nutrition, and psychological intervention on sleep health are conflicted by RCTs of unimodal preoperative interventions (i.e., exercise and education) demonstrating no improvement to sleep health [146, 147]. Although meta-analyses have established the benefit of CBT-I, it has not yet been used within the context of prehabilitation. A derivative of CBT-I that may be particularly useful within prehabilitation is brief behavioural treatment for insomnia (BBTI) [148]. The BBTI intervention consists of reducing time in bed, standardizing sleep and wake times, going to bed only when sleepy, and staying in bed only if asleep [149]. BBTI maintains the success of CBT-I as a first-line insomnia treatment while addressing some of its shortcomings: time burden and the need for highly specialized intervention deliverers. A meta-analysis of BBTI among older adults with chronic insomnia demonstrated significantly improved sleep quality (PSQI) relative to control [150]; improvements in actigraphy-assessed sleep parameters also significantly favoured BBTI [150]. With consideration of the findings of this narrative review, several recommendations can be made: (i) sleep health should be measured at each clinical encounter; (ii) sleep intervention in the preoperative period should be available, with the intensity of the intervention commensurate to the degree of sleep disturbance; and (iii) self-reported and device-assisted measures of sleep should be used concomitantly at key prehabilitation assessment time points.

Along with establishing a gold-standard sleep intervention, the research priorities of sleep prehabilitation are aligned with those of the field of prehabilitation: establishing high-certainty evidence of effectiveness, assessing long-term effectiveness, and implementation studies with evaluation of cost-effectiveness [151–153]. Furthermore, the use of validated measures and comprehensive reporting of interventions are essential for consistent evaluation of clinical trials that may be synthesized [117]. An exploration of the integration of wearable trackers within the clinical context is warranted along with an evaluation of satisfaction and effectiveness. Consensus regarding the optimal strategies for screening, measurement, intervention, and implementation are priorities for future research, though suitable strategies are proposed in this review.

Summary and Conclusion

Prehabilitation is a burgeoning area of study with promising evidence supporting its use to improve patient-centred and clinical outcomes. Despite the biological relationship between sleep health and surgical stress, as well as the deleterious impact of surgery on sleep health, it has been largely ignored as a prehabilitation target. In other contexts (e.g., postoperatively), sleep health interventions have demonstrated proof of principle as safe and efficacious. The implementation of sleep prehabilitation requires screening, assessment and measurement tools with acceptable psychometric properties as well as empirically supported intervention strategies. Other than a recognition of the importance of sleep, shifts at the cultural, operational, and policy levels are required to effectively improve sleep health through preoperative intervention. Sleep prehabilitation remains primarily theoretical until supported by future research.
