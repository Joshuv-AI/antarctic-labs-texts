‘Just opt for caesarean section and pick the birthdate’: a qualitative study exploring women’s delivery preferences after caesarean section in urban Tanzania
Authors: Daudi Gidion, Columba Mbekenga, Miriam Mgonja, Marleen Temmerman, Muzdalifat Abeid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo explore perspectives, preferences and decision-making regarding mode of delivery after a previous caesarean section among women in urban Tanzania.DesignA descriptive qualitative study using semi-structured in-depth interviews. Data were analysed using thematic analysis.SettingA private tertiary hospital in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania.Participants11 women with one previous caesarean section who delivered at the study site were interviewed on the third day after delivery. Participants were purposively sampled to include both modes of delivery: five women underwent repeat caesarean section and six attempted a trial of labour after caesarean (TOLAC).ResultsThematic analysis identified four key themes: (1) social and cultural influences, (2) physical and emotional experiences, (3) desire for emotional support and relief from labour pain and (4) balancing medical advice with personal beliefs. Misinformation, inconsistent counselling and strong social influences were prominent factors that often led women to prefer repeat caesarean section even when vaginal birth after caesarean (VBAC) was medically feasible.ConclusionsWomen’s delivery preferences after caesarean section are shaped by social influences, personal experiences and inconsistent healthcare counselling. While findings suggest opportunities to improve patient-centred communication, broader implementation of VBAC requires alignment across multiple levels of the healthcare system, including provider training, institutional protocols and supportive infrastructure which were beyond the scope of this study. Further research incorporating healthcare provider perspectives and diverse settings is needed.Keywords: Decision Making, OBSTETRICS, Pregnancy, Antenatal, GYNAECOLOGY

Introduction

Caesarean section (CS) rates, defined as the proportion of all births that occur via CS in a population over a specified period, are rising globally, with one in five (21%) deliveries performed by caesarean,1 2 far above the recommended WHO threshold of 15%.3 Although variation exists, with some countries performing fewer and others far above the WHO threshold.4 Evidence indicates that CS rates exceeding this level do not confer additional benefits to maternal or neonatal health.5 Despite this, the primary CS, which is the first CS performed on a woman, continues to increase, leading to most women with a previous scar undergoing repeat CSs. Analyses using the Robson classification across sub-Saharan Africa identify prior caesarean as a major contributor to the increase in overall CS rates.6 In Tanzania, caesarean rates among low-risk groups have been rising, with prior caesarean as a leading contributor.7 A study conducted in Ethiopia reported that women with a previous scar accounted for 21.1% of all caesarean deliveries,8 while a study in Sierra Leone reported that 95% of women with a previous caesarean underwent repeat surgery, contributing to an overall caesarean rate of 49.6%.9 A WHO multicountry analysis across 21 countries confirmed that women with a prior scar represent the single largest contributor to rising caesarean rates globally.10 Currently, there is variability in the average rate of CSs worldwide, with Latin America and the Caribbean having the highest rate at 42.8% and sub-Saharan Africa having the lowest rate at 5%.2 6 In Tanzania, CS increased from 7% in 2015 to 11% in 2022, a trend mirrored across other countries in the region.11 This increase is characterised by significant disparities, where 24% of births in the richest households are by CS, compared with 4% in the poorest, and 30% of deliveries in private health facilities are by CS, compared with 12% in public health facilities.5 6

A woman who has undergone a CS has two possible options for the mode of delivery in the subsequent pregnancy: to undergo an elective repeat caesarean section (ERCS) or a trial of labour, which may end up in vaginal birth after caesarean section (VBAC) or emergency CS. Studies have shown that VBAC is a safe mode of delivery,12 13 with a success rate range from 55% to 85%.14 15 Reflecting this evidence, major international guidelines support trial of labour after caesarean (TOLAC) as a reasonable option for women with one prior low-transverse CS. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists and the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists16 17 similarly support planned VBAC, noting that it is associated with fewer complications than repeat caesarean. The WHO recommends non-clinical interventions, including structured counselling and decision aids, to reduce unnecessary CSs and promote informed choice.18 Despite this fact, in current practice it is largely replaced by repeat CS, an observation explained by a combination of provider and patient concerns and preferences.19

In sub-Saharan Africa, VBAC practice remains particularly limited, with few countries having adopted formal national guidelines, leaving clinical decision-making largely at the discretion of individual providers.20

Repeat CS is associated with both long-term and short-term maternal and perinatal morbidity and may lead to complications in subsequent pregnancy, such as abnormal placentation (placenta accreta spectrum disorder), uterine rupture, hysterectomy and long-term complications, such as chronic pelvic pain and intra-abdominal adhesion.21,23 Beyond immediate obstetric outcomes, caesarean delivery has wider health, social and economic consequences that extend beyond the mother-newborn dyad.24 On the other hand, the rising caesarean delivery rate places a significant economic burden on the healthcare system, as CSs are more costly than vaginal deliveries.25 It also increases the burden of care for patients and their families.26

Previous studies have identified several factors influencing delivery mode among women with previous CS. These include fear of pain, birth complications and emergency CS27 preference for predictability, partner views and media portrayals.28 Systemic issues, such as poor maternity care and limited counselling, also shape preferences.29 Women favouring VBAC cite quicker recovery, fewer complications and empowerment.30 Knowledge gaps and lack of individualised information often leave women making decisions based on fear or experience.31 Importantly, these studies focused more on the clinical and demographic factors influencing the mode of delivery, leaving out women’s perspectives, preferences and decision-making.30 31 Additionally, it is not certain to what extent the factors described to influence women’s choice of mode of delivery are driven by pregnant women themselves, rather than being influenced by healthcare providers/systems.32

Drawing inspiration from the health belief model (HBM)33 and findings from previous studies, we set out to describe several factors influencing mode of delivery, namely patient-related factors including perceived benefits, perceived complications and the perceived sense of security associated with the different delivery options. Understanding women’s perspectives, preferences and decision-making regarding the mode of delivery is essential to inform responsive and supportive maternal care strategies.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a descriptive qualitative study. This approach allowed for exploration of women’s perspectives, preferences and decision-making regarding the mode of delivery; this work is reported according to Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research Checklist (COREQ)34 (online supplemental appendix 1).

Study setting

This study was conducted at the Aga Khan Hospital in Dar es Salaam (AKHD), Tanzania, from April to August 2024. AKHD is a 170-bed capacity private tertiary facility and a teaching site for Aga Khan University medical residents. The hospital offers comprehensive maternal healthcare services, including antenatal care (ANC), intrapartum management and delivery.

In 2024, the ANC clinic registered an average of 135 women each month. The clinic is staffed by nine full-time obstetricians and gynaecologists, four visiting specialists and three midwives responsible for assessment, counselling and care coordination. On average, women attend seven ANC visits during their pregnancies.

In 2024, the hospital recorded 1663 births, 56.4% of which were CS. Among these, 32% were repeat CS for women with a previous scar. Despite a policy that supports TOLAC, 16 women of those with one previous scar (0.05%) attempted a TOLAC, and 12 of them successfully achieved a VBAC.

The hospital permits TOLAC and follows a structured counselling process. Women are informed of the delivery options by their obstetrician and, if consenting to TOLAC, sign formal documentation. These discussions are revisited at each subsequent ANC visit.

The maternity unit includes five private labour rooms and is staffed by seven midwives who work 12-hour shifts. Midwives lead intrapartum care, supported by general doctors, residents and specialists who are on call throughout.

Participants and recruitment

We recruited postpartum women who had one previous caesarean scar and delivered their second baby at the AKHD. Eligible participants were identified in the postnatal ward and approached on the third day after delivery. Women were eligible if they were aged ≥18 years, had a previous CS for a non-recurrent indication and delivered at AKHD during the index pregnancy. Women with additional obstetric risks, such as pre-eclampsia, multiple gestation or other complications that necessitated CS during the index delivery, were excluded to minimise confounding factors.

Sampling and sample size

We employed purposive sampling by reviewing daily postnatal ward admission records to identify women who met the inclusion criteria. To achieve maximum variation, we sampled across predefined dimensions: age, education and current delivery mode (VBAC or repeat CS). Data saturation was assessed iteratively; after each interview, the research team reviewed the transcript and compared emerging findings against previously identified concepts. From the ninth interview onwards, no new codes or themes were generated and two additional interviews were conducted to confirm this assessment.35

Data collection tool

An interview guide was developed based on previous studies.27 31 It explored factors influencing delivery preferences, decision-making and birth experiences. A single pilot interview was conducted with a woman meeting the study’s inclusion criteria to assess clarity and flow, after which minor revisions were made to the interview guide. The semi-structured interview guide is provided in online supplemental appendix 2.

Data collection procedures

Individual in-depth interviews lasted 30–40 min and were conducted in private rooms by a Swahili-speaking male medical doctor (DG) with experience in qualitative research, who was not previously known to the participants. All interviews were conducted in Swahili and audio recorded. At the end of each interview, the interviewer summarised the key points discussed and sought immediate feedback from the participant to ensure accurate understanding. To enhance credibility, member checking was conducted at the end of each interview. This was done with all 11 participants at the interview premises, and participants often expanded on their responses, enriching the data with additional insights.

Data analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Kiswahili. The transcripts were translated into English by an independent bilingual translator to ensure accuracy and preserve the original meaning, and were supplemented by field notes and reviewed for accuracy by cross-checking against the original recording. Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-phase reflexive thematic analysis framework.36 Adopting an inductive approach that allowed themes to emerge from the data. In the first phase, two researchers (DG and MA) independently familiarised themselves with the data by repeatedly reading the transcripts line by line. In the second phase, both researchers independently generated initial codes. Intercoder reliability was established by comparing and discussing the initial codes; this coding approach was then applied to the remaining transcripts, with ongoing refinement as the analysis progressed. Coding was performed on the English-translated transcripts using NVivo V.14.37 Where English translations could not fully capture culturally specific expressions related to childbirth, the original Kiswahili terms were retained alongside their English translations during coding. Codes were compared, collapsed and organised to identify patterns, relationships and higher-order themes and subthemes. The coding researchers reviewed and discussed the transcripts to reach consensus on emerging themes. The final themes were presented to the wider research team to ensure the analysis accurately represented the data. We used the COREQ checklist34 to guide reporting (online supplemental appendix 1).

Reflexivity

The primary interviewer and coder (DG) is a medical doctor working within the healthcare system where this study was conducted. This insider position offered familiarity with the clinical context and facilitated rapport with participants but also carried a risk of assumptions shaped by clinical training. To manage this, reflexive memos were maintained throughout data collection and analysis, documenting how the researcher’s clinical background may have influenced interpretation. Regular team discussions were held to critically examine emerging themes and challenge potential biases. The involvement of a second independent coder and the wider research team in theme refinement provided additional safeguards against interpretation shaped by a single perspective.

In the third phase, codes were grouped into preliminary themes and subthemes. The researchers then met to compare their coding, discuss discrepancies and reach consensus through dialogue. In the fourth and fifth phases, themes were reviewed and refined collaboratively with the wider research team to ensure they accurately reflected the data. In the final phase, themes were defined and named. Analytic decisions, including code definitions, merging or splitting of themes and rationale for final theme selection, were documented in a shared audit trail throughout the process.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Social and cultural influences on childbirth decisions

Women’s delivery preferences were shaped by their social environment. Participants described receiving advice and opinions from family members, partners, peers and informal media sources, which often influenced how they perceived safety, recovery, pain and the meaning of childbirth. In several accounts, women described tension between personal preferences and socially constructed expectations regarding what constituted an acceptable or desirable mode of birth.

Influence of family and close social circles

Family members and close social networks frequently influenced women’s decisions regarding delivery mode. Some participants described being encouraged to undergo repeat CSs because it was perceived as more convenient, controlled and less painful than labour.

As one participant explained,

Everyone else told me, ‘Do you really want all that pain? Just opt for it and pick the birthdate for your baby’. (RCS 06)

These contrasting perspectives suggest that women’s delivery preferences were not formed in isolation but rather negotiated within broader family and cultural expectations. Decisions regarding childbirth were therefore influenced not only by medical information but also by social meanings attached to pain, womanhood, recovery and maternal identity.

Impact of media and informal information sources

Participants described how anecdotal stories, social conversations and media portrayals shaped perceptions regarding caesarean delivery and VBAC. Informal narratives often amplified fear and uncertainty, particularly when women lacked clear and consistent counselling from healthcare providers. Several participants described hearing negative stories regarding surgery and postoperative complications, contributing to fear surrounding caesarean delivery, as one participant shared,

I was very afraid because people say when you deliver by operation, you will have complications. (VBAC 01)

At the same time, some participants questioned media portrayals that appeared to minimise the realities of postoperative recovery.

Even the day before, I was asking why, in the media, we see actors saying they are okay after an operation; they are moving around fine. For me, getting out of bed is difficult. (RCS 06)

These findings suggest that women frequently relied on anecdotal experiences and media representations to interpret the risks and benefits of different delivery options. In the absence of balanced information, these narratives appeared to reinforce misconceptions regarding recovery, safety and the physical demands associated with childbirth.

Role of previous birth experiences, body image and autonomy in shaping physical and emotional recovery

Women described childbirth and recovery in terms extending beyond clinical outcomes. Experiences of mobility, independence, body image, emotional fulfilment and the ability to resume caregiving responsibilities all shaped how participants evaluated different delivery options.

Postpartum body image and autonomy in recovery

Participants who preferred VBAC commonly associated vaginal birth with quicker recovery, physical freedom and independence during the postpartum period. They viewed caesarean recovery as involving dependency, restricted movement and prolonged physical discomfort. One participant explained:

If I had chosen a C-section, I would have just come out of the operating theatre, unable to walk or bend. (VBAC 03)

Body image also emerged as an important consideration in women’s preferences. Some participants associated vaginal birth with returning more quickly to their pre-pregnancy body.

I want a quick recovery and to avoid having a big belly. (VBAC 02)

Collectively, these accounts suggest that women evaluated childbirth not only through clinical safety but also through how different delivery modes affected bodily autonomy, self-image and everyday functioning after birth.

Motherhood and fulfilment

Women linked the mode of delivery with their anticipated ability to care for their children and actively participate in motherhood. Participants who preferred vaginal birth often described recovery in practical terms, emphasising the importance of mobility and independence when caring for their families.

If I had a C-section scar, I would not be able to carry and play with my son as easily. (VBAC 01)

For several participants, vaginal birth also carried symbolic significance and was associated with fulfilment, womanhood and active participation in childbirth.

I thought that as a woman, I should deliver naturally. (VBAC 04)

These findings suggest that childbirth preferences were closely connected to broader social understandings of femininity, motherhood and maternal capability.

Influence of previous childbirth experience

Previous childbirth experiences influenced how women interpreted future delivery options. Participants who had experienced prolonged labour, emergency surgery or distress in prior pregnancies often approached subsequent births with fear and uncertainty. One participant reflected,

I might try and experience labour and end up with operations because this pregnancy wasn’t different from the previous one. (RCS 01)

Past birth experiences appeared to function as reference points through which women interpreted future risks, anticipated outcomes and their confidence in attempting labour after caesarean.

Seeking relief from prolonged labour pain and discomfort

Labour pain was interpreted differently across participants and influenced delivery preferences. While some women viewed prolonged labour as exhausting and emotionally distressing, others perceived enduring labour pain as meaningful and central to active participation in childbirth.

Exhaustion and the desire for relief from prolonged and painful labour

Participants who preferred repeat CS often described prolonged labour as emotionally and physically overwhelming, leading them to perceive surgery as a practical means of relief.

The cervix was not opening. I asked myself, why endure the pain for so long when there’s a simpler solution? (RCS 03)

In contrast, women who preferred vaginal birth acknowledged the intensity of labour pain while simultaneously describing the process as emotionally meaningful.

I didn’t like the pain of vaginal birth; it was incredibly intense, but I loved the process. (VBAC 05)

These differing interpretations suggest that labour pain was understood not only as a physical experience but also as something carrying emotional and symbolic meaning. While some participants associated labour with suffering and uncertainty, others viewed enduring labour pain as part of a fulfilling childbirth experience.

Need for emotional support and encouragement during labour

Women described reassurance, encouragement and supportive communication as important factors shaping their labour experiences. Participants who felt emotionally supported during labour described greater confidence and resilience, whereas lack of reassurance intensified fear and distress. One participant explained:

I felt like I needed someone to understand what I was going through and give me a different option. (RCS 05)

In contrast, another participant described how reassurance from healthcare providers strengthened her confidence:

When the doctor reassured me and said that I was doing fine, it gave me the strength to continue. (ERCS 06)

These findings highlight that women’s experiences of labour were shaped not only by physical pain, but also by the quality of emotional support and communication received during childbirth.

Navigating their choices by balancing medical recommendations with personal beliefs and comfort

Women described balancing medical advice with their own fears, beliefs, experiences and perceptions of safety. Decisions regarding mode of delivery were often shaped by attempts to minimise uncertainty and achieve emotional reassurance regarding both maternal and neonatal well-being.

Perception of safety and risk

For some participants, repeat CS represented a safer and more predictable option because it avoided prolonged labour and the possibility of emergency complications.

Undergoing an operation is usually not as complicated as a normal delivery. (RCS 04)

Other women expressed confidence in modern surgical techniques and perceived surgery as manageable and relatively safe.

Nowadays, doctors are advanced; you get a very small scar. (RCS 01)

However, fear surrounding surgery remained prominent among several participants.

Theatre is scary, you know. Some people say that when you go there, you might not come back. (RCS 01)

These contrasting accounts demonstrate that perceptions of safety extended beyond objective clinical risk. Women interpreted risk through emotional experiences, previous childbirth encounters, social narratives and trust in healthcare systems.

Ambiguity, uncertainty and risk communication

Participants described uncertainty during decision-making, particularly when counselling appeared inconsistent or focused on complications. Discussions regarding risks sometimes intensified fear and emotional conflict surrounding TOLAC.

I had moments of dilemma … ‘Am I really sure about this?’ (RCS 04)

The way healthcare providers communicated risk strongly influenced women’s confidence in their delivery choices. Counselling was perceived as overly alarming, heightened anxiety and discouraged attempts at TOLAC.

He told me, ‘Your uterus could be removed. It’s risky. Don’t risk your life’. (RCS 01)

Conversely, balanced and reassuring counselling appeared to strengthen confidence and reduce fear.

The doctor told me it has been done before, and we can still try. She didn’t show me any signs of worry. (VBAC 02)

These findings suggest that women were influenced not only by the content of counselling, but also by the tone, consistency and confidence with which information was communicated.

Discussion

This study explored women’s perspectives, preferences and decision making regarding the mode of delivery among women with a prior caesarean scar. Four key themes emerged: (1) social and cultural influences, (2) physical and emotional experiences related to delivery and recovery, (3) desire for emotional support and relief from prolonged labour pain and discomfort and (4) balancing medical advice with personal beliefs and emotional comfort.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that women’s delivery preferences were shaped not only by clinical considerations but also by emotional experiences, social expectations, perceptions of safety and the desire for certainty and control during childbirth. However, they only represent patient-level perspectives. To interpret these findings, we applied the HBM.38 The HBM offers a useful interpretive lens for understanding how personal perceptions and external influences may shape delivery choices in this context. In particular, three constructs’ cues to action, perceived threats and perceived benefits offer insight into how women’s experiences and social context influence their preferences, as illustrated in figure 1.

Perceived threats, as defined in the HBM, comprise perceived susceptibility and severity. In the present study, participants frequently expressed concerns about complications associated with VBAC, particularly fears of uterine rupture, neonatal safety, prolonged and painful labour and the possibility of requiring an emergency CS. These fears are not unique to our setting. A 10-year retrospective review of VBAC outcomes at a Nigerian tertiary hospital demonstrated that VBAC remains a safe and viable option in sub-Saharan African settings, yet its uptake continues to be limited by persistent safety concerns among both women and providers.39

Furthermore, a cross-sectional study conducted at Tanzania’s largest tertiary hospital corroborated these findings, demonstrating that negative childbirth perceptions shaped by prior obstetric complications and past pregnancy experiences contributed to maternal requests for CSs.40 Our study findings are consistent with a recent cross-sectional study from Saudi Arabia, which found that 67.5% of women had inadequate knowledge about VBAC, with fear for their child’s safety being the most common reason for rejecting VBAC. Despite the majority being highly educated, most women lacked a comprehensive understanding of the actual risks and benefits of VBAC, suggesting that knowledge gaps rather than informed assessment drive preferences towards repeat CS.41 Evidence indicates that VBAC success rates range from 60% to 80%, with uterine rupture occurring in only 0.5%–0.9% of cases17 suggesting that the perceived threat often far exceeds the actual clinical risk.

Unlike previous studies, our findings highlight the role of healthcare providers’ inconsistency in shaping perceived threat. Women described shifting their preferences based on conflicting counselling from different clinicians a finding that has not been prominently reported in prior literature from this region.

Although this study did not directly explore provider perspectives, previous research from Tanzania suggests that provider-level concerns, including fear of litigation, limited resources and institutional constraints, may influence counselling practices and clinical recommendations towards CS.42

Social narratives also shaped perceived threats. Women cited peers’ negative TOLAC stories as influential in decision-making. This resonates with findings from a qualitative study in Ghana which demonstrated that intra-familial decision-making, particularly the influence of mothers-in-law and elder female relatives, affected women’s access to and utilisation of maternal healthcare services.43 Similarly, a Nigerian study found that sociocultural factors, including gender roles, religious ideologies and community norms, influenced women’s attitudes towards CS, with some communities viewing surgical delivery as a sign of reproductive failure while others considered it modern and desirable.44

Our study extends this understanding by illustrating how in our setting, anecdotal narratives intersect with media portrayals to collectively reinforce a fear-driven discourse that can deter women from considering TOLAC. A systematic review on preference of mode of delivery and associated factors among mothers in East Africa supports this finding, showing that women’s birth choices are frequently negotiated within complex social hierarchies that include partners, families and community leaders.45 This underscores the necessity of addressing misinformation not only at the individual level but also within the broader social ecosystem that shapes delivery preferences.46

A systematic review of women-centred interventions to increase VBAC found that supportive care models, including continuity of carer, structured counselling and birth planning, were associated with higher VBAC uptake and positive maternal experiences.47 These findings suggest that the high perceived threat observed among our participants may be modifiable through targeted, evidence-based interventions, though such efforts must also address the provider and institutional factors that shape the environment in which women make these decisions.

Perceived benefits

In the present study, women described choosing TOLAC for faster recovery, fewer surgical risks and emotional fulfilment from a ‘natural’ birth experience. These findings align with research conducted in countries with high VBAC rates,48 such as Finland, the Netherlands and Sweden, where women expressed a strong desire for a more positive and empowering childbirth experience.46 While limited qualitative research from sub-Saharan Africa has explored women’s positive motivations for TOLAC, a meta-analysis of TOLAC across sub-Saharan Africa found that VBAC was associated with shorter hospital stays and fewer surgical complications, supporting women’s perceptions of faster recovery as a key benefit of vaginal birth.45

Consistent with prior literature, in which women from comparable settings have described the physical effort of childbirth as deeply fulfilling and meaningful.44 Our participants similarly conveyed a sense of empowerment tied to the experience of labour. Conversely, women in our study who favoured repeat CS emphasised its predictability and scheduling convenience, benefits that were particularly appealing to those who valued control over timing and sought to reduce uncertainty surrounding labour. Our study adds depth to these findings by illustrating how the preference for predictability is further reinforced in contexts where emergency care infrastructure is perceived as inadequate or unreliable. In Tanzania and similar settings, the fear of complications arising from an unsuccessful TOLAC attempt heightened women’s inclination towards a planned repeat CS. While evidence suggests that continuity in midwifery care improves experiences.47 Our participants reported that inconsistent communication between the provider intensified anxiety. This highlights the need for clear, empathetic and consistent health messages.

Cues to action

External inputs, including advice from family and the media, shaped choices. Clear, confident inputs encouraged TOLAC. In contrast, cautious or contradictory advice led to uncertainty and repeat CS. Our findings extend this by showing how inconsistency in advice can heighten perceived risks and undermine trust in VBAC as a safe option. Among our participants, family advice varied; some advocated VBAC as natural and safe, while others saw CS as the safer choice. These dynamics reflect culturally shaped risk perceptions rather than medical reasoning and are consistent with findings from a global qualitative evidence synthesis on women’s preferences for mode of birth, where cultural norms and familial advice influenced women’s preferences.27

Our study’s findings emphasise the need for community-level interventions that address both individual and familial misconceptions, therefore creating a more supportive environment for informed decision-making.

We acknowledge our potential biases as clinicians and continuously reflect on how that might have influenced the interpretation of the data. At the end of each interview, we summarised the key points discussed and sought immediate feedback from the study participants to ensure accurate understanding. Participants expanded on their responses, enriching the data with deeper insights. This ensured that the data accurately reflected the participants’ perspectives and experiences.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was conducted at a single private tertiary hospital, which may limit the applicability of the findings to public or rural settings. Additionally, recruiting participants from the postnatal ward may have introduced selection bias, as women with early discharge might have been underrepresented. Social desirability bias cannot be ruled out, especially when discussing satisfaction with medical advice. The interviewer was a male clinician, which could have affected participants’ openness on sensitive topics despite efforts to build rapport and maintain confidentiality. Importantly, this study only captured women’s perspectives. Healthcare providers act as gatekeepers in delivery decision-making, and factors such as fear of litigation, monitoring capacity and institutional readiness, which may independently limit VBAC uptake, were not explored. Furthermore, although interviews were conducted and transcribed in Kiswahili, coding was performed on English translations of the transcripts, which may have resulted in the loss of subtle linguistic nuances.

Conclusion

This study indicates that women’s delivery preferences after CS are shaped by social influences, personal experiences and inconsistencies in counselling. These findings highlight the importance of communication within the clinical encounter, while also suggesting that broader implementation of VBAC may depend on factors beyond patient preferences, including provider practices and health system capacity, which were not examined in this study. Further research incorporating multiple perspectives is needed to better understand these dynamics.
