AND Logic Gate-Based Dual-Specificity DNA Circuit for Isothermal HBV rcDNA Detection
Authors: Jingyi Si, Yifan Gao, Zhenzhou Yang, Gang Liu, Xizhong Shen, Qunyan Yao, Changfeng Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The quantitative analysis of blood HBV DNA is essential for controlling the spread of HBV and improving the prognosis of individuals infected with the virus. Yet the HBV genome exhibits complex variation, such as multiple-site mutations for entecavir resistance, closely adjacent mutation loci, and the need to distinguish missense from nonsense mutations due to genetic code degeneracy and amino acid mutation conservatism, creating challenges for detection specificity and mutation identification. Herein, this study introduced an AND logic gate-based dual-specificity DNA circuit for HBV DNA detection, integrating toehold-mediated DNA strand displacement and RNase H cleavage through a well-designed probe containing an identification element and an amplification element. By this DNA circuit, we achieved enhanced sensitivity (limit of detection: 29.11 fM) and high specificity to discriminate adjacent single-base mismatches with excellent performance in clinical samples. We envision that this innovative and convenient assay advances point-of-care HBV DNA testing.

Introduction

The global public health burden caused by chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection has become a major challenge that urgently needs to be addressed. According to authoritative epidemiological data estimates, there are approximately 316 million HBV-infected individuals worldwide currently. − Although antiviral therapies have been widely used, existing intervention methods cannot completely eliminate HBV in the intrahepatic viral reservoir. , This therapeutic bottleneck directly leads to the death of about 1 million people each year due to HBV-related liver diseases, such as liver cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma. − In the prevention and control system of HBV infection, early detection is a key link to block disease progression and virus transmission. As a core biomarker reflecting the activity of viral replication and infection potential, the accurate detection of the serum HBV DNA load is of great significance for clinical diagnosis and treatment.

In blood, HBV DNA mainly exists as relaxed circular DNA (rcDNA) within viral particles. HBV rcDNA is a partially double stranded DNA consisting of a full-length negative strand and a shorter positive strand, with a unique single stranded gap region within the negative strand. , This unique structure eliminates the step of high-temperature unwinding of the DNA double helix, making it an ideal target for HBV nucleic acid detection. However, the HBV genome exhibits complex variation characteristics. For instance, entecavir resistance requires mutations at multiple sites, and part of adjacent mutation sites are highly close in sequence distance. , Moreover, due to the degeneracy of the genetic code and the conservatism of amino acid mutations, it is crucial to accurately identify missense mutations from nonsense mutation.

However, the current mainstream HBV DNA detection technology still has many limitations. Taking quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) as an example, this technology relies on precise temperature control of Taq DNA polymerase and expensive experimental equipment. , Meanwhile, the development of resistance to the commonly used antihepatitis B virus (HBV) drug entecavir usually requires the combined mutation of three or more amino acids. These mutations involve common sites, such as rtM204 V/I, rtL180M, rtT184G, rtS202G, and rtM250 V, with some of these sites being very close to each other. It is difficult to effectively identify mutations at two adjacent loci through a traditional qRT-PCR primer design. Although Sanger sequencing (first-generation gene sequencing) can achieve mutation detection, its operation process is cumbersome and time-consuming, failing to meet the demand for rapid clinical detection. In addition, although isothermal amplification techniques such as loop mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), − strand displacement amplification (SDA), , and rolling circle amplification (RCA) − have been applied to HBV detection with the advantages of speed and low cost, their detection specificity is far inferior to qRT-PCR due to the lack of high-temperature melting process and correction function polymerase, making it difficult to cope with the complex and diverse HBV genome in reality. Therefore, an isothermal method that integrates specificity, convenience, and mutation identification capabilities is urgently needed to meet the demands of detecting the complex and diverse HBV genomes in real-world scenarios.

Based on the existing technological limitations and morphological characteristics of HBV DNA, this study proposes a dual-specificity DNA circuit (DSDC) detection strategy based on the AND logic gate. The detection probe contains an identification element and a signal amplification element for HBV rcDNA. Only when the target DNA satisfies the specificity of both elements will the cyclic amplification of the fluorescence signal be triggered. The specificity of the identification element is provided by toehold-mediated DNA strand displacement (TMSD). , TMSD allows a single DNA strand to undergo multiple hybridization and strand-displacement cycles via toeholds, offering kinetic control over the reaction pathway to reduce the kinetic gap and enhance the specificity. Mismatched DNA, due to thermodynamic barriers, cannot overcome energy barriers to initiate reactions, thereby eliminating nonspecific binding at the source and achieving high specificity identification of target HBV DNA. The specificity of the signal amplification system is achieved by the strict base pairing requirement of RNase H for the cleavage element. RNase H only recognizes structures in the DNA-RNA double strand where RNA bases are complementary to the DNA strands by at least four bases. , When there is a base mismatch, the cutting efficiency is greatly reduced. Only when both of the above conditions are met simultaneously, the AND logic gate opens to cyclically amplify the fluorescence signal.

The workflow of the DSDC strategy was outlined in Scheme . This assay utilizes the characteristic of RNase H that can only cleave DNA-RNA heteroduplexes but not RNA simplexes for probe design as well as converts the presence of the target strand T into a substantial output of reverse probe R. When T does not exist in the system, the prime probe P binds with R and forms a P-R duplex. The RNA base sequences of prime probe P exist as single strands that cannot be cleaved by RNase H. However, if HBV DNA is present in the system, it can bind to the single stranded RNA portion of probe P, utilizing thermodynamic advantages to replace the probe R to form a DNA-RNA heteroduplex P-T that can be cleaved by RNase H. The cleavaged P′-T allows the separation of the 6-FAM group from the BHQ-1 group and the emission of fluorescence. In addition, when probe R is replaced by T, the exposed middle part of probe P forms a toehold that can bind to fuel probe F, which replaces T through thermodynamic and kinetic advantages. At this point, T is released into the system once again for the next cycle. Therefore, an increase in fluorescence intensity will indicate an increase in the total amount of HBV DNA. By this, our strategy achieves high-sensitivity detection and accurate identification of adjacent mutation sites under isothermal conditions and convenient operation, providing more efficient technical support for early diagnosis and precise diagnosis and treatment of HBV infection.

Results and Discussion

Definition and Feasibility Verification of RNase H Light-Up Probe

We first defined the RNase H light-up probe. The two partitions of probe P respectively identify the amplification element (red part) corresponding to the complementary segment of P-R, and the identification element (pink part) corresponding to the part of probe P existing in a single chain form (Figure A). RNase H can efficiently cleave DNA-RNA duplexes. There have been previous reports that single strand RNA adjacent to RNA-DNA duplex could be cleaved by RNase H. Therefore, we first explore whether RNase H cleaves single strand RNA adjacent to the DNA–DNA duplex by designing R probes whose 3′ ends with different distances to RNA bases of probe P (Figure B). As shown in Figure C, after reaction for 1 h, the fluorescence intensity of each group increased. When the 3′ end of probe R is too close to the RNA bases, they will form a structure that can be cleaved by RNase H. However, if probe R is too short, the background will also increase, possibly due to a decrease in the binding between probe R and probe P, and an increase in the binding between probe P themselves. We explored the length of probe R using signal-to-noise ratio (SNR = ΔF/ΔF0) as a guiding indicator. The results show that the reactions involving R2, R3, R4, R5, and R6 have higher SNRs, and R5 has the highest SNR (Figure D). Samples containing different concentrations of HBV DNA ranging from 1 to 50 pM were investigated by RNase H light-up probe. Calculations from the linear fitting process revealed a significant correlation between the concentration of HBV DNA and the fluorescent intensity (Figure E), with a corresponding linear equation of Y = 5.874X + 1.116 (R 2 = 0.9989) (X represents HBV DNA concentration). The limit of detection (LOD) was evaluated at 1.17 nM.

Feasibility Verification of AND Logic Gate-Based DSDC

To further improve detection sensitivity of the RNase H light-up probe, we introduced a single stranded DNA probe F that promotes HBV DNA target circulation in the reaction to complete the circuit. The native PAGE results validate the feasibility of the proposed assay. As shown in Figure B, when the target HBV DNA does not exist, probe P is almost not cleaved (lanes a, b, c, d). Although fluorescence detection showed that after incubating probe P-R with F and RNase H, an enhanced fluorescence signal was observed, there was no band of the cleaved product observed in the page image. This may be due to the low sensitivity of the PAGE method for nucleic acid detection. In the presence of target HBV DNA, probe P could be cleaved (lane e), and the amount of cleaved probe P increases significantly after the addition of fuel probe F (lane f). For fluorescent intensity, probe P-R in stranded buffer shows weak fluorescent intensity (Figure C curve a and Figure D histogram a). At this point, when only target HBV DNA is added to the system, the fluorescence intensity remains almost unchanged (Figure C curve b and Figure D histogram b). When probe P-R is incubated with RNase H added, there is a slight increase in fluorescence intensity (Figure C curve c and Figure D histogram c). However, when the target HBV DNA and RNase H were added to the system simultaneously, the fluorescence intensity increased significantly (Figure C curve e and Figure D histogram e). Subsequently, we verified the effect of probe F on the entire reaction as a recycling agent. It emitted a much stronger fluorescence than in the absence of probe F (Figure C curve f and Figure D histogram f). In order to eliminate the influence of probe F on fluorescence intensity, we incubated probe P-R with F and RNase H and performed fluorescence intensity detection. The results showed that probe F had little effect on the cleavage of probe P-R (Figure C curve d and Figure D histogram d). This result indicates that probe F doubles the fluorescence intensity by driving the circuit of target HBV DNA rather than participating in the reaction itself. Then, we optimized experimental conditions, including the length of probe R, the concentration ratios of probe F vs P-R, and the reaction temperature. Subsequent experiments will be conducted with R5 under 30 °C with the optimal concentration ratio of F vs P-R was established to 1:1 (Figure S1).

Specificity of AND Logic Gate-Based DSDC

The recognition ability of this strategy for single-base mismatches occurring at different positions is shown in the heatmap (Figure A). Only when the target HBV DNA sequence satisfies both the specificity of the identification element and the specificity of the amplification element can the fully amplified signal be output. To perform specific tests, we first used nucleic acids of other hepatitis viruses or DNA viruses with the 10-fold as negative controls. Fluorescence signals similar to blanks were observed (Figure B). The ability to distinguish single-base mismatches and highly homologous HBV DNAs poses a significant challenge in clinical diagnostics. Thus, we subjected the system with similar DNA strands containing only one differential nucleotide (located at different positions of HBV DNA) compared to wild type HBV DNA. When a single-base mismatch occurs in the identification element (Figure C), the increase in fluorescence intensity was approximately 23–57% of that induced by the same concentration of the wild type HBV DNA target (Figure D). When a single-base mismatch occurs in the amplification element (Figure E), the increase in fluorescence intensity was approximately 2–39% of that induced by the same concentration of the wild type HBV DNA target (Figure F). The fluorescence intensity of DNA strands that are mismatched with RNA bases (Mis-A2) differs the most from that of wild type HBV DNA.

We simulated clinical practice scenarios and applied the regional specificity of the DSDC to distinguish adjacent point mutations. Resistance to entecavir, one of the most commonly used anti-HBV drugs, typically requires the combined mutation of three or more amino acids. These mutations involve common sites, including rtM204 V/I, rtL180M, rtT184G, rtS202G, rtM250 V, among others, among which some sites are very close to each other. Due to the degeneracy of the genetic code and the conservation of amino acids, the identification of viral drug-resistant mutations demands precise recognition of missense mutation sequences rather than distinguishing all gene sequences that differ from the wild type. In this section, mutations at the rtL180M, rtT184G, rtS202G, and rtM204 V sites were taken as examples to demonstrate the ability of this strategy to identify mutations at two adjacent sites. The mutant sequences and detection strategy are shown in Figure A: Probe P1 detects mutations at rtL180 M and rtT184G, while Probe P2 detects mutations at rtS202G and rtM204 V. We verified the ability of the DSDC strategy to identify 2 adjacent point mutations associated with entecavir resistance using artificially synthesized viral nucleic acids. When mutations occurred simultaneously at rtL180 M and rtT184G, green fluorescence was emitted (Figure B). When mutations occurred simultaneously at rtS202G and rtM204 V, red fluorescence was emitted (Figure C). Furthermore, we explored the multichannel detection capability of DSDC. Probe P1, Probe P2, and their corresponding detection targets were added to the same system. The results showed that when mutations occurred at all four aforementioned sites, both red and green fluorescence were detected simultaneously in the unified system; this indicated the occurrence of entecavir-resistant mutations (Figure D). Otherwise, only red or green fluorescence was detected, or no fluorescence was detected.

Quantitative Performance of AND Logic Gate-Based DSDC

In quantitative detection, the reaction time was extended to further enhance sensitivity. A series of samples containing different concentrations of HBV DNA ranging from 20 fM to 20 nM were investigated under the optimized conditions for LOD measurement. Consistent with expectations, the fluorescence intensity gradually increased with an increasing HBV DNA concentration. The fluorescence spectra for the increasing concentration of target were shown in Figure A. The calibration curve was depicted in Figure B, with a corresponding linear equation of Y = 52.37X + 787.2 (R 2 = 0.9935) (X represents the logarithm of HBV DNA concentration). The LOD was evaluated at 29.11 fM. The sensitivity of the proposed method was significantly improved compared to that without a DNA circuit. Moreover, it is either comparable to or superior to that of previously reported assay based on DNA circuit for single stranded DNA detection (Table S3). As blood transmission is the main route of HBV transmission, detecting HBV DNA in the blood is crucial for diagnosing an HBV infection, monitoring treatment progress, and preventing further transmission. To evaluate its performance, we conducted recovery experiments in healthy human serum samples diluted 10 times, which were doped with 20 pM, 200 pM, and 20 nM HBV DNA, respectively. Figure C shows that the detection results in the serum are very close to those in the standard buffer. The recoveries of HBV DNA in spiked serum samples were in the range of 97.85–103.98% for fluorescence detection (Figure D). The above experiments indicate that the proposed method has sensitivity and accuracy for HBV detection, showing great potential in clinical monitoring.

Furthermore, we investigated the applicability of AND logic gate-based DSDC in the clinical diagnosis of HBV infection. The overall assay procedure includes: serum collection, DNA extraction, and AND logic gate-based DSDC reaction (Figure A). Using this strategy, we analyzed 10 serum samples from HBV DNA-positive patients and compared with the quantitative results of qRT-PCR (Figure B). The variance analysis results indicated that there was no statistical difference in the detection results obtained using the DSDC strategy and RT-qPCR. In addition, the quantitative results obtained by the two detection methods were very close and had a strong correlation (Figure C). The above results confirmed the potential value of this method in clinical practice.

Materials and Methods

Apparatus

The fluorescence emission spectra were recorded by a Lengguang F97Pro fluorophotometer (Shanghai Lengguang Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) with an excitation wavelength at 494 nm. All the enzymatic reactions were conducted at the optimal temperature in a TC1000-G PCR Thermal Cycler (DLAB Scientific Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Gel images were captured using an AlphaImager Mini (ProteinSimple, CA). qRT-PCR was carried out by QuantStudio 5 (AppliedBiosystems, CA).

Materials and Reagents

RNase H was obtained from New England Biolabs (MA). All oligonucleotides (the sequences of theses oligos are listed in Table S1), DNA Marker (25–500 bp), acryl/bis 30% Solution (19:1), ammonium persulfate (APS), N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED), 4S Gel-red (10,000× in water), Ficoll Gel Loading Buffer, and other chemicals were obtained from Sangon Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and used without further purification. Serum HBV DNA was extracted by a TGuide Virus DNA/RNA Extraction Kit (OSR-M202, TIANGEN, Beijing, China). HBV DNA qRT-PCR detection was carried out with a Hepatitis B virus nucleic acid quantitative detection kit (PCR-fluorescence method, Sansure Biotech, Hunan, China). The tips and tubes used in this work are RNase-free, and all solutions were prepared using RNase-free water.

HBV DNA Detection by AND Logic Gate-Based Dual-Specificity DNA Circuit

In the probe synthesis stage, an aqueous solution of P and R strands was combined in a 1:1 ratio in a reaction buffer containing 2 mM MgCl2. Following incubation at 95 °C for 10 min, the solution was annealed from 95 to 4 °C to form the P-R duplex at a final concentration of 2 μM. In a typical experiment, different concentrations of T (HBV DNA) were added to a mixture containing 1× RNase H buffer (pH = 8.3), 200 nM P-R, 200 nM F, and 5 U RNase H. After incubating the mixture at 30 °C for 1 h, the fluorescence intensity was measured at excitation/emission wavelengths of 494/520 nm. To assess the specificity of our method, we challenged the system with similar DNA strands containing only one differential nucleotide compared to HBV DNA at a concentration of 20 nM.

Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (PAGE) Characterization

The feasibility of the proposed assay was demonstrated using PAGE. In detail, the 10% polyacrylamide gel was employed for electrophoresis in a 1× TBE buffer at 80 V constant voltage for 100 min at room temperature. Then, the gel was stained with Gel-Red for 10 min to indicate the position of DNA and photographed. At this point, we increased the corresponding concentrations of the various DNA elements as follows: P-R, F, and T at 1 μM and 100 nM each

Clinical Sample Analysis

Serum samples were acquired from the Zhongshan Hospital Affiliated to Fudan University. SST separation gel and coagulator (Becton Dickinson, USA) were used to collect venous blood from all subjects included in the study. After centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min, serum samples were collected and stored at −80 °C until use. This study had been approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital Affiliated to Fudan University (B2022-482R2), and all patients signed an informed consent. DNA extraction and qRT-PCR detection were performed according to the instructions provided by the reagent manufacturer.

Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully developed an isothermal amplification fluorescent sensing platform for detecting DNA targets. We first designed an RNase light-up probe containing an identification element and an amplification element. Two elements ensure the specificity of the strategy through AND logic gating. The platform was operated entirely at a constant temperature of 30 °C, enabling this strategy to achieve POCT satisfyingly. Additionally, this platform utilizes a DNA circuit as a signal amplifier, enabling the target HBV DNA to repeatedly engage in the cleavage of probe P, thus facilitating signal amplification. It is highlighted that the cleavage by RNase H does not depend on specific nucleic acid sequences, providing high adaptability and universality. This characteristic allows the method to be extended for detecting various DNA targets through simple modifications of the probe sequences. Moreover, by altering different probes with diverse fluorescent groups, multichannel detection becomes feasible. Our method not only achieves the identification of adjacent point mutations but also maintains favorable detection sensitivity while exhibiting excellent detection stability in serum samples. Given these advantages, this strategy holds significant promise for biomedical and clinical applications.
