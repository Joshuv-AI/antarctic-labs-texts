Elesclomol-mediated cuproptosis overcomes cisplatin resistance by restricting epigenetically activated mitochondrial respiration: Novel therapeutic insights into cisplatin–elesclomol combination therapy
Authors: Tianyu Zhu, Yongyun Li, Jieling Tang, Huimin Lin, Xiang Gu, Shengfang Ge, Lin Ye, Pengsen Wu, Xianqun Fan, Ai Zhuang, Peiwei Chai, Renbing Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemoresistance remains a significant challenge in cancer treatment, substantially limiting therapeutic efficacy. Cuproptosis is copper-induced cell death driven by mitochondrial protein aggregation and metabolic dysfunction. However, the involvement of cuproptosis in chemoresistance remains enigmatic. This study reveals that epigenetic remodeling augments mitochondrial respiration, thereby sensitizing cells to elesclomol-induced cuproptosis in cisplatin-resistant uveal melanoma (UM). First, we established cisplatin-resistant UM cell lines, which were validated in vitro and in vivo. A multi-omics analysis, including transcriptomics, metabolomics, and histone acetylation profiling (H3K9Ac/H3K27Ac CUT&Tag), revealed an upregulation of mitochondrial respiration and downregulation of glycolysis in cisplatin-resistant cells due to changes in histone acetylation. This metabolic reprogramming was associated with increased sensitivity to elesclomol-mediated cuproptosis, characterized by the diminishment of Fe–S cluster proteins and DLAT aggregation. Our research further delineated that the absence of FDX1 not only mitigated the sensitivity of chemoresistant UM cells to elesclomol but also attenuated copper-induced cell death, thereby substantiating the pivotal role of cuproptosis in this context. Therapeutically, employing zebrafish models, orthotopic xenografts, and patient-derived xenografts (PDXs), we confirmed the therapeutic efficacy of elesclomol in overcoming chemoresistance. Collectively, our study highlights a novel avenue for the development of a combinatorial therapeutic approach employing cisplatin and elesclomol to improve chemotherapy outcomes.Key words: Chemoresistance, Metabolic reprogramming, Uveal melanoma, Mitochondrial respiration, Cuproptosis, Histone remodeling, Elesclomol, FDX1

Introduction

Uveal melanoma (UM) is the most prevalent intraocular malignancy in adults, with a significant proportion of patients experiencing metastasis despite effective primary treatment1, 2, 3. Approximately 50% of these individuals develop metastatic disease, predominantly in the liver, which is associated with a starkly unfavorable median progression-free survival of 2.9 months4, 5, 6. Conventional chemotherapy, such as cisplatin and dacarbazine, shows limited response rates and offers only marginal survival benefits, with a median overall survival of less than one year2,7, 8, 9. Therefore, the exploration of UM chemoresistance represents a remarkable clinical challenge in managing UM.

Current research has illuminated the role of cell stemness remodeling and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition in the development of cisplatin resistance in UM. Notably, the jumonji domain-containing protein 2C (JMJD2C) promotes histone demethylation, leading to the upregulation of mouse double minute-2 homolog (MDM2) and P53 ubiquitination, thereby conferring resistance to cisplatin10. Additionally, UM cell lines contain a stem cell-like subpopulation with heightened self-renewal and proliferative capabilities, significantly augmenting their resistance to chemotherapy11. Further exploration into these mechanisms is crucial to unraveling the complex interplay of factors contributing to cisplatin resistance in UM.

A burgeoning corpus of research has established a significant correlation between treatment resistance and metabolic reprogramming12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17. This metabolic shift is pivotal for key stages of the metastatic cascade, including intravasation, survival in the bloodstream, and colonization of distant organs18, 19, 20. For instance, lactate generated by glycolysis has been shown to modulate the activity of MRE11, enhancing DNA mismatch repair mechanisms and conferring resistance to conventional chemotherapy21. Concurrently, mitochondrial respiration and the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) sustain the viability of cancer stem cells in triple-negative breast cancer, highlighting the profound role of metabolic adaptations in chemotherapy resistance22. Albeit chemotherapy resistance poses a serious clinical challenge to the management of UM, the underlying metabolic mechanism remains enigmatic.

Herein, we developed DDP-resistant UM cell lines and rigorously authenticated them through IC50 assays and orthotopic xenograft models. Using a multi-omics strategy, we uncovered a distinctive metabolic profile in these DDP-resistant cells. This shift towards enhanced mitochondrial function correlated with increased sensitivity to elesclomol. Mechanistically, elesclomol neutralizes chemoresistant cells by diminishing levels of Fe–S cluster proteins and inducing Dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) aggregation. Therapeutically, a suite of models, including the zebrafish tumor-bearing model, orthotopic xenografts, and patient-derived xenografts, collectively demonstrated the efficacy of elesclomol against chemoresistant UM. Our results elucidate the metabolic vulnerabilities of chemoresistant UM cells and highlight the therapeutic potential of elesclomol in overcoming this resistance.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

Human UM cell lines 92.1, MUM2B, OMM1, OMM2.3, and Mel 290 were gifted by Prof. Martine J. Jager, Leiden University, and HEK293T from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were cultured in DMEM or RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and authenticated by STR profiling.

Establishment of DDP-resistant cell lines

92.1 and OMM2.3 cell lines underwent a regimen of incremental cisplatin exposure. After a three-month incubation period, the drug resistance profiles of the resultant 92.1-DDP-resistant and OMM2.3-DDP-resistant cell populations were reassessed. These cells were subsequently maintained in culture media supplemented with subtherapeutic concentrations of cisplatin. Before each experimental iteration, the drug resistance phenotype of the adapted cell lines was reevaluated.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout

Stable Cas9-expressing DDP-resistant cell lines were established through transduction with Cas9-encoding lentivirus (Genomditech, Shanghai, China). Small guide RNA for FDX1 knockout was sourced from Genomditech (Shanghai, China). Lentiviral particles were generated by cotransfecting HEK293T cells in 10 cm culture dishes using Polyjet (SignaGen, MD, USA) with targeting plasmid (5 μg), psPAX2 (10 μg), and pMD2. G (5 μg). After lentiviral packaging, polybrene (10 μg/mL) (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added for 24 h. Successfully transduced cells were subsequently selected using an appropriate concentration of puromycin. The efficiency of FDX1 knockout was validated by Western blotting. The FDX1 knockout sgRNA sequences are listed in Supporting Information Table S1.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed as previously reported23. Primary antibodies used are as follows: Actin (1:5000, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), Lipoic acid (1:1000, Abcam, USA), FDX1, LIAS, ACO-2, SDHB, POLD1, H3K9ac, H3K27ac, H3. The concentrations and sources for the latter ones are at a 1:1000 dilution from Proteintech.

Immunofluorescence

The immunofluorescence assay protocol for cells and tissues was carried out as previously described24. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and fixed, followed by blocking with 5% normal goat serum and incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Cultured cell lines were stained with 100 nmol/L Mitotracker (Thermo Fisher, Wilmington, USA) for 30 min, followed by fixation, blocking, antibody incubation, and imaging procedures.

Bioenergetic analysis

OCR and ECAR were analyzed using an XFe 96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer. The procedure was carried out following the steps outlined in previous reports25. To assess mitochondrial stress, sequential injections of oligomycin (2 μmol/L), FCCP (1 μmol/L), and a mixture of antimycin A (0.5 μmol/L) and rotenone (0.5 μmol/L) were performed. For glycolytic stress, glucose (10 mmol/L), oligomycin (2 μmol/L), and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (50 mmol/L) were injected sequentially.

Metabolomics analysis

Metabolomic analysis was performed using targeted LC–MS/MS, as described in previous reports26. The dataset has been deposited in the National Omics Data Encyclopedia (https://www.biosino.org/) with the accession number OEZ017533.

RNA isolation and quantitative RT-PCR

Methods for total RNA extraction, RT-qPCR, and data analysis were performed as previously reported27. The primer sequences used in this study for RT-qPCR are listed in Table S1.

RNA sequencing

RNA sequencing was performed as previously reported27. The sequencing libraries were subjected to sequencing on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform. Data are deposited in the GEO database, accession number GSE 271497.

CUT&Tag

CUT&Tag was executed in accordance with our prior methodology28. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against H3K9ac (1:500, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and H3K27ac (1:500, Proteintech). The data is deposited in the GEO database, accession number GSE 271539.

ChIP-qPCR

ChIP-qPCR was performed as previously reported29. Tumor tissues were first homogenized and digested into single cells. Immunoprecipitation was conducted using antibodies against H3K9Ac (1:500) and H3K27Ac (1:500). Three pairs of primers (P1–P3) were designed for ACLY, SUCLG1, ATP6V0B, CS, NDUFA1, SDHD, and UQCRQ, with P1 located at the distal promoter region and P2/P3 positioned in close proximity to or spanning the enrichment peak near the transcription start site (TSS). Primers targeting the specific DNA regions are detailed in Table S1.

Tumor orthotopic xenograft models

Five-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were maintained within an SPF facility. After anesthesia, the mice underwent a procedure where the eye's sclera was initially pierced with a fine 30-gauge needle. Subsequently, cells expressing luciferase were introduced into the suprachoroidal space employing a 33-gauge needle with a blunt tip (Hamilton, USA). Post-injection, the eyes received treatment with erythromycin eye ointment to facilitate recovery. The development of tumors was monitored through bioluminescence imaging in vivo and by examining sections stained with H&E. The animal studies were sanctioned by the Animal Ethics Committee and were executed following established ethical protocols.

Patient-derived xenograft models

Primary UM tissues obtained at surgery were processed under sterile conditions and dissected into 1–3 mm3 fragments. Fragments were orthotopically implanted into the suprachoroidal space of BALB/c nude mice to establish PDXs. Tumor growth was monitored at regular intervals. When tumors reached a predefined volume, they were excised and reimplanted for passaging. A PDX model was considered established when it could be stably propagated across three consecutive generations while retaining the histopathological characteristics of the original tumor.

Established PDX models were evaluated for cisplatin sensitivity. Tumor-bearing mice were randomized to receive cisplatin (3 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, three times per week for 21 days) or vehicle. Tumor volume and body weight were recorded at regular intervals, and tumors were resected and weighed at the end of treatment to assess response.

PDX showing persistent growth or failure to regress under cisplatin were classified as putatively resistant and were subjected to repeated cisplatin exposure in subsequent passages to confirm and stabilize resistance. Mice bearing these tumors received additional cycles of cisplatin (3 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, three times per week for 21 days), followed by further passaging until a stable resistant phenotype was achieved. Four DDP-resistant PDX models (PDX#1, PDX#2, PDX#6, PDX#7) were selected for therapeutic studies. Tumor-bearing mice were randomized to one of four arms: vehicle (saline), cisplatin (3 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, three times per week), elesclomol (25 mg/kg, intravenously, three times per week), or the cisplatin–elesclomol combination. In accordance with ethical guidelines, patient-derived tissues were collected with written informed consent. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (SH9H-2019-T185-2). Baseline clinical characteristics are summarized in Supporting Information Table S2.

Xenograft models in zebrafish

UM xenograft models in zebrafish were established as previously reported29,30. The 92.1-DDP parental and 92.1-DDP-resistant cells were stained with CM-Dil (Invitrogen, USA) and injected into the yolk sac of anesthetized 2-day post-fertilization (dpf) Tg (kdrl:egfp) zebrafish embryos. Treatment commenced 24 h post-injection. Drug-containing egg water was replaced every two days. Image processing and analysis were performed using ImageJ software to quantify red fluorescence intensity in the embryos.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded by distributing 1000 cells into each well of 12-well plates (Corning, NY, USA). The next day, the cells were subjected to drug treatment. The medium for both the experimental and control groups was refreshed every third day. Following a two-week incubation period, the cells were rinsed with PBS, followed by fixation using a 4% solution of paraformaldehyde for a duration of 15 min. Subsequently, the cells were treated with a 1% crystal violet solution for a period of 1 h to facilitate visualization. Afterward, the surplus stain was eliminated through washing, and the plates were left to dry in the open air. The formation of cell colonies was then documented using a flatbed scanner (Hewlett-Packard, Houston, USA), and the colonies were enumerated using ImageJ software.

Transwell assay

Transwell assay was conducted as described before29. The invasive potential of DDP-parental and DDP-resistant cells was compared under conditions with or without treatment with 1 μmol/L cisplatin.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well culture dishes at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells per well. Subsequently, artificial wounds were meticulously created by dragging sterilized pipette tips across the cell layer. The culture medium was then refreshed with medium containing 1% FBS to facilitate the wound healing assay. Time-lapse photography was systematically conducted from an identical perspective to document cellular migration into the wounded area at 0, 24, and 48 h post-wounding.

Statistical analysis

All the analyses were performed utilizing GraphPad Prism version 9.0. Each experimental procedure was conducted in triplicate, and data are expressed as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). For statistical comparison, we employed two-tailed Student's t-test to assess pairwise differences and one-way ANOVA to evaluate differences among multiple groups.

Results

Establishment of cisplatin-resistance UM models in vitro and in vivo

In our quest to uncover the fundamental mechanisms behind chemoresistance in UM, we embarked on the establishment of DDP-resistant UM cell lines. Our assessment of the IC50 values for cisplatin across five prominent UM cell lines revealed a dichotomy in their sensitivity profiles. Notably, the 92.1 and OMM2.3 cell lines exhibited an increased vulnerability to cisplatin with respective IC50 values of 1.10 and 1.22 μmol/L, whereas the Mel 290 and MUM2B cell lines displayed a comparatively higher resistance, with IC50 values of 16.11 and 11.39 μmol/L (Fig. 1A). Based on the lower resistance observed in the 92.1 and OMM2.3 cell lines, we then induced cisplatin resistance in vitro over 3 months as previously described10, which culminated in a re-evaluation of their IC50 values (Fig. 1B). The resultant DDP-resistant cell lines exhibited a significant increase in their IC50 values, with 92.1 and OMM2.3 reaching 8.18 and 3.57 μmol/L, respectively (Fig. 1C). Under cisplatin treatment, DDP-resistant cells exhibited a stronger clonogenic ability compared to DDP-parental cells, which substantiated the development of chemoresistance (Fig. 1D and E).

The invasive capacity of the DDP-resistant cells remained largely unaffected by a treatment regimen of 1 μmol/L cisplatin, in stark contrast to their parental counterparts, which showed marked inhibition, as elucidated by the Transwell assay (Fig. 1F and G). In congruence with these findings, DDP-resistant cells demonstrated a superior wound healing capability at both the 24- and 48-h points post-1 μmol/L cisplatin exposure, surpassing that of DDP-parental cells (Supporting Information Fig. S1).

To validate our in vitro findings in a more physiologically relevant context, we established orthotopic tumor models using luciferase-labeled 92.1-DDP-parental and DDP-resistant cell lines, as previously described24. On Day 7 post-implantation, we quantified the bioluminescence to assess the initial tumor burden and grouped the animals accordingly. Subsequently, we administered vehicle or cisplatin (3 mg/kg) intraperitoneally over a 14-day period, followed by bioluminescence imaging on Days 14 and 21 to evaluate the dynamics of treatment response (Fig. 1H, n = 4 for each group). At the culmination of the treatment period, we harvested the tumors for further analysis of the treatment outcomes. Notably, DDP-parental tumors exhibited a pronounced sensitivity to cisplatin, as evidenced by markedly reduced fluorescence signals and decreased tumor weights (Fig. 1I–K). In stark contrast, DDP-resistant tumors demonstrated a diminished response to the treatment. Moreover, DDP-resistant tumors exhibited heightened Ki-67 expression, indicative of a robust proliferative capacity, and reduced TUNEL staining, suggesting a decreased rate of apoptosis under cisplatin treatment (Fig. 1L and M). These observations collectively demonstrate that the long-term exposure to cisplatin effectively induced an acquired chemoresistance phenotype in the UM cell lines.

Cisplatin-resistant UM cells exhibit heightened mitochondrial respiration

To delve deeper into the molecular underpinnings of cisplatin resistance in UM cells, we performed RNA sequencing on both DDP-parental and DDP-resistant 92.1 cell lines (deposited in the GEO database: GSE271497). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) uncovered a marked upregulation of oxidative phosphorylation (NES = 1.25; P < 0.01) and a concomitant downregulation of glycolysis (NES = –2.03; P < 0.001) in DDP-resistant cells (Fig. 2A and B). The specific genes involved in the two pathways are detailed in Supporting Information Fig. S2A. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis corroborated these findings, indicating a significant enhancement of oxidative phosphorylation in DDP-resistant cells (Fig. S2B). Furthermore, GSEA pathway enrichment analysis highlighted increased activity in oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV (NES = 1.57; P = 0.03), and mitochondrial respirasome (NES = 1.44; P = 0.02) pathways, whereas the glycolysis pathway activity was notably decreased in DDP-resistant cells (Fig. S2C). To further investigate these findings, we assessed the mRNA expression levels of genes associated with mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis. The results suggested that the mRNA levels of mitochondrial respiration-related genes were elevated, while those of glycolysis-related genes diminished in DDP-resistant cells compared to DDP-parental cells (Fig. 2C and D).

Subsequently, we examined the mitochondrial respiratory and glycolytic capacity to measure the bioenergetic profiles of DDP-parental and DDP-resistant UM cells. This analysis demonstrated that DDP-resistant cells exhibited higher oxygen consumption rates (OCR) and lower extracellular acidification rates (ECAR). The basal mitochondrial respiration and maximal respiration rates were significantly increased in DDP-resistant cells, indicating enhanced mitochondrial function, while glycolytic activity was reduced (Fig. 2E–J). In addition to the DDP-resistant cells we constructed, we also performed bioenergetic assays on five UM cell lines. Among these, the relatively resistant Mel 290 and MUM2B cells exhibited the highest OCR and the lowest ECAR, while the most sensitive cell lines, 92.1 and OMM2.3, demonstrated the opposite trend (Supporting Information Fig. S3). These findings further corroborate that DDP-resistant UM cell lines rely more heavily on mitochondrial respiration rather than glycolysis, agreeing with previous observations in breast cancer and lymphoblastic leukemia22,31.

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the metabolic alterations, we conducted a targeted metabolomic profiling study using targeted liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS, data are deposited in National Omics Data Encyclopedia, https://www.biosino.org/, accession number OEZ017533). This analysis unveiled a pronounced upregulation of tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates in DDP-resistant cells, notably succinate, citrate, and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), indicative of an enhanced metabolic flux and increased reliance on mitochondrial function. Furthermore, the metabolomic profiling indicated a reduction in the levels of pyruvate, a key end product of glycolysis. Concurrently, there was an observed accumulation of upstream glycolytic metabolites, such as fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (F1,6-P), β-D-fructose 6-phosphate (β-D-F6P), and glucose 6-phosphate (G-6-P) (Fig. 2K; Fig. S2H). These findings are consistent with the impaired glycolysis observed in hepatocellular carcinoma25 and suggest a metabolic reprogramming in DDP-resistant cells that may favor the utilization of alternative metabolic pathways to sustain cell growth and survival under the selective pressure of cisplatin treatment (Fig. 2L).

In the realm of patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, we obtained specimens from ten UM patients and processed them as previously reported29,30. Each specimen was divided, and the portions were orthotopically implanted into the suprachoroidal space of mice. Starting on Day 7 post-engraftment, the animals received intraperitoneal injections of either vehicle or cisplatin three times weekly. By Day 21, the tumors were harvested to assess the efficacy of cisplatin treatment (Fig. 3A and B). We determined the tumor growth ratio for each case by comparing the tumor weights from cisplatin-treated groups with those from vehicle-treated controls.

Our analysis revealed that four cases (4/10, 40%) displayed intrinsic resistance (defined as <20% reduction in tumor weight) to cisplatin (Cases #1, #2, #6, and #7), with no significant disparity in tumor weight between the two treatment groups. Additionally, three cases (3/10, 30%) showed partial resistance (defined as 20%–60% reduction in tumor weight) to cisplatin (Case #4, #5 and #8), while the remaining three cases were deemed sensitive (defined as >60% reduction in tumor weight, case #3, #9 and #10) to the cisplatin (Fig. 3C).

To further investigate the molecular basis of this observed chemosensitivity, we randomly selected two tumors from each of the DDP-resistant, DDP-partially resistant, and DDP-sensitive groups for immunofluorescence staining. The results were in line with our previous findings from the tumor-bearing cell experiments, demonstrating that the expression levels of three proteins associated with mitochondrial respiration (SDHD, COX6A2, and NDUFA1) were highest in the DDP-resistant group and lowest in the DDP-sensitive group. In contrast, the expression of three proteins related to glycolysis (ALDH2, PKM, HK2) was found to be lowest in the DDP-resistant group (Fig. 3D and E). This is consistent with the protein expression profile observed in the tumor tissue from the orthotopic mouse model (Fig. S3A and S3B). These findings underscore the potential role of metabolic reprogramming in the development of cisplatin resistance in UM.

Cisplatin-resistant UM cells exhibit vulnerability to elesclomol

We subsequently investigated therapeutic approaches to surmount chemoresistance of UM. Given the increased susceptibility of cells reliant on mitochondrial respiration to the copper ionophore elesclomol, in contrast to those dependent on glycolysis32, we assessed the potential of elesclomol to revert chemoresistance in UM cells (Fig. 4A). Initially, we ascertained the IC50 values of elesclomol for both 92.1 and OMM2.3 cell lines, DDP-parental and DDP-resistant variants. The findings revealed that DDP-resistant cells exhibited heightened sensitivity to elesclomol (Fig. 4B and C). Clonogenic assays further confirmed that the capacity for colony formation by DDP-resistant cells was markedly diminished under elesclomol treatment at varying concentrations compared to their parental counterparts (Fig. 4D and E).

To extend our evaluation to an in vivo context, we developed an embryonic zebrafish xenograft model as previously described. After 6 days, xenograft growth in the yolk sac was monitored (Fig. 4F and G). At five and 10 μmol/L elesclomol treatments, the tumor burden per embryo in the DDP-resistant group was significantly reduced compared to the DDP-parental group (Fig. 4H).

Concordantly, we orthotopically implanted 92.1-DDP-parental and DDP-resistant cells into the suprachoroidal space of the mice and treated them with vehicle or 25 mg/kg of elesclomol, followed by harvesting tumors after 21 days. Both fluorescence intensity and tumor weight measurements indicated that DDP-resistant tumors were more sensitive to elesclomol compared to DDP-parental tumors (Fig. 4I–L). These results are in perfect alignment that the cells dependent on mitochondrial respiration are more sensitive to elesclomol-induced cuproptosis25,32.

Elesclomol abrogates cisplatin resistance by inducing FDX1-mediated cuproptosis

To investigate whether elesclomol reverses chemotherapy resistance in UM by enhancing copper-mediated cell death, we treated 92.1 and OMM2.3 DDP-parental and DDP-resistant cells with elesclomol and assessed the levels of cuproptosis-related proteins. The results indicated that after elesclomol treatment, there was a significant decrease in the lipoylation levels of DLAT and Dihydrolipoamide S-succinyltransferase (DLST), as well as a pronounced reduction in the expression levels of Fe–S cluster proteins (Fig. 5A and D), agreeing with previous observations32. Concordantly, while this effect was observed in both DDP-sensitive and DDP-resistant groups, it was more pronounced in the DDP-resistant cells, aligning with increased sensitivity towards elesclomol in the DDP-resistant group. Furthermore, treatment with elesclomol resulted in a substantial increase in the aggregation of DLAT in DDP-resistant cells, while less significant changes were observed in DDP-parental cells (Fig. 5B, C, E and F). Immunofluorescence staining of intraocular tumors in mice also indicated that DDP-resistant cells exhibited more pronounced DLAT aggregation following elesclomol treatment compared to DDP-parental cells (Fig. S3C and S3D). Furthermore, Seahorse XF analysis showed that treatment of 92.1-DDP-resistant cells with elesclomol significantly reduced OCR, basal mitochondrial respiration, and maximal respiration rates compared to DDP-resistant cells (Fig. S3E–S3F), thereby partially reversing the high-OXPHOS phenotype of the DDP-resistant cells and demonstrating that elesclomol suppresses mitochondrial respiration.

FDX1 functions as the upstream rate-limiting enzyme in the cuproptosis cascade. Its expression is required for elesclomol to operate as a copper ionophore, and its absence effectively abolishes copper-mediated cell death triggered by elesclomol32,33. Therefore, we conducted CRISPR-Cas9-mediated FDX1 knockout in DDP-resistant 92.1 and OMM2.3 cells to elucidate its influence on elesclomol sensitivity. IC50 assays illustrated a significant reduction in sensitivity to elesclomol following FDX1 knockout (Fig. 5G–J). In concordance, colony formation assays indicated that the FDX1 knockout in DDP-resistant cells exhibited a diminished sensitivity to various concentrations of elesclomol relative to DDP-parental cells (Fig. 5K and L, Supporting Information Fig. S4A and S4B). These findings imply that the ablation of FDX1 attenuates copper-mediated cell death, consequently reducing the susceptibility of UM cells to chemotherapy and their reduced sensitivity to elesclomol.

To validate our hypothesis, we subjected DDP-resistant 92.1 and OMM2.3 cells with FDX1 knockout to elesclomol treatment and compared their protein profiles to those of control cells, focusing on alterations in proteins implicated in cuproptosis. Our findings revealed that the absence of FDX1 mitigated the reduction in lipoylation levels of DLAT and DLST following elesclomol exposure. Furthermore, the downregulation of Fe–S cluster proteins was significantly less pronounced in the FDX1-deficient cells (Fig. S4C and S4D). Moreover, treatment with elesclomol precipitated a pronounced increase in DLAT aggregation in DDP-resistant cells with non-targeting controls (sgNC), whereas minimal alterations were detected in cells with FDX1 knockout (Fig. 5M and N, Fig. S4E and S4F). These observations underscore the regulatory role of FDX1 in the sensitivity of chemoresistant UM cells to elesclomol and copper-mediated cell death pathways.

Furthermore, we assessed the impact of FDX1 knockout on the in vivo responsiveness of DDP-resistant cells to elesclomol. DDP-resistant cells, either engineered to express luciferase and either control (sgNC) or FDX1-targeting (sgFDX1) constructs, were treated with either vehicle or elesclomol. Quantitative fluorescence measurements and tumor weight analyses indicated a reduced sensitivity to elesclomol in the FDX1-deficient group (Supporting Information Fig. S5A–S5D). Immunofluorescence staining of intraocular in situ tumors in mice further revealed that DDP-resistant tumor cells displayed more significant DLAT aggregation following elesclomol treatment compared to DDP-parental cells (Fig. S5E and S5F). These comprehensive findings collectively demonstrate that elesclomol selectively targets DDP-resistant UM cells by counteracting the metabolic shift towards mitochondrial respiration and by inducing FDX1-dependent cuproptosis, a form of copper-mediated cell death.

Histone acetylation remodelling induces metabolic reprogramming in DDP-resistant cells

To decipher the metabolic reprogramming in chemoresistant UM cells, we delved deeper into the underlying mechanisms. As pyruvate is a critical precursor for acetyl-CoA biosynthesis, its accumulation can increase nuclear acetyl-CoA levels through the PDH–citrate–ACLY axis34, 35. Acetyl-CoA, as a direct substrate of histone acetyltransferases, subsequently enhances histone acetylation at H3K9ac and H3K27ac36. Therefore, we further investigated whether this metabolic shift contributes to establishing the epigenetic landscape of DDP-resistant cells by modulating histone acetylation. In pursuit of elucidating these changes, we conducted CUT&Tag assays for histone marks H3K9ac and H3K27ac to map acetylation across the genome (datasets deposited in the GEO database with the accession number GSE271539). The occupancy heatmaps depicted pronounced enrichment of H3K9ac and H3K27ac in the 92.1-DDP-resistant cells, predominantly in regions proximal to the transcription start sites (TSS) of genes (Fig. 6A). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further underscored an upregulation of oxidative phosphorylation (NES = 1.35; P = 0.048) and respiratory chain (NES = 1.82; P = 0.01) pathways in 92.1-DDP-resistant cells, as evidenced by CUT&Tag data (Fig. 6B). Western blot analysis corroborated these findings, indicating elevated levels of H3K9ac and H3K27ac in both DDP-resistant 92.1 and OMM2.3 cell lines (Fig. 6C).

Our investigation then honed in on the regulatory influence of H3K9 and H3K27 acetylation at genomic loci associated with genes implicated in mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis. Remarkably, genes pivotal to mitochondrial respiration, such as ATP Citrate Lyase (ACLY), Succinyl-CoA Ligase Subunit Gamma 1 (SUCLG1), ATPase H + Transporting V0 Subunit B (ATP6V0B), Citrate Synthase (CS), NADH: Ubiquinone Oxidoreductase Subunit A1 (NDUFA1), Succinate Dehydrogenase Complex Subunit D (SDHD), and Ubiquinol-Cytochrome C Reductase Complex Assembly Factor Q (UQCRQ), displayed intensified H3K9ac and H3K27ac signals in DDP-resistant cells (Fig. 6D). In alignment with the CUT&Tag findings, ChIP-qPCR assays substantiated the heightened recruitment of H3K9ac and H3K27ac to the promoters of these genes in DDP-resistant cells and DDP-resistant PDX tumor tissue (PDX#7) (Fig. 6E–G). In contrast, genes associated with glycolysis did not exhibit a remarkable increase in their H3K9ac and H3K27ac signals (Supporting Information Figs. S6 and S7; Supporting Information Table S3).

Taken together, these observations suggest that in DDP-resistant UM cells, the upregulation of histone acetylation at genes pertinent to mitochondrial respiration orchestrates a metabolic transition from glycolysis to mitochondrial respiration, thereby facilitating chemoresistance (Fig. 6H). This epigenetic-mediated metabolic reprogramming offers a compelling target for therapeutic intervention in chemoresistant UM.

Elesclomol abrogates cisplatin resistance in PDX models

To assess the capacity of elesclomol to surmount chemoresistance in UM within preclinical frameworks, we deployed established DDP-resistant PDX models (PDX#1, PDX#2, PDX#6, PDX#7). These models, previously subjected to nine cycles of cisplatin treatment over three months, were passaged and utilized to appraise the therapeutic impact of elesclomol. Post a three-week interval for tumor engraftment, we implemented a variety of treatment protocols: vehicle, cisplatin, elesclomol, and a synergistic combination of cisplatin and elesclomol. The tumors were subsequently harvested after a two-week treatment period for an exhaustive assessment (Fig. 7A). The cisplatin monotherapy group corroborated the resilience of our PDX models, characterized by an absence of significant tumor regression. In stark contrast, elesclomol monotherapy exhibited significant antitumor activity. Most notably, the combinatorial approach involving both cisplatin and elesclomol culminated in a pronounced decrease in tumor weight, underscoring a potential synergistic therapeutic effect (Fig. 7B).

We proceeded with histological staining of the tumor tissues to meticulously delineate and quantify the tumors, thereby enabling a comparative analysis of tumor size dynamics. These findings provided further validation of the beneficial therapeutic impact of elesclomol on DDP-resistant tumors (Fig. 7C and D). Immunofluorescence staining also disclosed a substantial upregulation of DLAT expression within tumor tissues exposed to elesclomol, with an even more striking increase observed in the cohort treated with the combination of cisplatin and elesclomol (Fig. 7E and F). Consequently, our study indicates that elesclomol not only triumphs over cisplatin resistance in UM by intensifying cuproptosis but also enhances the efficacy of cisplatin when administered concomitantly. This positions elesclomol as a candidate of considerable promise for the clinical management of chemotherapy-resistant UM, offering a novel avenue for therapeutic intervention (Fig. 7G).

Discussion

Chemotherapy resistance is a persistent and daunting obstacle in UM management. A phase II clinical trial utilized cisplatin, dacarbazine, andvinblastine as first-line treatment for patients with unresectable metastatic UM, demonstrating a mere 20% partial remission rate7. This underscores the modest impact of standard chemotherapy protocols on advanced UM stages. Additionally, studies suggest that conventional chemotherapy offers no overall survival (OS) advantage to metastatic UM patients when juxtaposed with alternative therapeutic approaches, including hepatic intra-arterial chemotherapy, transarterial chemoembolization, and selective internal radiation therapy2. The enigmatic origins of chemoresistance in UM have long captivated the scientific community2. In our research, we have unveiled that a metabolic transition from glycolysis to mitochondrial respiration is the cornerstone of chemoresistance in UM. Elesclomol stands out as a novel, targeted intervention against chemoresistant cells, markedly increasing the incidence of cuproptosis. Notably, the modulation of histone acetylation patterns is shown to intensify the enhancement of mitochondrial respiration in chemoresistant UM cells, offering a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention.

The heightened mitochondrial respiration observed in chemoresistant UM cells underscores the imperative need to investigate innovative therapeutic strategies that can effectively target this metabolic pathway32. Importantly, copper can bind directly to lipoylated components within TCA, precipitating the aggregation of lipoylated proteins and the loss of Fe–S cluster proteins, culminating in a unique form of cell death termed “cuproptosis”. Notably, cells that are heavily reliant on mitochondrial respiration exhibit a thousand-fold increase in sensitivity to copper compared to those that predominantly utilize glycolysis32.

In alignment with these findings, our study has identified that DDP-resistant UM cells, characterized by their pronounced reliance on mitochondrial respiration, exhibit an enhanced sensitivity to copper ionophores. Furthermore, we have discovered that the ablation of FDX1, a key mediator of cuproptosis, can reverse the sensitivity of DDP-resistant cells to elesclomol. This indicates that the heightened sensitivity to elesclomol observed in chemoresistant cells is indeed attributable to the augmented FDX1-mediated cuproptosis. These revelations provide a solid foundation for the development of personalized treatment strategies aimed at overcoming cisplatin resistance in UM by inducing cuproptosis, offering a new frontier in the battle against this formidable disease.

Epigenetic reprogramming represents an important pathogenetic trigger in diversified chemoresistant events in multiple cancers37, 38, 39. Notably, loss-of-function mutations in SMARCA4 and simultaneous epigenetic suppression of SMARCA2 characterize subsets of ovarian and lung cancers, leading to disrupted intracellular organelle calcium ion release and chemoresistance40. Moreover, the administration of EZH2 inhibitor Tulmimetostat demonstrated therapeutic effectiveness across various tumor models of bladder and ovarian cancer with mutations in the BAF chromatin remodeling complex subunit ARID1A, and it improved cisplatin response in chemoresistant models. However, an in-depth understanding of the crosstalk between epigenetics and metabolism in resistance to chemotherapy is still lacking. The current study initially revealed that histone acetylation is important for the activation of mitochondrial respiration and chemotherapy resistance, which bridges current understanding of these two cancer hallmarks.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings emphasize the intricate interplay among epigenetic remodelling, metabolic reprogramming, and copper-induced cell death, ultimately resulting in elesclomol-induced cuproptosis within chemoresistant UM cells. Therefore, we propose elesclomol as a promising therapeutic strategy to counteract UM chemoresistance, offering a novel treatment approach for UM patients refractory to conventional chemotherapy.
