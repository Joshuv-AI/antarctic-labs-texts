Exploring opportunities to strengthen maternal and perinatal death surveillance and response: a landscape analysis of surveillance and health information systems in the Eastern Democratic Republic of Congo
Authors: Christine Chimanuka Murhima’alika, Meighan Mary, Christian Chiribagula Zalinga, Christian Mugisho Byamungu, Pacifique Mwene-Batu, Rosine Bigirinama Nshobole, Gaylord Ngaboyeka, Salomine Ekambi, Paul Spiegel, Hannah Tappis, Ghislain Bisimwa Balaluka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe World Health Organization encourages all countries to implement Maternal and Perinatal Death Surveillance and Response (MPDSR), a continuous quality improvement cycle of death identification, reporting, and review to prevent future mortality. However, MPDSR implementation in humanitarian settings requires contextual adaptations for effective implementation. The aim of this study was to understand the landscape of existing health surveillance and information systems that capture maternal and perinatal mortality in crisis-affected areas of Eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) to inform future implementation of MPDSR.MethodsA mixed-methods study was conducted in North Kivu and South Kivu in Eastern DRC. Within each province, three health zones were targeted. We conducted 109 key informant interviews to identify and understand how existing surveillance and health information systems capture data on maternal and perinatal mortality. Surveys were administered for each identified system (N = 53). Data collection occurred in December 2022 in South Kivu and in June 2023 in North Kivu. Descriptive statistics of survey findings were conducted to compare key characteristics of reporting systems. Thematic content analysis of interview transcripts was conducted and triangulated with survey findings to understand implementation realities by system and health zone type.ResultsTwo categories of death reporting systems were identified: health systems (National Health Information System, MPDSR, and their extensions into communities via community health workers) and administrative systems (civil registration and other community-based systems). Commonly reported implementation challenges in all health zones included insufficient human and financial resources, unavailable tools, and complex socio-cultural dynamics which created obstacles in the identification, reporting, and review of deaths. Insecurity within the region often limited system functionality. However, promising practices related to health authority and community engagement were implemented to overcome implementation challenges.ConclusionOur findings uncover a wealth of implementation experience that is essential to inform the development, implementation, and extension of MPDSR systems tailored for optimal functionality in crisis-affected contexts. Structural system inputs must be addressed alongside socio-cultural dynamics that influence reporting and review of maternal and perinatal deaths. The intervention mechanism in crisis contexts must include a component aimed at strengthening the community networks involved in information gathering.Keywords: Maternal mortality, Neonatal mortality, Surveillance, Death review, Maternal and perinatal death surveillance and response, MPDSR, Humanitarian, Democratic Republic of Congo

Introduction

Maternal and perinatal health remains a public health priority worldwide. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates more than 287,000 maternal deaths occurred in 2020, nearly 95% of which occurred in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. Neonatal mortality is estimated at 2.3 million deaths per year, most of which occur in the first seven days of life [2], and stillbirths at 1.9 million per year [3]. In sub-Saharan Africa, the maternal mortality ratio (MMR) is estimated at 545 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births, with significant regional variations. Trends are highest in West and Central Africa, where the MMR is estimated at 754 and 539 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births, respectively [1]. As with maternal mortality, the risk of neonatal mortality is highest in LMICs; within sub-Saharan Africa, the rate is estimated to be 27 neonatal deaths per 1,000 births [2].

Despite these alarming figures, a recent meta-analysis estimated that maternal mortality is underestimated by 30–48% due to incomplete reporting and/or misclassification of maternal deaths [4]. Primary challenges cited include: (i) inadequate documentation and/or storage of data relating to maternal deaths (medical records), (ii) stigmatization linked to cultural and administrative beliefs concerning pregnancy and/or its termination, and (iii) gaps in the process of notifying and registering maternal deaths. Similar issues have been reported in crisis-affected settings in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). A 10-year record review at 242 health facilities in North Kivu and South Kivu provinces of Eastern DRC revealed notable gaps in record completeness, resulting in an institutional MMR that equated to only 10% of previously reported estimates in neighboring provinces [5].

Effective implementation of Maternal and Perinatal Death Surveillance and Response (MPDSR) could address these issues. The World Health Organization (WHO) provides a roadmap for MPDSR, a quality improvement approach that employs a 4-step cycle of identification, notification or reporting, review, and use of data to guide actions to prevent future maternal and perinatal deaths [6–9]. However, to implement MPDSR effectively in humanitarian settings, systems must be adapted to the local context and responsive to a range of globally documented implementation challenges [10–12]. These include issues related to system coordination, availability of guidelines, tools, and data systems, health and surveillance actor capacity, and community involvement – all which tend to be exacerbated in crisis-affected contexts complicated by fractured health systems, ongoing conflict, socio-ethnic tension, and/or fluctuating population displacement [11]. Nonetheless, MPDSR and related systems provide an opportunity in crisis-affected contexts to address data gaps by institutionalizing a process to understand the maternal and perinatal mortality burden, encourage an environment of learning and exchange within and between health facilities and communities, and strengthen health service delivery to the most vulnerable.

In the DRC, MPDSR was adopted into national policy in 2015 [13, 14], albeit with limited stakeholder commitment, financial and technical support for implementation. The aim of this study was to understand the landscape of existing health surveillance and information systems that capture maternal and perinatal mortality in crisis-affected areas of Eastern DRC to inform contextual system adaptation and future MPDSR implementation.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a concurrent mixed methods design with equal weighting across qualitative and quantitative paradigms [15]. Qualitative key informant interviews (KIIs) and quantitative surveys were conducted in Mweso, Karisimbi, and Katwa health zones in North Kivu province and Mulungu, Kadutu, and Walungu health zones in South Kivu province. KIIs served as a mechanism to identify existing surveillance and health information systems and document contextual insights related to implementation of each system. Surveys documented surveillance and health information system characteristics and processes related to the identification, reporting, and review of maternal and perinatal deaths. Data collection was conducted in December 2022 in South Kivu and June 2023 in North Kivu.

Study location

North and South Kivu provinces have been fraught with armed conflict for over twenty years. Here, communities faced clashes between the Congolese army and elements of various armed groups, resulting in spikes of displacement of thousands of people and a deteriorated health care system [16].

Six health zones were examined (3 of 34 in North Kivu and 3 of 34 in South Kivu) (See Fig. 1). Selection was based on the state of conflict prevailing at the time of study design, with diversity in stability and geographical location prioritized to understand differences in implementation experiences. Karisimbi and Kadutu, health zones within the capital cities of each province, were selected as ‘stable urban’ health zones. Similarly, Walungu and Katwa were selected as relatively ‘stable’ rural health zones. Mweso and Mulungu, known to be some of the most crisis-affected areas in the region marked with fluctuations in violence, population displacement, and high mortality rates [17], were selected to represent ‘unstable rural’ health zones. Internally displaced persons (IDP) and refugees live in all selected health zones. Mweso has formal IDP and refugee camps and Mulungu has informal IDP settlements, while refugees and IDPs live dispersed with the local populations in the remaining health zones. Each zone is comprised of one general referral hospital (tertiary level) and a range of 3–20 secondary-level hospitals and 19–23 health centers.

Study sample

Forty health facilities were included in the sample. Within each zone, the general referral hospital, two secondary hospitals, and health centers were selected, with the exception of Mulungu which does not have a general referral hospital and only had one accessible secondary hospital at the time of data collection. Health facilities were purposively selected to prioritize geographical variation and diversity in the types of managing authorities (e.g., public, non-governmental organization, private for-profit, or mission/faith-based management structures). In hospitals, two personnel were selected for interviews including (1) the chief of obstetrics department or maternity ward and (2) a maternal health provider (midwife, nurse, or obstetrician). In health centers, one maternal health provider (midwife or nurse) was purposively selected for an interview. Purposive sampling gave priority to health personnel with long-standing experience with surveillance and health information systems at their facility. In addition, KIIs were conducted with one representative at the Central Health Zone Office in each health zone.

KII participant selection at the community level was nested within the health facility sample. Within each health zone, one health center and its affiliated health area (i.e., catchment population) were randomly selected. In each selected health area, up to five KIIs were conducted with community, cultural, and religious leaders and were estimated to reach saturation, for a total of 30 interviews across the six selected health areas. Purposive and snowball sampling approaches were employed to identify participants that were currently involved and/or well-versed in the implementation of existing community-based health information systems. Finally, KIIs were also conducted with up to two representatives of the civil registration and vital statistics system (CRVS) and other administrative systems within each selected health zone.

Key informant selection criteria are outlined in Table 1. Potential key informants were identified in consultation with local Ministry of Health authorities (i.e., Mweso chief medical officer and health center chiefs) and health program implementers. The final sample included 109 KIIs (Table 2), which was estimated to reach saturation for the range of typologies of surveillance and health information systems as well as key insights on the implementation realities for capturing maternal and perinatal deaths.

Key informant participant characteristics

For each surveillance and health information system identified during KIIs, a survey was conducted. Key informants either participated in the survey themselves or referred the research team to the system’s manager to complete the survey on the functionality of the identified system. For national or large-scale systems identified (e.g., the national health management information system or civil registration and vital statistics system), a survey was conducted at each site (health facility, village, or office) where the system was identified.

Data collection

All interviews were conducted in French and Swahili by trained qualitative data collectors - public health and social science professionals who underwent a five-day training held by the Catholic University of Bukavu and Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health research teams. Interviews were facilitated using a piloted semi-structured interview guide (Supplementary Materials) designed to capture challenges and best practices in the identification, reporting, and review of maternal and perinatal deaths in alignment with key processes outlined in the WHO MPDSR guidance [18]. KIIs were audio recorded and lasted approximately 30–60 min. They occurred in a confidential and private location of the key informant’s choosing. The research team provided flexibility in scheduling the interviews and allowed for pauses or breaks as needed to reduce burden on key informants, especially health personnel with competing clinical duties.

The research team administered the surveys using a piloted electronic survey questionnaire (See Supplementary Materials) to systematically collect information for each identified system using the REDCap tablet application on secured tablets [19]. Surveys were developed using WHO MPDSR guidance [18] and assessed system characteristics (i.e., population served, length of operation, etc.), processes (steps of MPDSR cycle), and included a review of health registers and other source documentation (e.g., logs, reports, or charts) to record the availability of data related to maternal and perinatal mortality. Completion of the survey took 30–120 min, depending on the type of system identified.

Data analysis

All KIIs were transcribed in French and thematic content analysis conducted using Dedoose (version 8.3) [20]. While codes related to implementation challenges and best practices were defined a priori based on existing evidence [10], an iterative process was implemented to develop sub-codes and refine the initial codebook with emergent themes [21]. Coding was undertaken by a team of four trained researcher team members (CMM, MM, and two research assistants) who met on a weekly basis to discuss codebook application, key themes arising, and reflexivity in interpretation of the data. Thematic tables were constructed to elucidate findings by health zone and system typologies. Health zones were classified based on their current security situation: stable urban, stable rural, and unstable rural health zones. Qualitative findings were triangulated with system characteristics captured by the survey to yield in-depth contextualized results.

Survey data was imported into Stata (version 16.6) [22] for analysis. Descriptive statistics were conducted to compare key system characteristics. Two categories of surveillance and health information systems were identified: (i) health systems managed by the Central Health Zone Office and/or health facility leadership, and (ii) administrative systems including civil registration systems and community reporting systems.

Data were triangulated between the qualitative interviews and quantitative surveys to contextualize the identified systems [15]. The shared realities of the key informants provided a lens through which the reported system characteristics and processes were understood and grounded in practical experience.

Results

Existing surveillance and health information systems

A total of 53 surveys were conducted which identified two categories of systems that collect data on maternal and perinatal deaths: health systems and administrative systems (Table 3). Identified health systems include the national health information system (NHIS; n = 40) which is limited to maternal and perinatal death identification and reporting and MPDSR-related systems (n = 4) which involve death reporting, review, and response activities. Administrative systems comprised of the CRVS system (n = 5) and other community-based administrative systems (n = 4) including village chief and neighborhood reporting systems, community morgues, and cemeteries. The NHIS was identified at all health facilities within the sample (n = 40) and served as the cornerstone for health service reporting. Maternal and perinatal deaths that occur within health facilities, along with other health statistics, are reported to NHIS and aggregated at the health zone, provincial, and national levels. Community-based maternal and perinatal deaths are also captured via community health workers (CHWs) who are tasked with conducting home visits to pregnant and postpartum women and reporting health activities and events at the community-level. All facilities in the sample reported coverage of the population native to communities within their catchment areas; maternal and perinatal deaths among internally displaced populations and refugees were only documented by 75% and 30% of sites, respectively.

MPDSR-related systems were identified in all categories of health zones, albeit to varying scale. The most comprehensive MPDSR system was led by the Central Health Zone Offices; upon NHIS notification of the occurrence of a maternal death in their respective zone, the Chief Medical Officer was tasked with visiting and conducting a maternal death review at the reporting health facility. Other independent facility-based MPDSR-related systems were also implemented based on the initiative of the heads of health facilities or departments within hospitals. In these cases, facility leadership investigated reported maternal and/or perinatal deaths within the health facility. One identified MPDSR-related system, in stable rural health Zone (Katwa), also conducted maternal death investigations within the community to understand what happened to a patient before arriving at a health facility and/or to explore the circumstances of reported community-based deaths. Notably, none of the systems reported active case-finding efforts to capture deaths that were missing (not identified or reported) or to resolve potential misclassification issues. All MPDSR-related systems covered deaths among the host communities within their catchment areas; however, only one system in a stable urban health zone also covered displaced populations, refugees, and migrants.

The CRVS system was identified in all but one unstable rural health zone. Implemented for over 10 years, the DRC’s CRVS system is standardized and reports on vital events across the country. Nonetheless, site-specific documentation demonstrates variability in data collected and populations covered. While all sites reported on maternal deaths, some did not report on stillbirth or neonatal deaths. In addition, maternal and/or perinatal deaths of internally displaced and migrant populations were not consistently captured by CRVS across all health zones. Similar to NHIS, community-based implementation occurred via enumerators tasked with reporting deaths from the community.

Other administrative systems identified in stable health zones (i.e., village chief and neighborhood reporting systems, community morgues, and cemeteries) employed passive surveillance approaches, relying on community members to notify them of maternal and/or perinatal deaths. Information collected by these systems was often reported to or linked to the NHIS, other MPDSR-related systems, or the CRVS system. Neonatal deaths were documented across each of the systems, whereas maternal deaths and stillbirths were only captured by a few due to limited knowledge of the definitions and capacity to determine the cause of death. Like other system types, deaths within host communities were consistently captured, while only one community-based system in an unstable rural health zone (Mweso) captured refugee maternal and perinatal deaths.

Implementation challenges

a. Structural challenges

Key informants across all health zones reported challenges in implementing surveillance and health information systems (Table 4). Insufficient financial and human resources significantly impeded implementation and underscored most reported challenges. Limited or no financial support was available to implement reporting, review, and response activities including field visits for surveillance efforts within communities. Within unstable rural health zones, functionality was often dependent on non-governmental organizations’ (NGO) support, which was often reduced or ceased during peaks of conflict. Given such resource shortages, key informants also reported that community leaders within unstable health zones were known to request bribes or incentives before allowing or undertaking surveillance activities within their communities.

Resource limitations also translated into shortages in health providers, CHWs, and community surveillance actors, and ultimately, an overworked and underpaid workforce. Community-based health and administrative systems largely relied upon the volunteer labor of CHWs and community actors. Some CHWs and community actors received compensation, albeit insufficiently, resulting in many becoming demotivated and inactive. Many health providers and CHWs never received training on data collection, reporting, or review processes and reported low capacity in determining causes of death.

Challenges with data collection and entry were also common. Stockouts of registers to document information about deaths were reported in all health zones and system typologies. Within identified health systems, many also reported a lack of tools for maternal and perinatal death reviews and documentation of response activities and processes. Only NHIS systems were digitized; however, unstable internet often created connectivity issues which in turn hindered data entry and report submission. Key informants also shared that patient records were disorganized, incomplete, and easy to lose. Across both health and administrative systems, key informants reported limited space to archive data; and in the case of the civil registration system, years of archives had been lost due to offices being burned during peaks of conflict in Walungu (rural, relatively stable at the time of research) and Mweso (unstable rural, health zone at the time of research).

b. Contextual challenges: impact of insecurity

While residual effects of past insecurity and conflict were mentioned by key informants in all health zones, the current humanitarian crisis in Mweso and Mulungu significantly impacted the functionality of surveillance and health information systems. Key informants detailed how the evolving conflict had ceased or limited both facility and community-based reporting and review of maternal and perinatal deaths. During peaks of conflict, participants described needing to flee and/or being confined to their homes out of fear, which led to fluctuations in system functionality. Key informants affiliated with the CRVS recounted moments when their offices were targeted and burned by armed groups. However, for the most part, surveillance and health information systems within unstable rural health zones were able to continue functioning at a reduced capacity.

Facility-based death reporting and review within health systems was often limited by the looting of health centers. Within the context of unreliable internet connection, the physical transmission of NHIS data to the provincial level was also reported to be sometimes intercepted by armed groups. In addition, health facility staff dynamics were affected; not only did health providers experience ongoing trauma and stress, but tribal and ethnic conflict within the province transcended the walls of the facility resulting in discrimination during health service provision to patients of opposing ethnic groups and a strained professional environment fraught with mistrust between health providers from different cultural groups.

“We’re working under stress, we’re not going to work as we should, because all the time we’re thinking about how to escape the war.” – Key informant in Mweso Health Zone, North Kivu.

“Tribal discrimination in hospitals was also a cause of death, and discrimination against doctors was also a problem.” – Key informant in Mweso Health Zone, North Kivu.

Community-based activities for both health and administrative systems were impacted by population mobility or displacement and when movement restrictions or limitations were imposed. Conducting home visits and/or collecting data within communities was sometimes limited due to the occupation of armed groups who either regulated access to communities or rendered them completely inaccessible to health and surveillance actors. When communities were accessible, key informants shared that health workers were often harassed and even killed while conducting community-based surveillance activities within unstable health zones fraught with ongoing conflict.

Key informants reported challenges in identifying and reporting maternal and perinatal deaths within communities. In both stable and unstable health zones, participants shared that traditional medicine practitioners, churches, husbands, and mothers sometimes purposefully conceal information about maternal and perinatal deaths. Sentiments of taboo, shame, and fear of the consequences of notifying deaths often impeded community members from reporting deaths to surveillance and health information systems managed by the health system.

“If they know how sick she was in the house, she didn’t go to the consultation, she didn’t do anything… many don’t like to give this information. It’s as if they’re hiding it. It’s as if it’s a taboo. Because with the reduction in prices already in the health facilities and you still find a woman giving birth in the house, it doesn’t honor either the father or the mother.” – Key informant in Kadutu Health Zone, South Kivu.

“Culture, culture… There are people who are used to living in such a remote way. Instead of giving news as it is, he will see that it is taboo. A woman should not give clear the news of the birth, that’s taboo.” – Key informant in Mulungu Health Zone, South Kivu.

“We learn that she died in a prayer room [the death was not reported]. We don’t know what happened to cause her death.” -Key informant in Mweso Health Zone, North Kivu.

Cultural interpretations also influenced the identification and reporting of maternal deaths. Across all health zones and system types, participants discussed how maternal and perinatal deaths were often attributed to witchcraft. A key informant in Mweso Health Zone shared his experience at a health facility: “The mother fell ill, [but] rather than take her to hospital, they think of witchcraft and refuse to go to hospital. They gave her infusions, and she dies. They took her first to a prayer room or to a traditional practitioner, that’s all I know. And when they take her to the maternity ward, those who accompanied her tell us that she’s been bewitched.” Others also shared how people within their communities believed “having demons” caused maternal mortality. Key informants reporting on administrative systems also shared how local customs often downplay the significance of perinatal death.

Another factor relevant to underreporting is the influence of social and gender norms, which dictated interactions between communities and the surveillance and health information systems. For example, participants in unstable health zones reported that in some cultures “children are not allowed to seek care at health facilities” and health workers are not authorized to touch the corpse of a fetus or baby following perinatal death. Gender norms further complicate death reporting: participants disclosed that husbands may be reluctant to report or discuss maternal deaths due to fear of being blamed, women may be prohibited from owning or using telephones, and mothers may be blamed for perinatal deaths.

Key informants in all health zones also noted that community members lacked awareness of the critical role that surveillance systems play. Within unstable rural health zones, limitations in capacity to identify maternal and perinatal deaths were also reported; CHWs may have a limited capacity to identify maternal deaths and community members may be unaware of the definition and/or what a maternal or perinatal death is altogether.

At the facility level, key informants in all health zones revealed that health providers also conceal deaths, leading to maternal and perinatal deaths frequently going unreported.

“I hear through rumors that they didn’t report but that there was a death there. If I question him, he tells me ‘No… she was already dead, she started at home.’ With all that, when I see things like that, I hold back, but I announce to the health zone [Central Health Zone Office] that there’s been a maternal death, but that the [person] just doesn’t want to declare it.” – Key informant in Karisimbi Health Zone, North Kivu.

“People think that if we give their report to the zone [Central Health Zone Office] or elsewhere, that we’ll think they don’t know how to treat [patients]. So, they often tend to hide information [about maternal deaths].” – Key informant in Katwa Health Zone, North Kivu.

“If they commit accidents in terms of maternal or perinatal death, they hide the information directly.” -Key informant in Kadutu Health Zone, South Kivu.

“There are many who don’t accept to record [neonatal deaths]. They say ‘No, we’re going to ask too much. We’re going to investigate.’ and then you find they’re being hidden.” -Key informant in Walungu Health Zone, South Kivu.

In addition, participants in unstable rural health zones shared that maternal deaths reported by CHWs to health facility supervision often did not get recorded in the NHIS and/or via the appropriate channels.

Challenges in conducting maternal and perinatal death reviews were also shared. Across all health zone types, key informants revealed that families often concealed, withheld, or lacked necessary information requested about reported maternal and perinatal deaths, most often out of fear of blame during the review process. Within unstable health zones, participants noted that some community members were unwilling to participate and contacting and/or reaching families for verbal autopsies was challenging. Overall, insufficient information at the community level made it difficult to determine the cause of reported maternal and perinatal deaths.

Within health facilities, participants highlighted the lack of maternal and perinatal death review committees; most reviews were conducted and/or led by one person either a representative from the Central Health Zone Office or the head of the facility or maternity ward. Across all health zone types, key informants reported staff hostility, resistance, frustration, and/or reluctance to participate in or share information during reviews. Some believed that this phenomenon was due to health providers’ limited understanding of the importance of maternal death reviews.

“Some people don’t understand the purpose of the death review. Some take it as an accusation. So, when you do the review, some people tend to defend themselves. They react as if they were defending themselves, as if they were being accused. So sometimes that causes a bad climate.” – Key informant in Katwa Health Zone, North Kivu.

Issues conducting reviews of deaths reported within the private sector were highlighted across health zones. Private health providers were reported to sometimes hide information and prevent the Central Health Zone Office from collecting data and/or conducting maternal death reviews on their premises.

“There are some people in charge, especially in the private sector… when they think they may have had a hand in it [a maternal or perinatal death case], they hide the information.” – Key informant in Kadutu Health Zone, South Kivu.

“The managers of these private structures do not at least give us the opportunity to investigate or analyze the data of maternal deaths in their structures.” - Key informant in Mulungu Health Zone, South Kivu.

“If there’s a private dispensary where there’s been a maternal death and I’ve only heard about it through rumors, there’s no way I can go and do the audit there. Because when he’s [head of the dispensary] hidden it, he doesn’t want his story to be known. If I go there, he might come after me to kill me.” – Key informant in Karisimbi Health Zone, North Kivu.

Discussion of challenges putting recommendations from death reviews into action was limited. Most key informants reported that the response component of the system was not being implemented due to inadequate financial resources. Within the one MPDSR-related system implementing response activities, in Karisimbi (stable urban zone), delays in feedback on maternal and perinatal death cases were shared as an obstacle in learning from the case and improving future care.

Implementation promising practices

Promising practices were identified across all health zone and system typologies (Fig. 2). Involvement of community actors in the reporting, review and response activities was a common theme as well as strong coordination and collaboration with stakeholders at all levels of the systems. Facilitators highlighted include: (i) training and increasing the capacity of community health workers, mobilization teams, and leaders, (ii) regular household visits by CHWs, (iii) the involvement of provincial and health zone authorities in mortality reporting and reviews, and (iv) a favorable work environment. Within rural health zones (both stable and unstable), key informants highlighted the promising practice of positioning MPDSR as a quality improvement initiative to encourage team participation and transparency in reporting and reviewing maternal and perinatal deaths without fear of blame, punishment, and/or retaliation.

Additional promising practices highlighted within unstable rural health zones illustrate a variety of adaptations and strategies to ensure the continuity of surveillance and health information system functionality amidst active and or recent conflict. For example, some key informants discussed efforts to collaborate with leaders of armed groups to maintain access to communities and utilized satellite dishes with solar panels to maintain real-time reporting within remote or inaccessible communities. Due to high turnover and population mobility within unstable health zones, ongoing supervision visits, debriefings, and refresher trainings were highlighted as necessary to ensure that health providers and community actors report and review deaths.

Discussion

Our study presents results from the first in-depth landscape analysis of surveillance and health information systems intended to inform the development, implementation, and scale-up of MPDSR systems adapted for optimal functionality within crisis-affected contexts in DRC.

Although MPDSR has been in the DRC Ministry of Health’s strategic plan since 2015 [23], its implementation is still limited, with most systems facing challenges with reporting, review, and response. Challenges outlined in Table 4 are similar to those reported in other Central and West African countries. For example, Burkina Faso, Burundi, and Chad, reported MPDSR implementation challenges included a lack of financial and human resources, inadequate training of personnel involved in MPDSR, lack of motivation among staff, and a shortage of appropriate tools and equipment [24–26]. Capitalizing community actor involvement can support MPDSR implementation despite staffing shortages within the health system. Our study results demonstrate the value in engagement from a variety of stakeholders from the health zone down to the community level. Our findings suggest that the introduction of verbal autopsy within the community in the collect and review of maternal and perinatal deaths is also needed. Indeed, studies carried out in 15 resource-limited countries in Africa, Asia and South America using standardized WHO verbal autopsy tools to investigate maternal and perinatal mortality have demonstrated that this tool goes beyond research environments and can well be used to improve quality of care, inform policy as well as health planning, contributing to progress towards universal health coverage and other health goals [27]. In addition, it is necessary to position MPDSR as a quality improvement initiative to encourage the participation of community actors, health providers, and authorities, and to promote transparency in reporting and review of maternal and perinatal deaths without fear of blame, punishment, and/or reprisal. Finally, stronger involvement and collaboration of provincial authorities and health zones is needed to improve the quality of the reporting and review of deaths.

Insecurity has been cited as a limiting factor in the implementation of the MPDSR. In Eastern DRC, the disruption of health facility operations varies from simple closures to even destruction or relocation of the facility [28]. To support MPDSR implementation within crisis-affected contexts, the government should develop guidelines on how to maintain minimum levels of implementation during periods of insecurity. For example, by establishing mobile health posts in collaboration with local authorities and humanitarian health partners, MPDSR could be maintained at community level [24]. In addition, CRVS and NHIS systems present in almost all health zones can be capitalized on, despite the challenges observed, if health, finance, and planning ministry decision-makers are committed to fund and link these systems. Several partnership models have been considered as a way of extending CRVS systems into contexts lacking resources and capacity, promoting improved health system performance and, ultimately, reduction in avoidable mortality [29]. Given that CRVS and MPDSR systems are monitoring the same vital events among the same populations, they should work in a collaborative manner [30]. Both require resources and support to function optimally, and may need to be adapted and simplified to overcome the challenges of reporting and reviewing maternal and perinatal deaths in humanitarian setting [18]. Synergies at the community level could be a first step as both systems rely on community-based actors (enumerators or CHWs) to support identification and reporting of deaths.

In view of the shortage of appropriate tools and equipment, it is necessary to provide the facilities with adapted, standard, internationally validated data collection tools. In crisis-affected contexts globally, MPDSR-related systems have adapted WHO, UNFPA, and UNHCR tools and systems to meet their needs for facility and community-based report and review of maternal and perinatal deaths [18]. However, standardized tools for CHWs and community actors are not readily available. Researchers have called for guidance and research on how to adapt tools to lower literacy actors while maintaining the reliability of collected data [12].

Our findings have several limitations. Our first limitation is linked to our sampling strategy; by selecting only six health zones for data collection, it is possible that some surveillance and information systems capturing maternal and perinatal deaths have been overlooked. Secondly, the use of snowball techniques in communities affected by ethnic and tribal conflict may have limited our ability to identify all systems. Finally, issues of geographical accessibility and security may have influenced our results, as some health areas not visited may have different realities to those that were accessible to research teams. However, in our methodology, we sought to minimize the effect of these limitations by diversifying the profiles of key informants among health personnel and within the community.

Even with limitations, this formative study provides important insights into the current state of surveillance and information systems for maternal and perinatal deaths that can inform the future implementation and expansion of the MPDSR in conflict-affected provinces of the DRC, as well as future programs aimed at improving the health of mother and child in other crisis zones. Indeed, in light of our results and with the full involvement of various international partners and the DRC government, it would be necessary to ensure (i) that MPDSR data collection tools are available in all health zones to improve the reporting of maternal and perinatal deaths; (ii) support for monitoring and reporting systems is strengthened in all areas; and (iii) finally, the strengthening of community networks involved in data collection should be supported in the package of interventions in crisis contexts. Given the size of a country such as the DRC, it is more practical for the implementation of its recommendations to take into account the mechanism in place for regulating the health system, hence the need to start by developing pilot areas in the various provinces in order to better capitalize on the various experiences in implementing the SDMPR before systematic expansion.

Conclusion

Our study presents evidence from the first in-depth analysis of the landscape of maternal and perinatal health surveillance and information systems in crisis-affected contexts in DRC. Our findings demonstrate significant implementation experience that is essential to inform the development, implementation and scale-up of MPDSR systems tailored for optimal functionality in crisis-affected contexts; systems that not only build on available strengths and resources, but also consider the challenges faced among a wide range of health surveillance and information systems in the region. Future research is needed to understand how best to link and leverage existing health surveillance and information systems to effectively implement the MPDSR in crisis settings. Specific investments targeting the distribution of data collection tools and the strengthening of community reporting networks should be encouraged.
