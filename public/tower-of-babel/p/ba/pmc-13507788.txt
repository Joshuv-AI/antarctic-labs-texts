Off-label use of a PHILOS plate in tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis with variable medial cannulated screw support: a retrospective evaluation of 32 ankles
Authors: Ahmet Yurteri, Numan Mercan, Ahmet Fevzi Kekeç, Fatih Tahak, Ahmet Yıldırım
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:To evaluate the clinical and radiographic outcomes of tibiotalocalcaneal (TTC) arthrodesis performed using a lateral transfibular approach with the off-label application of a Proximal Humerus Internal Locking System (PHILOS) plate and to exploratorily assess outcomes according to the use of supplemental medial cannulated screw fixation.Methods:Thirty patients (32 ankles) who underwent TTC arthrodesis between January 2019 and June 2024 and had ≥12 months of follow-up were retrospectively reviewed. Demographics, comorbidities, surgical indications, implant configuration, fusion rates, complications, reoperations, and patient satisfaction were analyzed. Subgroup findings regarding medial screw support were evaluated descriptively.Results:The mean follow-up duration was 37.3 ± 19.2 months, and fusion was achieved in 90.6% (29/32) of ankles. Complications occurred in 18.8% (6/32), including 3 nonunions, 2 peri-implant tibial fractures, and 1 superficial infection. Reoperation was required in 3 ankles (9.4%). Patient-reported satisfaction was “excellent” in 71.9%, “good” in 12.5%, “fair” in 6.3%, and “poor” in 9.4%.Conclusion:Tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis performed using the PHILOS plate with a lateral approach demonstrated acceptable fusion and complication rates in this cohort. Subgroup observations regarding supplemental medial screw fixation should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis generating.Level of Evidence:Level IV, Therapeutic Study.

Introduction

Tibiotalar and subtalar joint osteoarthritis may develop due to trauma, inflammatory diseases, neuropathic processes, or degenerative changes, and in some cases, it may be idiopathic in origin. These pathologies disrupt the biomechanics of the ankle complex, leading to chronic pain, difficulty walking, and a significant decrease in quality of life.1,2 In these patients, arthrodesis is one of the surgical treatment options aimed at restoring function if conservative treatment fails. The main purpose of arthrodesis is to reduce pain, provide stable fusion, and increase functional capacity and daily life activities.

Tibiotalocalcaneal (TTC) arthrodesis is a widely used procedure for complex hindfoot pathologies due to its reliable clinical outcomes.3 However, the optimal fixation strategy remains debated. Contemporary literature suggests that union rates are broadly comparable among plate, nail, and external fixation constructs (approximately 82%-86%), with differences more commonly related to complication profiles and soft-tissue considerations than to fusion success itself. 2, 4-7 Cross-lag screws may be insufficient in osteoporotic bone, intramedullary nails can be technically demanding and associated with alignment challenges, and plate constructs may increase soft-tissue irritation or stress concentration at implant ends.

Locking plate systems have gained popularity because they provide angular stability and reduce plate–bone compression, thereby preserving periosteal blood supply.8,9 Nevertheless, concerns remain regarding stress risers, limited axial compression, and implant-related complications. In complex hindfoot cases, particularly those involving deformity, bone loss, or poor bone quality, surgeons continue to seek constructs that combine stable fixation, multiplanar screw orientation, and acceptable soft-tissue tolerance.

In this context, the off-label adaptation of the Proximal Humerus Internal Locking System (PHILOS, Trumed Medical, Türkiye) plate for TTC arthrodesis has been proposed as a strategy to address several practical challenges: the need for angular-stable fixation in osteoporotic bone, improved screw trajectory options in the talus and calcaneus, and avoidance of intramedullary canal violation in selected patients. However, available reports on PHILOS-based TTC arthrodesis are limited to small case series with short-term follow-up.10-14 Therefore, the present study aims to evaluate the clinical and radiographic outcomes and complication profile of TTC arthrodesis performed using a PHILOS plate in a relatively larger cohort with extended follow-up. Additionally, outcomes according to supplemental medial cannulated screw fixation were analyzed in a hypothesis-generating, exploratory manner.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study included 30 patients (32 ankles) who underwent TTC arthrodesis using the PHILOS plate via a lateral approach between January 2019 and June 2024. The study was approved by Necmettin Erbakan University Non-Pharmaceutical and Non-Medical Device Research Ethics Committee (Decision/Protocol No.: 2025/25511 Date: 13.06.2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Cases with a minimum of 12 months of follow-up were included in the study; isolated tibiotalar arthrodesis, presence of active infection, peripheral artery disease, and polio sequelae were determined as exclusion criteria. Surgery was performed only in patients with Charcot neuroarthropathy during the consolidation phase (Eichenholtz stages 2-3). Patients with active trophic ulceration or ongoing infection were excluded from surgery. No patient included in the study cohort had an active ulcer at the time of surgery. Surgical indications included secondary arthrosis due to trauma, primary arthrosis, Charcot arthropathy, nonunions, and revision cases due to deformities (pes equinovarus sequelae, drop foot, and Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease).

The previously described transfibular plating method (PHILOS plate and preferably attached cannulated screw support) was preferred in this cohort. This technique was chosen to avoid plantar entry in patients with diabetes, neuropathy, or compromised soft tissues, to allow wide joint exposure for thorough chondral preparation, to enable routine use of autologous fibular grafting, and to provide stable distal fixation with locking screws in the talus and calcaneus.

Although TTC nailing is often preferred for high-comorbidity patients due to its minimally invasive nature, it carries notable risks—particularly plantar portal complications and increased distal tibial stress in neuropathic or diabetic feet.15,16 For these reasons, a lateral approach with meticulous soft-tissue handling was adopted in this cohort. Additionally, fibular autografting was routinely performed to maintain length and support fusion biology.

Data collection and evaluation parameters

Demographic characteristics (age, gender, and body mass index [BMI]), comorbidities (diabetes, peripheral neuropathy, and smoking), surgical history, and intraoperative details (used implant configuration) of the patients were recorded retrospectively. In the postoperative evaluation, fusion success (clinical painless full weight-bearing + radiological anteroposterior (AP)/lateral/axial radiographs showing bone trabeculation passing the fusion line), developing complications, need for reoperation, and patient satisfaction (with a 4-category scale: excellent, good, fair, poor) were analyzed. The mean follow-up duration was 37.3 ± 19.2 months (range, 12-76 months).

Radiographic union assessment

Union assessment on patients’ radiographs was performed by evaluating both anteroposterior and lateral radiographs. Computed tomography (CT) was not routinely used for fusion assessment. Radiological union was defined as trabecular bridging of ≥50% in the desired fusion area. The ≥50% trabecular bridging threshold was selected based on prior ankle and hindfoot arthrodesis studies, where it is accepted as a practical and reproducible indicator of radiographic union, particularly when hardware limits full visualization of the fusion site.17 All radiographs were evaluated independently by 2 orthopedic surgeons, who were blinded to the patients’ clinical outcomes and satisfaction scores during the evaluation process. Inter- and intra-rater reliability for fusion assessment was calculated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, which demonstrated significant agreement (inter-rater κ = 0.82, intra-rater κ = 0.86). Because this was a retrospective series based solely on standard AP/lateral radiographs with substantial hardware overlap, separate assessment of tibiotalar and subtalar fusion was not considered sufficiently reliable. Therefore, radiographic union was recorded as a global TTC construct outcome rather than joint-specific fusion. Even in cases with borderline or ambiguous radiographic findings, the final decision regarding union status was reached by consensus of the 2 observers based on plain radiographs in conjunction with clinical findings (pain-free full weight-bearing and absence of mechanical symptoms). During the study period, quantitative alignment parameters such as tibiocalcaneal angle, talar tilt, and hindfoot alignment were not routinely measured or documented according to a standardized protocol, and therefore could not be reliably extracted and analyzed in this retrospective cohort.

Functional outcomes

Functional outcomes in this study were assessed using both a four-point patient satisfaction scale and the validated Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS). The FAOS is a standardized and widely accepted scoring system for assessing foot and ankle–related outcomes.18 The FAOS questionnaire was administered as part of routine clinical care, preoperatively and postoperatively at scheduled follow-up visits.

Given the high-comorbidity burden in this cohort, a four-point patient satisfaction scale was used as a practical and reliable indicator of functional outcome. Previous studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between simplified satisfaction grading and fusion success in TTC arthrodesis.19 Patients were classified into “excellent,” “good,” “fair,” or “poor” categories based on their overall satisfaction and functional improvement, consistent with prior literature.

Surgical technique

All patients included in the study were operated on by a single orthopedic surgeon. Patients were prepared in the supine position, a tourniquet was applied at 250 mmHg pressure, and antibiotic prophylaxis was provided with a single dose of 1 g intravenous cefazolin.

An incision approximately 10 cm long was made starting approximately 1 cm distal to the fibula tip. The sural nerve was identified and protected in the distal part of the approach by exploration. Approximately 7 cm of the distal part of the fibula was resected (Figure 1A). After this resection, the tibiotalar and subtalar joints were reached. Chondral surfaces were excised with a curette and osteotome, subchondral bone surfaces were exposed, and the joints were prepared for arthrodesis application (Figure 1B).

In order to obtain a plantigrade position, the foot was planned to be in a neutral position in the sagittal plane with the hindfoot and ankle, the subtalar joint in 5° of valgus, and the rotation between 5° and 10° in line with the opposite foot. First, the position was temporarily fixed with 2 mm diameter Kirschner wires and checked under fluoroscopy. After the alignment was found appropriate, in order to prevent the joint from going into valgus deformity due to the compression of the plate from the lateral side and to prevent the formation of a medial gap, a cannulated screw was inserted percutaneously approximately 3 cm proximal to the medial malleolus tip to fix the talus and calcaneus, and the fusion area was compressed (Figure 1C). In cases where the surgeon thought that sufficient compression could not be achieved, the second and third cannulated screws were also used. The decision to use 1, 2, or 3 cannulated screws was based solely on intraoperative assessment of stability and compression. In addition, according to the surgeon’s preference, if the patient’s vascular status or soft-tissue coverage is poor, the screw application is placed from plantar to medial proximal (retrograde) rather than medial to proximal (antegrade). In 2 patients, a screw was not placed because malalignment developed in the joint when the cannulated screw was applied.

After the screw was placed, the PHILOS plate was placed with its distal end screwed to the distal tibia and its proximal end screwed to the talus and calcaneus to provide fixation (Figure 1D). The plate was used in its standard configuration and, when required, was gently contoured with plate benders at the metaphyseal region to improve adaptation to the lateral contour of the distal tibia, talus, and calcaneus. Care was taken to avoid excessive bending around the locking holes, and no additional structural modifications of the plate were performed. To prevent shortening of the operated extremity, the resected fibula was divided into small pieces and placed as autologous grafts at the fusion site. This technique was applied in all cases, and no additional grafts (iliac crests, etc.) were required. Radiological imaging was not used to compare the length with the contralateral extremity; however, no clinical length difference was observed in any patient, as limb length was assessed using bilateral malleolar level comparison and functional gait evaluation. After the fusion position was completed with the PHILOS plate, fluoroscopic images were taken in the AP, lateral, and axial planes to check the position. After determining that the radiological and clinically appropriate fusion alignment was achieved, the tourniquet was terminated before the surgical site was closed, bleeding control was performed, and no drain was placed.

Postoperatively, a below-knee splint was applied to all patients. The sutures were removed in the second week. Patients were called for follow-up at postoperative second, sixth, and twelfth weeks; then follow-up continued at 6-month intervals. Patients were advised not to bear weight for the first 6-8 weeks; then, partial weight-bearing was gradually initiated depending on clinical tolerability. The postoperative weight-bearing protocol was applied uniformly to all patients, including those with diabetes or Charcot neuroarthropathy; however, progression was individualized based on wound status, radiographic findings, and overall clinical assessment rather than diagnosis alone.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS, version 22.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed for continuous variables using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data not showing a normal distribution are reported as median (minimum–maximum), and data with a normal distribution are reported as mean ± SD.

The difference between preoperative and postoperative FAOS scores was analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, depending on the distribution of the data. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare fusion rates among subgroups (e.g., diabetes, Charcot neuropathy, smoking, previous surgery, implant configuration). P < .05 was considered statistically significant in all tests. Given the limited sample size and low number of events, no multivariable regression modeling or matching was performed to adjust for confounding. Instead, these potential confounders were explored using univariable subgroup comparisons, and the resulting associations were interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Results

Patient population and baseline characteristics

A total of 32 ankles were identified in the database search that underwent TTC arthrodesis using the PHILOS plate via a lateral approach. These procedures were performed on 30 patients, including 2 bilateral cases. The mean follow-up duration was 37.3 ± 19.2 months (range, 12-76 months). The mean patient age was 54.4 years, most were female, and the mean BMI was 29.4 kg/m2. Common comorbidities included diabetes, peripheral neuropathy, and smoking, details of which are summarized in Table 1.

The number of ankles that had not undergone any surgical procedure before was 19 (59.4%), while 13 ankles (40.6%) had previously undergone surgical intervention for different reasons. Of these patients, 8 had a history of internal fixation after trauma, 3 had nonunion surgery, 1 had pes planus surgery, and 1 had external fixation due to a tibial diaphysis fracture (Table 2).

Surgical indications and technical details

When the surgical indications leading to the need for fusion were evaluated on an ankle basis, the most common causes were post-traumatic arthritis (secondary arthrosis due to trauma) (31.3%; n = 10) and Charcot arthropathy (28.1%; n = 9). Other indications were recorded as primary arthrosis (12.5%; n = 4), drop foot deformity (12.5%; n = 4), previous nonunion surgery (9.4%; n = 3), Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (6.2%; n = 2; bilateral ankle in 1 patient), and pes equinovarus sequela (3.1%; n = 1) (Table 3).

When the implant configurations used in the surgical technique were examined, it was determined that 1 PHILOS plate and 1 cannulated screw were combined in 23 ankles, 2 cannulated screws were used together with a PHILOS plate in 4 ankles, and 3 cannulated screws were used together with a PHILOS plate in 3 ankles. In addition, it was determined that only a PHILOS plate was applied alone in 2 ankles. A total of 5 ankles had a retrograde cannulated screw configuration (Table 3). X-ray images of a PHILOS plate and a cannulated screw configuration applied to a patient with a drop foot deformity (case number 10 in Table 2) are shown in Figure 2.

When stratified by fixation construct, fusion was achieved in 0/2 ankles treated with PHILOS only (0%), 22/23 with PHILOS + 1 screw (95.7%), 4/4 with PHILOS + 2 screws (100%), and 3/3 with PHILOS + 3 screws (100%). Fisher’s exact test indicated significantly lower fusion in the PHILOS-only group compared to PHILOS + 1 screw (OR not estimable due to zero cell counts, 95% CI: 0.0-0.72, P = .01) and compared to PHILOS + 2-3 screws (OR not estimable due to zero cell counts, 95% CI: 0.0-0.88, P = .028). These results should be regarded cautiously because of the very smallsample sizes. A systematic analysis was not possible because postoperative radiographic alignment measures (talar tilt, hindfoot alignment, and tibiocalcaneal angle) were not documented in this retrospective dataset. Therefore, all comparisons according to implant configuration should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Fusion success and complication profile

The primary radiographic outcome in this study was overall TTC construct union, defined as a combined tibiotalar–subtalar fusion status rather than separate joint-specific fusion. In the postoperative radiological evaluation, successful fusion was achieved in 29 of 32 patients (90.6%; 95% CI: 74.9-98.0) (Table 4). Details of the 3 nonunion cases are as follows:

The first of the 3 nonunion patients was a case in which TTC arthrodesis was performed due to pes equinovarus sequelae (case number 22 in Table 2). Arthrodesis was performed only with a PHILOS plate in this patient, and no cannulated screw was used. There was no history of Charcot neuropathy or diabetes mellitus, but he was an active smoker. Although the fusion was unsuccessful, reoperation was not performed because the patient stated that he was asymptomatic.

The second patient had diabetes mellitus and Charcot arthropathy, and TTC arthrodesis was performed only with a PHILOS plate (case number 13 in Table 2). Deep infection developed during the follow-up period; therefore, the implants were removed, debridement was performed, and Ilizarov fixation was applied, but union could not be achieved.

The third patient also had diabetes mellitus and Charcot arthropathy. In this case, TTC arthrodesis was achieved with a PHILOS plate and a cannulated screw (case number 8 in Table 2). However, since the patient stated that he was asymptomatic and could perform his daily activities, reoperation was not preferred. Fusion was not achieved in either of the 2 patients who underwent arthrodesis with only a PHILOS plate, without the addition of a cannulated screw. However, a third nonunion occurred in a patient who had PHILOS fixation with 1 cannulated screw.

Complications occurred in 6 out of 32 ankles (18.8%). The complication profile summarized in Table 4 included 3 nonunions, 2 peri-implant tibial fractures remote from the fusion site, and 1 superficial wound infection. Although fusion was successful in 2 patients, a fracture developed adjacent to the implant in the tibial region outside the fusion area, and these fractures were successfully treated with an intramedullary nail. The superficial wound infection that developed in 1 patient healed with oral antibiotic treatment without requiring surgical intervention. The 3 nonunion cases are summarized below, and their detailed characteristics are presented in Table 5.

Reoperation was required in 3 ankles (9.4%) after surgery. In 2 of these cases, intramedullary tibial nailing was performed due to peri-implant fractures that developed independently of the fusion site. One of these 2 cases (case number 12 in Table 2) is shown in Figure 3. In 1 patient, the existing implants were removed due to nonunion and deep infection; re-stabilization was achieved with an Ilizarov fixator, and long-term antibiotic treatment was given (Table 4).

Subgroup analysis revealed fusion in 75.0% (6/8) of diabetic patients versus 95.8% (23/24) of non-diabetic patients (95% CI: 0.59-99.5, P = .147). Charcot patients demonstrated fusion in 77.8% (7/9) compared to 95.7% (22/23) of non-Charcot patients (95% CI: 0.49-80.2, P = .184). Smokers achieved fusion in 83.3% (10/12) versus 95.0% (19/20) of non-smokers (95% CI: 0.31-47.2, P = .540). Patients with a history of prior surgery had a fusion rate of 100% (13/13) compared to 84.2% (16/19) in those without prior surgery (95% CI: 0.009-4.47, P = .253). In contrast, PHILOS-only fixation without medial screw support resulted in 0% fusion (0/2) compared to 96.7% (29/30) in those with additional screw support (95% CI: 1.3–upper limit not estimable, P = .006). These subgroup differences should be interpreted as exploratory due to small sample sizes (Table 6). Given the extremely small cell counts in several strata, these findings should be interpreted with particular caution and considered hypothesis-generating only. Accordingly, all subgroup comparisons are presented as exploratory findings and should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of association.

As detailed in the Methods section, union was assessed as an overall TTC construct on plain radiographs, without joint-specific or alignment-based measurements.

Patient satisfaction and functional outcomes

The mean preoperative FAOS score was 65.3 ± 8.7, which significantly improved to 84.1 ± 14.6 postoperatively (P < .001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). The magnitude of improvement (mean increase: 18.8 points) exceeds the previously reported minimal clinically important difference thresholds for FAOS in foot and ankle surgery, indicating that the observed change is not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful.18 This change was statistically significant (P < .001) (Table 4).

When evaluated on an ankle basis, patients rated 23 (71.9%) of 32 ankles as “excellent,” 4 (12.5%) as “good,” 2 (6.3%) as “fair,” and 3 (9.4%) as “poor” (Table 4). It was determined that both of the 2 patients reporting “fair” results developed peri-implant fractures. Two of the 3 patients reporting “poor” results had Charcot neuroarthropathy (both were diabetic patients); the other patient had undergone surgery for the sequelae of pes equinovarus, and all 3 patients had nonunion. Both of the 2 patients who underwent bilateral surgery reported good and excellent results overall (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, TTC arthrodesis performed using the PHILOS plate via a lateral approach appeared to be a feasible off-label option for the treatment of complex ankle pathologies, with a fusion success rate of 90.6% and an excellent/good patient satisfaction rate of 84.4% in this small retrospective cohort.

The fusion rate obtained is consistent with standard techniques reported in the literature, such as retrograde intramedullary nailing (80%-92%) and blade plates (85%-95%).4,20-28 The wide fusion rates of 29.7%-100% reported in arthrodesis studies performed with the PHILOS plate indicate a lack of standardization in the application technique. In contrast, the high success rate obtained in this study can be explained by the fact that the procedures were performed by a single surgeon, the distal fibula was used as an autologous graft, and a biomechanically critical element such as the medial support screw was applied. While the overall fusion rate aligns with the ranges reported for retrograde nailing and blade plating, it is important to acknowledge that patient populations across studies differ substantially. While the inclusion of high-risk patients enhances the real-world relevance and external validity of these findings, it simultaneously complicates direct comparison with more homogeneous cohorts and limits internal interpretability. Therefore, the findings should be viewed as exploratory rather than definitive.

The technical advantages of TTC arthrodesis using a PHILOS plate can be summarized under 3 key points. First, distal fibula resection provides wide joint exposure and allows the resected segment to be used as an autologous graft, eliminating donor-site morbidity; however, the potential impact on lateral stability should be considered. Second, the locking plate construct offers rigid fixation and reduces screw loosening, which is particularly beneficial in osteoporotic bone. Third, the addition of a medial percutaneous screw was associated with improved alignment control in this series, which may contribute to a more favorable mechanical environment for fusion. In this study, the absence of a medial screw in 2 of the 3 nonunion cases parallels the 100% fusion reported by Fan et al 12 in their medial screw–supported series and may support the potential biomechanical contribution of this technique. Given the emphasis on variable screw support, subgroup analysis demonstrated an association between the absence of a medial cannulated screw and lower observed fusion rates compared with constructs supported by 1 or more screws. However, because of the very small sample sizes, these results should be considered exploratory rather than definitive. Importantly, these observations do not establish a causal relationship between medial screw support and fusion success. The occurrence of a nonunion despite the use of a single medial screw indicates that biological factors and overall construct stability likely also play important roles.

The PHILOS plate, originally designed for proximal humerus fixation, is used off-label in the ankle and hindfoot, and its anatomical configuration does not specifically correspond to the morphology of the talus or calcaneus. Although its locking screw design provides stable fixation, plates intended for tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis ideally require region-specific geometry to optimize load distribution and minimize the risk of collapse. Additionally, distal fibula resection performed to gain exposure for this approach removes attachment points for lateral ligaments and peroneal tendons, making the effects on adjacent joint biomechanics difficult to predict.

Recent studies using modern plating systems for TTC or TT/TTC arthrodesis report fusion rates broadly comparable to the present findings. Perisano et al 29 achieved 100% fusion in 20 patients with severe post-traumatic deformities using a Silverback TT/TTC plate, whereas Vinogradov et al 30 reported a 90.9% fusion rate in a heterogeneous cohort that included Charcot patients. The fusion rate of 90.6% with PHILOS plating is consistent with these results, despite this cohort containing a higher proportion of high-risk patients such as those with Charcot neuroarthropathy, diabetes, smoking, and prior failed surgery. Across 7 published PHILOS-based series (Table 7), fusion rates ranged from 29.7% to 100%, reflecting differences in patient characteristics, fixation strategies, and follow-up duration. Overall, these findings suggest that PHILOS plating may provide fusion outcomes comparable to dedicated TTC plating systems, although more standardized prospective data are needed.

Fusion and reoperation rates were equal, and this was observed in 3 of 32 ankles (9.4%), and the rates obtained are similar to those in other studies in the literature. It was noted that the PHILOS plate was not supported by a cannulated screw in 2 of the 3 patients in whom fusion could not be achieved. Although this observation suggests that nonunion may be due to the lack of a cannulated screw, the lack of a comparative control group in this study prevents us from statistically confirming this relationship and limits the power of interpretation. However, when evaluated from a biomechanical perspective, it is known that locking plate systems are effective in keeping the bones fixed in their current positions; however, their capacity to apply compression force is limited.10,31 Additionally, plate-only constructs may fail due to increased construct stiffness, limited axial compression across the fusion interface, and geometric mismatch between the humeral-specific PHILOS plate and hindfoot anatomy, all of which may compromise the mechanical environment required for fusion. On the other hand, it is thought that axial compression provided by cannulated screws may improve union rates by increasing mechanical stability in the fusion line and reducing the risk of implant failure. In another case where nonunion was observed, union could not be achieved despite the use of a cannulated screw together with a PHILOS plate. In this case, it is highly probable that biological risk factors such as uncontrolled diabetes mellitus and Charcot neuropathy present in the patient contributed to the nonunion. Because this cohort intentionally reflects a heterogeneous real-world population, subgroup analyses should be interpreted cautiously, as limited sample size reduces statistical power despite enhancing clinical representativeness. Separate comparisons based on the presence of Charcot neuroarthropathy, diabetes, and smoking did not reveal any statistically significant differences, although fusion rates were numerically lower in the groups with these factors. However, this comparison is descriptive rather than definitive because it was conducted in a relatively small group of patients. Moreover, because of the small sample size and event counts, we were unable to perform multivariable adjustment for these risk factors; therefore, residual confounding by diabetes, smoking, Charcot neuroarthropathy, and prior surgery cannot be excluded. Indeed, it has been demonstrated in various studies in the literature that diabetes and Charcot neuropathy negatively affect the success of ankle arthrodesis. So, although the applied surgical method is considered an effective technique in terms of achieving fusion, the failure obtained in this case once again emphasizes the importance of biological support and patient selection.

Complications occurred in 6 out of 32 ankles (18.8%), which is relatively high compared to studies reported in the literature. We attribute this situation to the high-comorbidity burden in the patient group. 40.0% of the patients were active smokers, 26.7% were diabetic, and 37.5% had a history of previous surgery. Patients who had undergone surgery due to trauma accounted for 31.3% (10 ankles), those with Charcot arthropathy accounted for 28.1% (9 ankles), those with a history of unsuccessful fusion accounted for 9.4% (3 ankles), and patients with primary arthrosis who had not undergone surgery before accounted for 12.5% (4 ankles). Although this complexity increases the clinical applicability of the present results to high-risk practice settings, it may also have influenced complication rates and limits extrapolation to lower-risk populations. Despite this, the 90.6% successful fusion rate obtained demonstrates that the arthrodesis technique applied with the PHILOS plate is a reliable and effective method. Furthermore, although intraoperative fluoroscopy and clinical assessment were used to obtain and confirm a plantigrade foot position, the lack of standardized, quantitative alignment measurements precluded a more detailed analysis of how residual malalignment might have influenced fusion or complication rates in this cohort.

Recent studies using FAOS scores to evaluate the outcomes of TTC arthrodesis yield results consistent with these findings. For example, Elgoyoushi et al 16 reported significant FAOS increases in their series of 84 patients treated with TTC nailing (preoperative: 65, postoperative: 84), similar to the improvement in this study. Similarly, Park et al 32 reported high fusion rates and significant FAOS improvements in their series of 11 patients treated with talar allografts. In their cohort, preoperative FAOS scores were quite low (24.1 in some subgroups), but postoperative scores increased to over 70, similar to the increase in this series. These results suggest that this procedure also provides the same functional improvement as fusions performed with TTC nailing or allografting.

According to the literature review, we encountered 2 remarkable complications that are rarely seen after ankle arthrodesis. In 2 of the patients, peri-implant fractures developed during the follow-up period after successful fusion. Both peri-implant tibial fractures were stress-related injuries occurring at the level of the proximal screw rather than traumatic events. We think that this situation is due to biomechanical changes that occur due to the tibia, talus, and calcaneus acting as a single rigid segment after TTC arthrodesis.33 Following rigid fusion, load transfer across the tibia–talus–calcaneus complex may become more concentrated at the proximal fixation level, potentially increasing local stress in the distal tibia. Such stress concentration near the most proximal screw, where cancellous bone predominates, may have contributed to fracture development over time. Unlike some reports suggesting increased risk with short constructs, these implants were not relatively short and extended adequately into the diaphysis. This suggests that factors beyond construct length, such as patient bone quality, comorbidities, and stress redistribution after fusion, may have contributed. While longer plates or more proximal fixation might still be biomechanically advantageous, the present data do not support plate length as the sole explanation.34 In both cases, complete union was achieved after tibial intramedullary nailing was applied to cover the fusion area. In order to prevent such complications, it is recommended to prefer longer plates extending to the tibial diaphysis and to medically optimize bone mineral density.

This study has several important limitations related to its retrospective design and methodological constraints. The absence of a control group limits causal inference, and fusion assessment relied solely on plain radiographs without CT confirmation, which may have reduced diagnostic sensitivity in ambiguous cases. Furthermore, fusion was reported as a single TTC entity without separate evaluation of the tibiotalar and subtalar joints, and radiographic alignment parameters (tibiocalcaneal angle, talar tilt, hindfoot alignment) were not systematically recorded. These factors collectively limit the precision of radiographic and joint-specific outcome interpretation.

In addition, heterogeneity in fixation strategy and patient characteristics reflects real-world surgical decision-making but introduces variability that may confound interpretation. A uniform weight-bearing protocol was applied to all patients, including those at higher risk, which may not represent individualized rehabilitation strategies. Finally, all procedures were performed by a single surgeon; while this enhances internal consistency, it reduces generalizability. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized fixation protocols, individualized rehabilitation approaches, detailed alignment analysis, joint-specific fusion reporting, and comprehensive patient-reported outcomes are warranted.

This study demonstrated that TTC arthrodesis performed using a laterally applied PHILOS plate is a feasible off-label option for complex hindfoot pathologies, with encouraging fusion (90.6%) and patient satisfaction (84.4%) rates in this small retrospective cohort. The technique may be particularly useful in patients with osteoporosis, deformity (e.g., Charcot arthropathy), or previous surgical failure, and the observed complication rate of 18.8% appears acceptable given the high-comorbidity burden. However, given the predominantly high-risk and heterogeneous composition of the current cohort, these findings should not be generalized to low-risk patients or to isolated TTC arthrodesis populations. Adjunctive medial cannulated screw support may enhance construct stability and potentially improve fusion outcomes; however, this association requires validation in adequately powered prospective comparative studies. Overall, these short- to mid-term results should be regarded as exploratory and warrant further validation in methodologically robust future trials.
