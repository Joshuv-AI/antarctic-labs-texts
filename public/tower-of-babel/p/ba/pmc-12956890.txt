Reactive oxygen species–resistant ultrastable super-resolution DNA framework dots
Authors: Chengpin Liang, Qingting Li, Bin Chen, Tingting Zhai, Xiaoqian Luo, Yang Yang, Jiang Qian, Dan Zhao, Shihua Luo, Fei Wang, Qian Li, Jianlei Shen, Chunhai Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceNatural systems like green fluorescent protein (GFP) exploit nanoconfinement to stabilize molecules within protective cavities. Mimicking this bioinspired strategy, we engineer DNA frameworks that programmably confine fluorophores, replicating GFP’s protective microenvironment. This confinement shields dyes from reactive oxygen species, yielding probes with 50-fold enhanced photostability. By decoupling molecular stability from specific fluorophore chemistry, our DNA cages provide a universal platform to create ultrastable emitters. These advances directly enable long-term super-resolution imaging and the construction of multiplexed barcodes with nanoscale precision. This biomimetic confinement approach transcends imaging, offering broad utility for designing robust functional materials in catalysis, sensing, and biomedicine.

Super-resolution fluorescence imaging is a powerful method for studying the spatiotemporal distribution of biomolecules and tracing their dynamics within cells (1–6). The choice of fluorophores is critical to optimizing imaging performance. An ideal fluorophore should exhibit high photostability, brightness, and the ability to conjugate with biomolecules in a controllable manner (7). Enhancing the photostability of probes not only extends the timescale for molecular dynamics tracing but also improves imaging resolution. This is particularly important for high temporal resolution techniques such as structured illumination microscopy (SIM) and stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy, which rely on fluorescence excitation and depletion saturation. For instance, in SIM imaging, creating a super-resolution image requires multiple intermediate frames, and excessive photobleaching can result in signal loss between frames, compromising the final image quality. Organic fluorophores often suffer from severe photobleaching and low brightness, limiting their utility for long-duration super-resolution imaging. Alternative probes, such as quantum dots and fluorescent nanoparticles, offer advantages like high brightness and photostability but are hindered by issues such as severe blinking and uncontrollable conjugation ratios (8). Thus, there is an urgent need for a new class of fluorescent probes that combine photostability, high brightness, small size, and precise conjugation capabilities.

Organic fluorophores are prone to photobleaching through photo-oxidation reactions in oxygen-rich solutions (9). Traditional methods to enhance their photostability involve adding enzyme-catalyzed oxygen scavenging systems and triplet-state quenchers (10). However, these approaches require multiple exogenous solution additives (11), and the poor water solubility and high biotoxicity of triplet-state quenchers make them unsuitable for live-cell applications. Covalent attachment of triplet-state quenching groups to fluorophores can reduce the need for solution additives but introduces challenges such as complex chemical modifications and reduced brightness (12). Nature provides inspiration through polypeptides and RNA scaffolds that form biological fluorophores by creating chromophores during protein folding. Barrel-like peptide frameworks inhibit nonradiative conformational changes in chromophores while isolating them from water molecules and reactive oxygen species (ROS) (13, 14). Inspired by this nanoconfinement effect, various synthetic molecular cages have been developed to encapsulate fluorophores, including cucurbit[7]uril, supramolecular coordination complexes, metal-organic frameworks, and covalent organic frameworks (15–17). Despite these innovations, challenges remain in precisely regulating the number and arrangement of fluorophores within these frameworks and achieving efficient biological functionalization. Thus, there is a pressing need for a biocompatible framework structure that can precisely control the number and spatial arrangement of fluorophores while maintaining their functional adaptability for biological applications.

Structural DNA nanotechnology enables the construction of three-dimensional DNA frameworks with near-atomic precision (18–21). DNA frameworks are highly customizable in shape and size, offering addressability and ease of modification. Functional molecules can be precisely positioned on the DNA framework, allowing researchers to achieve specific spatial arrangements. For instance, by controlling the modification sites on the DNA framework, researchers have successfully encapsulated alkyl chains, liposomes, nucleic acid aptamers, and proteins within the DNA framework’s nanopores via covalent bonding (22–25). Beyond serving as a passive scaffold, DNA frameworks can actively engineer the microenvironment of fluorophores, modulating critical properties such as hydrophilicity, charge, and intermolecular distance to enhance optical performance and biocompatibility. Previous studies have shown that fluorophores conjugated to the external surface of DNA frameworks can generate high-brightness fluorescence spots, barcodes, or optical devices (26–29). Vale and coworkers demonstrated that labeling six dyes outside of a cubic DNA nanostructure could significantly increase the photostability (27). However, while peripheral fluorophore spacing on DNA framework has been well studied, the photostability of dyes confined within DNA framework cavities, akin to the protective environment of GFP, remains largely unexplored. Here, we investigate the distinct emission behaviors of dyes positioned on the external surfaces vs. those encapsulated within the DNA cavity. Specifically, we evaluated how the hydrophobic cavity enhances photostability by shielding dyes from ROS, mimicking GFP’s protective mechanism.

Results and Discussion

Design, Synthesis, and Characterization of SDF Dots.

Our design was inspired by the structure of GFP and its RNA mimics. In GFP, the barrel-like peptide scaffold forms a nanoconfinement for the formation of the para-hydroxybenzylidene-imidazolinone chromophore, shielding it from the bulk solvent through an extensive hydrogen-bonding network (14). Recently, DNA frameworks have been reported to efficiently scavenge ROS in solution, making them valuable for mitigating ROS in biological inflammatory diseases (30, 31). We reasoned that fluorophores encapsulated inside the cavity of DNA frameworks could be efficiently shielded from ROS. We selected a tetrahedral DNA framework (TDF) with an edge length of 20 base pairs (~6.8 nm) as the structural scaffold (Fig. 1A) and chose the commonly used hydrophobic Cy3 dye as a model fluorophore. The TDF was chosen for its rigidity and the three-dimensional addressability of its modification sites. The orientation of fluorophores was regulated by modulating the helical orientation along the edges of the tetrahedron (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1), which has been well studied for enabling molecules or proteins to face either the interior or exterior of the structure (32, 33). To ensure the helical orientation directed inward, we selected the ninth base (from 5’ to 3’) along each edge as the nick site (34) (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig S1). To facilitate intermolecular interactions between hydrophobic fluorophores inside the cavity, four thymine (T) bases were added between the fluorophores and the TDF scaffold, providing additional flexibility (TDF-Cy3-edge, Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Table S1). For comparison, we engineered nick points at the four vertices of the TDF with four fluorophores facing outward to construct an SDF dot isomer (TDF-Cy3-corner, Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2B and Table S2). Following this design principle, various SDF dots can be tailored by adjusting fluorophore orientation, valence state, and type within the DNA framework (Fig. 1 B and C).

The SDF dots and its isomer were formed by the self-assembly of equimolar amounts of four complementary single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) strands (S1, S2, S3, S4 in SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2) through heating and annealing. After purification using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), the successful assembly of the two dots was verified by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) imaging. AFM results showed a uniform size distribution for both SDF dots (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). We subsequently investigated the distribution of Cy3 on the SDF dots in comparison to our design. Compared to TDF-Cy3-corner, the migration rate of TDF-Cy3-edge during PAGE analysis was faster, indicating the smaller size of the latter (Fig. 1C). HPLC results further supported this conclusion, as evidenced by the longer retention time of TDF-Cy3-edge compared to TDF-Cy3-corner (Fig. 1D). The size reduction was attributed to the inward orientation of the fluorophores within the cavity of the DNA framework, leading to a decrease in the overall volume of the structure. To further validate our speculation, we systematically constructed TDFs with Cy3 conjugated at specific positions along the tetrahedron edge (the 6th, 9th, 12th, and 15th nucleotides from the 5’ end) while varying the length of thymine spacer (SI Appendix, Table S3). This design allowed us to distinguish between effects due to overall molecular weight and those arising from global shape. PAGE analysis confirmed that increased spacer length reduced the electrophoretic mobility of all constructs. Strikingly, for any given spacer length, TDFs with fluorophores at the 9th or 12th positions migrated significantly faster than those modified at the 6th or 15th positions (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Since faster migration under native conditions indicates a more compact hydrodynamic volume, this result indicated that the 9th/12th-nucleotide configurations lead to a contracted, inward-facing geometry, whereas the 6th/15th-nucleotide configurations yield a more extended, outward-facing structure (25). This demonstration of conformational control via helical orientation control validated our structural design principle and established the foundation for subsequent optical characterization. We further verified the orientation of the fluorophores inside the TDF cavity using Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET). By comodifying two Cy3 and two Cy5 molecules onto the TDF (Fig. 1E), the FRET results revealed significantly higher energy transfer efficiency in the inward-facing structure compared to the outward-facing structure (Fig. 1E), demonstrating that Cy3 and Cy5 molecules in the inward-facing structure are closer in proximity. Single-molecule FRET experiments further confirmed this result, showing higher FRET efficiency in the TDF-Cy3-edge, which indicates closer interfluorophore distances (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

To further prove the formation of fluorophore oligomers inside the cavity of the TDF-Cy3-edge, we replaced the cyanine dye with tetraphenylethylene (TPE), a molecule exhibiting aggregation-induced emission (AIE) effects (35). TPE molecules can sense the crowding of their surrounding environment and adaptively change their internal motion, reporting the degree of molecular crowding via an increase in fluorescence intensity. First, a commercially available alkynylated TPE molecule was attached to azido-modified DNA (ssDNA-N3) through a copper(I)-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition reaction. After HPLC purification of TPE-modified ssDNA (ssDNA- TPE, SI Appendix, Fig. S5) and the DNA self-assembly process, TDFs modified with one, two, three, and four TPE valencies were prepared by varying the molar ratio of TPE-modified ssDNA and unmodified DNA (TDF-1TPE-edge, TDF-2TPE-edge, TDF-3TPE-edge, TDF-4TPE-edge, and TDF-1TPE-corner, TDF-2TPE-corner, TDF-3TPE-corner, TDF-4TPE-corner, SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–C). The fluorescence intensity of TPE on the TDF-TPE-corner structure increased linearly with TPE valency, similar to the concentration-dependent fluorescence intensity increase observed for ssDNA-TPE (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). In contrast, the fluorescence intensity of inwardly oriented TPE increased nonlinearly with TPE valency (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). Notably, the fluorescence intensity of TDF-4TPE-edge was significantly higher compared to TDF-3TPE-edge, exhibiting a characteristic AIE behavior. These results strongly support the proximity of the four fluorophores in our inward-oriented SDF dot design. Thus, we demonstrated that orientation control of the double helix on each edge of the TDF can finely modulate the distribution of fluorophores on the DNA framework, providing a platform for studying the optical properties of fluorophore oligomer inside the cavity of DNA framework.

Optical Properties of SDF Dots.

Next, we investigated the photostability properties of the two SDF dots at the single-molecule level using total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy. Cy3-labeled DNA duplexes (dsDNA-Cy3, SI Appendix, Table S4) were selected as a control sample to evaluate the contribution of the DNA framework. All prepared fluorophores were immobilized on an imaging substrate via electrostatic adsorption (36, 37). The optical properties, including photostability and brightness, were quantified under TIRF microscopy. The photostability of the fluorophores was assessed by measuring the time at which 50% of the SDF dots underwent photobleaching (half-life), while brightness was indicated by the average number of photons emitted per frame. Photobleaching results showed that the four Cy3 molecules located inside the cavity of the TDF were significantly more photostable than four Cy3 on the vertices of the TDF or linear dsDNA helix (~1.8-fold higher) and single Cy3 on dsDNA (~50.0-fold higher) (Fig. 2 B and E and SI Appendix, Figs. S7–S9). Specifically, after 99% of dsDNA-Cy3 were photobleached, over 80% of the TDF-Cy3-edge fluorescence spots remained in the “on” state. We also immobilized three structures on substrate by biotin–streptavidin interactions (SI Appendix). The photostability results revealed a similar trend to that of electrostatic adsorption (SI Appendix, Figs. S8 and S9).

TIRF images also revealed that both SDF dots were brighter than dsDNA-Cy3 (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Statistical analysis of the average number of photons per fluorescence spot showed that TDF-Cy3-edge and TDF-Cy3-corner were approximately 2.6 times and 3.9 times brighter than dsDNA-Cy3, respectively (Fig. 2 D and G). Given that four Cy3 dyes were modified on the TDF, the observed deviation from the theoretical brightness of TDF-Cy3-edge (expected ~4-fold higher than dsDNA-Cy3) suggests a degree of fluorescence quenching caused by aggregation-induced quenching (ACQ) effects. This finding further confirms the presence of intermolecular interactions in TDF-Cy3-edge (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). The greatly improved photostability of TDF-Cy3-edge compensated for its decrease in brightness, leading to a ~13.0-fold increase in the total number of photons emitted compared to dsDNA-Cy3. This total photon output was also higher than TDF-Cy3-corner, which showed an 11.4-fold increase compared to dsDNA-Cy3 (Fig. 2 C and F). These results demonstrate the feasibility of enhancing the photostability of Cy3 by encoding its spatial arrangement within the DNA framework.

Photostability of SDF Dots in Biological Settings.

We further explored the photostability of SDF dots in biological settings, focusing on their use in long-duration STED imaging of live cell membranes. The high excitation light intensity used in STED imaging demands fluorophores with excellent photostability. To target cell membranes, we modified the structure of SDF dots by attaching two cholesterol (Chol) molecules, known for their membrane-targeting properties (Fig. 3A).

After incubating the Chol-TDF-Cy3-edge with HeLa cells, fluorescence imaging was conducted using STED super-resolution microscopy and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). The SDF dots demonstrated effective targeting to the cell membrane of HeLa cells (Fig. 3B). Analysis of fluorescence intensity distribution in narrower membrane regions revealed that the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of resolvable features in the STED images was 120 nm, significantly smaller than the 275 nm imaging resolution achieved with confocal microscopy. This indicates that the SDF dot is compatible with STED imaging and does not compromise its resolution (Fig. 3C).

To assess the kinetic performance of the photostable TDF-Cy3-edge dots in STED imaging, we conducted long-duration imaging experiments. Chol-TDF-Cy3-corner and Chol-ssDNA-Cy3, functionalized with two and one cholesterols, respectively, were assembled as controls. All three probes effectively targeted the cell membranes of HeLa cells (Fig. 3D). Fluorescence imaging showed uniform distribution of both SDF dots across the cell membrane without noticeable aggregation or bright spots, indicating good dispersion in the cell culture medium (Fig. 3D). The enhanced photostability of Chol-TDF-Cy3-edge enabled extended imaging times, lasting at least 30 min (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Statistical analysis of fluorescence intensity after 30 min of STED imaging revealed that the fluorescence intensity of Chol-TDF-Cy3-edge, Chol-TDF-Cy3-corner, and Chol-ssDNA-Cy3-treated cell membranes retained 77.4%, 24.0%, and 15.2% of their initial fluorescence intensities, respectively (Fig. 3E).

The Generality and Mechanism Study of Photostability Enhancement.

Next, we investigated the generality of this strategy in improving the photostability of commercial fluorophores and explored the underlying working mechanism. First, we examined whether the DNA framework itself could scavenge ROS, a major cause of photobleaching. Spectrophotometric assays revealed that the TDF itself exhibits dose-dependent scavenging activity against hydroxyl radicals (•OH), 2,2’-azino-bis-3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS) radicals, and superoxide anion (O2−) (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). For instance, at 4 µM, the TDF scavenged 52.4%, 32.9%, and 5.7% of ABTS radicals, •OH and O2−, respectively. This result establishes that the DNA framework can act as a protective matrix against oxidative damage. Our previous findings revealed that inwardly oriented Cy3 in the TDF could form stable oligomers that improved FRET efficiency (Fig. 1E) and restricted intramolecular motions (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). We extended this strategy to commercial fluorophores emitting across different spectral regions, including green-emitting FAM and Alexa488, red-emitting ROX, and near-infrared-emitting Cy5 and Alexa647. After optimizing the Mg2+ concentration during the self-assembly process, PAGE results confirmed the successful synthesis of SDF dots (SI Appendix, Figs. S13 and S14). Single-molecule evaluations of all SDF dots, with fluorophores oriented inwardly or outwardly, revealed that the photostability of hydrophobic fluorophores (Cy5, FAM, and ROX) with inward orientations was significantly enhanced compared to their outwardly oriented counterparts (1.1-fold, 1.6-fold, and 1.4-fold higher, respectively; Fig. 4 B and C). However, no significant difference in photostability was observed between inwardly and outwardly oriented Alexa488 and Alexa647 (Fig. 4 B and C). The variation in photostability enhancement among different fluorophores was attributed to their water-dispersity. Notably, Cy3, Cy5, FAM, and ROX are hydrophobic, while Alexa488 and Alexa647 are hydrophilic due to the presence of sulfonate groups (Fig. 4A). Hydrophobic interactions among Cy3, Cy5, FAM, and ROX within the inward-facing DNA framework formed a small, hydrophobic environment that further shielded the fluorophores from solvents, including ROS, similar to the protective mechanism seen in GFP.

To further verify the relationship between the hydrophobic environment and the observed photostability enhancement, we varied the number of Cy3 molecules inside the TDF cavity and investigated the impact of Cy3 valency on photostability (TDF-1Cy3-edge, TDF-2Cy3-edge, TDF-3Cy3-edge, and TDF-4Cy3-edge, Fig. 5A). The successful assembly of these structures was confirmed by PAGE analysis (Fig. 1C). Statistical analysis showed that the photostability of the SDF dots increased with Cy3 valency (Fig. 5 B and C). Comparing the half-life times of TDF-Cy3-edge and TDF-Cy3-corner (Fig. 5 D and E) with the same number of Cy3 molecules revealed distinct enhancement behaviors when the number of Cy3 exceeded two (Fig. 5 C and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). This result suggests that hydrophobic intermolecular interactions contribute significantly to further improving the photostability of Cy3. Notably, although TDF-1Cy3-edge, TDF-1Cy3-corner, and dsDNA-Cy3 contained the same number of Cy3 molecules, the photostability of TDF-1Cy3-edge and TDF-1Cy3-corner was significantly higher than that of dsDNA-Cy3. This confirms a baseline photoprotective effect conferred by incorporation on the TDF scaffold (27, 38).

To test this hypothesis, we analyzed the photobleaching process of the two SDF dots in detail. For both TDF-4Cy3-edge and TDF-4Cy3-corner, which contain four Cy3 molecules, their photobleaching curves exhibited typical four-step photobleaching patterns (Fig. 5 G and H). A comparison of the duration time for each step revealed distinct photobleaching fingerprints (Fig. 5 G–I), with the duration time increasing progressively with the number of photobleaching steps. Typically, the time required for the last step was significantly longer than that for the first step (Fig. 5I). This trend suggests the higher probability of bleaching for the first Cy3 molecule and a lower bleaching probability for the last one. It should be noted that the progressively longer intervals between later bleaching steps represent a mathematically expected result when all fluorophores have equal stability, rather than indicating differential stability among individual dyes within the same SDF dot. Interestingly, the duration time for each step in TDF-4Cy3-edge was much longer than that for TDF-4Cy3-corner, particularly for the final bleaching step. We attribute this phenomenon to the ongoing protection provided by the hydrophobic environment formed within TDF-4Cy3-edge. Even after some Cy3 molecules were photobleached, the hydrophobic core remained intact, shielding the remaining fluorophores from the damaging effects of ROS generated during the photobleaching process. These results demonstrate that the enhanced photostability arises from a synergy between the intrinsic ROS-scavenging property of the DNA framework and the formation of a protective hydrophobic cluster of fluorophores within its cavity.

Construction and Characterization of Subdiffraction Fluorescent DNA Barcodes.

We explored the application of ultraphotostable SDF dots in SIM imaging. Multiplex fluorescence imaging is a powerful tool for distinguishing distinct molecular species, but its capabilities are limited by the number of spectrally distinguishable fluorophores. Geometrically encoded fluorescent barcodes, constructed using DNA origami, offer a solution by enabling super-resolved imaging of barcodes with fluorescent zones spaced below the diffraction limit (28, 39–41). Previous studies have demonstrated multicolor imaging using DNA points accumulation for imaging in nanoscale topography (DNA-PAINT), however, ultraphotostable subdiffraction fluorescent barcodes suitable for SIM imaging remain elusive. Here, we integrated SDF dots with a rod-shaped DNA origami to construct ultraphotostable (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 and Table S5-1), geometrically encoded fluorescent DNA barcodes. This was achieved using orthogonal hybridization of DNA sequences extended from SDF dots (TDF-4Cy3-edge) and rod-shaped DNA origami, respectively.

To evaluate the imaging resolution of the fluorescent DNA barcodes, we constructed single-color fluorescent DNA barcodes with various binding zone spacings (70 nm, 85 nm, 100 nm, and 115 nm; SI Appendix, Table S5-4), all below the diffraction limit. A 240 nm long 10-helix bundle rod-shaped DNA origami (10HB) was selected as the scaffold (42), with two zones on the nanorod designated for fluorescent labeling with predesigned interzone spacings (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Table S5-2). Each binding zone consisted of a single staple strand displaying one orthogonal docking sequence to which a green emissive SDF dot (TDF-4Cy3-edge) could bind. To facilitate purification, one additional staple strands were designed with 5’-biotinylated extensions at the end to enable magnetic bead separation purification and DNA strand displacement reactions (SI Appendix, Table S5-5). AFM data confirmed the successful synthesis of the structure, showing two SDF dots positioned on the same side of the 10HB DNA origami (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S17). Super-resolution reconstruction from SIM imaging revealed clear differentiation of two SDF dots on the 10HB DNA origami, with a high yield of approximately 92.5% (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). For spacings of 85 nm, 100 nm, and 115 nm, distinct separation of the two points was achieved (Fig. 6C and SI Appendix, Fig. S19). However, at 70 nm spacing, only a subset of points could be reconstructed, indicating the current resolution limits of commercial SIM technology.

Next, we investigated the photostability of SDF dot-engineered fluorescent DNA barcodes under SIM imaging. A control fluorescent DNA barcode was prepared by hybridizing four Cy3-modified complementary ssDNA to two zones of the 10HB DNA origami, ensuring consistent dye numbers at each binding site (SI Appendix, Table S5-3). Photobleaching curves under SIM imaging demonstrated that the photostability of SDF dot-based fluorescent DNA barcodes was significantly higher than that of the control samples, with a half-life 1.6 times longer (SI Appendix, Fig. S20).

We extended the technique to three-color imaging by employing orthogonal imager strand sequences coupled to three spectrally distinct SDF dots (TDF-4Alexa488-edge, TDF-4Cy3-edge, and TDF-4Cy5-edge). A 10HB DNA origami with three binding zones arranged symmetrically was designed, with neighboring zones separated by approximately 115 nm (Fig. 6A and SI Appendix, Table S5-2). Each binding zone featured a single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) extension capable of hybridizing with one SDF dot. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) confirmed the successful anchoring of three SDF dots on the same side of the 10HB structure (Fig. 6D).

During super-resolution reconstruction of SIM images, we assigned pseudocolors to the three fluorophores: Alexa488 as “blue” (denoted as B but shown in purple in Fig. 6 for clarity), Cy3 as “green” (denoted as G), and Cy5 as “red” (denoted as R). Using this scheme, we designed 18 distinct super-resolution fluorescent DNA barcodes by varying the arrangement and relative number of the three spectrally distinct SDF dots. The fluorescent DNA barcodes included combinations such as GGG, GGB, GGR, GRG, GRB, GRR, GBG, GBR, GBB, RGR, RGB, RRR, RRB, RBR, BRB, RBB, BBB, and BGB. SIM imaging successfully reconstructed all 18 fluorescent DNA barcodes (Fig. 6E). Statistical analysis revealed the assembly yields for each barcode: 77% (GGG), 52% (GGB), 50% (GGR), 91% (GRG), 92% (GRB), 54% (GRR), 91% (GBG), 90% (GBR), 40% (GBB), 92% (RGR), 67% (RGB), 42% (RRR), 85% (RRB), 50% (RBR), 46% (BRB), 66% (RBB), 51% (BBB), and 83% (BGB) (SI Appendix, Figs. S21−S25). The SIM images showed that the fluorescent DNA barcodes assembled as designed and were resolved unambiguously.

In practical applications, the ability to differentiate multiple fluorescent DNA barcodes simultaneously is often required (43, 44). As a proof of concept, we randomly chose five distinct fluorescent DNA barcodes (GBG, RGR, BGB, GRG, and GRB) and mixed them in equimolar ratios for SIM imaging. SIM results demonstrated successful reconstruction of all five mixed fluorescent DNA barcodes (Fig. 6F and SI Appendix, Fig. S26), confirming the capability of super-resolution imaging to differentiate multiplex fluorescent DNA barcodes (Fig. 6F). Statistical analysis of 380 fluorescent DNA barcodes from 20 images showed an overall successful assembly and reconstruction rate of 84.4% (Fig. 6G). The remaining 15.6% of particles were not recognizable, likely due to poorly assembled structures.

Conclusions.

We utilized the ROS-shielding ability of the DNA frameworks to construct a collection of ultraphotostable SDF dots. Single-molecule evaluations revealed that the photostability of fluorophores within the DNA framework cavity was significantly enhanced, with up to a 50-fold improvement compared to fluorophores labeled on double-helix DNA. By controlling the orientation of fluorophores inside the cavities or facing outward, photostability was further improved, with inward-facing fluorophores exhibiting 1.8 times greater stability. Systematic investigations of diverse fluorophores demonstrated that the hydrophobic pocket formed inside the DNA framework cavity significantly enhanced photostability by shielding against ROS. This strategy is highly generalizable, enhancing photostability across six commercial fluorophores spanning the visible spectrum without requiring chemical modifications. Unlike previous studies focusing on peripheral base–fluorophore interactions (27, 38), our work highlights the critical role of nanoconfinement inside DNA frameworks in stabilizing fluorophores, akin to GFP’s protective mechanism.

The exceptional photostability of SDF dots supports long-term super-resolution imaging using SIM and STED techniques. We utilized SDF dots for long-term imaging of live cell membranes using STED microscopy, achieving imaging stability for over 30 min. Leveraging the robustness and programmability of DNA origami, we achieved high multiplexing capabilities. Using three spectrally distinct SDF dots, we constructed 18 distinguishable super-resolution fluorescent DNA barcodes for SIM imaging. Compared to unstructured fluorophores, SDF dots improved photostability under continuous laser excitation, resolving labeling zones spaced 70 nm apart. Moreover, SDF-based SIM achieves super-resolution reconstruction with only ~13 frames per channel, a significant temporal advantage over techniques such as DNA-PAINT, which typically require thousands of frames (28). This difference in speed originates in their distinct working principles, SIM modulates known illumination patterns to encode high-frequency spatial information, requiring only a limited set of raw frames for Fourier-domain reconstruction, whereas DNA-PAINT depends on stochastic blinking and single-molecule localization. It should be noted that the ~6.8 nm hydrodynamic diameter of SDF dots makes them well-suited for SIM and STED but limits their use in methods targeting resolutions below ~20 nm. Our SDF dot strategy provides a versatile platform for developing multiplexed super-resolution fluorescent probes, advancing super-resolution imaging and single-particle tracking.

Materials and Methods

Reagents and Materials.

Oligonucleotides used in this work were custom-synthesized, labeled, and purified by Sangon Biological Engineering Technology & Services Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The sequences of these oligonucleotides are listed in SI Appendix, Tables S1–S5. All the chemicals needed for the reactions were purchased from commercial sources and used as received without further purification. Dichloromethane (DCM) and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) were purchased from Adamas [99.8%, SafeDry, with molecular sieves, Water ≤ 50 ppm (by K.F.), SafeSeal]. 10× TAE (Tris-acetate-EDTA) were purchased from Sangon Biotechnology Inc. GelRed nucleic acid stain (10,000× in DMSO) was purchased from Invitrogen (Grand Island, NY, USA). Alkynylated tetraphenylethylene was purchased from Henan Alfa Chemical Co., Ltd (Henan, China). Streptavidin magnetic beads were purchased from NEB (New England Biolabs, Inc.). Cell line, a human cervical cancer cell line (HeLa), was obtained from the Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and trypsin/EDTA (0.25%) were purchased from Gibco (Gaithersburg, USA). NeutrAvidin and BSA-Biotin were purchased from Thermo Scientific. All other reagents used in this work were of analytical grade and directly used without further purification. All the other chemicals, including RTM solution (Minera oil rotational viscosity standards), n-Hexane, CuBr, sodium ascorbic acid, and PBS buffers, were obtained from Sigma Aldrich.

General Protocol for “click” Coupling Reactions.

To label alkynylated tetraphenylethylene molecule on single-stranded DNAs (ssDNAs), we employed a copper-catalyzed cycloaddition reaction known as click chemistry. First, we prepared stock solutions for the click chemistry reaction. CuBr (1 mg) was dissolved in 70 µL of DMSO, while tris(benzyltriazolylmethyl)amine (TBTA) (54 mg) was dissolved in 1 mL of DMSO. Additionally, sodium ascorbate (20 mg) was dissolved in 1 mL of double distilled water (ddH2O). Next, we premixed CuBr and TBTA stock solutions in a 1:2 volume ratio, forming the ligand-Cu(I) solution. Then, we dissolved alkynylated tetraphenylethylene molecule (0.2 mg, 0.13 µmol) in 90 µL of DMSO and added an aqueous solution containing azide-modified DNA (50 µL, 8.5 OD). After brief stirring, we introduced 10 µL of the premixed ligand-Cu(I) solution to the reaction mixture. Subsequently, 50 µL of the sodium ascorbate stock solution was added, and the mixture was shaken in a metal bath at 40 °C for 2 h. Following the reaction, we removed the solvent under vacuum and dissolved the crude product in ddH2O.

Purification Process for ssDNA-TPE.

Conjugated ssDNA-TPE was purified by HPLC with a C18 reversed-phase HPLC column. Triethylamine acetic acid (TEAA, 20 mM, pH 7.4) and acetonitrile were used as mobile phase and eluent (Gradient: 2 to 60% acetonitrile; Flow rate: 0.8 mL/min). Finally, we removed the solvent using lyophilization, and the resulting product ssDNA-TPE was dissolved in ddH2O and stored at 4 °C for future use.

Preparation of SDF Dots.

SDF dots were prepared by annealing four complementary ssDNA (1 µM). Four ssDNA were mixed in an equimolar ratio in PBS buffer (8 mM Na2HPO4, 2 mM KH2PO4, 2 mM Mg2+, pH 7.4). The solution was heated to 95 °C for 10 min and then quickly cooled to 4 °C, and kept at 4 °C for at least 2 h. By altering the relative ratio of the Cy3-ssDNA to unmodified ssDNA, the number of Cy3 molecules on the DNA framework could be quantitatively controlled. To prepare TDF-1Cy3-edge, Cy3-S1, S2, S3, and S4 were used. To prepare TDF-2Cy3-edge, Cy3-S1, Cy3-S2, S3, and S4 were used. To prepare TDF-3Cy3-edge, Cy3-S1, Cy3-S2, Cy3-S3, and S4 were used. To prepare TDF-4Cy3-edge, Cy3-S1, Cy3-S2, Cy3-S3, and Cy3-S4 were used. To prepare TDF-4Cy3-edge with two cholesterol molecules, Cy3-S1, Cy3-S2, Cy3-S3-Chol, and Cy3-Chol-S4-Chol were used. The information on DNA sequence and chemical modification can be found in SI Appendix, Table S1.

To prepare TDF-1Cy3-corner, Cy3-corner-S1, Corner-S2, Corner-S3, and Corner-S4 were used. To prepare TDF-2Cy3-corner, Cy3-corner-S1, Cy3-corner-S2, Corner-S3, and Corner-S4 were used. To prepare TDF-3Cy3-corner, Cy3-corner-S1, Cy3-corner-S2, Cy3-corner-S3, and Corner-S4 were used. To prepare TDF-4Cy3-corner, Cy3-corner-S1, Cy3-corner-S2, Cy3-corner-S3, and Cy3-corner-S4 were used. To prepare TDF-4Cy3-corner with two cholesterol molecules, Cy3-corner-S1, Cy3-corner-S2, Cy3-corner-S3-Chol, and Cy3-corner-S4-Chol were used. The information on DNA sequence and chemical modification can be found in SI Appendix, Table S2.

Purification of SDF Dots.

We purified SDF dots using an Agilent 1260 HPLC system with a size exclusion chromatography column (Phenomenex BioSec-SEC-4000, 300 mm × 7.8 mm). Chromatograms were recorded at 260 nm to monitor the purification process. We used a mobile phase consisting of a mixed solution of 30 mM Tris-HCl and 450 mM NaCl (pH = 8.0) with isocratic elution, and the flow rate was set to 1.0 mL/min. To concentrate the fraction collected at 3,000 g for 10 min, we used Amicon Ultra-0.5 mL centrifugal filters (MWCO 30 kDa). Finally, we determined the concentration of SDF dots using UV-vis absorption spectrophotometry (Perkin-Elmer, Lambda 850).

PAGE Analysis.

We characterized the prepared DNA frameworks, using 8% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gels. A 5 µL sample and 1 µL 6× loading buffer was loaded onto the gel, and electrophoresis separation was performed using a Bio-Rad electrophoresis system at 100 V for 1 h. After electrophoresis, the gel was stained with 1× GelRed solution for 10 min, washed with ddH2O, and then imaged using a Bio-Rad Molecular imager.

AFM Imaging.

We used an AFM to image the SDF dots. First, the freshly cleaved mica surface was treated with 5‰ APTES for 30 s, washed with ddH2O. Next, the samples of SDF dots and DNA barcodes were diluted to 5 nM and 1 nM using 1× TAE/Mg2+ buffer (40 mM Tris, 20 mM HAc, 2 mM EDTA, and 12.5 mM MgAc2, pH 8.0), respectively. 10 μL of the samples were then incubated on the treated mica for 5 min, and additional buffer was added to reach a total volume of 50 μL. Finally, we used a multimode eight AFM (Bruker) operating in peak force mode to scan the sample.

TIRF Data Collection and Analysis.

TIRF data were collected and analyzed by following a previously reported protocol (27, 37). TIRF imaging experiments were carried out in PBS buffer (10 mM PBS and 12.5 mM MgCl2, pH 7.4) at RT. The 29 mm glass bottom dishes (Cellvis, Catalog No. D29-10-1-N) were first treated with plasma cleaner to make them hydrophilic and negatively charged. Samples (100 μL, 200 pM) were piped on the glass surface and then incubated for 20 min. The images were captured using total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy with a 100× oil objective lens (HC PL APO 100×/1.47 oil) and an electron-multiplying charge-coupled device camera (Andor-iXon-Ultra-12577). An excitation laser (λex = 488 nm, laser power = 80 W cm−2) and a 505-555 nm emission filter were used for the detection of FAM and Alexa488 signal, an excitation laser (λex = 561 nm, laser power = 61.5 W cm−2), and a 577 to 633 nm emission filter were used for the detection of Cy3 and ROX signal, and an excitation laser (λex = 638 nm, laser power = 6.2 W cm−2) and a 662 to 738 nm emission filter were used for the detection of Cy5 and Alexa647 signal. The penetration depth was set to 130 nm. EM gain was set to 500. The acquisition length varied based on the experiment and how fast the respective probe bleached. Thus, before conducting an experiment we preestablished the acquisition length. The acquisition software was LAS X and data was analyzed in ImageJ. Detailed parameters (laser wavelength, laser power, exposure time, EM gain, and frame number) used in the TIRF imaging are listed in SI Appendix, Table S6. Single molecules were located and traced using the Spot Intensity Analysis plugin in ImageJ (https://imagej.net/Spot_Intensity_Analysis) with the following settings: electron per analog-to-digital unit (ADU) of 1.00 and spot radius of 3. The number of first frames to check and the noise tolerance are shown in SI Appendix, Table S7, since it varies for each sample depending on how fast they bleach. Afterward, the data were further analyzed and plotted with a custom written Python script and only localized particles with >400 photons were counted as in the “on” state. It should be noted that, in some cases the average total number of photons is an underestimate since not all samples fully bleached within the acquisition time. The statistical analysis of half-life was accomplished with Originlab 2022 software. We also immobilized the DNA samples on a BSA-biotin-avidin coated glass bottom dish using a previously described method (45). The 29 mm glass bottom dish (Cellvis, Catalog No. D29-10-1-N) was first treated with 50 μL of BSA-biotin (1 mg/mL in PBS buffer, NaCl 136.89 mM, KCl 2.67 mM, Na2HPO4 8.1 mM, KH2PO4 1.76 mM, pH 7.4), incubated for 5 min, and washed twice with PBS solution. Then, 50 μL of avidin (1 mg/mL in PBS) was added, incubated for 5 min, and washed twice with PBS solution. Finally, 10 μL of sample was added (~50 pM to 100 pM for purified SDF dots, 50 pM for ssDNA,) and incubated for 10 min before imaging using TIRF at RT.

Scavenging Ability of Hydroxyl Radicals (•OH) with TDF.

The hydroxyl radical scavenging efficiency was tested using a hydroxyl radical scavenging capacity assay kit (Micro method 100T/96S; Catalogue number: D799278-0100; Sangon Biological Engineering Technology & Services Co., Ltd., China). Different concentration of TDF was prepared for analysis. All tests were performed following the protocol provided by the manufacturers.

Scavenging Ability of ABTS Radicals with TDF.

The free radical scavenging capability of TDF was tested based on the reduction of •ABTS+ radicals by using the ABTS radical cation decolorization assay. 7 mmol of ABTS was dissolved in deionized water and reacted with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate to produce ABTS radical cation (•ABTS+), and the mixture was kept in the dark at room temperature for 24 h before use. Then, the absorbance at 734 nm of the pure solution of •ABTS+ radicals were measured. Different dilution of •ABTS+ radicals were prepared to adjust its absorbance at 734 nm around 0.7. Then, pure solution of •ABTS+ radical (250 μL) and •ABTS+ radical solution (200 μL) with different concentration of TDF (50 μL) were measured after 24 h incubation with gently stirring under room temperature. The inhibition rate of •ABTS+ radicals was calculated based on the ratio of neutralized •ABTS+ radicals to overall radicals.

Scavenging Ability of Superoxide Radical (O2−) with TDF.

The superoxide radical scavenging efficiency was assessed with a total superoxide dismutase assay kit with WST-8 (Catalog number: S0101M; Beyotime Biotechnology, China). All tests were performed following the protocol provided by the kits.

Self-assembly of Rod-shaped DNA Origami.

We selected 10-helix bundle rod-shaped origami (10HB) with a total length of 240 nm as scaffolds. The rod-shaped DNA origami structure was created via a one-pot reaction in a 100 µL solution containing 10 nM of scaffold strand (M13mp18) and 100 nM of folding staples in 1× TAE/Mg2+ buffer (40 mM Tris, 20 mM HAc, 2 mM EDTA, and 12.5 mM MgAc2, pH 8.0). The solution was annealed using a thermal ramp cooling process, starting from 80 °C and ending at 14 °C over 13 h (with 10 min at 80 °C, followed by a decrease of 1 °C every 5 min from 80 °C to 65 °C, and then a decrease of 1 °C every 20 min from 65 °C to 30 °C). The folded DNA origami was purified away from extra short staple strands by PEG fractionation. Please refer to SI Appendix, Table S5-1 for the DNA strand sequences used to construct the rod-shaped DNA origami structures, and to SI Appendix, Table S5-2 for the sequences used to create the rod-shaped DNA origami structures with two or three docking sites.

Preparation of SDF Dot-based Fluorescent DNA Barcodes.

Super-resolution fluorescent DNA barcodes were constructed by hybridizing SDF dots with 10HB structures. To achieve targeted assembly, extension DNA strands were strategically incorporated at specific positions on the 10HB, while complementary DNA strands were appended to the SDF dots. The assembly reaction was performed by mixing 10HB (10 nM) with a twenty-fold molar excess of SDF dots (200 nM) at RT. To facilitate downstream purification, a biotin-labeled DNA strand (100 nM) was also hybridized to the 10HB structures. The reaction mixture was incubated for 1 h to enable the specific hybridization of SDF dots to the designated sites on the 10HB through complementary DNA base pairing.

Purification of SDF Dot-Based Fluorescent DNA Barcodes.

The fluorescent DNA barcodes were purified using Streptavidin-coated magnetic beads in combination with strand displacement technology, as described in SI Appendix, Table S5-5. The reaction mixture containing hybridized 10HB and SDF dots was incubated with Streptavidin-coated magnetic beads at RT for 15 min. After incubation, the beads were washed three times with 1× TAE/Mg2+ buffer (40 mM Tris, 20 mM HAc, 2 mM EDTA, and 12.5 mM MgAc2, pH 8.0) to remove unbound components. The system was then transferred to a magnetic stand, and the supernatant containing excess SDF dots was discarded. To further minimize residual unbound SDF dots, the magnetic beads were subjected to an additional three washes with 1× TAE/Mg2+ buffer. Subsequently, the beads were resuspended in 1× TAE/Mg2+ buffer, and a 10-fold molar excess of displacement strands was added to competitively displace the fluorescent DNA barcodes from the beads into the solution. The reaction mixture was incubated at RT for 15 min, and then transferred to a magnetic stand to collect the supernatant containing the displaced fluorescent DNA barcodes. The purified barcodes were then characterized by AFM imaging.

SIM Imaging.

SIM imaging experiments were carried out in 1× TAE/Mg2+ buffer (40 mM Tris, 20 mM HAc, 2 mM EDTA, and 12.5 mM MgAc2, pH 8.0) at RT. The 29 mm glass bottom dishes (Cellvis, Catalog No. D29-10-1-N) were first treated with plasma cleaner to make them hydrophilic and negatively charged. Samples (100 μL, 100 pM) were piped on the glass surface and then incubated for 20 min. SIM images were acquired on a Zeiss Elyra7 super-resolution fluorescence microscope, equipped with dual sCMOS PCO Edge 4.2 cameras for simultaneous two channel acquisition, with a 63×/1.4 oil objective (Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.4 oil DIC M27). An excitation laser (λex = 488 nm, laser power = 20 mW) and a 495 to 550 nm emission filter were used for the detection of TDF-Alexa488-edge(B), an excitation laser (λex = 561 nm, laser power = 20 mW), and a 570 to 620 nm emission filter were used for the detection of TDF-Cy3-edge(G), and an excitation laser (λex = 642 nm, laser power = 30 mW) and a 655 to 1,000 nm emission filter were used for the detection of TDF-Cy5-edge (R). For each focal plane 13 phase images were acquired. SIM reconstruction was performed with the SIM2 processing Tool of the ZEN 3.0 SR (Black, v16) software. Detailed parameters (channel, laser wavelength, laser power, Detection wavelength, and exposure time) used in the SIM imaging are listed in SI Appendix, Table S8.

STED Imaging and CLSM Imaging of Cell Membrane.

Cells in 25 cm2 cell culture flask were harvested by trypsinization and washed with fresh medium for three times and suspended in fresh medium for following studies. A volume of 400 μL DMEM containing HeLa cells (5 × 104 cells per well) were seeded into 29 mm glass bottom dishes (Cellvis, Catalog No. D29-14-1-N). After incubation for 24 h, cells were washed twice with 1× PBS buffer. An aliquot of 200 μL 1× PBS containing 6 mM Mg2+ and 0.5 μM SDF dots with two Chol was added to the dishes. After incubation at RT for 15 min, cells were washed twice with 1× PBS (containing 10 mM Mg2+). Both of STED imaging and CLSM imaging were performed with a Leica TCS SP8 STED 3X system equipped with a white light laser for excitation and a 660-nm pulsed laser for STED. A ×100 oil-immersion objective (HC PL APO CS2 100×/1.40 oil) was employed. STED images were obtained under the same imaging conditions: excitation under 7 mW averaged power at 561 nm, STED beam of 1.3W average power at 660 nm, and 100 Hz scan speed. Acquisition spectral range: 570 nm to 590 nm. Deconvolution was performed by using Huygens Software embedded in the Leica TCS SP8 STED 3X system. The deconvolution process was completed by using an autosetting in Huygens. Detailed parameters were as follows: 1) background: automatic estimation, 2) signal-to-noise ratio: 7, 3) estimate mode: lowest, 4) deconvolution algorithm: CMLE, 5) area radius: 0.7, 6) maximum iteration: 40, 7) quality threshold: 0.05, 8) iteration mode: optimized, 9) PSFs per brick: one PSF and 10) brick layout: auto, 11) bleaching correction: if possible. Detailed parameters (channel, excitation laser wavelength, excitation laser power, STED laser wavelength, STED laser power, Detection wavelength, Scan speed, and EM gain) used in the STED imaging and CLSM imaging are listed in SI Appendix, Table S9.

Statistical Analysis.

Single molecules were located and traced using the Spot Intensity Analysis plugin in ImageJ (https://imagej.net/Spot_Intensity_Analysis) with the following settings: electron per analog-to-digital unit (ADU) of 1.00 and spot radius of 3. The number of first frames to check and the noise tolerance are shown in SI Appendix, Table S7, since it varies for each sample depending on how fast they bleach. It should be noted that, in some cases the average total number of photons is an underestimate since not all samples fully bleached within the acquisition time. The statistical analysis of half-life was accomplished with Originlab 2022 software.
