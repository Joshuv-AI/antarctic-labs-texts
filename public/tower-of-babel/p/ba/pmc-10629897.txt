The influence of aggressive exercise on responses to self-perceived and others’ pain
Authors: Zilong Wei, Yujuan Huang, Xiong Li, Min Shao, Huiling Qian, Bojun He, Jing Meng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious studies have reported relationships between exercise and pain. However, little is known about how aggressive exercise modulates individuals’ responses to their own and others’ pain. This present study addresses this question by conducting 2 studies employing event-related potential (ERP). Study 1 included 38 participants whose self-perceived pain was assessed after intervention with aggressive or nonaggressive exercises. Study 2 recruited 36 participants whose responses to others’ pain were assessed after intervention with aggressive or nonaggressive exercise. Study 1’s results showed that P2 amplitudes were smaller, reaction times were longer, and participants’ judgments were less accurate in response to self-perceived pain stimuli, especially to high-pain stimuli, after intervention with aggressive exercise compared to nonaggressive exercise. Results of study 2 showed that both P3 and LPP amplitudes to others’ pain were larger after intervention with aggressive exercise than with nonaggressive exercise. These results suggest that aggressive exercise decreases individuals’ self-perceived pain and increases their empathic responses to others’ pain.

Introduction

The benefits of exercise for physical and mental health have been widely recognized (Haskell et al. 2007). Regular exercise promotes mental health, enhances brain development, increases self-efficacy, and reduces the risk of chronic illnesses (Cecchini-Estrada et al. 2015; Lear et al. 2017; Brooks et al. 2021; Chi et al. 2021; Kim and Lee 2022; Zhang, Wang et al. 2022). However, there is a multiplicity of exercise types, and the benefits of different types of exercise for physical and mental health are diverse (Jorge et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2020; Tao et al. 2021). For instance, aggressive exercises, including combat sports such as boxing, have been found to offer many benefits, such as improvements in cardiovascular strength, reduce stress, and improve self-confidence (Wann and Branscombe 1990; Bozdarov et al. 2023). By comparison, nonaggressive exercises such as golf and jump rope have also been demonstrated to offer benefits, but without any association with aggression (Wann and Branscombe 1990; Hooshmand Moghadam et al. 2021). A previous study showed different effects of aggressive and nonaggressive exercises on individuals’ emotions (Pels and Kleinert 2016). However, the mechanism by which aggressive exercise influences one’s self-perception of pain and the response to others’ pain remains unknown.

Pain is an intrinsically unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage (Raja et al. 2020). Today, pain is a prevalent clinical condition and a globally recognized health problem (Goldberg and McGee 2011; Li and Hu 2016; Lin et al. 2022), and although medications can provide an effective treatment for pain, their use is associated with negative effects such as addiction and side effects (Staahl et al. 2009; Rütgen et al. 2018). Fortunately, non-pharmacological interventions, such as exercise, have shown positive effects on pain (Rice et al. 2019). Previous studies have indicated that exciting the motor cortex can activate brain regions involved in pain regulation, including the thalamus, anterior cingulate cortex, anterior insula, and periaqueductal gray matter (Peyron et al. 2007; Cha et al. 2013) and reduce neuropathic pain in patients (Tsubokawa et al. 1993; Li, Li et al. 2022). An event-related potential (ERP) study has found that isometric exercise caused a decrease in amplitudes evoked by heat pain stimuli on the exercise limb (Wu et al. 2022). Previous studies have identified a primarily negative ERP component occurring approximately 100–200 ms after the application of a pain stimulus. This ERP component, often named N1 by researchers, is widely understood to represent the initial processing of somatosensory input (Lee et al. 2009; Mouraux and Iannetti 2009). Furthermore, the N1 amplitudes exhibit temporal and spatial overlap with the subsequent N2 amplitudes (Cruccu et al. 2008). N1 is associated with the early stage of pain processing, and the decrease of N1 amplitudes implies a corresponding reduction in pain perception in response to the pain stimuli (Hu et al. 2014; Zhuo et al. 2023).

Previous studies have shown a positive correlation between pain and aggression. A study that involved 94 sports athletes showed that athletes who showed more aggression in sports tended to be less sensitive to pain stimuli (Ring et al. 2016). Another study with 195 college students who completed trait measures of aggression and a laboratory assessment of pain tolerance reported that pain tolerance was positively associated with aggression (Reidy et al. 2009). However, it is not only individuals with aggressive tendencies who exhibit diminished pain sensitivity; the engaging of a single aggressive exercise has also been shown to decrease an individuals’ self-perception of pain (Carey et al. 2017). Nevertheless, the mechanism of the specific effects of aggressive exercise on pain remains unknown. Thus, the primary aim of this study was to explore the behavioral and neural mechanism of aggressive exercise on individuals’ self-perception of pain.

As the perception of others’ pain is closely associated with the self-perception of pain (Rütgen et al. 2021), and the neural circuitries of the self-perception of pain and the perception for others’ pain are shared (Keysers et al. 2010; Lamm et al. 2011; Bos et al. 2015), aggressive exercise may not only have effects on an individuals’ self-perception of pain, but may also be associated with their responses to others’ pain. When individuals witness others’ pain, they are usually able to empathize and understand the emotions and feelings of others as if the pain is their own, which is considered to be empathy for pain (Meng et al. 2012; Meng et al. 2013; Meng et al. 2019). One study using functional near-infrared spectroscopy showed that a single bout of moderate exercise results in activating the mirror neuron system (Xu et al. 2020), which is implicated as the key brain region of empathy for pain (Timmers et al. 2018; Yu and Chou 2018). Although previous studies have shown that exercise can promote empathic responses in individuals (Shima et al. 2021), individuals with training in aggressive exercise exhibit greater levels of empathy compared to those without such training (Movahedi et al. 2013; Kozdras 2019; Phung and Goldberg 2021). But the influence of aggressive exercise on empathy for pain is still unknown. Based on the critical role of empathy for pain in social interactions (Haut et al. 2019), it is crucial to understand the relationship between individuals’ responses to others’ pain and aggressive exercise. Therefore, another aim of the present study was to explore the effects of aggressive exercise on responses to others’ pain.

Thus, our current study investigated the effects of aggressive exercise on individuals’ responses to self-perceived pain and others’ pain. More specifically, we examined 2 studies. In study 1, we aimed to investigate the influence of aggressive exercise on individuals’ self-perception of pain. We focused on whether engaging in aggressive exercise could lead to changes in pain perception and related neural responses. In study 2, we aimed to investigate the influence of aggressive exercise on individuals’ response to others’ pain. We focused on whether engaging in aggressive exercise could lead to changes in the response to others’ pain and related neural responses. According to previous studies, engaging in aggressive exercise triggers the activation of individuals’ defense mechanisms, leading to complex changes in pain and perception processing (Kozlowska et al. 2015). This activation assumes a critical role in directing the individual’s attention toward potential threats and facilitating a self-protective response (Deng et al. 2016; Gladwin et al. 2016; Klaassen et al. 2021). More specifically, when these defense mechanisms are activated, individuals redirect their attention from internal bodily sensations and instead remain vigilant toward their external surroundings in order to respond appropriately to potential hazards (Kozlowska et al. 2015). Consequently, we hypothesized that individuals might experience reduced self-perception of pain after engaging in aggressive exercise, as attention is diverted from internal bodily sensations. Furthermore, as others’ pain also represents a threat (Decety et al. 2012), we hypothesized that aggressive exercise might enhance individuals’ empathic responses to others’ pain. This is due to the exercise fostering a heightened level of concern about potential threats from the external environment.

Methods

Study 1: The influence of aggressive exercise on self-perceived pain

Participants

Participants were recruited by means of posters and online advertising in Chongqing Normal University, Chongqing, China. A priori power analysis conducted using G*Power 3 revealed that 30 participants were required to achieve statistical power of 0.9 to detect median-sized (f = 0.25) effects with an alpha value of 0.05 for a two-factor within-participants repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). To account for possible dropouts or errors during the study, our study 1 included 38 participants (19 female), aged between 19 and 26 years (M = 22.16 years, SD = 2.26 years). Criteria for inclusion were: normal or corrected-to-normal vision, no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders, no acute or chronic pain, or current use of any medication.

Written informed consent was provided by all participants prior to participation in the experiment. The study was approved by the Chongqing Normal University Research Ethics Committee, and all procedures were performed in accordance with ethical guidelines and regulations.

Stimuli

Exercise type

Study 1 included aggressive and nonaggressive exercises. Similar to a previous study (Pels and Kleinert 2016), involving aggressive exercise, participants were required to perform a single boxing session in a quiet, 26°C room for approximately 15 min. For the nonaggressive exercise, participants were required to jump rope for approximately 15 min in the same room as the aggressive exercise.

To ensure consistent physiological effects of both aggressive and nonaggressive exercises (Barlett et al. 2009), participants were required to complete 3 psychophysical (including emotion, arousal, and heart rate) assessments, before the engaging exercises (Psychophysical assessment 1), after completion of the exercise (Psychophysical assessment 2), and at the end of the study (Psychophysical assessment 3), see Fig. 1. Participant’s emotions (1 = very unhappy, 9 = very happy) and arousal (1 = extremely peaceful, 9 = extremely exciting) were measured using 9-point Likert scales. Participants’ heart rates were assessed using a heart rate monitor (Polar H10 heart rate monitoring system, Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland). To increase the reliability of this measurement, heart rates were measured 3 times during Psychophysical assessment 1, with the average of the 3 readings used as the baseline heart rate. Heart rates were also monitored during both exercises, and exercises were stopped when the heart rates reached 1.5 times a participant’s baseline heart rate. The detailed data of 3 Psychophysical assessments in Study 1 have been compiled in the Appendix, and their presentation can be observed in Fig. 2.

Pain stimuli

Pain stimuli were delivered through electrodes placed on the dorsum of the left hand using a constant current stimulator (SXC-4A, Sanxia Technique Inc, China). As physical pain sensitivity varies between individuals and can differ across body sites (Hu et al. 2014; Boll et al. 2018), after signing the informed consent form, we performed a psychophysical pain calibration on the dorsum of the left hand of each participant separately to match participants’ stimuli intensity and subjective pain level using the similar procedure as in a previous study (Yao et al. 2020). Before the assessment, we informed participants that they could withdraw from the experiment (by pressing “ESC” on the keyboard) if they could not tolerate the pain or were no longer willing to continue the experiment. Two participants subsequently withdrew from the study.

The intensity of electrical stimulation started at 300 μA and increased with 100 μA (stimuli duration = 50 ms) steps. After each stimulation, participants were instructed to rate the perceived pain intensity using a 9-point Likert scale (1 = no sensation, 4 = pain threshold, 9 = unbearable pain). This procedure was repeated twice for each participant, with a break of about 10 min. To determine the corresponding low-pain and high-pain stimuli intensities, we averaged each participant’s ratings for stimuli with pain levels of 5 (for low-pain) and 7 (for high-pain) over the 2 sessions, and they were used to determine the corresponding low pain and high pain stimulus intensities for the formal study. Across all participants, the calibrated electrical current for pain stimuli were 1441.67 ± 48.96 μA (low-pain) and 3819.33 ± 63.94 μA (high-pain) for female participants, and 2757.56 ± 166.17 μA (low-pain) and 4509.10 ± 172.48 μA (high-pain) for male participants.

Procedure

The participants were led into a quiet room with an ambient temperature of about 26°C. They were required to complete 2 blocks of exercise types (either aggressive or nonaggressive exercises) 1 week apart, and the order of the 2 blocks was counterbalanced between participants. After completing the exercise, participants were led to a table with a computer screen and required to complete the experiment while electroencephalography (EEG) recordings were documented and the subjective assessments. The experimental procedure used in study 1 is illustrated in the top panel of Fig. 1.

Stimuli were presented using the E-Prime (3.0) program (Psychology Software Tools, Inc, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). At the start of each trial of the experiment of EEG recordings, a 500 ms fixation cross was presented on a black screen, followed by a stimulus after 800–1,500 ms. The stimulus lasted for 50 ms, and participants were instructed to respond as accurately and quickly as possible by pressing a key (either “1” or “2”) to indicate whether the stimulus was low-pain or high-pain. Key-pressing was counterbalanced across participants to control for potential order effects. The intertrial interval was 4,000–6,000 ms. The study comprised 60 trials for each block of the intervention condition with a total of 120 trials.

After the experiment of EEG recordings, participants were instructed to complete the subjective assessments, to rate the pain intensity (1 = no sensation, 4 = pain threshold, 9 = unbearable pain) and emotional valence (1 = very unhappy, 9 = very happy) for each stimulus, using 9-point Likert scales.

E‌EG recording and data analyses

EEG data were recorded from 32 scalp sites using tin electrodes mounted on an antiChamp system (Brain Vision LLC, Morrisville, NC, US). The electrode on the frontal mastoid was used as the recording reference, and the medial frontal aspect was used as a ground electrode. All electrode impedances remained below 5 kΩ.

EEG data were preprocessed and analyzed via MATLAB R2016a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) and the EEGLAB toolbox (Delorme and Makeig 2004). Continuous EEG signals were band-passed, filtered (0.1–30 Hz), and segmented using a 1,200 ms time window. Time windows of 200 ms before and 1,000 ms after the onset of stimuli were extracted from the continuous EEG. EEG epochs were baseline-corrected by a 200 ms time interval prior to stimuli onset. Electro-oculographic artifacts were corrected with an independent component analysis algorithm (Jung et al. 2001).

After confirming scalp topographies in both the single-participant and group-level ERP waveforms, as well as on the basis of previous studies (Meng et al. 2013; Hu et al. 2014; Ring et al. 2016), the dominant ERP components were identified (see Fig. 4), including N1 and P2. These components have been strongly associated with the processing of pain stimuli (Kakigi et al. 2000; Tiemann et al. 2018; Zhuo et al. 2023).

Amplitudes of N1 were measured at the right-central electrodes (Fz, F4, FC2, Cz, C4) contralateral to the side of stimuli and calculated as average ERP amplitudes within latency intervals of 108–128 ms. Amplitudes of P2 were measured at the central electrodes (FC1, FC2, Cz, CP1, CP2) and calculated as average ERP amplitudes within latency intervals of 184–204 ms.

Statistical analyses

Data analyses were performed using SPSS 15.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Behavioral data (reaction times [RTs], accuracies [ACCs]), subjective assessments (pain intensity and emotional valence), and ERP data (amplitudes of dominant ERP components) were analyzed using a repeated measures ANOVA with 2 within-participant factors of “exercise type” (aggressive exercise vs. nonaggressive exercise) and “stimuli intensity” (low-pain vs. high-pain). If the interactions between the 2 factors were significant, simple effects analyses between the 2 exercise types were performed for each stimuli intensity.

Study 2: The influence of aggressive exercise on responses to others’ pain

Participants

Participants were recruited by means of posters and online advertising in Chongqing Normal University, Chongqing, China. A priori power analysis conducted using the G*Power 3 revealed that 30 participants were required to reach a good statistical power of 0.9 to detect median-sized (f = 0.25) effects with an alpha value of 0.05 for a 2-factor within-participants repeated measures ANOVA. To account for possible dropouts or errors during the study, our study 2 included 36 participants (17 female), distinct from the participants in study 1, aged between 19 and 26 years (M = 22.17 years, SD = 2.27 years). Criteria for inclusion were: normal or corrected-to-normal vision, no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders, no acute or chronic pain, or current use of any medication.

Written informed consent was provided by all participants prior to participation in the experiment. The study was approved by the Chongqing Normal University Research Ethics Committee, and all procedures were performed in accordance with ethical guidelines and regulations.

Stimuli

Exercise type

Same as study 1.

Painful and non-painful pictures

The stimuli in study 2 used in the experiment were 70 digital color painful and non-painful pictures (Meng et al. 2023) and all pictures depicted ordinary events that occasionally happen in daily life. All the events shown in the non-painful pictures corresponded to those in the painful pictures, but without the nociceptive component. The luminance, contrast, and color were well matched between others’ painful and non-painful pictures (Zhang and Chen 2022), as illustrated by the representative pair in Fig. 3. All were 6.67 × 5.03 inch (width × height) and 100 pixels per inch.

Procedure

Participants were required to complete 2 blocks of exercise types (either aggressive or nonaggressive exercises) 1 week apart, and the order of the 2 blocks of exercise types was counterbalanced between participants. After completing the exercise, participants were led to a table with a computer screen, where they were required to participate in an experiment. During this experiment, EEG recordings were taken, and participants also provided subjective assessments. The experimental procedure used in study 2 is illustrated in the bottom panel of Fig. 1.

Stimuli were presented on a 24-inch computer screen, using the E-Prime (3.0). All participants were seated about 100 cm from the screen, subtending a visual angle of 9.64° × 7.99° at viewing. At the start of each trial, a 500 ms fixation cross was presented on a blank screen, followed by 800–1,500 ms later by a picture, and participants were instructed to respond as accurately and quickly as possible by pressing a key (either “1” or “2”) to indicate whether the picture depicted pain. Key-pressing was counterbalanced across participants to control for potential order effects. The intertrial interval was 500 ms. The study comprised 140 trials for each of the 2 blocks, with a 1 week break between blocks.

After the experiment with EEG recordings, participants were instructed to complete the subjective assessments to rate the pain intensity (1 = no sensation, 4 = pain threshold, 9 = unbearable pain) and emotional valence (1 = very unhappy, 9 = very happy) of each picture.

E‌EG recording and data analyses

EEG recordings and data analyses were similar to those for study 1. Trials containing significant noise from gross movements were removed—removed epochs accounted for 6.25% ± 5.35% of the total number of epochs. After confirming scalp topographies in both the single-participant and group-level ERP waveforms, and on the basis of previous studies (Hu et al. 2014; Meng et al. 2019; Li, Wei et al. 2022), dominant ERP components were identified as follows: N1, N2, and P2 (Fz, F3, F4, FC1, FC2); P3 and LPP (CP1, CP2, P3, Pz, P4). Amplitudes of N1, N2, and P2 were calculated as the mean amplitudes with a latency of peak ±10 ms at electrodes displaying maximal responses. P3 amplitudes were measured at latency intervals of 380–400 ms, and the LPP was extracted within a time window of 500–600 ms.

Statistical analyses

Same as study 1.

Results

Study 1: The influence of aggressive exercise on self-perceived pain

Behavioral results

Results of the ANOVA showed that RTs were modulated by the main effects of “exercise type” (F (1, 37) = 6.21, P = 0.017, ηp2 = 0.14) and “stimuli intensity” (F (1, 37) = 10.72, P = 0.002, ηp2 = 0.23). Participants responded more slowly to the stimuli after intervention with the aggressive exercise (776.02 ± 31.09 ms) than the nonaggressive exercise (708.26 ± 26.46 ms), and responded more slowly to the high-pain stimuli (780.58 ± 31.40 ms) than the low-pain stimuli (703.70 ± 24.23 ms).

Results showed that ACCs were also modulated by the main effects of “exercise type” (F (1, 37) = 5.85, P = 0.021, ηp2 = 0.14) and “stimuli intensity” (F (1, 37) = 11.81, P = 0.001, ηp2 = 0.24). Participants judged the stimuli less accurately after intervention with the aggressive exercise (86.04% ± 1.64%) than with the nonaggressive exercise (90.79% ± 0.66%), and responded less accurately to the high-pain stimuli (84.16% ± 1.98%) than to the low-pain stimuli (92.67% ± 0.56%). ACCs were also modulated by the interaction between “exercise type” and “stimuli intensity” (F (1, 37) = 5.51, P = 0.024, ηp2 = 0.13). Simple effect analyses indicated that the high-pain stimuli were judged less accurately after intervention with the aggressive exercise (79.91% ± 3.63%) than with the nonaggressive exercise (88.77% ± 1.18%, P = 0.010); however, no significant difference was observed in response to low-pain stimuli between the 2 intervention conditions (P > 0.05).

Pain intensity ratings were modulated by the main effect of “stimuli intensity” (F (1, 37) = 152.59, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.81), indicating that the high-pain stimuli (6.23 ± 0.23) were judged as more painful than the low-pain stimuli (4.45 ± 0.13).

Emotional valence ratings were modulated by the main effect of “stimuli intensity” (F (1, 37) = 10.56, P = 0.002, ηp2 = 0.22), indicating that the high-pain stimuli (4.52 ± 0.16) were judged more negatively than the low-pain stimuli (5.01 ± 0.15).

Study 2: The influence of aggressive exercise on responses to others’ pain

Behavioral results

No significant main effect nor interaction was found for RTs (all ps > 0.05).

Results indicated that ACCs were modulated by the main effect of “stimuli type” (F (1, 35) = 5.66, P = 0.023, ηp2 = 0.14), indicating that participants responded less accurately to the painful pictures (93.69% ± 1.13%) than to the non-painful pictures (96.75% ± 0.38%).

Pain intensity ratings were modulated by the main effect of “stimuli type” (F (1, 35) = 768.27, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.96), indicating that participants judged the painful pictures (5.42 ± 0.14) to be more painful than the non-painful stimuli (1.30 ± 0.06).

Emotional valence ratings were modulated by the main effects of “exercise type” (F (1, 35) = 4.29, P = 0.046, ηp2 = 0.11) and “stimuli type” (F (1, 35) = 25.32, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.42), indicating that participants judged the stimuli more positively after intervention with the aggressive exercise (4.75 ± 0.10) than with the nonaggressive exercise (4.59 ± 0.09), and felt more negatively toward the painful pictures (4.05 ± 0.17) than the non-painful pictures (5.29 ± 0.13).

Discussion

The current study used EEG technology to investigate the influence of aggressive exercise on pain sensitivity and the response for others’ pain. Specifically, we investigated differences in individuals’ behavioral and neural responses to self-perceived pain and others’ pain when primed with aggressive versus nonaggressive exercises. Results showed that: (i) Smaller P2 amplitudes, longer RTs, and less accurate judgments in response to self-perceived pain stimuli (especially to high-pain stimuli) after intervention with aggressive exercise compared with nonaggressive exercise were found in study 1; (ii) Larger P3 and LPP amplitudes in response to others’ pain after intervention with aggressive exercise compared with nonaggressive exercise were found in study 2.

Study 1: Aggressive exercise decreases the perception of self-perceived pain

In line with previous studies (Meng et al. 2013; Peng et al. 2019; Zhang, Lu et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023), study 1 found larger N1 and P2 amplitudes in response to high-pain stimuli than to low-pain stimuli. As N1 and P2 amplitudes were interpreted as neural indexes encoding the painful stimulus, which were suggested to be related to the pain intensity of noxious stimulus (Hu et al. 2014), the ERP results in the present study suggested that high-pain stimuli were more painful than low-pain stimuli. These ERP results were also consistent with the behavioral results of study 1, which showed higher pain intensity ratings and more negative emotional valence ratings to high-pain stimuli than to low-pain stimuli.

P2 amplitudes were modulated by the interaction between “exercise type” and “stimuli type,” and smaller P2 amplitudes in response to high-pain stimuli were found after intervention with aggressive exercise than with nonaggressive exercise, whereas there was no such difference for low-pain stimuli. In other words, the effect of “exercise type” on neural responses to self-perceived pain was only found with high-pain stimuli. These findings suggest that the effect of exercise type on pain processing may depend on the intensity of the pain stimulus, and that aggressive exercise may have a greater effect on the neural response to high-pain stimuli.

Study 2: Aggressive exercise increases the empathic responses to others’ pain

Consistent with previous studies (Cui et al. 2015; Meng et al. 2020; Li et al. 2023), the present study found that ERP (N2, P3, and LPP) amplitudes were modulated by the main effect of “stimuli type,” and painful pictures resulted in more positive ERP wave deflections than non-painful pictures. In previous studies, these ERP components have been shown to be a good indicator of empathic responses to others’ pain. For example, N2 at the frontoparietal lobe reflects early empathy for pain and emotion sharing, and the P3 and LPP at the parieto-occipital lobe reflect late cognitive appraisal (Li et al. 2019; Meng et al. 2020). Study 2 used painful pictures of human limbs to elicit similar empathic responses to others’ pain as previous studies.

More importantly, P3 and LPP components were modulated by the interaction between “exercise type” and “stimuli type”; that is, for painful pictures, P3 and LPP amplitudes were larger after intervention with aggressive exercise than with nonaggressive exercise. Since P3 and LPP are the cognitive components of empathy for pain (Fan and Han 2008), these results suggest aggressive exercise enhances individuals’ empathic responses to others’ pain.

The influence of aggressive exercise on responses to self-perceived pain and others’ pain

Our results suggested that compared to nonaggressive exercise, aggressive exercise is more likely to reduce self-perceived pain and increase the responses to others’ pain. This phenomenon could be attributed to the connection between aggressive exercise and defense mechanisms, which represent coping mechanisms for individuals confronting threats or dangers (Kozlowska et al. 2015). Thus, engaging in aggressive exercise can activate human defense mechanisms, redirecting their attention from internal bodily sensations and reducing the self-perception of pain, thus preparing individuals to face potential threats and hazards (Deng et al. 2016). Therefore, the lower pain sensitivity (especially to high-pain stimuli) of individuals after engaging in aggressive exercise might be due to the activation of human defense mechanisms, which cause the body to redirect their attention from internal bodily sensations and reduce self-perceived pain.

And from the perspective of the perception of others’ pain, 1 possible explanation for this phenomenon is that engaging in aggressive exercise could trigger an activation of individuals’ defense mechanisms, which could remain vigilant toward their external surroundings in order to respond appropriately to potential threat (Kozlowska et al. 2015). Since others’ pain also represents a threat (Decety et al. 2012), this heightened concern may extend to others’ pain. Therefore, the increased response to others’ pain in individuals after engaging in aggressive exercise might result from the activation of their defense mechanisms, making them vigilant against potential threats, including the perception of others’ pain.

To summarize, engaging in aggressive exercise can reduce an individual’s self-perceived pain by triggering defense mechanisms that result in redirecting their attention from internal bodily sensations. Simultaneously, aggressive exercise can remain vigilant toward their external surroundings in order to respond appropriately to potential threat, including others’ pain, finally causing the better mental state and thus enhancing the perception of others’ pain. Consequently, aggressive exercise could reduce self-perceived pain and increase the perception of others’ pain.

Limitations

Despite the methodological rigor with which these studies were conducted, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the participants in this study were all healthy young adults and it is unclear to what extent these findings apply to older people or those with chronic pain or other medical conditions. Second, the ERP technique used in this study did not provide evidence regarding the area of brain activation, which may be further explored in future studies using fMRI. Finally, in spite of our efforts to control for external factors, there may be other variables that may have influenced the results of the study, such as individual differences in pain sensitivity or prior experience with active exercise.

Conclusion

To investigate the influence of aggressive exercise on individuals’ responses to self-perceived pain and others’ pain, this study primed participants with aggressive and nonaggressive exercises to assess both their own pain and empathy for others’ pain using ERP techniques. The results showed that compared to nonaggressive exercise, aggressive exercise decreased individuals’ sensitivity to self-perceived pain and increased empathic responses to others’ pain.
