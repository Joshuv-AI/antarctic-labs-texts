Tubeless uniportal subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy via percutaneous suspension technique for anterior mediastinal masses
Authors: Jun-Jie Wang, Jian-Ming Yue, Hao-Ji Yan, Jun-Min Zhu, Kai-Yuan Jiang, Cheng Chen, Yong-Xiang Song, Yong Xiao, Hai-Ning Zhou, Dong Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe previously developed the percutaneous suspension technique to improve the anterior mediastinal exposure in minimally invasive thymectomy. In this study, we aimed to integrate this technique with tubeless video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) for uniportal subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy and to evaluate perioperative outcomes of this combined approach.MethodsThis retrospective, multicenter study included patients with anterior mediastinal masses who underwent uniportal subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy using the percutaneous suspension technique combined with tubeless VATS at 4 centers between March and September 2025. Comprehensive early outcome data, including intraoperative and postoperative variables, were collected and analyzed.ResultsIn total, 15 patients were included, with a median age of 53 years (range, 25-75 years). Median operative and anesthesia times were 83 minutes (range, 35-192 minutes) and 140 minutes (range, 108-264 minutes), respectively. The median pain score was 2 (range, 1-3) on postoperative day 1 and was 0 in all patients on postoperative day 7. The median postoperative length of stay was 2 days (range, 1-4 days). No conversions and no major intraoperative or postoperative complications occurred. 5 patients (33.3%) developed mild pneumothorax and 3 (20.0%) developed mild atelectasis, all managed conservatively without chest drain insertion. No patient required reinsertion of a chest drain or urinary catheter during hospitalization or after discharge.ConclusionsTubeless uniportal subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy with the percutaneous suspension technique is safe and feasible for anterior mediastinal masses, although tubeless VATS potentially associated with a slightly higher incidence of minor complications.

Central Message

Tubeless uniportal subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy with the percutaneous suspension technique is a safe and feasible approach for anterior mediastinal masses, with controllable complication.

Perspective

Our newly developed Tian-Ping sternal lifter effectively addresses the challenge of combining uniportal subxiphoid minimally invasive thymectomy with tubeless VATS by improving the restricted retrosternal operative space and has been preliminarily validated and promoted in clinical practice.

Anterior mediastinal masses (AMMs) encompass a broad spectrum of benign and malignant lesions and account for approximately half of mediastinal masses, with thymic-origin diseases among the most common etiologies.1 Thymectomy remains the standard treatment for resectable AMMs.2,3 With advances in thoracoscopic techniques, minimally invasive thymectomy (MIT) has been increasingly adopted. Compared with the intercostal approach, the uniportal subxiphoid approach provides improved visualization of the anterior mediastinum, offering smaller incisions, reduced postoperative pain, and shorter hospital stays.4,5

In parallel, advances in anesthetic management have promoted the adoption of tubeless video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS).6, 7, 8, 9 In strict selected patients, a laryngeal mask airway (LMA) can safely replace endotracheal intubation and may reduce intubation-related complications.9, 10, 11 Moreover, avoiding urinary catheterization and postoperative chest drainage may reduce tube-related trauma, discomfort, and infection, thereby facilitating recovery and shortening hospital stay.12,13 Available evidence suggests that tubeless VATS for intercostal MIT is safe and feasible in patients with AMMs.9,10 However, owing to the inherently constrained retrosternal workspace in the uniportal subxiphoid approach, which may be further challenged under tubeless conditions by mediastinal motion and limited instrument maneuverability, tubeless VATS has not yet been reported for uniportal subxiphoid MIT, underscoring the need for adjunctive techniques to expand the retrosternal operative field.14, 15, 16

Sternal elevation is a key adjunct to enlarge the retrosternal workspace. Existing sternal elevation devices mostly rely on rigid traction on the sternum and ribs to expand the retrosternal space, which may increase the risk of bone-related injury and may be limited by stability and exposure.17, 18, 19, 20, 21 To overcome these limitations, we developed the Tian-Ping (aka, Tian's) sternal lifter (Hangzhou Chuangmeng Medical Equipment Co Ltd), a balance-shaped subcutaneous sternal elevation device that employs a percutaneous suspension technique to indirectly lift the sternum. We previously reported its initial clinical application and confirmed its feasibility in complex cases, including MIT with superior vena cava replacement without sternotomy and giant teratomas.22, 23, 24 The device is designed to provide a stable and spacious operative field for AMMs resection while minimizing iatrogenic injury.

Building on these advances, we first propose the tubeless uniportal subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy via percutaneous suspension technique. This study standardizes the operative workflow and evaluates the feasibility, safety, and perioperative outcomes of this approach for minimally invasive resection of AMMs.

Methods

Patients and Materials

This retrospective multicenter study included 15 patients with AMMs who underwent this new technique between March and September 2025 at 4 hospitals: West China Hospital of Sichuan University, Suining Central Hospital, the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University, and Sichuan Science City Hospital.

The criteria for inclusion of patients in this retrospective study were as follows: AMMs confirmed by contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT); treatment with uniportal subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy via percutaneous suspension technique; underwent tubeless approach, defined as LMA ventilation without endotracheal intubation, no urinary catheterization, and no postoperative chest drainage; and age younger than 75 years, body mass index <30, no significant cardiopulmonary dysfunction, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status ≤II, and no obvious invasion of adjacent organs. Exclusion criteria were as follows: prior neoadjuvant therapy, history of chronic pain or long-term use of analgesics, previous sternotomy, and presence of active infection or any concurrent malignant diseases. The clinicopathological characteristics, perioperative data, and follow-up data of eligible patients were collected.

This surgical protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the West China Hospital of Sichuan University (No. 2024-1591). Individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. The Tian-Ping sternal lifter was patented in China (No. ZL202420296704.7). The study device was manufactured by Hangzhou Chuangmeng Medical Equipment Co Ltd.

Anesthesia and Ventilation

During anesthesia induction, preoxygenation and denitrogenation were performed via facemask. Intravenous anesthetics, primarily propofol combined with opioids, or inhalational agents such as sevoflurane and isoflurane, were administered for induction and maintenance. A video LMA was recommended.25 Electrocardiogram, heart rate, blood pressure, pulse oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, and bispectral index were continuously monitored. During repositioning, the anesthesiologist protected the cervical spine and head and reassessed LMA ventilation once the position was secured. If needed, alignment and ventilation were optimized by cuff deflation-inflation, mandibular lift, or the up-and-down maneuver. In the event of intraoperative hypoxemia or hypercapnia, intermittent positive-pressure ventilation via an LMA was applied to maintain adequate ventilation, with appropriate adjustment of the inspired oxygen concentration, tidal volume, and respiratory rate. If this approach proved ineffective, conversion to endotracheal intubation was considered following assessment by the anesthesiologist.

Surgical Procedure

Our technique has been previously published,22,23 as detailed below: patients were positioned supine with the legs abducted (Video 1). The surgeon stood between the patient's legs, and 2 assistants stood bilaterally, with the camera assistant on the patient's right. During dissection of the retrosternal space, the thoracoscope is usually positioned at the lower edge of the subxiphoid incision without suspension. During dissection of the prepericardial space, the thoracoscope is moved to the upper edge of the incision and suspended with a urinary catheter, which helps maintain the scope above the working instruments and reduces assistant fatigue. Additionally, the shaft of the thoracoscope is positioned on either side of the incision rather than parallel to the incision.

Skin markings were placed at the suprasternal notch, xiphoid process, and subxiphoid region (Figure 1, A). After surgical disinfection and draping, the Tian-Ping sternal lifter was installed (Figure 1, B). A tunnel needle was advanced subcutaneously along the preoperative marking line in a longitudinal plane anterior to the sternum, from the xiphoid toward the suprasternal notch, over a distance approximately equal to the sternal length. A 3- to 4-cm longitudinal subxiphoid incision was made. The tunnel needle was elevated with the Tian-Ping sternal lifter, which lifted the overlying skin and indirectly raised the sternum through skin tension, thereby expanding the retrosternal operative space. The procedure adhered to tubeless VATS protocol, with no routine preoperative urinary catheter and no postoperative chest drainage tube. After meticulous hemostasis and confirmation of complete lung reexpansion by intermittent positive-pressure ventilation via an LMA, the Tian-Ping sternal lifter was removed, and the incision was closed in layers (Figure 1, D).

Emergency Management

In the event of intraoperative emergencies such as hemorrhage, cardiac arrest, or cardiac tamponade, emergency management would first be attempted thoracoscopically. If necessary, an additional lateral thoracic incision could be made to facilitate the procedure. If these measures prove ineffective, conversion to an open procedure may be undertaken at the surgeon's discretion. The management strategy is consistent with that used in conventional subxiphoid thoracoscopic surgery. Conversion to thoracotomy or median sternotomy can be performed without repositioning the patient. In an experienced team, the sternal elevation system can be removed within 1 minute. Depending on the clinical situation, the heart and mediastinal structures were exposed to permit vascular repair, internal cardiac massage, or pericardiotomy as indicated.

Postoperative Assessment

Pain was assessed with the visual analog scale (VAS) (0 = no pain, 10 = worst pain) on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7 and on the day of discharge. Postoperatively, all patients underwent chest CT and/or bedside ultrasonography to evaluate the operative field and pleural cavity and to exclude pneumothorax or pleural effusion. The Clavien-Dindo classification system was employed to assess and complications.28 After discharge, patients were followed for 1 to 3 months.

Results

Clinical Features

From March to September 2025, 15 consecutive patients underwent the procedure. AMMs were successfully resected, with histological diagnoses confirmed, and no cases required conversion to open surgery. seven (46.7%) patients were men, and 8 (53.3%) were women, with a median age of 53 years (range, 25-75 years) and a median body mass index of 25.0 (range, 18.6-29.3). The patients' pulmonary function was generally good, with a median forced vital capacity of 3.66 L (range, 2.82-5.04 L) and a median forced expiratory volume in 1 second of 3.05 L (range, 2.25-4.16 L). Seven (46.7%) patients had a smoking history. Four (26.7%) patients had coexisting hypertension, and none had concomitant myasthenia gravis (MG). All patients had an American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status of II at the time of the procedure. The median pathological length was 39 mm (range, 18-100 mm). Pathological analysis revealed that 6 cases were thymoma, 2 were thymic carcinoma, 1 was a teratoma and 6 were cysts. Perioperative Results

No patients required intraoperative conversion to endotracheal intubation. Total thymectomy was performed in 14 patients and extended thymectomy in 1 patient. R0 resection was achieved in all patients; 1 patient also underwent an additional lung resection. Median operative time was 83 minutes (range, 35-192 minutes); intraoperative blood loss was 5 mL (range, 5-20 mL); and anesthesia time was 140 minutes (range, 108-264 minutes). The median time to full emergence from anesthesia was 9 minutes (range, 6-14 minutes). Median postanesthesia care unit stay was 20 minutes (range, 14-31 minutes), and no patients required postoperative admission to the intensive care unit. Oral intake resumed at a median of 1 day (range, 1-2 days) postoperatively. The median postoperative length of stay was 2 days (range, 1-4 days). VAS scores on postoperative days 1 and 3 were 2 (range, 1-3) and 0 (range, 0-1), respectively. By postoperative day 7, all patients had a VAS score of 0. All observed complications were Clavien-Dindo grade <2.5. Five (33.3%) patients developed mild atelectasis, 3 (20.0%) had mild pneumothorax, and 1 (6.7%) experienced an irritative cough. Discussion

This study is the first to systematically describe tubeless uniportal subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy via percutaneous suspension technique. The percutaneous suspension technique markedly improves exposure of the anterior mediastinum, thereby enabling the successful application of tubeless VATS in uniportal subxiphoid MIT. This combined approach appears feasible and safe with controllable perioperative risk, promotes enhanced postoperative recovery, and provides a novel minimally invasive strategy for the management of AMMs.

As minimally invasive techniques have advanced, approaches for AMMs have been refined, chiefly the intercostal approach and the subxiphoid approach.14, 15, 16 Compared with open thoracotomy, the intercostal approach reduces surgical trauma and accelerates recovery but provides limited exposure of the contralateral mediastinum and poses technical challenges during manipulation.5,15 By contrast, the subxiphoid approach proposed by Kido and colleagues29 affords exposure comparable to open thoracotomy while reducing the risk of injury to critical structures.5,16 Use of the percutaneous suspension technique further augments the subxiphoid approach, allowing all surgical steps and specimen retrieval through a single 3- to 4-cm subxiphoid incision.22,23 This technique also minimizes intercostal nerve injury and obviates additional incisions. In keeping with oncologic principles, all patients underwent resection of the mass with concomitant thymectomy to reduce recurrence risk, and no thymus-related complications were observed during short-term follow-up.30

To enhance surgical exposure and working space, Cooper and colleagues17 and others18, 19, 20, 21 reported a mechanical sternum-lifting device that was later adopted and refined in clinical practice. In 2013, Zielinski and colleagues18 described a double-sternal retractor technique in which direct sternal elevation improved visualization of the anterior mediastinum. Our center also implemented this technique and achieved favorable clinical outcomes.20 However, despite iterative refinements, notable limitations persist: limited structural stability, constrained sternal elevation, a relatively high risk of sternal injury, and a cumbersome installation process. We therefore designed the Tian-Ping sternal lifter with a door-shaped base that distributes force bilaterally, reducing intraoperative chest wall tilt and providing a more stable operative field.22,23 This device elevates the sternum indirectly by lifting the skin with a tunnel needle, thereby avoiding bone injury associated with direct traction. In addition, its modular design simplifies preoperative preparation and enables convenient, efficient installation. During broader clinical adoption, the device showed intuitive operability and a short learning curve, allowing surgical teams to master the technique rapidly even with limited prior experience. Cosmetically, the percutaneous suspension technique requires only 2 5-mm puncture ports and a single 3- to 4-cm subxiphoid incision, and postoperative healing leaves minimal visible scarring. Follow-up data from our center showed significantly reduced postoperative pain. This benefit likely reflects the soft suspension technique, which minimizes periosteal injury, whereas the subxiphoid route protects the intercostal nerves.

It is well recognized that intraoperative placement of urinary catheters may increase tissue trauma and infection risk while also restricting patient mobility.31,32 Evidence indicates that early postoperative removal or perioperative avoidance of these catheters mitigate these complications.32,33 Similarly, postoperative chest drainage tube exacerbates pain, impairs pulmonary ventilation, and hinders early ambulation. Multiple studies show that omitting chest drainage tube placement after MIT is safe and feasible but and significantly reduces postoperative pain without increasing adverse events.34,35 By contrast, double-lumen endotracheal intubation remains the anesthetic standard in thoracic surgery, and avoiding intubation in this setting is more clinically challenging.

Since Pompeo and colleagues36 first reported non-intubated VATS in 2004, the technique has continued to evolve and is now widely adopted worldwide.6,12,13 In line with our institutional experience and recent literature, non-intubated VATS, compared with conventional double-lumen intubation, reduces postoperative airway complications and alleviates discomfort, facilitating earlier oral intake and ambulation.25 In this study, nonintubated general anesthesia was delivered via LMA, maintained procedural safety while avoiding intubation-related complications. Moreover, patients had a shorter median postoperative length of stay. To further enhance benefits, we combined nonintubated anesthesia with perioperative measures that support enhanced recovery, including omission of chest drainage tube and urinary catheter. With further refinement of perioperative management and postoperative surveillance, this tubeless strategy may be more widely applied in thoracic day surgery and enhanced recovery after surgery programs.

For patients considered candidates for the tubeless MIT, surgeons should conduct a thorough preoperative evaluation of baseline status, multiorgan function, and the anticipated extent of resection.6,12,36 We believe that tubeless VATS is suitable for patients with concomitant MG, although none of the patients in the present series had MG. Likewise, we consider this approach to be feasible for extended thymectomy in patients with generalized non-thymomatous MG. Intraoperatively, meticulous dissection, precise hemostasis, and a water-seal test to detect air leakage are essential. In this study, 5 patients developed mild atelectasis, likely related to limited postoperative lung recruitment with the LMA and intraoperative traction and compression of lung lobes. In addition, completion thymectomy necessitated opening both pleural cavities, which led to mild pneumothorax in 3 patients. Another patient developed irritable cough. All complications improved or resolved before hospital discharge. The proposed percutaneous suspension technique is not intended to replace conventional methods but to offer an additional minimally invasive option. For patients who are not candidates for tubeless MIT, standard endotracheal anesthesia combined with the percutaneous suspension technique remains a safe and effective strategy.22,23

Despite preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility of tubeless uniportal subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy with percutaneous suspension technique for resecting AMMs, several limitations remain. First, the sample size was small, and future multicenter, large-scale randomized controlled trials are needed to evaluate long-term efficacy and safety. Second, although no skin ischemia or necrosis occurred during postoperative follow-up, the theoretical risk of traction-related ischemic injury cannot be excluded. Future studies should systematically record suspension duration and traction force and assess postoperative skin viability to define safety thresholds. Finally, in patients requiring concomitant pulmonary resection due to tumor invasion, 1-lung ventilation via endotracheal intubation remains indispensable. Likewise, in complex cases involving a prolonged operative duration or a more extensive resection, the need for urinary catheterization should be reassessed to prevent urinary retention, and placement of a chest drain should also be considered.

Conclusions

Tubeless uniportal subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy via percutaneous suspension technique for AMMs is safe and feasible, with manageable complications in selected patients. This approach improves visualization and the operative working space, reduces postoperative pain, and enhances postoperative recovery, although further clinical trials are warranted to confirm its potential benefits.
