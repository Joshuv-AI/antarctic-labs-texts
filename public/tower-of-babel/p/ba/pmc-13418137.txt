Predicting growth, water-use efficiency and drought response through machine learning, GWAS and differential expression in Ponderosa pine
Authors: Sean M Collins, Madison J Cathey, Mariola Barrera, Brooke Harris, Kailey Baesen, Anna Lincoln, Aalap Dixit, Amanda R De La Torre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the molecular basis of phenotypic trait variation is key in improving field performance in plants. Many plants have high within seed source phenotypic variation, making trait-based inferences for performance difficult and inaccurate. Our study combined machine learning methods along with genomics and transcriptomics to understand the molecular drivers of important seedling traits in ponderosa pine. We measured height, specific leaf area, biomass related traits, d13C, d15N, percent carbon, percent nitrogen in well-watered and drought conditions using species’ range-wide seed sources. Seedlings from California’s seed sources were the fastest growing, while the ones from Montana and Wyoming were the slowest. Despite differences in growth, common responses to drought were seen across all regions. Needles per bundle was shown to be an extremely useful trait to screen for growth strategies of a seed source. We identified one to 36 unique genes (2-209 SNPs) per trait that provided accurate predictions for most traits (2-37% mean absolute percent error). We show that prediction accuracy is trait dependent, mostly higher for traits with high heritability and lower in traits sensitive to environmental change. Drought-stressed seed sources from contrasting elevations showed differential expression of phenylpropanoids, terpenoids and carotenoids genes. Our predictive models show promise for future studies to predict phenotypes upon germination instead of waiting several years to measure specific traits. This will allow for a faster, more accurate selection of best suited individuals and seed sources for any site, resulting in more efficient and successful outplanting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09113-5.Keywords: Drought, Machine Learning, Differential expression, Genomic Prediction, Conifers, GWAS, Ponderosa pine, Pleiotropy

Background

Seedling phenotypes measured in greenhouse settings are strong indicators of long-term survival in the field but can be time intensive and provide coarse inferences on seed source performance. Structural traits measured in optimal conditions of container grown seedlings can provide inferences on field relative growth rate, ability to respond to future stressors in the wild and where specific seedlings may be best suited to be successful [1–4]. For example, specific leaf area provides inferences on relative growth rate, leaf lifespan and ability to respond to drought stress [5–8]. Height and biomass allocation correspond to relative growth rate, competitive potential, water use and probability of successful establishment [4, 9, 10]. Stable isotopes and osmotic potential can complement structural traits helping to provide more finite inferences on water-use efficiency, secondary metabolites, drought response and photosynthetic rate which can further aid seedling quality assessments [11–14]. Relying on phenotypes alone to make inferences on seed source performance hasn’t fully addressed regeneration or seedling quality related challenges [1, 10, 15]. Even poorly performing seed sources can still have successful individuals and high-quality seed sources can have individuals that perform poorly or die immediately after planting due to the high level of phenotypic variation in many wild seed sources [15, 16]. Furthermore, mass failures in regeneration, and mortality events, are still seen in many parts of the world; suggesting a need for improvements in seedling quality assessments [17–20]. Measuring phenotypic traits is extremely time and labor consuming, due to the time required for seedling development and for physiological analyses. Therefore, being able to identify high quality seedlings for an array of site conditions at germination could greatly improve regeneration efficiency and success.

Determining the best tools for finding the molecular drivers of trait variation is the key to identifying high-quality seedlings at germination and improving regeneration. Determining whether phenotypes are influenced by genetic variation, changes in gene expression and/or plasticity can provide avenues for predicting phenotypes as soon as cotyledon needles are produced. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) can identify candidate genes that have statistical associations with certain phenotypic profiles, which has aided many plant improvement programs [21, 22]. These associations help to inform marker-assisted selection; where specific seed stock is chosen based on parents that have desirable genotype-trait associations, but exact phenotype values aren’t predicted beforehand. These associations have led to gene-editing where candidate genes for pleiotropic traits were cut and pasted to create consistent desired phenotypes [23–25]. Genomic prediction is focused on predicting phenotypes and less concerned with identifying or understanding specific causal genes. Genomic prediction has been used in plants where marker-assisted selection and gene-editing was not feasible [26–29]. Both GWAS and genomic prediction have aided plant programs, however they still have major critiques on their utility and worth [30, 31]. Furthermore, the minimal genomic resources many non-model organisms have makes gene-editing an unrealistic solution for many species. Therefore, better understanding how to leverage models that can identify candidate genes and accurately predict phenotypes simultaneously can help quantitative genomics overcome many of the critiques hindering GWAS and genomic prediction. Machine and deep learning can filter out unimportant variables to create accurate predictions and can overcome complex issues such as linkage disequilibrium and population structure using extremely flexible architectures, non-parametric approaches and/or regularization techniques [32, 33]. However, many of these models rely on proprietary importance metrics making it unclear whether the variables used are of biological importance or are just good at predicting a target. Applying these models is in its infancy in quantitative genomics, however they have shown extreme promise for overcoming many of the issues shown in plant GWAS and genomic prediction studies based on studies in other species [34, 35]. Leveraging these models can greatly aid in regeneration and restoration efforts to be more successful as high-quality seedlings for any site can be identified upon germination with known phenotypic characteristics that allow managers to know what to expect when it comes to stand establishment and development.

Ponderosa pine (Pinus ponderosa) has experienced regeneration failures and mortality events across its range and has limited studies on phenotypes of young seedlings, all of which indicate a dire need for improving seedling quality assessment tools. Ponderosa pine is a conifer that is distributed across North America, diploid, wind-pollinated, can live for hundreds of years and starts producing cones at seven to twenty years old, depending upon location. Many provenance trials have been conducted in adult or juvenile ponderosa pine, which show high within seed source variation and unique phenotypes across elevational bands and geographic regions [36–40]. However, very few studies have been conducted on young seedlings, growth strategies at this life stage are extremely important for long-term field survival and ultimately successful stand establishment [16]. This lack of understanding and knowledge gaps across the reforestation pipeline could be contributing to the mass regeneration failures seen in many portions of the range [20, 41, 42]. Mature ponderosa pine is experiencing similarly large mortality events due to a combination of climate induced abiotic and biotic stressors [41, 42]. Like most conifers, ponderosa pine is much slower growing than broad leaf trees, which greatly delays the timing of seedling quality assessments and the ability to measure multiple traits due to limited tissue availability. Adding to these challenges are sparse genomic resources in the species [43, 44], with no reference genome and only a draft transcriptome (Gilewski and Mattsson, unpublished data). A huge genome (24 Gb) further complicates whole genome sequencing of large amounts of seedlings, and no candidate loci have identified smaller genomic regions for targeted sequencing. The most recent genomic study shows four distinct genetic clusters; (1) a California cluster; (2) a northern coastal cluster; (3) an interior cluster; and a (4) northern interior cluster (Figure S1) [44]. To fill these knowledge gaps, we explored the following questions (1) Can machine learning algorithms identify biologically relevant loci that can accurately predict phenotypes important for seedling field performance in ponderosa pine?; (2) Are some of these structural, physiological and isotopic traits easier to predict by machine learning methods, and is this correlated to the relative contribution of genetic variation vs. environment in shaping those phenotypes?

Methods

Sample collection and common garden design

Seeds were gathered from 24 seed sources across the natural distribution of ponderosa pine (Table 1). All seed sources used in our study were named by their original collector(s) or collecting agency, and as such we present each seed source name in full in Table 1. The geographic range was divided into four regions based on our previous population structure analysis (Southwest, Great Plains, California and Pacific/Interior Northwest), with six seed sources per region (Table 1, Figure S1) [44]. Seeds were obtained from collections at Northern Arizona University (NAU), United States Forest Service (USFS) Rocky Mountain Research Station, USFS Placerville Seed Bank, and the USFS National Seed Laboratory. All seed was collected by each respective group on land they managed or had permission to collect from. A total of 50–60 seeds per seed source were sowed in November 2022 in the NAU Greenhouse. All seed was unfrozen, sowed and watered in the same day. Germination was checked weekly following sowing for 6 weeks. Circular 20 cm long by 2 cm wide pots were used for all seedlings for the duration of the experiment. Soil in all pots consisted of 1:1:1 ratio sphagnum peat: horticultural perlite: coarse vermiculite. Seed source germination rates at 6 weeks post sowing ranged from 10% to 90%. Ultimately, 404 seedlings (5–32 individuals per seed source) were selected randomly for use in this common garden study. The common garden study was conducted over the course of November 2022 – November 2023. Germinated seedlings were watered to field capacity 3 times a week, except during the drought portion of the study. Seedlings were fertilized using Jack's professional water soluble mix 20-20-20 (Allentown, PA, USA) at a rate of 30ppm. Seedlings experienced an average temperature of 23.88℃ with a peak daytime temperature of 28.33℃ and a low night time temperature of 15.55℃. Natural day length was used until Fall 2023 where supplemental lighting was used to avoid seedling senescence during the drought treatment. Supplemental lighting created a 16 hr day and 8hr night cycle.

Phenotypic data collection

A spectrum of structural, physiological and isotopic traits, all of which are strong indicators of field performance even when measured in controlled greenhouse settings, were measured in well-watered (90–95% field capacity) and drought conditions (60–65% field capacity). Field capacity weights were measured at the seed source level. The drought treatment started in October 2023 and finished in November 2023. Following a well-watered reference measurement, the drought treatment consisted of pots being left without water until they reached 60–65% field capacity, where tissue for all traits was then collected. The well-watered reference weight measured was determined by watering pots until saturation point (based on consistent water running out of the bottom of the pot). Pots were then allowed to drain for 15 mins and seed source weight measurements were then conducted. Pots were weighed every day following the withholding of water. Once seed sources reached the 60–65% field capacity, all seedlings within each seed source were destructively sampled. Destructive sampling consisted of removing seedlings from pots, washing away all medium and then freezing samples at −20℃. The number of individuals used for each trait varied due to tissue availability, number of individuals per trait is shown in Table 1.

Structural traits measured included height to apical bud (cm), specific leaf area (SLA) (cm2/g), needles per bundle category (only 2 needle bundles, a mix of 2 needle and 3 needle bundles, only 3 needle bundles), shoot length, shoot mass, root length, root mass and shoot/root length and shoot/root mass ratios. Height to apical bud was measured monthly starting 6 months post germination to the end of the study. SLA was measured on the first true needles (i.e. needles from fascicled bundles; not cotyledon needles) produced, which ranged from June 2023 to September 2023. For SLA, one fascicle was used and each individual needle from a fascicle was photographed using a Samsung Galaxy S21 Camera at 10 cm above the needle. Needles were flattened using a Fisher Scientific microscope slide (Catalog # 12550D, Waltham, MA, USA) for each photograph. ImageJ (v1.54) [45] was then used to calculate leaf area for all photographs. Following leaf area calculation needles were dried at 70℃ for 72 hours and then weighed using a Mettler AE 2400 scale. Leaf area and leaf weight was then totaled for each fascicle; SLA for the fascicle was then calculated by dividing total leaf area by total dry leaf weight. Needles per bundle was checked once a month as seedlings produced true needles from June 2023 to the end of the experiment with a final check prior to the drought treatment occurring in October 2023. Following destructive sampling, shoot length was measured from the start of the root collar to the apical bud. Root length was then measured from the start of the root collar to the end of the tap root. Shoot to root length ratio was calculated by dividing shoot length by root length. Following shoot and root length measurements, roots and shoots were dried at 60℃ for 24 hours and dry biomass was measured using a Mettler AE 2400 scale. Shoot to root biomass ratio was calculated by dividing shoot mass by root mass. Specific shoot length (SSL) was calculated by dividing shoot length by shoot mass. Specific root length (SRL) was calculated by dividing root length by root mass.

Isotopic traits measured included carbon isotope discrimination (d13C), nitrogen isotope discrimination (d15N), percent carbon, percent nitrogen and carbon nitrogen ratio. True needles from as high as possible on the stem were collected for well-watered and drought isotope measurements. One fascicle was used for well-watered and drought measurements respectively. Following collection, needles were oven dried at 65℃ for 72 hours. Dried samples were then ground to a fine powder using a SPEX Tissue MiniG 1600 Prep Machine. Following tissue grinding, three mg of ground tissue was then subsampled and analyzed at the Colorado Plateau Stable Isotope Laboratory at NAU using a DELTA V Advantage isotope ratio mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), configured through a Finnigan ConFlo III for automated continuous-flow analysis of d13C using a Carlo-Erba NC2100 elemental analyzer for combustion and separation of carbon and nitrogen.

Osmotic potential at full turgor (MPa) was measured in well-watered and drought conditions using a vapor pressure osmometer (VAPRO 5520, Wescor, Logan UT, USA). For both well-watered and drought measurements, true needles were sampled and then cut again under water. Cut needles stayed submerged underwater for 24 hours in the dark to get needles to full turgor. Following the 24 hours, needles were pat dry to get rid of excess water and frozen at −80℃. Needles were then put into a leaf press (LP-27, Wescor, Logan UT, USA). Using the leaf press, liquid was squeezed out of the needles through a stainless-steel filter and onto a paper disc. Osmolality was then measured by placing the paper disc in the osmometer chamber. Osmotic potential was then determined using the Van’t Hoff’s equation by converting osmolality to osmotic potential with a universal gas constant value of 8.3144598E−0.6 m3 MPa K−1 mol−1 at 298.15 K temperature (OSM100 = osmolality * - 0.002479).

Phenotypic data analysis

Each individual trait was tested for normality and homoscedasticity with respect to seed source using Shapiro-Wilk test for normality and Levene’s test of homoscedasticity. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test for differences in trait values among regions for traits that met parametric assumptions. To further examine which seed sources were driving region differences, pairwise t-tests were then used to examine which seed sources differed from one another. For traits that did not meet parametric assumptions, permuted one-way ANOVAs with 10,000 permutations were conducted to test for differences among regions. Similarly, to further examine which seed sources were driving region differences, pairwise Wilcox-tests were then used to determine which seed sources differed among one another. For needles per bundle, an ordinal trait, a chi-square test of independence was used to determine if needle per bundle abundance varied across regions. Box and whisker plots were used to visualize all continuous traits. Histograms were used to visualize frequency of needle per bundle category across regions. For traits that had well-watered and drought measurements, paired Wilcox-tests were used to examine significant effects of drought on trait values. These analyses were conducted in R using ggplot2 [46], stats [47], car [48] and RVadiormore [49] packages.

DNA extraction and DNA sequencing

The same sequencing samples from [44] dataset two were used in this study. 60-100mg of needle tissue was homogenized using a SPEX Tissue MiniG 1600 Prep Machine. DNA was extracted from homogenized foliar tissue using Nucleospin Plant DNA extraction kits (MACHEREY-NAGEL, Dueren Germany), and quantified with a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA). Samples were sequenced using 55,000 capture seq probes (40,000 coding region probes, 15,000 intergenic/non-coding region probes). Coding-region probes were designed using a ponderosa pine transcriptome (Gilewski and Mattsson, unpublished data), and intergenic-region probes were designed using the loblolly pine reference genome (v2.0)

(https://treegenesdb.org/FTP/Genomes/PIta/v2.0/genome/). An Illumina platform 150x150bp read length was used for sequencing. Probe design, library prep and sequencing were all conducted at Rapid Genomics (LGC Biosearch Technologies, Gainesville, FL).

DNA quality assessment (QA), quality control (QC) and SNP calling

Initial QA/QC consisted of trimming adapters and removing reads with a phred score lower than 30. QA/QC was done using fastp (v0.23.2) [50], FASTQC [51] and MultiQC [52]. The loblolly pine reference genome v 2.0 was used for mapping and SNP calling. Mapping was done with bwa-mem (v0.7.10) [53] with default settings. Sequences were then sorted by coordinates and had duplicates removed with samtools (v1.5) [54]. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were called with bcftools [55]. A total of 284,913,192 SNPs were initially called. SNPs then were filtered, using vcftools [56] and bcftools [55]. Filtering criteria were quality score greater than 20, call rate greater than 0.75, minor allele frequency greater than 0.05. A total of 649,507 SNPs were left for use in the genome wide association study.

RNA tissue collection

Needle tissue was collected in well-watered and drought conditions during the drought experiment in October-November 2023. Experimental design included 12 biological replicates per seed source (Upper Enterprise Reservation and Green’s Peak) per treatment (well-watered and drought). Needle tissue was cut and immediately flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at −80℃. A total of 60-100mg of RNA tissue was hand ground using mortar and pestles. RNA was then extracted using ThermoFisher Plant RNA reagent (FisherScientific Hampton, NH, USA) and a modified CTAB RNA protocol. RNA quality control was followed by mRNA library preparation (poly A enrichment), and Illumina’s NovaSeq X Plus Series (PE150) sequencing, all of which was conducted at Novogene, CA.

RNA sequence analysis and differential expression

Quality control of raw reads was performed by trimming adapters and filtering out reads with a phred score lower than 30 using fastp [50]. To filter out non plant RNA, filtered reads were taxonomically assigned using Kraken2 [57], using microbial and human references. Any reads not matching the reference were assumed to be plant RNA reads and were retained for further analysis. Four to seven biological replicates per seed source (Upper Enterprise Reservation and Green’s Peak) per treatment (well-watered and drought) passed all filtering criteria. Individual transcriptomes were assembled using each sample, and de novo transcriptome assembly was done with Trinity [58–61] using default parameters. Coding regions in all the transcriptomes were then identified using Transdecoder [62] and hmmer [63]. All individual transcriptomes were then merged using bash. Vsearch [64] was then used to cluster all transcripts in the composite transcriptome and then identify and remove redundant transcripts. Consensus transcripts were created to make a final transcriptome. Vsearch started with 750,254 transcripts and finished with 66,962 transcripts in the final transcriptome. Final transcriptome assembly quality was examined using gVolante with the Viridiplantae reference. Stats for our transcriptome assembly were as follows, 92.57% complete core transcripts were found, 5.88 % partial core transcripts were found, and 1.65% core transcripts were missing. An average of 1.32 orthologs per core gene were found with 25.70% of core genes having more than one ortholog. Results suggest a robust quality transcriptome with minimal redundancy. The composite transcriptome was then indexed using Kallisto [65]. Kallisto [65] was also used to quantify gene expression for each sample, using the composite transcriptome as the reference. Raw counts were then rounded to the nearest whole number and then used in differential expression analysis. Differential expressed transcripts were then examined using Deseq2 [66] with each possible contrast of seed source (Upper Enterprise Reservation and Green’s Peak) and treatment (well-watered and drought). Significantly differentially expressed transcripts for each contrast were visualized using a non-metric multidimensional scaling ordination method (nMDS) to understand similarity between seed sources and treatments using ae4 [67, 68] and vegan [69]. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed on gene expression data to identify modules associated with isotopic traits using PYWGCNA [70]. All samples were analyzed together as we felt the increase in transcript and phenotype variation would aid the model in finding significant relationships. PYWGCNA [70] examines the correlation between gene modules and categorical traits and as such samples were put into quartiles for each isotopic trait to run this analysis. Networks of transcripts for significant modules were created using functions found with PYWGCNA to better understand how specific transcripts interact with one another. Only isotopic traits were used in the weighted co-expression analysis. Functions of differentially expressed transcripts were examined using NCBIs blastx specific to the Arabiposis thaliana transcript references. Further information was examined using the uniprot id mapping tool (https://www.uniprot.org/id-mapping).

Over-represented analysis was then performed on all sets of differentially expressed transcripts. Panther [71, 72] was used to determine over-represented gene ontology categories of the Arabidopsis thaliana orthologs for all differentially expressed transcript sets based using the PANTHER Overrepresentation Test [72], Fisher’s exact test and a false discovery rate of < 0.05 as threshold. Arabidopsis thaliana orthologs in the de novo assembled transcriptome were used as the reference. Following annotation and over-representation testing transcripts of diterpenoid biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, hydrogen peroxide catabolic process and methylation were chosen for further examination based on previous literature showing their importance in drought responses in plants [73–76].

qPCR and differential expression validation

To confirm the results of Deseq2 [66], individuals from the same seed sources were water-stressed using the same field saturation measurement methods. The main difference was seedlings were housed outside in 40 cm long by 4 cm wide pots during the experiment and thus subject to much more arid conditions. Five transcripts that were differentially expressed in well-watered and drought settings were chosen at random for use in qPCR. Primers were designed by Eurofins Genomics (Louisville, KY USA). qPCR reaction volumes were 10µL. qPCR was conducted using SYBR reagents, the mixture consisted of 0.2 µM forward and reverse primers and 2µL RNA extract. qPCR protocol was as follows: one cycle of cDNA Synthesis – 50 °C for 15 min, one cycle of Thermo-Start Activation at 95 °C for 15 min, then forty cycles of Denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, Annealing at 57–63 °C for 30 s, Extension at 72 °C for 30, then followed by a Melt Curve with an initial Denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s then 80 cycles of increasing 0.5°C per cycle starting at 60 °C for 30 s Melting Step. qPCR was run on a Thermo Fisher QuantStudio 5 384well RT-qPCR. Amplification was examined using Thermo Fisher Design & Analysis 2 (DA2) software.

Identification of candidate loci

GWAS were conducted on all traits in well-watered and drought conditions. Eight-month height was randomly selected to represent all height measurements in GWAS testing. Missing SNPs were imputed using the most commonly occurring allele for each respective locus using a custom R script. Models tested for GWAS included, GEMMA’s linear mixed model (MLM) [77, 78], Blink with BIC as selection criterion [79] and XGBoost [80]. GEMMA and Blink both rely on multiple testing of linear models and thus the Bonferroni test for multiple correction was used to determine candidate loci. GEMMA is a univariate model architecture that tests one SNP with a specific trait, repeating until all SNPs are tested. Blink uses a similar approach to GEMMA but also uses BIC and linkage disequilibrium to include or remove SNPs from the model rather than p-value to create multivariate models. XGBoost is a tree-based machine learning algorithm that has variable- importance metrics that it uses to filter out variables for each target. GEMMA and Blink were conducted on the entire dataset for each trait and candidate loci were identified by any SNP lower than the Bonferroni multiple corrected p-value of 0.000000077.

For XGBoost, a random training and validation dataset was created using a random 75%/25% split for each phenotype using default XGBoost settings, this was initially to assess which SNPs have any positive predictive impact. Root mean squared error (RMSE) was used as the loss function for continuous traits, while classification accuracy was used for needles per bundle. All SNPs that had positive prediction impact were then selected to a separate dataset. To robustly test the influence of the subset of SNPs, all samples were combined and 10-fold cross validation was used with just this subset of SNPs as predictors using a combination of custom python scripts and functions found within sci-kit learn [81]. Folds were randomly created and RMSE and classification accuracy were again used as loss functions. During cross validation, all available hyper parameters in XGBoost were optimized to increase predictive accuracy as much as possible. The influence of the SNPs used in the XGBoost models was inferred based on the error metric and how well natural variation could be mirrored across all validation folds using boxplots as visualizations between predicted and actual values as well as Pearson correlation coefficients. XGBoost model training and implementation was conducted using the XGBoost python package. Visualizations were conducted using ggplot2 [46], correlations were conducted in python. Mean absolute percent error (MAPE) was also calculated to provide another error metric using custom python scripts.

Height was the only trait measured repeatedly in well-watered settings and to further test our XGBoost method of identifying candidate loci; the same SNPs identified in the 8 month height measurement were tested using 10-fold cross validation on the six month, nine months and 11 month measurements using the same hyper parameters. RMSE was again used as the loss function and cross validation folds were randomly created and assigned.

As a further test for our feature selection method and model parameters determined using 10-fold CV, we implemented a leave one group out cross validation (LOGOCV) using region as the group. This would be analogous to a 4-fold CV but the model is trained on three different regions and has a new region left out to try and predict. The parameters found in the 10-fold CV were used with SNPs identified in the 75/25 split. The same loss functions were used, and the same additional metrics were calculated for all traits.

Candidate loci annotation

To annotate candidate loci from the GWAS, candidate SNPs were mapped back to the Pinus taeda annotation (v2) using snpeff [82] to find coding sequences as well as intergenic regions where candidate loci occurred. While the statistical associations identified from linear models like GEMMA or Blink can provide biological relevant SNPs, the biological relevance of SNPs identified from XGBoost is unclear. To explore whether a predictive model such as XGBoost can identify variables worthy of downstream biological inference, we only annotated all SNPs identified from XGBoost. Variants identified from XGBoost that would cause effects on upstream and downstream coding sequences were also identified and included in the annotation. Entire transcripts from the differential expression were used for downstream functional annotation. Candidate loci from the GWAS and differential expression were then functionally annotated by identifying orthologs using databases found in NCBI’s blast and eggnog [83]. Uniprot’s ID mapping tool was also used to gain more information on annotations when possible (https://www.uniprot.org/id-mapping).

Results

Structural phenotypes show regional differences that mirror variety structure

Significant differences among the four regions (Tables 1 and 2) were found for every structural trait measured using ANOVA or permuted ANOVA and supported by pairwise tests at the seed source level (Fig. 1, Table 2, Table S1). California seed sources were the fastest growing, while the Montana/Wyoming seed sources were the most conservative growing (Fig. 1). California seed sources had the highest specific leaf area (SLA), with a mean of 71.30 cm2/g and a standard error of 1.73 cm2/g, whereas Montana/Wyoming seed sources had the lowest SLA, with a mean of 50.06 cm2/g and a standard error of 1.17 cm2/g (Fig. 1, Table 2, Table S1). Idaho/Washington seed sources had the largest within region variation in both shoot biomass (0.1085 g to 1.0375 g) and root biomass (0.31972 g to 1.32080 g) (Fig. 1). Needles per bundle showed that individuals with a mix of two or three needle bundles or only two needle bundles were more common in the Arizona/New Mexico and Montana/Wyoming seed sources. Interestingly, regions that had individuals with only two needle bundles also had the lower SLA or were some of the shortest and lightest (Fig. 1). Needles per bundle was also positively correlated with height and negatively correlated with specific root length (SRL), specific shoot length (SSL) and d15N (Figure S2).

Prediction quality is trait dependent

Twenty-two unique traits, including structural traits and pre-post drought traits, were tested for genome-wide associations (Table 3), and twenty-five traits for genomic prediction (Table 4). XGBoost identified SNPs with positive predictive impact for all traits in this study. The number of predictive SNPs varied from two (i.e., drought osmotic potential and root length), to up to 209 SNPs for height at eight months (Table 3). XGBoost prediction error ranged from ca. 2–37% across traits (Table 4). Low RMSE and MAPE were not indicative of a higher correlation between predicted and actual values (Table 4). For better performing predictions, predictive values mirrored observed values extremely closely (Fig. 3a, b). While poor predictions consistently over or underestimated the actual value and struggled with accurately predicting the upper most values (Fig. 3c, d). Prediction of height at eight months height showed similar performance at six months, nine months and eleven months in 10-fold cross validation (Table 4). For LOGOCV, predictive performance was generally poorer ranging from 0.21–16% higher MAPE (Table 4). The Blink multivariate-MLM was the second-best performing algorithm to identify significant SNPs, ranging from one to seven SNPs per trait, for all traits except for root biomass, well-watered d13C, well-watered osmotic potential and drought osmotic potential (Table 3). Correlation values for B link models chosen via BIC were comparable with XGBoost models (Table S3). GEMMA’s MLM was only able to identify significant SNPs for a small number of phenotypes with a variance explained ranging from ca. 1–2% per trait. Two of those SNPs (scaffold27215_67783_98355 and C4791179_924_600731 associated with well-watered % C) were coincident between XGBoost and GEMMA’s MLM. Because XGBoost identified candidate loci in all traits and showed strong predictive performance using cross-validation, the loci identified from XGBoost were annotated to better understand the functions of predictive loci. Candidate SNPs were predominantly identified in intergenic regions (Table 5). Snpeff showed that most candidate SNPs identified for each trait had a modifier effect on protein structure (Table 5). Ts/TV ratios varied from 1 to >20. Unique proteins produced from candidate loci ranged from one to 36 for each trait with one to 50 total orthologous genes identified per trait (Table 5, Table S4). Genes producing Transcription factors such as 8 Myb, 5 Elongation factors, 1 NAM, 3 Cytochrome P450, 2 HLH and 3 WRKY were identified by XGBoost. Multiple genes were associated with more than one trait. Five cysteine repeat genes on scaffold 4375 were all associated with root biomass, shoot to root length ratio and SRL (Table S4). Four WD40 protein producing genes within four kb of one another on scaffold 53300 were associated with shoot to root length ratio and specific leaf area (Table S4). Twelve genes producing Pentatricopeptide repeat-containing proteins (PPR) were associated with height at eight months, root biomass, shoot to root length and biomass ratios, well-watered d13C and well-watered osmotic potential (Table S4).

Differential expression shows unique drought response strategies

The two seed sources used in the differential expression experiment, Upper Enterprise Reservation (UER) and Green’s Peak, were chosen based on their close geographic proximity but very different elevations (1524m and 2743.2m) (Table 1, Fig. 5). Differences in gene expression were found between treatments (well-watered vs. drought) and among seed sources (low elevation vs high elevation) (Figs. 5 and 6). In the low elevation seed source (Upper Enterprise Reservation, (UER)) in well-watered vs drought, 321 transcripts were differentially expressed, 253 (79%) of those were upregulated and 68 (21%) were downregulated (Table S5). The comparison of the high elevation seed source (Green's Peak) in well-watered vs drought identified 434 differentially expressed (DE) transcripts. Amongst those, 261 (60%) were upregulated and 173 (40%) were downregulated (Table S6). The comparison between the low elevation seed source vs the high elevation seed source in well-watered conditions identified 1362 DE transcripts (Table S7); while the comparison in drought conditions found 1144 DE transcripts (Table S8). The high elevation seed source had 1808 unique gene ontology (GO) terms from which 31 were over-represented (Table S9). The low elevation seed source had 1445 (GO) unique terms, from which 56 were over-represented (Table S9). Only three over-represented GO terms were common across the two comparisons, terpenoid biosynthetic process (GO:0016114), isoprenoid biosynthetic process (GO:0008299), and terpenoid metabolic process (GO:0006721) (Table S9). While terpenoid biosynthesis/metabolic processes were over-represented in both seed sources, differential expression of diterpenoid biosynthetic transcripts was found between high and low elevation seed sources (Fig. 6a). Phenylpropanoid biosynthetic and metabolic processes were only over-represented in the low elevation seed source and there were stark differences in gene expression between both seed sources (Fig. 6b). Hydrogen peroxide catabolic process and methylation also showed differential expression between seed sources (Fig. 6c, d). RT-qPCR supported the DESeq2 results (Figure S3); upregulation or downregulation across all 5 transcripts were validated in both raw RNA counts and Cq produced by RT-qCPR.

WGCNA highlights important gene networks responding to water use efficiency

WGCNA was able to identify gene modules that were significantly correlated with different quartiles for all isotopic traits (Figure S4). d13C quartiles had up to five different modules correlated with the same quartile, and percent carbon/nitrogen had up to three different modules correlated with the same quartile. Coincident results were found between the differential expression and co-expression analyses, with many DE transcripts found across all 14 significant gene modules. These included 50% transcripts from the low elevation well-watered vs low elevation drought, 48% transcripts from the high elevation well-watered vs high elevation drought, 44.7% transcripts from the low elevation vs high elevation in well-watered comparison, and 45% in the low elevation vs high elevation drought comparison. Pentatricopeptide repeat-containing proteins were the most commonly occurring protein families found among different gene modules (Table S10, S11). Networks of these modules show a complicated regulatory network governing these processes as many transcripts interact with another (Figure S5).

Discussion

Identifying loci that can forecast growth, water-use efficiency and drought response

Our results indicate that predictive variables identified by XGBoost can be biologically relevant and can provide biological inference when explored. We were able to accurately predict faster growth, higher water-use efficiency, higher photosynthetic rate and higher osmolites in well-watered and drought conditions in our dataset. Therefore, our models show promise for expediting seedling quality assessments, allowing managers to forecast how different seedlings may perform at germination rather than waiting several years, hopefully leading to better seedling choice in future restoration efforts. The quality of our predictions suggests a high potential for informing seedling quality assessments, but future studies should explore the accuracy of our models when conducting seedling assessments by including new seed sources and testing the models in field restoration settings. Predictive genes were mostly involved in stress responses, growth, carbohydrate metabolism and transport supporting the concept that XGBoost can identify biologically relevant variables (Table S4). Several transcription factors including 8 Myb, 5 Elongation factors, 1 NAM, 3 Cytochrome P450, 2 HLH and 3 WRKY were found to be accurate predictors of growth and drought-related phenotypes in ponderosa pine. From them, eight Myb-DNA-binding transcription factors from five different scaffolds were associated with drought d13C, height at eight months and SRL (Table S4). While Myb-DNA-binding transcription factors roles in growth and abiotic stress response are well-documented in flowering plants [84, 85], our study is the first to document their role in ponderosa pine and provide foundational information on where these important genes reside in the ponderosa pine giga-genome.

We identified several pleiotropic genes in the predictive genes identified by XGBoost (Table S4). Pleiotropic genes included five Cysteine-rich repeat secretory genes within 5kb of one another on scaffold 4375 (Table S4). Cysteine-rich repeat secretory proteins are well known to mediate plant growth [86] and defense response [87, 88], in this study we show they are also important for growth in ponderosa pine. The four WD40 pleiotropic genes within four kb of one another on scaffold 53300 represent a single gene or cluster of genes producing the same protein. WD40 proteins are involved with plant cell wall formation and response to abiotic stress [89]. We show in this study that the same gene can manage two very different structural traits. We also identified coarser evidence for pleiotropic genes using gene functions alone, which could represent a single gene or clusters of genes. Pentatricopeptide repeat-containing proteins (PPR) were found to be associated with numerous traits and distributed across 12 scaffolds. PPR proteins are involved in mediating growth and response to stressors [90–92]. Future studies can further the understanding of each candidate gene as annotations and assemblies improve; but our results identify several important regions within the genome.

Varied prediction accuracy highlights conserved response

Our results suggest that prediction accuracy is trait dependent, generally higher in traits with high heritability and lower in traits with a large environmental component. The accuracy of isotopic and osmotic predictions meets expectations for traits with a large genetic component in governing similar responses to drought at the species level. Isotopic traits are very sensitive to year-to-year environmental changes [93] but have been shown to have a high heritability [94–96]. The lower percent error in both well-watered and drought conditions for isotopic traits meets the expectations for highly heritable traits [94–96] and supports that genomic prediction can be accurate for traits that are sensitive to different water levels. Structural traits such as height and specific leaf area had similarly accurate predictions to most isotopic traits, meeting expectations of high heritability in structural traits [97–100]. Biomass related traits consistently had the highest predictive error. Previous literature shows biomass is sensitive to the environment and also has a significant genetic component, but some studies show low heritability for biomass [101–104]. The poor prediction of biomass related traits shows trait dependent nature for genomic prediction of traits that have a strong environmental component.

Seed sources show plasticity in secondary metabolite expression in well-watered and drought conditions

Differences in gene expression and over-representation of functional categories revealed distinct strategies in response to drought between the high elevation and low elevation seed sources. The number of unique over-represented GO terms (Table S9) and unique expression of functional categories (Fig. 6) across seed sources strongly supports reliance on different functional pathways. The number of co-occurring transcripts between the differential expression and WGCNA further support the biological relevance of these transcripts and the importance of gene expression in managing photosynthetic rate and water-use efficiency. This is further supported by the differential expression (based on qPCR results) of a CCHC zinc finger across seed sources and treatments (Primer 2, Figure S3). This gene can modulate the Abscisic Acid (ABA) signaling pathway, further regulating stomatal closure and other stress response pathways [105]. Differences in gene expression of functional categories also suggest reliance on different mechanisms for handling oxidative damage and other drought related stress between seed sources. This is supported by the differential expression of a zinc metallopeptidase (Primer 4, Figure S3) involved in thylakoid formation and removal of D1 to protect photosystem II, which is a strategy seen in response to drought stress and light stress in other plants [106, 107]. Further supporting this, are the differences in expression of phenylpropanoids and diterpenoid biosynthesis, and the over-representation of carotenoids biosynthesis in the high elevation seed source (Table S9). Phenylpropanoids, terpenoids and carotenoids are all known antioxidants and are involved in various facets of plant drought response [108–111]. Our results suggest an either/or strategy for these three compounds in the two seed sources examined, that could be due to plasticity in response to drought. Green’s Peak (high elevation seed source) had upregulated diterpenoid biosynthesis in both well-watered and drought conditions; as well as relatively lower phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and no over-represented transcripts involved in carotenoid biosynthesis. Whereas UER (low elevation seed source) had relatively low diterpenoid biosynthesis but higher abundances of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis transcripts and carotenoid biosynthesis transcripts that were over-represented.

Trait Relationships and broader impacts of physiological findings

Our results indicate a tradeoff between growth rate and water-use efficiency, especially between California and Montana/Wyoming seed sources, which is coincident with previous studies in the species [36–40]. Despite different growth strategies, we see common responses to drought stress across regions in isotopic and osmotic traits. Certain traits also provide information on how seed sources balance conserving water and growing. The well-watered and drought d13C results suggest that while California and Idaho/Washington seed sources may invest more resources into growing taller or increasing mass, and less into water saving strategies, they still maintain a similar level of water used per gram of carbon produced as most other seed sources (Figs. 1 and 2). This may be a way to conserve some water while growing faster and having thinner leaves compared to other seed sources. Furthermore, the percent nitrogen results suggest most California seed sources may have lower photosynthetic capacity, but California seedlings were taller and had greater biomass than other seed sources, which increases water demand but also increases total photosynthetic rate, via increased leaf area. The Arizona/New Mexico seed sources having the most negative d13C, suggests they may have to use slightly more water per gram of carbon produced, but invest more energy into water saving strategies, i.e. slower growth, thicker leaves, more below ground growth.

Needles per bundle provided an easy, and unexpected, trait for measuring growth strategies of a seed source. Seed sources that had individuals with only two needle bundles showed the most conservative strategies in many other traits. Whereas seed sources that had only or predominantly three needle bundles, tended to have much more acquisitive strategies. With only nine candidate loci offering 80–87% classification accuracy, this trait can provide a coarse but cheap tool for marker-assisted selection for seedling quality assessment. Examining needles per bundle in seedlings by hand is also very easy and not time-consuming once true needles are produced. Sequencing the nine candidate loci using cotyledon needles post germination can still provide a faster estimation of the trait as no waiting is required. Conkle and Critchfield [112] and Haller [113] link needles per bundle to climate and growth conditions, which indirectly suggests a relationship between growth strategies and needles per bundle that is more governed by the environment. Our study shows that there is a strong genetic component of needles per bundle. To our knowledge our study is the first to present an explicit relationship between needles per bundle and growth strategies.

Comparison of methods for GWAS/Genomic prediction

Our study shows that machine learning algorithms can predict well and identify SNPs associated with phenotypes of differing sample sizes and water conditions helping to bridge the gap between genomic prediction and GWAS. As mentioned above, we identified relevant candidate loci, for many important traits in both well-watered and drought conditions using machine-learning models in a giga-genome plant species with minimal genomic resources.

Furthermore, our genomic predictions using just these relevant candidate loci performed quite well compared to other recent literature. Correlations between predicted and actual values are predominantly presented in forest tree genomic prediction studies to show model quality, as such we use correlations instead of error metrics to compare our study to previous literature. Predicted and actual correlations in our study ranged from ⍴ = 0.44–0.85 in the 10-fold cross validation, and 0.003–0.77 in the LOGOCV, which exceeds or overlaps with many recent studies in conifers; ⍴ = 0.2–0.9 [114], ⍴ = 0.2–0.5 [115], ⍴ = 0.25–0.75 [39] and ⍴ = 0.5–0.6. Studies mentioned above use generalizations of linear models or polynomial models, i.e. ridge regression, Bayesian ridge regression and kernel models. Our results suggest that tree-based regression/classification can be a robust tool for genomic prediction in conifers. With that stated, the lack of congruence in error metrics and correlations (Table 4) strongly suggests that future studies should avoid using correlations to show predictive performance.

Our results show that multivariate models tend to be the best performing for polygenic traits. The low number of significant SNPs identified from GEMMA’s mixed linear model highlights the difficulty in identifying SNP-phenotype associations in heavily structured species that have polygenic strategies. GEMMA tends to be extremely powerful at elucidating pathways for many pleiotropic traits; but our study shows that it may perform poorly for highly polygenic traits. Blink was able to identify SNPs for almost all traits but struggled on traits with lower sample sizes. For the traits that Blink successfully identified significant SNPs; this multivariate linear model was able to mirror natural variation similarly well to XGBoost. Proper implementation of machine learning models is difficult, especially considering that data leakage is difficult to overcome in certain cases. Creating holdout validation datasets, that have truly unique observations, can be extremely challenging when traits are conserved and thus making robust tests of predictive models very difficult to perform in one study. Furthermore, feature selection methods are even more challenging due to the stochastic nature of machine learning models. While models performed well in our dataset, follow up studies using new seed sources would be the best way to assess our predictive models as this would be a truly new dataset that would robustly test our models and feature selection methods. Furthermore, the varied performance of all models highlights the importance of testing different models in quantitative genomics studies and that model performance is highly dependent upon population structure, pleiotropy and polygenic effects.

Conclusions

Finding the optimal approach to identify causal loci and predict phenotypes is paramount for quantitative genomics to positively influence management. Our study uses machine learning to identify candidate loci that accurately predict phenotypes important for seedling field performance. The results of our study show promise for providing faster, more accurate seedling quality assessment tools to increase regeneration success, and a step forward for improving GWAS and genomic prediction which can be transferred to any plant species.
