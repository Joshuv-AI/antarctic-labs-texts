Imaging manifestations of a rare case: giant coronary artery aneurysm with thrombosis
Authors: Lianze Du, Yao Yu, Qinghe Han, Sarandeep Kaur Marwaha, Arif Albulushi, Gautam Sen, Alexandru Patrascu
Published: 2025

A 66-year-old female patient presented with chest tightness and lower limb oedema. Initial echocardiography revealed severe mitral/tricuspid regurgitation with an anomalous linear structure in the left atrium (Figure 1A, arrow), suggestive of cor triatriatum sinister (CTS). However, CTA identified a 9.8 cm × 9.5 cm mass (Figure 1B) communicating with the left coronary artery (Figure 1C, arrow; Supplementary material online, Videos S1 and S2), with a non-enhancing thrombus. Cardiac MRI(CMR) demonstrated slow-flowing blood within the mass (Figure 1D, Supplementary material online, Video S3), consistent with a giant coronary artery aneurysm (GCAA) rather than CTS. Subsequent surgical intervention confirmed the presence of the mass (Figure 1E). The patient underwent coronary artery aneurysm resection, coronary artery aneurysm thrombectomy, and valvuloplasty under cardiopulmonary bypass. Six months later, the patient remained asymptomatic with complete resolution of chest tightness and oedema. CTA demonstrated successful aneurysm resection, showing only a suture line shadow without evidence of residual mass, leakage, or recurrence (Figure 1F).

Imaging of a 66-year-old female patient with a giant coronary artery aneurysm and thrombosis. (A) Echocardiography raised suspicion for cor triatriatum sinister due to a linear structure in the left atrium. RA, right atrium; RV, right ventricle; LA, left atrium; LV, left ventricle. (B and C) Colour-encoded volume-rendered image and axial-rendered image from CTA showed a 9.8 cm × 9.5 cm mass communicating with the left coronary artery, with a non-enhancing thrombus. (D) Dynamic contrast-enhanced first-pass perfusion sequences on CMR indicate the presence of thrombus within the mass. (E) Intraoperative image clearly demonstrates the location, morphology, and colour of the mass. (F) A follow-up CTA six months postoperatively demonstrated successful aneurysm resection, showing only a suture line shadow without evidence of residual mass, leakage, or recurrence.

GCAA are extremely rare.1 In this case, the GCAA developed as a result of the patient's prolonged exposure to an overwhelmingly stressful environment, and the eddy currents within the mass contributed to the development of the thrombus. Surgery is the preferred treatment option for GCAA.2 Although coronary angiography remains the gold standard for diagnosis according to the guidelines of the American College of Cardiology,3 modern multimodal imaging methods are gradually gaining recognition. Coronary angiography is the gold standard for the diagnosis of GCAA. This exceptional case illustrates how advanced non-invasive imaging modalities can provide sufficient diagnostic certainty to guide definitive surgical management of complex coronary pathology without preliminary invasive angiography, while highlighting CMR's unique capability for comprehensive haemodynamic and tissue characterization that complements anatomical assessment by CTA and overcomes limitations of conventional echocardiography.
