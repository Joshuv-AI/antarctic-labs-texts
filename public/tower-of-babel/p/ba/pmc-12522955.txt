The stem cell within the vessel wall: multipotent pericytes modulating β-cell function and diabetic complications
Authors: Huanjing Bi, Ruiyang Ma, Zuhan Chen, Ying Wang, Xiaoming Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic islet pericytes are emerging as critical regulators of β-cell development, maturation, and function beyond their traditional role in vascular support. While the roles of signaling pathways and transcription factors in β-cell development have been extensively studied, the contribution of non-endocrine components, particularly pericytes, has gained recognition as a critical regulator of β-cell biology. Originally characterized as critical component of the vasculatures, pericytes were later recognized for their role in tissue development beyond angiogenesis. This review focuses on the multifaceted contributions of pericytes to islet biology, highlighting their regulatory effects on β-cell proliferation, differentiation, and functional maturation through paracrine signaling and extracellular matrix interactions. We outlined key signaling pathways modulated by pericytes to support β-cell maturation and function. Furthermore, the complex interactions between pericytes and non-endocrine intraislet cell populations are thoroughly analyzed to elucidate the integrated microenvironmental network essential for islet development and functional homeostasis.Keywords: Pancreatic islets, Pericytes, β-cell development, Diabetes, Cellular crosstalk

Introduction

The precise regulation of blood glucose homeostasis is primarily governed by pancreatic islets of Langerhans, specialized micro-organs embedded within the pancreatic parenchyma that harbor diverse endocrine cell populations, most notably the insulin-secreting β-cells [1]. Dysfunction or degeneration of these β-cells is a central pathological feature of diabetes mellitus [2]. Understanding the developmental processes that govern the formation and maturation of functional β-cells is therefore paramount for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies [3]. While the roles of various signaling pathways and transcription factors in directing pancreatic and endocrine cell fate have been extensively investigated, the contribution of non-endocrine cellular components within this niche has emerged as a crucial yet previously underappreciated determinant of β-cell biology and function.

The microvasculature forms an integral part of the islet microenvironment, providing not only structural support but also a platform for complex cellular interactions [4]. These microstructures exhibit exceptional vascular density, with each islet penetrated by a dense network of capillaries that facilitate glucose sensing and insulin delivery into the peripheral circulation [5, 6]. Although constituting only 1–2% of pancreatic mass, islets receive up to 20% of direct arterial blood flow to the pancreas [7]. The high level of vascularization ensures that nearly all β-cells maintain direct physical contact with capillaries, a structural prerequisite for their rapid endocrine responsiveness. The pancreatic capillary structure consists of a highly fenestrated layer of luminal endothelial cells (ECs) surrounded by pericytes, which function as outer luminal wall cells embedded within the microvascular basement membrane [8]. While numerous researches have elucidated the fundamental role of ECs in islet microcirculation, the specific role of pericytes is still under investigation. Nevertheless, accumulating evidence suggests that their influence extends beyond these classical vascular roles. Recent studies have demonstrated that pericytes exert critical regulatory effects on regulating β-cell growth and function [9, 10], which positions pericytes as multimodal regulators bridging vascular biology and endocrine physiology.

The present review synthesizes the current understanding of the multifaceted roles of pancreatic pericytes in regulating β-cell fate and function. We explore fundamental mesenchymal and stem-cell-like properties of pericytes, with special focus on their regulatory role in the differentiation and maturation of pancreatic β-cells. Additionally, we examine their interactions with other intraislet cells to establish an integrated microenvironmental network essential for islet development and functional homeostasis.

Phenotypic and molecular identity and multipotent capabilities of pericytes

Mesenchymal identity and phenotypic plasticity of pericytes

Pericytes originated from the mesenchyme that are intimately associated with the microvasculature through the deployment of elongated cytoplasmic processes that circumferentially envelope capillaries and postcapillary venules [11]. A hallmark anatomical feature that distinguishes pericytes from other vascular cell populations is their integration within a shared basement membrane architecture with adjacent ECs [12]. This structural arrangement contrasts markedly with the organization of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) in larger caliber vessels, wherein these cells remain physically separated from the endothelium by an intervening intimal layer.

Despite their well-defined anatomical location, the precise identification of pericytes has been substantially complicated by the absence of unique molecular marker that is universally expressed across all pericyte populations while remaining absent in other cell types. The molecular signature of pericytes demonstrates considerable heterogeneity and plasticity, exhibiting variations that correlate with tissue type, developmental stage, and pathological conditions [13]. The most extensively validated markers include Platelet-Derived Growth Factor Receptor β (PDGFRβ), a receptor tyrosine kinase that serves as a canonical pericyte identifier whose cognate ligand PDGF-BB is secreted by ECs and proves essential for pericyte recruitment, proliferation, and survival during vasculogenesis and angiogenesis [14]. Neuron-Glial Antigen 2 (NG2; CSPG4), a large transmembrane chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan, represents another frequently employed marker that demonstrates particular utility in identifying nascent, proliferating, or reactive pericyte populations [15]. Alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), a contractile apparatus component, exhibits heterogeneous expression patterns wherein arteriolar and select capillary pericytes demonstrate positive staining while many capillary pericytes remain α-SMA negative under quiescent conditions [16]. Nehls and Drenckhahn [17] proposed a binary control mechanism regulating α-SMA expression in capillary pericytes that pericytes expressing α-SMA could regulate capillary blood flow analogously to smooth muscle cells, whereas α-SMA negative pericytes are involved in stabilization of endothelial barrier. Additional markers employed in pericyte characterization include desmin, an intermediate filament protein reflecting contractile potential shared with VSMCs [18], Regulator of G-protein Signaling 5 (RGS5), which demonstrates specific upregulation during active angiogenesis [19], the cell adhesion molecule CD146 [20]. In addition to the well-known markers, Baek et al. performed single-cell transcriptomic study identified tissue-specific pericytes marker candidates, including Kcnk3 (in the lung); Rgs4 (in the heart); Myh11 and Kcna5 (in the kidney); Pcp4l1 (in the bladder); and Higd1b (in lung and heart) [21]. A more recent high-throughput single‐cell RNA expression profiling revealed that SLC6A12 and SLC19A1 are novel markers for human brain pericytes [22]. These signatures not only distinguish pericytes from ECs and smooth muscle cells but also provide insights into tissue-specific pericyte specialization and developmental programming. In the pancreas, the transcription factor Nkx3.2 was reported to be a developmental marker for mesenchymal progenitors that differentiate into pancreatic pericytes [23].

Unfortunately, the absence of a definitive molecular marker for pericytes complicates their precise identification (Table 1). This challenge is further compounded by the heterogeneous cellularity within the perivascular microenvironment, including microglia, fibroblasts, adventitial cells, macrophages, and VSMCs [38]. Consequently, researchers have established a multi-parameter approach requiring the integration of at least two distinct criteria: anatomical positioning relative to blood vessels, characteristic cellular morphology, and the documented absence of endothelial and glial cell-specific markers [39]. This methodological framework represents the current standard for reliable pericyte identification, acknowledging the inherent complexity of the perivascular cellular landscape and the necessity for comprehensive characterization studies to ensure accurate research outcomes and clinical applications.

Molecular markers for pancreatic pericytes

Stem cell-like properties and multipotency

Beyond their role as vascular mural cells, pericytes constitute a primary reservoir of adult mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs). Substantial evidence supports that pericytes represent the in vivo counterparts or direct precursors of MSCs isolated from vascularized tissues, explaining the ubiquitous distribution of MSCs via their microvascular origin [12, 40]. The apparent distinction between pericytes and MSCs reflects a cellular continuum, rather than discrete lineages, wherein pericytes represent the quiescent, niche-resident state, which transitions to a migratory, proliferative, and multipotent MSC phenotype upon injury-induced activation [41]. Such interrelationship between pericytes and MSCs is crucial role in wound healing by promoting early granulation tissue formation and stabilizing newly formed blood vessels [42]. This relationship is further substantiated by molecular evidence, as both pericytes and MSCs exhibit shared surface markers, including CD44, CD73, CD90, and CD105 [43, 44], reflecting the absence of exclusive markers for either population. Moreover, pericytes have been shown to express pluripotency-associated transcription factors in certain contexts [45, 46], suggesting that they might represent a continuum of cellular states from fully differentiated vascular support cells to multipotent progenitors capable of transdifferentiation.

Both in vitro and in vivo studies confirm the differentiation capacity of pericytes into multiple lineages. Under osteogenic conditions, pericytes upregulate osteogenic markers such as Runx2 and alkaline phosphatase and synthesize mineralized matrices, recapitulating osteoblastic phenotypes [43, 47]. Similarly, during adipogenic differentiation, accumulate intracellular lipid droplets and express adipogenic transcription factor PPARγ, confirming adipocyte lineage commitment [48]. In addition to mesenchymal differentiation, pericytic multipotency extends to myogenic and chondrogenic differentiations. Dellavalle et al. reported pericyte-myoblast fusion contributes to skeletal muscle regeneration, particularly relevant for muscle wasting disorders and traumatic injuries [49]. Characterized by Sox9 expression and cartilage-specific extracellular matrix synthesis, the chondrogenic differentiation of pericytes further highlights their differentiation versatility [50]. Furthermore, pericytes exhibit differentiation potential beyond mesenchymal lineages. Multiple experimental models document brain pericyte conversion into neuron-like cells, astrocytes, and oligodendrocyte progenitors. In cerebral ischemia models, brain vascular pericytes express neural stem cell markers (nestin, Sox2) and mature neuronal marker MAP2, contributing to neural network formation under permissive conditions [45, 51]. This cross-lineage plasticity indicates that pericyte multipotency transcends single germ layer boundaries, potentially encompassing ectodermal derivatives under specific microenvironmental stimuli. The pericytic transition from a quiescent state to an active regenerative phenotype reflects a dynamic response that is characteristic of stem cells, as these cells must both maintain a reservoir for future differentiation and respond rapidly to tissue damage. Additionally, pericytes drive regeneration through paracrine mechanisms essential for angiogenesis and inflammation [52]. Teng et al. Cultured pericytes, similar to MSCs, can modulate immune cell behavior and participate in leukocyte recruitment in inflamed tissues.

This integration of direct differentiation and paracrine signaling establishes pericytes as a potent endogenous repair system and a compelling therapeutic target for regenerative medicine.

Pericytes in pancreatic development and islet formation

Origin and recruitment of pancreatic pericyte

The heterogeneous developmental origin of pericytes was demonstrated among various organs [53]. Pericytes in the heart, lung, liver, and gut are derived from the mesothelium [54], while those in the central nervous system and thymus are developed from the neural crest [55]. During embryogenesis, pancreatic pericytes originate from the embryonic mesenchyme, sharing developmental pathways with VSMCs [23].

The recruitment occurs in a temporal manner during islet development, with early recruitment playing a role in guiding EC proliferation and migration, which is critical for proper islet vascularization and function. Interestingly, the temporal dynamics of pericyte recruitment appear to be tightly regulated, with earlier stages of islet development showing greater pericyte recruitment than later stages. Harari et al. found that pancreatic pericyte originate from the Nkx3.2-positive embryonic pancreatic mesenchyme, which surrounds the pancreatic epithelium and supports pancreatic development [23]. Specifically, their lineage-tracing results showed that the appearance of pancreatic mesenchyme-derived pericytes began at e13.5 and differentiated into a more mature pericyte phenotype in subsequent stages of pancreas organogenesis. Notably, previous studies have shown that disruption of pericyte recruitment during islet development disturbed islet vascularization and β-cell function, leading to impaired glucose homeostasis and diabetes [26, 56], which further highlighting the importance of studying the temporal dynamics of pericyte recruitment in islet development.

Paracrine signals driving β-cell growth and differentiation

The pancreas originates from budding of the embryonic endoderm at the foregut-midgut junction, and its development is a precisely regulated process that involves the concerted actions of various transcription factors and signaling molecules at specific temporal and spatial domains [57]. The dorsal pancreatic bud and two ventral pancreatic buds emerge during the initial stages of pancreas development at e8.5, coinciding with the appearance of vasculature adjacent to the endoderm. The initiation of pancreatic development is marked by the expression of the pancreas-specific transcription factor Pdx1, which is only observed in areas where the gut tube endoderm contacts the endothelium of the dorsal aorta or ventral vitelline veins [58]. Resection of the aorta was found to disrupt the development of the endocrine pancreas, indicating the instructional contribution of vascular paracrine signaling to the pancreatic organogenesis [58].

Pancreatic endocrine cells, including β-cells, arise from NGN3+ endocrine progenitor cells [59]. These progenitors undergo further differentiation, guided by specific combinations of transcription factors, to give rise to the hormone-producing cells of the islets. Transcriptional regulation mediated by pericyte-derived factors ensures β-cell identity (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 2). Sakhneny et al. identified Tcf7l2, a Wnt signaling effector, as a critical mediator of pericyte-β-cell communication [60]. Pericyte-conditioned media upregulated Tcf7l2 expression in β-cells, enhancing glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) and protecting against glucolipotoxicity. Similarly, Winkel et al. revealed that pericyte-derived trefoil factor 3 (TFF3), upregulated by gestational low-protein diets, accelerates β-cell maturation by promoting insulin granule biogenesis and glucose responsiveness [61]. These findings underscore the role of niche-derived Wnt and TFF3 signals in fine-tuning β-cell functional maturation.

Key pericyte-derived molecules and their roles in pericyte-β cell interaction

Notably, the embryonic pancreatic mesenchyme (the origin of pericytes) expresses growth factors such as Insulin-like Growth Factor 2 (IGF2) [62], Transforming Growth Factor-β (TGF-β) [63], and Connective Tissue Growth Factor (CTGF) [64, 65], all of which are associated with β-cell proliferation. Whether neonatal pericytes continue to secrete these specific factors to promote β-cell expansion remains to be further investigated. The study by Epshtein et al. also found that pericyte-dependent β-cell expansion requires the involvement of β1-integrin signaling [9], suggesting that ECM components produced by pericytes may play a crucial role in presenting or mediating the action of these growth factors. The neonatal period is a peak time for β-cell proliferation [66], and the secretion of soluble factors by pericytes during this stage directly stimulates β-cell proliferation, indicating that the rapid expansion of β-cells is not solely an intrinsic property but requires the active support of the islet microenvironment, particularly pericytes (Fig. 2). A deeper understanding of these specific growth factors and their mechanisms of action holds promise for developing diabetes treatment strategies that mimic the neonatal microenvironment to promote β-cell regeneration.

Pericytic regulation of β-cell maturation

The functional maturation of pancreatic β-cells is a tightly regulated process that extends into the postnatal period and is influenced by both intrinsic cellular programs and extrinsic cues from the islet microenvironment [72]. A growing body of experimental evidence from both in vitro and in vivo studies supports the crucial role of pericytes in β-cell maturation and function (Table 3). In vivo studies using genetic mouse models, where pericytes are either depleted or their function is altered, have consistently demonstrated significant effects on β-cell mass, proliferation, and function. Epshtein et al. reported that depletion of pericytes in neonatal mice leads to a reduced rate of β-cell proliferation [9]. Islet transplantation models have also been utilized to observe the role of pericytes in islet revascularization and β-cell function within a new microenvironment [73]. Similarly, pericyte ablation in adult mice results in impaired glucose-stimulated insulin secretion and β-cell dedifferentiation [31]. Studies in transgenic mouse models demonstrate that pericyte depletion induces β-cell dedifferentiation, marked by reduced expression of maturity-associated transcription factors MafA and Pdx1, alongside impaired GSIS [67, 74]. Recent evidence suggests that the dedifferentiation of β-cells is a critical factor in the progression of diabetes [75]. The dedifferentiation is triggered by misregulation of signaling pathways and/or metabolic stress, leading to the repression of canonical β-cell genes, resulting in nonfunctional β-cells [76].

Key in vivo and in vitro studies investigating intraislet pericyte-β-cell crosstalk in islet function

In vitro co-culture experiments between pericytes and β-cell lines or primary β-cells have shown that pericyte-derived factors can stimulate β-cell proliferation and influence their function [69]. Notably, studies using human islets and stem cell-derived islets are increasingly being employed to investigate pericyte-β-cell interactions, providing more direct insights into the relevance of these findings to human physiology and disease [77]. Although not reported from direct pericytic production, Karanth et al. identified two pericyte-derived growth factors, angiopoietin-1 and angiopoietin-2 (ANG-2), augmented the expression of β-cell transcription factors and increased the glucose responsiveness of stem cell-derived islet cells [78]. In addition, angiopoietin-1 is crucial for maintaining glucose homeostasis by stabilizing blood vessels within the islet, which is essential for proper insulin secretion and β-cell function [79]. The consistent findings across these diverse experimental systems, particularly the impact of pericyte perturbation on β-cell function, strongly support a direct and significant role for pericytes in β-cell development and maturation. The use of human islets and stem cell-derived islets is particularly important for translational relevance, as these models can help to bridge the gap between animal studies and human physiology.

Furthermore, the presence of pericytes and their interaction with the ECM components are essential for maintaining the structural and functional integrity of the islet, thereby supporting β-cell maturation and function (Figs. 1 and 2) [80]. Pancreatic pericytes synthesize laminin isoforms, such as Laminin-421 (composed of α4, β2, and γ1 chains), which directly enhance β-cell gene expression, including Ins1, MafA, and Glut2, while improving GSIS [69]. Furthermore, pericytes contribute collagen IV and hyaluronan to the islet basement membrane, which not only provides structural support but also modulates β-cell receptor signaling (e.g., integrins) to prevent dedifferentiation under stress conditions [69]. The pericyte-ECM axis thus operates as a biomechanical rheostat for β-cell functional maintenance.

Pathologically, the loss or dysfunction of pericytes has profound implications for β-cell maturation in diabetic contexts. diabetic islets exhibit pericyte hypertrophy, vascular basement membrane thickening, and reduced laminin-α4 deposition, all of which correlate with β-cell dysfunction and disrupted ECM-receptor crosstalk [31, 74, 81]. These pathological remodeling correlates with disrupted β-cell identity retention, suggesting that pericyte-targeted interventions may recalibrate islet matrix topology to rescue functional β-cell mass in diabetes.

Signaling pathways involved in pericyte-mediated islet development

Notch pathway

The Notch signaling pathway represents a critical regulatory mechanism governing endocrine differentiation during islet development. Notably, this pathway exhibits a dual role for the differentiation of pancreatic progenitors at the single-cell level, with high Notch activity maintaining quiescence while reduced signaling promotes progenitor expansion and subsequent differentiation into endocrine lineages [82, 83]. Pericytes fine-tune this balance through paracrine signaling. Specifically, pericyte-derived VEGF enhances endothelial-endoderm contact, where both dorsal and ventral Pdx1 domains evaginate [58]. The Notch pathway modulates VEGF signaling in differentiating cells, establishing a feedback mechanism that refines endocrine progenitor dynamics [84]. In addition, Pericyte-derived TGF-β indirectly modulate Notch activity by altering the expression of Notch ligands or receptors on target cells [85]. This regulation is nuanced, as TGF-β can antagonize Notch via SMAD-dependent pathways, balancing progenitor renewal and endocrine differentiation [86]. Such dual regulation ensures that endocrine progenitors differentiate synchronously with vascular maturation, a process critical for functional islet morphogenesis. Moreover, direct cell-cell communication occurs via pericyte-expressed Notch ligands, such as Jagged1 and Delta-like 4, which engage Notch receptors on adjacent cells [87]. Disruption of this interplay impairs islet vascularization and may lead to diabetic phenotypes [30].

BMP signaling

Activation of the bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4) signaling has been implicated in β-cell differentiation and maturation [77]. Sakhneny et al. demonstrated that mice with disrupted intraislet pericyte function were glucose intolerant with impaired GSIS and reduced expression of β-cell specific genes. Their research identified BMP4 as a critical pericyte-derived factor regulating β-cell function. Intraperitoneally administration of exogenous BMP4 to intraislet pericyte-deficient mice significantly ameliorated islet dysfunction and upregulated mature β-cell gene expression [60]. This work aligns with a recent finding that BMP4 is primarily produced by pericyte in the pancreas in an age-dependent manner during the late postnatal period, concurrent with β-cell maturation [77]. This temporal correlation suggests a potential regulatory involvement of BMP signaling in islet development. Further supporting this regulatory axis, Sui et al. reported that BMP4 signaling is required for the proliferation and proper expression of NKX6.1 of pancreatic progenitors [88]. Mechanistically, pericytes produce BMP4 in a Tcf7l2-dependent manner, and exogenous BMP4 administration rescues GSIS defects in Tcf7l2-deficient models, suggesting a WNT-BMP signaling axis as a central mediator of pericyte-endocrine crosstalk [89]. The translational relevance of this axis is reinforced by in vitro study showing that BMP4 enhanced stem cell-derived β-cell differentiation and maturation in the late stages [77]. Given that BMP4 has been safely tested in clinical settings [90], its potential utility to stimulate in situ β-cell regeneration represents a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention.

ERK signaling

In pancreatic islets, ERK1/2 activation plays dual developmental roles: it is indispensable for embryonic beta-cell precursor expansion and maturation [91], while postnatally supports beta-cell mass adaptation and insulin exocytosis in response to metabolic demands [92]. Several potential mechanisms could mediate pericyte regulation of ERK signaling in islet cells. Notably, pericyte-derived IL-33 exerts immunometabolic control by regulating islet immune cell populations and retinoic acid synthesis [35], a metabolite essential for endocrine differentiation and insulin secretion. Although direct evidence in β-cells remains limited, IL-33 has been shown to activate ERK signaling through the canonical MyD88/IRAK/TRAF6 cascade in other cell types [93], suggesting a potential mechanism for enhancing endocrine precursor proliferation. As discussed in the previous section, pericyte-regulated ROS is a crucial factor regulating endocrine commitment. Mechanistically, ROS-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation promotes β-cell differentiation, as demonstrated by Hoarau et al., where pharmacological ERK1/2 inhibition (U0126) markedly reduced NGN3+ endocrine progenitor populations in cultured rat pancreata [94]. Additionally, pericytes shape the islet niche through ECM remodeling and integrin activation [69, 95], creating a pro-ERK microenvironment that amplifies signaling in fetal islet cells. While established ERK activators like TGFβ and PDGF are secreted by pericytes in other tissues [96, 97], their direct role in pancreatic endocrine ERK regulation remains unconfirmed. This knowledge gap highlights the need to systematically characterize the ERK-modulating capacity of pericyte-derived cytokines within islet-specific contexts.

Hippo pathway

Pericyte plays a multifunctional role in regulating Hippo signaling during islet development, primarily through paracrine signaling, ECM remodeling, and microenvironmental mechanotransduction. A key mechanism involves pericyte-derived TGF-β, which synergizes with Hippo pathway inactivation to form YAP/Smad3 transcriptional complexes [98]. These complexes upregulate CTGF, a critical mediator of ECM remodeling that concurrently supports β-cell maturation [99]. Complementing this paracrine axis, pericytes produce laminin isoforms (e.g., Laminin-421) that bind β1-integrins on β-cells, triggering mechanotransduction pathways that suppress Hippo kinases LATS1/2, thereby stabilizing YAP/TAZ to promote β-cell differentiation and insulin gene expression [69, 100].

Notably, pericyte-immune crosstalk further modulates Hippo signaling. Through TLR/MyD88-dependent pathways, pericytes coordinate localized production of cytokines such as CXCL1 and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), recruiting macrophages and dendritic cells to the islet niche, potentiating β-cell function through NF-κB activation, a pathway known to crosstalk with YAP/TAZ to fine-tune β-cell proliferation and survival [71]. Additionally, pericytes regulate islet vascular tone via sympathetic innervation, controlling blood flow dynamics that influence mechanical stress on β-cells. These mechanical cues are transduced via integrins and cytoskeletal rearrangements to inhibit Hippo kinases, thereby activating YAP/TAZ-driven transcriptional programs essential for β-cell function [101].

Redox homeostasis

ROS levels critically determine endocrine cell fate decisions during islet development. Moderate ROS elevation (< 100 µM H2O2 equivalents) activates Nrf2/ARE pathways, enhancing insulin gene expression during the development of embryonic mouse pancreas [102]. In contrast, excessive ROS (> 100 µM H2O2 equivalents) triggers p38 MAPK-mediated apoptosis in immature endocrine cells [103]. The strategic anatomical positioning of pericytes adjacent to islet capillaries establishes these mural cells as potential key regulators of this redox balance. Mechanistically, pericyte-derived TGF-β activates NADPH oxidase 4 in neighboring cells, elevating intracellular ROS concentrations that stimulate β-cell regeneration through the differentiation of endocrine progenitors in the murine pancreas by promoting SOX9 and NGN3 expression [102]. Concurrently, pericyte-derived PDGF signaling encourages robust proliferation of endocrine progenitors. The diminished PDGF activity observed in adults corresponds with reduced proliferation and, under diabetic conditions, with pathogenic ROS levels [104]. Maintaining a delicate balance of ROS is paramount, as excessive levels can induce oxidative stress, impairing the function and survival of beta-cells. Notably, pericytes can also modulate the activity of immune cells within the islets through the secretion of cytokines such as IL-33 [35]. Since immune cells represent a known source of ROS that influences islet cell function [105], this presents another indirect pathway through which pericytes might regulate ROS signaling in endocrine cells.

Pericytic interaction with non-endocrine intraislet cells

Interaction with intraislet ECs

The structural basis of pericyte-endothelial cell interaction in islets lies in their anatomically integrated spatial association. Pericytes are embedded within the vascular basement membrane, establishing direct contact with the abluminal surface of ECs (Fig. 1) [106]. This intimate proximity facilitates a bidirectional exchange of signals and enables coordinated functional responses between the two cell types [107].

A key function of pericytes within the islet is the regulation of capillary diameter and blood flow, thereby influencing the delivery of nutrients and the dispersal of hormones [108]. Due to the large size of red blood cells relative to the capillary lumen, small reductions in capillary diameter can result in large decreases or stagnation in RBC velocity [109]. Notably, pericytes were discovered on approximately 30% of the intra-islet capillaries, only 50% were considered contractile [26]. This partial coverage suggests a level of fine-tuned regulation and the potential for localized responses within different regions of the islet microvasculature. The contraction and relaxation of pericytes is associated with signaling factors secreted by surrounding cells. ATP-derived adenosine, co-secreted with insulin, has been shown to relax pericytes, dilates islet capillaries, and increases local blood flow [26].

ECs themselves contribute to this regulatory landscape by releasing various vasoactive factors, which can influence pericyte activity [30, 74]. Stratman et al. elucidated a critical morphogenetic mechanism in vascular maturation, demonstrating that EC-generated vascular guidance tunnels act as specialized migratory scaffolds. These dynamic ECM-rich structures mediate pericyte recruitment by enabling abluminal pericyte motility along developing vessels, thereby facilitating pericyte-mediated vessel stabilization, including spatiotemporal assembly of the vascular basement membrane and establishment of physiological endothelial tube diameters [110]. This bidirectional signaling between ECs and pericytes ensures a coordinated vascular response to both local and systemic signals. During vasculogenesis, ECs recruit pericytes through the creation of vascular guidance tunnels within the extracellular matrix [111] and the secretion of platelet-derived growth factor-B [112]. This recruitment subsequently stimulates the assembly of the endothelial basement membrane matrix. Such cooperative interaction highlights the integrated roles of pericytes and ECs in the construction and maintenance of a functional vascular network within the islets.

Disruptions in the normal interaction between pericytes and ECs have significant implications for the proper function of β-cells. A hallmark of diabetic islet vasculopathy is the observed loss of pericyte coverage and function in diabetes [26, 113, 114]. This impairment can lead to dysregulation of blood flow, increased vascular permeability, and ultimately, compromised beta cell function. Supportively, pericytes can undergo a transition into myofibroblasts, contributing to islet fibrosis and further exacerbating vascular abnormalities in diabetic islets [70]. This transformation involves increased production of extracellular matrix components, further disrupting the delicate balance of the islet microenvironment. Moreover, diabetic islets exhibit impaired vascular responses to glucose and other vasoactive stimuli, indicating a breakdown in the normal regulatory mechanisms of islet blood flow [31]. Consequently, pericyte dysfunction is strongly associated with impaired insulin secretion, suggesting that the disruption of pericyte-endothelial interactions plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of the disease by affecting both β-cell function and survival.

Interaction with intraislet neural cells

The human pancreatic islets exhibit extensive innervation by sympathetic and parasympathetic nerve fibers, forming highly organized neuro-insular complexes (NICs) [115]. Many studies have demonstrated the pivotal role of the nervous system in regulating and synchronizing hormone secretion from endocrine cells [116–118]. Additionally, both the parasympathetic and sympathetic nervous systems exhibit unique growth dynamics during prenatal development [119], exerting significant influence on postnatal islet development and adult plasticity [120]. For example, Borden and colleagues showed that the absence of sympathetic innervation during islet development resulted in disrupted architecture and impaired functional maturation [121].

Pericytes are known to interact with various cell types within NICs, including glial cells and neuronal cells [122, 123], but whether pericyte regulation is through physical contact or paracrine signaling is not well documented in the human pancreas. Several studies have identified the expression of adhesion molecules on pericytes, such as vascular cell adhesion molecule and neural cell adhesion molecule, which have been suggested to regulate neurogenesis within developing NICs [124–126]. However, high-resolution imaging revealed that pericytes physically connected to neighboring pericytes, ECs, and smooth muscle cells, but not to surrounding neurons or glia in the living retina [127]. The exclusive nature of direct pericyte-neural interactions offers crucial insights into pericytic signaling mechanisms. This specificity may explain the postulated role of pericyte-EC connections in the capillary region, which spatially confines vasoactive signals, preventing non-specific regulation of intraislet blood supply.

Therefore, it was proposed that the pericytes may regulate NIC resident cells function through secreting extracellular vesicles, in which neurotrophic factors have been identified such as brain-derived neurotrophic growth factor, neuron growth factor, and glial-derived neurotrophic factor [128]. Genetic inactivation of glial-derived neurotrophic factor in mice results in decreased neuronal and glial populations during pancreas development and diminished islet innervation postnatally [129]. Similarly, neuron growth factor deficiency leads to reduced sympathetic islet innervation [130]. Kisler et al. found that dysregulation of pericyte function leads to neurovascular uncoupling [131], which in turn impairs neuronal function and survival [132]. Furthermore, pericyte-derived extracellular vesicles may also modulate the function of Schwann cells [133], which play crucial roles in maintaining the homeostasis of the NICs. Understanding the intricate pericyte-neural cell interactions within the islets could provide valuable insights for islet microenvironment, integrity, and function, and may offer new therapeutic strategies for development of novel therapies to preserve islet innervation and restoring pancreatic islet function in diabetes.

Interaction with intraislet immune cells

In the fetal pancreas, a substantial proportion of macrophages were found in close association with developing islets [134]. While the precise role of macrophages in islet morphogenesis remains largely undefined, accumulating evidence highlighted their significance in endocrine pancreas development, particularly in the context of β-cell differentiation [135]. For instance, macrophages release IL-1β, a cytokine implicated in the regulation of insulin secretion and β-cell survival [136, 137]. Similarly, previous research has shown that macrophage colony-stimulating factor enhances IL-6 production [138], and IL-6 further promotes islet hyperplasia and increases preproinsulin mRNA expression [139, 140]. These findings align with observations from macrophage-deficient mice, which exhibited severely impaired islet development and decreased β-cell proliferation [139]. An in vivo study conducted by Saunders et al. found that the intraislet ECs recruited macrophages, which is required for β-cell proliferation [141]. This interplay is further complicated by the pericytic regulation, with β-cells responding to ECs and macrophages, both of which are modulated by pericytes during physiological and pathological conditions.

Pericyte-macrophage crosstalk involves signaling pathways such as CCL2/CCR2 [142], CSF1/CSF1R [143], and bFGF/FGFR1 [144], where pericyte-derived cytokines promotes macrophage recruitment and polarization towards an M2-like phenotype. Using an acute ischemic stroke model, Shibahara et al. demonstrated that the recruitment of macrophages was significantly attenuated in pericyte-deficient mice [145]. Supporting this notion, in vitro studies have shown that that pericyte conditioned medium enhances the migration and proliferation of cultured macrophages [145, 146]. Additionally, studies on macrophage cells have identified the macrophage-produced bFGF and platelet-derived growth factor-B cooperatively activate PDGFRβ signaling pathway within the pericytes [147, 148], showcasing the complicated reciprocal interaction between pericytes and macrophages. The macrophage-derived cytokines further induce production of pericytic ECM proteins such as laminin and fibronectin [149, 150]. The dynamic remodeling of ECM proteins, particularly fibronectin, are known to play a crucial role in regulating islet survival and development [151–153].

In addition to macrophage, the immunoregulatory role of pericytes was also revealed by a recent study that the pericyte-produced IL-33 was indispensable for the recruitment of T and dendritic cells in the islets [35]. Notably, although IL-33 administration has been shown to attenuate insulitis in T1D mouse models [154], exogenous IL-33 failed to increase islet T cell numbers [155], implicating the recruitment of this cell population depends on the endogenous pericytic cytokine. Upon recruitment, macrophages and DCs support postnatal β-cell development, promote β-cell proliferation, and enhance β-cell functional maturation by producing cytokines such as retinoic acid, TGF-β, IGF-1, and WNT [155–157]. The immunoregulatory activities of pericytes within the islet microenvironment further highlight their importance in regulating islet development and subsequent glucose homeostasis, especially under inflammatory conditions. Understanding the complex interplay between pericytes, immune cells, and pancreatic islet cells will provide important insights into β-cell differentiation, pathogenesis of diabetes, and potentially identify novel therapeutic targets.

Pericyte in the diabetic pathology

Pericyte detachment and phenotypic instability in diabetes

Traditionally, pericytes were considered as passive victims of diabetes-induced apoptosis. However, recent evidence obtained through high-resolution in vivo imaging has revealed that the pericytes actively participate in their own dysfunction through detachment and migration preceding overt cell death [158]. In response to diabetic conditions, particularly hyperglycemia and the upregulation of cytokines like ANG-2, pericytes can undergo a phenotypic transformation, which involves cellular retraction, detachment from ECs and basement membrane, and migration into interstitial spaces, frequently resulting in “pericyte bridges” spanning adjacent capillaries [31]. This process physically uncouples pericyte from ECs, disrupting the PDGF-B/PDGFRβ signaling required for pericyte viability and homeostasis [159]. The induction of a migratory phenotype is primarily driven by ANG-2, which further sensitizes pericytes to pro-apoptotic stimuli from hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, and inflammation [160]. The detachment and phenotypic transition of pericyte reveals a broader therapeutic strategy that therapeutic strategies could target not only terminal apoptosis but also prevent initial detachment through enhancing pericyte-endothelial adhesion. The reversibility of the pericyte bridge phenotype following insulin treatment substantiates this therapeutic approach [161].

Notably, pericyte pathology in diabetes exhibits significant tissue-specific heterogeneity. The retinal, renal, and peripheral nerve microvasculature displays high susceptibility to diabetic complications [13], while the central nervous system microvasculature demonstrates exceptional resistance to identical hyperglycemic conditions [162]. This differential vulnerability is likely due to multiple mechanisms: (1) developmental heterogeneity, wherein CNS pericytes derive predominantly from neural crest cells, while others are mesodermal origin [163]; (2) structural distinctions, including variable pericyte coverage density that may influence microvascular stability [164]; and (3) microenvironmental signaling, whereby brain pericytes function within the neurovascular unit and receive cytoprotective signals from astrocytes and neurons that may be absent in other organs [165]. Collectively, this evidence refutes a unitary “diabetic pericytopathy” concept, necessitating organ-specific therapeutic strategies targeting pericyte vulnerabilities.

Pericytes in diabetic retinopathy

The selective loss of pericytes is the earliest and most specific histopathological hallmark of DR, often detectable prior to clinical manifestations. This loss severely compromises blood-retinal barrier integrity by impairing pericyte-dependent maintenance of endothelial tight junctions and transendothelial transport regulation, subsequently triggering plasma protein extravasation and resulting in diabetic macular edema [166], a principal cause of visual impairment characterized by fluid accumulation within the macula. At the molecular level, this barrier dysfunction correlates with disruption of critical pericyte signaling cascades, including Notch3 [167] and circular RNA cPWWP2A pathways [168], which are fundamental to endothelial barrier maintenance.

Concurrently, pericyte loss followed by EC apoptosis results in acellular, non-perfused capillary formation, whose accumulation generates retinal ischemic foci that constitute primary drivers of advanced proliferative diabetic retinopathy [169]. The profound loss of pericytes in the diabetic retina ablates their inhibitory function over EC proliferation. Together with the upregulated VEGF under retinal ischemia, the ECs undergo dysregulated proliferation, resulting in pathological neovascularization [170]. These newly formed vessels lack adequate pericyte coverage, manifesting as hyperpermeable, fragile networks prone to vitreous hemorrhage-causing acute vision loss and complications including tractional retinal detachment and neovascular glaucoma.

Pericytes in diabetic nephropathy (DN)

In the diabetic kidney, pericytes undergo pericyte-to-myofibroblast transition (PMT), which is characterized by α-SMA expression and acquisition of contractile and migratory properties, establishing pericytes as the predominant source of collagen-producing myofibroblasts that accumulate within the renal interstitium [171]. The resultant excessive extracellular matrix deposition culminates in tubulointerstitial fibrosis, which represents the pathognomonic histological feature of DN and constitutes the final common pathway to kidney failure. Concomitantly, peritubular capillary rarefaction occurs as pericyte detachment destabilizes the microvasculature, precipitating vessel loss, tissue hypoxia, and perpetuating a pathological cycle that exacerbates fibrosis [172].

Recent research has identified novel signaling that alters pericyte function fine-tune the PMT process. For instance, downregulation of integrin β8 on pericytes in diabetes facilitates PMT, whereas its restoration ameliorates renal fibrosis [173]. Conversely, Pontrelli et al. reported that the E3 ubiquitin ligase WWP2 is upregulated in diabetic pericytes and actively promotes their transition to myofibroblasts, making it a promising candidate for therapeutic inhibition [174]. In addition, specialized pericyte-like podocytes within the glomerulus are also adversely affected by the diabetic milieu. Podocyte injury and depletion contribute to the breakdown of the glomerular filtration barrier, leading to albuminuria, another cardinal feature of DN [175, 176].

Pericytes in diabetic neuropathy (DNp)

Within the peripheral nervous system, pericytes function as critical regulators of the blood-nerve barrier (BNB), a highly selective permeability barrier that protects peripheral nerves from systemic toxins while maintaining endoneurial microenvironmental homeostasis. They directly modulate endothelial tight junction protein expression (e.g., claudin-5, occludin) and are essential for synthesizing and maintaining the surrounding basement membrane [177]. In DNp, pericyte dysfunction leads to a breakdown of the BNB. The accumulation of Advanced glycation end-products in pericytes stimulates pericytes to secrete pro-fibrotic factors like TGF-β and VEGF [178], which promote aberrant basement membrane protein deposition, resulting in the pathognomonic basement membrane thickening characteristic of DNp. Additionally, these secreted factors downregulate tight junction protein expression in ECs, thereby increasing the BNB permeability [178]. The resulting barrier leakage permits plasma protein extravasation and neurotoxic substance infiltration into the endoneurial compartment, precipitating local inflammatory responses, nerve ischemia, and subsequent axonal degeneration.

Pericyte in other diabetic complications

The pathogenic impact of pericyte dysfunction extends beyond the classic triad of microvascular complications (Table 4). In diabetic erectile dysfunction, pericyte loss and dysfunction within the microvasculature of the corpus cavernosum contribute to the severe endothelial and neurovascular damage, frequently rendering the condition refractory to standard therapies such as PDE5 inhibitors [179]. Interestingly, Anita et al. demonstrated that Pericyte-derived extracellular vesicle-mimetic nanovesicles promotes the recovery of erectile function in STZ-induced diabetic mice [180], highlighting the therapeutic potential for pericyte-derived growth factors and cytokines in ED treatment.

Summary of pericyte involvement in Organ-Specific diabetic complications

The management of diabetic foot ulcers presents another major clinical challenge where pericyte dysfunction plays a pivotal role in impaired wound healing. The diabetic microenvironment creates conditions wherein pericytes become dysfunctional and fail to adequately stabilize newly formed blood vessels during the healing process [31], resulting in insufficient neovascularization and poor wound bed perfusion, which critically delays or wound closure. The pathological phenotype of diabetic pericytes further compounds these complications. Jin et al. reported that the pericytes isolated from diabetic wounds display a pro-inflammatory phenotype characterized by decreased proliferative capacity, increased susceptibility to apoptosis, and elevated oxidative stress levels, exacerbating the non-healing environment [181].

These findings collectively demonstrate that pericyte dysfunction represents a unifying pathological mechanism across diverse diabetic complications, suggesting that while “upstream” approaches targeting pericyte regulatory functions in pancreatic islet development and metabolic control may offer systemic benefits, effective “downstream” therapies must be precisely tailored to counteract the distinct pathological behavior of pericytes within specific organs.

Emerging research and future directions

Novel methodologies for studying pericytes

Traditionally, pericyte studies were limited by the complexity of their isolation and the lack of specific markers. However, the integration of advanced techniques such as single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) [21, 182] has revolutionized our understanding of pericytic diversity and function. By analyzing the transcriptomes of individual pericytes, researchers identified distinct subtypes and their unique gene expression signatures in both healthy and diabetic state [30], which facilitated the discovery of novel markers and signaling pathways that govern pericyte function within the islet microenvironment. Complementing scRNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics allows for the mapping of gene expression patterns onto the spatial architecture of the islet tissue [183], which enables the investigation of pericyte interactions with neighboring endocrine, endothelial, and immune cells within the complex islet microenvironment, providing crucial insights into cell-cell communication and the spatial organization of molecular processes. The integration of scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics offers a comprehensive, high-resolution molecular understanding of pericyte biology within the islet (Fig. 3).

The integration of scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics uncover pericytic heterogeneity, identify disease-specific gene signatures and signaling pathways, and map dynamic cellular interactions within the islet niche during islet development. Advanced genetic tools and mouse models help elucidation of pericytes in regulating islet development, blood flow, hormone secretion, and glucose homeostasis. Novel imaging techniques enable longitudinal observation of pericyte behavior, capillary dynamics, and blood flow regulation in living organisms

The development of sophisticated genetic tools and mouse models has been instrumental in dissecting the specific functions of pericytes in the pancreatic islet (Fig. 3). The G recombinase system, often driven by pericyte-specific promoters such as NG2 or Nkx3.2, allows for targeted genetic manipulation within pericytes [9, 35]. With specific gene deletion or overexpression in pericyte, researchers can directly investigate their impact on islet development and maturation. Optogenetic approaches, involving the expression of light-sensitive ion channels like channelrhodopsin-2 in pericytes, provide unprecedented temporal and spatial control over pericyte activity [73]. By precisely activating or inhibiting pericyte activity with light, the pericytic contributions to islet blood flow regulation, hormone secretion, and overall glucose homeostasis were analyzed. These advanced genetic tools and mouse models have significantly advanced our ability to study pericytes by enabling highly specific and controlled experiments that can establish causal links between pericyte function and islet physiology.

Notably, in vivo imaging of intraocular islet grafts has emerged as a powerful tool for longitudinal studies, allowing researchers to observe pericyte behavior, capillary dynamics, and blood flow within a living organism over time [184, 185]. This technique has provided critical insights into how pericytes modulate islet blood flow in response to physiological cues, such as fluctuations in blood glucose and sympathetic nervous system activity. Additionally, high-resolution imaging techniques, including advanced confocal microscopy and live-cell imaging, have enhanced the visualization of pericytes in both in vitro and in vivo studies [186–188]. By adapting these technologies, researchers can observe real-time dynamics of pericyte engagement with their surrounding microenvironment, tracking their movement, protein expression changes, and response to pharmacological manipulation (Fig. 3). This is particularly useful for understanding how pericytes contribute to vascular remodeling and stability, ultimately affecting islet hormone secretion and systemic glucose homeostasis.

Therapeutic strategies targeting pericytes in diabetic complications

The central role of pericytes in diabetic microvascular complications has established them as prime therapeutic targets, prompting the development of diverse interventions ranging from pharmacological agents to advanced regenerative medicine approaches. These strategies aim to protect, restore, or replace dysfunctional pericytes through modulation of the diabetic environment and specific cellular signaling pathways.

Pharmacological interventions represent the current mainstay of pericyte-protective therapy, operating through multiple mechanisms to preserve vascular integrity. Anti-VEGF agents such as aflibercept demonstrate dual functionality, serving as standard care for diabetic macular edema while providing secondary pericyte protection through vascular stress reduction [166]. Antidiabetic medications exhibit unexpected vascular benefits beyond glycemic control, with linagliptin normalizing pathological angiogenesis and glimepiride enhancing pericyte density through distinct mechanisms [189]. SGLT2 inhibitors like dapagliflozin protect retinal cells through ERK1/2/cPLA2/arachidonic acid pathway inhibition [190], while metabolic modulators such as dimethyl-2-oxoglutarate improve pericyte-endothelial communication by optimizing cellular metabolism [191]. More targeted approaches focus on critical signaling pathways, particularly the angiopoietin/Tie2 system, where bispecific antibodies like faricimab simultaneously inhibit VEGF-A and Ang-2 to achieve comprehensive vascular stabilization [192]. However, the PDGF-B/PDGFRβ pathway presents therapeutic challenges, as enhancing this central pericyte survival pathway must be carefully balanced against potential adverse effects in diabetic patients.

Advanced therapeutic frontiers are emerging through cutting-edge technologies that promise more precise and effective pericyte targeting. Gene therapy approaches utilize transgenes such as OXR1 to protect pericytes from oxidative stress [181], while research into non-coding RNAs like microRNA-15a/16 − 1 reveals novel regulatory mechanisms [193]. The primary challenge remains the delivery problem, driving development of pericyte-specific promoters and engineered viral vectors with preferential tropism. Nanoparticle-mediated drug delivery offers another promising platform, utilizing surface modifications with targeting moieties like VCAM-1 high-affinity peptide to concentrate therapeutic payloads directly at pathological sites [194]. Novel molecular targets including ANGPTL4 and the cGAS-STING pathway continue to emerge from ongoing biological discoveries [195].

Given the challenges in isolating and expanding primary pericytes, alternative stem cell sources capable of pericyte differentiation represent a viable therapeutic approach. Adipose tissue-derived stem cells (ASCs) demonstrate particular suitability for this application. Under defined culture conditions, ASCs differentiate into pericyte-like cells (pmASCs) that express characteristic pericyte markers and retain essential vascular support functions. Critically, in vitro studies demonstrate that pmASCs exhibit significant resistance to hyperglycemic conditions [196]. Furthermore, Additionally, pmASCs demonstrate anti-inflammatory and anti-angiogenic properties in pathological contexts, positioning them as a promising therapeutic intervention for diabetic retinopathy [197] and related vascular complications.

The translation of these advances into clinical practice is being revolutionized by adaptive optics scanning laser ophthalmoscopy, which enables non-invasive quantification of individual retinal pericytes [198], providing sensitive endpoints for a new generation of clinical trials focused on pericyte density and morphology as direct measures of therapeutic efficacy.

Challenges and future trends

Despite the significant progress in understanding islet pericytes, several critical knowledge gaps remain. Standardizing pericyte isolation protocols and phenotypic characterization are critical methodological limitations compromising reproducibility across studies. The lack of definitive pericyte-specific markers makes it difficult to precisely identify and investigate these cells during islet development [199]. The inherent heterogeneity within pericyte populations also necessitates further investigation to understand the functional diversity of these cells within the islet microenvironment. The limited knowledge of islet cell interactions and the scarcity of living human pancreatic tissue further compound these challenges [200].

Future research in understanding the precise mechanisms include: (1) spatiotemporal regulatory mechanisms governing pericyte-endocrine cell crosstalk during islet morphogenesis; (2) functional zonation of pericyte subtypes within islet microdomains; and (3) diabetes-stage-specific phenotypic alterations in pericyte-ECM signaling axes. Another significant trend will be the continued investigation into the role of pericytes in islet regeneration and their potential to contribute to novel diabetes therapies. Understanding how pericyte function is altered across the spectrum of diabetes development and progression will be crucial for identifying therapeutic targets and developing effective interventions. Advances in stem cell technology and tissue engineering are anticipated to play a significant role [201], facilitating the development of bioengineered tissues that incorporate pericyte functionality.

Conclusion

Emerging research continues to unveil the critical roles of pericytes in orchestrating pancreatic islet development and β-cell maturation, extending far beyond their traditional functions in vascular support. Pericytes contribute to β-cell differentiation and maturation through a complex interplay of signaling pathways, secreted factors, and interactions with the extracellular matrix. They are essential for maintaining β-cell maturity, proper insulin secretion, and regulating islet blood flow, all of which are crucial for glucose homeostasis. Dysfunction of islet pericytes is increasingly recognized as a significant factor in the pathogenesis of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, contributing to impaired β-cell function and survival. Several unanswered questions remain regarding the precise mechanisms of pericyte-β-cell interactions at different developmental stages and the heterogeneity of pancreatic pericytes and their distinct roles. Future research should focus on investigating these specific signaling pathways and molecular factors, developing better in vitro models, conducting longitudinal in vivo studies, and exploring the potential of targeting pericytes for therapeutic interventions in diabetes. Understanding the intricate role of pericytes in islet development holds significant promise for advancing our knowledge of diabetes and developing novel strategies for its prevention and treatment.
