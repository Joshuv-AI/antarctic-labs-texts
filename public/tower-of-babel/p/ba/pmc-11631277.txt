Value of APACHE II, SOFA and CardShock scoring as predictive tools for cardiogenic shock: A single‐centre pilot study
Authors: Małgorzata Chlabicz, Wojciech Łaguna, Remigiusz Kazimierczyk, Ewelina Kazimierczyk, Paulina Łopatowska, Monika Gil, Bożena Sobkowicz, Karol A Kamiński, Agnieszka Tycińska
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe aim of this study was to determine the value of the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and CardShock scoring systems in predicting the risk of in‐hospital, 30 day and 3 year mortality in patients with cardiogenic shock (CS).MethodsThis was a single‐centre observational study conducted between May 2016 and December 2017. Data from consecutive patients with CS admitted to the intensive cardiac care unit (ICCU) were included in the analysis.ResultsThe study group comprised 63 patients with CS {median age 71.0 [interquartile range (IQR), 59–82]; 42 men}: 32 patients with ischaemic and 31 with non‐ischaemic aetiology. The median APACHE II, SOFA and CardShock scores were 13 (IQR, 9.9–19.0) points, 8.0 (IQR, 6.0–10.0) points and 3.0 (IQR, 2.0–5.0) points, respectively. The in‐hospital, 30 day and 3 year mortality rates were 39.7%, 41.3% and 77.8%, respectively. APACHE II and SOFA scores were significantly higher in the group of patients who died at 30 days (P = 0.043 and P = 0.045, respectively). The CardShock score was higher in patients with CS who died in hospital (P = 0.007) and within 30 days (P = 0.004). No score was statistically significant for 3 year mortality. Area under the curve (AUC) analysis showed that the CardShock score had the highest value in predicting in‐hospital and 30 day mortality relative to APACHE II and SOFA, with a cut‐off score of 5 points [AUC: 0.70; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.59–0.81; P = 0.001] and 4 points (AUC: 0.71; 95% CI: 0.60–0.82; P < 0.001), respectively. The Bayesian Weibull model demonstrated the utility of all scales in estimating short‐term risk in patients with CS, with the impact of APACHE II and SOFA on patient life expectancy decreasing to a non‐significant level at approximately 32 days and CardShock at 33 days. The forest plots derived from the Bayesian logistic regression analysis show significant estimated coefficients with 94% highest density interval (HDI) for in‐hospital and 30 day mortality. The use of invasive or non‐invasive ventilation, a higher heart rate and a less negative fluid balance showed an unfavourable prognosis. Survival was associated with being in the pre‐CS class, with a higher glomerular filtration rate and a higher platelet count.ConclusionsAPACHE II and SOFA could be used for the risk stratification of patients with CS admitted to the ICCU. CardShock proved to be a more appropriate tool for assessing short‐term prognosis in patients with CS of all aetiologies, suggesting that there is potential for its promotion for use in daily clinical practice.Keywords: APACHE II; Bayesian Weibull model; cardiac care facilities; CardShock; shock, cardiogenic; SOFA

Introduction

An intensive cardiac care unit (ICCU) is a specific healthcare facility department that provides care to patients with severe cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). It was reported that CVD is the second‐leading cause of mortality worldwide. 1 Patients are usually admitted to the ICCU due to severe heart diseases, such as acute coronary syndrome (ACS), cardiomyopathies, life‐threatening arrhythmias, acute heart failure (AHF) or cardiogenic shock (CS). These patients require intensive care, continuous monitoring and assessment with validated scoring systems to facilitate quick decision‐making.

AHF is the onset or rapid deterioration of symptoms and signs of HF that require urgent medical treatment. AHF is the most frequent reason for unplanned hospital admission in patients over 65. 2 , 3 AHF is a sudden, life‐threatening condition associated with a 4%–12% in‐hospital mortality rate and a 21%–35% 1 year mortality rate. 2 , 3 CS represents the most severe manner of AHF, defined as a low cardiac output condition due to cardiac dysfunction, leading to severe end‐organ hypoperfusion associated with tissue hypoxia and increased lactate levels. In‐hospital mortality of patients with CS is in the range of 30%–50%. 4

A prognostic scoring system was first introduced in the 1980s and has become an essential tool for distinguishing the most critically ill patients with poor short‐ or long‐term outcomes. Therefore, the scoring often enables healthcare providers to plan adequate treatment and apply special measures for patients at high risk of death. Discrimination and power calibration are key characteristics that should be met by the prognostic scoring system. 5 For the evaluation of patients with ACSs, there are validated appropriate scales to facilitate clinical decisions, such as the Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) or Can Rapid risk stratification of Unstable angina patients Suppress ADverse outcomes with Early Implementation of the ACC/AHA guidelines (CRUSADE). 6 The Killip test predicted mortality in ACS and was validated for both ST‐elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non‐ST‐elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). 7 Similarly, the IABP‐SHOCK II risk score, 8 derived from the Intraaortic Balloon Pump in Cardiogenic Shock trial, is a short‐term (30 day) mortality score in patients with CS, but only in patients with infarct‐related CS, which limits its accuracy and generalizability.

In contrast, for the evaluation of patients with AHF or CS, there are still no standard scales to optimize clinical management; even the European Society of Cardiology's (ESC) 2021 guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of AHF and chronic HF do not provide such scales, except the Interagency Registry of Mechanically Assisted Circulatory Support (INTERMACS) scale for assessing indications for mechanical circulatory support (MCS). 9 These guidelines highlight the shortcomings of evidence‐based research on treatment methods and therapeutic algorithms for patients with CS.

Scoring systems for predicting outcomes have long been developed in intensive care medicine and are widely used in intensive care units (ICUs). They are well known to medical professionals. Scoring systems, including the APACHE II (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II) and SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) scores, are widely used in general ICUs. 10 , 11 The APACHE II score consists of three components: acute physiology score, chronic health points and age points. It is used to assess the patient's clinical condition and the risk of death during hospitalization. 10 Another popular score is the SOFA score, and it is widely used to estimate the risk of morbidity and mortality due to sepsis and to assess the patient's clinical condition daily. The SOFA score includes assessing six organ systems: respiratory, coagulation, liver, cardiovascular, neurologic and renal. 11

The novel CardShock risk score 12 stratifies the risk of short‐term mortality in patients with either ACS or non‐ACS causes of CS. Nevertheless, the most common cause of CS (81%) during the study was ACS (68% STEMI); 94% underwent coronary angiography, of which 89% underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). The main non‐ACS causes were severe chronic HF and valvular causes. In clinical practice, it is important that the score be useful for all patients with CS, regardless of aetiology.

The study therefore aimed to determine the value of the well‐known APACHE II, SOFA and the novel CardShock scoring systems in predicting the risk of in‐hospital, short‐term (30 day) and long‐term (3 year) mortality in patients with CS, including the whole spectrum of aetiologies.

Methods

Ethical issues

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Białystok (Poland) on 27 April 2017 (No. R‐I‐002/147/2017). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants gave written informed consent.

Study design and population

This study was conducted at a tertiary care academic medical centre with a 10‐bed ICCU in Poland between May 2016 and December 2017. Consecutive patients aged over 18 within 24 h of the identification of CS were included in the analysis. The inclusion criteria were CS defined according to ESC HF guidelines, divided into pre‐CS, CS and refractory CS. 4 , 13 This was diagnosed at admission based on clinical signs and symptoms of left‐ and/or right‐side heart congestion accompanied by hypotension and peripheral hypoperfusion (CS class and refractory CS class) or signs and symptoms of left‐ and/or right‐side heart congestion without hypotension and peripheral hypoperfusion (pre‐CS class). 4 The pre‐CS stage was diagnosed when there was hypoperfusion and systolic blood pressure (SBP) above 90 mmHg without the need for circulatory support; the CS stage was diagnosed when SBP was below 90 for more than 30 min or there was a need for pharmacological agents or an intra‐aortic balloon pump to maintain SBP above 90 mmHg; and the refractory CS stage was diagnosed with an inadequate response to treatment with two vasoactive agents. 4 All CS stages were included in the analysis. Exclusion criteria were septic shock and shock in the course of pulmonary hypertension (Figure 1).

Flow diagram of study patients. ICCU, intensive cardiac care unit.

The aetiology of CS was the responsibility of the investigators. Ischaemic causes were defined as previously diagnosed ischaemic heart disease (IHD) and ACS during hospitalization, or non‐ischaemic causes. ACS aetiology was defined as shock due to myocardial infarction (MI) [with (STEMI) or without ST elevation (non‐STEMI)].

At the entry study, basic vital signs, biochemical analysis [morphology, creatinine, arterial blood lactate, pH, bilirubin and high‐sensitivity troponin T (hs‐TnT)], electrocardiography (ECG) and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) were performed. The TTE has the following parameters: left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), right atrium area (RAA), tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) and tricuspid regurgitation peak gradient (TRPG). Furthermore, ultrasound measurements of the inferior vena cava (IVC) were taken during inspiration (IVC min) and expiration (IVC max). The central venous pressure (CVP) was measured at the time of presentation using the fluid fill tube connected to the water column in the supine position. 14 The daily fluid excretion (diuresis) and net fluid balance during the first 24 h were evaluated.

Disease severity scores, including APACHE II, SOFA and CardShock, were calculated for each patient using an online calculator (https://www.mdcalc.com, https://cardshock.org). Patients were treated according to local clinical practice, and treatment and procedures were recorded. Dates of death were obtained from the Polish Ministry of Digital Affairs as of 17 February 2020. In‐hospital, 30 day and 3 year all‐cause mortality were the primary endpoints.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (%). Continuous variables are expressed as means [standard deviation (SD)] or as medians [interquartile range (IQR)] [25th and 75th percentiles], depending on the distribution. The distribution of all variables was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical analysis was performed using the Student t‐test or Mann–Whitney test for continuous data. Pearson's χ 2 test was used for categorical variables. DeLong's test determined the P‐value in a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.

ROC curves were plotted to determine the area under the curve (AUC) and the sensitivity and specificity of the optimal cut‐offs. DeLong's test was used to compare two AUC results. The SciPy library 15 and a statistical software package, Statistica 13.3.0 (Tibco Software; Palo Alto, CA, USA), were used for the analysis.

We used a Bayesian Weibull survival model to investigate the relationship between the probability of death and days since admission. The Weibull distribution is a parametric model that captures various shapes of hazard functions, making it suitable for modelling time‐to‐event data. In our study, the shape and scale parameters were estimated using a Bayesian framework, resulting in posterior distributions for the parameters. 16 A Bayesian Weibull model was utilized to scrutinize the 3 year hazard rate and estimate the temporal impact of APACHE II, SOFA and CardShock scores on patients' lifespans. This estimation was achieved by computing the difference between the hazard rates at the maximum and minimum scores. Subsequently, piecewise regression based on the Trust Region Reflective algorithm 17 was employed to determine the change point in the difference between the hazard rate functions. This change point was interpreted as the day beyond which the scores no longer significantly impacted patients' lifespans. Confidence intervals (CIs) for these estimations were established through bootstrap resampling of the posterior distributions of the model parameters, specifically identifying the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the days where the piecewise regression lines demonstrated a change point in the different functions of the hazard rates.

Bayesian logistic regression was used to evaluate the risk factors associated with in‐hospital and 30 day mortality. This approach provides a probabilistic framework for inference, which is advantageous in the context of our study's small sample size. The estimated coefficients from the regression model are presented in terms of log‐odds, offering insights into the direction and magnitude of the association between each predictor and the outcome. To quantify the uncertainty of these estimates, we computed the 94% highest density interval (HDI) for each coefficient. The HDI represents the range within which the true value of the coefficient lies with 94% credibility, given the data and the model. Variables with HDIs that do not cross zero were considered statistically significant predictors of mortality.

Results

Clinical characteristics

The study group comprised 63 patients with CS [median age 71.0 (IQR, 59–82); 42 men; median LVEF 25.0 (IQR, 15–42)]: 32 patients with ischaemic and 31 with non‐ischaemic aetiology. At enrolment, there were 25 patients in the pre‐CS stage, 29 in the CS stage and 9 in the refractory CS stage. Twelve patients had ACS, of whom seven had coronary angiography and six had successful PCI with at least one stent placed. One patient had no coronary artery lesions (Takotsubo syndrome). The remaining patients did not have coronary angiography due to contraindications (e.g., active gastrointestinal bleeding) or a high probability of ACS other than type 1 MI resulting from other causes (e.g., rapid atrial fibrillation and hypoxia). In most of these cases, the status of the coronary arteries was known from previous hospitalizations. Causes of non‐ischaemic CS included supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias (n = 11), exacerbation of dilated cardiomyopathy (n = 8), valvular defect (n = 6), post‐myocarditis or myocarditis (n = 3) and toxic aetiology (n = 3) (Figure 1). Twelve patients previously had an implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) or an ICD with resynchronization therapy (CRT‐D). The median length of the ICCU stay was 6.0 (IQR, 4–8) days. The median number of days to death was 26 (IQR, 8–371). Most patients received inotropic agents: dobutamine in 11 patients, dopamine in 3 patients and norepinephrine in 6 patients. In 36 patients, both dobutamine and norepinephrine were used intravenously. A nitroglycerin infusion was administered to four patients. Most patients (89.2%, n = 66) required intravenous furosemide therapy. Almost 60% of patients (n = 44) required either invasive (IV) or non‐invasive ventilation (NIV) support, and in 8.1% of patients (n = 6), continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was applied. A stroke was reported in one patient.

The median APACHE II, SOFA and CardShock scores were 13 (IQR, 9.9–19.0) points, 8.0 (IQR, 6.0–10.0) points and 3.0 (IQR, 2.0–5.0) points, respectively. The in‐hospital, 30 day and 3 year mortality rates were 39.7%, 41.3% and 77.8%, respectively. The detailed characteristics of patients are shown in Table 1.

In‐hospital mortality

The in‐hospital mortality rate was 39.7% (n = 25). A higher mortality rate was observed in patients with ischaemic CS aetiology compared with those with non‐ischaemic aetiology (P = 0.027). Furthermore, a higher mortality rate was observed in patients who received IV/NIV ventilation compared with those who did not (P < 0.001). The analysis identified the following risk factors for death: a higher level of lactates (P = 0.003), a smaller IVC dimension during inspiration (P = 0.049) and a lower negative fluid balance (P = 0.036) (Table 2). APACHE II and SOFA scores were not statistically significant in predicting in‐hospital mortality. In contrast, CardShock demonstrated the ability to predict in‐hospital mortality (AUC: 0.70; 95% CI: 0.59–0.81; P = 0.001) (Table 3 and Figure 2).

Thirty‐day mortality

The 30 day mortality rate was 41.3% (n = 26). A higher mortality rate was observed in patients who received IV/NIV ventilation compared with those who did not (P < 0.001). The analysis identified the following risk factors for death: a higher value of creatinine (P = 0.024), a lower value of glomerular filtration rate (GFR) (P = 0.024), a higher level of lactates (P = 0.003), a lower negative fluid balance (P = 0.019) and lower diuresis in the first 24 h (P = 0.032). APACHE II, SOFA and CardShock scores were statistically significantly higher in the deceased group at P = 0.043, P = 0.045 and P = 0.004, respectively (Table 2). The AUC analysis revealed that the cut‐off APACHE II score of 13 points had a similar moderate value for predicting 30 day mortality as the cut‐off SOFA score of 8 points (AUC: 0.65; 95% CI: 0.53–0.77; P = 0.012 and AUC: 0.65; 95% CI: 0.53–0.77; P = 0.014, respectively). The better predictor of 30 day mortality was the CardShock score of 4 points (AUC: 0.71; 95% CI: 0.60–0.82; P < 0.001) (Table 3 and Figure 2).

Three‐year mortality

The 3 year mortality rate was 77.8% (n = 49). The statistical analysis demonstrated that the population who died during the 3 year follow‐up exhibited higher creatinine values (P = 0.027), lower GFRs (P = 0.031), lower negative fluid balances (P = 0.004) and lower diuresis in the first 24 h (P = 0.002). The study showed no differences between the analysed groups with respect to APACHE II, SOFA or CardShock scores (Table 2). The analysis of the AUC demonstrated that the APACHE II, SOFA and CardShock scores exhibited low efficacy in predicting the 3 year mortality rate (Table 3 and Figure 2).

Additional analysis

Analysis of subgroups with or without an ICD/CRT‐D showed no association with in‐hospital (P = 0.876), 30 day (P = 1.000) or 3 year (P = 0.229) survival.

Discussion

This study provides a real‐life characterization of patients admitted to the ICCU with CS requiring urgent diagnosis and intensive monitoring and treatment. The main advantage of the study is that CS patients are consecutively admitted to the ICCU, including those with ACS, stroke, renal failure requiring CRRT or respiratory failure requiring invasive ventilation. Our results suggest that the APACHE II and SOFA scores can be used to assess the risk of death within 30 days of admission. However, the CardShock score was a better predictor of a poor in‐hospital and 30 day prognosis. Neither score can be used to assess long‐term mortality in critically ill patients with CS admitted to the ICCU.

The study population was small but consisted of CS patients. This small population may be explained by the relatively low number of patients admitted to the ICCU with this critical condition. Gorlicki et al. 1 showed that of 3677 patients with diagnosed AHF during 5 years, only 109 patients (3%) had CS. Bazmpani et al. 2 showed that 2%–5% of patients with AHF present with CS. In this study, there were consecutive ICCU admissions of critically ill patients for CS over 19 months.

Despite continuous advances in therapeutic approaches and management strategies, the mortality of patients with CS remains high. In the Ro‐AHFS registry, which included patients with a primary diagnosis of HF, the mortality rate was 15.3% for patients admitted directly to the ICU and 18.4% for those transferred to the ICU during hospitalization. 3 , 4 In the Olmsted Registry cohort in the United States, mortality at 1 and 5 year follow‐up after diagnosis in patients with all types of HF, from 2000 to 2010, was 20% and 55%, respectively. 4 Prins et al. 5 reported that patients with AHF 6 and cardiorenal syndrome have a poor prognosis, and rescue therapy using CRRT was associated with an in‐hospital mortality rate of 62%. Shaefi et al. 7 revealed that the in‐hospital mortality of patients with CS ranged from 37.01% to 41.87%. Bazmpani et al. 2 reported, for CS, in‐hospital deaths ranging from up to 60% and 1 year mortality ranging from 50% to 60%, with the majority occurring in the first 2 months. Thus, the mortality rate observed in our study is in line with previous research.

Mortality risk stratification for patients with CS remains challenging due to the heterogeneity of patient clinical profiles. The strongest predictors of mortality in the literature include age, SBP, HR and blood glucose levels. 13 , 18 Other studies have identified ACS aetiology, age, previous MI, previous coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), confusion, low LVEF, blood lactate levels and use of norepinephrine as risk predictors of mortality. 8 , 12 , 19 In the current study, higher lactate levels and lower negative fluid balances were indicators of in‐hospital mortality; higher lactate levels and impaired renal function with lower negative fluid balance and lower diuresis in the first 24 h were predictors of 30 day mortality, while impaired renal function with lower negative fluid balance and lower diuresis in the first 24 h predicted in‐hospital, 30 day and even 3 year mortality.

Individuals with chronic kidney disease are more likely to die from CVD than to develop kidney insufficiency. 9 , 10 Renal failure was previously described as an independent risk factor for death in patients with ACS, 11 , 13 valvular heart defects 16 and acute decompensated HF (ADHF), 16 which is in line with our study.

An increase in blood lactate is a well‐known prognostic indicator in critical conditions. It results from increased lactate production in hypoperfused conditions. 12 , 18 Previous data have shown that ACS, previous MI or previous CABG were associated with increased mortality compared with non‐ACS. 12 However, some researchers have shown no such association. 20 , 21 In the current study, the ischaemic aetiology of CS was associated with mortality in patients with CS, whereas ACS was not statistically significant. Ischaemic aetiology may reflect limited reserves for recovery, which is also a strong predictor of poor outcomes. 12

ICD therapy is indicated in a subset of patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction to prevent sudden cardiac death. 22 CRT‐D is a device‐based therapy that has contributed significantly to improvements in the treatment of HF. 23 In our study, previous ICD or CRT‐D implantation was not associated with mortality in the analysed CS population. This may be due to the small sample size of the study.

The APACHE II score was first described in 1985. Since then, it has been widely used in clinical practice, especially among general ICU patients. 17 It was found that an increased APACHE II score on admission can predict higher in‐hospital mortality and a poor long‐term prognosis in ICU patients, including those with respiratory disease, severe sepsis or pancreatitis. 15 , 17 , 24 , 25 However, the data regarding severe cardiac patients are not clear. Okazaki et al. 26 revealed that the APACHE II scoring system did not have an adequate AUC to predict adverse mid‐term outcomes defined as all‐cause death or readmission due to HF in patients with ADHF. This led to the development of the APACHE‐HF 26 scoring system, which was based on significant APACHE II factors and predicted adverse outcomes in a selected group of ADHF patients. Nevertheless, we found that the APACHE II score has an adequate AUC to predict mortality in cardiac patients with AHF hospitalized at the ICCU at 30 days. In the current study, the primary endpoint was all‐cause death. Importantly, the APACHE II score was originally developed for patients with various diseases requiring ICU monitoring and treatment. In this study, we found that the APACHE II score could be a tool for use in clinical practice in a group of patients with CS hospitalized at the ICCU.

The SOFA score 27 was initially developed to determine the severity of organ dysfunction in critically ill patients with sepsis. Its components include the assessment of respiratory, coagulation, hepatic, neurologic, cardiovascular and renal function within 24 h of admission. The SOFA scores range from 0 to 24, with the highest score reflecting the worst condition. In our study, the SOFA score had a modest value to predict only short‐term mortality, possibly because it was developed to assess patients with sepsis during hospitalization. Ferreira et al. 28 showed that the mean and highest SOFA scores had the strongest correlation with mortality, followed by the Δ‐SOFA score (defined as a fixed day minus the initial day SOFA) and the initial SOFA score (AUC: 0.79; 95% CI: 0.75–0.83). In our study, we assessed only the initial SOFA score; therefore, we were only able to show moderate discrimination for short‐term mortality.

A CardShock risk prediction model for in‐hospital mortality was constructed using variables independently associated with all‐cause death in the CardShock trial. 12 This was a European, prospective, observational, multicentre and multinational study of CS. Seven categorical parameters were included in the CardShock risk score, with a maximum score of 9 points. 12 The trial enrolled patients with CS who had either ACS or non‐ACS aetiologies. Nevertheless, the most common cause of CS in this study was ACS (81%). In the current study, only 19% of CS patients had a diagnosis of ACS, highlighting the versatility of this score, which can be applied to the general population of CS patients in the ICCU. The advantage of the CardShock risk scale 12 is that it includes typical clinical variables that are readily available from clinical assessment. The score can be calculated within a very short time after the onset of shock, facilitating early risk assessment.

To estimate the day when the impact of scores on patients' lifespan begins to taper off, a piecewise regression approach was employed. As demonstrated by Banesh et al., 29 this technique serves as an invaluable tool for pinpointing change points within a function or dataset. It has proven particularly effective in fields where relationships between data variables do not follow a linear pattern, much like the examples in this study. 30 Within the realm of medical research, piecewise regression is particularly useful. It assists in identifying critical points where health outcomes or disease progression rates undergo significant shifts, as Senapati et al. have shown. 31 In our study, the forest plots derived from the Bayesian logistic regression analysis show significant estimated coefficients with 94% HDI for in‐hospital and 30 day deaths. For both time intervals, we showed an unfavourable prognosis for the use of IV or NIV, a higher HR rate and a less negative fluid balance. In contrast, survival was associated with being in the pre‐CS class, a higher GFR value and a higher PLT count.

However, a SOFA score > 8, an APACHE II score > 13 and a CardShock score > 4 were associated with a worse short‐term prognosis and should prompt more aggressive early treatment and may help to select patients for transfer to the ICCU from other non‐cardiac units where there is much less experience in managing such severe patients. We conclude from the analysis that APACHE II and SOFA may estimate the risk during the first 30 days of hospitalization. However, the better predictor of in‐hospital and 30 day poor prognosis was the CardShock score.

Study limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, it included a relatively small number of patients, which may limit significant statistical findings and conclusions. Second, the original APACHE II score was based on the worst physiological value during the first 24 h, assuming that all physiological values were available. In the current study, we used vital signs and laboratory data obtained on admission. Finally, the study was conducted at a single centre, which limits the generalizability of the results to other patient populations.

Conclusions

Despite major advances in the management of cardiac patients, critically ill patients with CS admitted to the ICCU remain at high risk of mortality. As the APACHE II and SOFA scales have been validated in ICU patients and are widely used in clinical practice, they may become valuable in the risk stratification of cardiac patients with CS admitted to the ICCU. Nevertheless, CardShock was found to be an appropriate tool for assessing short‐term prognosis in patients with CS of all aetiologies. This suggests that it may be possible to promote it as a tool for use in everyday clinical practice.
