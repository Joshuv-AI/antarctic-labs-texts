Defect controlled UV-to-visible down conversion in Bi3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 monoclinic-like phosphors
Authors: Ashok Kumar Srivastava, D. Anand, K. Ramachandran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cost-effective, defect-controlled, lanthanide-free and stable down-conversion UV-visible phosphors play an important role in solid-state lighting and optoelectronic applications. Down-conversion phosphor materials transform intense UV light into visible emission. The development of defect-controlled phosphors is challenging because it is commonly seen in many reported UV-visible phosphors that disorder increases with dopant concentration, which results in the creation of coulombic traps and scattering centres. These traps impede further electron and hole transport. To overcome this kind of challenge, Bi3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 distorted monoclinic-like phosphors were synthesized by a straightforward and affordable conventional high-temperature solid-state reaction method. X-ray diffraction confirms the formation of a distorted monoclinic-like phosphor with a slight variation in lattice parameter. The morphological study was done using scanning electron microscopy, which confirms agglomerated, submicron-sized particles, spherical in shape. Electronic states and oxidation states were calculated using XPS, which suggests the formation of oxygen vacancy defect states and the successful doping of Bi3+ in the host. Optical band gap and Urbach energy analysis, derived from UV diffuse reflectance spectroscopy, revealed a narrow bandgap and stabilized disorder with increasing dopant concentration in the host. PL confirms broadband visible light under 350 nm excitation via a UV-to-visible down-conversion emission mechanism, which is facilitated via Bi3+ level emission and defects states associated with an oxygen vacancy mechanism. Chromaticity analysis reveals stable CIE coordinates near standard coordinates with a high correlated color temperature (CCT) value, which is the characteristic of a cyan–blue emission. Overall, the results show that Bi3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 distorted monoclinic-like phosphors had good UV-to-visible down conversion capabilities, which could be good candidates for optoelectronic and solid-state lighting applications.

Introduction

Phosphor materials with effective UV-to-visible down-conversion are promising in various applications, like materials science, lighting, security, forensics, biology, and environmental monitoring.1 In the past few decades, such kinds of materials have gained a lot of attention for research due to their unique feature of light conversion. Nowadays, rare-earth-doped materials like Gd2O2S:Er (green/red emission), Y2SiO5:Pr, and Li2SrSiO4:Pr are frequently used in conventional applications for effective UV-to-visible down-conversion phosphors, and these materials transform ultraviolet UV (∼380 nm) light into visible emission (400–700 nm).2,3 Whenever a rare earth element is doped in such kinds of host matrices, like vanadates, niobates, tungstates, aluminates, and pyrochlores, they display emission band tuning from the blue to red/near-IR area due to activator ion concentrations.4 Here, in this study, we focused on pyrochlore-like materials (A2B2O7) because they have been researched for diverse applications due to their exceptional chemical and thermal stability, high lattice stiffness, and unique ability to tolerate extensive cation and anion site disorder over the past few decades.5 They also served as ideal hosts for rare-earth activators in phosphor applications due to their high solubility limits and adjustable local site symmetry.5 They allow precise tailoring of the emission colors, such as shifting from orange to intense red to improve color rendering in white LEDs.5–7 They are excellent hosts for UV-to-visible down-conversion because their crystal structures combine high structural tolerance for rare-earth activators with relatively low phonon energies, which minimize non-radiative losses and enhance emission efficiency.8 The substitution of a large cation at A sites through compositional tailoring of local symmetry triggers intense electric dipole transitions, which convert UV energy into high-purity visible light, like vibrant red or white.5 Additionally, materials like titanates and zirconates possess strong charge transfer bands that efficiently absorb UV photons and sensitize dopant ions, ensuring consistent performance for pc-WLEDs and forensic applications.9,10 It is seen in many reported articles that oxygen-assisted vacancy also plays an important role in the emission mechanism when activator ions are doped in such a host matrix. For example, Tb3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 ceramics achieved defect-regulated high optical transparency and luminescence tunability for optical shielding windows.6 Eu3+/Bi3+:Y2Zr2O7 transparent ceramics with excellent color converters for UV-excited WLEDs were produced by the vacuum sintering method.11 The blue–green emission from polyol-synthesised Tb3+, Bi3+:Y2Zr2O7 was investigated for display technology applications.12 White light emission from Y2Zr2O7 crystalline powders co-doped with Eu3+ and Dy3+ was examined.13 Band-gap engineering has been successfully employed to tune Bi3+-centered emissions in solid-solution phosphors, such as the LiIn1−xScxGeO4 host, for anti-counterfeiting applications.14 In 2025, the synthesis of Bi-doped Y2Zr2O7 using the co-precipitation method was reported, but a detailed investigation and study on its optical properties was not conducted.15 The defect-assisted emission and a detailed structural and electronic state study of the Y2Zr2O7 host induced by Bi3+ incorporation were missing from the study. The present work fills this gap by demonstrating a Bi3+ ion level and oxygen vacancy-assisted defect-related UV-to-visible down-conversion mechanism with a detailed structural and electronic study in Bi3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 as a potential phosphor candidate.16,17

Experimental methods

Synthesis

Bi3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 disordered monoclinic-like phosphor powders were synthesized via the high-temperature solid-state reaction method. All precursors were purchased from Sisco Research Laboratories Private Limited, India. Firstly, all precursors were heated at 200 °C for 2 hours to remove moisture and carbonate impurity. The stoichiometric balanced reaction formula is as follows:1(1 − x)Y2O3 + xBi2O3 + ZrO2 = Bi:Y2Zr2O7where x = 0.01 and 0.02. After that, the samples were taken out from the furnace and weighed in a stoichiometric batch, yielding calculated amounts of Y2O3 (99.99%), ZrO2 (99.99%), and Bi2O3 (99.99%) according to eqn (1). The molar weight precursors were mixed with ethanol for 1 hour using an agate mortar and pestle. The mixed powder was again kept inside the muffle furnace (oxygen environment) at 1200 °C for 12 hours at a heating rate of 15 °C min−1 to initiate the solid-state reaction. After 12 hours, the furnace was closed and cooled slowly to room temperature at a cooling rate of 5 °C min−1. Again, the calcined powder sample was ground for 1 hour and pelletized using a pelletizer under 150 kg cm−2 pressure. The pellets were sintered at 1300 °C for 12 hours to achieve phase purity and densification at the same heating and cooling rates as during calcination, up to room temperature. The obtained pellet was further broken and made into a fine powder for further characterization.

Characterization

Structural analyses were performed on a PANalytical X-ray diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) operating at 40 kV and 15 mA. Diffraction patterns were recorded in the 2θ range of 5.01087° to 99.98487° with a step size of 0.022°. Surface morphology was examined using an SNE-3200M SEM operated at 15 kV and 110 µA with secondary electron imaging. FTIR spectra were recorded in transmittance mode using a Shimadzu IRTracer-100 over the spectral range 400–4000 cm−1 with 4 cm−1 resolution. The chemical states and surface composition of the samples were investigated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using a monochromatic Al Kα radiation source operated at 25 W, with a pass energy of 55 eV, an analysis area of 100 µm, and a take-off angle of 45°. The diffuse reflectance UV-visible spectra of the samples were recorded using a Shimadzu UV-3600 Plus spectrophotometer in reflectance mode over the wavelength range of 200–800 nm with a scan speed of medium, sampling interval of 1.0 nm, slit width of 5.0 nm, and time constant of 0.2 s using the UVProbe software (version 2.70). Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded using a Horiba Fluorolog system with excitation at 350 nm, scanning emission from 370 to 680 nm in 1 nm increments, side entrance and side exit slit of a 3 nm bandpass filter, grating density of 1200 and the integration time of 0.1 seconds. CIE Color Chromaticity was used to check the color coordinate in the visible color space.

Results and discussions

Structural and microstructural analysis

The absence of prominent superlattice reflections, such as (111), (311), and (331), a unit cell parameter of ∼7.5 Å and peak positions confirm that the synthesized material is a monoclinic-like disordered structure rather than an ordered pyrochlore. Surface morphology

According to SEM (Fig. 2), the morphological analysis of Bi3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 disordered monoclinic-like phosphor samples is presented through particle size distribution and EDX spectra. Fig. 2(a–c) shows the agglomerated and clustered spherical particles with sizes at the submicron level, as shown in the histograms (Fig. 2(g–i)). The pure sample exhibits a broader grain distribution with a mean size of 0.62 µm. However, Bi3+ doping of 1% and 2% acts as a grain growth inhibitor, narrowing the distribution and creating a more uniform sub-micron (∼0.57 µm) powder. This structural homogenization is chemically validated by the EDX spectra (Fig. 2(d–f)), where the characteristic peaks for yttrium (Y), zirconium (Zr), and oxygen (O) are clearly resolved alongside the emergence of bismuth signals (Mα and Lα) in the doped variants. The systematic increase in bismuth peak intensity from 1% to 2% doping levels, coupled with the absence of extraneous impurity peaks, confirms the successful and high-purity stoichiometric incorporation of Bi3+ into the Y2Zr2O7 matrix.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis

The chemical composition and oxidation states of the constituent elements in Bi3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 monoclinic-like phosphors were investigated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The survey spectrum in Fig. 3(a) confirms the presence of Y, Zr, Bi and O elements without any detectable impurity peaks, indicating the successful incorporation of Bi3+ ions into the Y2Zr2O7 host lattice. The high-resolution Y 3d spectrum (Fig. 3(b)) exhibits two characteristic spin–orbit split peaks located at 158.31 eV and 164.01 eV, corresponding to Y 3d5/2 and Y 3d3/2 states, respectively. These binding energies confirm the trivalent oxidation state of yttrium (Y3+) in the host matrix. Similarly, the Zr 4d core-level spectrum (Fig. 3(c)) shows two well-resolved peaks centered at 181.37 eV and 183.7 eV, assigned to the Zr 3d5/2 and Zr 3d3/2 levels, respectively, verifying the presence of Zr4+ ions in the lattice. The Bi 4f spectrum presented in Fig. 3(d) consists of two deconvoluted peaks located at 158.00 eV and 164.14 eV, corresponding to Bi 4f7/2 and Bi 4f5/2 states.21 The observed spin–orbit splitting confirms that bismuth predominantly exists in the +3 oxidation state. The successful substitution of Bi3+ ions at Y3+ lattice sites is expected because of the comparable ionic radii and identical valence states of Bi3+ and Y3+ ions. The O 1s spectrum (Fig. 3(e)) deconvoluted into two distinct components centered at 529.20 eV and 531.27 eV. The lower binding energy peak is associated with lattice oxygen (O2−) bonded to Y and Zr ions, whereas the higher binding energy component originates from oxygen-deficient regions or oxygen vacancy-related defect states.22 The oxygen vacancy defect states introduced near the band structure act as intermediate trapping centers within the band gap, facilitating efficient UV-to-visible down-conversion emission. Furthermore, Bi-induced lattice distortion and defect-assisted energy transfer enhance the radiative recombination.

Optical properties

The UV-optical properties of pure and Bi3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 disordered monoclinic-like phosphor were analysed using diffuse reflectance spectroscopy. The reflectance spectra (Fig. 4(a)) of the pure sample show a significant absorption edge in the deep-UV region, confirming its wide band gap nature.23 After Bi doping (1% and 2%), the reflectance edge progressively shifts towards longer wavelengths, demonstrating enhanced UV absorption and band gap narrowing. The absorption coefficient (Fig. 4(b)) shows that the substantial increase in absorption intensity in the visible-UV range for Bi-doped samples is caused by the formation of Bi3+-induced localised states inside the host lattice.24 Optical band gap values computed using the Kubelka–Munk function and Tauc formalism show that the direct band gap (Fig. 4(c)) gradually drops from ∼5.4 eV (pure) to ∼5.2 eV (2% Bi3+).

Simultaneously, the XPS results also support the existence of oxygen assisted vacancy defects in Y2Zr2O7 monoclinic-like phosphors.25,26 In this host, after Bi doping, it is seen that the effective creation of Bi 6s and O 2p hybridised states alters the UV electronic structure of Y2Zr2O7,27 which makes the Bi3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 disordered monoclinic-like phosphor suitable for UV-excited down conversion phosphor applications.

Photoluminescence and CIE analysis

Fig. 5(a) shows the room-temperature photoluminescence emission spectra of pure and Bi3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 disordered monoclinic-like phosphors. The undoped and Bi3+-doped samples exhibit broad emission bands centred at ∼385 nm and ∼497 nm, respectively. The first peak, centered between 385 nm and 419 nm, for all the samples, is attributed to intrinsic deep-level defects within the Y2Zr2O7 lattice (Fig. 5(b)), while Bi3+ incorporation (1% and 2%) results in a slight blue shift of the emission peak (Table 2). A broad emission band centered near ∼497 nm is observed, which most likely originates from the Bi3+ ion due to the 3P1 to 1S0 transition (almost ∼96% of the total emission), and defect-assisted emission is approximately ∼4% in the system.28,29 CIE chromaticity (1931) and correlated color temperature (CCT) analysis values of pure and Bi3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 disordered monoclinic-like phosphors are summarized in Table 3. With chromaticity coordinates (x = 0.209, y = 0.327) and a high CCT of 14 526 K, the pure Y2Zr2O7 sample shows a cool bluish emission. While the coordinates (x = 0.207, y = 0.333) with a CCT of 14 483 K are observed in the 1% Bi-doped sample, additional doping to 2% causes a slight decrease in CCT to 13 767 K. Due to improved defect-assisted radiative recombination caused by Bi3+ ions, this slow decline in CCT indicates a regulated shift towards the blue–green area. The consistently high CCT values and stable chromaticity coordinates indicate its suitability as a good phosphor system.30

Conclusions

In this study, we have reported cost-effective, stable, lanthanide-free element Bi3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 disordered monoclinic-like phosphors synthesized using the traditional high-temperature solid-state reaction method. A polycrystalline disordered phase was observed with a slight lattice expansion with Bi3+ doping and localized compressive microstrain. This is due to the ionic size mismatch between Bi3+ and Y3+ ions, as established by structural studies using X-ray diffraction and Williamson–Hall analysis. Bi3+ substitution on Y3+ sites enables efficient bandgap engineering, lowering the direct bandgap from 5.4 eV to 5.2 eV, according to optical investigations. Interestingly, Urbach energy analysis showed that Eu decreased as Bi3+ concentration increased, which is a very interesting property, suggesting that Bi3+ controls band tail states as a defect stabilizer instead of causing harmful structural disorder, which is very interesting in a disordered monoclinic phosphor-like system. Defect trap states arise due to oxygen vacancies, as confirmed by the asymmetric peak of oxygen 1s in the 529–531 eV binding energy range in XPS. The potential for down conversion is highlighted by their photoluminescence characteristics as the blue–green emission intensity is greatly enhanced by Bi3+ doping, peaking at around 492–497 nm with spectral Q factor for peak 1, Q ∼4 (approx. ∼4% emission due to trap states) while Q factor for peak 2, Q ∼16 (approx. ∼96% emission due to Bi3+ assisted level) when excited at 350 nm, which are very important characteristics for UV-to-visible down conversion. The material goes through associative radiative recombination inside the disordered host lattice to efficiently convert UV light into visible light. It produced a bluish-green emission by maintaining high CCT values (up to 14 526 K) and stable CIE chromaticity coordinates, which are very beneficial for phosphor applications. According to our results, we concluded that, unlike traditional disordered systems where doping increases disorder, the Bi3+-doped Y2Zr2O7 disordered monoclinic phosphors exhibit a unique defect-stabilization mechanism, as evidenced by the reduction in the Urbach energy (Eu) and dislocation density, making it a good candidate for high-stability UV-visible down-conversion in optoelectronic devices.
