Development and validation of a clinical-adipometric nomogram for predicting tumor response to transarterial chemoembolization in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Juan Zhao, Guiming Fu, Yingwei Wang, Han Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) is the first-line therapy for intermediate-stage hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), yet treatment response exhibits marked heterogeneity and robust preoperative predictive tools are lacking. This study developed and validated a novel nomogram integrating CT-quantified body composition parameters with clinical factors to predict tumor response in 209 HCC patients after TACE. Using a multicenter retrospective design, patients from one center were randomly split at a 7:3 ratio into a training (n = 112) and an internal validation cohort (n = 46), while patients from the other center served as an independent external validation cohort (n = 51), we extracted from adipose and muscle tissues at the L3 vertebral level to compute standardized indices. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors, which were incorporated into a nomogram for risk stratification. The combined model integrating alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), tumor number, visceral adipose tissue (VATI), and adipose tissue ratio (VSR) effectively discriminated responders from non-responders, achieving high predictive accuracy across cohorts (AUC: 0.805 training, 0.829 internal validation, 0.815 external validation). Calibration and decision curve analysis confirmed superior model performance and clinical net benefit. This nomogram quantifies the prognostic impact of adipose tissue distribution, providing an actionable preoperative tool to guide personalized TACE management—including alternative or intensified therapies for high-risk patients or standard care for low-risk individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47502-w.

Introduction

Globally, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) ranks among the most common malignancies and represents the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality, exhibiting a 5-year survival rate below 20%1. According to the widely adopted Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system, surgical resection is reserved for early-stage HCC2. Nevertheless, over 80% of Chinese HCC patients present with advanced-stage at diagnosis, precluding curative resection and contributing to the nation’s second-highest cancer-related death rate. According to the guidelines3,4 of the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) and the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO), transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) is the first-line therapy for intermediate-stage HCC or advanced HCC without extrahepatic metastasis, owing to its broad indications and 30–70% objective response rate. Multiple TACE sessions are typically required for HCC tumor control, though therapeutic outcomes exhibit marked interindividual variability due to tumor biological heterogeneity5. Repeated TACE may paradoxically enhance angiogenesis and induce embolization-related hepatotoxicity, potentially counteracting therapeutic benefits and compromising overall survival (OS). Recent studies6,7 have shown that the variability in TACE response also leads to a high rate of local tumor recurrence, with rates reaching 46%, 58%, and 63% at 2, 3, and 5 years after treatment, respectively. An urgent need exists for precise TACE response prediction to transform HCC therapy, aligning with personalized medicine objectives8,9.

To predict tumor response after TACE, a number of scoring systems have been devised. A scoring system known as the Assessment for Retreatment with TACE (ART) was proposed by Sieghart and colleagues10. The ART score comprises radiological tumor response, an increase in Child–Pugh grade, and a ≥ 25% increase in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) level, with scores ranging from 0 to 8. An ART score ≥ 2.5 points indicates that repeated TACE may be ineffective. Adhoute et al.11 constructed the ABCR score, which incorporates alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) level, BCLC staging, Child–Pugh grade, and tumor response. An ABCR score ≥ 4 suggests the patient is unlikely to benefit from further TACE therapy. However, current external validation results demonstrate that the aforementioned scoring systems have not achieved desirable performance. They lack sufficient validity to inform conclusive clinical judgments, and further exploration of existing clinical datasets is required to assess tumor response after TACE.

Body composition encompasses the quantity and distribution of muscle mass, specific adipose tissue depots, and other weight components such as water, bone, and organs. Traditionally, metrics such as body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, and body weight have been the primary tools for assessing body composition. However, a significant limitation of these conventional indicators is their inability to differentiate between muscle and adipose tissue. This limitation is particularly problematic for patients with obesity, who often exhibit substantial variations in the distribution of muscle and fat. Conversely, substantial changes in body composition can occur even when body weight and BMI remain relatively stable. Among the various body composition parameters, skeletal muscle (SM) mass and adipose tissue—comprising subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), visceral adipose tissue (VAT), and intermuscular adipose tissue (IMAT)—have been extensively studied. Alterations in these parameters are associated with adverse outcomes in cancer patients, including those with HCC12–15. Computed tomography (CT) enables in vivo quantification of distinct radiodensities within anatomical compartments, directly differentiating adipose and lean mass. SM and adipose tissue are the most extensively analyzed body composition components, with alterations linked to poor oncological outcomes including HCC progression16. However, the mechanisms linking body composition to treatment response remain poorly understood. Advanced analytical approaches, such as those recently applied to cancer genomics, may help bridge this gap17,18. Despite growing interest in the role of body composition in oncology, the potential relationship between adipose tissue distribution and TACE response in HCC patients remains underexplored. Therefore, the aim of this study was to preoperatively develop and validate a noninvasive body-composition predictive model that can accurately predict the TACE response of HCC patients, and to assess the feasibility of its clinical application.

Materials and methods

This research complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and current ethical guidelines. As a retrospective study involving anonymized patient data, it was granted approval by the Institutional Review Board of Sichuan Cancer Hospital (Cancer Hospital Affiliated to University of Electronic Scienceand Technology of China), with an exemption from obtaining informed consent (Approval No.: k202502-16). The reporting of this study follows the TRIPOD checklist guidelines.

Study population

This study consecutively enrolled HCC patients undergoing TACE at our institutions (January 2018-April 2025) meeting these criteria: (a) AASLD-confirmed HCC diagnosis via pathology, cytology or imaging4; (b) age 18–75 years; (c) receiving TACE treatments twice within three months; (d) patients with the stage of BCLC A or B; (e) Pre-treatment contrast-enhanced CT ≤ 2 weeks before initial TACE. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) incomplete clinical and imaging data; (b) history of prior treatment for HCC; (c) > 3-months interval between TACE sessions; (d) concurrent antitumor therapies; (e) extrahepatic metastasis or combination of malignant tumors from other sites; (f) lack of short-term follow-up. TACE treatment

TACE was conducted within a fortnight following HCC diagnosis. The procedure—conventional TACE (c-TACE)—was performed on all enrolled patients by two interventional radiologists, each possessing more than a decade of experience. Using the Seldinger technique under local anesthesia, a 5F arterial catheter was inserted into the femoral artery. Under digital subtraction angiography (DSA) guidance, a 2.7F microcatheter was selectively advanced through the hepatic artery into the tumor-feeding arteries. Prior to treatment, all patients underwent angiography of the abdominal trunk and superior mesenteric artery to evaluate hepatic vascular circulation. For the chemoembolization treatment, the therapeutic emulsion was prepared by combining 4–18 mL of iodized oil with a chemotherapeutic agent—either platinum (10–40 mg) or epirubicin (10–40 mg). Post-embolization, tumor-feeding vessels were occluded with subsequent disappearance of tumor staining on angiography. The tumor-feeding vessels were then embolized with gelatin sponge particles to eliminate any residual perfusion.

CT imaging protocol

CT examinations were acquired with multi-row spiral CT scanners (GE DISCOVERY CT 750; UNITED CT 960) at two centers. The scanner utilized a tube voltage of 120 kV with automatic tube current modulation, and the reconstruction thickness was set to 5.0 mm. Abdominal scans were performed with non-contrast and triple-phase contrast enhancement. The contrast agent was administered via the arm vein at a rate of 3.0 ml/s, with a dosage of 1.5 ml per kilogram of body weight. The arterial phase, portal venous phase, and delayed phase scans were carried out at 25–30 s, 60 s, and 90–120 s, respectively. All patients were instructed to fast for 4–6 h prior to the examination and were required to hold their breath during the scanning process. The detailed parameters for each CT scanners are listed in Supplementary Data S1.

CT-based body composition evaluation

In this study, the cross-sectional areas of SM, VAT, SAT, and IMAT at the L3 level on venous-phase CT images were measured separately using SliceOmatic software (https: //www.tomovision.com/). The SM area at the L3 level encompassed the psoas major, rectus abdominis, internal/external obliques, transverse abdominis, quadratus lumborum, and erector spinae muscles. The following Heinz unit (HU) ranges were defined to differentiate body composition compartments: SM was quantified between − 29 and + 150 HU, subcutaneous and intermuscular adipose tissue from − 190 to − 30 HU, and visceral fat between − 150 and − 50 HU. (Fig. 2). The cross-sectional area of each component was then normalized by division with height squared (m2) to obtain corresponding indices (cm2/m2). The skeletal muscle index (SMI) was derived from the third lumbar vertebra (L3) skeletal muscle area normalized to height squared (cm2/m2). Similarly, the visceral (VATI), subcutaneous (SATI), and intermuscular adipose tissue indices (IMATI) were calculated as their respective adipose tissue areas divided by height squared (cm2/m2). Two radiologists, each with over 10 years of experience in abdominal diagnosis, jointly measured SM, SAT, VAT, and IMAT and discussed their findings until consensus was reached for each assessment.

Definition and follow-up of tumor response after TACE

Tumor response to TACE was assessed using modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST)19. Treatment outcomes were categorized based on the following four criteria: complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD).

Pre- and post-procedural contrast-enhanced CT or Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) images were acquired for all patients following the first and second TACE sessions. Two abdominal radiologists, blinded to body composition analyses, independently assessed follow-up CT/MRI examinations obtained 1–3 months post-TACE to evaluate HCC treatment response according to mRECIST criteria. In this study, patients were stratified into two groups: the TACE response group (TR group, comprising patients with CR or PR) and the non-TACE response group (nTR group, including those with PD or SD).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics software (version. 26) and python software (version. 3.13.5, https://www.python.org/). Continuous clinical variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), while categorical variables were presented as percentages. Comparisons between intergroups were conducted utilizing independent t-tests for continuous variables exhibiting normality, and the Mann–Whitney U test for those demonstrating a skewed distribution, and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Cohen’s kappa statistic (κ) was used to assess inter-rater reliability between the two radiologists (κ values < 0.4, poor agreement; 0.4–0.6, moderate agreement; 0.6–0.8, good agreement; > 0.8, excellent agreement). Univariate and multivariable analyses were conducted to identify clinical independent clinical risk factors associated with TACE response. Factors demonstrating P < 0.05 in univariate logistic regression were entered into the multivariable model. Independent predictors were established through multivariable logistic regression. Model discrimination was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) in training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts. Nomograms were constructed to visualize the prediction models and compute individual patient risk probabilities. Model performance was evaluated using calibration curves and decision curve analysis. Based on optimal cutoff values derived from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk subgroups for subsequent analyses. To evaluate the risk of overfitting, we calculated the events per variable (EPV) for the final multivariable model, defined as the number of non-response events divided by the number of predictor variables. An EPV ≥ 10 was considered indicative of adequate model stability20. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the patient’s clinical and body composition

The study cohort consisted of 209 patients with a median age of 57.4 years (range: 28–87), including 44 females (21%) and 165 males (79%). For analytical purposes, the participants were categorized into three distinct groups: a training cohort, an internal validation cohort, and an external validation cohort. The training cohort consisted of 112 patients (median age: 56.5), including 21.4% (24/112) females and 78.6% (88/112) males. The internal validation cohort included 46 patients (median age: 58.7), including 23.9% (11/46) females and 76.1% (35/46) males. The external validation cohort comprised 51 patients (median age: 57.2), including 19.6% (10/51) females and 80.4% (41/51) males. TACE non‑responders from institution A were 66 (47 training, 19 internal validation), and 17 from institution B (external validation). Cohen’s kappa for inter-observer agreement on mRECIST response was 0.81 and 0.83 (all P < 0.001), respectively, indicating excellent agreement in both centers. Per mRECIST criteria, TACE treatment responses demonstrated the following distribution: CR 18.2% (38/209), PR 42.1% (88/209), SD 33.5% (70/209), and PD 6.2% (13/209). Independent risk factors for TACE Treatment Response

Table 2 summarizes the results of both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses performed on clinical and body composition parameters within the training cohort. The univariate analysis revealed several statistically significant predictors: tumor number (P = 0.014), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) ≥ 400 ng/mL (P < 0.001), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (P = 0.010), VATI (P = 0.006), and VSR (P = 0.007). Multivariate logistic regression confirmed tumor number [odds ratio (OR) = 2.27, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.35–3.83; P = 0.004], AFP ≥ 400 ng/mL (OR = 3.12, 95% CI 1.85–5.33; P < 0.001), VATI (OR = 1.12, 95% CI 1.01–1.31; P < 0.001), and VSR (OR = 3.96, 95% CI 1.56–10.28; P = 0.002) as independent predictors of non TACE-response in HCC patients. Clinical and body composition model construction

Clinical prediction models incorporating AFP level and tumor number as independent predictors were developed, with corresponding ROC curves generated.

VATI and VSR served as independent variables for constructing body-fat prediction models, followed by ROC curve plotting. The clinical model demonstrated AUC values of 0.665 (95% CI 0.574–0.755), 0.712 (95% CI 0.637–0.787), and 0.638 (95% CI 0.587–0.700) in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively. Similarly, the body-fat model yielded AUCs of 0.762 (95% CI 0.682–0.842), 0.775 (95% CI 0.706–0.844), and 0.797 (95% CI 0.734–0.861) across the same respective cohorts.

Construction and evaluation of a combined model

A combined clinical-body fat prediction model was developed by integrating AFP level, tumor number, VATI, and VSR. The final model included above four independent predictors. In the training cohort, there were 47 non-response events, yielding an EPV of 11.75, which exceeds the recommended threshold of 10, indicating a low risk of model overfitting20. ROC curve analysis (Fig. 4A–C) indicated that this combined model exhibited superior predictive performance for TACE response across the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts. Specifically, it achieved an AUC of 0.805 (95% CI 0.730–0.880) in the training cohort, with a sensitivity of 75.3% and specificity of 82.5%. In the internal validation cohort, the AUC reached 0.829 (95% CI 0.769–0.889), accompanied by a sensitivity of 77.6% and specificity of 83.9%. Similarly, within the external validation cohort, the model attained an AUC of 0.815 (95% CI 0.752–0.877), demonstrating a sensitivity of 76.4% and specificity of 82.6%. The efficacy of the different models in predicting TACE response is summarized in Table 3. The calibration curve demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed TACE response across the training, internal validation and external validation cohorts (Fig. 4D–F). Subgroup analysis

The optimal cutoff value of 76.5 was derived from the Youden index using the combined model’s ROC curve in the training cohort. Patient total scores were calculated and stratified into high-risk (≥ 76.5) and low-risk (< 76.5) groups based on this cutoff. In training, internal validation and external validation cohorts, patients scoring < 76.5 were classified as low-risk, while those scoring ≥ 76.5 were classified as high-risk. TACE response rates differed significantly between low- and high-risk subgroups across all cohorts: training (18.5% vs. 89.1%; P < 0.001), internal validation (25.9% vs. 84.2%; P < 0.001) and external validation (20.6% vs. 88.2%; P < 0.001) (Table 4). Our analysis revealed significantly higher prevalence of TACE non-response in all high-risk subgroups compared to low-risk counterparts. These findings demonstrate the model’s clinical utility for risk-stratified identification and management of patients at elevated probability of TACE non-response.

Discussion

Non-invasive imaging is crucial for predicting survival in HCC patients treated with TACE; however, a robust model for accurately predicting TACE treatment efficacy is currently lacking. Our study developed a convenient non-invasive model for the preoperative prediction of tumor response in HCC patients undergoing TACE. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to establish an association between TACE response in HCC patients and body composition indices, specifically the VSR and VATI. The clinical model incorporating AFP and tumor number demonstrated suboptimal performance. However, integrating CT-derived body composition features—particularly VATI and VSR—significantly improved non-response prediction accuracy. Consequently, we developed and validated integrated models incorporating specific clinical, radiological, and adipose tissue metrics to stratify patients. Overall, the combined nomogram demonstrated superior risk stratification performance for TACE response compared to other models, facilitating personalized treatment planning.

TACE is the standard and effective treatment for intermediate-stage (BCLC stage B) HCC patients21. However, its efficacy may be limited by the potential development of non TACE-response in patients. As recommended by the Japanese Society for Hepatocellular Carcinoma (JSH)22, ineffective TACE should not be repeated in patients demonstrating non TACE-response. Instead, a shift to systemic therapies such as sorafenib is advised for these patients. Predicting response to TACE has led to the development of several clinical scoring systems, aiming to promptly identify high-risk patients and facilitate timely transition to combination therapies or molecular targeted agents. For instance, the ART score incorporates three parameters10: (1) an aspartate aminotransferase (AST) increase > 25% from baseline, (2) an elevation in the Child–Pugh score of either 1 or ≥ 2 points relative to baseline, and (3) a lack of radiologic tumor response. Patients demonstrating ART scores ≥ 2.5 before the second TACE session experience suboptimal clinical outcomes and derive limited benefit from additional TACE procedures. However, since radiologic response constitutes a component of the ART score itself, prospective validation studies incorporating the mRECIST are warranted to establish its clinical utility. In another study, Wang et al.7 developed a “six-and-twelve” model to predict individual prognosis with favorable discriminative ability. This model stratified patients into three prognostic tiers based on the sum of tumor size and number: ≤ 6 points, > 6 to ≤ 12 points, and > 12 points, demonstrating significantly distinct median overall survival of 49.1 months, 32.0 months, and 15.8 months, respectively. The “six-and-twelve” model proved to be an easily applicable tool recommended for prognostic stratification of TACE-treated patients. However, studies11,23 indicate suboptimal performance of both scoring systems upon external validation, necessitating further efforts to evaluate non TACE-response. Consequently, clinical decision-making for TACE in HCC patients can be significantly aided by a preoperative, quantitative prediction of treatment response.

Although body BMI is established as one of the most widely applied metrics for obesity classification, it constitutes an imprecise instrument for assessing body composition. CT permits accurate volumetric and mass-based quantification of body composition, enabling researchers to objectively characterize patient phenotypes. Moreover, given that CT scans represent standard clinical practice in HCC surveillance, they simultaneously provide precise, objective, and reproducible body composition metrics. In recent years, sarcopenia has emerged as one of the most extensively evaluated body composition abnormalities. Its presence has been demonstrated to correlate with adverse outcomes, recurrence, and overall complications in HCC patients24,25. In our study, neither SMI nor IMATI demonstrated significant associations with TACE response in HCC patients, warranting validation in larger cohorts. Our results revealed that elevated VSR and VATI correlated with TACE non-response, whereas SATI showed no such association. These findings indicate that adipose tissue distribution—rather than absolute quantity—constitutes the primary determinant of TACE response in HCC. Adipose tissue regulates the function of other organs by secreting adipokines. VAT differs significantly from SAT in terms of cytokine production. Accumulation of visceral fat increases the level of pro-inflammatory adipokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor-alpha, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 and interleukin 6) secreted by visceral adipocytes and decreases the level of anti-inflammatory adipokines (cytokine lipocalin), which are secreted by subcutaneous adipocytes26,27. Given that free fatty acids and adipokines released from visceral adipose tissue directly drain into the liver through the portal circulation, the hepatic microenvironment is profoundly impacted by these mediators. In contrast, subcutaneous adipose tissue confers hepatoprotective effects by efficiently sequestering excess lipids28. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the visceral-to-subcutaneous adipose tissue balance critically influences both the pathogenesis and progression of liver disease. Besides, Aromatase, abundantly expressed in adipose tissue (particularly visceral adipose depots), serves as the primary extragonadal enzyme responsible for converting adrenal androgens into biologically active estrogens. This endocrine activity establishes adipose tissue as a significant source of systemic estrogen, even in postmenopausal women and males. Critically, elevated estrogen levels resulting from this conversion activate estrogen receptor-mediated signaling pathways (e.g., ERα/STAT5, PI3K/AKT) within hepatocytes. These pathways promote proliferative, anti-apoptotic, and angiogenic effects that facilitate TACE non-response in HCC. Epidemiological and molecular studies29,30 further associate sustained high estrogen exposure with both increased HCC incidence—exhibiting marked male predominance due to their lower baseline estrogen levels—and aggressive tumor phenotypes characterized by vascular invasion, impaired treatment response, and reduced overall survival.

AFP and tumor number have been validated as independent risk factors for treatment response following TACE in HCC patients across multiple studies31–33, and remain incorporated parameters in established classical HCC prognostic scoring systems22. For clinical factors, the results showed that AFP and tumor number were independent risk factors associated with non TACE-response. The predictive model established based on above two clinical factors demonstrated suboptimal performance, with AUCs of 0.665 (95% CI 0.574–0.755), 0.712 (95% CI 0.637–0.787) and 0.638 (95% CI 0.587–0.700) in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively. For body composition, our study showed that VSR and VATI were independent risk factors associated with non TACE-response, aligning with previous study24 establishing elevated VAT as a significant risk factor for HCC development in male cirrhosis patients and post-liver transplantation recurrence. The body-fat prediction model established based on above two factors demonstrated moderate performance, with AUCs of 0.762 (95% CI 0.682–0.842), 0.775 (95% CI 0.706–0.844) and 0.797 (95% CI 0.734–0.861) in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively. The prediction model incorporating both clinical and body-fat independent risk factors demonstrated significantly improved predictive performance. Among all evaluated models, the four-parameter integrated model achieved optimal discrimination, with AUCs of 0.805 (95% CI 0.730–0.880) in the training cohort, 0.829 (95% CI 0.769–0.889) in the internal cohort, and 0.815 (95% CI 0.752–0.877) in the external validation cohort. Calibration curves demonstrated the robustness of the combined prognostic model. DCA confirmed its substantial clinical utility. Using established risk score thresholds, the high-risk group exhibited significantly higher rates of TACE non-response compared to the low-risk cohort in all three cohorts, validating the model’s capacity for stratifying non-response probability. These results collectively establish both clinical parameters and body composition metrics as critical determinants for predicting TACE treatment response.

The primary objective of the present study is to preoperatively identify HCC patients unlikely to respond to TACE. Whenever feasible, a single TACE session should be withheld from those with a predicted non-response. Furthermore, subsequent management must be tailored to individual disease trajectories: if the target lesion continues to progress after two TACE cycles, TACE should be discontinued and alternative therapies initiated. These may include systemic approaches—such as targeted agents or immune-checkpoint inhibitors—combined with locoregional modalities (e.g., hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy, yttrium-90 radioembolization, thermal ablation, or iodine-125 brachytherapy). When post-TACE vascular invasion or extrahepatic dissemination emerges, continuation of TACE for intrahepatic control remains valuable, ideally integrated with additional local treatments, including ablation, yttrium-90 radioembolization, and particle-based therapy, alongside systemic targeted plus immunotherapy combinations.

It is important to acknowledge several limitations in our study. A primary constraint is the limited sample size, potentially affecting the robustness of the statistical analyses. Furthermore, as an observational study, this research precludes definitive causal inference regarding the association between body composition and TACE response. Future investigations should incorporate mechanistic studies to elucidate underlying biological pathways. Thirdly, despite incorporating single-center external validation in our study, future research should employ multi-center cohorts to rigorously assess model robustness and generalizability. Finally, the present study was conducted in a cohort of predominantly hepatitis B virus-related HCC patients, which limits the generalizability of our findings to other HCC etiologies such as HCV infection, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, or alcoholic liver disease. The association between body composition and TACE response may vary across different etiologies due to differences in underlying liver function, tumor biology, and systemic metabolic status. Therefore, external validation in non-HBV HCC populations is warranted in future studies.

Conclusion

In summary, this study developed and validated a reliable preoperative noninvasive approach for predicting tumor response following TACE in HCC patients. This facilitates timely selection of personalized therapeutic strategies—such as combination therapies or molecular targeted agents—by interventional radiologists, thereby enhancing clinical outcomes for HCC patients undergoing TACE.
