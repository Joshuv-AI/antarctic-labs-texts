Trends and evolution of metabolic bariatric surgery over a decade: analysis of IFSO global registry reports’ data collection, clinical trends, and a FAIR-informed appraisal of report-level transparency
Authors: Mohamed Hany, Bart Torensma, Mohamed H. Zidan, Hashem Altabbaa, Ahmed Amgad, Ricardo V. Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe global burden of obesity continues to rise, and metabolic and bariatric surgery is the most effective intervention for durable weight loss and improvement of obesity-related diseases. The International Federation for the Surgery and Other Therapies for Obesity (IFSO) Global Registry was established in 2013 to provide standardised, multinational surveillance of MBS. No longitudinal synthesis of the successive IFSO Global Registry reports has been undertaken.MethodsWe performed a retrospective secondary analysis of all nine IFSO Global Registry reports published from 2014 to 2024; all source reports were accessed on October 18, 2025. Following RECORD principles, we prespecified core domains, harmonised definitions across editions, and documented transformations in a reproducible extraction log. Report-level patterns were described for registry participation, procedure volumes and mix, operative approach, patient demographics, perioperative outcomes, and methodological evolution. A FAIR-informed appraisal of report-level transparency was conducted using a pragmatic, registry-oriented rubric. Because the registry underwent a structural transition from individual-level or mixed reporting to aggregated national reporting between the sixth and seventh editions, analyses were structured into two non-equivalent reporting eras: Reports 1–6 and Reports 7–9. Cross-era comparisons were therefore interpreted as descriptive contrasts shaped by reporting architecture, rather than as continuous epidemiological trends.FindingsReported metabolic and bariatric surgery activity ranged from 100,092 operations across 18 countries in the First Report to 598,736 operations across 36 countries in the Ninth Report, with the highest submitted volume in the Fifth Report. These values reflect changing contributor networks and reporting frameworks and should not be interpreted as directly comparable estimates of global procedural incidence. Within submitted primary-procedure data, Sleeve gastrectomy increased from 22.0% in the First Report to 60.4% in the Ninth Report, while Roux-en-Y gastric bypass decreased from 66.0% in the First Report to 29.7% in the Ninth Report; adjustable gastric banding became uncommon, and one-anastomosis gastric bypass accounted for 4–7% where reported. Median age and body mass index were broadly stable, and Female patients represented 71.3–79.5% of patients in reports with comparable sex reporting. Revisional procedures showed higher rates of unplanned reoperation, intensive care unit admission, readmission, and mortality than primary procedures. Report-level Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable transparency scores improved modestly, mainly through gains in findability and accessibility, while interoperability and reusability remained limited.InterpretationAcross successive public reports, sleeve gastrectomy became the most frequently reported primary procedure within submitted registry data, with substantial regional heterogeneity and important limitations in cross-edition comparability. Further harmonisation of definitions, improved follow-up completeness, and privacy-preserving access to more granular data would enhance the registry’s value for benchmarking, policy, and patient-level research.FundingNo specific funding was received for this study.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

The International Federation for the Surgery and Other Therapies for Obesity (IFSO) Global Registry has published successive public reports describing submitted metabolic and bariatric surgery activity, procedure distribution, patient characteristics, outcomes, and registry reporting practices. Before undertaking this analysis, we accessed the official IFSO Global Registry webpage and downloaded all publicly available registry reports from the First through the Ninth Report; the webpage was last accessed on October 18, 2025. These reports were the source documents for the present study.

To determine whether these reports had previously been synthesised longitudinally, we also searched PubMed/MEDLINE and Google Scholar from database inception to October 18, 2025, without language restrictions. Search terms included “IFSO Global Registry”, “International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity and Metabolic Disorders”, “bariatric surgery registry”, “metabolic and bariatric surgery registry”, “global bariatric surgery”, “worldwide bariatric surgery survey”, “sleeve gastrectomy trends”, “Roux-en-Y gastric bypass trends”, “registry report”, “FAIR principles”, “data transparency”, and “data reusability”. We also screened the reference lists of the IFSO reports and relevant registry-related articles. We did not identify a prior study that synthesised all publicly available IFSO Global Registry reports across successive editions while evaluating cross-edition comparability, methodological evolution, and report-level transparency.

Added value of this study

This study analyses all publicly available IFSO Global Registry reports together to describe how reported metabolic and bariatric surgery activity, procedure mix, regional practice patterns, patient characteristics, perioperative outcomes, and registry reporting evolved across the last decade. It explicitly accounts for the structural transition between earlier individual-level or mixed reporting and later aggregated national or regional reporting, avoiding the assumption that all editions represent a single continuous epidemiological time series. The study also provides a FAIR-informed appraisal of the transparency and reusability of the published reports themselves, rather than of the underlying registry datasets.

Implications of all the available evidence

The IFSO Global Registry reports provide an important public record of global metabolic and bariatric surgery practice, but interpretation across editions is limited by changing contributor networks, evolving denominator definitions, aggregated reporting, and inconsistent availability of several clinical and outcome variables. Future registry reporting would be strengthened by clearer documentation of denominators, harmonized variable definitions, explicit reporting of data completeness, and privacy-preserving mechanisms for more granular research access. These improvements would enhance the value of the registry for benchmarking, policy planning, quality improvement, and patient-level research.

Introduction

The global obesity epidemic is a major public health challenge, impacting over 650 million adults and causing significant morbidity and healthcare costs.1 Metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) is the most effective obesity treatment, achieving better long-term outcomes compared to medical management.2, 3, 4

Founded in 1995, the International Federation for the Surgery and Other Therapies for Obesity (IFSO) has been pivotal in advancing MBS standards globally. In 2013, IFSO established its Global Registry, which has since evolved from a pilot in 18 countries to a multinational database enabling comparative analysis of surgical practices and outcomes.

The registry has grown in scope, with updates adding new procedures and refining variable definitions while evolving data governance and privacy practices. Compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) prompted a shift from individual-level submissions to aggregated national and regional data, focusing on standardising datasets and harmonising definitions. The Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR) principles have become an increasingly relevant framework for evaluating data stewardship in large-scale registries.5

To date, no comprehensive synthesis of IFSO Global Registry reports over time has been published; existing studies focus on individual editions or specific chapters. A decade-long analysis is needed to understand the evolution of registry participation, surgical practices, and gaps in data quality.

This study examines all nine IFSO Global Registry reports (2014–2024) with the following aims: (1) to track changes in registry participation and procedure volumes; (2) to analyse trends in surgical techniques and patient demographics; (3) to assess regional differences in clinical practices; (4) to summarise perioperative outcomes and early weight-loss patterns; (5) to outline the registry’s methodological and governance evolution; and (6) to conduct a FAIR-informed appraisal of the transparency and documentation of the published reports.

Methods

Study design and data

We conducted a retrospective analysis of IFSO Global Registry reports, following the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely collected health Data (RECORD) extension of Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) for secondary use of routinely collected data.6 We adapted our approach to the mixed format of successive editions (aggregated tables, summary statistics, and graphical outputs). Core domains of interest were defined before data extraction: participation and volume timelines, primary vs. revisional surgery distributions, procedure-type evolution, regional practice variations, operative approaches, burden and remission of obesity-related diseases, length of stay, perioperative complications, and a FAIR-informed appraisal of report-level transparency.5

We included the first through the ninth IFSO Global Registry reports (2014–2024),7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 sourced from the official IFSO website (https://www.ifso.com/ifso-registry.php, which was last accessed on October 18, 2025). Each report was treated as a self-contained snapshot for its respective year. The reports represent publicly available summaries of submitted registry data and should not be interpreted as complete population-level estimates of global MBS activity. Representativeness, completeness, and coverage varied across editions, contributors, countries, procedures, variables, and follow-up intervals. No report was excluded based on contributor structure or completeness, to describe the full evolution of the public IFSO Registry reporting program. Due to methodological changes over time, we did not reconstruct a unified patient-level cohort but instead created a longitudinal series of edition-level metrics by aligning definitions and denominators as detailed in each report. Early reports were therefore interpreted as submitted registry data rather than nationally representative estimates, particularly where single-center or limited-center submissions represented countries.

Variables and definitions

Registry participation and procedural volume were abstracted from each edition, noting the number of contributing countries and total operations recorded. Procedure intent was classified as primary or revisional surgery based on each reports definitions; published counts and percentages were used, with counts back-calculated when only proportions were available.

Procedure type followed a harmonised taxonomy: sleeve gastrectomy, Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), adjustable gastric band (AGB), biliopancreatic diversion (BPD), duodenal switch (DS), one-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB), single-anastomosis duodeno-ileal bypass with sleeve (SADI-S), and an “other” category. Procedures labelled in source reports as “mini-gastric bypass,” “single-anastomosis gastric bypass,” or “OAGB/MGB” were harmonised under OAGB for analysis.

Patient case-mix variables (age, sex, BMI, and associated medical problems) were abstracted as published. When only ranges or medians were available, we reported them without imputing standard deviations or confidence intervals, maintaining the ecological integrity of edition-level summaries.

Data extraction

Two investigators independently reviewed all nine reports and extracted quantitative data from tables, figures, and appendices into a structured template. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion and consensus, with a third reviewer consulted when agreement could not be reached. For numeric values presented only in graphical form, WebPlotDigitizer (version 4.6) was used to digitise coordinates, and these were verified against textual descriptions within each report.

The dataset included participating countries; procedural volumes by primary or revisional status and procedure type; regional distributions of procedure type, BMI, and age; associated medical problems and remission; perioperative complications; mortality; length of stay; and Obesity Surgery Mortality Risk Score (OS-MRS) categories where available.16 Data availability was assessed at the report-variable level rather than the patient level, because the source material consisted of published aggregate IFSO Global Registry reports. For each major domain, we recorded whether the variable was reported across the nine editions and whether definitions and denominators were sufficiently comparable for descriptive synthesis. Submitted procedure volume and contributing-country counts were available in all reports, whereas regional data, remission outcomes, length of stay, one-year weight-loss outcomes, perioperative complications, and OS-MRS categories were available only in selected editions or under changing definitions. Unavailable data were therefore not considered missing at random, but primarily structural and report-dependent, reflecting changes in registry architecture, procedure taxonomy, variable definitions, denominator construction, contributing countries, data governance, and follow-up reporting. No imputation was performed; variables were analysed only when explicitly reported or transparently derivable, and non-reported or non-comparable values were treated as unavailable. Report-level availability for major extracted variables is summarised in the Appendix.

FAIR-informed appraisal of report-level transparency

We conducted a FAIR-informed appraisal of the publicly available reports, distinct from a FAIR assessment of the underlying datasets. Specifically, we evaluated what could be observed from the published reports themselves: documentation quality, definition transparency, report accessibility, and data-dictionary availability. We did not evaluate—and could not assess from published reports alone—the underlying data provenance, completeness rates of individual country contributions, or governance of raw data transfers between national registries and IFSO.

We operationalised the FAIR principles using a pragmatic, registry-oriented rubric adapted from Wilkinson et al.5 This rubric is not a validated psychometric instrument, but an exploratory tool designed to systematically describe observable features of the published reports. For each dimension, we defined indicators: for findability, regularity of report release, explicit listing of participating countries and registries, clarity of reporting years and procedure counts, and presence of structured identifiers; for accessibility, descriptions of data-protection constraints and public routes for accessing reports; for interoperability, existence of a minimum core dataset, data dictionary, and harmonised definitions; and for reusability, completeness documentation, auditing information, licensing or conditions for secondary analysis, and availability of open datasets. Each dimension was scored 0–100, with higher scores reflecting more explicit documentation, standardisation, and openness. We aligned the rubric’s domain structure with established FAIR-evaluation approaches used in biomedical registries, including the FAIR Data Maturity Model and FAIRshake frameworks,17,18 adapting indicators and thresholds to the constraints of report-level assessment. To improve transparency, the supplementary Appendix now provides the complete FAIR-informed rubric structure, including the four FAIR domains, observable report-level indicators, and plain-language interpretation of each indicator.

To improve reproducibility, two reviewers independently applied the FAIR-informed rubric to all nine reports before consensus adjudication. Each FAIR domain was scored on a 0–100 scale using predefined anchors: 0 indicated absence of the relevant feature; 25 indicated minimal or implicit reporting; 50 indicated partial reporting; 75 indicated structured but incomplete reporting; and 100 indicated complete, explicit, and reusable reporting. The overall FAIR score was calculated as the unweighted mean of the four domain scores.

Inter-rater agreement was assessed before consensus using a two-way mixed-effects, absolute-agreement, single-measures intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), because the same two raters evaluated all reports. Average-measures ICCs were also calculated to describe the reliability of the two-rater mean. To examine sensitivity to scoring assumptions, we compared the revised item-level scores with the prior appendix scores and calculated mean differences and mean absolute differences. The interpretation was considered robust if the direction of change and the main domain-level conclusions were unchanged.

Handling heterogeneity across editions

Due to changes in registry architecture, participating countries, and variable definitions over time, we took a conservative approach for harmonisation. Procedural volumes were normalised to total operations per edition, with procedure types shown as relative shares (%). If a procedure category (e.g., OAGB or SADI-S) was not included in earlier editions, it was coded as “not reported” rather than zero.

For metrics such as Obesity Surgery Mortality Risk Score (OS-MRS) distribution, length of stay, remission rates, and complications, we restricted analyses to editions in which the relevant data were clearly defined and internally consistent. We did not combine numerators and denominators across editions when case definitions differed, or follow-up windows were not comparable.

Structural break. We identified a structural break between the sixth and seventh registry editions, corresponding to the transition from individual-level or mixed clinic-level submissions to exclusively aggregated national reporting. This transition changed the unit of observation, the denominator definition, the inclusion criteria, and the analytic framework. Accordingly, analyses spanning this break point should be interpreted as descriptive contrasts across non-equivalent reporting frameworks rather than as a continuous time series. In all trend figures, the break point is indicated by a vertical marker.

Ethics

This study was based exclusively on publicly available, aggregate, report-level data from successive IFSO Global Registry reports. No individual-patient-level data were accessed, no identifiable data were used, and no new data were collected from participants. Formal ethics committee approval was therefore not required, and no approval reference number was applicable. Informed consent was not required because the study did not involve direct participant contact or the use of identifiable individual-level data.

Statistics

Descriptive statistics were generated using Microsoft Excel and IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25.0). Continuous variables are presented as means or medians with ranges or confidence intervals where available, according to the summary format provided in each source report; categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Because individual-patient-level data were unavailable, normality was not formally assessed. Report-level patterns were summarised using tables and figures, with absolute values, proportions, and percentage-point differences presented where denominators were clearly reported. No parametric or non-parametric inferential tests were applied to continuous variables.

Given the aggregated, heterogeneous nature of the data and the structural break between reporting eras, no formal inferential statistics were applied to cross-edition comparisons. Formal segmented regression and joinpoint analysis were considered but not performed because the reports do not represent repeated measurements from a stable population or a consistent denominator. The small number of edition-level observations, changing contributor composition, variable definitions, and the presence of only three post-transition reports would make model-based estimates potentially misleading.

Accordingly, analyses were limited to descriptive within-era and cross-era contrasts, without p-values or formal estimates of temporal slope. These contrasts should be interpreted as report-level summaries rather than inferential trend estimates. Subgroup analyses focused on relative patterns in procedure preference, BMI, age, and revisional surgery rates across IFSO chapters where comparable denominators were available.

Reliability analyses for the FAIR-informed appraisal were treated separately from temporal trend analyses. Inter-rater agreement for FAIR scores was assessed using ICCs. Mean differences and mean absolute differences between the revised item-level scores and prior appendix scores were calculated as a sensitivity analysis of scoring assumptions.

Role of funding source

No specific funding was received for this study. There was no funder involvement in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, manuscript writing, or the decision to submit the manuscript for publication.

Results

The First Report included 100,092 submitted operations from 18 countries, whereas the Ninth Report included 598,736 submitted operations from 36 countries (Appendix Table S1). Because contributor composition, denominator definitions, and reporting architecture changed across editions, these values are interpreted as report-level submitted activity rather than directly comparable estimates of global MBS incidence.

The data collection trajectory was divided into three phases: rapid expansion (2014–2018) with the addition of new datasets, peak participation in 2019, and subsequent decline followed by recovery in 2023–2024 (Appendix Fig. S1). The Fifth Report11 recorded the highest reach with 833,687 operations from 61 countries, while the Seventh Report13 showed a drop to 311,441 procedures from 23 countries. This decline reflects both pandemic-related service disruption and the concurrent structural transition to aggregated national reporting, which mechanically changed what was counted and by whom; the two effects cannot be separated cleanly from these data.

Participation reflected global disparities in registry maturity (Fig. 1A), with the European Chapter (EC) showing consistent dominance across editions. Early reports (2014–2018) showed variability in the reported distribution of primary and revisional procedures, with revisional surgery representing 106,526/196,188 (54.3%) in the Third Report and 204,254/394,431 (51.8%) in the Fourth Report. These values are unlikely to be directly comparable with later reports because the definition and ascertainment of primary, revisional, and conversion procedures changed over time, and some early datasets may have included overlapping or inconsistently categorised entries.

In the aggregated-reporting era, primary procedures represented 278,581/311,441 (89.5%) in the Seventh Report, 449,815/502,150 (89.6%) in the Eighth Report, and 543,000/598,736 (90.7%) in the Ninth Report (Appendix Table S1). This apparent stabilisation should therefore be interpreted primarily as a reporting-architecture and denominator effect, rather than as evidence that revisional surgery became intrinsically less frequent at a global level.

The procedure mix changed over time. In 2014, RYGB was the most common procedure (35,840/53,197 (67.3%) of operations), while sleeve gastrectomy comprised 10,713/53,197 (20.1%). By 2018, sleeve gastrectomy had surpassed RYGB and has since accounted for 87,015/189,719 (45.9%) of primary procedures, while RYGB declined to 72,639/189,719 (38.3%). Throughout the reporting period, median age at surgery remained stable (40–43 years), with females comprising 71.3–79.5% of surgical patients (Appendix Table S4). Preoperative BMI ranged from 41 to 45 kg/m2, with higher values in NAC populations.

Remission data were available from the third to sixth editions9, 10, 11, 12 (Appendix Table S5). Importantly, remission definitions varied across editions (e.g., medication cessation vs. biochemical criteria), and denominators, follow-up completeness, and contributing registries also differed. Therefore, these data are presented as edition-specific descriptive summaries and cannot be interpreted as pooled estimates or temporal trends.

For sleeve gastrectomy, reported T2DM remission ranged from 23,874/39,137 (61.0%) in the Third Report to 65,732/128,382 (51.2%) in the Sixth, with similar patterns for hypertension, dyslipidemia, and OSA. Depression and musculoskeletal pain remission fluctuated, while GERD values showed negative trends in later reports. For RYGB, T2DM, and OSA remission consistently exceeded those of sleeve gastrectomy, while hypertension and dyslipidemia showed modest differences.9, 10, 11, 12

OAGB remission data from the Fifth and Sixth Reports11,12 indicated higher rates for T2DM, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and OSA in 2019 than in 2021. Early combined analyses from the First and Second Reports7,8 indicated remission rates over 80% for most categories, reflecting different criteria and follow-up structures.

From 2014 to 2021, laparoscopic procedures dominated and reached nearly 100% by 2019 (Appendix Fig. S3). Open surgery remained below 2–3% and was concentrated in RYGB and complex revisional operations. Laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy was consistently the most common individual operation, followed by laparoscopic RYGB. Endoscopic and endoluminal therapies appeared in limited volumes (∼1% of total interventions), reflecting early adoption (Appendix Fig. S3).

Obesity Surgery Mortality Risk Score (OS-MRS) distributions, available for 2014–2019,7, 8, 9, 10, 11 showed overall stability with some fluctuation between risk groups (Appendix Table S6). Group A (lowest risk) generally formed the majority but its proportion varied substantially (54.3–63.5%), though the data cannot distinguish whether case-mix changes, reporting completeness, or registry participation accounted for these shifts.

Length-of-stay data, reported in the first to sixth reports,7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 are presented as edition-specific administrative summaries (Appendix Table S7). For sleeve gastrectomy, same-day discharge ranged from 212/88,333 (0.24%) to 6456/304,528 (2.12%), while one-day stays ranged from 3826/37,994 (10.07%) to 117,711/305,267 (38.56%). For RYGB, same-day discharge ranged from 80/25,806 (0.31%) to 1157/162,958 (0.71%), and one-day stays ranged from 7823/35,820 (21.84%) to 45,223/94,313 (47.95%). AGB consistently showed the highest proportion of same-day and one-day discharges (Appendix Table S7). Because definitions of same-day discharge, admission duration, procedure mix, and contributing registries may differ across editions, these values should not be interpreted as a pooled longitudinal trend.

1-year percentage weight loss, reported only in the third to sixth reports,9, 10, 11, 12 increased progressively across BMI categories for sleeve gastrectomy, RYGB, and OAGB (Appendix Table S8). RYGB generally exceeded sleeve gastrectomy by a small but consistent margin across BMI strata. OAGB produced comparable or higher percentage weight-loss values in the highest BMI categories.

Complication rates were reported across the Seventh to Ninth Reports,13, 14, 15 with revisional procedures consistently demonstrating higher values than primary surgery (Appendix Table S9). Unplanned return to theatre occurred in 3970/342,241 (1.16%) – 746/55,672 (1.34%) of primary cases vs. 1062/38,618 (2.75%) – 1382/21,035 (6.57%) of revisional cases. Unplanned ICU admission ranged from 2188/303,889 (0.72%) – 468/42,545 (1.10%) (primary) to 81/6480 (1.25%) – 463/34,812 (1.33%) (revisional). Hospital readmission occurred in 1216/45,887 (2.65%) – 9874/324,803 (3.04%) of primary and 653/12,754 (5.12%) – 2238/36,870 (6.07%) of revisional procedures. Absolute mortality rates remained low: 21/70,000 (0.03%) – 203/135,333 (0.15%) for primary operations and 4/8000 (0.05%) – 95/21,591 (0.44%) for revisional surgery.

In the early phase, data were collected from national registries and individual centres using Dendrite’s platforms, with a contributor charter for data protection and validation. During the middle phase, the registry expanded to over 50 countries, supported by uniform upload protocols and collaboration with the University of Bristol to standardise MBS registry inputs internationally, improving data completeness to over 70%. In the recent phase, the registry shifted to an aggregated-data model, reporting only national and regional summaries to comply with GDPR and reduce re-identification risks. The IFSO/Bristol Core Dataset, comprising 12 standardised variables, has been adopted globally since 2021. The transition to a hybrid REDCap-based pipeline has improved operational metrics, though follow-up completeness declined to 45% (Fig. 5A).

The report-level FAIR appraisal showed gradual but incomplete improvement across editions, with composite scores rising from 45/100 in early reports to 59/100 in the Ninth Report.15 Findability scores increased from 50 to 63, reflecting improved metadata and documentation. Accessibility rose from 55 to 66 due to clearer data-governance descriptions, though all editions relied on report-level summaries rather than machine-readable datasets. Interoperability saw minimal improvement (35–52), hampered by inconsistent definitions. Reusability increased from 40 to 55, aided by improved methodology documentation, yet remained limited by incomplete follow-up and the absence of openly available datasets. The overall average across all editions was 57/100 (Appendix Tables S11 and S12). To improve interpretability, the complete rubric structure, scoring anchors, domain-level scores, and inter-rater reliability results are now provided in the Supplementary appendix.

Inter-rater agreement for the overall FAIR score was high. The two-way mixed-effects, absolute-agreement ICC was 0.859 for single measures (95% CI, 0.414–0.968) and 0.924 for average measures (95% CI, 0.586–0.984). Domain-level agreement was more variable: single-measures ICCs were 0.578 for findability, 0.818 for accessibility, 0.614 for interoperability, and 0.318 for reusability. The lower agreement for reusability was consistent with the more interpretive nature of judging licensing, auditability, secondary-use conditions, and open-data availability from published reports alone (Appendix Table S13).

In the sensitivity comparison between revised item-level scores and prior appendix scores, the mean overall difference was +1.83 points, and the mean absolute difference was 1.94 points on the 0–100 scale. The direction of the overall FAIR trajectory was unchanged, supporting the robustness of the main descriptive interpretation while reinforcing the need for cautious interpretation of individual domain scores.

These scores reflect the observable transparency features of the published reports and should not be interpreted as an assessment of the underlying data governance, provenance, or completeness of the registry’s infrastructure.

Discussion

This study synthesises the publicly available IFSO Global Registry reports from 2014 to 2024, but the findings should not be read as a single uninterrupted epidemiological time series. The registry underwent a major architectural transition between the sixth and seventh reports, moving from individual-level or mixed reporting to aggregated national/regional reporting. Accordingly, the observed patterns are best interpreted as report-level descriptions across two non-equivalent eras, shaped by changes in contributor composition, denominator definitions, case ascertainment, and reporting governance. Within this framework, sleeve gastrectomy became the dominant procedure, increasing from 22.0% in the First Report to 60.4% in the Ninth Report, though regional variations persist, while RYGB and AGB represented smaller shares in later reports. However, these observations describe submitted registry reports rather than global procedural incidence.

COVID-19 and the expanding availability of incretin-based pharmacotherapies should be considered plausible contextual factors when interpreting recent report-level changes in MBS activity. The COVID-19 pandemic directly disrupted elective surgical services in many settings, and the IFSO reports describe pandemic-related reductions in operative activity and variable recovery across countries.19 This interpretation is supported by external registry data: the UK National Bariatric Surgery Registry reported that elective bariatric surgery volume fell to one-third of pre-pandemic levels, from 8615 to 2930 cases, while MBSAQIP data showed a 12.1% reduction in total elective bariatric surgery cases and a 13.8% reduction in cases per accredited centre during 2020.20,21 The Scandinavian Obesity Surgery Registry Norway–Sweden also reported significantly reduced numbers of operated and reported patients during 2020–2021, most likely due to the pandemic.22

More recently, GLP-1 receptor agonists and related pharmacotherapies have become increasingly relevant to obesity care and may influence referral patterns, patient preferences, treatment sequencing, and surgical demand.23,24 However, the present report-level analysis cannot isolate or quantify the independent contribution of COVID-19, pharmacotherapy uptake, changes in contributing countries, reimbursement structures, or registry architecture. These factors should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating explanatory contexts, rather than as demonstrated causal drivers of the observed changes in submitted surgical volume.

The IFSO Global Registry evolved through three phases, each leaving a recognisable imprint on the observable FAIR profile of the published reports. In the initial phase (2014–2017), the Registry acted as a hybrid repository, combining center-level or patient-level data with limited national aggregates. This led to significant variability in variable definitions across contributors.7, 8, 9 Documentation and metadata were relatively sparse, constraining both findability and interoperability in the published reports.

The middle phase (2018–2020) saw rapid international growth and efforts toward harmonisation through collaboration with the University of Bristol for a core dataset.10, 11, 12 Findability and accessibility improved with more structured reports and clearer variable definitions. However, interoperability gains were limited because associated medical problems, definitions, complication grading, and follow-up outcomes were not fully aligned across contributing registries. Dissemination remained through static report formats, keeping reusability for independent secondary analysis low.

The enforcement of GDPR in May 2018 materially increased the legal and operational burden of cross-border transfer of individual-level health data.25 Consistent with the broader European experience, the IFSO Registry pivoted by 2021–2022 to an exclusively aggregated national/regional reporting model.13, 14, 15,25 Under this framework, contributors submit summary statistics using a shared dictionary, eliminating the transfer of raw patient records while increasing representativeness through prioritising mature national registries.13,14 This redesign coincided with the adoption of the IFSO/Bristol Core Dataset: 12 internationally ratified minimum items spanning demographics, procedure variables, and core effectiveness/safety outcomes.26

The observable FAIR profile of the published reports improved in parallel with the structural transitions described above. Findability and accessibility improved most clearly in Phase 3 as core variables and definitions became explicit and consistently documented.14,26 Interoperability improved modestly: while the mandatory core dataset reduced definitional drift in key variables, non-core items remain variably defined, and the aggregated-table format is not inherently machine-readable.13,14 Reusability remains the most constrained domain, since aggregated reporting precludes patient-level re-analysis or multivariable modelling (Appendix Table S11).

Composite report-level FAIR scores increased from the mid-40s in early editions to 59/100 by 2024, with the largest increase in findability and accessibility. Whether these improvements were directly caused by the architectural transitions or occurred in parallel due to broader trends in data stewardship cannot be determined from the present analysis.

Based on the report-level patterns observed in this analysis, several areas for potential future development emerge. First, the data suggest that enforcing a harmonised international taxonomy for procedures, outcomes, and clinical endpoints would strengthen cross-edition and cross-country comparability. Clear distinctions between primary procedures, conversions, and revisions, alongside uniform remission criteria, could reduce misclassification in future editions.

Second, the observed ceiling in interoperability and reusability scores points toward opportunities for more interoperable data architecture. Public, version-controlled data dictionaries with transparent change logs and machine-readable outputs could strengthen these dimensions while remaining compliant with GDPR.

Third, the persistent reusability constraints inherent to aggregate reporting suggest that a controlled pathway for qualified access to de-identified line-level data could enhance the registry’s research utility. A tiered access model—with routine aggregate reporting as the default and patient-level analysis available through a secure data enclave for approved projects—would preserve privacy while enabling analyses that summary statistics alone cannot support.

Fourth, the evolving interface between surgery and obesity pharmacotherapy suggests that future editions would benefit from linkage to medication or claims databases, to distinguish genuine shifts in surgical demand from pharmacotherapy-driven trends.23,24,27

Several limitations should be considered. First, the structural transition from individual-level to aggregated national reporting between the sixth and seventh editions represents a fundamental change in the unit of observation, the denominator definition, the contributor composition, and the case ascertainment. Therefore, pre-transition (pre-2021) and post-transition (post-2021) data are not directly comparable. Any apparent cross-era increase, decrease, stabilisation, or recovery should be regarded as a descriptive report-level pattern rather than a confirmed epidemiological trend. Valid patient-level prediction modelling, causal inference, or adjusted temporal analysis would require access to harmonised individual-level data, consistent definitions, and stable denominator structures.

Second, the FAIR appraisal evaluates what is observable from the published reports and does not assess the underlying data infrastructure, governance, provenance, or completeness of individual country contributions. The rubric used is a pragmatic, non-validated, report-level tool rather than a validated psychometric instrument. Although duplicate scoring showed high agreement for the overall FAIR score and sensitivity analyses showed minimal differences between revised item-level and prior appendix scores, domain-level agreement varied, particularly for reusability. Therefore, the FAIR scores should be interpreted as structured descriptive indicators of report transparency rather than definitive measures of registry quality.

Third, remission rates for associated medical problems were used as non-standardised definitions across editions and cannot be compared as a temporal trend.

Fourth, the decline in procedural volumes after 2019 conflates pandemic-related service disruption with the structural transition to aggregated reporting; these effects cannot be disentangled.

Fifth, representativeness and contributor composition varied across editions. In earlier reports, some countries were represented by one or a small number of centres rather than population-level registries. In contrast, later reports were restricted to aggregated national/regional registry submissions. Apparent changes in procedure distribution, case mix, and outcomes may therefore partly reflect changes in which countries and registries contributed data, rather than true changes in clinical practice alone. These reports were retained to preserve the complete public evolution of the IFSO Global Registry, but were interpreted as report-level snapshots rather than nationally representative or country-weighted estimates. Although contributing countries were generally identifiable, the public reports did not provide consistently harmonised country-level denominators, contributor coverage, case ascertainment, variable completeness, and outcome definitions across all editions; therefore, reliable country-level weighting or decomposition of contributor effects was not possible. Although the later shift to aggregated national/regional reporting reduced single-centre representation, residual bias may remain due to differences in case ascertainment, follow-up completeness, auditing, and variable definitions.

To conclude, through successive public reports, the IFSO Global Registry has documented report-level changes in submitted MBS activity, procedure mix, regional variation, and registry architecture. Sleeve gastrectomy is now the most frequently reported primary procedure, while other procedures vary substantially across editions and regions. However, because the Registry transitioned to aggregated national/regional reporting, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive patterns across non-equivalent reporting eras rather than as continuous estimates of global epidemiological change. COVID-19 and the expanding GLP-1 pharmacotherapy era are plausible contextual factors, but their independent effects cannot be isolated from the available report-level data.

The Registry has matured through improved standardisation, clearer governance, and greater alignment with GDPR requirements, although challenges remain in balancing privacy protection with analytical depth. Future development should prioritise harmonised definitions, version-controlled data dictionaries, machine-readable outputs, improved follow-up completeness, and privacy-preserving access to more granular data. These steps would strengthen the Registry’s value for benchmarking, policy development, and integration of surgery, pharmacotherapy, and long-term chronic disease management.
