Assessing collection and trapping methods for diagnostics and surveillance of psyllids (Hemiptera: Psylloidea) in and around citrus orchards
Authors: Michael L Edwards, Mark J Blacket, Brendan C Rodoni, Jessica C Lye, Francesco Martoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe economic impacts of Huanglongbing disease have prompted global surveillance of citrus psyllid vectors. While yellow sticky traps (YSTs) are widely used to detect pests such as the African citrus psyllid (Trioza erytreae) and the Asian citrus psyllid (Diaphorina citri), alternative methods may capture unique species and greater taxa richness and abundance, which could impact downstream species identification and diagnostics. We assessed psyllid richness and abundance across 4 sites within major citrus-growing regions of eastern Australia, and found that combining hand sampling with YSTs captured more species compared to light trapping and pan traps, but all sampling techniques collected unique taxa. Species were identified via an integrative taxonomy approach combining host plant data, morphology, and DNA sequences. We generated 196 cytochrome oxidase reference sequences from 87 species, 27 genera, and 5 families. Of these, 65 morphospecies and a described species were previously unrecorded in public DNA databases. This allowed us to build a baseline reference for eastern Australian native psyllids found around citrus orchard environments, thereby enhancing species-level molecular identification, and strengthening surveillance and biosecurity in Australia.

Introduction

Psyllids (Hemiptera: Sternorrhyncha: Psylloidea), or jumping plant lice, include around 4,000 described species worldwide (Ouvrard 2020). Australia is a psyllid biodiversity hotspot (Hollis 2004, Ouvrard 2020) with at least 450 known species, 414 of which are formally described and hundreds awaiting description (Martoni et al. 2024). Globally, many psyllid species exhibit strong host plant specificity (Hodkinson 1974, Burckhardt and Basset 2000, Percy et al. 2004, Ouvrard et al. 2015), and over 40 species are considered plant pests (Percy 2014, Martoni and Blacket 2021).

Among these pests, the African citrus psyllid, Trioza erytreae (Del Guercio, 1918), and the Asian citrus psyllid (ACP), Diaphorina citri Kuwayama, 1908, vector plant pathogenic Candidatus Liberibacter species (Jagoueix et al. 1994, Teixeira et al. 2005, Manjunath et al. 2008), the causative agents of Huanglongbing, a devastating citrus disease responsible for billions of dollars in global losses (Boa 2023). Australia remains free of these exotic pest psyllids but ACP is present in neighboring countries such as Indonesia and Papua New Guinea (Monzó Ferrer and Vanaclocha 2022, Vanaclocha and Monzó Ferrer 2022), and has been intercepted at the Australian border in recent years (Inspector-General 2019).

The Australian citrus industry is currently in a strong biosecurity position since there is no psyllid species in Australia that is known to use citrus as a host plant (Ouvrard 2020, Martoni et al. 2024). Further, no exotic Candidatus Liberibacter species are known to be present in Australia (CABI 2022, Cook et al. 2022, Wang 2022). A native Candidatus Liberibacter species has been reported in the country, Ca. L. brunswickensis, but is not a plant pathogen (Morris et al. 2017, 2024). To maintain this pest- and pathogen-free status requires surveillance programs to continuously monitor for the presence of exotic species (Floyd et al. 2010, Hodgetts et al. 2016, IPPC 2016, 2018).

Therefore, effective collection and trapping of psyllids is essential, but it may be biased by the methods adopted. Numerous studies have collected psyllids using diverse methods such as hand sampling (Burts and Brunner 1981, Tedeschi et al. 2008, Cooper et al. 2019), light trapping (Sétamou et al. 2012, Hodge et al. 2019, Martoni et al. 2020), ground-based pan traps (Miles et al. 1997, Eatough Jones and Paine 2012, Yen et al. 2012, Percy et al. 2020, Ullah et al. 2023), sticky traps (Zartaloudis et al. 2007, Cameron et al. 2009, Hall 2009, Yen et al. 2012, Monzó et al. 2015), and active suction traps (Teulon et al. 2009, Sjölund et al. 2019, Greenslade et al. 2020, Martoni et al. 2023).

Each method offers strengths depending on the study goals. For example, light trapping can detect nocturnally active species (Martoni et al. 2020), and hand sampling provides ecological context by linking specimens to host plants and habitats (Zartaloudis et al. 2007). Yen et al. (2012) assessed multiple methods (yellow sticky traps [YSTs], water traps, and hand sampling) to detect the pest Tomato Potato Psyllid, Bactericera cockerelli (Šulc, 1909), in New Zealand crops and shelterbelts, but this study did not test light traps. Since then, recent surveys in Australia suggested some psyllid taxa are attracted to light (Martoni et al. 2020). Together, these studies demonstrate the feasibility of sampling psyllids using various methods, but none has compared psyllid richness and abundance in the habitats around Australian citrus orchards by combining multiple techniques.

Traditional psyllid identification involves examination of key morphological characters and remains a commonly used approach to psyllid diagnostics (Percy 2003b, Hollis 2004, Martoni and Brown 2018, Martoni et al. 2024). In recent years, morphological diagnostics has been increasingly supported by molecular methods (Martoni et al. 2021a), which can provide a species-level identification using DNA analysis. For example, DNA barcoding is a molecular technique that mostly targets the subunit I of the mitochondrial gene cytochrome oxidase (COI) (Hebert et al. 2003), and has been widely used for species delimitation in psyllids (Taylor et al. 2016, Percy 2017, Martoni et al. 2018, Martoni et al. 2024). Beyond species delimitation, in order to identify a species using DNA barcoding, it is necessary to have DNA sequences on databases that includes records of native and exotic psyllid species. However, incomplete reference databases that do not include representative species, or include sequences that have not been reliably identified, limit biodiversity assessments and complicate biosecurity efforts (Boykin et al. 2012, Piper et al. 2019).

Integrative taxonomy (Dayrat 2005) enhances traditional identification by combining diagnostic elements such as morphological, molecular, and host plant data. This system has already been applied in psyllid research (Martoni 2017), and is a robust tool for biodiversity and biosecurity research (Armstrong and Ball 2005, Hajibabaei et al. 2007, Padial and De la Riva 2007).

This study assessed different psyllid collection methods and used integrative taxonomy to delimit the number of species captured. Here, we focused on 4 major citrus-growing regions across eastern Australia, with new field collections of psyllids conducted in and around private citrus sites. The major aims of this study were to: (i) compare collecting and trapping methods to assess psyllid richness and abundance across 2 collection seasons; (ii) compare the effect of trap placement on catch richness and abundance around citrus sites; and (iii) enhance molecular diagnostics by contributing COI DNA sequences to improve psyllid species coverage in public genetic databases.

Materials and Methods

Psyllid Collection Sites

Four sites within major citrus-growing regions were sampled due to their importance to the Australian citrus industry: Tablelands/QLD1 and North Burnett/QLD2 in Queensland, Riverina/NSW in New South Wales, and Sunraysia/VIC in Victoria. Each region contained a single sampling site consisting of one commercial citrus orchard surrounded by an area of native vegetation up to ∼200 m wide around the perimeter. The size of each site differed: Tablelands/QLD1 = 3.18 km2 (1.22 miles2), Sunraysia/VIC = 2.95 km2 (1.13 miles2), Riverina/NSW = 1.64 km2 (0.63 miles2), and North Burnett/QLD2 = 1.26 km2 (0.48 miles2). These sites averaged 2.25 km2 (0.86 miles2), and featured mixed native or non-native vegetation that was intentionally planted, and bushland with local native plant species adjacent to the orchard.

Each site was sampled twice: from August to December 2023 (Season 1; S1) and from April to June 2024 (Season 2; S2). These periods were roughly aligned with seasonal vegetation patterns, sampling from early spring to late summer (S1), followed by resampling during autumn to early winter (S2). Sampling targeted periods of active vegetative growth, including new flush in Citrus and growth phases in common native plants such as eucalypts and Acacias. While the phenology of native vegetation broadly coincided with non-native Citrus flushing, some Citrus growth occurs independently (eg, flush due to pruning). All sites contained a mixture of planted native and non-native trees, and adjacent patches of local native vegetation forming relatively contiguous habitats.

All psyllid sampling was conducted by single person except for the initial 5 days of the first sampling trip in Tablelands/QLD1 (29 August 2023 to 3 September 2023), where 2 people hand sampled, conducted light trapping, and deployed both pan and YSTs.

Psyllid Collection Methods

Adult psyllids were the primary targets for all collection methods (Fig. 1) because morphological identification could be more effectively applied to this life stage (Martoni et al. 2024). However, nymphs were also collected to provide additional samples for molecular analysis, while lerps and leaf galls were retained as additional materials that could be used to help infer the presence of certain species.

Satellite image G) shows New South Wales trap deployment lines A (bush), B and C (citrus orchard); yellow dots represent YSTs and red dots represent pan traps.

From here on, pan and YSTs are referred to as “passive” traps because they could be left unattended, whereas light trapping and hand sampling are “active,” requiring the operator to be present during collection. Passive traps operated continuously, while hand sampling occurred only during the day, and light trapping was restricted to the night. Passive traps were placed at the orchard edge and slightly towards the interior, but never in the center of the citrus orchard. Table S2 summarizes psyllid counts per individual passive trap, which were used for statistical analysis.

The sampling workflow was consistent across all sites and seasons. On day 1, passive traps were deployed in a linear grid, and hand sampling was conducted while walking through the site. On day 2, traps were inspected and repaired if needed, additional hand sampling was conducted, and evening light trapping occurred in the native vegetation adjacent the orchard. These procedures continued until passive traps were collected on the final day.

Hand Sampling Methods

At each site, hand sampling was conducted by a single person for 3 to 4 h by beating and sweep netting (Fig. 1A, C, and D) native vegetation and commercial citrus varieties of grapefruit, lemons, limes, mandarins, and oranges (the most common being varieties of Citrus reticulata “Afourer” and “Imperial” and varieties of Citrus x sinensis “Washington Navel” and “Valencia”). Psyllids were beaten onto a tray or swept using an extendable entomological net (Ø 60 cm, BugDorm, Taiwan), and aspirated using an unmodified electronic Nasal Aspirator (Rite Aid, Australia) before preserving them in 100% ethanol in 1.5 ml screw-cap vials (SSIBO, USA).

Light Trapping

Two light trapping sessions were conducted at each site per season, resulting in a total of 16 light trapping events over the course of this study. Light trapping sessions were conducted in native bushland adjacent to the citrus orchards (Fig. 1A, E, and G), and lasted 4 to 5 h starting from dusk around 6:00 PM (18:00 h) until ∼10:00 PM (22:00 h), following the methodology presented elsewhere for psyllids (Martoni et al. 2020).

The light trapping apparatus consisted of a white cotton sheet (2.4 m × 1.37 m) suspended between 2 trees on ropes, with the base of the sheet being close to the ground and the top of the sheet being ∼2 m in height from the ground. As a light source, a single LepiLED 1.5 light (Brehm 2017) was used which projected UV, blue, and green wavelengths onto the sheet from 10 to 30 cm. This was powered by a portable 72 W power bank model ChargeUp Edge+ 27K (Cygnett, China). Psyllids were then collected from the sheet using an aspirator and preserved in 100% ethanol, as described above. To avoid bycatch, light trapping was conducted at least 30 m away from the nearest passive traps.

Yellow Sticky Traps

AgriSense-type traps (200 mm × 100 mm, AgriSense, UK) and yellow/green ACP-type lure traps (200.3 mm × 139.7 mm, Alpha Scents Inc. Oregon, USA) were deployed with a dark green protective cage to mitigate potential bycatch (Sétamou et al. 2019), and are referred to as YSTs from hereon.

Twenty-one YSTs (Fig. 1A, B, and G) were deployed at each site for one week during each collection season, totaling 168 traps over the course of this study. Traps were secured to branches ∼1.5 m above ground, and arranged in a 3 × 7 grid forming 3 deployment lines: 7 Line “A” traps were positioned in native bush adjacent to the citrus orchard, 7 Line “B” traps were placed between the first and second citrus trees of each row at the orchard edge, and 7 Line “C” traps were located between the third and fourth citrus trees of each row closer to the orchard interior. These trap Lines were ∼30 m wide and 10 m apart. Most sites featured orchard rows perpendicular to adjacent bushland (where Line A traps were deployed) which allowed a gap between the rows. However, NSW had orchard rows running parallel to the nearest native bush due to site orientation constraints.

The mean trapping area across all sites was ∼1,628 m2 (∼17,526 ft2). Trap spacing and coverage varied among sites due to local constraints. QLD1 had a mean grid spacing of 11 m and a total trapping area of ∼1,440 m2 (15,500 ft2), QLD2 had 9.8 m spacing and ∼1,104 m2 (11,883 ft2), NSW had 14.3 m spacing and ∼2,220 m2 (23,896 ft2), and VIC had 29.1 m spacing and ∼1,749 m2 (18,720 ft2) coverage. The greater spacing (30 to 46 m) between Line A and B traps at NSW and VIC is explained by vehicles access gaps that separated the citrus from the nearest native vegetation. All traps were carefully positioned to minimize exposure to strong winds and sunlight where possible, while still allowing insect capture (Fig. 1).

Yellow sticky traps were collected after one week by removing each trap from its protective plastic cage and covering the traps adhesive surface within an A4 transparent sleeve. In the laboratory, to separate the specimens from the adhesive surface, each psyllid was cut from the main trap, but kept attached to some of the card. Psyllids were soaked in a glass container with orange oil (Orange Power, Planet Ark, Australia) for 1 to 2 min, helping to release the specimens from their adhesive card, following a similar method to Miller et al. (1993). Further soaking was required if insects did not release, or a fine-tipped brush was used to separate the psyllids from the sticky trap card before the specimens were preserved in vials of 100% ethanol using the brush or forceps.

Pan Traps

Twenty-one pan traps (Fig. 1A, F, and G) were deployed at each site for one week during each collection season, for a total of 168 traps over the course of this study. Where possible, all pan traps were deployed on the ground at the shaded base of trees, within 2 m of the nearest YST, making them easier to locate and access particularly where there was dense vegetation. Each pan trap consisted of one 17 cm × 11 cm × 6 cm yellow plastic container stacked inside another transparent plastic container of equal size. Yellow containers were filled with 250 ml of a preserving solution consisting of 50% propylene glycol and 50% water (Martoni et al. 2021b). Pan traps were secured in place by two 12 cm steel pegs driven into the soil, and placed in a way to mitigate the effects of excessive rainfall or irrigation which would further dilute the preservative. Pan traps were checked one day after deployment to ensure trapped insects were submerged, and were topped up with additional propylene glycol solution when required.

In the laboratory, samples were removed from the propylene glycol solution, any debris (eg, dust, etc.) on the insects was washed off with ethanol, and all psyllids were separated and placed into vials of 100% ethanol.

Morphological Examination and Host Plant Association

The integrative taxonomy approach workflow to identify the psyllids included initial morphological examinations using available keys (Hollis 2004, Martoni et al. 2024), separating specimens that appeared to be morphologically different (morphospecies) into different vials, species delimitation through molecular methods (see below), species validation (when required) through secondary morphological comparison, which included host plant information and distribution, following a psyllid diagnostic methodology applied elsewhere (Martoni 2017).

All materials were examined under a Leica WILD M3Z microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Photographs were often taken of the psyllids to enable comparisons between the samples using a Leica M205C stereo microscope with a Leica DFC500 camera (Leica Microsystems, Germany). When possible morphospecies were identified to genus using morphological keys (Hollis 2004, Martoni et al. 2024).

Different plant samples were also collected and photographed as additional information to try and delimit psyllid species/genera through their host plant associations. Plants were identified using online resources such as iNaturalist (www.inaturalist.ala.org.au), the Australian Virtual Herbarium (avh.ala.org.au), and Lucid keys for genera such as Acacia (Maslin 2018).

Molecular Analysis

Both male and female representatives of each morphospecies were selected for molecular analysis because sexual dimorphism in psyllids can make species-level identification challenging (Martoni et al. 2018, Martoni and Blacket 2021). DNA was extracted from 203 individual specimens (202 adults and one nymph) using a non-destructive protocol previously applied to psyllids (Martoni et al. 2020). Briefly, adult specimens were incubated overnight (∼17 h) in 180 µl of ATL buffer and 20 µl of Proteinase-K from the DNEasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany), and then processed following the manufacturer’s instructions. After the non-destructive DNA extractions and subsequent polymerase chain reaction (PCRs), DNA voucher specimens were retained and unextracted specimens from multiple populations were deposited in the invertebrate collection at Museums Victoria, Melbourne, Australia.

Polymerase chain reaction assays targeted a 570 bp fragment of the COI Subunit I mitochondrial gene using the primer pair PsyCOI-F3 (5’—ACAATTGTTACWGCWCAYGC—3’; Martoni et al. 2020) and HCO2198 (5’—TAAACTTCAGGGTGACCAAAAAATCA—3’; Folmer et al. 1994). When amplification failed, alternative PCR attempts targeting the same region of COI were made using the primer pair C1-J1709 (5’ – AATTGGWGGWTTYGGAAAYTG—3’); (Simon et al. 2006) and HCO2198.

PCRs were conducted in a 25 μl volume using the MyFi DNA Polymerase kit (Bioline Meridian Biosciences, USA). PCR master mix included 14.7 μl of 100 ug/ml Bovine Serum Albumin (New England Biolabs, USA) diluted in ddH20, 5 μl of 5X MyFi Reaction Buffer, 1 μl of both the forward and reverse primers (10 μl), 0.8 μl of MyFi DNA Polymerase and 2.5 μl of DNA template. The PCR cycling included: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 30 s at 94 °C, 45 s at 50 °C, 45 s at 72 °C, and a final extension of 1 min at 72 °C (Martoni et al. 2020). For several samples, the protocol was adjusted to 35 cycles and a final extension of 7 min at 72 °C to create products suitable for successful DNA sequencing. PCR results were checked on 1% agarose gels after running at 100 V for 30 min. Successfully amplified samples were sent for dual-direction Sanger sequencing (Macrogen, South Korea; AGRF, Australia).

The software MEGA 11 v.11.0.13 (Tamura et al. 2021) was used to manually check electropherograms, merge forward and reverse sequences, and to check for stop codons and pseudogenes. Clean consensus sequences were then aligned and distance matrices were created using the Kimura-2-parameters (K2P) model (Kimura 1980), from which a neighbor-joining COI tree (bootstrapped 1,000 times) was generated (Fig. S1). Six sequences from 6 species of aphids (Accession numbers DQ499025 to DQ499028, DQ499050, and DQ499056) were used as an outgroup. Sequences were compared with records on public genetic databases including GenBank (NCBI) and BOLD (BOLD Systems). All sequences generated in this study were uploaded on GenBank under the accession numbers PV663191 to PV663382 and PV983797 to PV983800.

Species Delimitation and Nomenclature

Species-level delimitation was conducted using an integrative taxonomy framework (Dayrat 2005). Rather than applying molecular operational taxonomic units (MOTUs) which uses sequence data as the primary delimitation driver (Floyd et al. 2002, Blaxter 2004), this study combined morphological characteristics, COI sequences, and ecological information (eg, distribution or host plant) to inform species identifications, as a complementary delimitation approach that was used in psyllid studies elsewhere (Percy 2003a, Martoni et al. 2018).

A 97% sequence similarity threshold was applied for species-level delimitation, based on comparisons with sequences in public molecular databases (eg, GenBank), which has been used in previous psyllids studies (Taylor et al. 2016, Martoni et al. 2018, Percy 2018, Martoni et al. 2020). For this study, when COI sequences matched reference databases to >97% similarity, and when morphology was consistent, formal binomials were assigned (eg, Cryptoneossa triangula). Delimited species without formal names were assigned provisional names (eg, sp. “A”, sp. “B”, etc.) following the naming convention used in Martoni et al. (2020), and extending the series where necessary. All individuals identified as belonging to the same morphospecies and molecular taxon were grouped under the same species name using integrative taxonomy for subsequent analyses.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses focused on species count data to test how sites, seasons, trap types, and the trap deployment position might affect richness and abundance. For statistical modelling, the analyses were restricted to 2 passive methods, pan and YSTs, because they were not biased by sampling efforts. All statistical modelling was performed in R v. 2024.12.1 + 563 (R Core Team 2024).

A negative binomial generalized linear model fit was tested for overdispersion using a χ2 test of the residuals and using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and a negative binomial generalized linear model (NB GLM, AIC = 406.85) (Venables and Ripley 2002) was applied using the MASS package v.7.3.61 (Ripley 2023).

Post hoc comparisons of factors affecting species counts were conducted using the emmeans package v.1.11.1 (Lenth 2022). The negative binomial GLM estimated differences between groups (eg, Season 1 vs 2; YST vs. PAN) on log-transformed data to account for skewed trap results. Standard errors assessed the precision of these estimates, which were tested for significance using z-values. Resulting P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Tukey’s method (Tukey 1949) to reduce the risk of false positives. Model estimates were exponentiated, converting log-scale differences into ratios of species counts, for clearer interpretation of how strongly each factor influenced species counts.

Results

A total of 87 species, 27 genera, and 5 families was captured from this study (11 described and 76 morphospecies). All 728 individual psyllids were initially sorted by genus, representatives of both sexes (to account for sexual dimorphism) were selected from morphospecies populations, and a total of 725 adults and 3 nymphs were sequenced. Of the 203 individuals for which DNA extraction was attempted, 196 (96.5%) yielded successful COI sequences. The 7 individuals that were not included in the final NJ tree, but were morphologically identified, comprised 6 samples from YSTs (Acizzia sp. “ZR”; Anoeconeossa unicornuta; Blastopsylla sp. “D”; Cryptoneossa triangula; Dasypsylla brunnea; Haplaphalara sp. “B”), and a single Acizzia viscosae from pan traps.

Overall, sequences from 17 species shared high similarity to sequences available on GenBank. These included 4 species with 97% to 98% similarity to other sequences (Acizzia sp. B, Acizzia viscosae, Anoeconeossa communis, and Cryptoneossa triangula), and a further 12 species that shared above 99% similarity to other sequences (Anoeconeossa sp. “F” and “M”, Anoeconeossa unicornuta, Blastopsylla occidentalis, Creiis sp. “K”, Eucalyptolyma maideni, Heteropsylla cubana, Lasiopsylla sp. “B”, Phellopsylla sp. “K” and “M”, Platyobria sp. “T”, and an undescribed Triozidae genus sp. “A”).

All specimens from hand sampling (n 64) and light trapping (n 25) had 100% sequencing success rate and were included in the final neighbor-joining tree (Fig. S1). Pan traps yielded 17 sequences from 18 individuals (94.4%), while YSTs had the lowest sequence success, with only 90 successful sequences from 96 individuals (93.7%).

While Season 1 and Season 2 yielded similar numbers of specimens (390 and 338, respectively), richness and abundance was notably lower in Season 2. Season 1 captured 70 species from 23 genera and 5 families, whereas Season 2 recorded 39 species from 15 genera and 4 families.

When considering the different regions, no single species was found across all 4 sites. The site with the highest species counts was North Burnett/QLD2 (32 species from 20 genera), followed by Sunraysia/VIC (28 species, 12 genera), Riverina/NSW (21 species, 8 genera), and Tablelands/QLD1 (15 species, 8 genera).

Of the 87 species identified across all sites, 55 (63.22%) belonged to the family Aphalaridae, 22 (25.29%) to Psyllidae, 7 (8.05%) to Triozidae, 2 (2.30%) to Liviidae, and one (1.14%) to Carsidaridae (Fig. 2; Table S1).

A total of 54 species were captured using YSTs, 40 using hand sampling, 15 using light trapping, and 13 using pan traps. Of the total 87 species, 26 (29.9%) were captured with more than one collection method (Fig. 3A; Table S1), with only Glycaspis sp. “S” collected by all 4 methods. The remaining 61 (70.1%) species were recorded from a single collection method: 30 species (34.5%) were unique to YSTs, 23 (26.4%) to hand sampling, 6 (6.9%) to light trapping, and 2 (2.3%) to pan traps (Fig. 3A; Table S1).

Richness at the genus-level included 18 genera each from hand sampling and YSTs, and 8 genera each from both light trapping and pan traps. Of the total 27 genera, 15 (55.6%) were sampled from multiple collection methods. No genus was exclusively found using pan traps, but 7 genera were recorded only with hand sampling, 4 only with YSTs, and one only with light trapping (Fig. 3B). Genera found exclusively from a single collection method include: Agelaeopsylla, Anomalopsylla, Casuarinicola, Cecidopsylla, Heteropsylla, Protyora, Schedotrioza from hand sampling; Aphalaridae genus “B”, Australopsylla, Dasypsylla, Haplaphalara from YSTs; and Hyalinaspis from light trapping–totaling 12 genera (44.4%) (Table S1).

Species counts from YSTs and hand sampling consistently outnumbered light and pan trapping. Across all methods, YSTs captured around 4.2 times more species than pan traps, 3.6 times more than light trapping, and 1.3 times more than hand sampling, a pattern consistent across sites.

Focusing only on YSTs and pan traps (both passive systems), species counts varied significantly across sites, seasons, trap types, and trap deployment lines, as shown by Tukey’s post hoc comparisons derived from the negative binomial GLM. Overall, YSTs captured 54 species, while pan traps captured 13 species, with 11 species shared between both methods. This means YSTs recorded 43 species not found in pan traps, while pan traps had 2 species that were not captured on YSTs.

Richness across sites from YSTs and pan traps combined was highest in Sunraysia/VIC (21 species, 9 genera), followed by North Burnett/QLD2 (17 species from 10 genera), Tablelands/QLD1 (13 species, 7 genera), and the Riverina/NSW (12 species, 5 genera). On these passive traps, VIC captured significantly more species than NSW (Log est. = 1.356, z = 6.045, P < 0.001), QLD1 (Log est. = 0.663, z = 3.807, P = 0.001), and QLD2 (Log est. = 1.141, z = 5.529, P < 0.001). QLD1 also had higher species counts than NSW (Log est. = 0.693, z = 2.830, P = 0.024). The number of psyllid species resulted in no statistically significant differences between both Queensland sites, QLD1 and QLD2 (Log est. = 0.478, z = 2.091, P = 0.156), or those between QLD2 and NSW (Log est. = 0.215, z = 0.800, P = 0.854).

Traps in Season 1 captured significantly more species than Season 2 (Log est. = 0.708, z = 4.743, P ⩽ 0.001), but similar numbers of individuals (199 and 210, respectively). From the passive traps, the most abundant species in Season 1 included 26 individuals of Eucalyptolyma sp. “D”, 14 specimens of Cryptoneossa triangula, and 7 each from Acizzia sp. “M” and Anoeconeossa sp. “R”. In Season 2, passive traps collected 97 individuals across all sites, of which 80 specimens (82.5%) were Cryptoneossa triangula, which were only found on YSTs at the Tablelands/QLD1 site.

In Season 1, YSTs captured 44 species from 184 individuals, while pan traps collected 12 species from a total of 15 individuals. In Season 2, YSTs collected 24 species and 153 individuals, while pan traps captured only 3 species and 57 individuals. All samples from pan traps in Season 2 were from Sunraysia/VIC, which comprised 52 individuals from Glycaspis sp. “R”, one from Glycaspis sp. “S”, and 4 from Glycaspis sp. “T” (Table 1: Table S2).

The position of the trap deployments within a site also significantly impacted the species counts. Line A traps deployed in the native bush adjacent to the orchard (Fig. 1G) captured around 14 times more species than those in citrus trap Line B (Log est. = 2.650, z = 9.234, P < 0.001), and ∼30 times more than citrus trap Line C (Log est. = 3.423, z = 8.252, P < 0.001). Differences between Lines B and C were not significant (Log est. = 0.773, z = 1.567, P = 0.260). Across all sites, traps deployed in bushland adjacent to orchards captured significantly more species than those inside the citrus orchards (Fig. 4). Discussion

Psyllid Identification and the Importance of a Genetic Reference Database

Species-level identification is fundamental for biosecurity and biodiversity research because it enables native taxa to be distinguished from morphologically similar exotic species, including related pests. Genera such as Trioza are diverse and include both native Australian species and economically important pests (Ouvrard et al. 2015, Martoni et al. 2024). In Australia, native Trioza detected on citrus trees caused concerns that they were the exotic pest African Citrus psyllid (Trioza erytreae), but combined morphological and molecular analyses de-escalated the potential biosecurity response by confirming the identity was a different native species (Martoni and Blacket 2021). Similarly, relying only on DNA similarity based on poorly curated databases may flag some species as potential exotics, and a morphological examination is needed to determine a correct ID (Martoni et al. 2023). These examples highlight the importance of coupling expert morphological diagnostics with curated COI sequences, which can enable rapid and accurate species identification for biosecurity monitoring.

Taken on its own, morphological identification can be challenging when samples are degraded, undescribed, or have cryptic morphology (Rapisarda 1989, Wenninger and Hall 2008). To address the first challenge of sorting 728 individuals into morphospecies, we confidently assigned psyllids to genus before designating provisional names (eg, sp. “B”). This system has been repeated in many psyllid studies (Powell 2011, Martoni et al. 2020), and allowed efficient sorting for downstream molecular analysis. Similarly, the MOTU concept can rapidly flag potential species (Floyd et al. 2002, Blaxter 2004), but it is limited when attempting to link sequences to formally described taxa if these do not have an accession in the sequence database.

To complement these techniques and overcome their respective shortfalls, we applied an integrative taxonomy approach (Dayrat 2005), which incorporated morphological, molecular, and host plant data as complementary checkpoints to inform species identification (Hanner et al. 2009, Martoni et al. 2018, Martoni et al. 2021a). This approach balances the rapid detection of cryptic diversity with robust, reproducible identification that can be linked to existing taxonomic knowledge.

One group where morphological identification is especially challenging is the genus Acizzia, which has at least 49 described Australian species (McClelland et al. 2026), and an estimated 200 undescribed (Martoni et al. 2024). As a result of the integrative taxonomy approach, 21 Acizzia species were delimited in this study. Two of the species, Acizzia sp. “B” and Acizzia viscosae, matched COI sequences on public genetic databases, but no other publicly available COI sequences matched the remaining 19 Acizzia species, which are now available on GenBank for the first time.

Furthermore, the use of a non-destructive DNA extraction protocol enabled the retention of DNA voucher specimens, which could morphologically be re-examined. For example, sequences of Acizzia sp. “B” from Tablelands/QLD1 in this study matched sequences and morphology of the same species collected in Queensland in 2019 (Martoni et al. 2020), which had also been reported from Norfolk Island (Martoni et al. 2025), extending its known distribution by at least 2,600 km. While Acizzia sp. “B” is not described at present, DNA analysis and morphological examination confirmed that it is certainly widespread. Beyond species identification, COI sequences can be used to trace psyllid lineages across regions, revealing population connectivity and historical origins of species (Percy 2003a, Martoni et al. 2018).

One of the major strengths of COI barcoding is the ability to delimit species that may be undescribed (Hebert et al. 2003) or underrepresented in public sequence databases (Martoni et al. 2024). We recovered 17 species sharing >97% similarity to existing database sequences (7 described and 10 morphospecies with provisional identifications). Barcoding was robust enough to identify these 17 species (∼20% of the data), but this means ∼80% of species could not be identified because they are underrepresented on public database.

Species identification using public genetic databases is constrained by mislabeled reference sequences (Meiklejohn et al. 2019), incomplete metadata such as voucher images or host and habitat information (Cheng et al. 2023), and the limited coverage of curated psyllid reference sequences, which may result in taxon misattribution when similarity thresholds are applied with less caution (Martoni et al. 2023). For example, a sequence from our data of Platyobria sp. “T” shared 99.4% similarity to an existing sequence on GenBank labelled “Phyllolyma sp. A”, but a morphological re-examination confirmed it as a Platyobria. This example highlights the value of using sequence data to complement morphology during species delimitation.

In total, this work generated 196 COI sequences across 83 species, of which 163 sequences from 66 species (one described and 65 morphospecies) are new database records that significantly contribute to Australian and global psyllid references, streamlining molecular diagnostics and surveillance (Piper et al. 2019).

Psyllid Collection and Trap Comparison

Surveillance using sticky traps has long been a method to detect exotic and native psyllid species across the world (eg, Ogol and Spence 1997, Brennan and Weinbaum 2001, Tedeschi et al. 2009, Yen et al. 2012). In this study, YSTs captured more species than any other collection method. However, if only YSTs were deployed, they would have missed 33 species that the other collection methods captured, potentially missing important pests or species of ecological interest, such as rare psyllid species. Additionally, because YSTs do not link specimens to their host plants, hand collection remains a widespread sampling technique in ecological and biodiversity studies of host-specific insects. With psyllids, host plant information is crucial as these insects require specific plant hosts to complete their life cycles (Hodkinson 1974, Hodkinson 2009, Burckhardt et al. 2014). Hand sampling, therefore, provides more context about psyllid ecology, which is not possible to glean from traps. Hand sampling also yielded nymphs and lerps, which can provide additional insight into species living in an area, even when the adults are not present. Therefore, while hand sampling can be labor-intensive and time-consuming (Tedeschi et al. 2008, Yen et al. 2012, Monzó et al. 2015), it complements trapping by capturing data that passive systems might miss.

Of the 2 passive methods, YSTs captured over 4 times more species than pan traps, results which contrast with previous studies that showed a much more even distribution (Yen et al. 2012). In addition to capturing greater psyllid richness and abundance, sticky traps were also inexpensive, easy to transport and deploy, and simple to replace and collect (Hall and Hentz 2010, Monzó et al. 2015). However, YSTs and pan traps yielded a lower sequencing success rate (93.7% to 94.4%) compared to hand sampling and light trapping, which both had 100% success (likely due to the immediate preservation of live psyllids in high grade ethanol). A higher proportion of YSTs was also observed to have morphologically damaged specimens, which negatively impacted morphological examination and preservation of useful DNA vouchers. Importantly, it should also be noted that for this work, YSTs were collected after one week–a shorter exposure time than typical surveillance programs, which often leave traps in the field for several weeks or months (Flores et al. 2009, Monzó et al. 2015, Wenninger et al. 2019). Similar one-week deployments have been used in psyllid monitoring studies (Tedeschi et al. 2009, Hall and Hentz 2010, Rosa García et al. 2014), which allow for recovery of fresher DNA and better-preserved morphological features, particularly under hot and dry field conditions.

In the field, compared to YSTs, pan traps were more cumbersome since they were larger, slower to deploy, and harder to pack up due to potential spillage. Furthermore, pan traps required more frequent maintenance. For example, in several instances, the liquid preservative in the pan traps had to be replaced due to animal damage, evaporation from ambient heat, or excessive dilution if they were impacted by rain or irrigation. In the laboratory, separating psyllids from the other organisms was time consuming, especially where some of the pan traps had more debris from foliage and dust. Nevertheless, pan traps may be useful for surveillance in low-lying vegetation (eg, potato crops) (Yen et al. 2012), but overall, YSTs were a much more effective method for psyllid surveillance.

Line A traps deployed in native bushland captured ∼14 to 30 times more psyllids than those placed within the citrus, in Line B at the orchard edge, and Line C in the orchard interior, respectively. When considering Line B and Line C, no significant differences were observed in the abundance or richness of psyllids captured. Greater insect richness and abundance is commonly observed in areas around the perimeter of orchards (Grafton-Cardwell et al. 2013, Anco and Gottwald 2015, Sétamou and Bartels 2015, Anastasio et al. 2021, Martoni and Blacket 2021), and the results of this study agree. In all sites, passive traps captured an increase in psyllid abundance and richness closest to Line A, in the bushland with high native plant diversity adjacent to the orchards. Although the orchards were hand sampled (mostly through sweep netting), no psyllids were collected or observed to be feeding on the citrus trees. Passive traps deployed in Lines B and C captured 11 species in very low numbers, none of which are known to be associated with Citrus (Martoni et al. 2024), and no immature psyllids or eggs were observed.

A single specimen each of Acizzia sp. “ZZC” in QLD1, Cryptoneossa sp. “D” in QLD1, and Haplaphalara sp. “B” in QLD2 were captured only by YSTs within the orchard. These species were never found in native vegetation, and are reported here to be present around citrus only as “casual” visitors (Burckhardt et al. 2014), meaning they were not hosted by the plant but they were likely windblown on it as previously reported psyllids (Sétamou and Bartels 2015, Agarwal et al. 2023, Martoni et al. 2023). Furthermore, none of the 3 species are known to use Citrus (Rutaceae) as a host, and are instead associated with native Malvaceae, Myrtaceae, and numerous other Australian plants which are not in the Rutaceae family (Martoni et al. 2024).

Within Rutaceae, there are 6 native Australian Citrus species (Mabberley 1998, Johnson et al. 2025), but we did not encounter any during sampling. To date, no psyllids are known to use Citrus as a host in Australia (Ouvrard 2020, Martoni et al. 2024), and no psyllids were captured during our orchard sweeps. However, 2 Anomalopsylla species (“A” and “B”) and Cecidopsylla paterae were sequenced from Geijera parviflora (Rutaceae). Currently, only Boronia and Geijera are recorded as native rutaceous hosts of psyllids in Australia (Martoni et al. 2024). Unlike Murraya, an alternative host of ACP (Diaphorina citri), both Boronia and Geijera are more distantly related to Citrus, and diverge at the subfamily level. Collectively, these records indicate that native Australian psyllid associations within Rutaceae are taxonomically restricted and do not currently involve Citrus, though broader sampling of other Rutaceae genera could further clarify host associations.

Across all citrus sites, Eucalyptus was a common host to different psyllid taxa, including the species-rich Glycaspis genus (Moore 1961b, 1970, Martoni et al. 2024), which can have abundant colony expansions (Moore 1961b, Steinbauer et al. 2015, Faast et al. 2020). In contrast to VIC, the NSW site recorded few Myrtaceae-feeding psyllids and no Glycaspis across both seasons. Several factors may influence psyllid presence and abundance, including the historically modified structure of native bushland in the region (Bedward et al. 2007, NSW Government 2023), local variation in eucalypt assemblages (González-Orozco et al. 2014, Davidson 2020), and seasonal conditions affecting psyllid development and host phenology (Moore 1961a, Sharma et al. 2013, Laudonia et al. 2014). These patterns warrant broader sampling across the region to resolve localized endemic species (Yen 2002).

There are widespread Australian native psyllid species such as Glycaspis brimblecombei that are present in other countries (Valente and Hodkinson 2009, Karaca et al. 2015, Dittrich-Schröder et al. 2021), so understanding which species may be more localized, and which are adaptable outside of their native range can be important for forestry, agriculture, and conservation.

Past trap comparison studies did not include light trapping, as psyllids were thought not to respond to light stimuli (Yen et al. 2012). However, more recently, UV light has been hypothesized to influence psyllid behaviour (Paris et al. 2017, Hodge et al. 2019, George et al. 2020). In this study, light trapping yielded 15 species, which included a single specimen of the genus Hyalinaspis that was not found with other methods. Light trapping also captured Creiis sp. “K” and Lasiopsylla sp. “B”, both previously found in Queensland during a separate light trapping study (Martoni et al. 2020). Results presented here agree with previous studies (Sétamou et al. 2012, Farnier et al. 2015, Martoni et al. 2020, Kalile et al. 2022), suggesting that some psyllid species may be more attracted to light sources.

Combining YSTs with hand sampling yielded the highest species counts for both seasons. Pan traps captured 2 species, Acizzia sp. “ZI” and Platyobria sp. “D”, which were not detected by other methods. Although pan traps can be effective for capturing species that feed closer to the ground, such as Bactericera cockerelli (Yen et al. 2012), they captured significantly fewer species than YSTs in this study, highlighting the superior performance of the latter around citrus orchards. For both passive traps, placement within the bushland (Line A) captured significantly more species and individuals than at the orchard edge (Line B), and those towards the orchard interior (Line C). These patterns suggest that native vegetation is the primary driver of local psyllid richness, and that citrus plantings act as occasional spillover sites for Australian Psylloidea.

Light trapping captured several rarer species, suggesting that some psyllids may be more active under low-light conditions, an observation that could improve sampling strategies. These trapping analyses give a valuable insight into the best methods to conduct psyllid monitoring for maximum sensitivity, and expected by-catch which may be observed while conducting surveillance for exotic psyllids. This research provides a more comprehensive record of native psyllid species found in and around Citrus orchards, which serves as a baseline for comparison in future surveillance programs conducted by the Australian Citrus industry.

Species counts were similar in Sunraysia/VIC and the Riverina/NSW; however, Tablelands/QLD1 had less than half of the species captured in North Burnett/QLD2. Species counts in QLD1 were lower than expected, given its proximity to tropical rainforests, which can contain very heterogeneous plant communities (see Percy et al. 2012 for an example in California) that may support a greater number of psyllid species. At the QLD1 site, sampling for Season 2 was conducted following a prescribed burn in the native vegetation around the citrus orchard, which occurred 1 to 2 months prior to sample collection. At this site, post-fire flush plant growth was observed, and could be linked to observed psyllid richness and abundance. Of the 137 individuals from 15 species captured at this site, Cryptoneossa triangula accounted for 68.6% of the composition, which was much higher than at any other site. This psyllid does not make lerps, and adults and nymphs tend to congregate on new growth leaves and branches, which is a relatively common behaviour for several psyllid species (Martoni et al. 2024). This suggests that some psyllid species may have a faster rate of post-fire recolonization, and highlights that the important relationships between fire and many Australian native plant species (Hester and Hobbs 1992, Dixon et al. 1995, Gallagher et al. 2021) may also apply to psyllids. Post-event recolonization can inform conservation status of psyllid species and their host plants (Taylor and Moir 2014, Moir et al. 2016), aid bird recovery (Loyn and McNabb 2015), affect urban tree health (Moore and Lefoe 2020), and help to re-establish biocontrol strategies of invasive plant species such as Boreioglycaspis melaleuca feeding on Melaleuca quinquenervia in Florida and California (Brooks 2008).

Seasons significantly impacted the observed psyllid richness and abundance, with Season 1 recording almost twice the number of species compared to Season 2. This likely reflects seasonal plant growth cycles, which are strongly linked to temperature (Marsham 1789, Hopkins 1938). Psyllids respond to environmental variation (Hodkinson 1974, Valterová et al. 1997, Hodkinson 2009, Burckhardt et al. 2014), and their development often coincides with flush growth or other host plant cues (Hall et al. 2008, Kumral et al. 2008, Hall and Hentz 2011). For example, 12 Acizzia species were uniquely captured in Season 1 compared to only 5 Acizzia species that were unique to Season 2. Many Acizzia species are well known to be associated with Acacia host plants in Australia (Martoni et al. 2024), so the greater psyllid richness and abundance in Season 1 likely reflect more prevalent flush growth in Acacia between the spring-summer periods compared to the later seasonal episodes typically associated with plant senescence.

Tolerating broader climate variations, which allow some species such as ACP to remain active for longer in other countries (Hall and Hentz 2014), could also be a factor for native Australian species such as Acizzia and Glycaspis being more widespread than some other psyllid genera. Therefore, it is important to capture ecological knowledge such as this in order to design improved sampling, monitoring, and surveillance strategies that aim not only to protect native and rare psyllid species, but also to support biosecurity preparedness. Forty psyllid species from 10 native host genera and one exotic plant genus were captured in this study, offering valuable insights into host plant associations and providing a foundation for targeted biosecurity training and interventions. Through analyzing morphological, molecular, and ecological data, only one exotic species (Heteropsylla cubana) was detected among the remaining 87 species captured. Furthermore, greater Australian psyllid diversity on public genetic databases is now represented with the inclusion of 163 COI sequences of 66 native species as first records on GenBank, therefore enhancing molecular diagnostics and biosecurity readiness.
