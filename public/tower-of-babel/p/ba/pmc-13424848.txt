Physical Activity as a Therapeutic Strategy for Functional Dyspepsia
Authors: Yeon Ji Kim
Published: 2026

Functional dyspepsia (FD) is a disorder of gut-brain interaction affecting approximately 10-20% of adults worldwide and one of the most frequent reasons for gastroenterology consultation.1,2 Despite its high prevalence, pharmacotherapy for FD remains limited in efficacy: proton pump inhibitors, prokinetics, and neuromodulators each provide modest and often transient benefit, and no single agent reliably achieves sustained symptom control.3 In this context, growing attention has been directed toward lifestyle and other non-pharmacological approaches as adjunctive strategies in the management of FD. The 2022 British Society of Gastroenterology guidelines discussed gut–brain behavioral interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy and hypnotherapy, together with lifestyle measures, as part of FD management.4 The 2022 Japanese Society of Gastroenterology guidelines also acknowledged the potential role of lifestyle-related factors in the management of FD.5 However, practical recommendations regarding the optimal exercise regimen for FD have remained limited because of the lack of supporting clinical trial data. The study by Wei et al6 helps strengthen the limited evidence base for non-pharmacological management of FD.

The association between physical inactivity and FD has been documented in observational studies. A population-based study of 3160 Australians reported an association between FD and lower levels of physical activity, particularly among patients with the postprandial distress syndrome subtype.7 However, these observational studies cannot exclude the possibility of reverse causation, as patients with more severe symptoms may be less physically active because of symptom-related limitations. To further explore causality, Wei et al6 performed a Mendelian randomization analysis using genome-wide association study data from the UK Biobank, including 7662 FD cases and 455 348 controls. Six physical activity phenotypes were evaluated as genetically predicted exposures. Participation in strenuous sports was associated with a significant reduction in FD risk (inverse variance weighted: OR = 0.924; 95% CI, 0.875-0.976; P = 0.005). A suggestive inverse association was also observed for activities including swimming, cycling, and fitness training, which broadly represented moderate-intensity exercise (OR = 0.964; 95% CI, 0.937-0.993; P = 0.013). Light domestic activity and recreational walking did not reach statistical significance after sensitivity analyses. These findings suggest that the protective association may be more closely related to structured aerobic exercise than to general physical activity alone. Wei et al6 subsequently conducted a single-center randomized controlled trial involving 260 patients with FD diagnosed according to the Rome IV criteria. Participants were assigned either to a 12-week moderate-intensity exercise program consisting of jogging or cycling at least 5 times weekly for 30-60 minutes per session, or to a control group receiving usual lifestyle guidance with weekly telephone follow-up. Of 260 enrolled participants, 227 completed the study (114 exercise, 113 control). Adequate overall symptom relief, defined as complete relief during at least half of the assessed weeks between weeks 3 and 12, was significantly more frequent in the exercise group than in the control group (46.9% vs 30.8%, P = 0.008). Exercise participants also showed significantly greater reductions in total 8-item dyspepsia symptom scores, Short-form Leeds Dyspepsia Questionnaire (SF-LDQ) total score, most SF-LDQ subscale scores, and all 5 Short-form Nepean Dyspepsia Index quality-of-life domains. Among individual symptoms, epigastric pain, epigastric burning sensation, postprandial fullness, abdominal distension, and nausea showed greater improvement in the exercise group, whereas early satiety, belching, and vomiting did not differ significantly between groups. Anxiety and depression scale improved similarly in both groups. However, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that the benefits of exercise are unrelated to gut–brain mechanisms, as the effects of physical activity may not be fully reflected by conventional assessments of anxiety and depression. Exercise may influence symptoms through pathways related to gut–brain interaction, in addition to its effects on gastrointestinal function. Further studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying the benefits of physical activity in FD. The randomized controlled trial represents one of the largest exercise intervention studies in FD conducted to date, exceeding the size of a previous trial by Rane et al8 (n = 72) by more than threefold, and is the first to consistently apply Rome IV diagnostic criteria. That said, several limitations deserve acknowledgment. In the Mendelian randomization analysis, the causal estimate for strenuous sports was sensitive to a single genetic variant (rs12571549): its exclusion in leave-one-out analysis nullified the association, introducing uncertainty about the robustness of that specific estimate. The randomized controlled trial was conducted at a single tertiary center in China, limiting generalizability to primary care and to other ethnic groups. Exercise adherence was assessed by self-report during telephone follow-up rather than by objective measures such as accelerometry or heart rate monitoring, which may have resulted in variability in compliance. The use of concomitant medications was not controlled and may have influenced the observed treatment effect. Finally, the 12-week follow-up period may be relatively short for a chronic and relapsing condition such as FD, and the long-term durability of exercise-related benefits remains uncertain.

Recent years have seen growing interest in non-pharmacological and lifestyle-based approaches to FD. This study adds clinical trial data to the limited evidence supporting exercise as a potential non-pharmacological approach for FD. Moderate-intensity aerobic exercise, such as jogging or cycling for 30-60 minutes at least 5 times per week, was associated with adequate symptom relief in nearly 50% of patients with FD. For clinicians managing a condition that is common, chronic, and often difficult to treat, this degree of symptom improvement is noteworthy. However, the findings should be interpreted with caution, given the single-center design, self-reported exercise adherence, and lack of long-term follow-up. Future multicenter trials with objective adherence monitoring, longer follow-up, and subgroup analyses by FD subtype will be needed to refine these recommendations further. The present findings provide a useful starting point for future studies aimed at clarifying the role of physical activity in FD, including the effects of different exercise types, intensities, and durations, as well as potential differences in treatment response according to FD subtype.
