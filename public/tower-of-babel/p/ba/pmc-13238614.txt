Ectopic Olfactory Neuroblastoma of the Maxillary Sinus: A Case Report
Authors: Hesam Jahandideh, Farzad Yadmehr, Mehdi Abrishami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOlfactory neuroblastoma is a rare neuroendocrine tumor typically arising from the superior nasal cavity, and primary occurrence in the maxillary sinus is exceptionally uncommon, often leading to diagnostic confusion with other sinonasal malignancies. A 34‐year‐old man presented with bilateral nasal obstruction, recurrent epistaxis, and frontal headache, and imaging revealed a right maxillary sinus mass. Initial endoscopic biopsy suggested a differentiated neuroendocrine tumor, but whole‐body octreotide scintigraphy was negative. The patient underwent endoscopic endonasal medial maxillectomy with complete tumor resection. Final histopathology demonstrated small round blue cells in lobular architecture, strong chromogranin and synaptophysin positivity, absence of mitoses or necrosis, and a low Ki‐67 index, confirming Hyams grade I olfactory neuroblastoma. Adjuvant intensity‐modulated radiotherapy was administered. The postoperative course was uneventful, and at 12‐month follow‐up there was no evidence of recurrence or metastasis. This case highlights that ectopic maxillary sinus olfactory neuroblastoma can mimic neuroendocrine carcinoma on limited biopsy, emphasizing the importance of complete resection and thorough histopathological evaluation for accurate diagnosis and grading. Hyams grade I disease in atypical locations carries an excellent prognosis with combined endoscopic surgery and radiotherapy, underscoring the prognostic value of Hyams grading and the need for long‐term surveillance.

Background

Olfactory neuroblastoma (ONB), also known as esthesioneuroblastoma, is a rare malignant neoplasm originating from the olfactory neuroepithelium, typically arising in the upper nasal cavity near the cribriform plate. It accounts for approximately 3%–6% of all sinonasal tumors, with an estimated annual incidence of 0.4 cases per million population [1, 2]. The tumor exhibits a unimodal age distribution, with peak incidence in the fifth to sixth decades of life, though a bimodal pattern has been described in some earlier studies, and it shows a slight male predominance [1].

While the vast majority of ONB cases originate in the superior nasal vault or olfactory recess, ectopic or primary presentations in other paranasal sinuses, including the maxillary sinus, are exceedingly uncommon and often pose diagnostic challenges due to atypical location and overlapping clinical features with more common sinonasal pathologies [3, 4]. Such ectopic cases may present with nasal obstruction, epistaxis, and headache, as seen in classic ONB, but their origin outside the olfactory region can lead to initial misdiagnosis as neuroendocrine tumors or other small round blue cell neoplasms on preliminary biopsy.

Histopathologically, ONB is characterized by small round cells with neurofibrillary stroma, lobular architecture, and neuroendocrine differentiation, confirmed by positive immunoreactivity for markers such as synaptophysin and chromogranin, with variable cytokeratin expression. The Hyams grading system remains a key prognostic tool, stratifying tumors from grade I (well‐differentiated, lobular pattern, minimal mitoses) to grade IV (poorly differentiated, high mitoses, necrosis), with low‐grade tumors (I–II) associated with significantly better survival outcomes compared to high‐grade (III–IV) lesions [1, 5] Recent multicenter analyses and refinements have further supported the incorporation of Hyams grade into staging systems to improve the prediction of recurrence and metastasis risk [5, 6].

Standard management involves surgical resection—often endoscopic approaches for accessible tumors—followed by adjuvant radiotherapy, with chemotherapy reserved for advanced or high‐grade disease [1]. Long‐term follow‐up is essential due to the potential for late local recurrence or distant metastasis.

Here, we report a rare case of ONB primarily involving the right maxillary sinus. The patient achieved an excellent outcome with adjuvant radiotherapy and no recurrence. This case highlights the diagnostic pitfalls of ectopic ONB and underscores the value of comprehensive histopathological evaluation and multimodal therapy in achieving favorable results.

Case History/Examination

A 34‐year‐old man with no significant past medical history or family history of malignancy presented with a two‐month history of bilateral nasal obstruction, recurrent epistaxis, and frontal headache. Physical examination revealed a mass in the right nasal cavity. No other abnormal systemic findings were noted.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Initial nasal endoscopy confirmed a mass in the right nasal cavity originating from the maxillary sinus region.

Computed tomography (CT) scan of the paranasal sinuses showed a soft tissue density mass occupying the right maxillary sinus (Figure 1). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a heterogeneous, iso‐ to hyperintense mass with heterogeneous enhancement after contrast administration on coronal and axial post‐contrast T1‐weighted images.

Endoscopic biopsy was performed. Histopathology revealed sheets of fairly monotonous epithelioid cells with salt‐and‐pepper chromatin and moderate eosinophilic cytoplasm. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was positive for cytokeratin (CK) and synaptophysin, with Ki‐67 positivity in approximately 1% of tumor cells. The preliminary diagnosis was differentiated neuroendocrine tumor.

A whole‐body Tc‐99m octreotide scan revealed normal tracer distribution, with no abnormal uptake in either the early or delayed phases.

The patient underwent endoscopic endonasal medial maxillectomy under general anesthesia. The procedure involved wide antrostomy, resection of the medial wall of the maxillary sinus, and complete removal of the tumor along with the attached mucosa and underlying bone. The inferior turbinate was partially resected to improve access and ensure clear margins. Using 0° and 30° endoscopes, the tumor was resected en bloc from its origin in the maxillary sinus, with meticulous dissection to achieve macroscopically clear margins. Frozen section analysis was performed intraoperatively to confirm negative surgical margins. The final histopathology of the resected specimen demonstrated small round blue cells in a lobulated growth pattern within fibrillary stroma, without mitotic figures or necrosis. IHC was positive for chromogranin and synaptophysin, negative for pan‐cytokeratin (PanCK), leukocyte common antigen (LCA), and p63. Ki‐67 was positive in approximately 2% of tumor cells (Figure 2). The definitive diagnosis was olfactory neuroblastoma, Hyams grade I. All surgical margins were reported as negative (R0 resection).

IHC staining showed positive reactivity for Synaptophysin (A) and Chromogranin (B).

Differential diagnoses considered included sinonasal neuroendocrine tumors (e.g., neuroendocrine carcinoma), sinonasal undifferentiated carcinoma, lymphoma, and other small round blue cell tumors. These were excluded based on the characteristic lobular architecture, specific neuroendocrine marker profile (positive chromogranin/synaptophysin, negative PanCK/LCA), low proliferation index, absence of aggressive histologic features, and lack of somatostatin receptor expression on octreotide scan.

Postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy was delivered at a total dose of 60 Gy in 2 Gy fractions.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient recovered uneventfully after surgery with no perioperative complications. At the 12‐month follow‐up, clinical evaluation, nasal endoscopy, and imaging revealed no evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis. The patient remains asymptomatic and continues regular surveillance.

Discussion

ONBpredominantly arises from the olfactory neuroepithelium in the superior nasal vault or cribriform plate region, rendering primary involvement of the maxillary sinus highly unusual [1, 7]. Ectopic ONB (eONB) cases, including those centered in the maxillary sinus without a clear connection to the olfactory cleft, represent a distinct and undercharacterized subset, with recent systematic reviews and pooled analyses confirming their rarity and potential for unique biological behavior [7, 8].

For lesions centered in the maxillary sinus, several surgical approaches are available, ranging from purely endoscopic endonasal techniques (e.g., endoscopic medial maxillectomy) to traditional open approaches (such as lateral rhinotomy, Weber‐Fergusson incision, or subtotal maxillectomy) and combined cranioendoscopic procedures. Endoscopic approaches provide superior magnification and visualization of tumor margins, no external incisions with superior cosmetic outcomes, reduced intraoperative blood loss, shorter hospital stay, faster recovery, and lower overall morbidity compared to open techniques. Limitations of the endoscopic approach include technical challenges in cases with extensive lateral or anterior maxillary wall involvement, significant orbital or infratemporal fossa extension, or highly vascular tumors. In contrast, open approaches offer wider exposure for advanced disease but are associated with greater facial scarring, longer operative time, higher complication rates, and prolonged hospitalization. Recent meta‐analyses have shown that, in appropriately selected patients with sinonasal malignancies including ONB, endoscopic resection achieves comparable or even superior oncologic outcomes (locoregional control and survival) to open surgery, particularly in low‐grade and early‐stage tumors [9, 10].

In the present case, the tumor was confined to the right maxillary sinus with origin from the medial wall and without evidence of extensive lateral wall, orbital, or skull base invasion on preoperative imaging. Therefore, an exclusively endoscopic endonasal medial maxillectomy was selected. This approach allowed en bloc resection with R0, excellent visualization using 0° and 30° endoscopes, and preservation of surrounding structures while minimizing surgical trauma. The low‐grade histology (Hyams grade I) further supported the suitability of this minimally invasive strategy [9].

In this patient, the initial biopsy suggested a differentiated neuroendocrine tumor, likely due to the predominance of epithelioid cells, neuroendocrine marker expression (synaptophysin positivity), and cytokeratin reactivity in limited tissue sampling. The definitive diagnosis of Hyams grade I ONB was established only after examination of the resected specimen, which demonstrated the hallmark lobulated architecture, fibrillary stroma, chromogranin positivity, PanCK negativity, and low proliferative activity (Ki‐67 2%). This diagnostic evolution illustrates a recognized limitation in small biopsies for ONB, where architectural features critical for differentiation from sinonasal neuroendocrine carcinoma or other small round blue cell tumors may be incompletely represented [1, 5]. The absence of somatostatin receptor expression on octreotide scintigraphy further aided in excluding typical neuroendocrine tumors, consistent with the generally low somatostatin receptor profile in ONB compared to other neuroendocrine neoplasms.

Recent evidence indicates that eONB may exhibit distinct clinicopathologic traits compared to orthotopic ONB. Pooled data from case series and systematic reviews suggest a higher association with paraneoplastic syndromes, particularly syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), in eONB (18.3% vs. 2.0% in orthotopic cases; p < 0.0001), potentially reflecting differences in tumor biology or microenvironment despite shared neuroendocrine differentiation [7]. Although our patient lacked paraneoplastic features, the ectopic maxillary origin aligns with reports of altered disease control, including shorter recurrence‐free survival and earlier time‐to‐recurrence in eONB cohorts, independent of traditional prognostic factors [11]. Despite this, the low‐grade histology (Hyams I) in the current case likely mitigated any adverse impact of the atypical site.

Hyams grading remains one of the most robust histopathological predictors in ONB. Low‐grade tumors (I–II) consistently demonstrate superior outcomes, with 5‐year overall survival often exceeding 80%–90% and markedly lower recurrence and metastasis rates compared to high‐grade (III–IV) lesions [1, 5, 12]. Recent multicenter analyses have advocated integrating Hyams grade into refined staging systems (e.g., modified Kadish, Dulguerov, or AJCC adaptations) to enhance prognostic precision, particularly for estimating disease progression and survival [5]. In low‐grade disease like the present case, endoscopic resection with negative margins followed by adjuvant radiotherapy represents the cornerstone of management, yielding excellent local control and long‐term survival without routine chemotherapy [1].

The patient's favorable 12‐month outcome—no recurrence on clinical, endoscopic, and imaging surveillance—aligns with expectations for Hyams grade I ONB treated multimodally. This supports the paradigm that, even in ectopic locations, low histologic grade and complete resection with adjuvant radiation can achieve durable remission. Nonetheless, the potential for late recurrences in ONB necessitates prolonged follow‐up beyond 5–10 years [1]. Reporting rare ectopic presentations such as this contributes to the evolving understanding of ONB heterogeneity, highlighting the need for meticulous histopathological correlation and consideration of site‐specific nuances in future staging and management algorithms.

Conclusion

This rare ectopic olfactory neuroblastoma arising in the maxillary sinus highlights diagnostic challenges in atypical locations and the pivotal role of thorough histopathological review of the resected specimen for accurate Hyams grade I classification. Despite potential biologic differences in ectopic cases, the low histologic grade, combined with complete endoscopic resection and adjuvant radiotherapy, yielded excellent local control with no recurrence at 12 months. These findings reaffirm Hyams' grade as the strongest independent prognostic factor in olfactory neuroblastoma and support aggressive multimodal therapy for optimal outcomes even in non‐classic sites. Long‐term follow‐up remains essential, and such reports help refine diagnostic and management approaches for this uncommon malignancy.
