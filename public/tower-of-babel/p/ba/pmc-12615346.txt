Association between different dietary patterns and the risk of major brain disorders: a prospective multi-cohort study
Authors: Yisen Shi, Yixiang Lin, Yiling Zheng, Xinxi Yu, Binfan Ou, Kaitai Yang, Fabin Lin, Xinyang Zou, Jiayi Zheng, Ruitian Zeng, Xuan Lin, Qinyong Ye, Lina Chen, Yuqi Zeng, Guoen Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundWhile diverse dietary patterns are emerging from nutritional research, comprehensive comparisons of their effects on brain health are lacking. Furthermore, the underlying biological mechanisms through which diet influences neurological and psychiatric outcomes remain largely unclear.MethodsUsing data from the UK Biobank, a large-scale prospective cohort (with participant enrolled between April, 2009, and June, 2012), we used Cox proportional hazards models to analyse the associations between ten dietary patterns (including the Mediterranean diet, the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, and the Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diet, the alternative healthy eating index (AHEI), the healthful plant-based dietary index (hPDI), the planetary health diet index (PHDI), the healthy low-carbohydrate diet (HLCD), the healthy low-fat diet (HLFD), the dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM), and the reversed energy-adjusted diet inflammatory index (rEDII)) and ultra-processed food (UPF) intake with the incident risk of five major brain disorders, including dementia, stroke, depression, anxiety, and Parkinson's disease (PD) in participants with available dietary data who were free of major brain disorders at baseline. The most neuroprotective diet identified was then validated for its associations with a range of brain health outcomes in the U.S. Health and Retirement Study (HRS, with participant data collected between 2013 and 2014) and the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS, with participant data collected in 2018) using generalized linear models to analyze the data. Participants from these two cohorts were required to have complete data on diet, outcomes, and covariates. Finally, we employed a four-way decomposition model with multi-omics data as mediators to explore the underlying mechanisms.FindingsIn the UK Biobank cohort (N = 166,916), MIND diet demonstrated the broadest neuroprotective effects. Over a median follow-up of 10·5 years, 1870 participants were diagnosed with dementia, 980 with PD, 3121 with stroke, 5113 with depression, and 5697 with anxiety. Adherence to the MIND diet was significantly associated with a reduced risk of dementia (hazard ratio [HR] = 0·87, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0·77–0·98), stroke (HR = 0·89, 95% CI = 0·81–0·98), depression (HR = 0·77, 95% CI = 0·71–0·82), and anxiety (HR = 0·82, 95% CI = 0·76–0·88), but not PD (HR = 0·94, 95% CI = 0·80–1·11). In the HRS (n = 4496) and CLHLS (n = 9099) validation cohorts, the MIND diet remained significantly associated with lower risks of depression and anxiety, as well as better cognitive performance (in HRS: better cognitive function (β = 0·78, 95% CI = 0·47–1·10), fewer depressive symptoms (β = −0·22, 95% CI = −0·39 to −0·06), and fewer anxiety symptoms (β = −0·40, 95% CI = −0·78 to −0·02); in CLHLS: lower odds of cognitive impairment (odds ratio [OR] = 0·45, 95% CI = 0·37–0·55), depression (OR = 0·44, 95% CI = 0·38–0·52), and anxiety (OR = 0·49, 95% CI = 0·41–0·60)). In contrast, the intake of UPF was associated with an increased risk for dementia (HR = 1·40, 95% CI = 1·25–1·56), PD (HR = 1·26, 95% CI = 1·08–1·47), depression (HR = 1·42, 95% CI = 1·34–1·50), and anxiety (HR = 1·26, 95% CI = 1·19–1.33) in the UK Biobank cohort. Mechanistically, multi-omics analyses revealed that the MIND diet's protective effects were mediated through several key pathways. A favourable metabolic signature explained a substantial proportion of the reduced risk for stroke (proportion mediated = 60·63%), depression (38·97%), and anxiety (26·06%). Furthermore, slower biological ageing significantly mediated the reduced risk of dementia (19·40%). A structural equation model confirmed the overall protective pathway linking the MIND diet to better brain health via these mediators. Conversely, higher UPF intake was found to increase brain disease risk through detrimental changes in these same metabolic, and ageing pathways.InterpretationThis study supports the MIND diet as a promising intervention for promoting brain health and mitigating neuropsychiatric disorders, while highlighting UPF intake as a significant modifiable risk factor. By identifying specific metabolic, inflammatory, and ageing-related biological pathways, our findings provide potential targets for future research and targeted interventions. However, these observational associations require definitive confirmation through further experimental studies, such as randomised controlled trials.FundingNational Natural Science Foundation of China and the Fujian Provincial Science and Technology Plan Project.Keywords: Dietary pattern, Brain disorders, Metabolomics, Proteomics, Ageing, Cohort study

Research in context

Evidence before this study

To assess the existing evidence, we performed a literature search of PubMed and Google Scholar from inception to July 15, 2025, using a combination of keywords related to dietary patterns (e.g., “dietary pattern”, “Mediterranean diet”, “DASH”, “plant-based diet”) and brain health outcomes (e.g., “brain health”, “cognition”, “dementia”, “stroke”, “depression”). Our review confirmed that while many studies have investigated the effects of single dietary patterns on one or more brain disorders, there is a scarcity of research directly comparing the differential effects of multiple dietary patterns across a spectrum of common brain diseases. Furthermore, studies exploring the underlying mechanisms of these associations from a multi-omics perspective are also lacking.

Added value of this study

This study, using data from a large UK cohort of middle-aged and older adults, identified the Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diet as the most beneficial among ten dietary patterns for brain health. Adherence to the MIND diet was associated with a reduced risk of dementia, stroke, depression, and anxiety.The MIND diet also showed superior risk reduction for dementia, depression, and anxiety when compared to other dietary patterns. These protective associations, particularly for cognitive performance, depression, and anxiety, were validated in two large independent cohorts from the US and China. Mechanistically, our findings suggest the MIND diet may influence brain health by reshaping its metabolic signature, thereby modulating biological pathways related to aging and obesity.

Implications of all the available evidence

This study highlights the substantial value of the MIND diet for brain health intervention and presents an effective strategy for comparing multiple dietary patterns. However, further research is warranted to address the limitations of this study. As an observational study, the findings may be subject to residual confounding, and the reliance on self-reported dietary data could introduce measurement error. Therefore, the definitive confirmation of these diet-disease relationships and their underlying biological pathways will require validation through future experimental research, such as randomised controlled trials.

Introduction

Brain disorders, involving structural and functional impairments, are the leading cause of disability and the second leading cause of death worldwide.1 These conditions include age-related neurological diseases such as dementia, Parkinson's disease (PD), and stroke, alongside mental health disorders like depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder. As the global population continues to age, it is estimated that approximately one-third of individuals will experience a brain disorder in their lifetime.2 Recognising this profound burden, the World Health Organization has identified brain health as a critical global public health priority.3 Given the limited curative options, there is an urgent need to shift toward prevention. Identifying modifiable risk factors and implementing effective primary prevention strategies are paramount in preserving brain health and reducing the global burden of these disorders.

Numerous epidemiological studies and meta-analyses have linked adherence to healthy dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean diet (MedDiet),4,5 Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet,6 and the recently prominent Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diet,7,8 with a reduced risk of specific brain diseases. These patterns are typically rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, high-quality proteins (like fish and poultry), and unsaturated fats, while limiting processed foods, red meat, and refined sugars. Furthermore, mounting evidence links high ultra-processed food (UPF) intake to adverse brain health outcomes.9 However, current literature has several critical limitations. First, studies often assess few dietary patterns or a narrow range of brain outcomes. For instance, some studies focus on a limited number of diets, such as one comparing only four dietary indices with dementia risk,10 while others investigate a limited set of outcomes, as seen in a study linking plant-based diets to only dementia or depression.11 Consequently, a comprehensive comparison and understanding of the relative benefits and generalisability of different dietary archetypes across a broader range of neurological and psychiatric disorders are still lacking. Second, many observational studies are susceptible to reverse causality (i.e., subclinical disease influencing diet), which can obscure the role of diet in primary prevention. To mitigate this, future studies could implement stricter exclusion criteria, such as selecting only participants free of neurological or psychiatric diseases at baseline. Finally, while numerous studies have highlighted potential mechanisms,12,13 a systematic and comprehensive characterisation of the biological pathways linking diet to brain health is a critical gap in the current literature.

To address these critical knowledge gaps, we conducted a comprehensive longitudinal investigation using the large-scale UK Biobank cohort. We systematically compared associations between ten distinct dietary patterns—including the well-known MedDiet,14 MIND diet,15 DASH diet,16 and alternative healthy eating index (AHEI),17 as well as several emerging or specific indices such as the healthful plant-based dietary index (hPDI),18 planetary health diet index (PHDI),19 healthy low-carbohydrate diet (HLCD), healthy low-fat diet (HLFD),20 dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM),21 and dietary inflammatory index (DII)22—and the incidence risk of five major brain diseases (dementia, stroke, depression, anxiety, and PD). A key innovation is our multi-level mechanistic exploration, integrating extensive biological data, which specifically includes: a series of diet-related key clinical biomarkers, such as body mass index (BMI) reflecting obesity,23 Phenotypic Age Acceleration (PhenoAgeAccel) reflecting ageing,24 and the INFLA score related to chronic inflammation25; high-dimensional multi-omics data from metabolomic and proteomic analyses; and neuroimaging markers. Furthermore, we employ advanced statistical methods, including four-way decomposition mediation models and structural equation modelling (SEM), aiming to delineate the complex associations among these factors and systematically map out the specific biological pathway maps by which dietary pattern may influence brain health.

We hypothesise that although various healthy dietary patterns generally exhibit protective effects, the strength and specificity of these associations will vary across different types of brain disorders and dietary pattern. By systematically evaluating multiple dietary patterns and their underlying mechanisms, this study aims to provide a holistic understanding of the “diet-brain axis”. Ultimately, we aim to identify dietary strategies with broad benefits for brain health and elucidate their key biological pathways, providing a robust scientific foundation to inform targeted public health advice, advance personalised nutrition research, and enhance brain resilience to reduce the global burden of brain disorders.

Methods

Data sources and population

This prospective multi-cohort study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) and the STROBE-MR (Mendelian Randomisation) reporting guideline. The UK Biobank is an extensive cohort study that recruited around 500,000 individuals aged 37–73 between 2006 and 2010. Participants supplied comprehensive baseline information through touchscreen surveys, interviews, as well as physical and functional assessments. Additionally, genetic and biological data were collected at 22 assessment centres across England, Scotland, and Wales. We included data from participants between April 2009 and June 2012. After excluding participants with missing dietary data, a baseline diagnosis of neurological or psychiatric disorders, or unreliable questionnaire data, the final analysis included 169,916 eligible participants. Unreliable questionnaire data were defined as a questionnaire completion date after death or a self-report of atypical dietary intake (Field ID: 100020).

To validate our findings, we utilized data from two independent cohorts: the U.S. Health and Retirement Study (HRS, 2013–2014) and the 2018 wave of Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS). Detailed descriptions of these validation cohorts and their inclusion/exclusion criteria are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Ethics

UK Biobank received approval from the National Information Governance Board for Health and Social Care and the National Health Service North West Centre for Research Ethics Committee (reference number: 11/NW/0382). The HRS was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Michigan and the National Institute on Aging (reference number: HUM00061128). The CLHLS protocol was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (reference number: IRB00001052-13074) All participants gave written consent for participation.

Assessment of brain health

In the UK Biobank, selected outcomes included brain diseases such as neurological disorders (dementia, PD, and stroke) and psychiatric disorders (i.e., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, and anxiety). Disorders were identified and classified using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) versions 9 and 10 (ICD-9 and ICD-10) codes (Table S1). Data were obtained from the UK Biobank health outcome dataset (Category 1712) and algorithm-defined outcomes (Category 42). Analyses for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder were omitted, as the low incidence of these conditions during follow-up would have precluded a statistically powerful investigation. Follow-up began on the completion date of the last dietary questionnaire and continued until disease diagnosis, death, loss to follow-up, or the last date of inpatient data collection (for England, October 31, 2022; Scotland, August 31, 2022; Wales, May 31, 2022), whichever came first. The definitions of the brain health indicators in the HRS and CLHLS cohorts are detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

Assessment of dietary pattern score

Based on participants' reported food and nutrient intakes (details of the dietary assessment were provided in the Supplementary Methods), we calculated the following dietary scores: MedDiet, MIND, DASH, AHEI, hPDI, reversed energy-adjusted diet inflammatory index (rEDII), HLFD, HLCD, PHDI, and DI-GM. The UK Biobank dietary data, collected via a validated online questionnaire,26,27 have been proven robust for constructing dietary pattern scores in numerous high-impact studies.28,29 For the CLHLS cohort, a China-specific version of the MIND diet (cMIND) was used.30 Detailed information on the components and scoring criteria for the 10 dietary patterns can be found in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table S2–S11.

UPF were defined according to the NOVA classification (group 4) and categorised into eight groups in the UK Biobank dietary data: beverages, dairy, fruits and vegetables, meat/fish/eggs, sauces/soups, starchy foods, sweet snacks, and savory snacks (Supplementary Table S12). Total UPF intake (g/day) was calculated as the sum across all categories and expressed as a percentage of total food and beverage intake.31

According to the following formula, all dietary pattern scores are standardised based on their respective increment from the 10th to the 90th percentile: xstd=x−q10q90−q10 ,where x is the original score, and q10 and q90 are the 10th and 90th percentiles of the score distribution, respectively.32

Assessment of covariates

Building on prior research on healthy dietary patterns and employing a directed acyclic graph (Supplementary Figure S1), we selected several covariates for our analyses.32,33 A comprehensive list of covariates for all cohorts is detailed in the Supplementary Methods. Potential mediating variables, such as BMI, were excluded from adjustment in the primary analysis to avoid over-adjustment and potential false negatives in this exploratory study.

Definition of potential mediating variable

We investigated three key clinical indicators as potential mediators: BMI, PhenoAgeAccel,34 and an INFLA score (a composite score reflecting chronic inflammation).35 The detailed calculation formulas for PhenoAgeAccel and the INFLA score are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Brain imaging, metabolite, proteomic data

Brain imaging data collection for the UK Biobank follow-up began in 2014, encompassing approximately 60,000 participants. We utilized T1-weighted neuroimaging data from a subset of UK Biobank participants to derive structural brain features, including cortical and subcortical volumes, as well as total white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volume. All brain structural data were normalised into Z-scores for analysis. The detailed imaging acquisition protocol, quality control procedures, and processing software are described in the Supplementary Methods.

This study included 168 metabolic measurements from baseline plasma samples of 86,696 UK Biobank participants. Metabolomic profiling was performed using a high-throughput targeted nuclear magnetic resonance platform. A detailed description of the sample collection, handling, and metabolite quantification methodology is available in the Supplementary Methods. To assess for potential selection bias in the metabolomics sub-analysis, we compared the baseline characteristics of participants with and without available metabolic data and found them to be highly comparable, with only minimal, clinically non-meaningful differences (Supplementary Table S13).

We analysed 2923 plasma proteins from UK Biobank participants, quantified using the Olink Proximity Extension Assay. Protein levels were recorded as normalized protein expression (NPX) values.36 The detailed protocols for sample handling, quality control, and NPX normalization are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

We employed a multi-stage analytical strategy (Supplementary Figure S2). In the primary analysis, we used Cox proportional hazards models within the UK Biobank cohort to examine the associations between 10 dietary pattern scores and 5 brain-related disorders. All models were adjusted for pre-specified covariates, except for alcohol consumption in analyses of alcohol-containing dietary patterns. The Schoenfeld residual method tested the proportional risk hypothesis of the Cox proportional hazards models; all tested hypotheses passed. We applied a Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction to 50 tests (10 patterns × 5 diseases), and used restricted cubic splines (RCS) to model potential non-linear relationships. Furthermore, standardised dietary component intakes within each pattern were analysed to identify disease-specific influential factors.

The optimal dietary pattern was identified as the one exhibiting statistically significant protective associations across the most health outcomes after FDR correction. By this criterion, the MIND diet emerged as the most compelling candidate for our subsequent mechanistic investigation. The optimal dietary pattern was then validated in the HRS and CLHLS cohorts.

To elucidate potential mechanisms, we first employed linear regression models to analyse the associations between the MIND diet and various brain structural features. All model assumptions were verified (see Supplementary Methods for details). To visualize these findings, we utilised the ENIGMA toolbox, chosen for its well-validated, pre-parcellated surface templates. This enabled us to map our statistical results onto these standardised brain surfaces to generate intuitive and anatomically precise heatmaps.37

Subsequently, to investigate potential mediation pathways, we applied a four-way decomposition model, consistent with prior research.38,39 Using the “med4way” command in STATA,40 we evaluated how diet-related clinical indicators (BMI, PhenoAgeAccel, and INFLA score) mediated the MIND diet–disease association. A key advantage of this model is its capacity to explicitly handle potential exposure-mediator interactions, decomposing the total effect into four components: the controlled direct effect, pure indirect effect (PIE), reference interaction, and the mediated interaction. When such an interaction exists, traditional methods can produce biased estimates of direct and indirect effects, or they merge the interaction effect into the direct path, making it difficult to interpret.

For multi-omics analyses, we used linear regression followed by Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression to build and test a MIND diet-related metabolic signature as a mediator.29 Similarly, we identified MIND diet-associated proteins that were also linked to disease risk and performed mediation analysis. Proteins with significant mediating effects then underwent functional enrichment analysis using the Metascape platform.41 Specific statistical parameters for these analyses, including variable selection and significance thresholds, are detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

Finally, we employed a two-sample MR approach (see Supplementary Methods) to assess potential causal effects of the MIND diet-related metabolic signature on PhenoAgeAccel, BMI, and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels. This analysis aimed to validate the causal links between the diet's metabolic footprint and key markers of ageing, obesity, and inflammation. Building on these findings, we constructed a SEM using the R package “lavaan” to systematically investigate the complex interrelationships among the MIND diet, its metabolic signature, ageing, obesity, and brain health outcomes. The model was fitted using a large cohort of 86,696 participants with complete data on dietary intake, plasma metabolomics, BMI, and PhenoAgeAccel. Through this SEM analysis, we sought to quantify the direct and indirect effects linking these factors, evaluating the strength and significance of each pathway within a theoretically informed framework. We did not include above inflammation markers as separate variables in the SEM. This is because these markers, or their core components, are integral to the calculation of the PhenoAgeAccel biomarker. Including them separately would introduce significant multicollinearity. Therefore, the inflammatory pathway is represented by the PhenoAgeAccel variable in this integrated model.

Additionally, we conducted several sensitivity analyses to verify the robustness of our findings, including subgroup analyses, competing risk models, adjustments for reverse causality, and analyses restricted to specific subpopulations. A full description of all nine sensitivity analyses is provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Data processing and primary analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1), with the decomposition analysis carried out in STATA (version 15.1; College Station, TX) using the “med4way” command. The “ENIGMA Toolbox” was utilised in Python (version 3.11.7).

Longitudinal association between 10 healthy dietary pattern adherence scores and brain disorders

In UK biobank, increased adherence to all 10 healthy dietary patterns was associated with a significantly reduced risk of depression (FDR-Q < 0·05; Fig. 4), with adjusted HRs (95% CI) ranging from 0·69 (0·64–0·75; FDR-Q < 0·0001) to 0·92 (0·86–0·99; FDR-Q = 0·041). Similarly, increased adherence to nine dietary patterns, excluding hPDI, was associated with a reduced risk of anxiety. For stroke, after correction for multiple comparisons, adherence scores for MedDiet, MIND, AHEI, DASH, PHDI, and DI-GM were found to be significantly associated with a reduced stroke risk. For dementia, adherence to the MIND (HR = 0·87, 95% CI = 0·77–0·98) and HLCD (HR = 0·87, 95% CI = 0·78–0·98) was significantly associated with a reduced risk. Regarding PD, no significant association was observed for any healthy dietary pattern after multiple comparison correction. Similar results were observed when dietary scores were analysed as categorical variables (by quartiles) (Supplementary Tables S14–S18).

Association between adherence to different dietary patterns and risk of brain diseases (n = 169,916). The forest plot displays hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for risk of brain diseases associated with an increase from the 10th to 90th percentile of adherence to different dietary patterns. HRs are represented by the centre of the error bars and 95% CIs by the extent of the error bars. The model was adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol consumption status (not adjusted for dietary patterns containing alcohol), physical activity, Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI), total energy intake, education level, assessment centre, healthy sleep duration, whether living alone, and family history of disease (selected based on the specific brain disease outcome). FDR-Q reported in the P value were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR).

Using RCS, we further identified non-linear associations between adherence to several dietary patterns and disease risk (Supplementary Figure S3–S12). For instance, although no linear association was observed for the rEDII or HLFD with stroke, RCS analysis revealed a significant U-shaped non-linear relationship for both (Pnonlinear < 0·05). As summarised in Supplementary Figure S13, the MIND diet is a promising strategy for brain disorder intervention. It was the only evaluated pattern to show a linear association with a reduced risk of four brain disorders: dementia, stroke, depression, and anxiety.

In the HRS cohort, higher adherence to the MIND diet was also associated with better cognitive performance and fewer depressive and anxiety symptoms (Supplementary Table S19). Similarly, in the CLHLS cohort, increased adherence to the cMIND diet was associated with a lower prevalence of cognitive impairment (odds ratio [OR] = 0·45, 95% CI = 0·37–0·55), depression (OR = 0·44, 95% CI = 0·38–0·52), and anxiety (OR = 0·49, 95% CI = 0·41–0·60; Supplementary Table S20). The results remained robust in weighted analyses (Supplementary Tables S21 and 22).

Association between dietary pattern components and brain disorders

Fig. 5a illustrates the components of the 10 dietary patterns and their respective roles. Correlations between these patterns showed considerable variability (Fig. 5b); for instance, we found a strong positive correlation between adherence to the MIND and MedDiet, but only a weak correlation with the HLCD. Analysis of individual dietary components revealed that while higher intake of certain dietary components (e.g., sugar-sweetened beverages and sweets) was linked to an elevated risk of multiple adverse outcomes like dementia, depression, and anxiety, higher consumption of others (e.g., olive oil, fruit, and whole grains) was associated with a reduced risk (Fig. 5c & Supplementary Table S23).

Dietary pattern adherence and its association with brain structure volumes

Higher MIND diet adherence was predominantly associated with greater brain structural volumes (Supplementary Figure S14 and Supplementary Tables S24 and 25). These associations were significant in several cortical structures (left inferior parietal and precentral gyri, bilateral lingual gyri) and subcortical structures (bilateral pallidum and thalamus). Furthermore, a higher MIND diet score was significantly associated with a smaller WMH volume.

Inflammation, ageing, and obesity as mediators of the association between MIND diet and brain disorders

In the UK Biobank, greater MIND diet adherence was associated with lower BMI, PhenoAgeAccel, and INFLA scores. In turn, lower levels of BMI, PhenoAgeAccel, and INFLA were associated with a reduced risk of depression, anxiety, and stroke. Subsequent covariate-adjusted models showed that BMI, PhenoAgeAccel, and INFLA all significantly mediated the associations between MIND diet adherence and the incidence of depression, anxiety, and stroke. Specifically, BMI accounted for the highest proportion of the mediation between the MIND diet and depression (proportion mediated = 24·17%, 95% CI: 17·01%–31·33%), while PhenoAgeAccel accounted for the lowest (Supplementary Figure S15). BMI was also the primary mediator for the diet–anxiety association (Supplementary Figure S16). For stroke, BMI and PhenoAgeAccel were main mediators, whereas INFLA's contribution was smallest (Supplementary Figure S17). For dementia (Supplementary Figure S18), only PhenoAgeAccel was a significantly mediator of the MIND diets's protective effect (proportion mediated = 19·40%). Within the HRS cohort, BMI was also found to mediate the link between the MIND diet and depression (Supplementary Figure S19).

Plasma proteomics links MIND diet to brain disorders

Among 17,787 participants with proteomics data, the MIND diet was uniquely associated with a reduced risk of depression (Supplementary Table S26). After Bonferroni correction (nominal P < 0·05/2923), we identified 1300 plasma proteins associated with MIND diet adherence (Supplementary Figure S20 & Supplementary Table S27). Of these MIND-associated proteins, 64 were also linked to depression risk (nominal P < 0·05/1300; Supplementary Table S28). Subsequent mediation analysis confirmed that 63 proteins (e.g., leptin, growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15)) significantly mediated the MIND diet-depression association (Supplementary Figure S20). Pathway enrichment analysis of these protein mediators revealed that processes related to inflammation (e.g., regulation of lymphocyte-mediated immunity), ageing (e.g., efferocytosis and death receptor activity), and metabolism (e.g., negative regulation of lipid storage) are key potential biological mechanisms (Supplementary Figure S20).

Associations among the MIND diet, metabolic features, and brain disorders

In the metabolomics sub-cohort, MIND diet adherence remained significantly associated with a lower risk of dementia, stroke, depression, and anxiety (Supplementary Table S29). To identify the underlying metabolic signature, linear regression first identified 136 metabolites significantly associated with the MIND diet (nominal P < 0·05/168; Supplementary Figure S21 & Supplementary Table S30), from which LASSO regression selected 117 representative metabolites to construct a metabolic signature score (Supplementary Table S31). This signature was composed of various metabolic classes, predominantly cholesterol, lipoproteins, and triglycerides. Mediation analysis revealed that this metabolic signature played a significant mediating role in the associations between the MIND diet and reduced risk of depression (proportion mediated = 38·97%), anxiety (26·06%), and stroke (60·63%). In contrast, its mediating effect on the diet–dementia association was not statistically significant (Supplementary Figure S21).

Causal association between diet-related metabolic signatures and obesity, ageing, and inflammation

Prior to the main SEM analysis, we performed a two-sample MR to test the causal associations of the MIND diet-related metabolic signature with CRP, PhenoAgeAccel, and BMI. We first identified 325 genome-wide significant loci (P < 5 × 10−8) from our GWAS of the metabolic signature in UK Biobank to serve as instrumental variables (Supplementary Figure S22). After clumping, 8 SNPs were included. Using these instruments and public GWAS summary statistics for the outcomes, the primary inverse-variance weighted (IVW) analysis revealed that a genetically predicted higher metabolic signature was causally associated with lower levels of BMI (β = −0·48, P = 0·029), CRP (β = −0·70, P = 0·036), and PhenoAgeAccel (β = −3·91, P = 0·036; Supplementary Figure S23). These causal estimates were directionally consistent with our observational analyses and remained robust across multiple sensitivity tests (e.g., MR-PRESSO, leave-one-out analysis), with no evidence horizontal pleiotropy (Supplementary Tables S32–34, Supplementary Figure S24).

Structural equation model of the diet-brain disorder pathway

SEM in 86,696 participants confirmed several pathways linking the MIND diet to brain health. We identified significant indirect pathway where the MIND diet was linked to a protective metabolic signature (β = 0·333), which in turn lowered brain disorders risk through its association with both lower BMI (β = −0·367) and lower PhenoAgeAccel (β = −0·271). The final paths from BMI (β = 0·054) and PhenoAgeAccel (β = 0·025) to disease risk were also significant. In addition to these mediated pathways, the MIND diet also demonstrated a significant direct protective effect on brain health (Supplementary Figure S23).

Association of ultra-processed foods with brain diseases and potential mechanisms

In a comparative analysis of the 10 healthy dietary patterns and the UPF pattern, we found that after applying FDR correction to all 55 tests (11 patterns × 5 diseases), adherence to the MIND diet remained significantly associated with a lower risk of four brain diseases (FDR-Q < 0·05; Supplementary Table S35). Conversely, high UPF intake was significantly associated with an increased risk of dementia (HR = 1·40, 95% CI = 1·25–1·56, FDR-Q < 0·0001), PD (HR = 1·26, 95% CI = 1·08–1·47, FDR-Q = 0·0076), depression (HR = 1·42, 95% CI = 1·34–1·50, FDR-Q < 0·0001), and anxiety (HR = 1·26, 95% CI = 1·19–1·33, FDR-Q < 0·0001; Supplementary Table S36).

Neuroimaging analysis revealed that UPF intake was associated with a reduced volume of the right pallidum (Supplementary Tables S37 and 38) and an increased volume of WMH (Supplementary Figure S25). To investigate the molecular mechanisms, we conducted a mediation analysis using 2923 plasma proteins (Supplementary Figures S26–S28). The analysis identified GDF-15 as a significant mediator of the association between UPF and dementia. For anxiety, four proteins, including Carbonic Anhydrase 14 (CA14), were found to mediate its association with UPF. Notably, GDF-15 and CA14 also significantly mediated the link between UPF and depression. Pathway enrichment analysis of the protein mediators for the UPF-depression link revealed significant involvement of immune, metabolic, and ageing-related processes. Furthermore, SEM confirmed that UPF intake may increase the risk of brain diseases by altering metabolic signatures, accelerating ageing, and promoting obesity (Supplementary Figure S29).

Sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses revealed several significant interaction effects. For instance, some diet–disease associations were stronger in females or varied by living arrangement (P for interaction <0.05), while stratification by physical activity or smoking status showed no substantial effects (Supplementary Figures S30–34). Additionally, A healthy diet was specifically associated with a lower risk of ischaemic stroke but not haemorrhagic stroke (Supplementary Table S39).

To test stability, a stringent analysis of participants with at least two dietary assessments showed the MIND diet's links with dementia, depression, and anxiety remained significant (Supplementary Table S40). Although the linear association with stroke was no longer significant, RCS analysis revealed a non-linear relationship (Pnonlinear = 0·027), suggesting a protective effect up to a certain threshold (Supplementary Figures S35). The SEM still support the pathway linking the MIND diet to brain diseases via metabolic features, accelerated ageing, or BMI (Supplementary Figures S36). Additionally, our primary findings were consistently replicated after excluding participants with unreliable energy intake (Supplementary Table S41, and Supplementary Figures S37), applying inverse probability weighting (Supplementary Table S42), and performing the analysis after excluding participants incident disorders in the first two-year (Supplementary Table S43). The competing risks analysis also confirmed that higher adherence to the MIND diet was significantly associated with a lower risk of dementia, stroke, depression, and anxiety (Supplementary Table S44).

To test the independence of the associations for the MIND diet, we adjusted for nine other healthy dietary scores in the models. The protective associations with dementia and stroke were attenuated after these adjustments, suggesting their benefits may be attributable to features shared across healthy diets (Supplementary Table S45 and S46). In contrast, the protective associations with depression and anxiety remained significant, indicating a more independent effect (P < 0.05; Supplementary Table S47 and S48).

After adjusting for cardiovascular risk factors, higher MIND diet adherence remained associated with a reduced risk of both stroke and dementia (Supplementary Table S49). Furthermore, mediation analysis identified systolic blood pressure and the triglyceride-glucose index as significant mediators of the MIND diet–stroke association (Supplementary Figures S38).

Discussion

This study comprehensively investigated the associations between 10 different dietary patterns, UPF and multiple brain disorders. The MIND diet was most consistently protective, linked to a reduced risk of dementia, stroke, depression, and anxiety. Conversely, UPF intake was associated with an increased risk of dementia, PD, depression, and anxiety. Mechanistically, the protective effects of the MIND diet on brain disorders were significantly mediated by inflammation, biological ageing, and obesity. Integrating proteomics, we pinpointed protein mediators of the MIND diet-depression link, which were enriched in inflammatory and metabolic pathways. The SEM further revealed that the MIND diet likely acts by first remodelling metabolic signatures, which in turn affect obesity and ageing to ultimately reduce brain disorder risk. Similar mechanisms appeared to underlie the adverse effects of UPF.

Analysis of the full cohort showed that various healthy dietary patterns were significantly associated with a lower risk of depression or anxiety, with the MIND diet being particularly effective. In mutual adjustment models, the protective effect of the MIND diet was found to be independent of most other dietary patterns, except for the MedDiet. While direct comparisons are scarce, one meta-analysis systematically evaluated diets such as the Mediterranean, DASH, and DII in relation to depression. This review identified the strongest evidence for the link between the Mediterranean diet and depression incidence.42 Extending these observations, our research further demonstrates that the MIND diet is also highly effective as an intervention for depression. Subgroup analysis revealed that the benefits of the MIND and MedDiet on anxiety were non-significant for individuals living alone, possibly because social isolation impairs long-term dietary adherence.43 These results underscore the need to consider individual contexts, like the social environment, in dietary recommendations for mental health.

For stroke, increased adherence to six dietary patterns (MedDiet, MIND AHEI, DASH, PHDI, and DI-GM) was significantly associated with a reduced risk. When analysing stroke subtypes, these patterns were primarily linked to a reduced risk of ischaemic stroke, with no significant associations for haemorrhagic stroke or subarachnoid hemorrhage. Our findings reinforce known protective associations for MedDiet, MIND, AHEI, and DASH.44, 45, 46 Furthermore, two novel dietary indices, DI-GM and PHDI, also demonstrated protective effects against stroke, further supporting the findings of previous exploratory studies.19,47 For rEDII, RCS analysis revealed a U-shaped association with stroke risk, aligning with another study on dietary inflammatory index and brain disorders.48 For HLFD and HLCD, the lack of association is consistent with previous findings from a large randomised controlled trial (RCT) and a meta-analysis, respectively, suggesting these patterns may be suboptimal for stroke prevention.49,50

Our findings provide strong evidence for an association between the MIND diet and a reduced risk of dementia. Notably, recent RCTs of the MIND diet have yielded mixed results. For instance, a large-scale trial by Barnes and colleagues found no significant effect on its primary cognitive endpoint over three years.51 In contrast, a trial by Arjmand et al. in healthy women with obesity demonstrated that the MIND diet could ameliorate the adverse effects of obesity on cognition and brain structure.52 Considering our observational and mechanistic findings, we hypothesise that these discrepancies may be driven by factors such as intervention duration and population heterogeneity, including the specific metabolic profiles of participants (e.g., obesity). In our analysis, the MIND diet showed a stronger protective effect against dementia than the MedDiet, likely because it specifically emphasises neuroprotective foods like berries and leafy green vegetables. This finding is consistent with a systematic review which concluded that the MIND diet offers greater protection against cognitive decline than the MedDiet or DASH diet.53 Thus, our results do not diminish the general value of the MedDiet but instead suggest that for dementia prevention, a dietary strategy highly targeted at neuroprotection may be more potent than adhering to a broadly healthy pattern. The previously reported association between the DII and dementia was not replicated,22 possibly because our exclusion of individuals with baseline brain disorders interrupted an indirect pathway mediated by depression.54 The lack of an association for PHDI, in contrast to other studies,55 may also reflect population-specific effects, as its benefits can be dependent on factors like socioeconomic status.56

Previous research on dietary patterns and PD has yielded mixed findings.57 In our study, while no healthy dietary pattern was significantly protective, we identified UPF intake as a significant dietary risk factor for PD. Furthermore, a study involving 42,853 participants linked long-term UPF consumption to non-motor prodromal features of PD.58 In our research, high intake of UPF was also observed to be associated with an increased risk of dementia, depression, and anxiety, and similar linear associations have been observed in other studies.9,31

Building on prior work linking diet to brain morphology,59 our analysis reveals that the MIND diet may mitigate brain atrophy by impacting specific brain regions. The MIND diet was associated with the volumes of the pallidum and thalamus, crucial for cognitive and emotional regulation.60,61 Consistent with this, UPF intake was linked to reduced pallidum volume. The MIND diet was also associated with the integrity of the temporal and orbitofrontal lobes, key regions for memory and emotional control.62,63 We also observed opposing effects of different dietary patterns on WMH volume, an established biomarker for dementia,64 stroke,65 and depression.66 Specifically, we confirmed a negative association for the MIND diet with WMH volume, whereas UPF intake was positively associated.

Healthy diets improve systemic health by lowering BMI, reducing chronic inflammation, and slowing biological ageing.23,25,67 Furthermore, there is broad scientific consensus that plasma metabolites,68 physiological ageing processes,69 chronic inflammatory states,70 and obesity71 are all closely associated with brain health. Moreover, prior studies have begun to show that individual factors, including BMI, PhenoAgeAccel, and inflammatory markers mediate the effects of diet on outcomes such as dementia,72 depression,54,73,74 and stroke.75,76 Building on this, our mediation analysis further reveals a non-uniform pattern, where the pathways linking the MIND diet to various brain disorders differ. Specifically, a substantial portion (24·17%) of the diet's protective effect on depression was mediated by BMI, a contribution quantitatively larger than that of PhenoAgeAccel. Furthermore, a comprehensive metabolic signature also a large proportion of the diet-depression association (38·97%) and a majority of the diet–stroke association (60·63%). In stark contrast, the diet–dementia association was not significantly mediated by these metabolic factors, but rather through a more specific, ageing-related pathway, with PhenoAgeAccel explaining 19·40% of this relationship. In the HRS validation cohort, mediation analysis revealed that while BMI significantly mediated the association between the MIND diet and depression, no such effect was observed for cognitive performance, a finding that both supports our primary results from the UK Biobank and extends previous research within HRS cohort.7 These findings suggest that the benefits of the MIND diet for mental and cerebrovascular disorders are largely driven by broad metabolic improvements, whereas its benefits for dementia may operate through ageing-related biological pathways. To test the complex network through which the MIND diet influences the five brain diseases as a composite outcome, we utilized SEM, which confirmed a key pathway: the MIND diet appears to influence the onset of brain disorders by first remodelling its associated metabolic signature, which subsequently impacts the processes of ageing and obesity. Further supporting this, our MR analysis provided causal evidence supporting the link from the metabolic signature of MIND diet to these same markers of ageing and obesity. Our findings are corroborated by a prior study suggesting that the MIND diet-frailty (a manifestation of ageing) association may be mediated by metabolic signatures77; however, our study provides several novel contributions that extend this work. First, our analysis specifically focuses on a healthy baseline cohort free of multiple brain disorders, a key distinction as diet–metabolite associations may differ from the general population. Second, by integrating MR and SEM as described, we offer a significant mechanistic extension, providing a more comprehensive causal chain linking diet, metabolic signatures, ageing, and brain diseases. In conclusion, by systematically integrating multiple dietary patterns, biological mediators, and brain disorder endpoints, our study provides an unprecedentedly detailed roadmap of the potential pathways for dietary intervention in brain health.

Although limited by its smaller sample size, our proteomics mediation analysis nonetheless identified key biomarkers. For instance, both leptin and GDF-15 were found to be significant mediators of the association between the MIND diet, UPF and depression. While prior studies have established associations of leptin and GDF-15 with depression,78,79 our research is the first to link them to the MIND diet and UPF, offering new mechanistic insights. Furthermore, proteomics-based enrichment analysis for the diet-depression link identified ageing-related and metabolic mechanisms, corroborating our SEM findings and highlighting the utility of proteomics in elucidating diet–disease associations. The anticipated completion of proteomics sequencing for the remaining 450,000 UK Biobank participants promises to further delineate these potential diet-brain disease mechanisms.

Key strengths of this study include its prospective design, large sample size, and systematic comparison of 10 dietary patterns against risks for multiple major brain disorders (dementia, stroke, PD, depression, and anxiety). Furthermore, we integrated multi-level biological data, employing advanced statistical methods like four-way decomposition and SEM, to deeply explore how diet influences brain health. This provided comprehensive evidence for specific physiological pathways, identifying both common and unique protective routes.

However, limitations exist: First, as an observational study, residual confounding and reverse causality cannot be fully excluded despite adjustments. Second, dietary data relied on self-reporting, which is prone to recall bias, measurement error, and may not adequately capture long-term dietary dynamics. Finally, the definitive confirmation of the revealed biological pathways requires further validation through targeted experimental research (e.g., RCTs or basic science experiments).

In conclusion, this comprehensive longitudinal study strongly demonstrates that adherence to the MIND diet is associated with a reduced risk of multiple brain disorders, including depression, anxiety, stroke, and dementia, showcasing its broad neuroprotective potential. Conversely, the intake of UPF represents a dietary pattern detrimental to brain health. Our multi-faceted mechanistic investigation reveals that the benefits of the MIND diet are mediated through multiple pathways, including improved metabolic health, attenuated biological ageing, reduced chronic inflammation, favourable body composition, and preserved brain structure, whereas UPF harms brain health through similar but detrimental pathways. Overall, this research provides a detailed, multi-layered roadmap of potential diet-brain interactions, significantly advancing our understanding of how dietary choices can impact neurological and psychiatric health.
