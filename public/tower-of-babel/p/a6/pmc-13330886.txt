Outcomes of open abdominal surgery at a single-centre tertiary referral hospital in Tanzania: a prospective, observational, single-arm cohort study
Authors: Nathan Brand, Larry Akoko, Emmanuel Obedi, Ally Mwanga, Godfrey Sama Philipo, Charles Komba, Marissa Boeck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess the outcomes of patients undergoing open abdominal surgery at a National Referral Hospital in Tanzania.DesignA prospective, observational, single-arm cohort study.SettingDar es Salaam, Tanzania.ParticipantsPatients who underwent open abdominal surgery between August 2022 and July 2023 were included. Patients were enrolled if they provided informed consent to participate and were ≥18 years of age. Patients were excluded if they were lost to follow-up, were admitted outside the department of surgery or were still admitted since the index surgery at the end of the study.Main outcome measuresThe primary outcome was a composite measurement of mortality that includes patients who died during the index admission and those who died up to 30 days postdischarge. Secondary outcomes included surgical complications, unplanned readmissions, economic burden and functional outcomes. Clinically relevant factors identified a priori and with a p value of ≤0.2 in the univariable model were tested in a multivariable logistic regression to identify independent risk factors for composite mortality, with backward stepwise elimination using a statistical significance set at a p value <0.05 and 95% CI not including 1.0.ResultsOf 626 patients who had an open abdominal surgery, 555 (88.7%) met the inclusion criteria, of which 120 (21.6%) died. Of the deaths, 61 (51%) occurred during the index admission, 48 (40%) outside the hospital and 11 (9%) following unplanned readmission. Among the surviving 435 (78%) patients who participated in the 30-day follow-up, those without insurance were less likely to return to work (7.3% vs 16% insured) and had higher out-of-pocket expenses ($321 vs $82 insured).ConclusionsOpen abdominal surgery carries a composite mortality rate of 22% with almost half of deaths occurring after index hospital discharge. These findings underscore the need for routine follow-up, early complication identification and management and universal health coverage to improve outcomes, and reduce financial hardships.

Introduction

In 2015, the World Health Assembly recognised surgical and anaesthesia services as essential components of universal health coverage.1 This declaration prompted many countries to prioritise improving their surgical capacity. Tanzania responded by developing its first National Surgical, Obstetrics, and Anesthesia Plan (NSOAP) in 2018.2 The plan identified surgical care as a public health priority, noting that 19.3% of deaths were attributable to surgically treatable diseases. It set ambitious goals to increase access to emergency surgical services and protection from catastrophic expenditures for surgical patients by 2030. To enhance the quality of existing surgical services, the Ministry of Health emphasised the importance of measuring surgical outcomes and implementing process improvement programmes at all facilities.2

Despite the ambitious NSOAP targets, detailed data on surgical outcomes remain limited in Tanzania. While a retrospective study at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH) found a 7.6% emergent return-to-theatre rate for laparotomies, postoperative outcomes for the majority of patients were not documented.3 Another study of 765 non-obstetric laparotomies occurring at 20 different healthcare centres around Lake Victoria reported a 3.9% in-hospital postoperative mortality rate over a year.4 However, both studies lacked postdischarge outcome data, limiting our understanding of the long-term impact of surgical interventions. Demographic analysis from these studies revealed that 70% of laparotomy patients were under 50 years of age, emphasising the economic burden of illness on working-age individuals and their households. As 26.4% of Tanzanians are reported to be unable to meet their basic needs, healthcare costs can be catastrophic.5 The NSOAP estimated that 85% of Tanzanians are at risk for impoverishing expenditure from surgical services,2 a finding confirmed by a recent study on musculoskeletal injuries.6

The financial implications of laparotomy have been studied in Rwanda, where 28% of 280 patients undergoing surgical treatment for peritonitis were found to be at risk for catastrophic medical expenses.7 However, this finding must be interpreted cautiously in the Tanzanian context because health insurance coverage in Rwanda was 98% among study participants. In contrast, for Tanzania, the penetrance of health insurance is estimated to be only 7.1%,8 and the effect of insurance status on catastrophic medical expenditure and other economic outcomes in the general surgery population in Tanzania is unknown. A recent study of trauma patients at Muhimbili found that over 85% of injured patients experienced catastrophic medical expenditure,9 but the rate of out-of-pocket expenditure and catastrophic medical expenditure among surgical patients is currently unknown.

To address the need for more comprehensive, country-specific data, we aimed to assess the clinical, functional and economic outcomes of patients undergoing open abdominal surgery at MNH, a busy National Referral Hospital conducting general surgery in Tanzania, through the EXploring LAParotomy Outcomes study for Surgical Quality Improvement at a Tertiary Facility in Tanzania. We hypothesised a significant proportion of patients would experience postoperative complications and death, especially post hospital discharge when patient outcomes typically are not captured, and would be subjected to catastrophic expenses from medical care received.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted an observational, prospective, single-arm cohort study of patients undergoing both elective and emergency open abdominal surgery at MNH in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for prospective cohort studies to ensure accurate reporting of our results (online supplemental material).10 Tanzania is a low-middle income country in East Africa with a population of 65 million, and Dar es Salaam is the commercial capital with a population of 6 million people. The hospital is a public entity serving people living in Dar es Salaam and is a National Tertiary Referral Centre and a teaching hospital for Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences. The hospital has 1500 beds, of which 200 and 16 are general wards and intensive care unit (ICU) wards reserved for surgical units, respectively. The hospital receives both emergency and elective surgical cases from within the city and around the country and has operating theatres dedicated to emergency surgery and qualified surgeons available 24/7.

Participants

We included all consenting patients equal to or over the age of 18 years who were admitted to the general surgery wards and underwent an open abdominal surgery between 1 August 2022 and 31 July 2023. Patients were excluded from the study if they were lost to follow-up, were admitted to a different department than surgery within MNH or remained admitted at the time of study closure.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome of interest was a composite measurement of mortality, recorded as any postoperative death occurring during the index hospital admission or up to 30 days postdischarge. Secondary outcomes were: (1) occurrence of complications, defined as any unexpected event requiring medical intervention following surgery; (2) readmissions, defined as unplanned return to the inpatient hospital ward after index hospital discharge following surgery; (3) economic burden, measured by impact of medical expenditures related to surgical care on daily living (eg, inability to pay basic bills defined as food and housing expenses, out of pocket expenses for surgical care, return to work) and (4) 30-day postdischarge functional outcomes. Catastrophic medical expenditure for this study was defined as a surgical expense that was >10% of the annual household income.11

Predictor variables

Data collected included patient socioeconomic demographics: age, sex, level of education, household income, insurance status and residential home ownership. Clinical characteristics collected included: American Society of Anesthesiologist (ASA) classification, comorbidities, smoking history, type of surgery (elective or emergent), wound classification, postoperative disposition, postoperative complications, 30-day readmissions, postoperative clinical diagnosis and procedure performed.

At 30 days postdischarge, we collected data on ongoing symptoms, current functional status, new diagnoses and ability to return to work. Variables to determine the economic impact of open abdominal surgery included health insurance status, total number of people in the household, household income, ability to pay for medical expenses, need to borrow money to pay for medical expenses, change in household income since surgery, change in food consumption within the household since surgery and total cost of surgical intervention that was paid out of pocket.

Data collection

Data were prospectively collected directly from patients in-person by our study team during the patient’s index hospitalisation and at 30 days postdischarge over the phone. The study team rounded daily on patients to identify any postoperative complication(s). 30 days after discharge from the hospital, participants were contacted via mobile phone by the study team to assess their health status and the financial impact of their surgical treatment on their household via a structured questionnaire. If a patient was readmitted, data were collected directly from the patient in-person. The data collection tools used in this study are provided in online supplemental file 1.

Data analysis

Study data were collected and managed using REDCap12 13 electronic data capture tools hosted at University of California, San Francisco. REDCap data were analysed using STATA BE 17. Participants lost to follow-up were excluded from data analysis. Categorical variables were summarised using descriptive statistics while continuous variables were summarised as measures of central tendencies. We identified missing values ranging from 0.2% to 25%, impacting eight variables (sex, insurance status, type of surgery, home ownership, active/past smoker, comorbidity, ASA, household income), which we examined via a missingness plot and noted in the footnotes of the tables with percent missing and updated denominators. Associations between variables and the study outcome measures were assessed using χ2 and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests as appropriate. Fisher’s exact test was used instead of χ2 for any analysis that had a frequency of <5 for categorical variables, and Student’s t-test was used for continuous variables.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors for death among patients undergoing open abdominal surgery. To prevent bias, all variables with p≤0.2 from table 1 were initially included in our model using the methodology described by Bursac et al.14 Of note, these variables were from our data collection tools, which were intentionally created to capture clinically meaningful items relevant to our outcome(s) of interest a priori. A total of 17 candidate variables were included in the initial model: age over 75, ASA classification, presence of comorbidity, graduation from primary school, graduation from secondary school, being uninsured, owning a home, emergency surgery, dirty wound, postoperative admission to ICU, preoperative diagnosis of cancer, preoperative diagnosis of peritonitis, small bowel resection, enteral bypass, relaparotomy, biopsy and presence of postoperative complication. Social determinants or drivers of health, such as education and housing, were included as these factors have repeatedly been shown to impact health outcomes, potentially more than direct medical care.15

We then used reverse stepwise selection technique to remove variables with p>0.1, which resulted in the final model. 32 (5.8%) of participants had missing values and were not included in the complete case multivariable analysis. Multiple imputation by chained equations was performed using 20 imputed datasets and included all variables with missing data (n=8) alongside the outcome variable, regardless of whether they were retained in the final logistic regression model (n=2, ASA and insurance status), consistent with recommended inclusive imputation strategies. Results from the imputed analysis were compared with the complete case results as a sensitivity analysis and are shared in online supplemental table 1. Broadly consistent results between the two analyses suggest missing data did not significantly influence our study conclusions. We assessed multicollinearity among predictors using variance inflation factors (VIFs) from a regression model using the final predictor variables. The largest VIF was 1.24 and the mean VIF was 1.1, indicating negligible collinearity. Furthermore, the final model demonstrated adequate fit based on the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (χ²=6.81, df=8, p=0.56) and an area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve of 0.82.

While stepwise regression has well-recognised limitations, including potential instability of variable selection and optimism in model performance, this approach was chosen to achieve model parsimony given the number of outcome events relative to candidate predictors (120 events/deaths). While the final multivariable model contained eight predictors yielding 15 events per variable, well above the well-recognised 10 events per variable threshold16 the initial candidate variable list had 17 variables (seven events per variable), which could be considered a limitation of the study, although the 10 events per variable threshold has recently been challenged.17 To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, on all 17 candidate variables with the optimal penalty parameter selected by cross-validation. This selected 10 variables, seven (87.5%) of which were consistent with those identified through our stepwise backward elimination. Three additional variables were selected by LASSO (comorbidity, emergency case, small bowel resection) and one variable was not retained (intra-abdominal infection), potentially reflecting differences in how each method handles collinearity between variables. The broad consistency between methods supports our variable selection approach. Interaction terms were not explored in our analysis.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not directly involved in the design of the study nor in the interpretation of the results due to lack of funding for this scale of involvement. The authors will aim to pursue patient and public involvement in future studies.

Findings

Participant demographics

In total, 626 patients underwent open abdominal surgery during the 1 year study period. Of these patients, 45 (7%) were not enrolled, either due to inadequate research staff coverage over the holiday season or because consent was not able to be obtained (figure 1). Of the 581 patients enrolled, 23 (4%) were lost to follow-up after discharge and an additional 3 (0.5%) had not been discharged from their index hospitalisation by the end of the study, leaving 555 for the final analysis (figure 1). Median age was 50 years (IQR 35–62), 307 (55%) were men, 457 (82%) graduated primary school, 396 (81%) owned a home and 265 (48%) were uninsured (table 1). The most common diagnosis was cancer 255 (46%), followed by peritonitis 50 (9%) and peptic ulcer disease 47 (8.5%). The top three procedures performed were colon resection and/or colostomy 144 (26%), small bowel resection 89 (16%) and enteral bypass 51 (9.2%). 151 (27%) had a postoperative complication and 78 (14%) were readmitted within 30 days. 37% of the surgeries were emergent during the study period and of those, 68% were uninsured, p=<0.001.

Primary outcome: composite mortality (index hospitalisation or within 30 days of discharge)

Overall, 120 (22%) patients died postoperatively either during their index hospitalisation or within 30 days postdischarge. Specifically, 61 (51%) died during their index hospitalisation, 11 (9%) died after being readmitted to MNH and 48 (40%) died outside the hospital within 30 days of discharge. Those that died were more likely to be older, more likely to have a comorbidity and less likely to have graduated from primary school. Clinically, postoperative death was associated with emergency surgery, postoperative ICU admission, dirty or infected wounds and postoperative complications (table 1). Of the 151 (27%) patients known to have a postoperative complication, 75 (50%) died.

Multivariable analysis using a stepwise approach described in the Methods section showed that higher ASA Classification (OR 1.9, p=0.007), cancer diagnosis (OR 3.2, p=0.001), open abdominal surgery for biopsy (OR 7.1, p<0.001) and postoperative complication (OR 4.9, p<0.001) were most significantly associated with postoperative death. In addition, being uninsured (OR 1.8, p=0.05) and specifically having a postoperative intra-abdominal infection (OR 3.2, p=0.01) were also significantly associated with death (table 2). Those who had a complication and died compared with those who survived were more likely to be uninsured (OR 5.4, p=0.003) and have a higher ASA classification (OR 3.2, p=0.007) by multivariable analysis.

Secondary outcomes: postoperative complications and hospital readmissions

During the index hospitalisation, 151 (27%) patients experienced at least one postoperative complication, for a total of 321 complications. The three most common complications, which comprised 54% of the total, were surgical site infection (22%), bacteremia (19%) and the need to return to the operating room (13%) (figure 2). Multivariable analysis showed that ICU admission (OR 5.5, p<0.0001), cancer diagnosis (OR 2.8, p<0.0001), dirty wounds (OR 2.3, p=0.04) and outside hospital transfer needing a re-laparotomy (OR 4.5, p=0.01) were all associated with having a postoperative complication.

Postoperative complication (patients could experience >1 complication) occurrences among patients undergoing open abdominal surgery. ICU, intensive care unit.

Of the 494 patients discharged from MNH after their index hospitalisation, 78 (14%) were readmitted to MNH within 30 days of discharge. Of these patients, 15 (26%) required an additional operation during their readmission. Using multivariable logistic regression, only cancer diagnosis was associated with risk of hospital readmission (OR 1.9, p=0.02).

Secondary outcomes: 30-day functional and economic outcomes

Among the surviving 435 patients who participated in the 30-day follow-up phone survey to assess their functional and economic postoperative outcomes, those without insurance compared with insured patients were less likely to return to work (7.3% uninsured vs 16% insured), had higher out-of-pocket expenses ($321 vs $82), were more likely to be unable to pay basic bills (64% vs 23%) and had a higher rate of catastrophic medical expenditure (56% vs 11%) (table 3).

Discussion

In this study of open abdominal surgery outcomes at a National Referral Hospital in Tanzania that provides care to general surgery patients, the composite measurement of mortality that includes patients who died during the index admission and those who died up to 30 days postdischarge was 22%, with close to half of those patients dying outside the hospital within 30-days of discharge. These findings highlight the need for postdischarge follow-up to accurately capture surgical outcomes. This composite mortality rate of 22% is significantly higher than the 3.9% reported in a recent analysis of hospitals in the Lake Zone of Tanzania.4 However, the previous study differs from ours in that it only included health centres, district hospitals and regional hospitals where the acuity and complexity of surgery being performed is not comparable to the national referral hospital. In addition, the Lake Zone study only followed patients during their index hospitalisation and did not include postdischarge follow-up. Our study population would be more comparable to patients at Mulago Hospital, the National Referral Hospital in Kampala, Uganda. There, a recent study of predictors of post-laparotomy morbidity and mortality reported an in-hospital postoperative mortality rate of 10.6%, but patients were not followed postdischarge.18 These findings from the National Referral Hospitals in Uganda and Tanzania demonstrate that open abdominal surgery in the East African context is a high-risk procedure with considerable postoperative mortality. This reality presents a significant challenge and opportunity to identify the causes of this high mortality rate and areas for improvement.

In our study, patients with cancer were more likely to die postoperatively than were patients whose open abdominal surgery was for benign disease. However, comparisons with other studies are difficult because despite the call for audits of surgical oncology outcomes by the recent Lancet Oncology Commission of Global Cancer Surgery to identify opportunities for standardisation and improvement,19 few studies have focused specifically on the outcomes of patients undergoing abdominal surgery for the treatment of cancer in Africa. In addition, it is important to note that in our study, cancer was diagnosed clinically by the operating surgeon and did not rely on a pathologic diagnosis, and stage was not routinely documented. A single-centre study of outcomes for patients treated with colorectal cancer in rural Kenya found a significant difference in 30-day survival between those treated with curative surgery (98%) and those treated with palliative surgery (86%).20 In our cohort, the surgical procedure most associated with composite mortality was an exploratory laparotomy and biopsy, suggesting that these patients had advanced cancer, and no additional surgical intervention could be done. This finding also suggests that Tanzanian patients with suspected cancer should undergo a thorough preoperative evaluation before an operation is considered, to ensure they are physically able to tolerate abdominal surgery and would benefit from an invasive intervention.

Not surprisingly, having a postoperative complication was a significant risk factor for postoperative death in our cohort, with 27% of those who died having at least one postoperative complication. The most common complication was surgical site infection. This complication rate is higher than the 18.2% reported in the Africa Surgical Outcomes Study (ASOS), which assessed postoperative outcomes among 11 422 patients from 247 hospitals in 25 countries.21 However, ASOS included all surgical patients, including those undergoing orthopaedic surgeries and caesarean sections, the two other bellwether procedures in addition to laparotomy.19 In that study, patients undergoing surgery of the lower and upper gastrointestinal tract comprised 2.6% and 8.3% of the entire cohort respectively, but accounted for 12.1% and 19.2% of all post-operative deaths, highlighting the disproportionate risk of abdominal surgery as compared with caesarean sections and orthopaedic surgery.21 The surgical site infection rate of 12% in our study is similar to the 14.4% reported in a recent meta-analysis of the morbidity and mortality of patients with abdominal surgical emergencies in Sub-Saharan Africa.22 In contrast, surgical site infections for the same emergencies are estimated to occur only in 2%–5% of surgical patients in the USA.23

In our study, postoperative complications were associated with a near fivefold increase in odds of composite mortality. While our study is purely observational and cannot assess temporal relationships or causality, this association suggests the importance of early identification and treatment of complications. This concept of ‘failure to rescue’ has been well described in high-income settings, but a recent African clinical trial looking at addressing this problem by enhanced surveillance by nurses and caregivers compared with standard of care did not significantly affect postoperative mortality.24 These contradictory findings imply that more research is needed on potential modifiable risk factors for post-operative mortality in low-resource settings.

Finally, our analysis identified uninsured patients to be at higher risk of death within 30 days of surgery, dying after a complication, undergoing emergency surgery and experiencing a catastrophic medical expenditure after being discharged from the hospital. Little has been published on the effect of insurance status on clinical outcomes of surgical patients in East Africa. However, our findings are consistent with studies looking at hospitalised medical patients at public referral hospitals in the region. For example, a review of medical patients admitted at Moi Teaching and Referral Hospital in Eldoret, Kenya, found that 24% of uninsured patients died compared with 11% of insured patients (p<0.001).25 Over half of uninsured patients reported experiencing a catastrophic medical expenditure compared with only 11% of insured patients. Given that an aim of the Tanzanian NSOAP is for no patient to suffer from a catastrophic medical expenditure, our findings provide evidence that substantial progress needs to be made in protecting vulnerable surgical patients. On 6 December 2023, the president of Tanzania signed into law the Universal Health Insurance Bill, which aims to provide universal health coverage to every Tanzanian citizen.26 While questions remain regarding how this bill will be financed, the association of insurance status with clinical and economic outcomes among open abdominal surgery patients in our cohort suggests that universal healthcare may have substantial benefit toward improving surgical access and safety in Tanzania.

Our study has a few limitations. Because it was an observational study that identified many risk factors for postoperative composite mortality and catastrophic medical expenditure, causality cannot be proven. Potential limitations of the statistical analysis with sensitivity analyses are described in the Methods section. Despite statistically significant ORs, wide CIs in the logistic regression model indicate limited precision in some of the effect sizes, which should be interpreted with caution. Variables with substantial missing data, notably household income (25% missing), may have been excluded from candidate variable selection for the multivariable logistic regression due to attenuation of their association with the outcome because of missingness rather than true lack of effect. While household income was included in the imputation model, and a similar variable regarding owning a home was included in the initial candidate variable selection model, residual confounding by socioeconomic factors cannot be excluded. A proportion of missing data is likely attributable to our inability to collect information from patients who died prior to study completion, suggesting data may not be missing at random. As multiple imputation by chained equations assumes data is Missing at Random (MAR), this represents an inherent limitation of the study. However, the broadly consistent results between complete case and imputed analyses provide reassurance that this potential violation of the MAR assumption did not influence our findings. This is a known challenge in prospective observational studies where the outcome of interest is mortality, and future studies are needed to confirm or refute these findings. In addition, this was a single-centre study at a high-volume referral hospital, with a substantially more complex patient population than is typically seen at district or regional referral hospitals, which may limit generalisability to other hospitals in Tanzania. Furthermore, cancer was diagnosed based on clinical assessment rather than pathologic confirmation. This approach may have resulted in some patients being incorrectly diagnosed with cancer, while others with cancer may have been missed. Finally, those who died outside the hospital had unknown cause of death and could have had a complication that was not captured, so the reported complication rate likely undercounts the true rate.

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of postoperative outcomes among patients undergoing open abdominal surgery at the National Referral Hospital in Dar es Salaam and identifies patients who develop postoperative complications as being at particularly high risk for perioperative composite mortality. These findings support prioritising early recognition and aggressive management of complications, strengthening perioperative risk stratification and systematically incorporating key patient metrics into routine health records. Furthermore, our results establish a baseline benchmark for national quality improvement initiatives and highlight the need to invest in postoperative monitoring systems, including structured post-discharge follow-up, to more accurately capture both clinical, functional and economic outcomes.
