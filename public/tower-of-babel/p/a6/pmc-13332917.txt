Cerium oxide and bismuth oxide nanocomposites as clay swelling inhibitors in water based drilling fluids
Authors: Ghaleb Oriquat, Basim Abdul Alrhym, A C Umamaheshwer Rao, Shivam P Chaudhary, Pardeep Singh Bains, Ripendeep Singh, Murotov Nurshod, Rasulbek Eshmetov, Ahmad Abumalek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWellbore instability driven by reactive clay swelling remains a critical and costly challenge in the application of water-based drilling fluids. The core objective of this study is to evaluate the fundamental clay hydration inhibition potential of CeO2–Bi2O3 bimetallic nanocomposite to ensure robust operational stability. The nanomaterial was synthesized via co-precipitation, subjected to 1-hour ultrasonic homogenization and 1-week static aging, and subsequently evaluated using dynamic light scattering, zeta potential, static filtration (100 psi, 60 °C), and hot rolling tests (105 °C). Experimental results demonstrated that an optimal nanocomposite concentration of 500 ppm fundamentally arrests clay hydration via double-layer compression (− 38.7 mV) and pore plugging, yielding an exceptional dynamic shale cutting recovery of 93%, significantly outperforming the standard 3 wt% KCl benchmark (78%). System compatibility assessments proved the additive acts as a non-damaging agent, maintaining vital plastic viscosity and yield point parameters while improving API filtration control in polymeric (9.5 to 7.8 mL) and weighted mud systems. In conclusion, the CeO2–Bi2O3 nanocomposite serves as a highly efficient chemical inhibitor that mechanically stabilizes wellbores and prevents fluid invasion, presenting a viable, non-damaging alternative for advanced drilling fluid formulations.Keywords: CeO2–Bi2O3 nanocomposite, Clay swelling inhibition, Water-based drilling fluid, Wellbore stability, Zeta potential

Introduction

The global demand for hydrocarbon energy continues to drive the petroleum industry toward the exploration of unconventional reservoirs and complex geological formations, where the drilling phase represents the most capital-intensive and risk-prone operation [1, 2]. A predominant cause of non-productive time and financial loss during this phase is wellbore instability, which cost the industry billions of dollars annually. While mechanical earth models assist in managing stress-related instability, physicochemical interactions between drilling fluids and formation mineralogy remain a persistent challenge. Shales, which constitute a vast majority of drilled formations, are sedimentary rocks rich in reactive clay minerals like montmorillonite [3, 3]. Upon contact with the aqueous phase of drilling fluids, these water-sensitive clays undergo rapid hydration, leading to osmotic swelling, crystalline expansion, and eventual borehole collapse. Therefore, managing the behavior of reactive clays is critical for safe and efficient drilling operations [4, 5].

To mitigate these instability issues, drilling fluids are engineered to act as the primary defense mechanism. Historically, oil-based muds (OBMs) have been the preferred choice for drilling reactive shales due to their inherent non-reactive nature and superior inhibition capabilities [7, 6]. However, stringent environmental regulations regarding the disposal of oily cuttings and the high operational costs associated with OBMs have necessitated a paradigm shift toward water-based muds (WBMs). Despite their eco-friendly profile and lower cost, WBMs inherently lack the inhibitive performance of OBMs. Consequently, the industry has focused heavily on developing high-performance shale inhibitors, chemical additives designed to prevent water invasion into the clay matrix and maintain the mechanical integrity of the wellbore [9, 10].

Recent advancements in WBDFs have increasingly focused on deploying environmentally friendly additives and advanced nanocomposites to combat wellbore instability under harsh conditions. The application of bio-nanoparticles, such as cellulose derivatives and single-walled carbon nanotube composites, has shown immense potential in modifying rheology and reducing fluid loss due to their complex network structures and high surface hydration capacities [7, 8]. However, maintaining the stability and filtration properties of these additives under extreme high-temperature and high-salinity environments remains a persistent challenge, often requiring the synthesis of complex temperature- and salt-resistant polymers or in-house functionalized carbon nanocomposites to preserve colloidal stability and prevent clay swelling [9, 10]. Furthermore, the unique swelling-deswelling behaviors of polymer-clay networks and the pore structure responses of reconstituted clays highlight the intricate microstructural mechanisms that dictate macroscopic shale deformation [11, 12]. Comprehensive reviews and studies on tailor-made nanocomposites, such as TiO2-bentonite and various structural modifiers, emphasize that following specific bridging rules at the nanopore level is critical for minimizing water infiltration and enhancing wellbore stability [13, 14].

Beyond primary shale inhibition, evaluating the broader fluid-rock interactions, mechanical property alterations, and interfacial dynamics is essential for designing robust drilling fluids. Advanced deep learning models, such as Multilayer Perceptrons, have been successfully deployed to accurately predict mud cake permeability by modeling the combined effects of nanoparticles and salt concentrations [15, 16]. Similarly, understanding the geomechanical impacts of reactive fluids, such as CO2-induced damage on sandstone porosity and the complex seepage erosion mechanisms in defective underground structures, provides critical insights into maintaining structural integrity under differential pressures [17–19]. Innovations from related fields, such as the use of amphiphilic bioagents for interfacial nanostructure regulation, the hydration kinetics of self-setting cements, and the dynamic mechanical properties of waste-incorporated composites, offer valuable parallel strategies for reinforcing interface adhesion and material compactness [20–23]. Additionally, leveraging nanofluid flooding mechanisms and managing polymer-extracted wastewater in enhanced oil recovery underscores the necessity of maintaining system compatibility and preventing pore blockage in complex reservoir environments [24, 25].

The mechanism of clay swelling inhibition generally involves preventing water molecules from entering the clay interlayer spaces or neutralizing the negative charges on clay surfaces. Conventional inhibitors, such as inorganic salts (potassium chloride, KCl), have been widely employed to suppress swelling via cation exchange mechanisms [26, 27]. However, high concentrations of salts are often required, which can flocculate polymers, adversely affect fluid rheology, and pose disposal challenges due to chloride contamination. Similarly, organic inhibitors like polyamines and quaternary ammonium compounds function by adsorbing onto clay surfaces to create a hydrophobic barrier. While effective, many organic additives suffer from thermal degradation at elevated downhole temperatures, rendering them ineffective in deep, high-pressure high-temperature (HPHT) wells [28, 29].

In recent years, nanotechnology has emerged as a transformative frontier in drilling fluid engineering. Nanoparticles, characterized by their high surface area-to-volume ratio and reduced size, offer unique physicochemical properties that allow them to interact with clay minerals at the nanoscale [29, 30]. Various nanomaterials, including nano-silica, graphene oxide, and metal oxides, have been investigated for their ability to plug shale pore throats and physically block water ingress. Specifically, metal oxide nanoparticles have shown promise due to their thermal stability and rigidity. However, single-component nanoparticles often face limitations regarding dispersion stability and cost-effectiveness, prompting research into composite materials that leverage synergistic effects [26, 27, 31].

Despite the progress in nano-based inhibitors, significant gaps remain in the literature regarding bimetallic oxide systems that combine high density with chemical stability. Previous studies have primarily focused on common oxides like silica or alumina, often neglecting the potential of rare earth and heavy metal oxide combinations. Mixed metal oxides (MMOs) or nanocomposites offer the potential to tailor surface charges and enhance interaction energies with clay platelets. However, the specific application of Cerium-Bismuth oxide systems in drilling fluids remains largely unexplored. Understanding the synergistic interaction between Cerium (IV) oxide and Bismuth (III) oxide could unlock new pathways for designing thermally efficient shale inhibitors [32, 33].

Addressing the shortcomings of existing inhibitors requires a material that not only provides superior physical plugging and chemical inhibition but also maintains stability under dynamic downhole conditions [34]. The synthesis of a nanocomposite that combines the catalytic and structural properties of CeO2 with the density and inertness of Bi2O3 presents an approach. Current research often lacks comprehensive workflows that bridge the gap from synthesis to practical rheological compatibility in fully formulated mud systems. Therefore, a systematic investigation into the synthesis, characterization, and application of such a nanocomposite is essential to validate its viability as a next-generation drilling fluid additive.

Maintaining wellbore integrity in highly reactive shale formations is crucial for safe and economical drilling operations. However, the vulnerability of current water-based drilling fluids to severe clay hydration necessitates more resilient chemical solutions. Therefore, the core objective of this study is to synthesize and evaluate the CeO2-Bi2O3 nanocomposite to surpass conventional inhibitors. We hypothesize that its unique bimetallic lattice structure fundamentally arrests clay exfoliation through synergistic electrostatic double-layer compression and physical pore-plugging. As outlined in Fig. 1, the research workflow includes crystallographic and elemental characterization, followed by macroscopic evaluations such as dynamic linear swelling, electrophoretic mobility, and high-temperature shale recovery. The study culminates in compatibility tests using field-standard, clay-free drilling fluid formulations to verify rheological stability independently of inhibition performance. Ultimately, by enhancing the viability of water-based fluids over hazardous oil-based alternatives, these non-damaging nanocomposite inhibitors support UN Sustainable Development Goals 9 and 12.

Materials

The synthesis of the CeO₂-Bi₂O₃ nanocomposite required analytical grade reagents with maximum purity to ensure the structural integrity of the final product and to prevent any performance degradation caused by impurities. Cerium (III) nitrate hexahydrate (Ce(NO3)3⋅6H2O, ≥ 99.99% purity) and bismuth (III) nitrate pentahydrate (Bi(NO3)3⋅5H2O, ≥ 99.99% purity) were utilized as the primary metallic precursors. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥ 99.0%purity) and absolute ethanol (C2H5OH, 99.8% purity) were employed as the precipitating agent and washing solvent, respectively. All aforementioned chemical reagents were procured from Sigma-Aldrich and were used exactly as received without any further purification. Ultrapure deionized water with a minimum resistivity of 18.2 M Ω⋅cm was produced in the laboratory and used exclusively throughout the synthesis and washing processes to eliminate any potential ionic contamination.

For the core evaluation of swelling behavior, a high-quality commercial bentonite clay was selected as the primary reactive medium. This specific bentonite, identified as a premium grade sodium montmorillonite (Na-MMT), was supplied by Itlavga Company. Sodium montmorillonite was chosen due to its exceptionally high cation exchange capacity and its notorious tendency to undergo severe hydration, basal spacing enlargement, and volumetric expansion upon contact with aqueous environments. The superior purity and consistent mineralogical profile of the Itlavga bentonite make it an ideal, reactive substrate for accurately testing the swelling inhibition efficiency of the newly synthesized nanocomposite under simulated wellbore conditions.

To establish a standard baseline for the inhibition performance, Potassium Chloride (KCl, 99.5% purity, Merck) was incorporated into the experimental design as a conventional, industry-standard benchmark clay swelling inhibitor. Additionally, the preparation of the simulated water-based drilling fluids necessitated specific standard additives to closely replicate realistic field mud formulations. High-purity Xanthan gum (XC polymer) was acquired to serve as the primary rheology modifier and viscosifier, while Polyanionic Cellulose (PAC) was obtained to control fluid loss and maintain mud stability. These drilling fluid additives, alongside sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) for calcium ion precipitation and pH adjustment, were sourced from top-tier oilfield chemical suppliers, ensuring that the formulated mud systems provided a realistic environment for testing the synthesized nanomaterials.

Synthesis of nanocomposites

The synthesis of the CeO2-Bi2O3 nanocomposite was achieved through a controlled chemical co-precipitation method. Initially, stoichiometric amounts of Ce(NO3)3⋅6H2O and Bi(NO3)3⋅5H2O were precisely weighed to yield a specific molar ratio of Cerium to Bismuth. These high-purity metallic precursors were introduced into a borosilicate glass beaker containing 100 mL of ultrapure deionized water. The resulting mixture was immediately placed on a magnetic heating stirrer and subjected to vigorous mechanical agitation at 600 rpm while maintaining a constant temperature of 40 °C for exactly 30 min. This foundational step guaranteed the complete dissociation of the nitrate salts, leading to the formation of a homogeneous, transparent aqueous solution containing the prerequisite metal cations [37].

In the precipitation equation provided above, Ce3+ and Bi3+ represent the fully dissolved cerium and bismuth precursor cations respectively, OH− denotes the hydroxide ions introduced by the precipitating agent, and the downward pointing arrows signify the instantaneous formation of the insoluble solid metal hydroxide intermediate compounds. Following the complete dissolution of the metallic salts, a freshly prepared 2.0 M solution of NaOH was introduced dropwise into the vortex of the precursor solution at a strictly regulated flow rate of 2 mL/min. The continuous addition of the alkaline solution progressively elevated the pH of the system, and the titration was deliberately halted the moment the pH meter registered exactly 10.5. This targeted alkaline environment triggered the simultaneous co-precipitation of cerium and bismuth hydroxides, creating a dense, cloudy suspension that was subsequently allowed to age under continuous mechanical stirring at 60 °C for a duration of 4 h to promote primary particle growth [38].

In the terminal calcination equation, O2 refers to the ambient atmospheric oxygen required to oxidize cerium from the + 3 to the + 4-oxidation state, Δ signifies the sustained application of severe thermal energy, and H2O↑ represents the structural water expelled as vapor during the high-temperature dehydration transformation. After the mandatory aging period, the hydroxide precipitate was systematically harvested using a high-speed laboratory centrifuge operating at 8000 rpm for 10 min. To eliminate any unreacted precursor ions and residual sodium nitrates, the collected solid pellet was washed sequentially three times with ultrapure deionized water and twice with absolute ethanol. The purified semi-solid paste was subsequently dried in a conventional laboratory air circulation oven at 80 °C for 12 h. Ultimately, the completely dried precursor was ground into a fine powder, transferred into an alumina crucible, and subjected to calcination in a programmable muffle furnace at 550 °C for 4 h with a slow heating ramp rate of 5 °C/min, yielding the targeted crystalline CeO2-Bi2O3 nanocomposite ready for characterization and fluid formulation.

Dispersion stability evaluation

The long-term physical stability and the ultimate uniform dispersion of the synthesized nanomaterials within unweighted aqueous systems are fundamentally critical for their effective field application as active swelling inhibitors. To quantitatively assess this specific dispersion stability over time, precise Dynamic Light Scattering and electrophoretic light scattering experiments were strictly conducted. The Dynamic Light Scattering methodology was specifically utilized to measure the real time hydrodynamic diameter and the complete particle size distribution of the nanomaterials suspended in the aqueous phase, providing critical fundamental insights into their inherent tendency to cluster or aggressively agglomerate within the fluid matrix over extended periods [38, 39].

To execute the Dynamic Light Scattering evaluations, dedicated nanoparticle suspensions were freshly prepared at various exact concentrations using pure deionized water and subsequently subjected to direct ultrasonic irradiation for a continuous period of 30 min to forcefully break apart any initial soft particle agglomerates. A calibrated small aliquot of each uniform suspension was then carefully transferred into a pristine clear disposable optical cuvette and placed securely inside the precision analyzer. Multiple consecutive measurements were recorded at a rigorously controlled constant temperature of 25 °C to accurately calculate the average hydrodynamic size and the distinct polydispersity index, which conclusively indicates the overall width of the particle size distribution and the macroscopic physical homogeneity of the fluid system.

Concurrently alongside the sizing measurements, the dynamic surface charge characteristics of the actively suspended particles were thoroughly evaluated by measuring the zeta potential through advanced electrophoretic light scattering protocols. The freshly prepared uniform suspensions were injected into a specialized folded capillary cell equipped tightly with solid gold electrodes. A precisely oscillating electric field was applied across the cell, and the resulting electrophoretic mobility of the particles was continuously tracked to mathematically derive the ultimate zeta potential values utilizing the standard Smoluchowski approximation. Substantially high absolute values of the measured zeta potential confirm strong electrostatic repulsion between adjacent solid particles, thereby indicating a stable fluid dispersion that is fundamentally resistant to rapid mechanical settling and unwanted flocculation. To mitigate the known interference of high background salt concentrations (such as the KCl used in comparative benchmark testing), which can compress the electrical double layer and artificially suppress electrophoretic mobility readings, baseline zeta potential measurements of the synthesized nanocomposite dispersions were conducted in deionized water. This methodological approach effectively isolated the inherent nanoparticle surface charge dynamics from the shielding effects of the saline environment, providing an accurate assessment of their electrostatic stabilization mechanism.

Linear swelling assessment

The macroscopic intrinsic hydration and the resulting volumetric expansion behavior of the reactive testing clay were quantitatively monitored using an industry standard automated linear swelling tester. Initially, precisely 10 g of the completely dried premium sodium montmorillonite clay powder was uniformly distributed inside a hardened cylindrical compaction cell. A heavy-duty hydraulic laboratory press was then aggressively employed to apply a constant consolidation pressure of exactly 10,000 psi for a continuous uninterrupted duration of 90 min at 60 °C, thereby perfectly creating a dense, compacted artificial shale core possessing strictly reproducible internal porosity and structural mechanical integrity [40, 41].

Following the initial compaction phase, the dense clay core was carefully extracted from the mold and positioned securely inside the main measuring cup of the precision linear swelling apparatus. A sensitive precision Linear Variable Differential Transformer sensor was placed in direct physical contact with the exact upper surface center of the artificial core to accurately record microscopic dynamic changes in sample height. Subsequently, the respective testing fluid was gently poured into the surrounding cup, completely submerging the compacted reactive core without creating mechanical disturbance. The dynamic linear physical expansion of the solid core was then continuously digitally recorded as a strict function of time for exactly 24 h at standard ambient temperature, allowing for a precise direct comparative analysis of the hydration inhibition efficiency between pure deionized water, the baseline 3 wt% KCl solution, and the precisely synthesized nanomaterial suspensions.

Filtration control evaluation

To properly simulate the static fluid loss conditions routinely encountered during actual field drilling operations and to thoroughly evaluate the microscopic sealing capability of the specifically formulated fluid systems, standard API static filtration tests were systematically conducted. A fundamental simple base drilling fluid was strictly formulated by actively dispersing exactly 5 wt% of premium sodium montmorillonite solid powder into ultrapure deionized water. To this actively hydrating base fluid, either exactly 3 wt% of the conventional benchmark KCl inhibitor or various distinct predefined optimal concentrations of the synthesized nanomaterial, specifically dictated by the prior dispersion stability optimization results, were systematically added and aggressively mechanically agitated to ensure absolutely complete system homogenization prior to pressure testing.

The perfectly homogenized fluid mixtures were subsequently poured directly into standard heavy metallic API filtration testing cells strictly equipped with standardized hardened filter paper possessing a specific uniform pore size. The loaded metallic cells were securely sealed top and bottom and placed carefully into a rigorously controlled thermal heating jacket preheated to a perfectly constant temperature of 60 °C. A regulated continuous differential pressure of precisely 100 psi was completely applied to the upper liquid surface of the test fluid using high purity compressed nitrogen gas. The exact total volume of the expelled clear filtrate fluid was carefully systematically collected in a transparent graduated glass cylinder and precisely recorded over a standard unbroken testing duration of 30 min [46, 42].

Sedimentation rate analysis

The comprehensive atmospheric sedimentation test was uniquely designed to visually and macroscopically evaluate the specific dynamic interactions between the reactive clay solid particles and the distinct various fluid chemical phases entirely under standard atmospheric conditions. The underlying physical principle governing this distinct experiment relies heavily on the documented fact that superior active swelling inhibitors rapidly completely suppress the thick electrical double layer surrounding the clay particles, leading instantly to severe particle flocculation and consequently, a substantially profoundly higher speed of macroscopic sedimentation. Conversely, easily heavily hydrated distinct clays suspended in entirely uninhibited pure fluids remain completely suspended and fully dispersed, effectively yielding a dramatically prolonged suspension time and a remarkably slow measurable settling rate.

To meticulously perform this fundamental physical evaluation, distinct unweighted fluid environments were prepared, primarily including pure blank deionized water, a heavily standard reference KCI benchmark aqueous solution, and specific aqueous suspensions containing the uniquely synthesized nanomaterials. The visual test was rigorously conducted under two distinctly different initial physical conditions to fully capture the complete broad spectrum of swelling clay behavior: a prehydrated state and a completely unhydrated initial state. For the unhydrated condition testing, exactly 1 wt% of entirely dry solid sodium montmorillonite powder was directly introduced into tall graduated glass cylinders directly containing the respective testing fluid chemistries, tightly sealed at the top, and intensely vigorously shaken manually to ensure complete initial physical dispersion of the powder.

For the strictly comparative prehydrated condition, the active dry clay powder was first allowed to fully swell undisturbed in pure deionized water for an entire unbroken period of 24 h before the distinct active chemical inhibitors were strictly introduced into the aqueous system. Once both complete sets of conditions were physically prepared, all tall graduated cylinders were placed perfectly upright in an entirely vibrationless isolated environment strictly at standard room temperature. The distinct observable physical height of the visible interface separating the perfectly clear supernatant liquid layer and the actively settling opaque dark clay suspension was continuously physically measured and carefully recorded at specific predetermined time intervals. By mathematically tracking the downward migration velocity of this specific interface over the testing time, the absolute sedimentation velocity was accurately calculated, serving effectively as a reliable critical secondary indicator of the total hydration inhibition strength provided by each specific tested aqueous phase.

Cuttings recovery evaluation

The critical physical cuttings recovery experiment, fundamentally commonly referred to within the industry as the dynamic hot rolling dispersion test, was physically executed to accurately successfully simulate the intense dynamic thermal and harsh mechanical stresses routinely experienced by native drilled reactive shale cuttings during continuous circulation up the narrow wellbore annulus. Specifically, blocks of pristine reactive testing clay samples were heavily physically crushed and subsequently carefully mechanically sieved to successfully isolate solid irregular aggregate particles strictly confined within the specific physical size range of 2 to 4 mm. Precisely 20 g of these accurately specifically sized fragile solid cuttings were carefully systematically weighed out and introduced directly into uniquely distinct heavy stainless-steel high-pressure aging cells.

Each loaded metallic aging cell was subsequently completely filled with a specific predetermined test fluid, strictly including the completely blank deionized water reference, the heavily standard KCl benchmark fluid, and the unique actively treated fluid thoroughly containing the successfully synthesized nanomaterials. The heavy metallic cells were tightly securely sealed using high pressure threaded caps to entirely prevent liquid vaporization and gently placed securely inside a continuously motorized hot rolling thermal oven. The encapsulated fluid samples were subjected to continuous violent dynamic physical rotation at a regulated constant extreme temperature of 105 °C for an absolute uninterrupted severe testing period of 16 h. Following the completion of this intense dynamic thermal aging process, the heavy metallic cells were safely cooled entirely to standard room temperature, and the surviving un-dispersed solid clay cuttings were carefully selectively captured utilizing the original standard analytical sieve, thoroughly gently washed using pure water, dried inside an oven to an absolutely constant final weight, and accurately reweighed to precisely mathematically calculate the final exact recovery percentage [43, 44].

Material characterization results

Novelties, comparisons, implications, and limitations

The current research introduces a significant incremental advancement in the domain of wellbore stability by demonstrating that CeO2–Bi2O3 nanocomposites provide superior shale inhibition without compromising the rheological integrity of the base fluids. Compared to recent literature that heavily relies on functionalized cellulose or carbon nanotubes which may degrade under extreme salinity [8, 15], the proposed bimetallic oxide nanocomposite leverages robust electrostatic double-layer compression to maintain dispersion stability. While previous studies utilizing tailor-made TiO2 or acrylic acid-functionalized polymers achieved notable swelling reductions by physically bridging nanopores [10, 14], our findings indicate that the CeO2–Bi2O3 system provides a more resilient chemical inhibition mechanism, yielding a highly controlled and non-damaging fluid loss profile even in heavily weighted mud systems.

The compatibility of these non-damaging nanocomposites with standard polymeric and high-density fluid systems highlights their strong potential for practical field implementation to maintain wellbore stability in challenging drilling environments. Because the additive does not artificially inflate fluid viscosity or yield point beyond operational windows, it can be seamlessly integrated into existing field mud formulations to prevent costly non-productive time associated with pipe sticking or wellbore collapse.

Despite these promising results, this study is subject to certain limitations. Primarily, a direct microstructural visualization of the shale surfaces (such as through SEM or TEM) did not be conducted to visually confirm the exact physical pore-plugging morphology. The mechanistic conclusions are currently derived macroscopically from zeta potential, dynamic light scattering, and filtration data, which, while robust, lack direct microscopic validation. Future development of this work will focus on extensive morphological imaging to map the surface coverage of the nanoparticles on the clay substrates. Additionally, future scope includes evaluating the nanocomposite’s performance under dynamic high-pressure, high-temperature (HPHT) flow loop conditions and exploring synergistic effects by pairing the bimetallic oxides with eco-friendly bio-polymers to further optimize environmental sustainability and field deployability.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated a synthesized CeO2-Bi2O3 nanocomposite as a high-performance clay swelling inhibitor for water-based drilling fluids. The experimental workflow demonstrated that an optimal 500 ppm formulation maximizes electrostatic repulsion (-38.7 mV) and effectively plugs nanoscale shale pores, achieving a superior 93% shale cutting recovery under dynamic conditions at 105 °C. By avoiding the runaway agglomeration documented at higher concentrations, this optimal dosage safely integrates into both polymeric and heavily weighted fluid systems, actively reducing static filtration volumes while preserving critical rheological parameters necessary for efficient hole cleaning. Translating these robust laboratory findings to operational outcomes indicates that the nanocomposite will directly enhance the rate of penetration and preserve long-term wellbore integrity, thereby suggesting a clear trajectory for field-scale deployment that lowers overall fluid life-cycle costs. A primary limitation of this current study is the absence of direct microstructural imaging to visually map the precise nanoparticle-clay surface coverage. Consequently, future research will prioritize SEM/TEM morphological characterizations alongside dynamic high-pressure, high-temperature (HPHT) flow-loop evaluations to fully validate the additive’s behavior under extreme operational conditions.
