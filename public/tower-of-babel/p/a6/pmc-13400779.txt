Dataset on the genetic diversity and phylogenetic relationships of Irvingia wombolu and Irvingia gabonensis using rbcL markers
Authors: Abiodun Oyelakin, Jacob Popoola, Amarachi Offor, Omotayo Shobowale, Esther Kasumu, Tinuola Olajide, Boluwatife Ogunsina, Favour Babalola, Oluwaseun Akinyemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This dataset provides a comprehensive molecular characterization of Irvingia gabonensis and Irvingia wombolu, two indigenous West and Central Africa tree species vital for food security. Genomic DNA was extracted from 14 accessions, followed by PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing of the rbcL chloroplast gene. The data includes aligned sequences, polymorphism indices, conserved regions, and Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) statistics. Analysis identified 593 nucleotide sites, 129 monomorphic sites, three haplotypes, and six mutation sites, with a gene diversity of 0.28. Notably, two highly conserved regions (1–249 bp and 311–408 bp, C = 1.0) were established as reliable reference blocks for the genus. Phylogenetic reconstruction using maximum likelihood (83% bootstrap support) grouped the accessions into two distinct clades, while a synonymous substitution rate (Ks) of 0.857 confirmed low inter-specific divergence. Codon usage analysis revealed specific preferences, such as ACU (Threonine, RSCU = 2.89). Despite the overall low genetic variability, minor sequence differences identified in accessions PV089637 and PV089642 suggest potential for hybridization. This dataset serves as a foundational genetic resource for conservation planning, germplasm development, and targeted breeding programs to improve these underutilized species.

Background

Irvingia gabonensis and Irvingia wombolu are high-potential, underutilized West African tree species vital for food security and economic sustainability [1]. Despite their value, they remain largely undomesticated, and conventional trait-based breeding is hindered by environmental influences on morphological selection [1,2]. Furthermore, because their recalcitrant seeds cannot survive standard seed-banking, conservation relies entirely on managing living germplasm.

To resolve these challenges, this dataset utilizes stable plastid markers via the chloroplast rbcL gene to assess intra- and interspecific genetic variation across 14 Nigerian accessions. By establishing baseline nucleotide diversity, mutation events, and codon usage statistics, this data provides a reproducible molecular foundation to directly support targeted breeding programs, hybridization strategies, and long-term conservation planning.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Plant materials

Seeds from fourteen (14) Irvingia accessions comprising eleven (11) I. gabonensis and three (3) I. wombolu were collected across eight Nigerian states: Lagos, Oyo, Osun, Abia, Imo, Benue, Rivers, and Ogun. Each accession was assigned a unique field identification number at the point of collection. Seeds were germinated in 10-L perforated planting buckets filled with topsoil and maintained in a screen house at the Department of Pure and Applied Botany, Federal University of Agriculture, Abeokuta. Fresh young leaves were harvested from seedlings, cleaned, silica-dried, labeled according to sample IDs, and transported to Inqaba Biotec West Africa Ltd., Ibadan, for molecular analysis.

Genomic DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from fresh leaf tissues using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method [3]. Approximately 100 mg of lyophilized tissue per sample was placed in 2 mL tubes with stainless steel beads and homogenized using a Genogrinder-2000 at 1500 rpm for 30–40 s. Pre-warmed CTAB buffer (800 µL) was added, and samples were incubated at 65 °C for 30 min with intermittent mixing. After cooling, chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (24:1, 600 µL) was added, mixed gently, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The aqueous phase was transferred to clean tubes, mixed with 2/3 volume of cold isopropanol, and incubated at −80 °C for 1 h 30 min. DNA was pelleted by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 30 min, washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in 97 µL TE buffer. RNase A (3 µL) was added, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 45 min to remove RNA contamination [4].

DNA quality and quantification

DNA quality and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Absorbance ratios (A260/A280) between 1.8 and 2.0 were considered indicative of high-quality DNA. For gel electrophoresis, 1 g agarose was dissolved in 100 mL 1X TBE buffer and stained with ethidium bromide (5 µL). DNA samples (2 µL DNA + 2 µL loading dye) were electrophoresed at 120 V for 40 min [5]. DNA integrity was visualized using a Gel Documentation System (Labnet ENDURO GDS, Aplegen). All samples showed intact, high-molecular-weight DNA suitable for downstream applications.

PCR amplification

PCR amplification was performed using rbcL-targeted primers H1F (CCACAAACAGAGACTAAAGC) and Fofana R (GTAAAATCAAGTCCACCGCG). Reactions were carried out in a 25 µL volume containing: 3 µL DNA template, 2.5 µL 10X buffer, 1 µL MgCl₂ (50 mM), 2 µL dNTPs (10 mM), 1 µL each primer, 1 µL DMSO, 0.1 µL Taq polymerase, and 13.4 µL nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling was performed on an Applied Biosystems GeneAmp PCR System 9700 with initial denaturation at 94°C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation (94 °C, 30 s), annealing (55 °C decreasing by 1 °C per cycle for 10 cycles, then 45 °C for remaining cycles, 1 min), extension (72 °C, 30 s), and a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. PCR products were confirmed on 1.5% agarose gel using a 50 bp DNA ladder [4].

Sanger sequencing

PCR products were purified using Zymo Research sequencing clean-up kits prior to Sanger sequencing. Cycle sequencing reactions contained BigDye terminator mix, sequencing buffer, primers, nuclease-free water, and purified PCR amplicons. Cycle sequencing was performed in a thermal cycler under standard denaturation, annealing, and extension conditions. Post-reaction clean-up was performed using Zymo-Spin columns, and purified products were subjected to capillary electrophoresis on a genetic analyzer [6,7]. DNA fragments were separated by size, and fluorescently labelled dideoxynucleotides were detected via laser excitation to generate sequence chromatograms.

Data analysis

Raw Sanger sequences were edited and aligned using ClustalW implemented in BioEdit (v7.2.5) and MEGA (v11.0.13) [8,9]. Sequence data were submitted to NCBI GenBank and accession numbers assigned (Table 2). Genetic diversity indices—including nucleotide site composition, polymorphic and monomorphic sites, haplotype number, haplotype diversity (Hd), nucleotide diversity (π), conserved regions, and average nucleotide differences (k)—were computed using DnaSP v6.010 [10]. Population structure and genetic differentiation were also assessed using haplotype- and nucleotide-based statistics (Hs, Ks, Kxy, Gst, GammaSt, Fst, Dxy, and Da) [10]. Codon usage bias was analyzed using CodonW, while phylogenetic relationships were inferred using the Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA) implemented in MEGA11 [8].

Limitations

A key limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size, comprising only 14 Irvingia accessions collected from selected locations in Nigeria. This limited sampling may not fully capture the complete genetic diversity and geographic variation present within I. gabonensis and I. wombolu across their entire natural range. Consequently, the observed levels of genetic variation should be interpreted as preliminary baseline information. Broader sampling across additional ecological zones and countries within West and Central Africa would provide a more comprehensive assessment of population structure and genetic diversity in the genus.

Ethics Statement

The samples used, as shown in Table 1 were collected across eight Nigerian states: Lagos, Oyo, Osun, Abia, Imo, Benue, Rivers, and Ogun states, Nigeria. No data or any parts were obtained from any Social Media platform.
