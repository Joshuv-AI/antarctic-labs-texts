Fatigue-induced changes in jump and hop performance and the limited explanatory value of Fatigue Index in professional Indian male soccer players- an analytical cross-sectional study
Authors: Cyanna Joseph Dsouza, Ajay Kumar, Vijayakumar Palaniswamy, Animesh Hazari, Rajasekar Sannasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFatigue can impair neuromuscular performance during sport-specific lower limb tasks. Although hop-based assessments are commonly used in sports rehabilitation and functional performance testing, rested assessments may not fully capture fatigue-related performance decline. Fatigue Index (FI) is commonly used to quantify decline in sprint performance, but its ability to explain fatigue-related changes in hop performance remains uncertain.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 195 professional Indian male soccer players who completed a vertical jump assessment using the validated MyJump2 application and standardised hop-for-distance, and side-hop tests before and immediately after a repeated-sprint ability fatigue protocol. FI was calculated as a positive sprint decrement, defined as the ratio of total sprint time to ideal sprint time, where ideal sprint time was set to the fastest sprint time multiplied by 6. Paired non-parametric analyses were employed to examine pre- and post-fatigue changes. Robust regression models were used to evaluate the independent association of FI with performance change after adjusting for age and playing experience. Sensitivity analyses using winsorised and outlier-excluded datasets assessed the stability of the estimates. Correlation network analysis was performed to explore relationships among baseline hop-performance variables.ResultsThe documented FI was 7.08 ± 0.90%, indicating a physiologically plausible positive sprint-decrement value. Fatigue significantly reduced all hop and jump performance outcomes. Median jump height decreased by 4.11 cm, contact time increased by 11.63 ms, mean power decreased by 39.33 W, and reactive strength index-modified decreased by 0.02 m/s. Hop-for-distance performance decreased by approximately 6.5–7.0 cm, while side-hop performance declined by 3–4 repetitions. After adjustment for age and playing experience, FI values showed minimal independent association with most fatigue-related change outcomes. Age showed stronger and more consistent associations, particularly with contact time and the modified reactive strength index. Sensitivity analyses indicated stable findings across primary, winsorised, and outlier-excluded models. Correlation network analysis identified an explosive-performance cluster in which jump height, reactive strength index-modified, and mean power were positively associated, whereas contact time was inversely associated with these variables.ConclusionHop-performance assessment under fatigued conditions revealed a consistent decline in explosive and reactive lower limb performance in this cohort of Indian male soccer players. FI values showed limited value as an isolated explanatory metric for fatigue-related performance decline. Outcome-specific changes, particularly contact time, reactive strength index-modified, jump height, and mean power, may provide more useful information than a single fatigue metric during fatigue-inclusive functional performance assessment.Trial registrationThe study was prospectively registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2024/08/072445) on August 12th, 2024.

Introduction

Soccer is a high-intensity intermittent sport characterised by repeated accelerations, decelerations, jumps, landings, cutting manoeuvres, and rapid changes of direction. These actions require rapid force production, efficient utilisation of the stretch-shortening cycle, and precise neuromuscular control during both non-fatigued and progressively fatigued states [1, 2]. During competitive play, players are repeatedly exposed to periods of accumulated fatigue that may compromise lower limb force generation, movement efficiency, and dynamic joint stability [3, 4]. Such fatigue-related alterations are clinically relevant because reductions in neuromuscular control and reactive strength performance may impair movement quality during periods of accumulated match load, which have been associated with a greater occurrence of non-contact injury mechanisms [5, 6].

Fatigue is a multifactorial phenomenon involving central and peripheral mechanisms that influence muscle activation, motor-unit recruitment, force production, proprioception, and movement coordination [7]. Experimental and sport-specific fatigue protocols involving repeated-sprint and intermittent high-intensity running tasks have demonstrated reductions in jump performance, reactive strength expression, postural control, sprint performance, and compromised landing mechanics following repeated high-intensity activity [8–10]. Fatigue may also prolong ground contact time and reduce reactive strength index (RSI) derived measures during countermovement jump and reactive jump assessments, while increasing movement variability during unilateral tasks [11, 12]. Because unilateral jumping and hopping tasks are frequently encountered during soccer-specific movements, evaluating fatigue-induced changes in hop performance may provide useful information for sports rehabilitation and sports-specific performance assessment.

Hop-based performance assessments, including single-leg hop, side-hop, and related dynamic tasks, are widely used in sports medicine because they evaluate unilateral lower limb performance, reactive strength expression, movement control and the ability to tolerate functional loading under sport-relevant conditions [13, 14]. Tests such as vertical jump assessment, hop-for-distance tasks, and Side-hop performance are commonly incorporated into rehabilitation progressions and return-to-sport (RTS) batteries following lower limb injury [15].

Most jump and hop performance assessments are conducted under rested conditions, despite the fact that competitive soccer is characterised by repeated bouts of high-intensity activity performed under progressive fatigue [16, 17]. Consequently, understanding how fatigue influences performance in healthy athletes is an important prerequisite for interpreting post-fatigue assessments in applied sports performance and rehabilitation settings. Establishing typical fatigue-related responses may help contextualise performance changes observed following injury, although further longitudinal research is required before such findings can be incorporated into RTS decision-making.

To address this limitation, fatigue-inclusive testing approaches have gained increasing attention in contemporary sports-performance and rehabilitation research [16]. Evaluating hop performance following fatigue may provide greater ecological validity by better replicating match-related demands and identifying performance characteristics that remain stable or deteriorate under accumulated physiological stress [18]. Previous investigations have also shown that fatigue can negatively influence jump height, landing stability, force attenuation, and change-of-direction performance [19–21]. Such information may complement traditional rested assessments by providing additional insight into an athlete’s capacity to maintain explosive and reactive performance when exposed to sport-specific loading.

Fatigue Index (FI) is frequently used to quantify performance deterioration during repeated high-intensity tasks and, among its advantages, provides a practical summary of fatigue-related sprint decrement in a single value [22]. When calculated as a sprint-decrement score, FI is interpreted as a positive value, with higher values indicating greater decline across repeated sprint efforts. While clinically appealing for its simplicity, FI may inadequately capture outcome-specific fatigue adaptations across different neuromuscular domains. Athletes demonstrating similar FI values may exhibit substantially different changes in RSI-mod, contact time, hop distance, or lateral hop performance [23, 24].

Although previous studies have investigated fatigue-related changes in isolated performance outcomes, limited evidence exists regarding the independent explanatory utility of FI values after accounting for relevant player characteristics, particularly in Indian male soccer players. The relationships among baseline hop-performance variables that may be sensitive to fatigue are also not fully understood. Moreover, the robustness of fatigue-related findings across different analytical approaches remains insufficiently explored. Recent data-reduction work in field-sport athletes has shown that force-time and lower limb performance metrics can be grouped into distinct physical performance domains such as strength, power, reactive strength, and neuromuscular control, supporting the need to examine whether commonly used jump and hop outcomes provide overlapping or complementary information [25].

Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to quantify fatigue-induced changes in jump and hop performance outcomes in professional Indian male soccer players and to determine whether FI independently explained performance decline after accounting for age and playing experience. We hypothesised that the fatigue protocol would significantly impair jump and hop performance and that FI would demonstrate limited independent explanatory value once relevant player characteristics were considered. Sensitivity and network analyses were performed to further characterise the robustness and interrelationships of the observed performance responses.

Methods

Study design

This observational cross-sectional study was conducted among professional Indian male soccer players recruited from competitive clubs and training academies across multiple Indian cities.

Ethical approval and consent

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, Srinivas University, India (IEC No. 10/AHS/EC/2024), and the study was prospectively registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2024/08/072445) on August 12th, 2024. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained before testing.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was performed a priori using parameters reported by Augustsson et al. [26] to ensure adequate statistical power to detect fatigue-related changes in hop performance. Using a two-sided confidence level of 95% (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Z}\alpha\:,\:80\%=1.96$$\end{document}), a standard deviation of 21 units derived from prior literature, and a minimum detectable difference set at 10% of the mean, the required sample size was calculated using the formula N = Z2 x s2/ d2. Based on these assumptions, a minimum sample of 195 participants was determined to be sufficient to detect meaningful differences.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

A total of 195 professional male soccer players were recruited using convenience sampling (Table 1). Players aged 18–30 years who were actively competing at the state, national, or international level and had at least 1 year of structured playing experience were included. Eligible participants were additionally required to engage in at least 2 h of routine soccer training per session on at least 3 days per week. Players were excluded if they had sustained a lower limb injury within the previous 6 months, reported current musculoskeletal pain or dysfunction affecting performance, had not participated in competitive soccer in the preceding year, or declined consent.

Procedures

All testing was conducted outdoors on third-generation artificial turf. To minimise environmental variability, testing sessions were conducted under similar daylight conditions and on the same surface type across sites. Participants wore standard soccer attire and soccer boots with studs. Following eligibility screening, baseline demographic and anthropometric characteristics, including body mass index (BMI) and limb dominance, were recorded (Table 1). Playing experience was recorded as the total number of years of organised competitive soccer participation. Before testing, players completed a standardised 10-minute dynamic warm-up comprising five minutes of light jogging, followed by 10 repetitions each of bodyweight squats, toe raises, and submaximal jumps, to improve neuromuscular readiness and explosive performance [27, 28]. Participants subsequently rested for 1 min before baseline testing. To minimise learning effects, all hop tests were explained and demonstrated by the investigator, and two familiarisation trials per limb were permitted before recorded testing.

Outcome variables and measures

Hop performance was evaluated at baseline and immediately after a repeated-sprint-ability (RSA) fatigue protocol. Three hop-based assessments representing vertical, horizontal and lateral lower limb performance were performed in a fixed sequence consisting of the vertical jump, single-leg hop-for-distance and single-leg side-hop tests. A standardised three-minute recovery interval was provided between assessments to reduce carryover fatigue while maintaining a consistent testing sequence across participants.

Vertical jump performance was assessed using the validated smartphone application MyJump2 (Fig. 1), which has shown excellent agreement with force-plate measurements [29–31]. Participants performed a maximal countermovement jump without arm swing, keeping their hands against the trunk throughout the movement. Tuck-jump actions were not permitted, and participants were instructed to jump vertically (Fig. 2). Trials were considered valid if a stable landing was achieved without loss of balance or additional foot contact. Three trials were recorded, and the trial demonstrating the greatest jump height was used for analysis. Outcome variables included jump height, contact time, mean power, and the reactive strength index-modified (RSI-mod), calculated as jump height divided by time to take-off.

The single-leg hop-for-distance test was used to assess horizontal lower limb power. Participants performed a maximal unilateral forward hop and were required to maintain landing stability to complete the trial successfully (Fig. 3a and b). Hop distance was measured from the take-off line to the posterior heel at landing. Three valid trials were recorded per limb, and the longest successfully completed hop distance was used for analysis. This test has previously demonstrated high reliability and clinical utility in evaluating unilateral lower limb performance in athletic populations [32, 33].

The single-leg side-hop test was included to assess multidirectional lower limb function under repetitive loading, reflecting key soccer-specific demands. The test challenges lateral force production, neuromuscular control, dynamic balance, and repeated unilateral power during rapid changes of direction. (Figure 4a and b). Participants stood on the test limb, hands positioned behind their backs, and performed repeated lateral hops between two parallel strips of tape spaced 40 cm apart. Players were instructed to complete as many successful hops as possible within 30 s while maintaining control of movement and landing stability. Hops were considered invalid if the contralateral foot contacted the ground, the participant failed to clear the designated distance, or the tape boundaries were contacted. The highest number of valid repetitions completed within 30 s was recorded for analysis. The side-hop test has demonstrated good reliability and ecological validity in assessing functional lower limb performance in athletic populations [34, 35].

To induce exercise-related fatigue, participants completed an RSA protocol immediately following baseline hop testing. The RSA protocol consisted of six maximal shuttle sprints over 2 × 15 m, requiring a change of direction at the 15 m mark, covering a total distance of 30 m per repetition, with 20 s of passive recovery between repetitions. Sprint times were recorded using a calibrated digital stopwatch by the same examiner throughout testing. The RSA protocol was selected because it is representative of the physiological and neuromuscular demands of competitive soccer and has been shown to induce measurable fatigue in intermittent team-sport athletes [36, 37]. Fatigue accumulation was quantified using the FI formula: FI = [(Total Sprint Time / Ideal Sprint Time) − 1] × 100. Total sprint time represented the cumulative time of all six sprint repetitions. Ideal sprint time was calculated as the fastest single sprint time multiplied by six before applying the FI formula. Higher FI values, therefore, indicated greater positive sprint decrement [38, 39]. Immediately following completion of the RSA protocol, participants repeated the entire hop-test battery in the same sequence. Consequently, the vertical jump assessment was performed first after fatigue induction, followed by the hop-for-distance and side-hop assessments.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 29.0.1.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. The distribution of continuous variables and pre- to post-fatigue change scores was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, visual inspection of histograms and normal Q-Q plots and boxplot screening fro extreme values. Because several performance variables and change scores were not normally distributed, pre-to-post fatigue-related changes were analysed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Results were reported as medians with interquartile ranges, median change scores, bootstrap-derived 95% confidence intervals, rank-biserial effect sizes, and Holm-adjusted p values. Rank-biserial effect sizes were interpreted as trivial (< 0.10), small (0.10–0.29), moderate (0.30–0.49), and large (≥ 0.50), consistent with recommendations for non-parametric effect size interpretation [40].

Robust median quantile regression with standardised predictors was used to examine whether FI independently explained fatigue-related change outcomes after adjustment for age and playing experience. This approach was selected because it provides robust estimates for non-normally distributed outcomes and is less sensitive to extreme observations than ordinary least-squares regression.

Spearman correlation analysis was used as an exploratory descriptive analysis to examine the baseline interrelationships among jump and hop performance outcomes. This analysis was not intended to duplicate the regression models. The regression models examined adjusted associations between FI and fatigue-related change outcomes, whereas the Spearman rank correlation network was used to explore whether baseline performance outcomes showed overlapping or distinct performance domains. Correlations with an absolute value of |ρ| ≥ 0.30 were displayed in the network to focus the figure on moderate or stronger associations and to reduce visual clutter. This threshold was used as a pragmatic visualisation threshold rather than as proof of clinical importance.

All jump and hop performance outcomes showed significant deterioration following the fatigue protocol. Jump height, mean power, RSI-mod, hop distance, and side-hop performance decreased after fatigue induction, whereas contact time increased (Table 2; Fig. 5).

The exploratory Spearman correlation network demonstrated a coherent baseline performance structure among hop performance outcomes (Fig. 8). Jump height, mean power, and RSI-mod showed positive interrelationships, suggesting that these variables captured related explosive and reactive performance characteristics. Contact time showed inverse associations with this cluster, indicating that longer contact time was associated with lower explosive and reactive performance at baseline. Hop-for-distance and side-hop outcomes showed partial connectivity with the main performance cluster, suggesting that these tests may share some neuromuscular qualities while also reflecting task-specific demands.

Discussion

Following fatigue induction, jump height, mean power, RSI-mod, hop distance, and side-hop performance declined, whereas contact time increased, indicating impaired explosive and reactive lower limb function. The observed reductions are consistent with impairments in force-production capacity and stretch-shortening cycle performance following repeated high-intensity sprint exposure [3, 41]. These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating fatigue-related impairments in countermovement jump performance, reactive strength expression, landing efficiency, and neuromuscular control following intermittent high-intensity exercise in team-sport athletes [3, 9, 11, 41].

The increase in contact time alongside reductions in RSI-mod may be particularly relevant from a neuromuscular perspective. RSI-mod reflects the ability to generate jump height relative to time to take off and is commonly used as an indicator of reactive neuromuscular efficiency. The observed increase in contact time may also represent a compensatory strategy whereby athletes increase ground-contact duration to maintain impulse despite reduced force-generating capacity. Such adaptations have been described during fatigued jumping and locomotor tasks, where longer contact times may partially offset reductions in mean force production [12, 42]. The concurrent decline in jump height and mean power observed in the present study further supports reduced capacity for explosive force production following sprint exposure [3, 41]. Collectively, these findings suggest that fatigue affects not only maximal output but also the temporal efficiency with which force is generated and transferred during explosive athletic tasks [11, 12, 42]. From an applied perspective, this may affect an athlete’s ability to accelerate and decelerate rapidly, change direction, and repeatedly perform high-intensity movements during the later stages of training sessions or competition. Consequently, fatigue-related performance decline may reflect changes in movement efficiency as well as reductions in absolute performance capacity [1, 2, 38].

The present findings also support the potential value of fatigue-inclusive testing in soccer populations. Most routine hop assessments are performed under rested conditions; however, competitive soccer frequently involves repeated accelerations, decelerations, and multidirectional actions performed under substantial physiological stress [1, 4]. Consequently, rested assessments alone may not fully capture how athletes respond to repeated high-intensity loading. In the present cohort, fatigue-related declines were consistently observed across vertical, horizontal, and lateral performance tasks, suggesting that post-fatigue assessments may provide complementary information regarding neuromuscular resilience and movement efficiency under sport-relevant conditions [1, 3, 9]. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the present study did not include injury surveillance, prospective RTS outcomes, longitudinal follow-up data, or direct assessment of physiological characteristics such as aerobic fitness and metabolic capacity, which may influence individual fatigue responses [23, 37].

Another important finding was the limited independent explanatory value of FI. After adjustment for age and playing experience, FI coefficients were consistently small and imprecise across change outcomes. This suggests that a single global fatigue summary score may inadequately capture the multidimensional nature of fatigue-related decline in neuromuscular performance. Although FI is attractive because it condenses repeated sprint decrement into a single value, athletes with similar FI scores may still show substantially different changes in RSI-mod, contact time, jump height, or lateral hop performance. Previous literature on RSA has similarly highlighted limitations in relying solely on fatigue decrement scores due to variability in pacing strategy, baseline sprint performance, and inter-individual neuromuscular responses [38, 39].

The findings of the present study indicate that fatigue significantly impairs jump- and hop-related performance measures; however, the fatigue index demonstrated limited explanatory value in capturing these neuromuscular decrements. These findings therefore support a more outcome-specific interpretation of fatigue response rather than reliance on a single summary metric. They also suggest that combining multiple fatigue-sensitive performance measures may provide a more comprehensive understanding of neuromuscular fatigue than reliance on FI alone [9, 11]. For example, changes in jump height, contact time, RSI-mod, hop distance, and side-hop performance appeared to capture distinct aspects of fatigue-related neuromuscular impairment that would not have been fully reflected by a single fatigue-decrement score.

Age demonstrated more consistent associations with selected fatigue-related outcomes than FI. Age demonstrated stronger and more consistent associations with selected fatigue-related outcomes, particularly contact time and RSI-mod. This finding should be interpreted with caution because the study design does not establish whether age itself caused greater fatigue-related decline. An alternative explanation is that older players in this cohort generally demonstrated higher baseline jump-height and power values, thereby creating greater scope for observable performance decrements following fatigue exposure [9, 11]. Consequently, larger fatigue-related changes may partially reflect differences in baseline performance rather than age-related physiological effects alone. These findings do not necessarily indicate poorer conditioning among older athletes but may instead reflect differences in neuromuscular strategy, cumulative training exposure, recovery characteristics, or the greater absolute mechanical output typically generated by more experienced players [1, 16, 43]. Similar age-related changes in reactive performance and neuromuscular efficiency have previously been observed in athletic populations exposed to repeated high-intensity loading [42, 44]. However, the magnitude of these associations in the present study remains modest and should not be overinterpreted clinically.

Beyond the fatigue-response findings, The exploratory correlation-network analysis was included to describe the baseline structure of the performance outcomes and to determine whether the selected hop and jump variables appeared to represent overlapping or complementary neuromuscular qualities. This analysis was conceptually distinct from the regression models. The regression models examined whether FI independently explained fatigue-related change, whereas the correlation network described how baseline performance variables were interrelated before fatigue exposure. The observed grouping of jump height, mean power, and RSI-mod is consistent with the view that lower-limb performance metrics may reflect related but distinct performance domains. Geneau et al. similarly used data-reduction methods to classify force-time metrics into lower-body strength domains in field-sport athletes [25]. In the present study, the correlation network should therefore be interpreted as an exploratory descriptive tool rather than as a confirmatory clustering or prediction model.

Therefore, the present findings suggest that clinicians and strength and conditioning professionals should avoid relying exclusively on a single FI score when interpreting fatigue response in similar soccer cohorts. A more clinically informative approach may involve monitoring a small set of fatigue-sensitive variables, including jump height, contact time, mean power, RSI-mod, hop distance, and side-hop performance. Such assessments may be useful following intensive training sessions, congested competition schedules, or other periods of accumulated load where practitioners wish to better understand fatigue-related changes in neuromuscular performance [45]. This information may assist practitioners in identifying performance qualities that are disproportionately affected by fatigue and in tailoring subsequent training, recovery, or rehabilitation strategies accordingly [45, 46]. In the present cohort, RSI-mod and contact time appeared particularly sensitive to fatigue-related changes and may therefore provide useful insight alongside traditional performance metrics. Fatigue-based testing may provide additional context on an athlete’s ability to maintain explosive and reactive performance under loading conditions that more closely resemble those of competitive sport participation [46, 47].

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional observational design precludes causal inference regarding mechanisms underlying fatigue-related performance decline. Second, although the RSA protocol was selected for its ecological relevance and ability to induce neuromuscular fatigue, it represents substantially less total work than that accumulated during a full soccer match and therefore cannot fully replicate the technical, tactical, cognitive, psychological, and physiological demands of competition. Third, the sample was predominantly composed of professional state-level players, with comparatively fewer national- and international-level athletes, which may limit the generalisability to broader competitive levels. Individual differences in aerobic fitness and metabolic capacity were also not assessed and may have contributed to variability in fatigue resistance and recovery characteristics within the cohort. Additionally, the cohort exhibited relatively modest anthropometric characteristics compared with some professional soccer populations; therefore, the findings should be interpreted in the context of this sample. Furthermore, RSA sprint times were measured using a digital stopwatch, which is less precise than electronic timing-gate systems. Because post-fatigue testing was performed in a fixed sequence, partial recovery may have occurred before completion of the later assessments, potentially influencing the magnitude of fatigue-related changes across outcomes. Finally, biomechanical variables, movement quality assessment, and physiological fatigue markers such as blood lactate or heart rate responses were not included in the current analysis. Future studies should integrate fatigue-based hop testing with biomechanical assessment, physiological monitoring, and longitudinal injury surveillance to better clarify the relationship between fatigue response and sport-specific performance outcomes.

Conclusion

Fatigue-based hop testing identified consistent impairments in explosive and reactive lower limb performance among professional Indian male soccer players. However, FI demonstrated limited independent utility in explaining fatigue-related performance decline once age and playing experience were accounted for. Outcome-specific changes, particularly those involving contact time, RSI-mod, jump height, and mean power, may provide more clinically relevant insight into fatigue response than a single global fatigue metric.
