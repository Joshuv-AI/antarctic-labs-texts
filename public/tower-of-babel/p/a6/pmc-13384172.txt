Beyond Resnick and Niwayama: a structured narrative literature review on the clinical and imaging phenotypes of diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH)
Authors: Amir Bieber, David Kiefer, Fabiola Atzeni, Iris Eshed, Ana Rita Couto, Mariela Geneva-Popova, Netanja I Harlianto, Shay Brikman, Nicola Pappone, Pasquale Ambrosino, Giuseppe Scalise, Jácome Bruges Armas, Piercarlo Sarzi-Puttini, Jorrit Jan Verlaan, Muhammad Asim Khan, Reuven Mader, Xenofon Baraliakos, The EULAR DISH study group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSince 1975, the Resnick and Niwayama classification criteria have served as the standard framework for defining diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) in research. These criteria predominantly identify advanced disease. Accumulating evidence towards an updated definition of DISH is warranted. In this review we aimed to re-evaluate the definition of DISH in light of contemporary evidence and identify elements that may inform future classification criteria.A literature review was conducted of studies published between 1975 and 2025. Data extraction targeted imaging features, including site-specific bone formation and pre-DISH characteristics, as well as clinical features, functional outcomes and available markers. Findings were qualitatively synthesised.119 studies were included. Imaging data indicated that degenerative spinal disease alone is insufficient to exclude DISH. Bony bridges most commonly affect the thoracic spine, though involvement of the cervical and lumbar regions is well documented. Disease progression is typically slow, and frequent entheseal involvement of pelvic, chest wall and peripheral sites. Epidemiological data demonstrate a rising prevalence with age and a male predominance. DISH shows strong association with metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, elevated body mass index, raised systolic blood pressure, dyslipidaemia, hyperuricaemia and cardiovascular disease. Inflammatory markers are generally within normal ranges. Evidence regarding physical impairment remains mixed, with some studies reporting reduction in spinal mobility and chest expansion alongside broader functional limitations.This review moves beyond the Resnick and Niwayama criteria to offer an updated framework to support the development of future DISH classification criteria, particularly to earlier disease stages.

Introduction

Clinical description of diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) was first provided by Forestier et al in 1950, who delineated spinal hyperostosis and highlighted features distinguishing it from ankylosing spondylitis. In 1975, Resnick and Niwayama expanded on this by analysing vertebral features in more than 200 cadavers and proposing radiographic classification criteria for DISH.1 Since then, numerous researchers have enriched the field with clinical and radiological data. Despite DISH being a relatively common condition, its pathophysiology and the clinical consequences remain incompletely understood.

DISH is primarily a radiographic finding, with its prevalence increasing with age, particularly in people older than 60 years, and most research has focused on this population, often overlooking ‘pre-DISH’ states. The most useful and accepted classification criteria for DISH were suggested by Resnick and Niwayama et al and require radiographically confirmed flowing ossification along anterolateral aspects of at least four contiguous thoracic vertebrae, with preserved intervertebral disc spaces and sacroiliac joint (SIJ) spaces. Other vertebral bony bridges or peripheral enthesopathies are not required for classification of DISH.1 Alternative criteria, such as those suggested by Utsinger, have been proposed but have not gained routine acceptance.2 Notably, none of these classification systems incorporate non-radiographic or clinical features, such as age, metabolic syndrome (MS) or laboratory findings.

This review synthesises updated DISH literature to identify emerging evidence, particularly imaging data and comorbidities, that could inform the development of updated classification criteria for earlier disease detection.

Methodology

Two experienced rheumatologists (AB and DK) conducted a question-driven literature review (focused literature review) on the imaging and clinical aspects of DISH. The search was performed in PubMed using the MESH term “diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis”, restricted to English language, peer-reviewed publications from 1975 to 1 June 2025. Additional English language publications from the same period were included manually. Exclusions comprised irrelevant articles and congress abstracts.

Data extracted included two main categories: imaging of DISH and comorbidity associations. Imaging data extracted included data about conventional radiographs (CR), CT, ultrasound (US) and MRI, the anatomical location of DISH-related findings (eg, spinal, pelvic, chest wall entheses and peripheral imaging), as well as data regarding the progression of DISH over time. The clinical features were extracted regarding comorbidities, such as MS and blood and serum measures when available. Full details regarding the data source, publication information, study design and DISH definition used were also extracted. Extracted data were summarised descriptively, and pooled means or medians were calculated for selected variables when sufficient comparable data were available. As DISH is an end-stage status of a bone forming process, most studies were expected to be retrospective. DISH definition with concomitant degenerative disease

In addition, they found that degenerative changes were more prevalent in younger patients with DISH as compared with a younger control group. Based on these results, they suggest that these changes might predispose an individual to developing DISH. Supporting the idea, Slonimsky et al analysed CT data from 158 patients, finding that intervertebral disc narrowing was more prevalent among DISH patients than controls. They concluded that degenerative disc changes should not be an exclusion criterion for DISH classification.4

However, a small study by Kuperus et al5 complicated this theory. Using CT scans and histology on 10 cadaveric specimens with DISH and 10 controls, they found comparable imaging measures of intervertebral disc height between the groups, somewhat lessening the support for the predisposition theory. Furthermore, a study by Kagotani et al, which examined DISH in large cohorts of patients with osteoarthritis (OA), either spinal or peripheral, noted a higher prevalence of spondylosis in the aged population as is also seen in DISH.6 Most recently, Ziade et al studied patients with low back pain who underwent imaging (CR, CT or MRI). They divided patients into three groups—degenerative disc disease, DISH and axial spondyloarthropathy (axSpA). Regarding degenerative and axSpA-like changes, they observed a significant overlap of degenerative imaging features across all groups.7 These findings, combined with the fact that DISH predominantly affects the elderly, consistently suggest that contrary to established classification criteria, degenerative disc disease should not be used to exclude classification of DISH.

Spinal anatomical location of bony bridges

Some studies addressed the question of location of DISH-related bone formation outside of the thoracic spine. Resnick et al examined 215 cadavers (with 100 radiographs), described the frequency of bony bridges as 97% in the thoracic spine, 93% in the lumbar spine and 78% in the cervical spine.8 Diederichs et al also found the thoracic spine to be more frequently involved than the lumbar spine, with the upper thoracic and lower lumbar spine being less commonly affected.9 This pattern was corroborated by Ikuma et al who reported that D9-10 vertebrae as the most involved, with the upper thoracic and lower lumbar spines being significantly less affected.10

While cervical spine involvement is less common, it is described in case reports and series, primarily in patients examined due to symptoms like dysphagia or impaired spinal movement.11 Yaniv et al evaluated the progression of bone formation in DISH patients using CT and found that of 234 vertebral units with bridging osteophytes examined, only 15 were located in the lumbar spine and 3 in the cervical spine.12 An Italian study analysed 130 plain radiographs of patients with DISH and reported thoracolumbar involvement in 12.8% of cases, isolated lumbar spine involvement in 1.5% and thoracic spine involvement in approximately 85%–88% of patients.13

Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament is recognised as a subtype of axial ligamentous ossification that shares features with DISH.14 A study by Nguyen et al demonstrated a significant overlap, finding DISH in the majority of patients (104 of 182, or 57%) who underwent whole spine CT.15

Studies have differentiated the shape and size of DISH osteophytes from those seen in radiographic axSpA (r-axSpA). Baraliakos et al examined disease progression and reported the mean score for radiographic progression measured by modified Stoke AS Spine Score (mSASSS) over a 3-year follow-up was comparable between DISH patients and r-axSpA patients (1.3±1.0 mSASSS units per year in the DISH patients and 1.3±2.5 mSASSS units per year in the ax-SPA group).16 Another study by Liao et al used 3D spine CT reconstructions and found that the volume and angle of osteophytes in DISH were significantly larger compared with those in AS for each vertebral unit.17

The location of DISH-related bony bridges relative to the major blood vessels offers insight into the condition’s pathology.18–20 It has long been observed that thoracic bony bridges in DISH are more commonly found on the right side of the spine. This observation led to the hypothesis that the pulsating left-sided descending aorta exerts a protective effect, inhibiting new bone formation on the left.21 This hypothesis was tested by Gliner-Ron et al who studied 31 patients with situs inversus (a condition where the aorta is on the right side) and DISH. They found that the bony bridges in these patients were predominantly located on the contralateral (left) side, directly corroborating the protective pulsation theory. However, the study also noted a higher frequency of bilateral bridges in the right-sided aorta group compared with those with a normal left-sided aorta, suggesting that other, non-elucidated factors may also influence the location of bone formation.18

Collectively, these findings support the conclusion that the thoracic spine is the most commonly and characteristically involved region in DISH, particularly given that ossification in the cervical and lumbar spine is frequently attributable to degenerative spondylosis.

Evidence consistently indicates that spinal bone formation in DISH is an ongoing process that progresses over time. Kuperus et al conducted two studies on large patient cohorts. Over a 5–6-year follow-up, 11%–31% of patients classified as pre-DISH and 45%–55% of those with established DISH demonstrated progression of spinal bone formation.22 23 A second validation cohort study (2267 patients) by Kuperus et al found that 99 patients meeting the criteria for early DISH progressed to definite DISH after a mean follow-up of 5.4 years.24 Yaniv et al reported that it took approximately 10 years for a single complete intervertebral bone bridge to develop.12 Li et al followed 255 elderly volunteers (mean age of 71) over 6 years and observed a 4.3% increase in DISH prevalence in the cohort. They also noted that bridge formation progressed in 71.7% of patients. Interestingly, this progression occurs in both DISH and non-DISH patients, while the study found no correlation between progression and body mass index (BMI), age or physical function.25

A study by Fournier et al provided crucial insight into predicting progression. They followed 48 patients with established DISH and 30 with pre-DISH ossifications for an average of 2.7 years. 20% of patients with pre-DISH progressed to DISH. Crucially, no patient without any anterior intervertebral ossification progressed to established DISH. The rate of progression was directly linked to baseline severity: patients with more severe DISH at the first scan were significantly more likely to progress (OR of 4.94) than those in the early phase (OR of 2.4).26 These findings suggest that a pre-DISH state likely involves some degree of bony bridging in the thoracic spine, and individuals without this initial bridging are unlikely to progress to full DISH. Furthermore, the progression rate is relatively constant and tends to affect those who are already hyperostotic, though the clinical significance of this gradual progression remains a topic for further investigation.

Early work by Resnick et al identified numerous sites of bony outgrowth in the pelvis of DISH patients, including the ischial tuberosities, iliolumbar ligaments, lesser and greater trochanters, acetabular margins, sacro-tuberous ligaments, as well as anterior joint bridging and ankylosing of the fibrous part of the SIJs.8 Larger studies confirmed these findings. Haller et al compared 93 DISH patients to 76 patients with degenerative spondylosis, concluding that DISH was associated with greater pelvic abnormalities. They specifically noted the relative specificity of ossification at the iliolumbar and sacro-tuberous ligaments.27 Ciaffi et al found a positive association between DISH and hyperostosis of ischiopubic rami, iliac crest and greater trochanter.13 A significant association was also found with hip OA. Fahrer et al suggested that the juxta-articular acetabular margin spurs were particularly specific to DISH.28

SIJ involvement

Leibushor et al analysed pelvic CT scans of 104 DISH patients and found higher frequencies of anterior, posterior, along with more entheseal bridging and ankylosis of the ligamentous part of the SIJs, compared with 106 controls.29 Yahara et al compared SIJ CT scans of 86 DISH patients to 85 controls and found that bony fusion was the most common abnormality in DISH, with 71.6% showing anterior para-articular bridging. Conversely, the vacuum phenomena were significantly more common among the non-DISH controls (57.1%).30 Takahashi et al found that DISH patients were much more likely to exhibit partial SIJ fusion (either unilateral or bilateral) compared with axSpA patients. They also reported that anterior bony bridges were far more frequent in DISH (35% one side, 47% both sides) than in r-axSpA. Yet, posterior bridging and entheseal bridging were not different between the groups.31 Same findings were also reported by Dar et al, emphasising both SIJ and pelvic enthesopathy involvement.32

These data suggest a higher prevalence of enthesopathy-related bone formation in the pelvis, which could aid in the radiographic evaluation and differentiation of DISH. However, it remains to be seen whether spinal bridging precedes pelvic ossification or vice versa.

The pattern of SIJ involvement in DISH has important differential diagnostic implications. DISH typically shows anterior para-articular bridging in the fibrous anterior compartment, with little posterior bridging or erosions. In contrast, r-axSpA mainly affects the posterior ligamentous and synovial compartments and is characterised by active inflammatory MRI findings. On CT, partial or unilateral anterior bridging with preserved posterior joint space and no erosions favours DISH, especially in patients over 50 with metabolic comorbidities, mechanical back pain and HLA-B27 negativity. Conversely, bilateral posterior bridging, complete fusion or MRI-confirmed active sacroiliitis strongly suggests axSpA. Misclassification can occur in both directions, making CT the preferred modality for assessing compartment-specific SIJ changes, while MRI is useful for confirming active inflammation when CT findings are inconclusive.

Chest wall involvement

Relatively few studies have investigated chest wall involvement in DISH. Broitman et al examined CT scans of anterior chest wall in 92 DISH patients, as defined by Resnick’s criteria, and 91 controls. Authors reported a significantly higher frequency of anterior chest wall joint enthesopathy in the DISH group.33 Ikuma et al noted hypertrophic costovertebral joints in DISH compared with matched control.10 Frager et al found significantly higher rates of rib hyperostosis in DISH patients, with patterns including rib sclerosis and ankylosis. They noted a propensity for the right side, most commonly involving 5th to 10th ribs.34 Huang et al demonstrated that costal hyperostosis (with other vertebral ossifications) was much more prevalent in DISH (21.6%) compared with other conditions like axSpA (10.3%), quadriplegia (6.6%) and healthy controls (0.5%).35

These studies suggest that manifestations such as rib hyperostosis and sternal joints enthesopathies are relatively specific imaging features of DISH.

Peripheral enthesopathies

Peripheral enthesopathies are well documented but less specific features of DISH (see table 5). Resnick et al published the foundational study, describing radiographic evidence for enthesopathies across the humeri, elbows, forearms, hands and wrists, femurs, knees, ankles, heels and feet.8 Subsequent radiographic and pathologic studies have reaffirmed that appendicular skeletal enthesopathies are more common in DISH subjects than in non-DISH controls. Specific findings include a correlation between shoulder hyperostosis and shoulder pain among patients with DISH, metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint involvement (56% of DISH patients, which is uncommon (less than 10%) in typical OA.36–38 Pathological evidence confirms the presence of enthesophytes at major tendon insertions, including the Triceps brachii, Quadriceps femoris, an Achilles tendon as studied in Castells Navarro et al.39 Mata et al aimed to develop a scoring system based on radiographic evidence of enthesopathies among DISH patients. Agreement was higher for patellar enthesopathies, and with a lesser extent for elbow and shoulder enthesopathies.40 Several pathologic studies evaluated the presence of peripheral osteophytes in DISH. Verlaan et al described peripheral characteristics of 17 medieval skeletons with DISH, finding evidence for calcaneal spurring, iliac whiskering and olecranon tufting.41 The main issue with peripheral involvement in DISH is its specificity for DISH patients comparing to other bone metabolic diseases and comorbidities such as MS. It remains to be elucidated and studied how and if to integrate peripheral radiographic evidence of enthesopathies in DISH classification. Indeed, in a 2013 Delphi study conducted by experts with a special interest in DISH, peripheral enthesopathies were considered insufficiently validated to be included as formal components of future DISH classification criteria.42

US and MRI in DISH

The use of US for the assessment of peripheral enthesopathies was rarely assessed. Mader et al assessed US as a diagnostic modality of peripheral enthesopathies in DISH. In a study involving 50 patients with DISH versus 34 controls with osteoarthritis, the investigators have found an increased Glasgow Ultrasound Enthesitis Scoring System among DISH patients, examining the tendons and ligaments of the Patella, Tibial tuberosity and Calcaneal tuberosity. Higher scores were associated with higher likelihood of DISH.43 Another work by Falsetti et al looked at peripheral enthesitis among patients with MS using US and found correlation with BMI, pain and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Of note 46% of patients had concurrent DISH.44 Another similar correlation was found for Achilles enthesopathy.45 We conclude that the evidence for the use of US in the context of DISH associated enthesopathy and peripheral involvement is still lacking and more research is needed.

Only few works were done regarding MRI in DISH. Arad et al explored whole spine MRI of 10 patients with DISH and have found both bone marrow oedema (BME) of vertebral corners (5/10 patients), as well as fatty changes (8/10 patients), resembling axSpA changes.46 Another study by Weiss et al compared whole body MRI of 33 axSpA versus 15 DISH patients. They also found both inflammatory like lesions (BME) and structural changes (fat corners and ankylosis) of vertebra, while those changes were less prevalent compared with the axSpA group. The authors suggested spine and SIJ MRI as a good discrimination tool between DISH and axSpA.47 Latourte et al have found BME in DISH patients with back pain, with more than half meeting Assessment of SpondyloArthritis international Society (ASAS) criteria for axSpA.48 Finally, a recent preliminary study of whole spine and SIJ MRI of patients with MS versus patients without it (all without DISH), found increased rate of both BME and fat lesions among patients with MS.49 Altogether, there is insufficient data regarding the role of MRI in the identification of DISH. While some preliminary results suggest a low grade local inflammation initiating a process of subsequent bone formation in DISH, this hypothesis needs further corroboration.

Epidemiological and clinical characteristics

The reported prevalence of DISH varies widely from 3.8% to 30% primarily due to differences in the age of the study population and the imaging modality used (DISH is detected more sensitively on CT than on CR). DISH is strongly correlated with age, with a robust positive correlation with age (r=0.853, p=0.007) between age decade and DISH incidence.10 13 50–55 DISH is more common in men,6 10 13 54 56–63 while only one study showed contradictory results.64 Although DISH is known to be more prevalent in older patients, it has also been reported in individuals under 50 years of age,63 65 including published cases of women younger than 40.66 67 Ethnic and genetic background may play a role in the pathogenesis of DISH. For instance, the prevalence of DISH was reported to be lower in African blacks, Native American and Asian populations.51 68 Also, while in Caucasian and Asian populations DISH is unequally distributed between males and females, the magnitude of the ratio varies depending on the underlying study population, this ratio seems to be more balanced in black Africans and Native Americans.68 69

Association with MS

The WHO produced the first formalised definition of MS in 1998. The working definition included impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), impaired fasting glucose or DM and/or insulin resistance together with two or more additional components. Additional components included hypertension (HTN) (defined as a blood pressure ≥160/90 mm Hg), raised plasma triglycerides (≥150 mg/dL) and/or low high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C) (<35 mg/dL for men and <39 mg/dL for women), central obesity (defined either as BMI >30 kg/m2 or waist to hip ratio >0.90 for males and >0.85 for females) and microalbuminuria.70 71 Since then, multiple definitions of the syndrome have been proposed.

In patients with DISH, the marked heterogeneity of studies, inadequate matching of control groups, and inconsistent definitions of metabolic factors render most published results difficult to compare, precluding the performance of meaningful systematic meta-analyses.72 Several studies demonstrated a strong association between DISH and MS: Mader et al confirmed this based on WHO criteria,73 Vezyroglou et al reported metabolic abnormalities in 70% of DISH patients versus 45% of controls (p<0.0001)74 and the AGES-Reykjavik Study found a highly significant association (OR 2.12, 95% CI 1.69 to 2.64, p=3.9×10⁻¹¹), mainly driven by BMI and blood glucose criteria.75

Association with excess body weight and body fat composition

DISH is strongly linked to excess body weight, particularly central obesity measured by waist circumference.6 9 54 56 60 73 76–83 Overweight is defined in adults by the WHO as BMI greater than or equal to 25 kg/m2; and obesity as BMI greater than or equal to 30 kg/m2.84 85 Mader et al described 47 DISH patients with age and sex-matched controls, where both groups were obese (BMI >30 kg/m2) and in which DISH patients had a significantly higher BMI than controls. This association between DISH and increased BMI or abdominal circumference was also reported in cohorts with cardiovascular diseases (CVD)73 79 80 86 87 as well as in relatively young obese patients.88 An Italian study investigating the prevalence of DISH in a cohort of patients diagnosed with CVD showed significantly more obesity in patients with DISH than controls and demonstrated increasing ORs for the presence of DISH for increasing tertiles of age and BMI.52

There is a higher prevalence of DISH in patients with DM and obesity than in controls.89 The effect of obesity was reported to be greater in women, with DISH prevalence in obese women being four times higher, and its incidence three times higher compared with non-obese women.56 Only a few studies, such as the cross-sectional study by Yoshimura et al, have examined the prevalence and characteristics of patients with thoracic DISH, and these studies did not identify differences in BMI between DISH patients and controls.69 81 Significantly more visceral fat area (cm2) in the abdominal CT than in controls in line with higher BMI and numerically higher waist circumference (cm) was found to be associated with DISH. Another study showed that DISH and axSpA patients had higher visceral fat areas on abdominal CT compared with controls, reaching statistical significance only in DISH, while subcutaneous fat did not differ.77

Association with IGT and T2DM

DISH is considered a metabolic-related condition, and its link to hyperglycaemia and DM is a cornerstone of its clinical profile. Most studies show a positive association between the prevalence of DISH and T2DM and/or IGT.13 54 63 73 74 76 90 One population-based study found DISH was more prevalent in both males and females with DM compared with non-diabetic patients, even after adjustment for obesity.56 DISH patients have significantly higher blood glucose levels, abnormal glucose tolerance tests (22%) and established DM (9%); ratios that were twice those in age, sex and weight matched controls.2 Some studies report post-glucose challenge hyperinsulinaemia in DISH patients, while others found no difference in fasting insulin levels.91–93 In line, DISH patients have significantly higher Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) (p=0.009) and serum glucose level (p<0.001) compared with no-DISH population with other risk factors for vascular disease76 while other data did not confirm this difference in HbA1c.94 A recent study reported higher HbA1c levels in DISH compared with matched controls among patients who underwent spinal CT at a local hospital in Japan, regardless of its indication.83 A study on African American patients revealed that the presence of DM was significantly higher in male patients with DISH than controls but no association between thoracic DISH and DM in female patients.69 A case–control study reported 12% prevalence of DM in DISH compared 6.8% in controls, yet this was not statistically significant.64 Taken together, few studies failed to find an association between DISH and DM, glucose tolerance or HbA1c.9 79 95 96

Importantly, DISH lacks association with type 1 DM,72 substantiating the theory that the link is to metabolic dysregulation, for example, insulin resistance and MS, rather than merely to a lack of insulin. In conclusion, while the association with glucose intolerance and DM is supported by most data (higher HbA1c and blood glucose), its exact nature and magnitude still require clarification.

Associated systemic morbidities

Many studies reported that DISH patients have significantly higher systolic blood pressure and pulse pressure, but typically not diastolic blood pressure as compared with controls.13 79 81 97 The higher systolic pressure was associated with comparable levels of B-type natriuretic peptide.81 A recent retrospective study from Italy with 1012 individuals showed a high association with DISH and HTN (OR 2.04, p=0.007) compared with controls.13 Of note is that this HTN-association with DISH was seen in males but not in females.25 Similarly, a retrospective cohort study from Taiwan matched for age, sex showed significantly higher prevalence of stroke and HTN in patients with DISH (p<0.05) compared with controls.90 DISH is strongly linked to multiple components of CVD disease risk (angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, transient ischaemic attacks, stroke or peripheral metabolic vascular changes). Furthermore, in their study, the Framingham score and the 10-year risk for the development of coronary vascular disease were significantly higher for patients with DISH.73 The same cohort was retrospectively investigated with a 10-year follow-up and found that approximately 39% of DISH patients developed CVD compared with an expected 28.6%.86

Patients with DISH also show higher rates of cerebral infarction and major arterial stenosis, increased thoracic aortic calcifications,98 higher risk for strokes90 and more extensive vascular calcifications, independent of age, sex and race.99 Other studies found no independent association with coronary artery disease or mortality10 77 while some reported higher rates of coronary and aortic calcifications and ischaemic heart disease in DISH.63 94 100

Pulmonary airway issues

DISH can affect lung function and airway clearance due to its direct impact on the rib cage. Bone formation in DISH results in hypomobility of costovertebral articulation that can restrict chest expansion.35 101 102 Although uncommon, cervical DISH can lead to airway obstruction and/or dysphagia, occasionally necessitating surgical removal of anterior osteophytes.62 103

Associated abnormal laboratory tests

Inflammatory markers: Due to several similarities between DISH and its inflammatory counterpart, axSpA, possible association to inflammation is discussed.104 However, most studies find no significant difference in C reactive protein (CRP) levels or erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR).15 64 76 81 A recent study observed higher CRP values for early DISH compared with established DISH (p=0.038), but both levels were within the normal limits.105 A recent study showed that morphological characteristics of paravertebral bone formation in the entire spine, including the SIJ, are likely to be associated with elevated serum IL-17 levels in DISH.106

Dyslipidaemia: Several studies have yielded conflicting results regarding the association between DISH and dyslipidaemia. While some found no significant relationship,52 64 73 75 76 81 86 87 96 elevated HDL-cholesterol was associated with DISH after adjustment for age and sex,107 and hyperlipidaemia was more prevalent in DISH patients in a large retrospective study.90 Other studies observed no associations with total cholesterol, HDL or low-density lipoprotein (LDL).56 73 86 Some studies reported higher dyslipidaemia prevalence in DISH compared with controls and higher triglyceride levels, particularly in women.75 Serum adiponectin was similar between the two groups, but correlated negatively with insulin resistance and positively with cholesterol, LDL and the extent of bony bridging in DISH.81 108

Hyperuricaemia: In most studies, no clear association has been demonstrated between DISH and hyperuricaemia or serum uric acid level,79 93 109 while some studies found significantly higher values of uric acid in DISH patients compared with controls.74 98 The number of patients with self-reported gout is higher but not statistically significantly different in DISH compared with controls.58

In summary, there is currently no evidence supporting an association between DISH and established serum inflammatory markers, such as CRP and ESR, nor with hyperuricaemia. The relationship between DISH and hyperlipidaemia remains controversial.

Associated physical and functional impairment

The expected functional impairment of DISH patients yields inconsistent results. Some studies report objective and subjective limitations.40 56 101 110 For example, the Rancho-Bernardo study reported a strong association between DISH and impaired physical function in both men and women, with affected men showing significantly greater difficulty in performing the chair stand test.54 Individual reports have described severe motion restriction due to hyperostosis of the hip joints.28 Furthermore, the spinal level matters; Banno et al found that thoracolumbar DISH was associated with reduced flexibility and reach performance, whereas thoracic DISH did not affect balance or grip strength.97 Conversely, some studies found preserved muscle strength, walking ability, and stable quality of life score (EuroQol-5 Dimensions:EQ-5D) values over time.25 111 In summary, although there is strong evidence of mechanical impairment on DISH patients with ankylosed spine, it remains unclear whether this impairment translates into limitations in daily physical activities.112 When present, functional impairment may be associated with clinically relevant disability, particularly in patients with extensive spinal involvement, thoracolumbar disease or relevant comorbidities, underscoring the potential value of standardised functional evaluation in this population.

Conclusions and future directions

The entity of DISH is becoming better understood, but its pathogenesis is not clearly deciphered, and the heterogeneity of study populations and methodology limits the ability to draw definite conclusions. It remains ambiguous whether DISH is a cause or a consequence of its commonly associated metabolic disorders. Potential sex-specific differences in DISH phenotype, metabolic associations, and radiographic progression remain insufficiently studied and should be addressed in future prospective and imaging-based investigations.

Regarding classification criteria, current findings challenge several aspects of the established Resnick’s criteria. Studies indicate that DISH patients often also suffer from degenerative spinal disease. The coexistence of significant degenerative changes should therefore not automatically exclude a DISH classification, especially in the elderly. The impact of this degenerative overlap on clinical features like pain and range of motion needs to be defined.

It is now accepted that pre-DISH states can be detected on simple images like chest CRs, spinal CT scans or 3-D CT reconstruction, primarily in the thoracic spine. Studies confirm an expected rate of ossification progression, suggesting the threshold for defining ‘definite DISH’ might need to be lowered. However, the clinical implications of lowering the threshold are not yet clear, and research is needed to determine if early interventions can mitigate progression to full DISH. A limitation of this review regarding this aspect is the predominant use of Resnick and Niwayama criteria, creating potential circularity bias by focusing mainly on established, end-stage DISH and underrepresenting early disease. Although findings at the SIJ, chest wall and peripheral entheses, as well as studies of progression from sub-threshold bridging, partly address this issue, the proposed early-disease features remain hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation outside Resnick and Niwayama defined cohorts.

Involvement of the cervical spine, SIJ and chest wall has been documented. The SIJ, originally excluded from the classification criteria, shows radiographic signs related to DISH (eg, anterior bridging) which might contribute to DISH classification. The clinical significance of chest wall involvement is emphasised due to its contribution to hypoventilation syndrome.

The role of peripheral enthesis and advanced imaging modalities like MRI or US in defining DISH remains understudied. Stronger evidence is necessary before these are incorporated into official classification criteria.

The strong, consistent association between DISH and MS (including obesity, DM and CVD) is a major finding. Subjects with DISH face an increased burden of metabolic disorders and coronary artery disease.

Despite some conflicting results, evidence suggests that DISH should be considered a predictor of poor physical function, although the extent of this daily limitation depends on the patient, employment and location.
