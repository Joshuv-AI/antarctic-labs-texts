The relationship between heterogeneous institutional investors and operational performance: Evidence from mergers and acquisitions
Authors: Young Soo Park, Jaeseog Na
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the relationship between institutional investors and the inventory performance of companies engaged in mergers and acquisitions (M&As). Specifically, we examine the role of institutional ownership in the selection of target firms and their post-M&A performance. Additionally, we analyze how the effects of institutional investors vary based on their investment horizons and strategies. Our regression analysis reveals that acquiring firms with a large proportion of short-term institutional investors are more inclined to target firms with low inventory levels. This tendency stems from the preference of short-term institutional investors for better current operational performance, which drives decision-makers to merge with leaner firms. In contrast, acquiring firms with long-term institutional investors are more focused on performance improvement, even if the target firms have large inventories at the time of the M&A.Keywords: Mergers and acquisitions (M&As), Short-term institutional investors, Long-term institutional investors, Inventory performance

Introduction

Transaction volumes in the global mergers and acquisitions (M&As) market reached $3.7 trillion in 2017 (J P.Morgan 2018 Global M&A Outlook). Although M&As are motivated by the desire to create value through increased size and market share [[1], [2], [3]], nearly 70 % of these deals fail to achieve the expected synergies or outcomes [4,5]. Given that M&A can be a risky strategy requiring substantial resources, previous studies have explored factors that may influence M&A performance. From the perspective of operations management, Gonzalez et al. [6] suggested that the inventory of target firms can be a significant factor affecting the likelihood of successful M&As in the retail and manufacturing industries. Similarly, Sagner [5] provided valuable insights into the role of inventory assets in M&A decisions. The common argument is that acquiring firms are likely to underestimate the value of the target firms' inventory assets. Therefore, it is necessary to study how acquiring firms evaluate the inventory assets of target firms during the pre-M&A decision process.

However, M&As often stem from agency cost problems, which arise from conflicts of interest between shareholders and managers [7]. Since shareholders are typically not involved in corporate decision-making regularly, they tend to delegate their responsibilities to managers, leading to information asymmetry and resulting in agency costs. To mitigate these costs, robust monitoring and governance systems within the firm are essential [8]. In this regard, we aim to explore the role of institutional investors in the M&A process, grounded in agency theory.

Institutional investors are one of the most significant investment groups in U.S. markets. As they are known to control over 40 % of the world's stock markets, they tend to participate in the corporate decision-making process actively [9]. Managers often seek to incorporate the demands of various stakeholders, considering the stakeholder salience theory [10]. In this regard, institutional investors can influence the company's overall operations by expressing their expectations and monitoring managerial decisions based on their large ownership. Specifically, institutional ownership has been considered one of the determinants of inventory performance following the two channels in the operations management area: liquidity and control [11,12]. First, institutional investors may impose restrictions on inventory levels, as they prioritize liquidity management to increase cash flow. The pressure from these investors leads firms to maintain low inventory levels to avoid holding large, non-liquid assets. The second channel is about the control of the firms. An excessive level of inventory can be a signal of poor operations in the market. Thus, institutional investors use their control power to monitor the firm’s inventory level to maximize shareholder value. In sum, the liquidity and control channels allow institutional investors to manage inventory efficiently. With this regard, Ameer [13] explored the role of institutional investors in inventory management. Elsayed and Wahba [11] also studied the moderating effects of institutional investors on the relationship between ownership structure and inventory performance. Nevertheless, research gaps remain, particularly concerning the investment horizon of institutional investors, as not all institutional investors share the same perspective on firm activities [14,15].

On the one hand, institutional investors with short-term perspectives are likely to underestimate intangible strategies, leading firms with short-term institutional investors to discontinue long-term projects such as research and development (R&D) activities [14]. On the other hand, institutional investors with long-term horizons have incentives to put their value on long-run projects [16]. They are known to monitor the operations of firms they invest in, aiming to maximize shareholder value. Despite the different ways in which each type of institution may influence firm activities, relatively little attention has been paid to the effects of investment horizon in the operations management area. Also, there might be concerns related to endogeneity issues that examine the effects of institutional investors on inventory management in previous studies.

This paper tries to bridge the gap by considering the institutional investment horizon in the context of M&A events. M&A is well known to be a good setting to explore the effects of institutional ownership because M&A decisions can affect shareholder value significantly [15]. The primary objective of this study is to investigate the role of institutional investors in M&As and the impact of their investment horizon on inventory performance. Hence, the first research question is, “Do institutional investors have different views on the inventory performance of acquired(target) firms according to their investment horizon?” If the answer is yes, we then ask, “Are institutions with long-term investment horizons positively associated with post-M&A performance?” To answer these questions, we collected a dataset of M&A events among manufacturing firms in the U.S. from 1981 to 2013. Our analysis show that acquiring firms with short-term institutional investors tend to merge with leaner firm that already exhibit efficient operations. Further, we provide empirical evidence that institutions with long-term investment horizons focus more on inventory performance improvement after the M&As because firms with poor operations are considered to have the potential to be restructured [17]. Thus, merging a firm with more inventory slack can provide opportunities for performance improvement after the deals.

This study contributes to operations literature by examining the relationship between inventory performance and institutional ownership. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first empirical study to explore the heterogeneous effects of institutional investment horizon on inventory performance in the context of M&As. Second, we focus not only on inventory performance but also on abnormal operating performance. Using the well-known method used in event studies [18], we find that the presence of long-term institutional investors has a positive effect on abnormal operating performance after the deal.

Literature review

Institutional investment horizon

In finance and strategic management literature, agency cost theory is one of the overarching theoretical frameworks for studying the effects of ownership structure, as mentioned above. Even though top managers such as CEOs seem to determine the overall corporate strategies, stakeholders also have the power to control the firm’s strategic decisions. According to Hawley and Williams [19], institutional investors control about 60 percent of the largest 1000 U S. firms, up from only 20 percent in the early 1970s, reflecting the shift in ownership from individuals to institutions. Institutional investors generally hold more stock and have more time than individual investors in collecting information. Based on the large ownership, they can express their expectations, monitor managers' behavior, and influence corporate strategic decisions, consistent with the stakeholder salience theory [20,21]. However, it is necessary to explore differences in investment horizons and trading practices because not all institutions have the same perspectives [14,15]. In the literature, institutions are typically classified into three groups based on the investment horizon. The first group with a short-term investment horizon is defined as transient institutional investors with a myopic perspective in their trading behavior [14]. Since institutional investors with short-term perspectives are known to underestimate the value of long-term investment or intangible strategies, they are likely to focus more on current earnings [14].

Moreover, due to short-termism behavior, such as frequently buying and selling a small number of stocks, short-term institutional investors pressure executives to focus on short-term investment strategies to improve the current earnings, sacrificing long-term value [16]. Based on the characteristics of short-term institutional investors, Choi and Na [22] identified the detrimental impact of institutional investors with a short-term perspective on corporate technological diversification. Similarly, Meng and Wang [23] proposed that short-term institutional investors discourage firms' engagement in CSR (Corporate Social Responsibility) activities. In sum, considerable attention has been devoted to the detrimental effects of short-term institutional investors on long-term strategic outcomes such as R&D and CSR initiatives. Nevertheless, it is pertinent to acknowledge the potential positive implications associated with the presence of short-term institutional investors. In fact, short-term institutional investors can be better informed and beneficial because they are more sensitive than long-term institutional investors during times of crisis, forcing firms to adapt to changing conditions [24].

In contrast, there are institutional investors with long-term perspectives. Bushee [14] identified those institutions as dedicated institutional investors with long-term perspectives. They are likely to invest in selected firms with large stakes for the long run [14,15,25], so they are expected to participate actively in the corporate decision-making process and have incentives to monitor firms to pursue greater long-term value [26]. Thus, previous studies found they prefer long-term-oriented projects such as R&D investment or corporate environmental responsibility (CER) activities to achieve long-term value [27]. Velte [28] also addressed that long-term and sustainable institutional investors have more incentive to lead firms to have better ESG (Environment, Social, Governance) and CSR activities. When examining existing literature, it can be observed that long-term institutional investors generally have a significant influence on long-term indicators such as technological diversification and ESG rather than short-term performance [22]. Lastly, there is a group of institutions that hold diversified investment portfolios, which are called quasi-indexer institutions. They usually follow the index investment strategies without fundamental analysis [14,29]. Zheng [29] referred to Bushee’s classification to analyze the effects of institutional investment horizons on the smoothness of earnings. He found that long-term institutional investors are positively related to earning smoothing. While abundant research streams explore the impact of institutional investment horizon on corporate decision-making, only a few studies have analyzed institutional investors' effects from the operational management perspective. For instance, Elsayed and Wahba [11] attempted to explore the relationship between institutional ownership and operations management. However, their research primarily examines the effects of institutional investors on firm inventory management, thus overlooking the importance of considering institutional investors' perspectives and investment characteristics thoroughly. Based on this theoretical gap, we aim to highlight the importance of expanding the scope of institutional investors' influence on corporate decision-making, especially considering that inventory management which is one of the most challenging difficulties for firms.

Inventory management

Inventory management has been regarded as an essential part of corporate strategy, given its significant effects on firm performance [30,31]. Based on the fact that firms can respond to demand uncertainty by holding inventories [32], Azadegan et al. [33] provided empirical evidence that operational slack resources such as inventory are able to act as a buffer to alleviate the risk of external uncertainty. Hendricks et al. [34] also argued that operational slack resources help firms react less negatively to supply chain disruptions, arguing that although inventory slack may be a signal of mismanagement under normal conditions, it allows firms to respond fluently to disturbances.

While holding inventory has some positive effects, firms have undertaken initiatives to develop the efficiency of their processes. Since lean operations focus on eliminating slack resources by minimizing setup times and process downtime [33,35,36], numerous studies have shown that rapid inventory turnover and lean inventory management have advantages over holding large inventories. Based on this point of view, inventory leanness can be explained as something “to be achieved” by the firm to maximize their outcome by minimizing costs (e.g., inventory holding cost, rework cost, etc.) [37].

The previous literature has suggested plausible channels for the effects of institutional ownership on inventory performance based on the perspectives of liquidity and control. The liquidity channel suggests institutional ownership allows firms to access more cash from creditors, reducing firms motivation to hold more cashable assets such as inventories [12]. On the other hand, the control channel indicates institutional investors try to control and monitor firms, leading them to hold less inventory because excessive inventories are not a positive signal in the market [11,12]. Based on those two channels, Tribo [12] showed that institutional investors generally lower inventory levels. Ameer [13] also explored the effects of the institutional investor on inventory and cash performance, focused on foreign banks' role, and argued institutions would influence firms to have lower inventory levels and more cash. Elsayed and Wahba [11] studied the moderating role of corporate governance factors on the relationship between institutional ownership structure and inventory management. However, research examining inventory performance through the lens of institutional investment horizons is still in its early stages. Although we find there is a similar consensus among institutional investors on the nature of inventory management, there may be some key limitations in the sense that they have not provided details about the investment horizon of institutional investors. Many studies have attempted to explore the effects of institutional ownership in M&As. While there exists a general consensus between institutional investors regarding inventory management, a significant limitation is the lack of detailed information on institutional investors’ investment horizons. Numerous studies have delved into the impact of institutional ownership on M&As [[38], [39], [40]] and explored the association between institutional ownership and inventory [[11], [12], [13],41]. However, to the best of our knowledge, only a few studies have examined institutional investment horizon and inventory performance within the context of M&A. In this regard, our research aims to fill this research gap and contribute to the operations management literature by investigating the effects of institutional investment horizons.

Hypothesis development

Based on the stakeholder salience theory, institutional investors of acquiring firms, which include mutual, pension, hedge funds, banks, and insurance companies, are likely to participate in decision-making related to the M&A process [15]. As we alluded to earlier, long-term institutional investors have a longer investment horizon, so they are motivated to induce decision-makers to involve more long-term projects [27,42,43]. In contrast, institutional investors with a short-term investment horizon are likely to postpone long-term projects to maximize current corporate performance based on their myopic perspectives [14]. In sum, institutional investors sometimes have competing views on strategic decisions. Therefore, we need to classify them according to their perspectives and investment periods. When considering the influence of short-term institutional investors, they tend to decrease their investment holdings in response to external uncertainties swiftly. These investors, prioritizing short-term gains, are inclined to abandon investments with immediate losses [44]. Hence, it is reasonable to predict that the short-term institutional investors want to acquire firms showing a better cost structure, which is closely related to lean inventory [45]. Thus, we predict that firms with higher short-term institutional investors are likely to target a leaner firm at the point of M&As. However, some previous studies have argued that short-term institutional investors may not closely engage in corporate strategies due to their focus on short-term gains, potentially leading to limited involvement in strategic decision-making within companies [46]. However, M&A is a significant strategy that substantially impacts the returns of short-term institutional investors, and short-term institutional investors are likely to choose firms they expect to excel in managing uncertainties in M&A contexts. Hence, it is anticipated to exert short-term investors’ significant influence in our research framework. Therefore, we state our hypotheses as follows.

Firms with higher shares of short-term institutional investors are likely to merge with a firm with low inventory levels.

In contrast, institutional investors with long-term investment horizons have a different objective than those with short-term investment horizons in M&As. Long-term institutional investors are known to have the incentive to analyze an acquired firm’s long-term potential, looking beyond its current performance [14,25]. noted that long-term institutional investors are actively involved in corporate strategies based on a long-term perspective, arguing that they sometimes exhibit risk-taking behavior in strategic decisions [16]. Further, the long-term institutional investors are likely to establish relationships with corporate executives [47], which leads managers to improve operational performance. Specifically, the improvement in inventory performance based on the reallocation of inventories can positively enhance the company's value by satisfying customer requirements [48]. Hence, long-term institutional investors are likely to exert influence on corporate decision-makers to pursue the improvement of inventory performance post-M&A.

Given that lean management has been generally accepted to contribute to firm profitability both directly and indirectly [49], we expect that the presence of the long-term institutional investors in the acquiring firms affects improvement inventory performance after M&As. Thus, we can suggest that institutional investors with a long-term horizon positively influence inventory performance improvement, which increases firm value after M&As. Given the multitude of factors affecting the outcomes of corporate M&A, it's plausible that the significant influence of long-term institutional investors may not be evident. However, by revisiting the stakeholder salience theory, it's notable that long-term institutional investors tend to deeply engage in strategic decision-making and monitor corporate behavior from a long-term perspective. Consequently, as posited in this study, it was expected that long-term institutional investors would exert significant influences on post-M&A operational performance.

In a similar vein, considering that long-term institutional investors are widely known to possess sustained monitoring power compared to short-term institutional investors, we are able to predict that the long-term institutional investors have positive effects on the post-M&A operating performance as well. Therefore, the presence of an acquiring firm’s long-term institutional investors has a positive effect on the operating performance after M&As. This discussion leads to the following two hypotheses.

The larger the acquiring firm’s share of long-term institutional investors, the greater Post-M&A inventory improvement will be.

The larger the acquiring firm’s share of long-term institutional investors, the greater the Post-M&A operating performance will be.

Methods

Data and sample

Our sample consists of U.S. M&As between 1981 and 2013. As the M&A-related information is provided by the Securities Data Company (SDC) Platinum from Thomson-Reuters, the sample includes firms in the intersection of Compustat, 13f, and Bushee’s dataset, including 212–387 M&A events between manufacturing firms in the U.S. over the 1981–2013 period.

First, we merge the collected M&A sample with institutional investor classification data from Bushee’s website. As Bushee noted that there might be concerns about significant errors beginning in 2016, the sample in our research models covers M&As from 1981 to 2013 because we need to analyze three-year post-M&A performance. The classification is merged with the CDC/Spectrum 13F database, which includes data for institutional investors' shareholding information beginning in 1980. We obtain accounting and financial information and the total common share data from Compustat and M&A information from SDC Platinum. We set five selection criteria following the procedure of Deng et al. [50]: (1) the deal value is greater than $1 million; (2) the acquirer holds less than 50 % of the acquired firm’s stock before the M&A; (3) the acquirer holds 100 % of the acquired firm’s stock after the M&A; (4) the acquirer is a publicly-traded firm with accounting and financial data available from Compustat; and (5) the acquirer and acquired firms are both in the manufacturing industries (i.e., firms with Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes between 2000 and 3999). Our sample consists of only M&As between domestic firms because accounting-based measurements are not appropriate for cross-border M&As [51]. Only deals in manufacturing industries are considered because M&As between manufacturing firms are more prevalent, and inventory is especially significant in manufacturing industries [52]. We analyze data from one year before the M&A effective date to examine the effects of short-term institutional investors of acquiring firms on the inventory performance of acquired firms and three years after it to explore the effects of long-term institutional investors of acquiring firms on post-M&A performance. Some control variables identified to affect M&A events in the literature, such as firm size, are included to prevent spurious effects. We remove missing variables and exclude observations with negative sales and inventories.

Inventory performance

To understand how institutional investors affect the acquired firm’s inventory performance, we use the empirical leanness indicator (denoted as ELI) as a proxy for corporate inventory performance [[53], [54], [55]]. Although inventory turnover and inventory days have been widely used in traditional studies that examine inventory performance [31,56], inventory needs to be understood using specific industry and year characteristics [54]. In fact, traditional inventory measures have a limitation in reflecting industry effects and economies of scale [54], making it difficult to investigate whether institutions have different views on inventory performance considering industry heterogeneity. Abnormal operating performance

Several studies in finance and strategy have attempted to investigate operating performance following M&As. Although various metrics are used to measure post-M&A performance and are reflected in the firm’s financial reports, unobservable effects of the deal cannot be captured [61]. Therefore, it is necessary to measure expected performance based on the assumption that the event did not happen to consider unobservable effects. By comparing actual performance and expected performance, we can evaluate whether the firm’s post-event performance is abnormally high compared to its competitors. Following Hendricks and Singhal [61], identifying benchmark firms consists of four steps. First, we identify firms that have the same two-digit SIC code as the sample firm and whose ROA is between 90 % and 110 % of the sample firm’s ROA. Second, if no firms are selected in the first step, we relax the criteria. We use a one-digit SIC code instead of two digits, keeping the rest of the criteria the same as in step 1. Third, if no firms are selected in the second step, we select benchmark firms whose ROA is within 90 % and 110 % of the sample firm’s ROA without considering the industry conditions. If no firms satisfy the criteria of steps 1 to 3, a firm with the closest ROA, regardless of SIC code is selected. Analysis and results

In this section, we discuss the analysis results for the effects of institutional investment horizons in M&As. Our analysis consists of two parts: the first part examines whether different types of acquiring firm institutional investors exhibit different behaviors regarding the inventory leanness of acquired firms. The second part investigates the positive effects of long-term institutional investors on inventory performance improvement and abnormal operating performance after the M&A deal.

Univariate test

To investigate the preliminary association between acquiring firms' institutional ownership and acquired firms' inventory performance, we conduct the univariate test and present the result in Table 4. We divide our sample into two groups: firms are classified as group 1 if the number of shares held by short-term institutional investors is larger than that of long-term institutional investors and group 2 otherwise. The results show the inventory leanness of acquired firms is significantly higher when short-term institutional investors dominate the acquiring firm’s ownership. Thus, acquiring firms with more short-term institutional investors select a leaner target for their deal. The results offer not only preliminary support for the heterogeneity of institutional investors but also provide initial supportive evidence for Hypothesis 2.

Results for hypothesis 2 and 3

The results in Table 5, Table 6 provide empirical evidence for the influence of institutional investment horizons on M&A outcomes. We found that firms with leaner operations are more attractive targets for short-term institutional investors. Interestingly, long-term institutional investors seem to favor firms with less lean inventories, which may be due to their strategic approach towards sustainable growth and operational efficiency post-M&A, as posited by Obermaier and Donhauser [65].

To assess the longer-term impact of these investment horizons, we extended our analysis to review inventory leanness at two and three years after the M&A. To this end, regression equations (9), (10) were utilized.

As predicted, the estimated coefficient for long-term institutional investors is significantly positive, indicating that those institutions of the acquiring firm in the pre-M&A year have a positive influence on inventory performance improvement after the M&A. We set year −2 as the base year and measured the improvement over the next three years after the M&A. It is interesting to note that the effect of long-term institutional investors is significant only in model 1 (coef = 9.531, p < 0.05). Based on the result, we assure that long-term institutional investors are interested in long-term goals even though they seem to pursue less lean acquired firms.

We further explore the effects of institutional investment horizons on financial performance by evaluating abnormal operating performance (ROA) from the second to the third year after the M&A. We hypothesize that long-term institutional investors are more likely to impact the firm's performance positively in the long run. The results summarized in Table 7 support this hypothesis with a strong positive correlation (coef = 0.061, p < 0.05), reinforcing the notion that long-term institutional investors are instrumental in enhancing post-M&A operating performance. From an economic perspective, this indicates that an increase of one standard deviation in LTO leads to an increase in 3-year improvement in ELI of the acquirer by 46.140 (9.531 × 4.841) and abnormal operating performance after 3 years by 0.002 (0.061× 0.030).

In summary, we find that long-term institutional investors positively affect not only improvement in inventory leanness but also in abnormal operating performance in the years following the M&A. Our findings indicate that each type of institutional investor exercises their influence on the acquired firm’s inventory leanness differently. We also found that the presence of long-term institutional investors has significantly positive effects on the post-M&A performance of inventory and ROA. Our results provide empirical support for the inventory and operating performance predictions from the perspectives of institutional investors.

Conclusion

In this research, we aim to bridge the gap between the role of institutional investors from the perspective of inventory management in M&As. We empirically examine the heterogeneous effects of institutional investors on inventory and post-M&A performance using various datasets on about 300 M&As from 1981 to 2013. We find that institutional investment horizons are associated with the acquired firm’s inventory performance. Based on stakeholder salience theory, we estimate that the acquiring firm’s institutional investors are associated with the acquired firm’s inventory performance. Further, the effects may differ depending on the institutional investor’s investment horizon.

On the one hand, there is a positive relationship between short-term institutional investors in the acquiring firm and the inventory leanness of the acquired firm. The conclusion to be drawn is that short-term institutional investors of the acquiring firm are likely to push firms to merge a leaner firm because they want to maximize their current earnings. Thus, firms with lean operations are, in turn, selected by acquiring firms governed by short-term institutional investors. On the other hand, it seems there is a negative relationship between long-term institutional investors in the acquiring firm and the inventory leanness of the acquired firms. Excessively low inventory levels are considered as the firms are exposed to risks such as safety violations and supply chain disruption [61,66]. Considering that institutions with long-term perspectives are likely to invest safely for a long-time in selected firms, they put more value on the improvement of performance than current performance. With this regard, we next examine whether long-term institutional institutions of acquiring firms have effects on the improvement of performance after the M&A. Based on the results of the positive relationship between long-term institutional investors and inventory leanness improvement, we confirm the monitoring effects of long-term institutional investors that they place more pressure on firms to achieve lean operations for profitability. From this finding, we could logically argue that institutions with long-term goals are interested in improving the firm’s inventory performance. In addition, we also investigate the effects of long-term institutional investors on post-M&A performance. By measuring the abnormal operating performance, our results suggest institutional investors with long-term investment horizons are more actively involved in a firm’s decision-making and want to avoid a reduction in the value of their investments [67].

Theoretical contributions

This study can provide the first contribution by demonstrating the heterogeneity of institutional investors by differentiating their investment horizons and characteristics from the perspective of inventory management in the M&A context. Previous research has limitations as it predominantly focuses on the role of institutional investors in corporate governance and inventory decision-making processes without considering the heterogeneity of institutional investors [[11], [12], [13]]. In fact, many studies have emphasized the importance of categorizing types of institutional investors to examine their heterogeneous effects, as the investors often pursue different investment perspectives [16,22]. In this regard, this study suggests that the effects of institutional investors may vary when assessing the inventory performance of a target firm during the M&A process, highlighting the academic implication that the heterogeneity of institutional investors should be taken into account. Moreover, this study provides significant theoretical insights by shifting the predominant focus on institutional investors’ influence from a corporate finance perspective to an operations management perspective. This shift broadens our understanding, suggesting that corporate governance factors can also impact operational performance, thereby offering a foundational basis for further research in this area. Finally, this study offers a non-trivial theoretical contribution that the methodological concern of endogeneity could be mitigated by focusing on the event of M&A when examining the effect of institutional investors on inventory performance. Although existing studies have examined the relationship between institutional investors and inventory management performance [[11], [12], [13],41], it is true that they had potential concerns regarding endogeneity issues due to the limitation of their research models. This study utilized M&A events to link the institutional investment horizon with inventory performance, as M&A events provide an appropriate research context for addressing endogeneity concerns between institutional investors and corporate performance, allowing for analysis based on clear pre- and post-event periods [15]. Consequently, this study demonstrates that the presence of institutional investors drives corporate inventory management performance in the M&A context by mitigating the endogeneity concerns.

Practical implications

Our results additionally provide several practical insights for both managers and industry professionals. First, managers should carefully consider the composition of their institutional investors when planning M&A transactions. Our findings suggest that firms with a substantial proportion of short-term institutional investors may prefer to merge with firms maintaining low inventory levels, focusing on short-term cost reduction. In contrast, institutional investors with long-term horizons are more supportive of strategies that prioritize long-term operational improvements, such as maintaining higher inventory levels that provide a buffer against market volatility. These findings imply that managers involved in M&A should carefully understand the intentions of their institutional investors when selecting a target firm in order to minimize potential conflicts with shareholders during the M&A process. More specifically, regular communication and transparency about inventory management decisions can help secure institutional investor buy-in and support, which is essential for successful M&A integration and long-term operational success considering the heterogeneous investment horizon. Also, our findings highlight the importance of long-term perspectives, offering valuable insight for industry professionals involved in M&As. Traditional operations management literature has emphasized the importance of efficient inventory management through high inventory turnover rates. However, as indicated by the findings of this study, even merging firms with high inventory levels can be beneficial for achieving successful M&A outcomes by improving inventory management performance after the M&As, aligning with the viewpoint of long-term institutional investors. Therefore, this study suggests to industry professionals that a target firm’s high inventory levels can also be seen as an opportunity in M&A contexts for a long run. Finally, the results indicate that inventory assets are considered a significant factor in M&A decision-making processes. While most of studies regarding M&As have focused on financial and strategic issues, this study highlights that institutional investors who are regarded as key decision-makers actively engage in M&A decisions with a strong emphasis on inventory assets. These findings suggest that firms need to make strategic decisions regarding inventory management to successfully participate in M&A activities.

Limitations and future research

This study has focused on M&A events between manufacturing firms in the United States. However, this focus may introduce limitations concerning the generalizability of our findings. Future research could beneficially extend the scope of this study to include other countries or industries. Such an expansion would allow for an examination of different market structures, cultural differences, and industry-specific dynamics, offering a broader perspective on the relationship between inventory performance and institutional ownership. Additionally, our study has specifically focused on M&A events between domestic firms. However, cross-border M&As embody a complex interplay of regulatory environments, cultural factors, and market conditions, which profoundly impact the behavior of institutional investors and the performance outcomes of acquired firms [68]. These dynamics present a rich area for further investigation. It would be interesting to investigate how the role of institutional investors differs between domestic and cross-border M&As, potentially uncovering new insights into how these investors influence company performance in diverse global settings.
