Role of Fat-Free Mass-Adjusted Cardiorespiratory Fitness in Predicting Hospitalization Risk in Patients with Heart Failure
Authors: Young Seok Kim, Wonhee Lee, Tae Im Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeReduced cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) is associated with hospitalization risk in heart failure (HF). Traditional peak oxygen uptake (VO2) scaling uses total body weight (TBW), potentially underestimating CRF due to adiposity. The prognostic value of fat-free mass (FFM)-adjusted peak VO2 remains unclear, particularly in Asian populations.Materials and MethodsA retrospective cohort study included HF patients who underwent cardiopulmonary exercise testing and bioelectrical impedance analysis. Two peak VO2 cutoffs—14 mL/TBW kg/min and 19 mL/FFM kg/min—were applied to predict all-cause and HF-specific 1-year hospitalization. The prognostic performance was assessed using Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for the Meta-Analysis Global Group in Chronic Heart Failure (MAGGIC) risk score. Likelihood ratio tests were conducted to evaluate the incremental value of adding the FFM-adjusted cutoff.ResultsA total of 83 patients (mean age, 60.0 years; 14 female; 37 obese) were analyzed. Both TBW- and FFM-adjusted cutoffs were significantly associated with increased risk of all-cause hospitalization [hazard ratio (HR)=3.86, 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.40–10.63 vs. HR=5.02, 95% CI, 1.98–12.72] and HF-specific hospitalization (HR=4.00, 95% CI, 1.00–16.05 vs. HR=7.26, 95% CI, 1.92–27.46). Adding the FFM-adjusted cutoff significantly improved model fit when added to a model with the TBW-adjusted cutoff (p<0.05). The difference in c-indices between the two cutoffs after bootstrapping was not statistically significant.ConclusionThe FFM-adjusted cutoff can complement the traditional TBW-adjusted cutoff by correcting the confounding bias of excessive adiposity or low muscle mass, providing incremental prognostic value for risk stratification in Asian patients with HF.

INTRODUCTION

Heart failure (HF) is a clinical syndrome characterized by structural and/or functional cardiac abnormalities leading to frequent hospitalization and death. The global prevalence of HF is approximately 1% to 3%, and nearly half of HF patients are readmitted within 1 year.1 Individuals with HF have low cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), defined as the functional capacity of the circulatory, respiratory, and musculoskeletal systems to meet oxygen demands during physical activity. CRF is often utilized to predict cardiovascular and all-cause mortality.2 Patients with higher CRF demonstrated a 58% reduction in all-cause mortality and a 73% reduction in cardiovascular mortality compared to those with lower CRF.3

Current guidelines recommend cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPX) to assess CRF for patients with HF.456 This technique quantifies CRF as peak oxygen uptake (VO2) by assessing the oxygen cascade from the atmosphere to mitochondrial respiration in skeletal muscle.6 Researchers often normalize peak VO2 to body weight, comparing relative CRF in patients of diverse body sizes.78

However, total body weight (TBW) includes metabolically inactive fat tissue, which has a weaker association with CRF, especially in obese patients.9 Including fat mass in the denominator of peak VO2 could underestimate CRF as adipose tissue does not directly affect VO2 or workload but instead adds a load during locomotion.10 In contrast, fat-free mass (FFM) shows a stronger correlation with peak VO2, as it reflects metabolically active components of the human body.11 FFM accounts for 85% of resting energy expenditure12 and correlates with peak VO2 better than body weight.11

Comparative evidence regarding the prognostic value of peak VO2 normalized by either TBW or FFM is limited. Currently, only one study has reported that peak VO2 predicts mortality or the need for heart transplantation more effectively in HF patients when adjusted for FFM rather than TBW.13 These findings led the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) to recommend using FFM-adjusted peak VO2 as a cutoff for obese HF patients.5 However, that study did not include Asian participants, and the recommendation has not been validated in subsequent research.

Given this gap in current research, we aimed to investigate whether FFM-normalized peak VO2 provides complementary prognostic value to TBW-normalized peak VO2 in predicting 1-year hospitalization risk in Asian patients with HF. Furthermore, we examined whether the prognostic value varied according to BMI level or HF subtype.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participant selection

We conducted a retrospective cohort study at a single center in the Republic of Korea. Individuals with HF who underwent maximal CPX between March 2020 and August 2023 were initially included. HF was confirmed based on the European Society of Cardiology diagnostic criteria.4 Two investigators independently reviewed and cross-checked electronic health records using standardized data collection forms. We excluded patients as follows: 1) age <18 years; 2) lack of bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) within 60 days of the CPX evaluation date; 3) gait disturbance due to neurologic or musculoskeletal conditions; 4) inconclusive echocardiographic data; and 5) severe edematous status. Since no consensus threshold exists for edematous status, we used the extracellular water-to-total body water ratio from BIA. We set the threshold at 0.43, based on the mean plus one standard deviation from a previous study on body fluid assessment in HF.14 The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Yongin Severance Hospital for human research (IRB No: 9-2023-0084).

Baseline characteristics

We collected baseline data on demographics, comorbidities, medication history, risk factors, and laboratory results. Obesity was defined as body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2, in accordance with the World Health Organization criteria for Asian populations. Echocardiography data were collected, including left ventricular ejection fraction (EF), E/e’ ratio, right ventricular systolic pressure, left atrial volume index (LAVI), and left ventricular mass index. Low activity level was assessed using the validated International Physical Activity Questionnaire. The Meta-Analysis Global Group in Chronic Heart Failure (MAGGIC) risk score was calculated for all participants.15

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing

CPX was conducted using a standardized treadmill protocol designed to achieve a test duration of approximately 10 minutes based on each patient’s predicted CRF.161718 After calibration, incremental data such as VO2, minute ventilation (VE), and carbon dioxide production (VCO2) were collected breath-by-breath every 20 seconds using the Quark CPX system (COSMED, Rome, Italy). Peak VO2 was defined as the highest average VO2 over the last 30 seconds of the exercise test. Peak VO2 was expressed as absolute (mL/min), weight-adjusted (mL/TBW kg/min), FFM-adjusted (mL/FFM kg/min), and percentage of predicted peak oxygen uptake (%ppVO2).18 The VE/VCO2 slope was measured up to the anaerobic threshold point. Tests with a respiratory exchange ratio of less than 1.05 were considered submaximal and excluded from the study.5

Body composition analysis

Body composition was determined using multifrequency BIA with the InBody S10 device (BioSpace, Seoul, Korea). BIA is an inexpensive and feasible method for estimating body composition in cardiovascular research.14 Compared with single-frequency BIA, the multifrequency approach showed better agreement with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry in estimating FFM.1920 Patients were instructed to fast for at least 3 hours with the bladder emptied; to refrain from caffeine, alcohol, and vigorous physical activity for 24 hours before the measurement; and to adopt a supine position with arms apart from the body and legs separated while resting for 15 minutes. Electrodes were attached to the dorsal surface of the wrist and ankle of the participants. Body composition and water-related variables were estimated using built-in equations based on multifrequency measurements. Low appendicular lean mass index (ALMI) was assessed according to the criteria of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia.21

Outcomes

We compared 1-year all-cause hospitalization using two cutoff methods suggested by previous guidelines513: 14 mL/TBW kg/min and 19 mL/FFM kg/min. Since the FFM-adjusted cutoff of 19 was less validated, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using 17, 18, and 19 mL/FFM kg/min. The index date was the CPX date. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to BMI category and HF subtype. HF-specific hospitalization was also analyzed based on documented clinical signs or symptoms of HF.

Statistical analysis

Data were reported as mean±SD, median with interquartile range, or number and percentage. The baseline characteristics of the obese and non-obese groups were compared using unpaired t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test or chi-square tests for categorical variables. The Shapiro–Wilk test assessed data distribution in all analyses. NT-proBNP values were log-transformed to approximate a normal distribution. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the linear relationship between %ppVO2 and the two adjustment methods of peak VO2 (TBW- and FFM-adjusted). Steiger’s modification of Dunn and Clark’s z-transformation was performed to compare dependent correlation coefficients. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the association between these cutoff values and 1-year hospitalization risk. To prevent overfitting due to the limited number of events, all Cox proportional hazards models were adjusted exclusively for the MAGGIC score, a validated composite variable that includes key clinical confounders in prognostic outcome studies among patients with HF.2223 Model performance was assessed using the Akaike information criterion. The discrimination ability was assessed with the concordance index (c-index), and statistical significance of the difference in c-indices was evaluated through bootstrapping with 1000 resamples. Likelihood ratio tests (LRTs) on nested Cox models were conducted to investigate the incremental prognostic value of each cutoff. Specifically, we compared a model containing the TBW-adjusted peak VO2 cutoff to a larger model that additionally included the FFM-adjusted peak VO2 cutoff, and vice versa. The extended model containing both VO2 metrics was constructed solely for statistical comparison to evaluate incremental prognostic contribution. It is not intended to represent a clinically applicable prediction model, given the inherent collinearity between the two VO2 indices. The p-for-interaction was calculated to examine whether the prognostic value of both TBW- and FFM-adjusted cutoffs differed by obesity or HF phenotype. Subgroup analysis for HF-specific hospitalization was not performed due to the low number of events. Continuous peak VO2 variables for both TBW and FFM were standardized to a one-standard deviation change to compare hazard ratios (HRs) for all-cause hospitalization. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.3, and a two-tailed p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Table 1 shows baseline characteristics of the 83 patients included in the analysis. The mean age was 60.0±13.8 years; 14 (16.9%) were female, and 37 (44.6%) were obese. The obese group had a higher EF than the non-obese group (47.8±11.8 vs. 40.6±13.0, p=0.011). The MAGGIC risk score was significantly higher in the non-obese group (18.6±7.4 vs. 13.5±6.4, p=0.001). No other demographic, comorbidity, medication, echocardiographic, or laboratory differences were observed between groups (p>0.05).

CPX and body composition analysis

Significantly higher peak VO2 was observed in the obese group compared to the non-obese group, both in absolute terms (1190.7±465.1 vs. 1514.5±475.7) and as a percentage of predicted peak VO2 (56.7±15.7 vs. 67.3±16.6) (p=0.004) (Table 2). There were no statistically significant differences between groups in TBW-adjusted peak VO2 (19.3±6.5 vs. 19.3±5.6, p=0.990), FFM-adjusted peak VO2 (25.5±7.8 vs. 28.4±7.2, p=0.085), and VE/VCO2 slope (36.1±7.9 vs. 34.3±6.4, p=0.270). Body composition analysis showed significantly higher body weight, FFM, fat mass, and percent fat mass in the obese group compared to the non-obese group (p<0.001). The proportion of low ALMI was significantly higher in the non-obese group (p<0.001).

Cox proportional hazards models for 1-year hospitalization

Twenty-three patients (30%) were hospitalized during follow-up; 15 (32.6%) in the non-obese group and 8 (21.6%) in the obese group. HF-specific hospitalization events occurred in 9 patients (10.8%) in the non-obese group and 2 patients (2.4%) in the obese group. Patients with FFM-adjusted peak VO2 below 19 had higher rates of all-cause (11 out of 17, 64.7% vs. 12 out of 66, 18.2%) and HF-specific 1-year hospitalization (8 out of 17, 47.1% vs. 3 out of 66, 4.5%) compared with those above 19. Similarly, patients with TBW-adjusted peak VO2 below 14 demonstrated higher rates of all-cause (10 out of 17, 58.8% vs. 13 out of 66, 19.7%) and HF-specific 1-year hospitalization (7 out of 17, 41.2% vs. 4 out of 66, 6.1%) compared with those above 14 (Supplementary Table 1, only online).

Both cutoffs were significantly associated with higher hazards of all-cause hospitalization {peak VO2 <14 mL/TBW kg/min [HR=3.86; 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.40–10.63, p=0.009], peak VO2 <19 mL/FFM kg/min (HR=5.02; 95% CI 1.98–12.72, p=0.001)} and HF-specific hospitalization [peak VO2 <14 mL/TBW kg/min (HR=4.72; 95% CI 1.08–20.57, p=0.039), peak VO2 <19 mL/FFM kg/min (HR=9.02; 95% CI 2.10–38.67, p=0.003)] after adjustment (Table 3). The difference in c-indices between the FFM-based model and TBW-based model was small, and a bootstrap analysis showed no statistically significant difference in c-indices for all-cause hospitalization (ΔC-index=0.008; 95% CI, -0.012 to 0.028; p=0.514) or HF-specific hospitalization (ΔC-index=0.019; 95% CI, -0.089 to 0.022; p=0.332). Adding the FFM-adjusted peak VO2 cutoff significantly improved model fit for both all-cause (LRT χ2=4.305, p=0.038) and HF-specific (LRT χ2=5.521, p=0.019) hospitalization when added to a model with the TBW-adjusted cutoff (Table 4). On the contrary, adding the TBW-adjusted cutoff did not improve the model fit (LRT χ2=0.008, p=0.927 for all-cause hospitalization; LRT χ2=0.148, p=0.701 for HF-specific hospitalization). No significant violation of the proportional hazards assumption was detected in any multivariable model (Supplementary Table 2, only online).

Subgroup analyses regarding obesity and HF phenotype demonstrated no statistically significant interactions for either TBW- or FFM-adjusted cutoffs (all p-for-interaction >0.05) (Table 5). Sensitivity analyses showed that the prognostic significance of the FFM-adjusted cutoff was consistent when lower thresholds of 17 and 18 mL/FFM kg/min were applied (Supplementary Table 3, only online). The standardized HRs of peak VO2 for all-cause hospitalization were similar regardless of the adjustment method (standardized TBW-adjusted peak VO2, HR=2.26, 95% CI 1.36–3.75, p=0.002; standardized FFM-adjusted peak VO2, HR=2.20, 95% CI 1.37–3.54, p=0.001) (Supplementary Table 4, only online).

Correlation of peak VO2 corrected for body weight and FFM with %ppVO2

Both TBW- and FFM-adjusted peak VO2 showed significant positive correlations with %ppVO2 after Bonferroni correction (Supplementary Table 5, only online). However, FFM-adjusted peak VO2 showed a stronger correlation coefficient than the TBW-adjusted measure (0.759 vs. 0.614, p<0.001). Only FFM-adjusted peak VO2 showed a significant correlation with %ppVO2 in the HFpEF and obese subgroups (p<0.05). Regression lines based on FFM-adjusted peak VO2 were more consistent across sex, type of HF, and obesity than those based on TBW-adjusted peak VO2 (Fig. 1). Steiger's z-tests confirmed a statistically significant difference between these two correlation coefficients in the total population and all examined subgroups (p<0.05), except the non-obese subgroup (Supplementary Table 5, only online). None of the three variables significantly deviated from a normal distribution (all p-values >0.05).

DISCUSSION

Our study not only demonstrated the prognostic value of the 19 mL/FFM kg/min cutoff but also established its complementary prognostic value beyond that of the 14 mL/TBW kg/min cutoff in patients with HF. Adding the FFM-adjusted cutoff significantly improved a prognostic model that included the traditional TBW-adjusted cutoff, whereas the reverse was not true. The prognostic value of the FFM-adjusted cutoff was consistent across obesity status and type of HF. Sensitivity analysis further supported the robust FFM-adjusted cutoff by showing its significance at lower thresholds of 17 or 18.

Peak VO2 measured by CPX reflects the cellular aerobic respiration needed to sustain elevated metabolic demands during exercise.24 Physicians can prescribe individualized exercise intensities that align with an individual’s physiological capacity to induce optimal mitochondrial stress, enhancing the effectiveness of exercise interventions.25 However, body composition can confound this process since individuals with similar height and body weight may vary significantly in fat mass.26 FFM and fat mass exhibit distinct mass-specific metabolic rates, with FFM contributing disproportionately to total energy expenditure.27 Normalizing peak VO2 solely to TBW can overemphasize the metabolic rate of fat mass, underestimating actual aerobic capacity.10 Instead, expressing peak VO2 relative to FFM provided a less biased measure of CRF than TBW.9 A study in a healthy population also showed that FFM normalization predicts all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality more accurately than TBW.28

We chose all-cause hospitalization as the primary outcome because it is a crucial endpoint in HF patients, affecting medical costs and quality of life.2930 HF-specific hospitalizations accounted for about one half of hospitalizations in our cohort (11 out of 23 events), consistent with previous findings.2930 A relatively higher proportion of hospitalizations in the obese group (6 out of 8) were due to non-HF-related causes, compared to the non-obese group (6 out of 15). This suggests that while non-obese patients experienced a higher burden of HF-related readmissions, obese patients were proportionally more affected by other comorbidities leading to hospitalization. This finding implies that comprehensive management of comorbidities may be important in patients with HF who are obese.

Our results showed that both TBW and FFM-adjusted peak VO2 cutoffs remained significant for all-cause and HF-specific hospitalization. The cutoff of 19 mL/FFM kg/min is outdated and has not been validated in recent literature. While determining the optimal FFM-based cutoff is beyond the objective of this study, our study confirmed that FFM-adjusted VO2 with a 19 mL/FFM kg/min cutoff was significantly associated with hospitalization risk, which was true even with lower cutoffs of 17 or 18 mL/FFM kg/min. Although standardized FFM- and TBW-adjusted peak VO2 showed similar HRs in our findings, the FFM-adjusted cutoff significantly improved model fit when added to a model with the TBW-adjusted cutoff, but not vice versa. This finding indicates that adjusting for FFM may refine the assessment of CRF, providing distinct prognostic information that complements the traditional TBW-adjusted peak VO2. Therefore, the FFM-based threshold from the ISHLT guideline13 could be valid for predicting the hospitalization risk of Asian patients with HF.

Interaction analyses revealed no statistically significant differences in the prognostic associations of peak VO2 across obesity or HF phenotypes. Nevertheless, exploratory subgroup analyses suggested that the FFM-adjusted cutoff might offer additional discriminatory value in the non-obese and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF)/heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF) patients, while the TBW-adjusted cutoff did not reach statistical significance. These trends are noteworthy as limitations in TBW-based peak VO2 have typically been emphasized in obese populations.9101331

One possible explanation is the confounding effect of low muscle mass. While direct diagnosis of sarcopenia or frailty was not possible in this study due to the absence of parameters such as handgrip strength or physical performance, the non-obese group showed a significantly higher proportion of low ALMI than the obese group—a key diagnostic criterion of sarcopenia.21 Cumulative evidence indicates that reduced skeletal muscle mass is associated with poor prognosis,32 and non-obese sarcopenic individuals experience worse outcomes than obese non-sarcopenic individuals.23 Similar limitations of TBW-adjusted peak VO2 were also observed in HFrEF patients, where the prevalence of sarcopenia is notably high in the literature.32 Furthermore, many Asian HF patients exhibit a lean fat phenotype.33 A Korean national survey found that more than 30% of normal-weight adults exceed proposed body fat cutoffs (20.6% for men; 33.4% for women).34 In our cohort, 31 (67.4%) non-obese participants were categorized as normal-weight obese, showing worse outcomes than the obese, non-fat individuals.33 Therefore, the FFM-adjusted peak VO2 may provide complementary prognostic value in patients with HF, particularly where low muscle mass or disproportionate adiposity limit the accuracy of TBW-adjusted peak VO2.

Obesity, often driven by excessive energy intake, is an independent risk factor for HF.35 However, the obese group in our study had fewer hospitalization events and lower MAGGIC scores than the non-obese group. Baseline cardiac function may partly explain the different prognosis between the groups. A significantly lower EF in the non-obese group may reduce the stroke volume, limiting oxygen delivery to working muscles during exercise.36 Moreover, the enlarged LAVI observed in the non-obese group may reflect elevated left atrial pressures indicating diastolic dysfunction and reduced CRF.37

The favorable outcome in the obese group also aligns with the so-called “obesity paradox”—the observation of a lower mortality rate in overweight or obese populations than lean populations.38 Numerous factors, including physical fitness and adiposity, have been proposed to explain this phenomenon.3139 For example, the obesity paradox was observed primarily in HF patients with peak VO2 less than 14 mL/TBW kg/min; patients with higher CRF did not show a discrepancy between BMI and clinical outcomes.31 Recent research also showed that the obesity paradox disappeared when adiposity measures were included alongside BMI.39

FFM-adjusted peak VO2 was slightly higher in the obese group (28.4±7.2 mL/FFM kg/min vs. 25.5±7.8 mL/FFM kg/min, p=0.085), although this did not reach statistical significance possibly due to limited sample size. In addition, %ppVO2, which was significantly higher in the obese group, exhibited stronger correlations with FFM-adjusted peak VO2 than with the TBW-adjusted measure across the total and subgroups, except for patients with BMI <25. These findings suggest that FFM-adjusted peak VO2 might capture the combined influence of CRF and body composition in patients with HF.

Several limitations are acknowledged in this study. First, an information bias may have been introduced owing to the retrospective study design. Future research is required in a prospective, multicenter setting. Second, a selection bias may have affected the findings as we included only patients able to perform maximal tests. Third, although exploratory subgroup analyses suggested potential differences across subgroups, all p-for-interaction values were non-significant. A larger cohort study is warranted to further investigate these subgroup-specific effects of FFM-adjusted peak VO2. Fourth, our adjusted survival model used the MAGGIC score as the sole covariate. While the score incorporates multiple key clinical and demographic predictors of HF outcomes, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, although we excluded severe edematous patients, BIA may be influenced by fluid retention. This could potentially overestimate FFM in patients with HF.40 Future studies may consider dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry to minimize this confounding effect.

In conclusion, FFM-adjusted peak VO2 provided significant incremental prognostic value for predicting all-cause and HF-specific hospitalization risk in Asian patients with HF. The cutoff value of 19 mL/FFM kg/min remained a significant risk factor regardless of obesity and type of HF. FFM-adjusted peak VO2 complements traditional TBW-based assessment by correcting the confounding bias of excessive adiposity or low muscle mass, thereby offering a robust stratification tool.
