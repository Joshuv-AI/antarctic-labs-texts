Sex differences in the prognostic value of urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio for coronary artery disease and cardiovascular events in people with type 2 diabetes and normoalbuminuria
Authors: Orlando Siverio-Morales, Ainhoa González-Luis, Carmen Mora-Fernández, Carolina Hernández-Carballo, Alberto Martín-Olivera, Juan F. Navarro-González, Javier Donate-Correa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUrinary albumin excretion is an established marker of cardiovascular (CV) risk in people with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). However, its prognostic significance within the normoalbuminuric range (< 30 mg/g) remains uncertain, particularly regarding sex-specific differences. This study examined whether urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR; KDIGO A1 range) is associated with coronary artery disease (CAD) severity and 6-year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in women and in men with T2DM and preserved kidney function (eGFR > 60 mL/min/1.73 m², UACR < 30 mg/g), treating sex differences as a co-primary objective.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study involving adults with T2DM who underwent diagnostic coronary angiography. Baseline associations between log-transformed UACR, CAD severity, CV risk factors, and inflammatory markers were evaluated using multivariable linear regression. MACE (defined as non-fatal myocardial infarction or unstable angina requiring urgent revascularization, stroke, or CV death) were recorded during 6-year of follow-up. Cox proportional hazards models, adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, smoking, BMI, lipid profile, hs-CRP, and ACEI/ARB use, were used to assess UACR–MACE associations.Results We included 420 adults (180 women, 42.9%) with a mean of age 65.3 ± 10.7 years and a median UACR 7.56 mg/g (IQR 4.12–15.5). Significant CAD was present in 310 participants (73.8%), and 78 experienced MACE during follow-up (35.5%). Higher UACR was independently associated with greater coronary stenosis (adjusted R² = 0.090, p < 0.001). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a significantly higher incidence of MACE in the highest UACR tertile (log-rank p = 0.039). In multivariable Cox models adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, smoking, lipid profile, hs-CRP, SSI, and ACEI/ARB use, higher log-UACR independently predicted MACE (adjusted HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.35–2.10; p < 0.01). In sex-stratified Cox models, higher log-UACR predicted MACE in both sexes and remained independently associated in multivariable analyses (adjusted HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.35–2.10; p < 0.01). Associations were directionally stronger in women, who showed higher cumulative event rates across UACR tertiles, although the formal UACR × sex interaction did not reach statistical significance.Conclusions Within the normoalbuminuric range, UACR is associated with greater CAD burden and higher 6-year MACE risk, with sex-specific differences. These findings suggest potential sex-related variation in the prognostic value of high-normal albuminuria, particularly among women, warranting validation in larger and more diverse cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-02996-y.

Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major global health concern and a leading contributor to cardiovascular disease (CVD), which remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in these patients. While traditional risk factors such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and poor glycemic control, are well-established, increasing evidence suggests that urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), traditionally associated with renal impairment, may also serve as an independent predictor of cardiovascular (CV) outcomes in T2DM [1, 2].

Albuminuria is one of the earliest markers of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and has been independently associated with increased risk of coronary artery disease (CAD) and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [2, 3]. KDIGO classifies albuminuria as A1 (< 30 mg/g; normal–mildly increased), A2 (30–300 mg/g; moderately increased; formerly ‘microalbuminuria’), and A3 (>300 mg/g; severely increased) [4]. Although A2–A3 clearly denote high cardiorenal risk, the clinical relevance of variability within A1 (normoalbuminuria) is less certain. Within KDIGO A1, higher UACR has been linked to endothelial dysfunction and higher CV risk in people with T2DM and in the general population, supporting a risk continuum [4–9]. Focusing on the KDIGO A1 range is clinically relevant for three reasons. First, albuminuria tracks cardiovascular risk on a continuum, so small differences within A1 may reflect early vascular injury before overt kidney disease. Second, A1 is highly prevalent in T2DM and sits below treatment thresholds emphasized by guidelines (which prioritize A2–A3), leaving risk stratification in this group uncertain. Third, restricting to A1 (with preserved eGFR) reduces confounding from advanced CKD and allows us to test whether the prognostic value of UACR differs by sex at an earlier stage.

Growing evidence indicates that T2DM confers a disproportionate CV burden in women, arising from both biological factors (hormonal milieu, adiposity distribution, endothelial/microvascular dysfunction, inflammation) and care differences (risk-factor control and use of cardioprotective therapies) [10–14]. Consistent with these mechanisms, recent syntheses show higher relative risks for women with T2DM versus men for coronary heart disease (CHD), acute coronary syndrome (ACS), heart failure, and CV mortality [15], in line with earlier works that estimates 50–60% higher excess CHD risk in women [16]. Contemporary population data also report sex-specific patterns in incident micro- and macrovascular complications across diabetes cohorts [17]. Despite this, the sex-specific prognostic value of UACR within KDIGO A1 for CV disease remains insufficiently defined, as many prior studies typically combined albuminuria categories and rarely report sex-interaction testing [18–20].

Clarifying the prognostic role of low-grade albuminuria in people with with T2DM and normoalbuminuria, and understanding the potential differences between men and women, is crucial for improving CV risk stratification and guiding personalized treatment approaches. Accordingly, in this study we aimed: (1) to evaluate the association between UACR levels within the KDIGO A1 range and the severity of CAD; (2) to determine whether baseline UACR predicts MACE over a 6-year follow-up period; and (3) to examine potential sex differences in these associations among people with T2DM and preserved renal function.

Methods

Population and study design

This retrospective cohort study aimed to evaluate the association between UACR levels and both the presence and severity of CAD, as well as the predictive value of UACR for MACE, defined as non-fatal myocardial infarction or unstable angina requiring urgent revascularization, stroke, or CV death. Between May 2015 and July 2016, a total of 1200 subjects with T2DM who underwent non-emergency diagnostic coronary angiography at University Hospital Nuestra Señora de Candelaria were screened. Inclusion criteria were age > 18 years old, diagnosis of T2DM for more than 2 years, preserved renal function (defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] > 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and UACR < 30 mg/g), no prior insulin treatment, and no history of CV disease. Exclusion criteria included individuals with previous myocardial infarction (MI); coronary revascularization (angioplasty, stent placement, or coronary artery bypass grafting); peripheral artery disease; stroke; major cardiac arrhythmias, defined as atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter (persistent/permanent), sustained ventricular tachycardia/ventricular fibrillation (≥ 30 s or requiring cardioversion/defibrillation), and high-grade atrioventricular block (Mobitz II or third-degree) or paced rhythm; and chronic immunologic or inflammatory diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, or inflammatory bowel disease). Minor or transient rhythm findings (e.g., isolated PACs/PVCs, first-degree AV block, brief self-terminating SVT) were not exclusionary unless requiring chronic antiarrhythmic therapy or a device. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board, and all procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data collection

Clinical variables (demographics, comorbidities, medications, routine labs), angiographic data (per-vessel stenosis and SSI), and outcomes were abstracted from the electronic health record and catheterization-laboratory database. Fasting venous blood samples were collected on the morning of the angiography study, processed within the clinical laboratory, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C. Biochemical parameters were measured using standard laboratory methods. UACR (mg/g) was measured from a single-spot urine sample collected on the morning of the angiography visit, contemporaneous with the fasting blood draw. Urine albumin was quantified by immunoturbidimetry on a Beckman Coulter AU5800 analyzer (limit of detection [LoD] 0.07 mg/dL; measuring range 0.7–45 mg/dL; intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) < 1.4% and < 4.3%, respectively). Urinary albumin values bellow LoD were imputed as LoD/√2 (0.049 mg/dL) for the primary analyses. Serum and urine creatinine were measured by an IDMS-standardized, compensated Jaffé method on a Roche cobas c system. Measuring ranges were 0.17–24.9 mg/dL (serum/plasma) and 4.24–622 mg/dL (urine). eGFR was calculated using the CKD-EPI 2009 creatinine equation (mL/min/1.73 m²). Concentrations of triglycerides (TG), fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were measured using an automatic biochemistry analyzer. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was measured using a particle-enhanced immunoturbidimetric assay with an analytical sensitivity of 0.3 mg/L and intra-/inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) of 1.6% and 8.4%, respectively.

Serum levels of inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα), soluble TNF receptors (sTNFR) 1 and 2, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and soluble interleukin-6 receptor (sIL-6R), were assayed in batch (single freeze-thaw) using commercial ELISA kits (Quantikine®, R&D Systems, Abingdon, UK). Minimum detectable concentrations were TNF-α (high-sensitivity, HSTA00E) 0.049 pg/mL; IL-6 (high-sensitivity, HS600C) 0.09 pg/mL; sTNFR1 (DRT100) 1.2 pg/mL; sTNFR2 (DRT200) 2.3 pg/mL; and sIL-6R (DR600) 15.1 pg/mL. Intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were below 10.8% across concentration levels. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated as an additional marker of systemic inflammation.

Assessment of coronary artery disease

CAD severity was assessed using diagnostic coronary angiography performed using standard projections. Percent diameter stenosis was determined independently by two experienced interventional cardiologists in the four major epicardial arteries: left main coronary artery (LCA), left anterior descending artery (LAD), right coronary artery (RCA), and circumflex artery (CA). Significant CAD was defined as ≥ 50% luminal narrowing in at least one vessel. CAD burden was summarized by the stenosis severity index (SSI), calculated as the mean of the maximum percentage stenosis across the four vessels [21].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic. Group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test or ANOVA for normally distributed variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normal distributions. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

UACR was log-transformed for primary analyses. For descriptive summaries and figures, UACR tertiles were created to yield equal-sized groups within the KDIGO A1 range. Robustness was assessed using restricted cubic splines for log-UACR and alternative thresholds. Correlations between UACR and clinical or laboratory parameters were evaluated using Pearson’s or Spearman’s coefficients. The independent association between UACR and CAD severity (SSI) was analyzed using multivariable linear regression, adjusting for age, smoking status, BMI, hypertension, lipid profile (LDL-C, TC, HDL-C, TG), inflammatory markers, and medication use. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUC) were used to evaluate the ability of the UACR to predict significant CAD.

For the prospective analysis, the primary endpoint was the occurrence of MACE (a composite of non-fatal myocardial infarction or unstable angina requiring urgent revascularization, stroke, or CV death) during a 6-year follow-up. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and log-rank tests were used to compare MACE-free survival across UACR tertiles. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine associations between UACR (log-UACR) and MACE risk; hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated for each 1-unit increase in log-UACR, corresponding approximately to a 2.72-fold increase in UACR. Multivariable models were adjusted for hypertension, smoking status, LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides, hs-CRP, SSI (% coronary stenosis), and ACEI/ARB use, based on clinical relevance and significance (p < 0.10) in univariable analyses; age and sex were retained a priori regardless of p-value. Because all participants had preserved renal function (eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) and exhibited minimal variability in eGFR values across UACR tertiles, this parameter was not included as a primary covariate to avoid collinearity and over-adjustment. A sensitivity analysis including eGFR yielded materially unchanged results, supporting the robustness of the associations observed. No automated forward, backward, or stepwise selection was applied after screening. The proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals (global and covariate-specific tests). Effect modification by sex was tested by including a UACR × sex interaction term in the multivariable Cox model, and its significance was evaluated using the Wald and likelihood-ratio tests. Multicollinearity was assessed in all multivariable models (linear and Cox regression) using variance inflation factors (VIFs).Because this was a retrospective cohort, the sample size was determined by all consecutive eligible participants (n = 420). During follow-up, 112 MACE events were recorded. Model adequacy was verified using the events-per-variable (EPV) criterion; with ≤ 10 covariates in the final Cox model, EPV was ≥ 11, satisfying standard thresholds for model stability. Using Schoenfeld’s approximation, 112 events provide around 80% power (two-sided α = 0.05) to detect a hazard ratio of approximately 1.70 for a binary exposure with around 50% prevalence (1.84 for 90% power). These calculations support the adequacy of the available sample and event count for the planned analyses.

All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1200 participants were initially screened for eligibility. Of them, 52 declined to participate, 240 did not meet the inclusion criteria, 160 met one or more exclusion criteria, 51 had incomplete clinical or laboratory data, and 277 were lost to follow-up (Fig. 1). Therefore, 420 subjects (mean age of 65.3 ± 10.7 years) were included in the final analysis.

After angiographic evaluation, 310 participants (57.7% male) were diagnosed with significant CAD. Compared to those without CAD, this group of patients had a higher prevalence of smoking (23.9% vs. 19.1%, p = 0.03), higher TG levels (161.8 ± 78.6 mg/dL vs. 138.7 ± 48.2 mg/dL, p = 0.018), elevated HbA1c (6.8 [6.2–7.4] % vs. 6.4 [5.9-7] %, p = 0.03), increased hs-CRP (4 [2.15–6.9] mg/L vs. 3 [1.3-6] mg/L, p = 0.032), and higher IL-6 levels (7.2 [3.4–13.9] pg/mL vs. 5.43 [2.61–8.9] pg/mL, p = 0.043). UACR was also significantly higher among those participants with significant CAD (8.35 [4.39-17] mg/g vs. 5.33 [4.54–6.7] mg/g, p = 0.018) (Fig. 2A).

UACR tertiles and CAD severity

Participants were stratified into three equal tertiles (n = 140) based on UACR levels: T1 (< 4.94 mg/g), T2 (4.94–12.9 mg/g), and T3 (≥ 13 mg/g). As shown in Table 2, the proportion of male was similar across tertiles (approximately 57% in each). Higher UACR tertiles were significantly associated with an increased prevalence of significant CAD (T1: 63.6%, T2: 75.7%; T3: 82.1%; p < 0.01) and smoking (p = 0.02). Higher UACR tertiles were associated with higher concentrations of sTNFR1 (p < 0.05), TNF-α (p < 0.01), and IL-6 (p < 0.01) compared with T1. These trends persisted after sex stratification (Supplementary Table 1). Thus, the proportion with significant CAD (≥ 50% stenosis) increased stepwise across UACR tertiles in both women and men (T1: 48.3%, T2: 58.3%; T3: 75%, for women; T1: 68.8%, T2: 86.3%; T3: 96.3%, for men; p < 0.001 for both). Although absolute CAD prevalence was higher in men, the relative increase across tertiles was comparable between sexes, suggesting that elevated UACR reflects a shared vascular injury pathway. The higher absolute burden in men likely reflects differences in traditional risk exposure (e.g., smoking, dyslipidemia, hypertension).

In the full cohort, Log-UACR showed no significant correlations with inflammatory parameters. However, in women, Log-UACR directly correlated with IL-6 (r = 0.267, p < 0.05) and with circulating levels of sTNFR1 and sTNFR2 (r = 0.251 and 0.297, respectively; p < 0.05 for both). Additionally, NLR was positively correlated with Log-UACR both in men (r = 0.207, p = 0.015) and in women (r = 0.257, p = 0.042). Although tertile analyses revealed significantly higher TNFα and IL-6 levels in the upper UACR tertiles (Table 2), linear correlations between log-UACR and inflammatory markers were not significant in the overall population (Table 4). This reflects a non-linear, threshold-like relationship—confirmed by spline diagnostics—and the presence of sex-specific correlations (notably in women) that become attenuated when both sexes are analyzed together.

In multivariable regression, UACR remained independently associated with SSI after adjusting for potential confounders (Table 5). Thus, in a backward stepwise multiple regression analysis, using SSI as the dependent variable, UACR, hs-CRP, TG, and HDL were significant predictors of CAD severity (adjusted R² = 0.090, p < 0.001). Although the multivariable model explained a modest proportion of variance in CAD severity (adjusted R² = 0.09), this is consistent with the multifactorial nature of atherosclerosis. Variables were selected based on both clinical relevance and statistical criteria to minimize overfitting, and diagnostic checks confirmed model adequacy. In sex-stratified multivariable linear models, higher log-UACR was independently associated with greater SSI in women (B = 25.9; 95% CI: 9.8–42.0; p = 0.003) and in men (B = 12.9; 95% CI: 2.9–22.9; p = 0.002), after adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors and medications. Among women, UACR and IL-6 were the primary predictors (R² = 0.179, p < 0.01). In men, UACR, TG, HDL-C, and use of oral antidiabetics were independent contributors (R² = 0.123, p < 0.001). No evidence of multicollinearity was observed among covariates in either model (all VIF < 5). Finally, an exploratory ROC analysis (Supplementary Figure S1) identified an optimal UACR cut-off of 6.72 mg/g for detecting significant CAD (AUC = 0.609, p = 0.037), with a sensitivity of 64.7% and a specificity of 48.4%. These parameters indicate modest discrimination, supporting the use of UACR as a continuous risk marker rather than a diagnostic threshold.

UACR and cardiovascular events

Over follow-up, MACE occurred more frequently with higher UACR: 26 participants (18.6%) in T1, 45 (32.1%) in T2, and 41 (55.7%) in T3. Among men, events occurred in 16 (20.0%), 24 (30.0%), and 41 (51.3%) across tertiles; among women, in 10 (16.7%), 21 (35.0%), and 37 (61.7%), respectively. Kaplan–Meier survival curves revealed a significant overall difference in event-free survival across UACR tertiles (log-rank p = 0.0039) (Fig. 3A). This trend was apparent in both sexes but more pronounced in women, among whom the cumulative MACE incidence in T3 reached 50% by the second year of follow-up (Fig. 3B).

Univariate Cox regression identified UACR as a predictor for MACE (hazard ratio [HR], 1.87; 95% CI: 1.69–2.91; p < 0.01), together with male sex, hypertension, smoking, SSI percent values, levels of TNFα, sTNFR1, and sTNFR2, and use of ACEI/ARB (Table 6). In the multivariable model, UACR remained an independent predictor (adjusted HR 1.67; 95% CI: 1.35–2.10; p < 0.01), together with hypertension (adjusted HR 1.82; 95% CI: 1.31–2.05), SSI (adjusted HR 1.91; 95% CI: 1.19–2.34), and ACEI/ARB use (adjusted HR 1.76; 95% CI: 1.34–2.87). Sex-stratified Cox analysis further confirmed the independent prognostic value of UACR in both sexes, though effect sizes differed. In women, UACR has a stronger predictive value for MACE (adjusted HR 1.8; 95% CI: 1.40–2.32; p < 0.01), along with hypertension and SSI (Table 7). In men, UACR also remained independently associated (adjusted HR 1.45; 95% CI: 1.10–1.92; p = 0.03), together with hypertension, SSI, and ACEI/ARB use. To formally assess effect modification, an interaction term between UACR and sex was included in the multivariable Cox model. The interaction was not statistically significant (Wald p = 0.18; likelihood-ratio p = 0.16), indicating no evidence of multiplicative effect modification. However, the direction and magnitude of hazard ratios suggested a stronger association between UACR and MACE risk among women. No multicollinearity was detected among covariates (all VIF < 5).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that among individuals with T2DM and preserved renal function, UACR levels within the normoalbuminuric range are independently associated with the presence and severity of CAD, as well as with a higher risk of MACE. These findings emphasize the clinical relevance of “high-normal” albuminuria as a prognostic marker, even in people with T2DM without overt kidney disease or established CV disease. Notably, the association between UACR and MACE was particularly robust in women, highlighting a potential sex-specific vulnerability to CV risk.

Our findings align with a growing body of evidence indicating that UACR values below the conventional threshold for microalbuminuria (< 30 mg/g) relates to increased CV morbidity and mortality [2, 4–8, 22–27]. In T2DM cohorts, low-grade albuminuria predicted incident CV events [28]. Likewise, a Danish nationwide T2DM cohort without baseline CVD found that higher UACR—well within KDIGO A1—was associated with greater risks of MACE and all-cause mortality [29]. Beyond events, UACR below 30 mg/g was also related to subclinical atherosclerosis and carotid intima-media thickness in T2DM and prospectively predicted CVD and all-cause death [30, 31]. In our cohort of asymptomatic participants with no prior CVD, those in the higher UACR tertiles exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of angiographically confirmed CAD, greater stenosis severity (as assessed by SSI), and a higher number of involved coronary branches, indicating that even within KDIGO A1, albuminuria marks greater coronary atherosclerotic burden in T2DM.

Although our exploratory ROC analysis (AUC = 0.61) suggested a threshold around 6.7 mg/g, its performance was modest and unvalidated; accordingly, we emphasize continuous UACR values rather than recommending any clinical cut-off. Moreover, the low specificity of our analysis (below 50%) implies a risk of overmedicalization if used in isolation; therefore, its greatest utility likely lies in combination with established cardiovascular risk factors to refine risk stratification rather than to guide treatment decisions directly.

Our findings complement current guidelines by highlighting that even within the A1 (< 30 mg/g) category, higher UACR values may carry incremental cardiovascular risk. Albuminuria is increasingly recognized as an indicator of systemic endothelial dysfunction and microvascular endothelial injury may underlie the heightened cardiovascular risk associated with even minimal increases in albumin excretion [32]. Moreover, it has been proposed that albuminuria may actively contribute to the pathogenesis of CV damage via proinflammatory and profibrotic pathways. Elevated circulating cytokines such as IL-6 and TNFα are known mediators of atherosclerosis and plaque instability [33, 34]. Previous studies demonstrate a strong correlation between albuminuria and systemic inflammation, supporting the role of albuminuria as a biological amplifier of systemic inflammation, thereby contributing to vascular remodeling and atherosclerosis [35–37]. In our study, participants in higher UACR tertiles exhibited significantly elevated levels of pro-inflammatory TNFα, sTNFR1 and IL-6. However, this cross-sectional co-occurrence does not establish causality or mediation; we interpret it as hypothesis-generating regarding a possible link between albuminuria and vascular inflammation. Importantly, in multivariable analysis adjusting for conventional cardiovascular risk factors, UACR remained independently associated with CAD severity. Inflammatory biomarkers, particularly hs-CRP and IL-6 (the latter exclusively in women), also contributed to SSI, suggesting an additive effect of subclinical inflammation. These results strengthen the pathophysiological link between albuminuria, inflammation, and coronary atherosclerosis. Importantly, given the observational design of our study, we cannot exclude a bidirectional relationship whereby systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction both elevate UACR and, in turn, are amplified by albuminuria-related vascular injury. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Our findings also revealed that UACR predicted MACE risk during follow-up. Participants in the highest UACR tertile experienced significantly more CV events, and this association persisted after adjustment for inflammatory markers, lipid parameters, and antihypertensive therapy. These results corroborate prior studies demonstrating the independent prognostic value of UACR for CV events [19–39]. Moreover, the integration of UACR into conventional risk models has been shown to improve prediction of adverse CV outcomes [38].

One of the most novel findings of our study is the potentially sex-specific nature of the prognostic value of UACR within the KDIGO A1 range. Although UACR independently predicted MACE in both sexes, the effect was substantially more pronounced in women. In our cohort, women in the highest UACR tertile experienced a 50% MACE incidence by year two of follow-up, underscoring a potentially greater sensitivity to vascular injury in women with high-normal albuminuria. Prior studies have shown that women, despite a lower overall CVD burden than men, may experience worse outcomes in early CKD stages [40]. Furthermore, both albuminuria and declining eGFR appear to confer a disproportionately higher mortality risk in women [41]. However, other studies have failed to demonstrate significant sex interactions within the A1 range, possibly reflecting limited sample sizes, heterogeneity in albuminuria definitions (A1 vs. A2/A3), or differences in baseline therapy and follow-up duration [19, 42, 43]. Our findings extend current evidence by demonstrating that even modest elevations in UACR within A1 predict future cardiovascular events more strongly in women. In our study, the formal UACR × sex interaction term did not reach statistical significance, indicating that while the difference in hazard ratios was not statistically confirmed as effect modification, the pattern aligns with these prior observations. This disparity may be multifactorial, reflecting sex-based differences in endothelial function, microvascular inflammation, and systemic vascular responsiveness, together with gender-related differences in clinical management [44, 45]. Beyond hormonal milieu, several complementary mechanisms may underlie the stronger prognostic association of UACR in women. Estrogen deficiency and altered sex hormone–vascular interactions in women with T2DM can exacerbate endothelial permeability, nitric oxide imbalance, and inflammatory activation—mechanisms that collectively heighten vascular vulnerability [46, 47]. Moreover, UACR correlates with circulating proinflammatory cytokines that contribute to vascular remodeling and plaque instability [33–37]. In our cohort, these correlations were more apparent in women, supporting a sex-linked inflammatory amplification. Finally, sex differences in RAAS activity, adiposity distribution, and autonomic regulation have been described in T2DM [11–14], potentially modulating renal albumin excretion and systemic vascular stress. Together, these mechanisms provide a biologically plausible context for the stronger predictive value of UACR observed in women. Further studies are warranted to validate these observations and clarify the biological and care-related mechanisms underlying this sex divergence.

Given that albuminuria often precedes eGFR decline, it may offer an earlier signal of systemic microvascular injury. In meta-analyses, UACR has emerged as one of the strongest predictors of renal and CV outcomes in individuals with T2DM, even when values are within the normal range [2, 48–51]. To date, there is limited evidence regarding the efficacy of treatments including sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), and non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) like finerenone in patients with UACR < 30 mg/g [52–60]. While albuminuria reduction is most consistently reported with SGLT2 inhibitors and finerenone, GLP-1RAs have demonstrated substantial reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events; whether benefits vary across the KDIGO A1 range warrants further study.

In T2DM, routine assessment of UACR is standard; our results indicate that even below the microalbuminuria threshold, higher UACR within A1 is associated with greater CAD burden and 6-year MACE risk. Thus, considering the continuous UACR value, not only whether it crosses 30 mg/g, may enhance sex-aware cardiovascular risk discussions and help prioritize the intensity of guideline-directed preventive care within current indications, a hypothesis that warrants external validation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, despite adjustment for traditional risk factors, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Second, our findings derive from a selected cohort composed of adults with T2DM with no prior CVD and no baseline insulin use, undergoing elective coronary angiography at a tertiary hospital, restricted to KDIGO A1 albuminuria and preserved eGFR. While this design reduces confounding by advanced disease, it introduces spectrum bias and may not reflect broader real-world populations; accordingly, effect sizes and risk estimates should be interpreted for similar clinical settings, and external validation in diverse cohorts is needed. Additionally, the sex distribution was unequal, with fewer women than men and some baseline differences between sexes; however, sex-stratified analyses were conducted to minimize this imbalance and explore sex-specific associations. Third, loss to follow-up affected a high proportion of otherwise eligible participants, mainly due to migration/ongoing care outside our system or lack of registry linkage; although CV deaths were captured via the regional registry and baseline profiles were broadly similar in those with and without follow-up, this attrition may introduce selection bias and limits generalizability. Fourth, laboratory values were assessed only at baseline, and temporal fluctuations in UACR or inflammatory markers were not captured. Persistent albuminuria and within-person changes over time could not be evaluated. The resulting random variability likely attenuates associations, suggesting our estimates may be conservative. Studies with repeated/time-updated UACR are warranted to examine trajectories and outcomes. Finally, because higher UACR was associated with greater CAD severity at baseline, partial confounding between existing coronary atherosclerosis and subsequent MACE risk cannot be excluded. Although SSI was included as a covariate in multivariable models, our design does not allow determination of whether UACR acts as an independent predictor or a surrogate for subclinical disease progression.

Additionally, variables such as dietary intake, physical activity, and medication adherence—which can influence UACR—were not recorded. Because our cohort was assembled before the widespread uptake in Spain of GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors, time-updated exposure to these agents was minimal and not captured. Finally, we used angiography to assess CAD severity; more granular imaging modalities (e.g., intravascular ultrasound or CT angiography) and lesion-level data to derive Gensini/SYNTAX were not uniformly available. To limit selection bias, we used full-cohort alternative metrics (significant CAD, vessel count), which yielded concordant results. Moreover, because we excluded participants with major arrhythmias to minimize confounding of MACE, our findings are most applicable to people with T2DM in sinus rhythm and may not generalize to those with atrial fibrillation/flutter or significant conduction disease.

Conclusions

Our study underscores the clinical importance of UACR levels within the normoalbuminuric range as independent predictors of CAD severity and future cardiovascular events in individuals with T2DM and preserved renal function. These associations appear to be sex-specific, with particularly strong prognostic significance in women. Recognizing high-normal albuminuria as a CV risk marker may support more personalized, earlier interventions—particularly as new therapeutic strategies evolve to reduce both albuminuria and CV burden. Prospective studies are warranted to determine whether early pharmacologic intervention targeting high-normal albuminuria improves CV outcomes and whether sex-specific risk models can enhance precision in diabetes care.
