Redox partner adrenodoxin induces substrate binding to steroidogenic cytochrome P450 11B2 and 11A1 by promoting a conformational change
Authors: Cara L Loomis, Michelle Redhair, Sang-Choul Im, Emily E Scott
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn humans, cytochrome P450 (CYP) 11A1 and 11B2 play crucial roles in the steroidogenic pathway. CYP11A1 is involved in the first step in the steroidogenic pathway, converting cholesterol to pregnenolone, whereas CYP11B2 completes the final step in aldosterone synthesis. Each is a drug target for a distinct disease, and detailed information about enzyme kinetics and function could inform drug development. Both enzymes require the common redox partner adrenodoxin. Adrenodoxin allosterically modulates CYP11B2 function separate from its role in electron transfer. Herein, stopped-flow investigations establish that adrenodoxin is also an allosteric modulator of CYP11A1 substrate binding. For both enzymes, a kinetic modeling approach was used to elucidate the substrate binding mechanisms in the absence and presence of adrenodoxin. This analysis determined that substrate binding to both enzymes is best described by complex, 4-state mechanisms. Substrate 11-deoxycorticosterone binds CYP11B2 primarily through a branched induced fit mechanism, and the presence of adrenodoxin shifts the mechanism to a 4-state closed model containing both induced fit and conformational selection steps. CYP11A1 primarily binds its substrate 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol through a 4-state closed mechanism, while increasing adrenodoxin results in enhancement of the conformational selection step. Overall, this demonstrates the complexity of both P450 substrate binding and its fine-tuning by interactions with redox partners.Keywords: adrenodoxin, cytochrome P450, CYP11A1, CYP11B2, allostery, stopped-flow, kinetic modeling

Six cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes are involved in human steroid synthesis, producing both sex hormones and corticosteroids with a wide array of biological functions (1). Three of these P450 enzymes are in the CYP11 family: CYP11A1, CYP11B1, and CYP11B2. While CYP11B1 performs a simple hydroxylation to generate the mineralocorticoid corticosterone, the other two perform complex, 3-step reactions. CYP11A1 performs the first step in steroidogenesis, the conversion of cholesterol to pregnenolone via side chain cleavage. To accomplish this cleavage, CYP11A1 performs two sequential hydroxylation steps on cholesterol to produce 22(R)-hydroxycholesterol, then 20R,22(R)-dihydroxycholesterol before cleaving the aliphatic tail to produce pregnenolone (Fig. 1) (1). The importance of CYP11A1 is that this pregnenolone is the common precursor to all steroids. CYP11B2 acts later in the steroidogenic pathway to produce the major mineralocorticoid aldosterone. CYP11B2 performs two subsequent hydroxylation steps on 11-deoxycorticosterone, first producing corticosterone, then 18-hydroxycorticosterone, followed by a final 18-oxidase step (2). The resulting aldosterone product is responsible for salt and water homeostasis.

Enzymatic reactions of CYP11A1 and CYP11B2. CYP11A1 converts cholesterol to pregnenolone in three steps, including two hydroxylation steps on the C-20 and C-22 positions, followed by a lyase step to remove the aliphatic tail from cholesterol. CYP11B2 converts 11-deoxycorticosterone to the major human mineralocorticoid aldosterone in three enzymatic steps. This includes two hydroxylation steps on the C-11 and C-18 positions, followed by an oxidation on C-18 to ultimately produce aldosterone. CYP, cytochrome P450.

Both CYP11A1 and CYP11B2 are unrealized drug targets. Substantial efforts have been invested in designing CYP11B2 inhibitors as a treatment for primary aldosteronism. In this disease, aldosterone overproduction leads to hypertension. However, the design and discovery of selective CYP11B2 inhibitors have been impeded by its 93% sequence identity with CYP11B1. Poorly selective inhibitors can lead to CYP11B1 inhibition and undesirable decreases in its product. This difficulty is illustrated by the development of CYP11B2 inhibitors recently repurposed for inhibition of CYP11B1 in the treatment of Cushing’s disease (3, 4, 5). CYP11A1 has only recently been explored as a drug target since its inhibition would impact the production of all steroid hormones. However, CYP11A1 inhibitors are currently being evaluated in clinical trials for castration resistant prostate cancer (6, 7) with supplementation of critical downstream steroids as needed. For both CYP11A1 and CYP11B2, a better understanding of their interactions with small molecules and catalysis has the potential to facilitate more effective drug development.

Cytochrome P450 substrate interactions are often complex and poorly understood. Many times, substrate binding is thought of as a simple two-state model where the enzyme is either ligand-bound or ligand-free. However, for many human P450 enzymes this model is much too simplistic, with evidence suggesting that multiple intermediate steps in the P450 catalytic cycle likely alter substrate binding (8). This complexity is further amplified for P450 enzymes with multiple substrates or multiple sequential oxidations since the binding mode must vary with the substrate or substrate orientation. For example, while CYP11B1 substrate binding is monophasic, suggesting a simple two-state binding mechanism, CYP11B2 substrate binding is multiphasic (9, 10), indicating a more complex relationship for this binding mechanism.

In addition to understanding basic enzyme-ligand interactions, it is also important to understand how other components required for P450 catalysis may impact ligand binding. Cytochrome P450 enzymes require direct binding of a redox partner protein to receive the electrons required to drive catalysis. Mitochondrial P450s, including the CYP11 enzymes, employ a two-component redox system. First, NADPH transfers two electrons to the FAD-containing adrenodoxin reductase. This membrane-bound protein then transfers one electron to the soluble 2Fe-2S protein adrenodoxin (11). Finally, adrenodoxin directly binds the proximal surface of a mitochondrial P450 enzyme to transfer one electron to its heme iron. As two electrons are required for a single catalytic turnover, the entire process must occur twice. It has previously been established that adrenodoxin not only transfers electrons but can also be an allosteric modulator of ligand binding in the P450 active site on the opposite side of the heme. For example, in the presence of adrenodoxin, CYP11B1 and CYP11B2 are more saturated with substrates and inhibitors and have a higher affinity for those ligands. Information on the effect of adrenodoxin on CYP11A1 is more limited (12).

Determination of the ligand binding mechanism for a particular enzyme involves comprehensive collection of presteady-state binding observations and detailed data analysis. Measuring the rate of ligand binding at multiple ligand concentrations typically requires the use of stopped-flow due to fast timescales. Analysis of such data has traditionally involved (1) fitting each dataset to exponential equations to determine kobs values and (2) analyzing the behavior of kobs versus the ligand concentration to determine values of kon and koff rate constants, and (3) interpreting the relationships of these values. However, more modern kinetic modeling approaches have the advantage of globally fitting all experimental binding observations to mechanism-specific equations. Each binding mechanism can be described by a set of differential equations that represent how each enzymatic state changes with respect to time. A complete set of stopped-flow observations can be simultaneously fit to the various binding mechanisms (e.g., Fig. 2) to determine which best represents the data. This strategy allows for a more informed investigation of complex enzymatic binding mechanisms.

Schematics of 3- and 4-state binding mechanisms. The yellow, green, and blue icons represent various conformational states of the enzyme (E, E∗, and E∗∗). The pink sphere represents a ligand. Three-state mechanisms include conformational selection and induced fit, whereas 4-state mechanisms include various linear and branched models. Linear mechanisms are those with two conformational selection steps and two induced fit steps, as well as a mixed model containing a conformational selection and induced fit step. Branched mechanisms include those with two branched induced fit or two branched conformational selection steps, and a closed (box) mechanism with a conformational selection and induced fit step that converge at one stage.

Herein, we use this modern modeling approach to understand both how substrates bind to the kinetically complex steroidogenic CYP11B2 and CYP11A1 enzymes and how their common redox partner adrenodoxin modulates this substrate binding. The outcomes suggest that conformational changes are key in both interactions of steroidogenic P450 enzymes.

Results

Steady-state CYP11A1 substrate binding is allosterically modulated by adrenodoxin

Although adrenodoxin has been established as an allosteric modulator of CYP11B1 and CYP11B2 substrate binding (13, 14), the corresponding information for CYP11A1 has not been fully elucidated. An artificial construct for adrenodoxin fused to CYP11A1 established that the adrenodoxin 2Fe-2S surface interacts with the proximal CYP11A1 surface as anticipated, but did not demonstrate a difference in the equilibrium Kd value compared to CYP11A1 alone (12).

Herein, the effects of free adrenodoxin were evaluated on CYP11A1 binding of all three of its substrates: cholesterol, 22R-hydroxycholesterol, and 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol. Each substrate alone causes a shift of the CYP11A1 heme Soret peak to lower wavelengths—a type I shift—as expected and previously reported (12), but the presence of adrenodoxin clearly facilitated this shift. For all three CYP11A1 substrates, the presence of 40-fold molar excess adrenodoxin markedly increased the magnitude of the spectral shift. Adrenodoxin caused the most pronounced effects in cholesterol binding, resulting in a 3-fold change in CYP11A1 saturation (ΔAmax increased from 0.011 to 0.035, Fig. 3A). The increases for the other two substrates were smaller but still significant. For 22R-hydroxycholesterol, the ΔAmax increased from 0.026 to 0.034 (Fig. 3B), and for 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol, the ΔAmax increased from 0.065 to 0.085 (Fig. 3C). In addition, adrenodoxin increased CYP11A1 affinity for two of the three CYP11A1 substrates (Fig. 3D). For 22R-hydroxycholesterol, adrenodoxin decreased the Kd 2-fold (from 163 to 78 nM), while for 20R, 22R-dihydroxycholesterol the Kd decreased 4.5-fold (from 1730 to 383 nM). Interestingly, adrenodoxin caused a moderate decrease in affinity for cholesterol (Kd increasing from 0.81 to 1.38 μM). The majority of these results suggest that adrenodoxin increases substrate binding/saturation for all three substrates and affinity for two of them. These results are similar to the previously reported effects of adrenodoxin on CYP11B1 and CYP11B2 binding of their substrates (13, 14).

CYP11A1 binding rates for substrate 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol

To evaluate whether the observed changes in the equilibrium dissociation constant Kd are due to increases in substrate binding rates or decreases in substrate release or both (Kd = koff/kon), stopped-flow was used to determine the individual koff and kon rate constants in the presence and absence of adrenodoxin. Attempts were made to do this for all three CYP11A1 substrates, but solubility issues and/or high affinities meant that only 20R, 22R-dihydroxycholesterol was feasible. Either 0.5, 1.0, 3, or 6 μM 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol was rapidly mixed 1:1 with 2 μM CYP11A1 in the absence or presence of 1, 10, or 40-fold excess adrenodoxin (Fig. 4). The changes in absorbance from 391 to 419 nm corresponding to the spin state change associated with substrate binding were calculated and plotted against time. Even in the absence of adrenodoxin, CYP11A1 substrate binding at all concentrations clearly had at least two components (Fig. 4A, black trace). It did not fit well to a single-phase association equation, but did fit well to a two-phase association equation (R2: 0.75–0.86 for single exponential equation and 0.97–0.99 for biexponential equation) where the two rate constants were designated kfast and kslow. The percent fast phase generally increased with increasing 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol concentration, and the percent slow phase generally decreased with increasing substrate concentrations. As expected, increasing 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol concentrations progressively drove the system to higher saturation levels, as evidenced by increases in ΔAmax (Fig. 4A). The fast observed rate (kfast) generally increased as a function of substrate concentration (Fig. 4C, black line), while the slow observed rate generally decreased as a function of substrate concentration (Fig. 4D, black line).

Next, the effects of increasing concentrations of adrenodoxin were evaluated by comparing the data described above without adrenodoxin to those collected with three increasing adrenodoxin ratios (Fig. 4B). Increases in the adrenodoxin ratio resulted in progressive increases in ΔAmax, consistent with more substrate binding. In the presence of adrenodoxin, 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol binding was still biphasic. The fast observed rate (kfast) generally increased as a function of substrate concentration (Fig. 4C), but these increases were nonlinear, indicating a complex binding mode. The slow observed rates (kslow) generally decreased as a function of substrate concentration (Fig. 4D).

Overall, three features suggest complexity in CYP11A1 substrate binding. The first is the multiphasic nature of substrate binding. Second, both in the absence and presence of adrenodoxin, the changes in kfast observed rates are nonlinear (Fig. 4C) with increasing ligand concentrations. Third, in both the absence and presence of adrenodoxin, decreasing kslow observed rates were observed with increasing ligand concentrations. This is typically indicative of either a conformational selection binding mode or a more complex binding mode. Altogether, this suggests that kinetic modeling is more appropriate to probe the binding mechanism of CYP11A1.

Kinetic modeling of binding mechanisms

Parallel experiments to the above were previously reported with CYP11B2 (Fig. S1) and revealed similar kinetic features (9), so the stopped-flow data for both enzymes were analyzed by kinetic modeling. Beyond the simplest two-state binding mechanism (E to ES), there are numerous possibilities of complex three- and four-state mechanisms (Fig. 2). The most well-known of the three-state binding mechanisms include conformational selection and induced fit. In both, there is an intermediate state between the free and ligand-bound enzyme. In the case of conformational selection, the enzyme itself cycles between two unbound conformational states, only one of which is capable of binding substrate and yields one bound state. Conversely, in induced fit binding, the ligand induces a conformational change in the enzyme, leading to an additional bound state. For some enzymes, ligand binding is best described by even more complex 4-state mechanisms where there are two intermediate states between ligand-bound and unliganded enzyme. These models fall into either the linear or branched categories (Fig. 2). Linear 4-state mechanisms include those with either two conformational changes before ligand binding or two ligand-induced conformational change steps after ligand binding (linear conformational selection or linear induced fit models) as well as a mixture of one conformational change occurring before and after ligand binding (mixed conformational selection/induced fit binding). Branched binding mechanisms contain multiple, nonlinear pathways leading to 1 to 3 bound states, and include branched induced fit, conformational selection, and a closed (box) mechanism (Fig. 2).

Each set of CYP11A1 and CYP11B2 stopped-flow data without adrenodoxin was fit to each of the binding models in Figure 2 to determine the most accurate representation of the substrate binding mechanism. This was then repeated at each adrenodoxin concentration to determine how adrenodoxin influences the binding mechanism: equimolar, 10-fold, and 40-fold excess adrenodoxin relative to the P450. Each of these eight binding mechanisms was represented by the appropriate set of differential equations describing how the concentration of each enzymatic state and the free ligand change with time. Initial protein concentrations in each state were estimated by approximating the distribution in each enzymatic state before the experiment (in the syringe), then dividing by the appropriate dilution factor (two for a binding experiment and twelve for a dilution experiment). Randomized initial values and a minimization function in MATLAB determined the best solution to each set of differential equations, generating a corresponding set of rate constants and extinction coefficients. How well the data fit each binding mechanism was determined by examining the distribution of the residuals and by using several statistical measures, including the largest log likelihood (LL) values, and the smallest values for the sum of residuals squared (RSS) and Akaike or Bayesian information criterion (AIC or BIC).

Initially, determination of the binding mechanism for both CYP11A1 and CYP11B2 was impeded by ligand depletion issues. Both enzymes have low-micromolar Kd values for their respective substrates, and additionally, the substrates have limited solubility due to the steroid backbone. Because of these experimental limitations, pseudo-first-order conditions were broken, and the enzyme concentration was less than 10-fold excess of the substrate concentration. Thus, the equations were adjusted to account for ligand depletion and better estimate initial conditions (see Experimental procedures section for detailed information and Supporting Information for differential equations).

Determination of CYP11A1 binding mechanism

The CYP11A1 binding mechanism for substrate 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol in the presence of increasing adrenodoxin was investigated by kinetic modeling. Stopped-flow data at the four different ligand concentrations (Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure 7, Figure 8) were fit to the eight binding mechanisms in Figure 2. Analysis of the fits and rate constants for these eight mechanisms (Tables S1-S4) revealed that the 4-state closed binding mechanism was the only model that adequately described all ligand concentrations, regardless of the amount of adrenodoxin present. The 4-state closed model was unmatched in terms of the RSS, AIC, and BIC values compared to the other models (Tables S1-S4).

Comparison of rate constants across the adrenodoxin concentrations (Table 1) is revealing. Figure 9 graphically illustrates these changes in rate constants with increasing adrenodoxin concentrations. The two kon rate constants were little changed by the addition of adrenodoxin. For k1, which represents ligand binding to state E, rate constants varied between 14.8 and 24.2 μM−1 min−1. For k4, which represents the ligand binding to state E∗, rate constants varied between 961 and 1490 μM−1 min−1. However, increasing amounts of adrenodoxin did have an effect on the rate constants k3 and k-3,which represent the forward and reverse rearrangements between E and E∗. When no adrenodoxin was present, k3 was 0.441 min−1, but this progressively increased to 1.52, 3.58, and 10.1 min−1 with the addition of equimolar, 10-, and 40-fold excess adrenodoxin, respectively. The reverse k-3 rate also increased overall, from 49.1 min−1 with no adrenodoxin to 119, 101, and 146 min−1 with the corresponding adrenodoxin concentrations. The overall 3-fold increase in k-3 and the overall 23-fold increase in k3 means that the overall Keq for this rearrangement was decreased by 87% in the presence of 40-fold adrenodoxin. This demonstrates that adrenodoxin increasingly enhances conformational change of CYP11A1.

Determination of CYP11B2 binding mechanism

For CYP11B2, initial determinations of the binding mechanism were impeded by model ambiguity, as all eight binding models tested (Fig. 2) initially fit the data equally well. To resolve this, the ligand concentration range was expanded. Stopped-flow ligand binding experiments with higher concentrations of 11-deoxycorticosterone (50 and 100 μM) were conducted to increase the upper bounds of the ligand concentration, and a 12-fold dilution experiment was conducted to produce kinetic data at lower substrate concentrations. This expanded data set (Figure 10, Figure 11, Figure 12, Figure 13) was again fit to all eight binding mechanisms. It is clear upon visual inspection (Figure 10, Figure 11, Figure 12, Figure 13) and review of the measures of fit quality (Tables S5-S8) that both induced fit and conformational selection 3-state models poorly describe the data in the absence and presence of different amounts of adrenodoxin, while more complex 4-state models substantially improved fits (Figure 10, Figure 11, Figure 12, Figure 13 and Tables S5-S8).

Specifically, for the data collected with either no adrenodoxin or equimolar ratios of adrenodoxin, comparisons reveal that the 4-state linear and 4-state branched induced fit models (Figs. 10 and 11) resulted in the best overall fits with the lowest RSS, AIC, and BIC values, and highest LL values (Tables S5 and S6). The branched induced fit model has slightly lower RSS values and was thus the best descriptor of the data, but it is difficult to definitively distinguish between these two mechanisms because of their similarities in statistical measures.

Differences in the rate constants for substrate binding without adrenodoxin (Table S5) and in the presence of equimolar adrenodoxin (Table S6) reveal the influence of even small amounts of adrenodoxin on substrate binding. Changes in the binding mechanism and rate constants change in response to increasing adrenodoxin concentrations (Table 2) are graphically illustrated in Figure 14. For the branched induced fit model, adding equimolar adrenodoxin caused slight increases to the kon (k1 in this mechanism) from 37.4 μM−1 min−1 to 54.5 μM−1 min−1, while slightly increasing the rearrangement rate to EL∗ (k2) from 80.7 to 109 min−1, and decreasing the rate of rearrangement to EL∗∗ (k3) from 0.378 to 0.0832 min−1. Interestingly, increasing the amount of adrenodoxin further caused clear changes to the binding mechanism. For the ligand binding experiments containing 10-fold (Table S7) and 40-fold (Table S8) adrenodoxin relative to CYP11B2, the best binding mechanism was clearly the 4-state closed model (Figs. 12 and 13). None of the other tested models were comparable in terms of RSS, LL, AIC, or BIC values (Tables S7 and S8). Since the amount of adrenodoxin was the sole change between these experiments, adrenodoxin must shift the CYP11B2 binding mechanism from branched induced fit to the 4-state closed model. While the branched induced fit model only has induced fit steps, the 4-state closed model has both induced fit and conformational selection steps (Fig. 2), demonstrating that adrenodoxin causes a conformational change of CYP11B2. Increasing the adrenodoxin from 10 to 40-fold excess relative to CYP11B2 caused several changes to the determined rate constants (Table 2 and Fig. 14). Increasing adrenodoxin increased both rearrangement rates in the forward direction. Rearrangement from EL to EL∗ (k2) increased from 1.62 to 4.68 min−1, while the rearrangement from E to E∗ (k3) increased from 1.65 to 6.19 min−1. Ligand binding rates in the forward direction (k1 and k4) slightly decreased from 53.5 to 17.8 μM−1 min−1 and 1300 to 981 μM−1 min−1, respectively. Reverse rearrangement rates (k-1, k-2, k-3, and k-4) mainly stayed constant despite changes in adrenodoxin, except k-4, which increased from 24.8 to 62.6 min−1.

Discussion

Adrenodoxin allosterically modulates CYP11A1

Although little functional data had been reported previously, the current data definitively define adrenodoxin as an allosteric modulator of CYP11A1. Increasing adrenodoxin affected both the Kd and the ΔAmax for steady-state substrate binding. Although adrenodoxin increased the total amount of spin shift (ΔAmax) associated with ligand binding for all three substrates, the directionality and magnitude of these adrenodoxin effects on Kd varied based on the structure of the three sequential CYP11A1 substrates. For cholesterol, the presence of 40-fold adrenodoxin slightly increased the Kd by 1.7-fold, and raised the ΔAmax 3.5-fold. For the other two substrates, 22R-hydroxycholesterol and 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol, the presence of adrenodoxin decreased the Kd by 2- and 4.5-fold, respectively, while slightly raising the ΔAmax ∼1.3-fold in each case. These substrate-specific differences in the adrenodoxin allosteric response illustrate that CYP11A1 is fine-tuned to respond to even small differences in the chemical structures of its substrates.

Interestingly, this type of adrenodoxin-mediated response has been documented in several other mitochondrial P450s in similar binding experiments. Both CYP11B1 and CYP11B2 display decreases in the Kd and increases in the ΔAmax for binding to their respective substrates. It is currently unknown whether this effect is limited to the CYP11 family or if it occurs in other mitochondrial P450 enzymes. One possibility is that this adrenodoxin-mediated increase in substrate binding affinity evolved as a way to progress the catalytic cycle over substrate dissociation. However, it is worth noting that cholesterol is the only substrate to date that does not increase in substrate binding affinity in the presence of adrenodoxin. One possible rationale for this could be that cholesterol is much more abundant throughout the tissues and involved in many other biochemical pathways than other substrates, which tend to have more specialized functionality.

CYP11A1 and CYP11B2 have complex, multistate substrate binding mechanisms

Extensive presteady state stopped-flow substrate binding data for CYP11A1 and CYP11B2 with varying adrenodoxin concentrations and kinetic modeling provided insight into the substrate binding mechanism for both enzymes. Even in the absence of adrenodoxin, both enzymes had complex, biphasic substrate binding profiles.

For CYP11A1, the stopped-flow binding data were first analyzed via individual fitting of each substrate concentration to biexponential equations to determine kobs values (Fig. 4). Plotting the kobs values against substrate concentration resulted in a nonlinear curve, which indicated that the binding mechanism was complex, but did not provide further mechanistic insights. Alternatively, kinetic modeling allows global data analysis, which is a much more rigorous and informative method of analysis. Usage of kinetic modeling definitively established that out of eight tested mechanisms, the 4-state closed model best represented the CYP11A1 substrate binding data. This model (Fig. 9) contains both an induced fit and conformational step, and these steps can occur in either order, eventually resulting in two ligand-bound states.

For CYP11B2, fitting the stopped-flow binding data to biexponential equations resulted in linear plots of substrate concentration against kobs and provided the kon and koff, but provided limited mechanistic insight (9). Again, kinetic modeling analysis was employed and revealed that in the absence of adrenodoxin, CYP11B2 most closely followed a 4-state branched induced fit model. In this model (Fig. 14), the enzyme first binds the ligand, and ligand binding induces one of the two distinct conformational changes, resulting in a branched step and three ligand-bound states.

For some P450 enzymes, ligand binding results in single exponential kinetics and is conclusively represented by a simple 2-state mechanism, and kinetic modeling is not necessary. This has been demonstrated for CYP11B1 binding substrates 11-deoxycortisol and 11-deoxycorticosterone (9), CYP2A6 binding coumarin and 7-hydroxycoumarin (15), and CYP27A1 binding its substrate retinol (16). However, kinetic modeling has been utilized with a variety of other human P450s to reveal more complex binding mechanisms (17, 18). For example, kinetic modeling has been used to characterize CYP3A4 with a number of different substrates and inhibitors. Recently, CYP3A4 binding of imidazole and cyanide was found to proceed through a 4-state branched induced fit model (19), while binding of inhibitors azamulin, retapamulin, pleuromutilin, and mibrefadil was best described by a 3-state induced fit (20). Isin and Guengerich initially established that CYP3A4 binding to inhibitor midazolam proceeded via an induced fit mechanism (21), but later reanalyzed the data in a kinetic modeling experiment and found that conformational selection was a more apt descriptor of the data (17). Recent kinetic modeling experiments with the steroidogenic aromatase or CYP19A1 revealed that both its substrate androstenedione and inhibitor 7-hydroxyflavone bind in a 3-state conformational selection model, while substrate testosterone binds as a 4-state mixed IF/CS model (22). Steroidogenic CYP17A1 was found to possess a conformational selection binding model to several substrates (18). These studies highlight the variability in P450 binding mechanisms between P450 enzymes, but in some cases the same enzyme can have different binding mechanisms for different ligands. As many P450s are drug targets, understanding the complexity and diversity of ligand binding modes lays an important foundation for drug design and development.

Adrenodoxin induces a conformational change in CYP11A1 and CYP11B2 to facilitate substrate binding

Beyond characterizing the binding mechanism for CYP11A1 and CYP11B2 alone, kinetic modeling was additionally used herein to probe the influence of adrenodoxin on the P450 substrate binding model and rate constants. Previously, it was determined that adrenodoxin allosterically modulates both CYP11B1 and CYP11B2 via increasing substrate binding affinity (13, 14). CYP11B1 possesses a simple, 2-state binding mechanism for primary substrate 11-deoxycortisol, and adrenodoxin increases the kon and decreases the koff. However, the more complex multiphasic substrate binding of both CYP11B2 and CYP11A1 required the usage of kinetic modeling to more accurately determine the model and rate constants.

In the case of CYP11A1, the binding mechanism for 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol is a 4-state closed system, both without and with adrenodoxin. Notably, however, increasing adrenodoxin increases k3, which is the rate constant governing the conformational change from E to E∗. Across the four concentrations of adrenodoxin examined (0, 1-, 10-, and 40-fold excess adrenodoxin), k3 increased from 0.441, 1.52, 3.58, to 10.1 min−1, respectively. In addition, ligand binding for state E∗ (961–1490 μM−1 min−1) is highly favored in comparison to state E (14.8–24.2 μM−1 min−1). The opposite was true for koff values, with 313 to 821 min−1 for state E and values 2.74 to 53.0 min−1 for state E∗. The combination is consistent with a substantially higher affinity for E∗ versus E, while adrenodoxin results in a P450 conformational change that promotes this substrate binding.

Interestingly, for CYP11B2, adrenodoxin shifted the binding mechanism to include a conformational selection step that is not present with no or low concentrations of adrenodoxin. Increasing adrenodoxin from 10- to 40-fold excess increased the rate constant governing this conformational selection process. Furthermore, the kon and koff for ligand binding to E∗ are much more favorable than for E. For state E, the kon for ligand binding is 53.5 and 17.8 μM−1 min−1 for 10 and 40-fold excess adrenodoxin, respectively, whereas the kon for state E∗ is much larger, 1300 and 981 μM−1 min−1, respectively. In addition, the koff for state E is much larger than for state E∗ (622 and 602 min−1 for state E and 24.8 and 62.6 min−1 for state E∗). Although the kon values are large, similarly high (∼107 M−1s−1) rates have been observed with CYP17A1 (22). Overall, this suggests that adrenodoxin binding to the proximal side of CYP11B2 enhances the rate of a conformational change that favors ligand binding via an increased kon and decreased koff.

These results substantially illuminate the adrenodoxin-mediated allosteric response in the CYP11 proteins, but much remains to be discovered. There are several next steps that could be considered. One is that while this work examined eight common binding mechanisms, there is always the possibility that the actual binding mechanism takes another form beyond those tested herein. Another approach beyond the scope of the current study would be to fit the data from all of the adrenodoxin concentrations globally, and treat this as a variable, much like the ligand concentration. Finally, while this study was intentionally completed in the oxidized state to separate adrenodoxin/P450 interactions from P450 reduction by adrenodoxin, it would be interesting to conduct similar studies in the reduced state to determine how redox state affects the allosteric modulation.

Conclusions

Overall, kinetic modeling is a powerful tool to investigate P450 binding mechanisms, which are often very complex and variable across different enzymes and substrates. The complex substrate binding mechanisms for the human steroidogenic CYP11A1 and CYP11B2 enzymes were thoroughly characterized, determining that CYP11A1 primarily has a 4-state closed binding mechanism for its substrate 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol, and CYP11B2 has a branched induced fit mechanism for binding its substrate 11-deoxycorticosterone. Interestingly, the addition of adrenodoxin alters substrate binding for both CYP11A1 and CYP11B2. An adrenodoxin-mediated shift in CYP11B2 binding mechanism from branched induced fit to a 4-state closed system was observed, suggesting that adrenodoxin adds a conformational selection step that is otherwise inaccessible in the absence of adrenodoxin. Although the CYP11A1 binding mechanism remains 4-state closed with high adrenodoxin concentrations, the rate constant governing the conformational change from E to E∗ increases monotonically with increasing adrenodoxin. For both CYP11A1 and CYP11B2, ligand binding is highly favored for state E∗, suggesting that adrenodoxin promotes a conformational change that enhances ligand binding. Overall, this work illustrates the complexity of P450 binding mechanisms and that they are fine-tuned to interact with their substrates and environments. The existence of these additional conformational states provides potential opportunities for selective P450 targeting, but would be facilitated by knowing adrenodoxin concentrations in various tissues and over time.

Experimental procedures

Materials

11-Deoxycorticosterone (64–85–7) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, and 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol was obtained from MedChem Express. Cholesterol was obtained from Fisher Scientific. 22R-Hydroxycholesterol was obtained from Cayman Chemicals.

Protein expression and purification

Expression and purification of human CYP11A1 and CYP11B2 were completed as described (23). Adrenodoxin was expressed and purified as described (24).

Equilibrium binding assays

Equilibrium ligand binding assays based on spectral shifts of the heme Soret peak were completed to measure the effect of adrenodoxin on CYP11A1 substrate binding affinity (Kd) and maximal absorbance (ΔAmax) as reported (14) but with the following changes. Binding assays were completed using each of the three major CYP11A1 substrates: cholesterol, 22R-hydroxycholesterol, and 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol. Each substrate was titrated to either CYP11A1 or CYP11A1 in the presence of a 40-fold excess of adrenodoxin. All studies were completed in buffer (50 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.4, 20% glycerol, and 0.5% Chaps). Substrates were in 100% ethanol.

Both 22R-hydroxycholesterol and 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol bound with quite high affinity (Kd <500 nM under one or more conditions), thus requiring a reduction in the protein concentration to determine more accurate dissociation constants. To compensate for the reduced signal, the path length was increased. Solutions of 0.2 μM CYP11A1 or 0.2 μM CYP11A1 plus 8 μM adrenodoxin (1:40 M ratio) were prepared and transferred to 5 cm cuvettes. Although substrate was added to the sample cuvette, an equal volume of the substrate solvent ethanol was added to the reference cuvette.

Cholesterol has more intrinsic absorbance than the other two CYP11A1 substrates, and this required the use of tandem cuvettes to reduce background compound absorbance. For these assays, solutions of either 1 μM CYP11A1 or 1 μM CYP11A1 plus 40 μM adrenodoxin were prepared and transferred to tandem cuvettes (total path length 2 cm). For both the sample and reference cuvettes, one half of the cell contained the protein(s) in buffer, and the other half contained only buffer. Cholesterol was titrated into the half-cell containing the protein(s) and the reference cell containing buffer, while an equal volume of ethanol was added to the sample half-cell containing buffer and the reference cuvette half-cell containing protein(s).

In all experiments, samples were incubated for 8 min after additions before recording difference spectra from 500 to 300 nm. Substrate binding induces a type I shift in CYP11A1, resulting in a peak at 421 nm and a trough at 390 nm. The difference in absorbance between the peak and trough was recorded for each titration and plotted against the titrated ligand concentration. These data were fit to the tight-binding or Morrison equation in GraphPad Prism. The Kd and maximal change in absorbance (ΔAmax) were recorded for each CYP11A1 substrate with and without 40-fold excess adrenodoxin (Fig. 3).

Stopped-flow determination of substrate binding rates

All substrate binding rates were measured using a High-Tech SF61DX2 stopped-flow spectrophotometer equipped with a temperature-controlled circulating water bath. The stopped-flow has a dead time of 1 ms.

CYP11A1 stopped-flow studies

Stopped-flow studies were completed to determine the effect of adrenodoxin on CYP11A1 substrate binding rate constants. Solutions containing 2 μM CYP11A1 were prepared in buffer (50 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.4, 20% glycerol, and 0.5% Chaps) containing varying amounts of adrenodoxin (0, 2, 20, or 80 μM). Stopped-flow studies were initially completed with the CYP11A1 initial substrate cholesterol, but low signal-to-noise limited the determination of substrate binding rates. Absorbance changes for 22R-hydroxycholesterol are in the same range. In addition, both cholesterol and 22R-hydroxycholesterol had a higher affinity for CYP11A1, which meant the protein concentration could not be reduced below the Kd in the stopped flow with a fixed path length. Fortunately, however, 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol induces a larger type I spectral shift in CYP11A1 and has a higher Kd value, permitting the desired studies. Thus, substrate solutions contained 0.25, 0.75, 1.5, or 3 μM 20R,22R-dihydroxycholesterol in buffer with a final solvent (ethanol) concentration <0.1%.

CYP11B2 stopped-flow studies

Stopped-flow was also used to determine CYP11B2 substrate binding rate constants in the presence of varying adrenodoxin concentrations. Some of the CYP11B2 stopped-flow data were reported previously (9). These conditions included CYP11B2 with 2.5, 5, 10, and 25 μM 11-deoxycorticosterone and CYP11B2 with 5, 10, 17.5, and 25 μM 11-deoxycortisol. However, initial kinetic modeling herein determined that the substrate concentration range needed to be expanded to include higher and lower substrate concentrations to determine the CYP11B2 binding mechanism.

To increase the substrate concentration range, CYP11B2 stopped-flow binding experiments were conducted with 50 and 100 μM of either 11-deoxycorticosterone or 11-deoxycortisol. These studies were conducted as described (9), but in brief, 2 μM CYP11B2 was preincubated with either 0, 2, 20, or 80 μM adrenodoxin in buffer (50 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.4, 20% glycerol, and 0.5% Chaps). The syringe containing CYP11B2/adrenodoxin was rapidly mixed 1:1 with either 100 or 200 μM 11-deoxycorticosterone or 11-deoxycortisol (prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide such that the final total solvent content was <0.1%).

Data collection and analysis for association experiments

For both CYP11A1 and CYP11B2, data collection and initial analysis were also completed as described previously (9). Briefly, absolute spectra were recorded from 700 to 300 nm, including 160 spectra collected on a logarithmic timescale. A combination of 5-, 15-, and 30-s shot lengths was collected. The difference between the absorbance at 419 and 391 nm was calculated and plotted against time. To produce the data shown in Figure 4, the stopped-flow data were fit to both one and two-phase association equations in Prism, and the best-fitting equation was selected. For CYP11A1, this was the biexponential equation. This equation was used to calculate the fast and slow observed rate constants (denoted here as kfast and kslow) and the percent amplitude occupied by the fast phase (% fast).

Stopped-flow dissociation (dilution) experiments

Decreasing the substrate concentration involved designing a new dissociation experiment to extend the range of observable ligand concentrations below typical signal-to-noise constraints. These dissociation experiments involved preincubation of the enzyme with a small amount of substrate, followed by rapid dilution with buffer via stopped-flow to monitor substrate dissociation. This required modification of the High-Tech SF61DX2 stopped-flow spectrophotometer to replace the two push plates from Drives 1 and 2 operating the two syringes with a single extended push plate (TgK Scientific; Opt-669) able to simultaneously activate four syringes (Positions A-D). Positions A and C were fitted with 0.5 ml syringes, and positions B and D were fitted with 2.5 ml syringes. With this configuration, enzyme in either of the 0.5 ml syringes and buffer in the remaining three syringes resulted in a 12-fold dilution.

To perform the dissociation experiment, 12 μM CYP11B2 was preincubated with either 0, 12, 120, or 480 μM adrenodoxin and 11-deoxycorticosterone. As a starting condition, the CYP11B2/adrenodoxin mixtures were preincubated with 2.5 μM 11-deoxycorticosterone. This experimental condition resulted in acceptable signal-to-noise for the experimental conditions containing any amount of adrenodoxin, but poor signal-to-noise for the experiments with no adrenodoxin. As discussed previously, adrenodoxin increases the magnitude of the CYP11B2 spectral shift, so this result was expected (13). To account for these differences, the experiments containing no adrenodoxin were repeated using CYP11B2 preincubated with 5 μM 11-deoxycorticosterone instead. Doubling the initial substrate concentration resulted in acceptable signal-to-noise ratio (Fig. 10).

Data collection and analysis of dissociation experiments

For each experimental condition, a minimum of three shots (technical replicates) were collected, each consisting of absolute spectra recorded from 700 to 300 nm, with 160 spectra collected on a logarithmic timescale. A combination of 5-, 15-, and 30-s shot lengths was collected to fully capture the dissociation kinetics. The difference between absorbance at 419 and 391 nm was calculated to monitor the CYP11B2 transition from high-spin to low-spin, and each shot was baselined by setting the first time point to 0. This change in absorbance was plotted against time, resulting in a decay function (Figure 10, Figure 11, Figure 12, Figure 13).

MATLAB analysis

Stopped-flow data were analyzed in MATLAB to determine rate constants and extinction coefficients for eight different models, including: 3-state induced fit, 3-state conformational selection, 4-state linear induced fit, 4-state linear conformational selection, 4-state mixed induced fit/conformational selection, 4-state branched induced fit, 4-state branched conformational selection, and 4-state closed induced fit/conformational selection (Fig. 2).

MATLAB code was originally sourced from Michelle Redhair and John Hackett (19, 22), and was revised to account for the ligand depletion present in both the CYP11A1 and CYP11B2 experiments (see Supporting Information). Data fitting was poor for every model before accounting for the ligand depletion. First, a series of differential equations was solved to estimate the initial concentration of each enzymatic state before dilution in the stopped-flow (in the syringe). Next, these initial conditions were used to calculate the concentration of each enzyme after dilution in the stopped-flow. For binding (association) experiments, this involved a 2-fold dilution, and for dissociation experiments, this involved a 12-fold dilution. Finally, these initial conditions were used to solve a series of differential equations to estimate the rate constants. The set of differential equations includes an equation for how each enzymatic state and the free ligand concentration change with respect to time.

The MATLAB code utilizes the multistart algorithm within the Global Optimization Toolbox to produce random initial values for the rate constants and extinction coefficients. The upper and lower bounds of the initial guesses and the number of initial guesses are user-customized and optimized to each experiment to avoid a solution in a local minimum. For the CYP11A1 and CYP11B2 experiments, 100 initial guesses with lower and upper bounds for the rate constants of 0 and 1500 μM−1 min−1 or min−1, respectively, and bounds of 0 to 100 for the extinction coefficients. After producing initial guesses for the rate constants and extinction coefficients, the ode15 solver was used to solve each set of differential equations describing the specific model by minimizing the RSS.

Several statistical measures were applied to select the best binding model for each set of experiments. Models were analyzed using RSS values, AIC and BIC values, and LL values. Although RSS values measure how well the model fits the data, AIC and BIC values also account for the number of parameters in the model, penalizing models with higher numbers of components. The best model representing the data will have a low RSS value; large, negative AIC and BIC values; and a large positive LL value. For each of the experiments presented herein, the RSS, AIC, BIC, and log likelihood values agreed for the model selection. Singular value decomposition analysis was performed, but did not provide additional useful information.
