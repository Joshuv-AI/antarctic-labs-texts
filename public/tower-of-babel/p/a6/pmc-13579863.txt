Modular plug-and-play engineering of Klebsiella phages with dual receptor-binding proteins for efficient host range design
Authors: Dorien Dams, Agnieszka Latka, Mathilde Hulsens, Zuzanna Drulis-Kawa, Yves Briers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe high capsular diversity restricting the host range of many Klebsiella phages has driven the evolution of branched receptor-binding protein (RBP) systems as a strategy for host range expansion. These dual RBP systems offer a unique opportunity for modular engineering. However, most previous approaches lacked a standardized and systematic framework to exploit an engineering platform that enables efficient and modular reprogramming of Klebsiella podophages with dual RBP systems.ResultsThe workflow integrates (I) the VersaTile technique for rapid assembly of chimeric RBP gene clusters, (II) Gibson assembly for in vitro genome construction, and (III) electroporation-based rebooting. By retaining one native RBP, the system ensures that a suitable host is always available for rebooting, reducing technical failure, and allowing a clear distinction between biological incompatibility and assembly issues. Using this approach, we systematically swapped full-length RBPs and receptor-binding domains (RBDs) at both positions of the dual RBP system in podophages K11 and KP32, confirming the modularity and interchangeability of structural and enzymatic domains. Advanced designs, including cross-swapping and position swapping, were successfully implemented, while attempts to graft phylogenetically distant RBPs revealed structural constraints that inform future design strategies.ConclusionThis work introduces a standardized, scalable, and plug-and-play framework for phage engineering that leverages the modularity of dual RBP systems. By ensuring rebooting through an unmodified RBP, the platform provides a robust foundation for systematic host range reprogramming and functional studies of RBP architecture, paving the way for rational design of therapeutic phages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13036-026-00724-y.Keywords: Bacteriophages, Receptor-binding proteins, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Phage engineering, Klebsiella phage K11, Klebsiella phage KP32

Background

Klebsiella pneumoniae is a priority pathogen designated by the World Health Organization (WHO) due to its increasing pathogenicity, virulence and emergence of multi- and pan-antibiotic resistance, including the emergence of hypervirulent carbapenem-resistant strains [1]. It is currently estimated to be the fourth leading cause of death directly attributable to antimicrobial resistance worldwide, accounting for around 158,000 deaths in 2021 [2]. Clinically, K. pneumoniae is associated with a variety of infections, such as pneumonia, bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections, and liver abscesses, particularly in healthcare settings [1].

Bacteriophages represent a promising class of precision antimicrobials capable of selectively targeting pathogenic bacteria while preserving the commensal microbiome. Therapeutic efficacy of Klebsiella phages have been demonstrated in multiple in vivo infection mouse models [3, 4] and clinical phage therapy cases [5, 6]. In addition, K. pneumoniae is also a highly relevant model organism for studying phage-host interactions due to its capsular diversity, which drives highly specific phage tropism. Klebsiella phage specificity is primarily determined by their receptor-binding proteins (RBPs) which often recognize and interact with the capsule (K-antigen). Given the immense capsule diversity, with over 79 distinct serotypes and 186 locus types, the continuous arms race between phages and bacteria has resulted in a high diversity of RBPs with a modular structure [7–9]. Beamud et al. experimentally demonstrated that the capsular tropism of phages can be predicted based on their RBP domains composition. However, the predictability is limited by post-adsorptive phage defense mechanisms within bacteria [10]. The recent development of machine learning-based tools, such as PhageHostLearn, further reinforced the critical role of RBPs by predicting Klebsiella phage-bacteria interactions at the strain level with more than 80% accuracy using only RBP sequences as predictors [11]. Klebsiella phages with a single RBP system generally have a conserved N-terminal anchor domain with a structural function (i.e., attaching the RBP to the phage tail), and a highly variable C-terminal enzymatic domain that contains depolymerase activity for capsule degradation. Enzymatic domains include an additional C-terminal module such as an (autocleavable) chaperone, lectin, or carbohydrate-binding domain for correct protein folding, trimerization, or capsular sugar binding [12–15]. In addition, Klebsiella phages have evolved more complex RBP systems containing two (or more) RBPs to expand their host range [7, 12, 13, 16–19].

The Klebsiella podophages KP32 and K11 serve as a model system for studying the dual RBP architecture, in which the RBPs are connected via a branching-docking domain. Their RBP system can be divided into five modular building blocks comprising (I) a N-terminal anchor for the attachment of the first RBP (RBP1) to the phage tail, including (II) a T4gp10-like branching domain; (III) a C-terminal receptor-binding domain (RBD1) with specific depolymerase activity for RBP1, and a second RBP consisting of (IV) a short, N-terminal conserved peptide (CP) that attaches (V) the C-terminal RBD2 of RBP2 to the T4gp10-like branching domain of RBP1 (Fig. 1).

The dual RBP architecture of Klebsiella phage KP32 and K11. The anchor-branched RBP system consists of five modular domains. RBP1 consists of (I) a N-terminal anchor for attachment to the phage tail, (II) a T4gp10-like branching domain for attaching RBP2, and (III) a RBD1 with specific depolymerase activity for RBP1. RBP2 consists of (IV) a N-terminal CP that docks to the T4gp10-like branching domain of RBP1, and an RBD2 with specific depolymerase activity for RBP2. RBP, receptor-binding protein; RBD, receptor-binding domain; CP, conserved peptide

Klebsiella phage RBPs are highly subject to horizontal gene transfer, with the structural domains being conserved, while the highly diverse RBDs are extensively exchanged within or across the phylogenetic borders of phage genera [10, 12, 20]. This flexibility allows them to adapt rapidly to new hosts and to evade phage defense mechanisms impacting the receptor recognized by the RBP. The growing understanding of the modular organization of RBPs has opened new opportunities for phage-host reprogramming [21]. Applying this principle, customized phages can be created by plugging RBPs from different phage sources into conserved, safe, and well-characterized phage scaffolds that offer therapeutic advantages in terms of specificity, resistance development, production capacity, and intellectual property [22]. In addition, engineered phages can be designed with tailored host specificity for biocontrol and diagnostic tools.

The foundational work of Latka et al. bioinformatically and experimentally demonstrated the autonomous function and folding of structural anchor and enzymatic RBD domains by domain swapping in the K11 phage scaffold [12, 23]. The horizontal exchange of enzymatic domains between closely related and unrelated Klebsiella phages resulted in a specificity swap corresponding to the newly introduced depolymerase domain. However, this study focused exclusively on the exchange of RBP1 in the dual RBP system, leaving the feasibility and implications of swapping RBP2 unexplored. Here, chimeric RBP gene clusters capable of encoding a dual RBP system were grafted into the K11 and KP32 phage scaffolds using an enhanced plug-and-play phage engineering approach. Using the VersaTile technique [24], specificity swaps for RBP1, RBP2, or both RBPs can be achieved. The VersaTile technique is a Type IIs restriction enzyme-based DNA assembly technique developed for the convenient creation of libraries of modular proteins such as RBPs. The approach implies two aspects: (i) the creation of a repository of modular RBP building blocks (“tiles”), which are flanked by location-specific position tags, and (ii) the assembly of new chimeric RBP clusters by combining the selected tiles in a specific order through a two-step restriction-ligation reaction using only one Type IIs restriction enzyme. Positioning the Type IIs recognition sites at the end of the tile enables their removal during restriction, thereby enriching only the correctly assembled products during iterative restriction-ligation cycles. In addition, cleavage within the position tags creates sticky ends that are specific to their position and compatible only with adjacent tiles in the final construct. The position tags are embedded in precisely defined sequences that maintain the reading frame and, where possible, maintain the wild-type amino acid (aa) sequence. Different studies applying this method to various modular proteins (RBPs, (inno)lysins, cellulosomes, glycolysomes, enzyme cascades) have been reported [15, 23–33].

To further investigate the role of RBP positioning and scaffold differences, RBPs of K11 and KP32 phages were mutually exchanged at different positions. In addition, RBD domains from KP34 (Drulisvirus) and KP36 (Webervirus) phages were plugged at the RBP2 position to evaluate whether KP32 and K11 serve as suitable phage scaffolds for more phylogenetically distinct RBPs or RBDs. The rebooted phages were further propagated to assess the impact of the RBP swaps on host specificity and on phage propagation efficacy.

Materials and methods

Bacterial and yeast strains, phages and media

Bacterial strains, phages, and their origin are listed in Additional File 1 Table S1. K. pneumoniae host strains were cultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB, Oxoid) and plated on tryptic soy agar (TSA, MP Biomedicals). E. coli TOP10 (Invitrogen) was grown in lysogeny broth (LB, 1% (w/v) tryptone (VWR); 0.5% (w/v) yeast extract (VWR); 1% (w/v) NaCl (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and plated on LB agar (LA, LB supplemented with 1.5% (w/v) bacteriological agar (VWR)). E. coli TOP10 transformed with pVTE or pVTD23 vector (Additional File 2 Fig. S1 and Fig. S2, respectively) was grown on LB supplemented with 100 µg/mL ampicillin (Fisher Scientific) or 50 µg/mL kanamycin (Carl Roth), respectively, and 5% (w/v) sucrose (Fisher Scientific). Super optimal broth with catabolite repression medium (SOC medium, 2% (w/v) tryptone (VWR); 0.2% (w/v) yeast extract (VWR); 0.05% (w/v) NaCl (Fisher Scientific); 2.5 mM KCl (Carl Roth); 10 mM MgSO4 (VWR) and 0.4 g/L glucose (Carl Roth)) was used to recover the cells after electroporation. All strains were incubated at 37 °C.

Constructing chimeric RBP gene clusters

Chimeric RBP gene clusters were constructed using the VersaTile technique according to Gerstmans et al. [24]. Modular RBP sequences (N-terminal anchor, T4gp10-like, CP and RBP and RBD domains) were converted into tiles. The DNA of interest was amplified by Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Additional File 1 Table S2) using VersaTile-specific primers with extended 5’ sequences to generate the different tiles (Additional File 1 TableS3). Following PCR amplification, the DNA fragments were verified by gel electrophoresis, purified using the GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and subsequently cloned into the pVTE entry vector (Additional File 2 Fig. S1) by restriction-ligation (SapI, Thermo Fisher Scientific; T4 DNA ligase, Life Technologies; Additional File 1 Table S4). To remove the BsaI restriction site present in the original DNA fragment, the tile vector was amplified with inverse PCR to linearize the plasmid. Primers were designed to create a silent point mutation in the BsaI recognition site and to extend both ends of the linear DNA fragment with SapI recognition sites (Additional File 1 Table S5). Template DNA was removed by treating the reaction mixture with the restriction enzyme DpnI (Thermo Scientific). The PCR fragment was circularized again with a restriction (SapI) ligation (T4 DNA ligase) reaction. Finally, these tiles were assembled into RBP gene clusters according to a predefined design into the pVTD23 destination vector (Additional File 2 Fig. S2) using the VersaTile technique (Additional File 1 TableS6). An overview of all constructed chimeric RBP gene clusters is provided in Additional File 1 Table S7. Nucleotide sequence, amino acid sequence and Alphafold2 predictions are provided in Additional File 3 Table S8. All transformations were performed using chemically competent E. coli TOP10 cells. Correctly assembled plasmids were verified by clone analysis using DreamTaq polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Additional File 4 Table S9) with the appropriate primers listed in (Additional File 1 Table S5) and extracted with the GeneJet Plasmid Miniprep Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RBP gene clusters integrated in pVTD23 were sequenced by Sanger sequencing (LGC genomics, Germany) or by Nanopore sequencing (Plasmidsaurus, USA); or Eurofins (Ebersberg, Germany). Primers used for Sanger sequencing are listed in Additional File 1 Table S5.

Amplification and purification of phage scaffold fragments

K11 and KP32 phage genomes were amplified in five phage scaffold fragments, using Q5 High-Fidelity polymerase (BIOKÉ; Additional File 4 Table S10) and Primestar HS polymerase (Takara Bio; Additional File 4 Table S11), respectively. Scaffold fragment four represents the native RBP gene cluster, which can be replaced by chimeric RBP gene clusters. Primers (Additional File 1 Table S5) were designed to include homologous overlap of approximately 200 bp to the adjacent phage scaffold fragments. The chimeric RBP gene clusters assembled in the pVTD23 vector were amplified with Q5 High-Fidelity polymerase (BIOKÉ). All PCR fragments were purified with the DNA Clean & Concentrator-5 kit (Zymo Research). DNA concentrations were measured with a Quantus fluorometer (Promega) and fragment sizes were verified by gel electrophoresis.

Phage genome assembly – phage rebooting – liquid phage propagation

Gibson assembly was performed using the Gibson master mix, which was prepared according to the method described by Gibson et al. [34]. Each reaction included 0.1 pmol of each phage genome fragment, ensuring that the total volume of DNA fragments did not exceed 10 µL. Three volumes of Gibson master mix were added to one volume of the fragment mixture. The assembly reaction was performed under the following conditions: 30 min at 37 °C, followed 60 min at 60 °C and 20 min at 80 °C. The assembly product was then dialyzed against ultrapure water for 20 min using a 0.025 μm nitrocellulose membrane (MF-Millipore, Merck, Germany).

Phage rebooting of the Gibson mixture was carried out by electroporation in triplicate. Specifically, 1 µL phage genome (50 ng/µL; positive control) or 4 µL Gibson assembly mixture was electroporated in 40 µL electrocompetent E. coli 10G ELITE cells (LGC genomics, with a minimum competence of 1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL). The rebooting mixture was immediately recovered with 1 mL SOC medium. After recovery, the three replicates were pooled for further processing and incubated for 3 h. To release the (engineered) phages, 5% (v/v) chloroform was added to the suspension followed by vortexing and centrifugation (10,000 x g for 10 min at 21 °C). Next, 500 µL of the clarified supernatant was then added to 200 µL of the Klebsiella host strain recognized by the unmodified RBP. The mixture was plated using the double agar overlay method (3 mL of liquid 0.5% (w/v) TSA was poured on a 1.5% (w/v) TSA plate) and incubated for 10–18 h [35].

Liquid phage propagations were performed according to Latka et al. [15]. A single plaque from each phage variant was selected and resuspended in 200 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 127 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl (Carl Roth), 10 mM Na2H2PO4 (Carl Roth), 2 mM KH2PO4 (Carl Roth); pH 7.4). Subsequently, the phage was propagated in liquid culture using the Klebsiella host strain recognized by the unmodified RBP. The phage suspension was mixed with the Klebsiella culture at a 1:9 ratio (OD600 ~ 0.1–0.2) and incubated for 90 min. Phage release was then facilitated by chloroform treatment, as described above. The phage stock was filtered using a 0.2 μm cellulose acetate membrane syringe filter (VWR) and phage DNA for sequencing was extracted by classical phenol: chlorofom extraction. Either the whole phage genome or a PCR amplicon of the RBP gene clusters were sent for Nanopore sequencing (Plasmidsaurus). Raw sequencing data (FASTQ files) were deposited in the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) under study accession ERP194061 (project accession PRJEB113715). The corresponding consensus sequence (FASTA format) and their alignment to the reference template DNA are provided in Additional File 5. Primers to amplify the PCR amplicon are listed in Additional File 1 Table S5.

Determination of the efficiency of plating (EOP)

Three rounds of liquid phage propagation, as described in above section liquid phage propagation, were performed on RBP1 and RBP2 host(s) of the wild-type and the engineered phages to assess whether the host range of the engineered, rebooted phages corresponds to the host range of the exchanged RBD(s). For the first round, the phage suspension was mixed with the Klebsiella culture (OD600 ~ 0.1–0.2) at a 1:9 ratio and incubated for 90 min. Phage release was then facilitated by chloroform treatment. The supernatant of the first round was used for the second round of propagation and the supernatant from the second round of propagation was used for the third round. Wild-type phages were three-rounds propagated as a reference.

The titer (plaque forming units; PFU) was determined by spotting a ten-fold dilution series of the phage stock before propagation (“start”) and each phage suspension after three rounds of propagation on a bacterial lawn of all respective propagation hosts. Bacterial lawns were prepared using the double agar overlay. Dilution series and Klebsiella cultures for bacterial lawns were prepared in triplicate. Plaques with typical plaque morphology were counted, and the log(PFU/mL) was calculated. The total plaque count of the “start” was divided by 1,000, as each propagation cycle led to a ten-fold dilution. Statistical comparisons were made using an ordinary one-way ANOVA against the “start” group for each host on which the phages were plated. A post-hoc Dunnett’s test was applied correcting for multiple comparisons to prevent Type I errors. Differences with a p-value < 0.05 were considered significant and annotated on the plot using standard significance level symbols (*** for p < 0.001, ** for p < 0.01, * for p < 0.05). All graphs were created using R Statistical Software (R 4.3.1) and further processed using Affinity Designer 2 version 2.4.2.

CP domain sequence and structural analysis

The aa sequence identity between the K11 and KP32 CP domains and their respective T4gp10-like branching domains was determined using Protein Blast (NBCI). Structural comparisons were performed using the DALI server and PyMOL (version 3.1.6.1). As the CP domains were too small (35 aa) for reliable alignment with DALI, RMSD calculations were performed manually with PyMOL. The structural confidence of the predicted models was evaluated based on AlphaFold pLDDT scores. Intrinsically disordered regions and within the RBP aa sequences were predicted using the DISOPRED3 server (https://bioinf.cs.ucl.ac.uk/psipred).

Results

A plug-and-play phage engineering strategy to reprogram the host range of Klebsiella phages

The phage engineering workflow used in this study consists of three key steps: (I) construction of RBP gene clusters, (II) in vitro phage genome assembly, and (III) phage rebooting of the engineered phage genome. The integration of the VersaTile technique enabled the efficient and standardized creation of dual chimeric RBP gene clusters, allowing for the exchange of both structural (anchor-branching and docking domains) and enzymatic (full RBP or RBD with depolymerase activity) building blocks at RBP1, RBP2, or both positions simultaneously. Modular RBP domains were sourced from four well-characterized Klebsiella phages: KP32 (RBPs KP32gp37 and KP32gp38 with K3 and K21 specificity, respectively; Przondovirus), K11 (RBPs K11gp17 and K11gp0043 with K11 and K31 specificity, respectively; Przondovirus), KP34 (RBP KP34gp57 with K63 specificity, Drulisvirus) and KP36 (RBP KP36gp50 with K63 specificity, Webervirus), similar to the study of Latka et al. [12, 15, 23]. While KP32, K11, and KP34 have a podovirus morphology, KP36 has a siphovirus morphology. Delineation of the building blocks was based on previous research on their RBP architecture [12, 13, 15]. Firstly, all modular RBP building blocks were transformed into tiles by flanking them with location-specific position tags (P1-P8) and recognition sites of a Type IIs restriction enzyme. These specific features were added during PCR amplification using tailed primers and cloned in the dedicated entry vector (Additional File 1 Table S3). Secondly, chimeric RBP gene clusters were assembled from either four, five or six tiles on a predefined position, depending on whether a single or both RBPs were swapped (Fig. 2, step 1; Additional File 1 Table S7).

Structural (anchor, T4gp10, CP) and enzymatic (RBP, RBD) building blocks were derived from Klebsiella phages K11, KP32, KP34 and KP36 and flanked with location-specific position tags (P1-P8). The position tags define the future location of the tile in the assembly construct. Subsequently, the building blocks were assembled into chimeric RBP gene clusters. The RBP gene from which each tile is derived is written on the tile. The syntax of the formal name, listed in Additional File 1 Table S3, is displayed above each group of tiles, and the tile number, which needs to be added on the dotted line, is displayed to the right of each tile. (Step 2) Phage genome assembly. Here, the phage scaffold fragments 1, 2, 3 and 5 and the chimeric RBP gene cluster fragment 4 were amplified from the DNA template by PCR and assembled using Gibson assembly. (Step 3) Phage rebooting. The assembled engineered phage genomes were introduced into competent E. coli 10G by electroporation. After three hours of phage replication, the E. coli cells were lysed and virulent phage particles were released by chloroform. To facilitate host selection during the rebooting step, engineered phages were mostly designed to retain one native RBP, allowing plating of the rebooted phage on the host of the unmodified, native RBP. Chimeric RBP gene clusters were grafted into the K11 and KP32 phage scaffold genomes using in vitro Gibson assembly (Fig. 2, step 2). This technique was chosen as it is a rapid (< 1 day), host-independent method with a higher DNA assembly efficiency than traditional genome editing techniques within the host bacteria, such as homologous recombination [21, 34, 36, 37]. The PCR-amplified RBP gene cluster and the four phage scaffold fragments (~ 10 kbp each) were assembled by homologous overlapping ends. As members of the T7 phage family, K11 and KP32 produce concatemeric DNA upon infection with repeating sequences delineating a single genome. This mechanism plays a crucial role during genome replication and packaging into new phage particles [38]. Given the mechanism of Gibson assembly, a circular genome is achieved that is closed at the position of the repeating ends.

Electroporation of the circular phage genomes into E. coli 10G cells was identified as a suitable method for phage rebooting (Fig. 2, step 3; Additional File 6) [15, 23, 27, 39]. Since KP32 and K11 both belong to the Autographiviridae, they encode their own RNA polymerase recognizing the cognate phage promotors, rendering them more independent of the host metabolism. In addition, K. pneumoniae and E. coli both belong to the Enterobacteriaceae, further supporting why Klebsiella phages can be rebooted in an E. coli cell.

Validation of the plug-and-play phage engineering platform

Although VersaTile is a highly convenient approach to concatenate non-homologous modular building blocks such as the RBP modules, it introduces a six-nucleotide junction where two tiles are fused [23, 24]. These junctions could potentially affect protein functionality and, hence, effective phage propagation. Therefore, the first step was to validate the effect of VersaTile junction(s) in the chimeric RBP gene clusters. To assess this, the native RBP was VersaTile-assembled and grafted into the KP32 and K11 phage. Three VersaTile-assembled phage genomes were generated: one derived from wild-type phage KP32, i.e. KP32gp38_RBD2::KP32gp38_RBD2 (Fig. 3C), in which the native KP32gp38_RBD2 (K21 specificity) was replaced by a VersaTile-engineered variant introducing a junction between CP and RBD2, and two controls derived from phage K11, i.e. K11gp17_RBD1::K11gp17_RBD1 (Fig. 3D) and K11gp0043_RBD2::K11gp0043_RBD2 (Fig. 3E) in which the VersaTile junction was integrated into RBP1 (K11 specificity) and RBP2 (K31 specificity), respectively. The VersaTile junction between the T4gp10 domain and RBD1 (P4) has been designed as such that the two encoded amino acids are identical to those natively present in both the K11 and KP32 scaffolds. Given the scarless VersaTile junction and the conserved modular architecture of KP32 and K11 RBP systems, we considered that testing the RBP1 VersaTile junction in only one of the two phage scaffolds confidently demonstrates that the VersaTile junction does not interfere with the protein folding or phage propagation. In contrast, the RBP2 VersaTile junction (P6) was tested in both phage scaffolds since the junction is only scarless in the KP32 scaffold, not in the K11 scaffold.

Phage genome rebooting with electroporation often yielded a high number of plaques (> 100), demonstrating that phage rebooting was functional and efficient (Additional File 6). Successfully rebooted VersaTile phages, along with wild-type K11 and KP32 phages, underwent three rounds of propagation on all propagation hosts to compare effective phage propagation. The titer and host specificity after propagation was determined by spotting the produced phages on the selected hosts (Additional File 4 Table S12). In addition, the final propagation titer was compared to the initial titer of the non-propagated phage culture (referred to as start) to determine whether the (engineered) phages were capable of propagation. All three engineered phages, regardless of whether the VersaTile junction was located in RBP1 or RBP2, reached comparable log10(PFU/mL) levels to the wild-type phages, approximately three logs higher than the start titer (Fig. 3). This demonstrated that the VersaTile junctions did not affect phage propagation. Notably, phage K11 was not able to propagate on its RBP2 host, even though the recombinant K11gp0043 RBP2 alone shows specificity for the K31 host [15]. So far, no host targeted by K11gp0043 RBP2 of phage K11 has been identified despite screening a large panel of isolates.

Grafting K11 phage RBD1 in the KP32 scaffold (on the RBP1 position)

In a previous study, both the full-length RBP1 of phage KP32 (KP32gp37, K3 capsular serotype) and a chimeric RBP consisting of the native K11 anchor fused to the T4gp10-like branching and C-terminal KP32 RBD1 were successfully grafted into the RBP1 position of the K11 phage scaffold, resulting in a specificity switch to K3 while losing its original K11 specificity [23]. This successful host range switch was expected, given the high genomic similarity between podophages KP32 and K11, sharing 91% genomic identity (88% query coverage) and 88% aa identity in their N-terminal anchor domain (157/178 aa) [12, 23].

To further investigate the feasibility of reciprocal domain swapping, we constructed a chimeric RBP by attaching the native KP32 anchor to the RBD1 of phage K11 (K11 RBD1, K11gp17, K11 capsular serotype) and grafting the chimeric RBP at the RBP1 position of the KP32 phage scaffold. Subsequently, we investigated the effect of RBD1 swapping on the propagation efficiency in the host of the unmodified, putative RBP2. Following successful rebooting and propagation, both capsular serotypes recognized by the native RBP and those recognized by the chimeric, swapped RBP were evaluated for a specificity switch.

The engineered KP32 phage KP32gp37_RBD1::K11gp17_RBD1 effectively switched from K3 to K11 specificity with an effective phage propagation of 9.5 ± 0.05 log10(PFU/mL) when propagated and plated on the K11 host (Fig. 4A, yellow panel). In addition, RBP1 engineering did not affect the propagation efficiency of the native, unmodified KP32 RBP2 host, which retained its K21 specificity. Notably, the PFU when propagated on the K21-specific host of the native RBP2 (10.3 ± 0.15 log10(PFU/mL)) was slightly higher than when propagated on the K11 host of chimeric RBP1 when plated on the K11 RBP1 host (Fig. 4A, yellow panel). This supports our selection strategy of screening engineered phages via the native, unmodified RBP.

Reciprocal specificity swapping in the phage K11 and KP32 scaffold (on the RBP2 position)

Horizontal RBD swapping on the RBP1 position in bivalent phages has already been demonstrated experimentally [23, 40]. However, the functionality and folding of chimeric RBPs consisting of the native CP domain but a non-cognate RBD at the RBP2 position remain unraveled. To further investigate the functional interchangeability of Klebsiella phage RBPs, RBD2 of phage K11 (K11 RBD2, K11gp0043, K31 capsular serotype) and phage KP32 (KP32 RBD2, KP32gp38, K21 capsular serotype) were reciprocally swapped on the RBP2 position of the opposite phage scaffold (Fig. 4B, C).

Grafting the K11 RBD2 into the RBP2 position of the KP32 phage scaffold (KP32gp38_RBD2::K11gp0043_RBD2), did not result in a corresponding specificity switch from K21 to K31 (Fig. 4B). However, sequence analysis confirmed the successful integration of the chimeric K11gp0043 RBP into the KP32 phage scaffold genome. This could be due to the high scaffold identity and, consequently, a shared susceptibility to a phage defense system in the bacterial host targeted by K11gp0043.

For the reverse combination, we attempted to graft the KP32 RBD2 at the RBP2 position into the K11 phage scaffold. This requires the construction of a chimeric RBP gene cluster consisting of the unmodified K11 RBP1 gene and the chimeric RBP2 gene with the native K11 CP and the non-cognate KP32 RBD2. However, repeated disassembly of the chimeric RBP gene cluster led to a frameshift at the site of the VersaTile junction (P6; Fig. 2), resulting in a non-functional RBP2. One possible hypothesis is that the P6 position tag flanks the CP_P5P6 tile, which encodes 35 amino acids of the N-terminal region of RBP2 and is located in the center of the RBP gene cluster. It is consequently flanked by the two largest RBD1 and RBD2 tiles, which range in size from 1,626 nucleotides (nt) to 2,241 nt. This large size difference between the CP tile and its neighbouring RBD tiles may contribute to assembly inefficiency. In addition, the sticky end of P6 (GCGG) has a 100% GC content and is therefore more prone to forming misassemblies. Although this position tag is more susceptible to frameshift events, these nucleotides were intentionally chosen as they are natively present at amino acids 36 and 37 in the KP32 phage scaffold, resulting in a scarless junction. To bypass the presence of the VersaTile P6 junction, the full-length KP32 RBP2 (including KP32gp38 CP) was grafted instead into the K11 phage scaffold.

Following rebooting, the engineered K11 phage K11gp0043::KP32gp38 was successfully propagated on both the RBD1 (K11 capsular serotype) and RBD2 (K21 capsular serotype) host strains (Fig. 4C). These results further demonstrate that in addition to the full-length RBP1 (KP32gp37) of phage KP32 [23], full-length RBP2 (KP32gp38) can also be successfully grafted into the closely related phage scaffold K11. In addition, KP32 CP could also successfully interact with the K11 T4gp10-like branching domain.

The K11 and KP32 CP domains share 37% aa identity, which is two-fold lower than the aa identity shared between their corresponding T4gp10-like domains (64% aa identity with 100% query coverage, Protein blast). Structural comparison of the K11 and KP32 branching-docking domains confirmed a strong structural match between the T4gp10-like domains (RMSD of 0.9). However, the CP domains could not be aligned using the DALI Server due to their small size (35 aa) and were aligned using PyMOL instead, resulting in a high RMSD score (8.1; Fig. 5). Nevertheless, the low pLDDT score (< 50) in AlphaFold predictions for this region, while the rest of the RBP was predicted with high confidence, indicates that the CP domain is highly flexible. This was confirmed by a DISOPRED3 sequence analysis that predicted the first 19 and 16 amino acids of KP32gp38 and K11gp0043, respectively, as intrinsically disordered peptides potentially involved in protein-binding, further supporting this hypothesis (Additional File 2 Fig S4). This flexibility was also observed during the crystallization of KP32gp38, where the electron density for the first 34 amino acids could not be defined [41]. The flexibility of the CP domains may explain the challenges in structural predictions and the high RMSD score. Furthermore, a low RMSD score of 1.55 for amino acids 15–35, which form the small α-helix, indicates partial structural similarity, suggesting that this region may adopt a comparable function and folding pattern. Considering these factors, it is plausible that the flexibility of the CP domain facilitates adaptation to interact with different branching sites with conserved folds, such as the T4gp10-like branching domains.

Cross-swapping of RBD2 between podoviruses K11 and KP32

As a next step, the KP32 RBD1 (KP32gp37, K3 capsular serotype) and the K11 RBD1 (K11gp17, K11 capsular serotype) were cross-swapped from the RBP1 to the RBP2 position in the opposite phage scaffold (Fig. 4D-F).

For engineered KP32 phage KP32gp38_RBD2::K11gp17_RBD1 (i.e. swap of K11 RBD1 at the RBD2 position in the KP32 scaffold), a successful specificity switch from K21 to K11 was observed for RBP2, while RBP1 retained its K3 specificity (Fig. 4D). Effective phage propagation remained stable when both plated on the K3 host, with a PFU of 9.8 ± 0.42 log10(PFU/mL) when propagated on the K3 host and 9.5 ± 0.09 log10(PFU/mL) when propagated on the K11 host. Similar results were observed when plated on the K11 host. Successful integration of the chimeric RBP consisting of the native KP32 CP and the non-cognate K11 RBD1 into the KP32 phage scaffold demonstrated that the KP32 CP could also fold and function autonomously.

In contrast, the engineered K11 phage K11gp0043_RBD2::KP32gp37_RBD1, in which the chimeric RBP2 was composed of the native K11 CP and the non-cognate KP32 RBD1, did not acquire K3 when integrated into the K11 phage scaffold (Fig. 4E). This may indicate that either the K11 CP or the KP32 RBD1 is not properly delineated and loses its proper folding. Alternatively, the correct orientation of the chimeric RBP may be corrupted. However, three plaques with the expected plaque-halo phenotype were observed when this phage was propagated on the K11 host but plated on the new host of RBP2 (corresponding to KP32gp37 with K3 capsular serotype). One plaque was selected for further analysis and was propagated and plated on both RBP1 and RBP2 hosts. Now, a successful specificity switch towards K3 with an PFU of 9.3 ± 0.01 log10(PFU/mL) was observed, but only when propagated on the RBP2 host. Additionally, this switch was at the expense of the activity of the native K11 RBP1.

Sequencing analysis of the RBP gene cluster after phage propagation revealed a shortened RBP gene cluster that resulted from a recombination event between ten identical nucleotides (GTGGTTCGGT) between the RBD of K11gp17 and KP32gp37 (Additional File 2 Fig. S3). This homologous recombination event produced an engineered K11 phage with a single, chimeric RBP, referred to as engineered K11 phage K11gp17_RBD1aa364::KP32gp37_RBD1aa304 (Fig. 4F). We hypothesize that these ten-nucleotide sequences may serve as a recombination hotspot in the RBP region, facilitating the exchange of RBDs [20, 42, 43]. This further underscores that the high plasticity of RBPs plays a crucial role in host adaptation under selective pressure.

Position swapping of RBD1 and RBD2 in the same KP32 phage scaffold

The interchangeability of RBPs within the same phage scaffold was tested by swapping the position of RBP1 and RBP2 in the engineered KP32 phage KP32gp38_RBD2::K11gp17_RBD1 (i.e. RBP1 with K3 specificity and RBP2 with K11 specificity; Fig. 4D). To achieve this position swap, the K11 RBP1 (K11gp17, K11 capsular serotype) was transplanted at the RBP1 position and the chimeric RBP2 with the KP32 CP and the KP32 RBD1 (KP32gp37, K3 capsular serotype) was plugged in at the RBP2 position. The successfully rebooted phage (KP32gp37::K11gp17 and KP32gp38_RBD2::KP32gp37_RBD1) exhibited dual host specificity with K11 specificity for RBP1 and K3 specificity for RBP2, with retained propagation efficiency (Fig. 4G). The PFU results demonstrated that the position of chimeric RBPs (integrated in RBP1 or in RBP2) did not affect the phage infectivity.

Furthermore, the chimeric RBP2 was able to fold and function properly, confirming the correct delineation of the KP32gp37 RBD at aa 259 in the flexible loop region in front of the α-helix introducing the enzymatic domain. This suggests that the loss of K3 activity of RBP2 (i.e., K11 CP fused to KP32 RBP1) in engineered K11 phage K11gp0043_RBD2::KP32gp37_RBD1 (Fig. 4E) was most likely due to incompatibility with or suboptimal delineation of the K11 CP. In addition, the creation of this engineered phage further confirmed the exchangeability of the full-length RBP1 of phage K11 (K11gp17) to the KP32 phage scaffold. The successful rebooting of this advanced engineered phage highlights the high versatility and engineering amenability of the dual RBP system.

RBP grafting of more distinct RBPs did not result in a successful host range switch

The RBP of podophage KP34 (KP34gp57, Drulisvirus), belonging to a different taxonomic group but still within the Autographiviridae family, and the RBP of siphophage KP36 (KP36gp50, Webervirus) from the distinct Drexlervirdae family, were selected as phylogenetically distant donors to evaluate whether the KP32 and K11 scaffolds could accommodate heterologous RBPs at the RBP2 position. Phages from the Drulisvirus and Przondovirus do not show amino acid similarity in their respective conserved N-terminal anchor domain, which indicates co-evolution with their cognate tail assembly components [12]. Despite this divergence, KP34gp57 has previously been successfully grafted into both KP32 and K11 phage scaffolds [23, 27]. Phage KP36 was included in this study to further extend the analysis across morphotype borders, as KP36 is a siphovirus with the same capsule specificity (K63). Although KP34gp57 and KP36gp50 share only 42.45% aa similarity (94% query coverage), their C-terminal RBDs show a high structural similarity, suggesting that they have a conserved function despite sequence divergence [12, 14].

The full-length KP34gp57 RBP, previously successfully fused to the K11 anchor and grafted into the K11 phage scaffold as RBP1 [23], was now fused to the K11 and KP32 CP domains and grafted into the corresponding phage scaffolds at the RBP2 position. Similarly, a chimeric RBP2 containing the KP32 CP and the KP36gp50 RBD was constructed and grafted into the KP32 phage scaffold. This combination was only tested in the phage scaffold KP32, as the KP36gp50 RBD had previously shown no activity as RBP1 in the K11 phage scaffold [23]. All three phages were successfully rebooted and selected on the host with the native RBP1 host. However, none of the engineered phages were able to propagate on the K63 host, despite sequencing confirmation that the correct RBP gene cluster was integrated in the engineered phage genome (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Successful reciprocal RBP swapping between closely related phage scaffolds

Phage RBPs are primary determinants of host specificity, with their evolution intricately linked to their host tropism [10, 44]. Therefore, RBPs are the major target for phage host reprogramming or host expansion. Horizontal domain swapping is one of the main evolutionary forces shaping host range adaptation in Klebsiella phages [11, 12, 20, 45]. In this follow-up study to Latka et al. [23], the functional interchangeability of RBPs in Klebsiella phages that express dual host specificity was investigated with a versatile and standardized approach. Full-length RBPs or modular RBD and branching-docking domains were swapped between the closely related Klebsiella podophages KP32 and K11 at the RBP1, the RBP2 or both positions within the dual RBP system. This study confirmed (I) the reciprocal swapping of the full-length RBP1 or the C-terminal RBD1 portion in the opposite phage scaffold. Furthermore, (II) the autonomous function and folding of the KP32 CP was successfully demonstrated, as KP32 CP could successfully adopt the K11gp17 and KP32gp37 RBD, resulting in infective phage particles with a specificity switch corresponding to the newly introduced RBD. This has also been demonstrated in Dams et al. [26] for tailocins with branched RBPs. However, the successful integration of the chimeric K11gp0043 RBP (KP32 CP fused to K11gp0043 RBD2) into the KP32 phage scaffold was only confirmed by sequence analysis, as we have not yet identified a K31 host that could be infected by the wild-type K11 phage, while the protein shows K31 specificity [15]. Confirmation of transcription of the chimeric RBP2 gene can be detected by RT-qPCR (or RNA-Seq to capture the broader transcriptional landscape of the engineered phage). To further investigate whether the transcription of the gene is subject to regulation, ONT-cappable-seq could be performed. This Nanopore-based cDNA sequencing approach is particularly useful for resolving regulatory features in densely coded viral genomes [46]. In addition, mass spectrometry-based proteomics could be used to improve our understanding of viral functionality and phage-host interactions. Therefore, this method can directly confirm translation and incorporation of the chimeric RBP2 into the phage particle [47–49]. Moreover, the interchangeability of KP32 CP was demonstrated as the full-length KP32gp38 RBP2 could be successfully interacted with the K11 T4gp10-like branching domain. While we have successfully demonstrated the autonomous functionality for the KP32 CP, this has not yet been demonstrated for the K11 CP. (III) RBP1 and RBP2 in the dual RBP system could be exchanged without affecting the phage efficacy of the unmodified RBP.

The majority of phage engineering studies reported so far have primarily focused on horizontal RBP swapping in phages with a single RBP system [22, 50–52] or exchanging only RBP1 of the dual RBP system [23, 40]. Therefore, RBP swapping in the branched RBP system represents a novel approach and has till now only been demonstrated for Klebsiella (dual RBP system; [23]), in E. coli-infecting Ackermannviridae phages (multi-RBP system with four tailspikes) [53, 54] and R2 tailocins [26]. Having an unmodified RBP and a suitable host for rebooting greatly facilitates phage engineering, which otherwise faces numerous technical challenges and makes it difficult to determine whether failure is due to technical or biological reasons.

RBP2 grafting of more distinct RBP in phage scaffolds

Transplantation of RBPs from more phylogenetically distinct phages, including podovirus KP34 and siphovirus KP36, at the RBP2 position of the K11 and KP32 phage scaffolds did not result in engineered phages with an altered host range. Instead of broadening host specificity, all three engineered phages lost their RBP2 function. The observation that K63 specificity was not acquired after grafting the KP36gp50 RBD into the KP32 phage scaffold aligns with the findings of Latka et al. [23], who reported similar difficulties with K11 engineering when the chimeric KP36gp50 RBP (composed of the K11 anchor and the KP36gp50 RBD) was introduced into the K11 phage scaffold. This supports the hypothesis that siphoviral RBPs are incompatible with a podoviral scaffold, possibly due to fundamental differences in the RBP architecture.

Notably, AlphaFold2 predictions of the chimeric RBPs yielded structurally plausible models with generally high confidence scores and did not reveal obvious misfolding or incompatibilities prior to cloning (Additional File 3 Table S8). However, given AlphaFold2’s known limitations in predicting the effects of point mutations, engineered chimeras, folding stability, and conformational dynamics, such models cannot rule out subtle folding defects, reduced solubility, or functional impairment [55]. Specifically, differences in RBP organization, portal-tail interactions, or the absence of a required cognate chaperone for proper protein folding can prevent the transplanted RBP from functioning [15, 39, 50, 52]. Phages in the T7-like family (to which K11 and KP32 belong) rely on a well-coordinated system involving the adaptor protein (portal-tail connection), nozzle protein (conformation of the ejection channel during host adsorption), and the N-terminal anchor domain (anchoring to the adaptor-nozzle complex) to transition from a mature state to DNA ejection upon host recognition by the C-terminal RBD [56, 57]. The unstable interaction between the branching and docking domain may lead to an ineffective signal transmission from RBP2 to RBP1, failing to trigger the conformational changes in the tail to drive DNA ejection. The necessity of swapping additional genes besides the RBP or RBD to achieve a successful host range switch between more phylogenetically distinct phages has been reported by Ando et al. [39] and Avramucz et al. [50]. Yet, the swapping of additional genes, such as the chaperone gene, is often described when a phage RBP/RBD is grafted into a more distantly related tailocin scaffold [52, 58–62].

Further, while the full KP34gp57 RBP fused to the native K11 and KP32 anchor successfully mediated a K63 specificity switch when introduced at the RBP1 position of the K11 phage scaffold [23] and KP32 phage scaffold [27], it lost functionality when placed at the RBP2 position of either the K11 or KP32 phage scaffolds in this study. This observation may rather suggest that the fusion between the CP and the KP34gp57 RBP did not result in a functional chimeric RBP. This loss of function could be attributed to the presence of two CPs, the non-cognate CPs of K11 or KP32 and the cognate KP34 CP, instead of one. Alternatively, it cannot be excluded that the presence of the VersaTile junction disrupted the fusion of phylogenetically distinct RBP domains, leading to loss of activity.

In line with previous observations, RBPs originating from phylogenetically related phages appear to be more easily interchangeable within closely related phage scaffolds. This makes it intuitive to exchange RBPs derived from phylogenetically related Klebsiella podophages [23, 63, 64], or RBPs with similar N-terminal domains [65] to reprogram the host range. However, this theoretical constraint contrasts with the findings of our recent study in which we demonstrated that the Klebsiella phage KP32 scaffold could be successfully engineered to enable cross-genus infection in both Klebsiella and Escherichia hosts. Specifically, the KP32 phage successfully adopted the RBD of the Escherichia phage K1F (K1Fgp17) at the RBP2 position despite the lack of amino acid sequence identity between the K1Fgp17 and KP32gp38 RBP. This finding underscores the platform’s potential for generating phages with expanded host ranges and for investigating structural compatibility of modular RBP domains. In addition, the VersaTile design enables the engineering platform to easily switch to other small phage genome scaffolds from different genera or with different morphotypes, broadening the diversity of RBPs that can be investigated in future studies.

Plug-and-play engineering framework to reprogram the host range of Klebsiella phages

Overall, our results demonstrate that this optimized plug-and-play phage engineering framework is a suitable and modular approach for reprogramming the host range of Klebsiella phages or for investigating the functional role of the different modular domains in the dual RBP system. This framework comprises (I) the VersaTile technique for RBP gene cluster construction, (II) Gibson assembly for phage genome engineering, and (III) electroporation for phage rebooting. The VersaTile technique is highly suitable for recombining sequence blocks that encode modular proteins, such as those in dual RBP systems, without requiring new primer sets for each phage design, as in homology-based methods. This standardization enables rapid addition of new building blocks to the existing set and their combinatorial recombination. Conversely, Gibson assembly is most effective for assembling four large, conserved genome fragments. Finally, electroporation proved more efficient than heat shock and L-form-based methods for rebooting Klebsiella phages. Together, these components create a truly plug-and-play system that is flexible, scalable, and adaptable to any new RBP or RBD, enabling systematic and efficient phage engineering. Alternative methods for RBP swapping in the phage genome include homologous recombination [22, 51–53, 63, 66], CRISPR-Cas9-assisted editing [66, 67], bacteriophage recombineering of electroporated DNA (BRED) [50] and yeast-based gap-repair cloning [23, 39]. However, these methods generally involve the replacement of a single RBP sequence per engineering cycle, requiring iterative cloning steps to generate more complex variants. By contrast, VersaTile enables the simultaneous combinatorial assembly of multiple RBP sequences, thereby enhancing the throughput and versatility of the engineering platform. Further, structure-guided mutagenesis can be used to create libraries of phage mutants by targeted or random mutagenesis of their RBPs [68–72].

Specifically, three differences between the phage engineering strategy in this study and the strategy described by our earlier work [23] can be identified. Firstly, the RBP gene cluster was divided into four modular building blocks instead of two, maximizing the modularity principle of RBPs. With this new approach, CP and RBD2 can also be exchanged in addition to the anchor and RBD1 domains, providing additional flexibility. Although we successfully rebooted and propagated several engineered K11 and KP32 phages with reprogrammed host range, we cannot rule out that some reboot failures were caused by the presence of a VersaTile junction in the chimeric RBP sequence, as we did not test the functionality of the chimeric proteins alone in this study. This contrasts with two other studies by Latka et al., where chimeric protein activity was assessed a priori [15, 23]. Though since all chimeric proteins created in those studies were biologically functional, we anticipated a similar success rate in this work.

Secondly, the yeast-based gap-repair method for phage genome assembly was replaced by the in vitro Gibson assembly method [39]. Although the yeast-based approach was functional, it proved to be time-consuming and less robust due to the low yield of engineered phage DNA and the requirement for high transformation efficiency of the rebooting host [36].

Third, the engineered, rebooted phage was screened and selected on the specific host for the unmodified RBP, granting a higher success rate for picking up rebooted phages, even when the specificity switch is unsuccessful. Thereby, we can distinguish the reason for unsuccessful phage rebooting as either a technical failure to assemble and reboot the genome, or biological reasons that exclude a functional phage particle based on the intended design. This is relevant since phage engineering remains non-trivial and a multivariate design process prone to unsuccessful attempts. Finally, it should be acknowledged that the Klebsiella phages selected in this study have relatively small genomes (± 40 kb), which facilitates in vitro genome engineering and phage rebooting. Phages with a larger genome would probably be easier to engineer using in vivo techniques such as homologous recombination or CRISPR-Cas [40, 66, 73–75].

Vision on future applications of engineered phages

Advances in synthetic biology that enable RBP engineering in conserved viral scaffolds offer the potential to create customized antibacterials with a narrow host. This approach can be used to generate engineered phages for developing clinical phage cocktails. Furthermore, this strategy can be used in a rotary mode to anticipate on the most common specificity determinants and resistance mechanisms. Specifically, RBP diversity may mitigate resistance development at the level of the bacterial receptor (the primary resistance mechanism), while the scaffold diversity may help to overcome intracellular bacterial defence systems (the secondary resistance mechanisms). Using a conserved repertoire of scaffolds may facilitate standardized production procedures, thereby reducing one of the major bottlenecks of phage therapeutics. Importantly, proof of concept for the clinical application of engineered phages has already been demonstrated. For instance, engineered phages from which the unwanted genes were removed, were successfully used in phage cocktail therapy to treat cystic fibrosis patients who were infected with multidrug-resistant Mycobacterium strains [76, 77]. More recently, phage cocktails comprising engineered phages with an expanded or altered host range have shown promising results in preclinical models and have progressed to Phase I clinical trials [78].

Beyond therapeutic applications, phages also have the potential to be used as biocontrol agents to eliminate bacterial contaminants during various technological processes, such as in the food industry. To comply with regulatory frameworks, this application could also benefit from well-characterized, GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe)-qualified phage scaffolds [79].

Furthermore, engineered phages can be used as diagnostic tools. Reporter phages, which are generated by insertion of a reporter gene into highly specific phages, enable the rapid and specific detection of viable bacteria and real-time antibiotic susceptibility testing by linking phage replication to a measurable signal. For instance, the recombinant reporter phage rTUN1::nLuc allows rapid detection of K. pneumoniae K64 strains and simultaneous assessment of antibiotic susceptibility directly in relevant samples [80]. Likewise, the scope of such applications could be further expanded by redirecting reporter phage scaffolds towards different capsular serotypes or other receptors through RBP engineering.
