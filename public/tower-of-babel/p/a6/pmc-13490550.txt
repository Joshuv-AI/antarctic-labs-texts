A unified catalytic mechanism in bifunctional DNA glycosylases with an evolutionarily conserved aspartate-lysine dyad
Authors: Aleem Syed, Leonardo F. Serafim, Andrew S. Arvai, Irina G. Minko, Henry Y. H. Tang, Joy L. Huffman, Clifford D. Mol, Kenichi Hitomi, Altaf H. Sarker, Sudip Parikh, Chi-Lin Tsai, Albino Bacolla, David S. Shin, Richard P. Cunningham, Shigenori Iwai, Dipanjan Chowdhury, R. Stephen Lloyd, Ivaylo Ivanov, John A. Tainer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bifunctional glycosylases, OGG1 for purines and NTH1 for pyrimidines, repair oxidized DNA bases via consecutive glycosylase and AP-lyase reactions, yet their catalytic relationships and lyase activity’s biological relevance remain unresolved. Here, we solved crystal structures of archaeal and human Ogg1 and Nth1 captured in key damage-recognition and catalysis-ready states, complemented by ab initio molecular dynamics simulations of their complete reaction trajectories. We thereby define a unified catalytic mechanism for OGG1 and NTH1 conserved over three billion years, distinct from the canonical oxocarbenium-ion mechanism of monofunctional glycosylases. While divergent in their oxidized substrate recognition, their chemistry converged on ribose protonation and ring opening that precede the deglycosylation step. Acid-base catalysis mediated by a conserved aspartate-lysine dyad lowers the C–N bond cleavage barrier, while the excised 8-oxoG base in OGG1 or a conserved aspartate in NTH1 facilitates the AP-lyase reaction. Moreover, structures of human OGG1 bound to product DNA and to product DNA plus a potent small-molecule agonist F51, within the catalytic pocket, reveal that agonists accelerate enzyme turnover by promoting product release. Together, these findings clarify the catalytic logic of bifunctional glycosylases, enabling the development of chemical tools to interrogate lyase activity and therapeutics for oxidative damage in cancer and aging.

Introduction

High levels of damage to purine and pyrimidine bases arising from cellular metabolism and exogenous agents pose a major threat to genetic integrity1–4. To counter this threat, all domains of life rely on the evolutionarily conserved base excision repair (BER) pathway5. BER enzymes remove damaged (oxidized or alkylated) or inappropriate bases, such as uracil arising from misincorporation or cytosine deamination3,6. Since these BER enzymes are conserved from microbes to humans7–9, insights into their fundamental mechanisms benefit from comparative structural and mechanistic analyses.

BER is initiated by DNA glycosylases through a nucleotide-flipping mechanism that extrudes damaged bases from the DNA double helix into the enzyme active site for recognition10,11. Oxidized DNA base lesions are typically excised by bifunctional glycosylase/lyases: glycosylase activity generates an AP (apurinic/apyrimidinic) site that is then incised by AP-lyase activity via either β−elimination, leaving a 3′-phospho–α,β−unsaturated aldehyde (3′-PUA) or β,δ−elimination, leaving a 3′-phosphate12. Following further processing of these 3′ modifications by apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease (APE1) or polynucleotide kinase-phosphatase (PNKP), respectively, templated resynthesis by either DNA polymerase β or δ replaces the missing nucleotides, and DNA ligase I or III seals the nicked DNA to complete BER13,14.

Oxidation of purine or pyrimidine bases, arising from endogenous metabolism, is exacerbated by oxidative stress, inflammation, radiation, smoking, and environmental toxins such as arsenic15–17, and generates diverse lesions18,19. Hydroxyl radicals can add to the C5-C6 double bond of thymine, producing thymine glycol (Tg). The resulting saturated pyrimidine loses ring planarity and has been shown to block repair and replicative polymerases in vitro20–24. Hydroxyl radicals can also add to guanine C4, C5, or C8 positions25. The C8-OH• adducts convert to the major stable product, 8-oxo-Guanine (8-oxoG), which can mispair with adenine via its Hoogsteen face, leading to GC→TA transversion mutations upon replication26,27. Most bifunctional purine/pyrimidine DNA glycosylases that catalyze β−elimination reactions belong to the helix-hairpin-helix (HhH) structural family7–9. Prominent members include endonuclease III-like protein 1 (NTH1), which excises oxidized pyrimidine lesions such as Tg, and 8-oxoG DNA glycosylase (OGG1), which excises the prevalent oxidized purine lesions (8-oxoG) from the genome28–37.

The first DNA glycosylase crystal structure, solved over 30 years ago, was that of the bacterial glycosylase/lyase NTH (also known as endonuclease III)33; yet its lesion recognition and reaction mechanism have remained elusive until now. A structure-based general reaction pathway for bifunctional purine/pyrimidine DNA glycosylase/lyases31,35 has been inferred largely from OGG1 studies38–43, which implied a cofactor role for excised base using 8-oxoG analogs44,45, plus comparative structural analyses of NEIL1–3 that belong to the helix-two-turn-helix (H2TH) family46–49. An intriguing study using a designer OGG1 double mutant K249C/C253K (which most likely altered the native mechanism) suggested analogy to monofunctional glycosylases, with direct nucleophilic attack on the glycosidic bond by an aspartic acid-activated water molecule50. Although NTH homologs share conserved catalytic residues and overall architecture with OGG1, their diverse substrate recognition pockets raise questions about mechanistic conservation8. Trapped intermediate structures and biochemical data from bacterial pyrimidine N-glycosylase/AP-lyases EcNth1 argue against product-assisted catalysis35. Furthermore, computational studies have focused primarily on the glycosylase step, leaving the AP-lyase reaction mechanism unresolved for both purine and pyrimidine bifunctional glycosylases. However, the biological relevance of the lyase activity is underscored by its improved contributions relative to APE1 for nucleosome-bound DNA51. Moreover, evidence for the importance of the DNA glycosylase/lyase activity for chromatin protection also comes from its damage surveillance of oxidation-susceptible sites to limit instability and cancer that likely originated ∼500 million years ago during the buildup of free atmospheric oxygen52.

Based on the evidence presented above, a key unanswered question therefore concerns the catalytic relationships connecting or separating the glycosylase and lyase chemical mechanisms. Monofunctional glycosylases, such as uracil DNA glycosylase12,53, are thought to cleave the glycosidic bond via a stepwise mechanism that generates a high-energy, short-lived oxocarbenium intermediate or proceed via oxocarbenium-like predominantly dissociative transition states. In this scenario, the excision yields an anionic departing base (a poor leaving group) and a positively charged oxocarbenium cation, both necessitating strong electrostatic stabilization in the transition state. Early models for bifunctional glycosylases proposed that an amine (typically from a lysine or proline) activates water or directly attacks the oxocarbenium ion to form an iminium crosslink, after the base departs. More recent studies support a “ribose-protonated” mechanism specific to bifunctional enzymes38,54–56. In this mechanism, a neutral Lys attacks the ribose C1′, opening the deoxyribose ring between C1′ and O4′ prior to glycosidic bond scission, with ring opening requiring O4′ protonation by a catalytic aspartic acid; the glycosidic bond remains intact until the iminium crosslink is fully formed.

In this work, to elucidate how prototypical purine and pyrimidine glycosylase/lyase OGG1 and NTH1 enzymes recognize and remove damaged bases, including the relationship between glycosylase and lyase steps, we examined the structural and computational basis of their complete catalytic mechanisms. Leveraging evolutionary conservation, we determined crystal structures of Archaeoglobus fulgidus (Af) Ogg1 and Nth1 crystallized in key catalytic states. Using catalysis-ready intermediate crystal structures of AfOgg1 bound to 8-oxoG and AfNth1 bound to Tg substrates, combined with ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) and metadynamics (MTD) simulations in a QM/MM framework, we mapped both glycosylase and lyase reactions leading to the β−elimination product. Despite distinct damage recognition, our integrated analyses uncovered functional equivalences, analogous steps, and comparable energy barriers for the individual steps of purine and pyrimidine bifunctional catalysis carried out by OGG1 and NTH1, respectively. We also captured the β−elimination product (3′-PUA) in product-bound structures of AfOgg1 and Homo sapiens (Hs) OGG1 crystal structures. Finally, by solving HsOGG1 product-bound structures in the presence of small-molecule agonists, we show that agonists function by mimicking the excised base, thereby validating a product-assisted catalytic mechanism. Collectively, these results establish a unified understanding of OGG and NTH family bifunctional glycosylase/lyase catalysis with implications for developing chemical agonists and inhibitors.

Results

AfOgg1 and AfNth1 HhH enzymes as robust systems to characterize complete catalytic mechanisms of purine/pyrimidine bifunctional glycosylase/lyases

To elucidate the structural basis and optimum trajectory of the complete reaction mechanism for removal of oxidized purine and pyrimidine bases by bifunctional glycosylases, we utilized hyperthermophilic AfOgg1 and AfNth1 enzymes to trap multiple functional states. AfOgg1 and AfNth1 structures typify HhH superfamily DNA repair enzymes7. In both, the classical two-domain HhH fold, first identified in EcNth134, comprises a conserved six-helix barrel domain and a less-conserved four-helix bundle domain (Fig. 1a–d). They share characteristic structural motifs (canonical HhH33, pseudo-HhH57, and a gap-filling loop involved in substrate maneuvering) and key residues (for damaged-base recognition and catalysis) conserved in their human counterparts (Fig. 1a–d, Supplementary Fig. 1). Beyond the core catalytic domains, AfOgg1 contains N- and C-terminal extensions (Fig. 1a) distinct from the N-terminal domain in HsOGG1 (Fig. 1b) or other HhH enzymes7,31. The AfOgg1 N-terminus is compact, forming a long helix followed by an antiparallel β-sheet that pack against a short C-terminal extension inserted into the interface between the two core HhH domains (Fig. 1). This packing is stabilized by numerous hydrophobic residues within the unique AfOgg1 terminus that interacts with the core HhH structure, exemplifying a tertiary structure whose primary role is to stabilize the buried C-terminus (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The buried C-terminal lysine (K198) is further stabilized by interactions of its main-chain carboxyl with H126, R129 and N130, and its side-chain amino group with E41 and Q60. This architecture contrasts with HsOGG1, which has a large N-terminal domain that inserts a loop into the enzyme active site (Fig. 1b) and forms a hydrogen bond between the glycine backbone carbonyl in this loop and the protonated N7 of 8-oxoG31.

Among the two enzyme classes, the NTH bifunctional glycosylases that act on oxidized pyrimidine substrates are structurally less characterized, limiting comprehensive comparisons and unified mechanistic insights. Building on the first crystal structure of Nth1 from E. coli (EcNth1)33, the AlphaFold2 (AF2)-predicted DNA-free and our Tg-DNA-bound AfNth1 crystal structures show a similar HhH fold containing two core domains and a C-terminal 4Fe-4S cluster (FCL), as also seen in a recently solved DNA-free human NTHL1 (HsNTH1) structure (Fig. 1c, d)36. In addition to these structural motifs that facilitate DNA binding, AfNth1 and EcNth1 harbor a characteristic A-B loop connecting the two major domains, with a different length than HsNTH1 (Fig. 1c, d and Supplementary Fig. 1b). Although this flexible loop is not resolved in the HsNTH1 crystal structure, it enables HsNTH1 to transition between the open and closed conformations essential for activity36. AfNth1 also contains key conserved residues (e.g., K122 (K212 in HsNTH1), D139 (D231), D46 (D136), H141 (H233), and N177 (N271)) that participate in catalysis and damaged-base binding in the active site. This conservation enabled us to crystallize AfNth1 bound to Tg-containing DNA, providing structural insights into how the oxidized pyrimidine is recognized and bound in the Nth1 active site.

Lesion recognition and binding in AfOgg1 and AfNth1 co-crystal structures with their cognate substrates

To define lesion recognition and binding, we determined five informative crystal structures of AfOgg1 and AfNth1 with their respective cognate DNA substrates, capturing reaction intermediates. For AfOgg1, we solved three structures: (i) two inactive mutants (K122S or D140N) bound to dsDNA containing a central 8-oxoG:C base pair (Fig. 1e, f and Supplementary Table 1), which adopt a similar global conformation (Supplementary Fig. 2b); and (ii) a WT selenomethionine (Se-Met) labeled DNA-free structure used for phasing in molecular replacement. AfOgg1D140N and AfOgg1K122S lack glycosylase activity, consistent with the captured intermediate structures. The K122S is assumed to be completely lyase deficient, given the key role it plays in the lyase reaction (the equivalent HsOGG1K249Q is shown to be inactive on an AP substrate58). In contrast, based on HsOGG1D268N data59, AfOgg1D140N may retain partial lyase activity. For AfNth1, we determined two AfNth1-DNA complex structures: an inactive mutant (K122A) enzyme bound to a Tg:A-containing DNA substrate (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Table 2), and a WT-DNA complex crystallized at low temperature that captured a pre-intermediate state (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Table 2). The AfNth1K122A mutant is deficient for both glycosylase and lyase activity. In all intermediate or pre-intermediate complexes, the intact 8-oxoG or Tg nucleotide is flipped out of the DNA helix through the major groove and are bound within a damaged-base recognition pocket of the active sites in the anti configuration (Fig. 1e–h).

Double flipping of 8-oxoG and its opposite nucleotide in lesion recognition

A striking feature of the AfOgg1D140N-DNA complex is the double flipping of both the damaged nucleotide and its opposing nucleotide out of the DNA helix (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2c). The resulting void is filled by residues from both the pseudo-HhH motif (R79 and F80) and the gap-filling loop (N50), stabilizing the DNA. This double-flipping may underlie the ability of AfOgg1 to recognize and cleave 8-oxoG mispaired with adenine60. Although AfOgg1 is less efficient on 8-oxoG:A than on 8-oxoG:C, the activity is substantial and appears to be unique to archaeal enzymes, as HsOGG1 is inactive on 8-oxoG:A mispairs31. The lack of contacts with the opposite (“estranged”) base likely contributes to AfOgg1’s reduced specificity for 8-oxoG:C, in contrast to HsOGG1, which prefers C > T > G»A opposite to 8-oxoG due to R154 and R204 hydrogen bonds to cytosine N3 and O2 that cannot accommodate adenine31; accordingly, the R154H mutation yields a promutagenic enzyme that cleaves 8-oxoG opposite A as well as C31. The estranged cytosine in the AfOgg1K122S and AfOgg1D140N structures does not overlay well (Fig. 2a), and in AfOgg1K122S:DNA the estranged C forms two hydrogen bonds with R79, resembling HsOGG1. In the AfOgg1-DNA structures, the enzyme makes multiple contacts with the damaged strand backbone, but almost none with the undamaged complementary strand (Fig. 2b), similar to HsOGG1 and other glycosylases31,61. Recognition and cleavage of 8-oxoG base by both human and archaeal Ogg1 do not rely on specific contacts to the C8-O8 keto group; instead, a hydrogen bond forms between now-protonated N7 of the 8-oxoG and a conserved acceptor (Fig. 2c). In HsOGG1, this acceptor is the backbone carbonyl oxygen of G42 in the N-terminal loop31, whereas in AfOgg1, it is the C-terminal carboxyl of K198, which participates in a charged triad (COO¯−R129−D185) engaging N7, O6, N1, and N2 of 8-oxoG. The C-terminal carboxylate, stabilized by H126 and a charge-neutralizing interaction between K198 and E41, reinforces the catalytic role of this buried residue.

Additional key interactions with 8-oxoG are mediated by W189, which stacks with the base, and H142, which forms a hydrogen bond with the 5′-phosphate. Relative to DNA-free AfOgg1, these residues undergo substantial rearrangement to form the 8-oxoG binding pocket (Fig. 2d). H142 is conserved in HsOGG1, whereas W189 is replaced by F319, which provides similar π−π stacking. The concerted movement of this His-aromatic pair appears critical for 8-oxoG binding. The catalytic residue K122 also reorients towards the active site upon 8-oxoG binding, whereas D140, which hydrogen bonds to the ribose, undergoes minimal displacement between the DNA-free and DNA-bound states (Fig. 2d). In the AfOgg1D140N, K122 fails to reorient despite proper 8-oxoG binding, indicating that D140 may also promote repositioning of K122 to assemble a catalysis-ready active site in AfOgg1.

Pre-intermediate and intermediate states and lesion recognition in AfNth1–TgDNA-bound structures

We determined two AfNth1 structures in complex with Tg:A-containing DNA: a WT complex crystallized at low temperature capturing a pre-intermediate state, and a catalytically inactive AfNth1K122A complex representing an intermediate state (Fig. 1g, h). The two states align well outside the DNA-binding regions but display pronounced differences in the DNA and in DNA-interacting elements (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). The pre-intermediate is more open, while the intermediate state is more compact. In the pre-intermediate state, the damaged nucleotide is flipped out, but not yet fully inserted in the active site, representing a pre-catalytic state.

In this pre-intermediate state, Tg is positioned at the mouth of the active site cleft between the two HhH core domains and makes direct, damage-specific interactions with the conserved D46, highlighting its critical role in discriminating damaged from undamaged bases (Supplementary Fig. 3c). The previously solved EcNth1 structures lacked a bound oxidized base35, whereas Tg-bound structures of other glycosylases (e.g., NEIL1) show no direct Tg-specific interactions24,47. This has led to the hypothesis that Tg is recognized before it is bound in the active site47, or through an indirect mechanism55, unlike OGG1, which showed a direct damaged-base-specific interaction. Our AfNth1 pre-intermediate structure instead reveals direct recognition of the modified C5-C6 of Tg by a pyrimidine glycosylase. Supporting this observation, mutation of the equivalent D136 in HsNTH1 severely impaired Tg excision, confirming its role in lesion detection (Supplementary Fig. 4).

In the intermediate state, AfNth1 flips Tg out of the helix and induces a kink in the DNA. The FCL, pseudo-HhH, and HhH motifs all contribute to localizing the kink at the lesion. The void created by nucleotide extrusion is filled by residues from the B-C (gap-filling) loop (R43) and pseudo-HhH loop (F83). R43 contacts the adenine opposite Tg via a water molecule, but the estranged adenine remains in the helix, unlike the fully flipped opposite base in AfOgg1. Additionally, AfNth1 makes extensive contacts with the backbone of both DNA strands (Fig. 2f). The pseudo-HhH and HhH motif form a “pincher” grasp on the DNA, as proposed for MutY57. Specifically, residues G118-S123 of the HhH motif engage with the DNA backbone 3′ to the lesion, while the FCL inserts into the minor groove 5′ to the lesion, and residues F83-V86 of the pseudo-HhH motif contact the undamaged complementary strand.

Our AfNthK122A-DNA intermediate structure provides a detailed view of how Tg is bound in the active site of pyrimidine bifunctional glycosylase. The flipped Tg interacts with the protein residues Y130 and N177 (Fig. 2g). Relative to the pre-intermediate state, the damaged strand shifts deeper into the pocket and Tg rotates to contact N177 (Fig. 2h). Specifically, N177 forms hydrogen bonds with Tg O2 and N3, while Y130 interacts with O4. N177 is conserved in HsNTH1, and mutation of the equivalent residue (N271A) in HsNTH1 resulted in ~3–7-fold reduced Tg excision, but did not affect the AP-site processing, indicating a specific role in Tg binding (Supplementary Fig. 4). Y130 is not conserved; HsNTH1 instead has V220 at the structurally equivalent position. In both AfNth1 and HsNTH1, the A-B loop caps the active site from the C5-C6 face of Tg. Given the longer, flexible A-B loop in HsNTH1, we hypothesized that a loop residue might contact Tg O4 in the human enzyme. An AF3 model of HsNTH1 bound to abasic dsDNA (since the Tg is not available in AF3), aligned with the AfNth1K122A intermediate state structure, suggests that T107 from the A-B loop in HsNTH1 may contact Tg O4 (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Consistent with the other glycosylase Tg-bound structures24,47 and in our AfNth1K122A intermediate structure, there are no direct protein contacts to the modified base; instead, conserved S41 interacts with the hydroxyl group at Tg C6 via a water molecule. Finally, as in AfOgg1, conserved H141 contacts the 5′ phosphate of the lesion, and catalytic D139 hydrogen bonds with the ribose and likely steers K122 to assemble a catalysis-ready active site.

Complete reaction paths for purine/pyrimidine bifunctional N-glycosylases catalysis

By combining experimental structures of catalysis-ready intermediate states and AIMD, we simulated the complete reaction mechanism for N-glycosylase/AP-lyases featuring a conserved Asp–Lys catalytic dyad that excise a major class of oxidative purine (8-oxoG) or pyrimidine (Tg) lesions from genomic DNA.

Enzyme substrate complex

We first optimized the Michaelis complexes for AfNth1 and AfOgg1 by classical MD and QM/MM. Despite their common reactions, AfNth1’s active site is more solvent exposed, whereas the AfOgg1 site contains the large hydrophobic residue W189 that provide π−π stacking and shield the 8-oxoG lesion from solvent. In both optimized Michaelis complexes, protonated aspartate, D139 in AfNth1 or D140 in AfOgg1, hydrogen bonds with the ribose oxygen of the lesion, positioning the catalytic Asp residue as the proton donor for the first activation step of the glycosylase reaction, consistent with other glycosylases31,38,44,59,62. Notably, AfOgg1D140N fails to rotate the K122 side chain towards the active site, implicating D140 in both proton shuttling and positioning K122 for the nucleophilic attack.

Ribose protonation

The glycosylase reaction comprises two chemical events: (1) ribose ring opening and (2) base excision. Ribose opening occurs synchronously with ribose oxygen protonation by D139AfNth1 or D140AfOgg1, which initiates the glycosylase reaction. Ring opening proceeds as a concerted nucleophilic substitution, wherein the nucleophilic K122 of AfNth1 (or AfOgg1) attacks the C1′ carbon atom of the ribose ring, immediately after C1′-O4′ bond dissociation, through transition states T1AfNth1 and T1AfOgg1 with similar activation energies, ~10 kcal/mol (Fig. 3). Yet, the ribose oxygen atom in AfNth1 can form an added water-mediated hydrogen bond with the D46 residue (Fig. 3a) that facilitates cleavage of the scissile C1′-O4′ bond, thus promoting a more compact transition state compared to AfOgg1. To evaluate the possibility of direct glycosidic bond cleavage54,63, we explicitly compared it against our proposed pathway involving ribose protonation first54. AIMD metadynamics simulations of both AfNth1 and AfOgg1 revealed that the direct glycolytic cleavage route consistently exhibits free energy barriers ∼20 kcal/mol higher than the ribose ring-opening mechanism. Thus, base excision in both purine and pyrimidine bifunctional glycosylases requires initial ribose protonation and ring opening. Another widespread assumption in previous mechanistic work has been that the glycosylase reaction could proceed through the formation of a stable oxocarbenium intermediate53. In all our AIMD simulations, we failed to observe such intermediates. Moreover, starting from an oxocarbenium intermediate, we performed geometry optimizations using the same BLYP-D3 level of theory as the AIMD simulations. The resulting intermediate was >30 kcal/mol higher in energy than the respective enzyme-substrate complex (RAfNth1 or RAfOgg1), which effectively precludes discrete oxocarbenium ion involvement in catalysis. Instead, our computational analyses favor a mechanism in which the K122 amine group first attacks the deoxyribose ring and opens it between C1′ and O4′. The ring-opening step necessitates O4′ protonation. In this case, the glycosidic bond remains intact until the iminium crosslink is fully formed, and the ordering of catalytic events precludes discrete oxocarbenium ion formation. Unlike the canonical mechanism for monofunctional glycosylases where the base departs first, our mechanism for bifunctional glycosylases shows that ring-opening precedes base excision, thereby avoiding formation of a discrete, high-energy oxocarbenium intermediate.

Severing the glycosidic bond

Ring opening is followed by glycosidic C-N bond cleavage and formation of a Schiff base (I3). How enzymes facilitate the breakage of the exceptionally strong glycosidic bond has been a conundrum for the field of DNA repair. Strikingly, non-enzymatic C-N bond cleavage imposes a vast energetic penalty of ~30 kcal/mol64. There are multiple strategies that bifunctional glycosylases such as NTH1 and OGG1 may employ to lower this energetic penalty and achieve efficient catalysis. One strategy is the formation of a stable double bond (C=N) in the Schiff base, which is indeed the driving force of this chemical transformation. This process is achieved through deprotonation of K122. In the AfNth1 enzyme, this is accomplished by D139 serving as a general base and by the carbonyl group (O2) of the Tg base. By contrast, in the AfOgg1 enzyme, analogous proton transfer is mediated by an active site water molecule proximal to the general base E123. In both enzymes, the dissociation of the N-H bond involves comparable free energy barriers, ~4 kcal/mol for AfNth1 and ~6 kcal/mol for AfOgg1 (Fig. 3b). The resulting protonated Schiff base (I2) is exergonic in comparison with the respective ribose protonated intermediates (I1AfNth1 and I1AfOgg1) for both enzymes. Despite the overall unified mechanism, there are a few differences between the two enzymes. Due to the opposite orientations of the proton-abstracting residues in the two proteins, the newly formed C-N partial double bonds have distinct geometric isomerism. In AfNth1, formation of a trans isomer is favored, in which the polar lysine hydrogen atom points away from D139. In AfOgg1, the product is a cis isomer with the equivalent hydrogen atom oriented towards D140.

The next step is the dissociation of the glycosidic bond after the C-N bond to the lesioned base has been significantly weakened by (1) ribose ring opening; (2) proton shuttling; and (3) Schiff base partial C=N bond formation. Nonetheless, in both enzymes, C-N bond dissociation is the rate-limiting step of the glycosylase reaction, yielding free energy barriers of ~18.4 and ~18.9 kcal/mol for AfNth1 and AfOgg1, from their respective intermediates I2 (Fig. 3b). In both systems, water molecules mediate protonation of the damaged bases, which additionally stabilizes the transition states. In AfNth1, D46 participates in the proton transfer, which occurs at the N1 atom of the Tg base. Correspondingly, in AfOgg1, E123 acts as the general acid protonating the O8 atom of the 8-oxoG base. Consistent with these roles, AfOgg1 E123A and HsNTH1 D136A (D46 equivalent) mutants are inactive on their respective substrates (Supplementary Fig. 4, 5).

Our computational analyses are support and extend previously reported comparable deglycosylation reaction rates for OGG1 and NTH136,50. In both AfNth1 and AfOgg1, water molecules mediate protonation of the damaged base and contribute to the stabilization of both the transition state and the product complex. The overall deglycosylation reaction is thermodynamically more favorable in AfNth1 (Supplementary Fig. 6, 7). This greater exergonicity arises primarily from differences in active-site solvation: the AfNth1 active site is more solvent-exposed, allowing more water molecules to participate and form an extended hydrogen-bonding network that strongly stabilizes the open-ribose conformation and lowers the energy of intermediate I1AfNth1. In contrast, AfOgg1 features a more enclosed active site with a hydrophobic residue (W189) shielding the base and limiting water access. This structural enclosure hinders escape of the excised 8-oxoG, trapping it in the active site. This both reduces solvation-driven stabilization and suppresses the entropic gain upon product release.

Schiff base hydrolysis

Similar to the glycosylase step, the AP-lyase activity of bifunctional glycosylases involves two separate chemical events: (1) Schiff base hydrolysis; and (2) β-elimination. In the first step, a water molecule carries out a nucleophilic attack onto the C1′ atom of the Schiff base. The negatively charged residues D46 and E123 (in AfNth1 and AfOgg1, respectively) abstract the proton from the water molecule to generate the nucleophilic hydroxyl ion (Fig. 4). This concerted step is associated with free energy barriers of ~7 kcal/mol for both enzymes (Fig. 4a). In the second step, the unstable carbinolamine intermediate collapses by a proton transfer promoted by residue D139 (D140 in AfOgg1). The deprotonation event also triggers the dissociation of the C-N bond, which releases K122 from the newly formed aldehyde group at the end of the ribose (I5) (Fig. 4b). At this stage, the enzyme/DNA complex is held together only by intermolecular forces, which allows dissociation of the glycosylases from product DNA, a process often facilitated by downstream BER factors such as APE165,66.

β-elimination

The β-elimination step is the final chemical event of the AP-lyase reaction and the rate-limiting step of the entire bifunctional catalysis. This reaction occurs in one single step with the synchronous elimination of one of the H2’ hydrogen atoms of C2′ and the dissociation of the C3′-O3′ bond that connects the ribose and the phosphate group. Due to the opposite orientation of these chemical bonds in I5, two catalytic residues (general base and general acid) are necessary for the elimination to occur. In AfNth1, we observe a water-mediated proton abstraction from C2′ to the D46 residue. Concurrently, D139 acts as a general acid, protonating the oxygen atom of the phosphate group and assisting its departure from the ribose. In AfOgg1, the excised 8-oxoG can act as a general base in a water-mediated proton abstraction from C2′. Although E123 is the final proton acceptor, the 8-oxoG base serves as a cofactor facilitating the shuttling of the proton by lowering the energy of the transition state associated with the reaction. Excluding 8-oxoG from the simulation increases the free energy of this step by ~5 kcal/mol, confirming its catalytic contribution (Supplementary Fig. 8). Since E123 is not conserved in the human enzyme, we tested whether this key feature could be transplanted into HsOGG1. However, both V250D and V250E mutants were inactive on 8-oxoG-DNA (Supplementary Fig. 9). This underscores that, although archaeal and human enzymes share the same overall mechanism, additional non-conserved active-site features modulate catalysis and cannot be recapitulated by single-residue substitutions.

The dissociation of the C3′-O3′ bond is assisted by the protonated D140, which donates a proton to the negatively charged oxygen atom of the phosphate group. In both enzymes, this concerted step is associated with a free energy barrier of ~21 kcal/mol (Fig. 4c). The final product is the 3′-PUA, which is exergonic by about 10 kcal/mol with respect to R. In contrast to the glycosylase step, where both chemical events target the C1′ atom and their order strongly influences energetics, the AP-lyase step involves events at two separate sites (Schiff-base hydrolysis at C1′ and β-elimination at C3′). This separation allows flexibility in the sequence of chemical events; however, the much faster rate of hydrolysis ensures it precedes β-elimination in practice, with negligible impact on the overall energetics (Supplementary Fig. 10). The calculated energy barriers are consistent with the experimentally observed trends in reaction rates, within the systematic computational errors expected for DFT calculations: NTH1 processes Tg nominally ~6–10 times faster than OGG1 processes 8-oxoG in bifunctional glycosylase reaction (Supplementary Figs. 4, 8, 9). This is striking given the distinct chemistry of their departing nucleobases. The reason why similar rates arise despite dissimilar departing nucleobases is an intriguing question. Gas-phase QM studies of uncatalyzed glycosidic bond cleavage show substantial variability in activation barriers67. The study also revealed strong dependence on the protonation state, exact protonation site, and chemical modifications to the base, although differences were predicted to diminish in condensed phase or enzymatic environments. For 8-oxoG and Tg, uncatalyzed barriers are relatively similar (135.2 vs 143.8 kJ/mol; ΔΔG ≈ 2.05 kcal/mol). Thus, we cannot exclude that we selected substrates with similar intrinsic barriers. Nevertheless, the sensitivity of reaction rates to protonation and hydrogen bonding suggests glycosylases may have evolved to constrain cleavage rates within a narrow, physiologically relevant range. Therefore, it is indeed interesting to consider that NTH and OGG operate with vastly different efficiencies expressly to compensate for the intrinsic dissimilarity between purine and pyrimidine substrates.

We also obtained the WT HsOGG1:8-oxoG-DNA product-bound structure at 2 Å (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 3). Strong density in the SA composite omit map confirms a product state in which HsOGG1 cleaved 8-oxoG and catalyzed β-elimination, generating a ssDNA break (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig 12b). The 5′-phosphate is stabilized by V269 and a water-mediated contact with K249. Clear density for 3′-PUA in the polder omit map supports our computational and prior biochemical data. The 3′-PUA binding is stabilized by S147, N150, I152 and K249 (Fig. 5e). The overall protein fold resembles the 8-oxoG-bound intermediate state31, but the active site is more open and similar to the DNA-free state: the H270-F319 pair reverts to its apo configuration, and no density is present for the excised 8-oxoG, indicating its release (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 12c).

Our QM/MM analyses indicate that excised 8-oxoG acts as a cofactor during the AP-lyase step of the AfOgg1, supporting and extending previous observations44. This finding supports that OGG1 activity could be enhanced by small-molecule agonists with therapeutic potential in inflammation and aging68–70. To investigate this further, we co-crystallized WT HsOGG1:DNA with agonist F01 (compound F in reference68), a proposed allosteric activator of HsOGG170. The 2.5 Å HsOGG1:DNA + F01 structure resembles the product-bound state but, critically, exhibits clear density for 8-oxoG retained in the active site (Fig. 5g-h and Supplementary Table 3). The H270-F319 pair is disrupted, with F319 now contacting 8-oxoG, while H270 is displaced (Fig. 5i). Although F01 density is not observed, the presence of bound 8-oxoG in the active site and the accompanying subtle rearrangements support an allosteric HsOGG1 activation, as mediated by the excised 8-oxoG in the active site. The 3′-PUA product has shifted to accommodate 8-oxoG (Supplementary Fig. 12d). This active-site flexibility suggests that a more potent agonist could replace the loosely bound 8-oxoG and further enhance enzymatic turnover. Consistent with this interpretation, five different HsOGG1:DNA datasets collected under identical conditions in the absence of F01 showed negative Fo–Fc density when 8-oxoG was modeled, indicating that the excised base is not retained without the agonist (Supplementary Fig. 13).

We next examined F5171, a slightly more potent HsOGG1 agonist (Supplementary Fig. 14a–c). In contrast to F01, F51 density is clearly visible in the active site, at the position occupied by 8-oxoG in the HsOGG1:DNA + F01 complex, indicating that F51 binds directly in the catalytic pocket rather than allosterically (Supplementary Fig. 14d-f). The density is consistent with a mixture of states: part of F51 is modeled too close to the 3′-PUA, implying that 3′-PUA either moves away or is absent when F51 is bound in the active site, and additional density, though weak, can be fit with a δ-elimination product whose occupancy is similar to that of F51. F51 binding also displaces K249 from the active site, weakening product binding (Supplementary Fig. 14e). This finding supports and extends the mechanism of action reported for the HsOGG1 agonist TH10785 (compound C in reference68), which enhances activity by binding in the active site and promoting δ-elimination and product release (Supplementary Fig. 14f)69. Overall, our product-bound structures support a model in which agonists stimulate HsOGG1 catalysis by enhancing the AP-lyase step and accelerating substrate turnover, either by stabilizing excised 8-oxoG in the active site or by occupying the active site to promote product release.

Discussion

Oxidized purines and pyrimidines are major forms of DNA damage across all domains of life, with repair initiation and biological outcomes dictated by damage-specific mono- and bifunctional DNA glycosylases. Bifunctional glycosylases fall primarily into two structural superfamilies: the helix–hairpin–helix (HhH) and the Fpg/Nei superfamilies. Yet a unified catalytic mechanism to guide biological understanding and therapeutic targeting has remained elusive for the HhH superfamily. Here, we establish a convergent, structure-based catalytic mechanism for two prototypic HhH bifunctional glycosylases: OGG1 and NTH1. Despite excising structurally divergent substrates, both enzymes rely on a conserved Asp–Lys dyad to execute their catalytic function. This contrasts with monofunctional HhH glycosylases, which lack the active-site lysine and proceed via a distinct oxocarbenium intermediate8,12,53. Ultimately, the mechanistic unity between OGG1 and NTH1 resolves a longstanding biochemical enigma, suggesting a conserved catalytic paradigm for bifunctional HhH glycosylases harboring this conserved Asp–Lys dyad.

Purine/pyrimidine glycosylase/lyases must first discriminate between damaged and undamaged bases. AfOgg1 belongs to the OGG2 superfamily, whereas HsOGG1 belongs to the OGG1 superfamily, with key catalytic residues and functional structural motifs conserved72. A major difference between OGG1 and OGG2 is how they distinguish 8-oxoG from G in the active site: HsOGG1 uses a damage-specific interaction between 8-oxoG N7 and the backbone carbonyl of Gly42, whereas AfOgg1 uses the extreme C-terminal carboxylate. Structurally, OGG2 family members lack the N-terminal A-domain present in OGG1. In HsOGG1, this A-domain harbors Gly42 and shields its interaction with 8-oxoG. Sequence alignment of reviewed OGG2 entries in UniProt shows that AfOgg1 is one of only two OGG2 enzymes with an additional N-terminal anti-parallel β-sheet that shields damage-specific interaction between 8-oxoG and the C-terminus, analogous to the A-domain in HsOGG1. AF2-predicted structures suggest that in AfOgg1 (and in Aquifex aeolicus Ogg) this N-terminal element appears necessary to stabilize C-terminal lesion recognition, whereas in the other OGG2 members the C-terminus is rigidified by a stable C-terminal hairpin, facilitated by the residue at the fourth position from the C-terminus.

For Tg processing enzymes, prior Tg-DNA structures showed no direct contacts between the enzymes and the oxidized thymine24,47,73, suggesting Tg might be recognized before binding in the active site or via an indirect mechanism47,55. Our AfNth1 pre-intermediate structure reveals the direct interactions between D46 and Tg hydroxyl groups at the C5-C6 positions, providing a mechanistic basis for lesion discrimination. Mutation of the equivalent D136 in HsNTH1 severely impaired Tg excision, confirming its role in lesion detection. In the AfNth1K122A intermediate, Tg is bound without direct protein contacts, with only a water-mediated interaction between Tg C6-OH and a conserved S41, consistent with earlier Tg-bound inactive mutant structures24,47,73. By strengthening and providing missing structural data on both OGG and NTH glycosylases, we establish a foundation for a comprehensive, comparative mechanistic analysis of these bifunctional glycosylases. Notably, the conservation of a specific Asp-Lys dyad mechanism across Archaea and Humans suggests that it represents an ancient and optimized solution for coupling base excision to strand incision within a single active site, preserved through evolutionary selection.

Our integrated structural and computational interrogation of purine and pyrimidine glycosylase/lyases reveals both conserved and divergent features. HsOGG1 selectively excises 8-oxoG most efficiently opposite C, whereas AfOgg1 excises 8-oxoG even when paired with other bases, enabled by double flip of both 8-oxoG and its opposite nucleotide. To the best of our knowledge, this provides the structural evidence of double flipping of 8-oxoG and its opposite nucleotide for an 8-oxoG DNA glycosylase. In contrast, recent AGOG structures show that double flipping can happen from the damaged strand74, proposed to explain relaxed opposite-base selectivity. However, prior studies on HsOGG1 and OGG2 enzymes indicate that the opposite-base selectivity is primarily determined by the number and nature of contacts with the estranged base31,75. HsOGG1 is highly selective for 8-oxoG opposite C due to multiple contacts made by R154 with C. This specificity is relaxed by the R154H mutation, which allows efficient cleavage of opposite A as well as C31. Double flipping of the damaged and estranged base has also been observed for other DNA glycosylases, including endonuclease IV76, uracil DNA-glycosylase77, MutY78,79, and ALKBH280.

Our findings advance the mechanistic understanding of both OGG1 and NTH glycosylase/lyases31,35, specifically informing on the differences in base binding, revealing distinct biochemical roles of excised bases for OGG131,35, and providing insights into the previously poorly characterized AP-lyase activity38–41. By integrating crystal structures and QM/MM simulations, we mapped the full reaction trajectories for both enzyme classes. We find that glycosidic C-N bond cleavage in Ogg1 and Nth1 does not proceed via an oxacarbenium ion intermediate, which is highly energetically disfavored (ΔG‡ ≈ 35 kcal/mol). Instead, both enzymes use a ribose ring-opening mechanism in which a conserved Asp–Lys pair mediates protonation of the ribose, lowering the energy barrier to ΔG‡ ≈ 18 kcal/mol. Thus, by exerting tight control over the protonation states of the attacking Lys and the ribose oxygen, the enzymes facilitate an iminium crosslink formation, which in turn lowers the barrier for glycosidic bond scission. This mechanistic dyad is supported by several structural observations: (1) AfOgg1D140N intermediate structure shows Asp controls the positioning of Lys; (2) Asp and Lys are in close proximity allowing proton sharing; and (3) proximity of Asp to the ribose oxygen atom enables hydrogen bonding with the nucleotide. These observations imply that for catalysis to occur, the catalytic Lys must remain neutral to initiate nucleophilic attack, meaning the proton is transferred to (or resides on) the Asp. This “ribose-protonated” mechanism is further supported by a recent HsOGG1 crystal structure that directly captures a ring–opened ribose intermediate in a pH-sensitive K249H mutant under conditions favoring Asp protonation and concerted nucleophilic attack by a histidine-bound water81. However, because this mutant proceeds via hydrolysis rather than the nucleophilic attack by Lys, direct capture of the intermediate in WT OGG1 or NTH1 would be valuable to further corroborate this step in the native catalytic trajectory. Nonetheless, this structure provides strong suggestive evidence that ribose ring opening represents a viable mechanistic scenario. We also identified additional catalytic residues (E123 in AfOgg1 and D46 in AfNth1) that play important roles in catalysis. In AfOgg1, E123 serves as the final proton acceptor, although 8-oxoG itself acts as a cofactor, facilitating proton shuttling and lowering the transition state energy. This effect is not seen in AfNth1. Our simulations are in close alignment with biochemical findings supporting product-assisted catalysis for purine, but not pyrimidine glycosylases, such as EcNth131,35.

Despite the clear structural and biochemical evidence from our work and numerous prior reports demonstrating AP-lyase activity of HsOGG1 in vitro, its biological relevance has been a matter of debate. OGG1 is often viewed as primarily monofunctional50, with APE1 processing the resulting abasic site. This view is supported by the slower AP-lyase than glycosylase activity of OGG182, consistent with our computed barriers, and by the ability of APE1 to stimulate OGG1 turnover by product release83,84. APE1 cleavage at an abasic site generates a 5′-deoxyribose phosphate (5′-dRP) that is normally processed by Polβ 5′-dRP-lyase activity, which is essential for the short-patch BER85. In the absence of Polβ, however, repair can proceed through long-patch BER mediated by other DNA polymerases that use the 3′-OH group created by APE1 after removing 3′-PUA, an AP-lyase product of OGG1. Thus, the lyase product (3′-PUA) can channel repair toward high-fidelity polymerases, particularly at the replication fork. Nevertheless, the weak AP-lyase activity of OGG1 in vivo presents a therapeutic opportunity to enhance this step using small molecules that mimic or augment base-assisted catalysis. Our product-bound HsOGG1 structure with an allosteric agonist F01 shows additional active-site electron density for bound 8-oxoG compared to the agonist-free product-bound state, supporting a cofactor-stabilizing mechanism, wherein allosteric agonists increase the residence time of the cleaved base, thereby promoting the AP-lyase reaction and increasing the overall turnover. In contrast, our HsOGG1 structure with F51, a slightly more potent OGG1 agonist, shows clear density for F51 directly in the active site at the position previously occupied by 8-oxoG, indicating catalytic-site rather than allosteric binding. The structural and biochemical data indicate that F51 promotes δ-elimination in the active site, and F51 binding also displaces K249, weakening product binding. Thus, our agonist structures support a model in which HsOGG1 agonists stimulate catalysis by enhancing the AP-lyase step and accelerating turnover, either by stabilizing excised 8-oxoG in the active site or by directly occupying the active site to promote product release.

An integrated OGG-NTH glycosylase/lyase catalytic mechanism has broad biological implications, as oxidized DNA damage and immune responses are interconnected86, and OGG1 is linked to inflammation, oxygen metabolism and obesity87. Oxidative damage also has epigenetic roles, influencing transcription via BER88. Just as conservation of APE1 active site arginine (R177) from E. coli to humans over three billion years underscored its central role in BER66, the analogous evolutionary conservation of catalytic lysine in both glycosylase and lyase steps of OGG and NTH argues for the biological significance of the lyase activity and supports the integrated mechanism reported here. This has potentially broad therapeutic relevance, as ionizing radiation and proton therapy generate oxidative DNA damage that recruits OGG1 and NTH1 capable of excising 8-oxoG and Tg, respectively, as part of a resistance pathway89. Multiple cancer-associated mutations in OGG1 and NTH1 affecting catalytic residues (Supplementary Data 1, 2) and oxidative stress is tightly linked to cancer and aging90,91, suggesting catalysis-inspired chemical modulators of OGG1 and NTH1 may inform biology and medicine.

Our agonist-bound HsOGG1 product structures provide sufficient active-site resolution in the presence of cleaved DNA, together with mechanistic insights from archaeal enzymes, to guide development of agonists and antagonists using compact Goldilocks libraries for virtual screens or experimental fragment libraries92–94. Importantly, we define crystal structures of intermediate states for 8-oxoG and Tg-bound glycosylase/lyases, enabling elucidation of the entire bifunctional reaction from a single active site for both purine- and pyrimidine-recognizing enzymes. The AfNth1 pre-intermediate structure shows how Nth1 discriminates oxidized lesions from undamaged thymine before forming the intermediate state, and our simulations reveal similar key catalytic steps in OGG1 and NTH1, supporting co-evolution of bifunctional glycosylase/lyases for efficient catalysis in nucleosomes and damage surveillance of oxidation-prone chromatin sites51,52. Together, our product-bound structures of OGG1 with and without agonist highlight distinct roles of the excised base and small-molecule-mediated stimulation of OGG1 AP-lyase activity. Collectively, these findings define conserved and variable features of BER catalysis for oxidized base removal, with broad potential implications for cell biology and emerging precision therapies in cancer, aging, inflammation, neurodegeneration, and other diseases linked to genome stability and oxidative metabolism.

Methods

Protein purification, crystallization, and data collection

The WT and mutant AfOgg1 were cloned and expressed from the pMAL-c2X plasmid and purified using the MBP-tag60. The affinity tag from the purified protein was cleaved, and the protein was dialyzed against a low-salt buffer (20 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 20 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT). Protein was then further purified using MonoS cation exchange chromatography using a linear gradient of 20 mM- 1 M NaCl, and protein-containing fractions were collected. For crystallization experiments, purified WT, mutants, and selenomethionine (Se-Met)-substituted AfOgg1 were concentrated to ~20 mg/mL in 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl. Crystals of DNA-free WT Se-Met-substituted AfOgg1 enzyme were grown by vapor diffusion from reservoir solutions containing 75% ammonium sulfate (saturated), 100 mM imidazole/malate pH 5.0. For cryoprotection, 20% ethylene glycol was added to the mother liquor. X-ray diffraction data were collected from a single crystal at beamline 9-2 of the Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Laboratory (SSRL).

For the AfOgg1-DNA complexes, AfOgg1WT (Se-Met) and AfOgg1K122S-mutant (native) enzymes were mixed in a 1:1 ratio with the blunt-ended, duplex 8-oxoG-containing DNA (5′-TAGAGTC(8-oxoG)ACCTGCA-3′ (Midland Certified Reagent Corp, Midland, TX)), in which the complementary strand had a cytosine opposite 8-oxoG and incubated on ice for 30 min. Similarly, AfOgg1D140N-mutant (native) was mixed with 2 mM blunt-ended duplex 8-oxoG-containing DNA (5′-GCGTCCA(8-oxoG)GTCTACC-3′ (Midland Certified Reagent Corp, Midland, TX)) in which the complementary strand has a cytosine opposite 8-oxoG. Co-crystals were grown by hanging drop vapor diffusion from reservoir solutions containing 20–30% PEG4000, 100 mM MES buffer, pH 6.5, and 5% potassium permanganate (saturated). For cryoprotection, 20% ethylene glycol was added to the mother liquor. X-ray diffraction data were collected at beamline 11-1 of SSRL.

AfNth1K122A mutant coding sequence was cloned and expressed from the pET24a (Novogen) vector and purified from induced E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells95. For the Tg-containing substrate, a 13-mer 5′-ACGCGA(Tg)ACGCCA-3′ sequence was synthesized using the phosphoramidite building block for the oxidized base. Tg-containing strands were annealed to a complementary sequence 5′-TTGGCGTATCGCG-3′, leaving a single nt overhang on both ends and adenine as the opposite base to the Tg. For the AfNth1WT, the enzyme coding sequence was cloned and expressed from the pET (His-GST-TEV, Addgene#29655) vector. For the AfNth1-DNA complexes, AfNth1WT or AfNth1K122A mutant enzymes were mixed in 1:1 ratio with the Tg-containing duplex and incubated on ice for 30 min. Since we obtained a pre-intermediate state in the case of the WT enzyme, we also purified the WT enzyme from an anaerobic Coy chamber (oxygen level <0.1 ppm), which made no difference with regard to crystal formation occurring only at low temperature (4 °C). We concluded that the product-bound state of AfNth1 was too flexible to crystallize. Crystals of pre-intermediate and intermediate states of AfNth1-DNA complexes were obtained by hanging drop vapor diffusion from reservoir solutions containing 20–30% PEG8000, 100 mM imidazole/malonate (pH 4.5–5.5), 5 mM DTT, and 10% glycerol. Data for AfNth1WT and AfNth1K122A complexes were collected at beamlines 12-2 at SSRL and 5.0.1 at the Advanced Light Source (ALS), respectively.

HsOGG1 (4-319 aa) coding sequence was cloned into a pET (His-GST-TEV, Addgene#29655)-based plasmid. His-GST was cleaved from the purified protein overnight in the cold room. Protein was then further purified using gel-filtration chromatography on Superdex200 Increase 10/300 GL (Cytiva). Protein-containing fractions were concentrated to 10 mg/mL in the gel-filtration buffer (40 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP). HsOGG1 crystals in a DNA-free state were obtained by hanging drop vapor diffusion from a reservoir solution containing 100 mM sodium citrate (pH 5.0), 22% PEG6000, 5% ammonium sulfate (saturated). 25% ethylene glycol was used as a cryoprotectant for DNA-free HsOGG1 crystals. The diffraction data were collected at the 12-2 beamline at SSRL. For the HsOGG1-DNA complex, HsOGG1 enzyme was mixed with an 8-oxoG-containing blunt-ended duplex in a 1:1 ratio and incubated on ice for 30 min. HsOGG1:DNA crystals were obtained by hanging drop vapor diffusion from reservoir solutions containing 50 mM MES (pH 6.0), 16–23% PEG6000, with 25% ethylene glycol was used as cryoprotectant for HsOGG1-DNA crystals. The diffraction data were collected at the beamline 8.3.1 at the ALS. For the HsOGG1:DNA complex crystals in the presence of agonists F01/F51 (F01 was synthesized and characterized by the OHSU Medicinal Chemistry Core71, and F51 was purchased from US Biologicals (cat# 164979), the same DNA substrate was used except that 4.5 mM of F01 or F51 were added to the crystallization conditions. Crystals were cryoprotected as described before, and the diffraction data were collected at the 12-2 beamline at SSRL.

Structure determination, refinement, and analysis

The AfOgg1 structure was solved by SAD as follows: diffraction data on SeMet-incorporated enzyme were collected at the selenium anomalous peak (0.9791 Å) at SSRL. Diffraction data were processed using XDS96, while phasing and model building were performed with phenix.autosol97 and phenix.autobuild98, respectively. Structure refinement was performed in phenix.refine99 with iterative manual fitting using Coot100. For the AfOgg1D140N and AfOgg1K122S mutant complexes with DNA, the structures were solved by molecular replacement using the refined AfOgg1WT DNA-free structure. Diffraction data were processed using XDS (for AfOgg1D140N mutant) or the autoPROC pipeline96,101–104 (for AfOgg1K122S mutant), and molecular replacement was performed with Phaser105 as implemented in the Phenix suite106. Structure refinement was done in phenix.refine, with iterative manual model building and fitting in Coot100. Ligand restraints for 8-oxoG and molybdate were generated using phenix.ready_set. The structure of AfOgg1WT-product bound state was solved using the intermediate (AfOgg1K122S:DNA complex) structure for the molecular replacement, with the lesion omitted in the model. The ligand restraints for the 3′-PUA product were generated using eLBOW utility in the Phenix suite107. The PUA product was built into the difference density map in the active site and iterative structure refinement using phenix.refine and manual fitting in Coot were performed as described above. Data collection and refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

AfNth1K122A mutant structure was solved by molecular replacement AmoRe108. A theoretical AfNth1 starting model was built based on EcEndoIII, and the MR search model was prepared by overlaying the theoretical model on Ec and BsEndoIII structures. Initially, refinement was performed with CNS109 and manual fitting with XFIT110. The model was further improved iteratively using phenix.refine suite and manual fitting in Coot. AfNth1WT:DNA complex structure was solved by using AfNth1K122A:DNA complex as an MR search model using Phaser as implemented in the Phenix suite. The ligand restraints for the Tg lesion (CTG) were generated using the eLBOW utility in the Phenix suite. The Tg lesion from the search model was omitted and rebuilt into the difference density. Structure refinement was done in phenix.refine, with iterative manual model building and fitting in Coot. Data collection and refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

DNA-free HsOGG1 structure was solved by molecular replacement using the previously published HsOGG1 structure (PDB:5AN4111) as a search model in Phaser as implemented in the Phenix suite106. Structure refinement was performed in phenix.refine, with iterative manual model building and fitting in Coot. The DNA-free HsOGG1 model was used to solve the structures of HsOGG1:DNA in the presence or absence of F01 agonist. DNA sequence and ligands in the active site were built according to the density. Ligand restraints for excised base (OXG), F51, β-elimination product (3′-PUA), and the δ-elimination product (DEP) were generated using the eLBOW utility in the Phenix suite. Structure refinement was performed in phenix.refine, with iterative manual model building and fitting in Coot. For both HsOGG1:DNA structures in the presence or absence of F01 agonist, we solved multiple structures to analyze the flexibility in the active site region, but only one representative dataset for each condition was deposited in the PDB. Data collection and refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

Mutagenesis and protein purification for enzymatic activity assays

AfOgg1 (UniProt#O29876), AfNth1 (UniProt#O28581), HsOGG1 (UniProt#O15527, residues 12–327) and HsNTH1 (UniProt#P78549, residues 83–312) coding sequences were cloned in pET (His-GST-TEV, Addgene#29655)-based expression plasmid. Mutations were introduced in these plasmids using site-directed mutagenesis by polymerase chain reaction.

HsOGG1 WT and mutants were expressed in RosettaTM cells and purified by TALON affinity chromatography. His-GST was cleaved from the purified proteins overnight at 4 °C, and the tag and TEV protease were removed by repassing the mixture over TALON resin. Proteins were further purified by gel-filtration chromatography on a Superdex200 increase 10/300. Protein-containing fractions were concentrated to 15.5 mg/mL (WT), 17.3 mg/mL (V250D), and 12.4 mg/mL (V250E) in gel-filtration buffer (40 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP), as determined by NanoDrop.

HsNTH1 WT and mutants were similarly expressed in RosettaTM cells. After affinity purification, the His-GST was cleaved overnight at 4 °C, and the tag and TEV protease were removed by repassing the mixture over TALON resin. Flow-through and low imidazole (10 mM) wash fractions containing HsNTH1 were pooled and concentrated to 8.8 mg/mL (WT), 11.8 mg/mL (D136A), and 12.2 mg/mL (N271A) in dialysis buffer (25 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM β-ME), as determined by NanoDrop.

AfOgg1 WT and E123A mutant were expressed and purified as described for HsOGG1. After gel-filtration chromatography on Superdex200 increase 10/300, protein fractions were concentrated in 40 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP to 3 mg/mL (WT) and 0.57 mg/mL (E123A) as determined by NanoDrop.

AfNth1 WT was expressed in RosettaTM cells and purified by affinity chromatography followed by overnight His-GST cleavage at 4 °C and gel-filtration chromatography on Superdex200 Increase 10/300 GL. Protein-containing fractions were pooled and concentrated to 12.5 mg/mL in gel-filtration buffer (40 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM β-ME) as measured by BCA assay.

Fluorescence-based DNA glycosylase activity assays

DNA glycosylase activities were assayed using double-stranded oligodeoxynucleotides conjugated with a TAMRA fluorophore at the 5′ terminus of the lesion-containing strand and Black Hole Quencher 2 (BHQ2) at the 3′ terminus of the complementary strand. The TAMRA-labeled lesion-containing oligodeoxynucleotides were 17-mers and included the following sequences: (1) 5′-TAMRA-TCACC(8-oxoG)TCGTACGACTC-3′; (2) 5′-TAMRA-TCACCT(Tg)CGTACGACTC-3′; and (3) uracil-containing oligodeoxynucleotide 5′-TAMRA-TCACCUTCGTACGACTC-3′ used for generation of AP site-containing substrates. The complementary 3′-BHQ2-conjugated 17-mers were designed to contain a cytosine opposite 8-oxoG or the AP site for assays of AfOgg1 and HsOGG1 or an adenine opposite Tg or the AP site for assays of AfNth1 and HsNTH1. The 8-oxoG was synthetically incorporated into a 17-mer oligo112. All other oligodeoxynucleotides were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies. For preparation of double-stranded DNA substrates, lesion-containing oligodeoxynucleotides (1 µM) were combined with complementary oligodeoxynucleotides (1.2 µM) in glycosylase reaction buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), 100 mM KCl, and 0.01% (v/v) Tween-20), heated at 90 °C for 2 min, and slowly cooled to 4 °C. AP sites were generated by incubating U-containing oligodeoxynucleotides (100 nM) with 0.5 U/µl uracil DNA glycosylase (New England Biolabs Inc.) for 30 min at 37 °C in glycosylase reaction buffer supplemented with 100 µg/ml bovine serum albumin (BSA).

HsNTH1, AfNth1, and HsOGG1 glycosylase activities were measured using 50 nM DNA substrate in glycosylase reaction buffer containing 100 µg/ml BSA, in a reaction volume of 20 µl. DNA substrates were aliquoted into a 384-well black flat-bottom microplate, and reactions were initiated by addition of enzyme dilutions using a multichannel pipette. Enzyme concentrations of 10, 100 and 1000 nM were used, with the latter two concentrations corresponding to single-turnover conditions. Fluorescence was monitored for 1 hour in a TECAN INFINITE M NANO instrument using a 525 nm (9 nm bandwidth) excitation filter and a 598 nm (20 nm bandwidth) emission filter, with relative fluorescence signal recorded for 60 min every 2 min at 37 °C. The initial rates of reactions were determined by fitting the linear portion of the reaction progress curve to a linear function using either Excel or Prism.

Compared to the other enzymes, AfOgg1 exhibited a pronounced lag phase in initiating the glycosylase reaction at 37 °C. We previously reported AfOgg1 glycosylase activity at 50 °C using gel-based assays60. However, our current fluorescence-based assay is not compatible with reactions at 50 °C; therefore, we measured the glycosylase activity of AfOgg1 WT and the E123A mutant at 42 °C instead.

Gel-based DNA glycosylase activity assays

Oligonucleotides containing 8-oxoG were 5′-end-labeled with [γ-32P]ATP (Revvity, Cat# BLU502A250UC) using T4 polynucleotide kinase (ThermoFisher, Cat# EK0031). Unincorporated labeled nucleotides were removed by MicroSpin G-25 columns (Cytiva, Cat# 27532501). Labeled oligonucleotides were annealed with 1.5-fold excess unlabeled complementary oligonucleotide in 10 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.8), 1 mM EDTA (HE), and 300 mM NaCl by heating at 90 °C for 3 min followed by gradual cooling to room temperature. The oligonucleotide sequences used were 5′-TAGAGTC(8-oxoG)ACCTGCA-3′ and the complementary strand 5′-TGCAGGTCGACTCTA-3′. The reactions were carried out in a reaction mixture (10 μl) that contained 26 nM HsOGG1 and 10 nM 32P-labeled oligonucleotide substrate with 2.5, 5, or 10 μM F51 in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 75 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min and terminated by the addition of an equal volume of gel loading buffer (95% deionized formamide, 20 mM EDTA, 0.02% bromophenol blue, 0.02% xylene cyanol) followed by heating at 90 °C for 3 min. Reaction products were resolved by electrophoresis on a 16% denaturing polyacrylamide gel containing 8 M urea in 89 mM Tris-HCl, 89 mM boric acid and 2 mM EDTA (pH 8.0). Experiments were performed in three independent replicates.

AlphaFold modeling

The AF3 server (https://golgi.sandbox.google.com/)113 was used to predict the model of HsNTH1:DNA. The protein sequence for HsNTH1 (UniProt accession # P78549) and a 13-mer duplex DNA sequence (ACGCCAT*ACGCCA, where the T* is an abasic site and paired with an A in the complementary strand) were used as input with default run parameters. AlphaFold2 model of the DNA-free structure of AfNth1 was generated using a locally installed ColabFold on a GPU machine114,115. For the prediction, the protein sequence for AfNth1 (UniProt accession# O28581) was used as input for alphafold2_multimer_v3 with default run parameters. Resulting models were deposited to the ModelArchive database (www.modelarchive.org).

Classical molecular dynamics

The classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed with the PMEMD module from the Amber22 software package116,117. The AMBER ff14SB force field was employed for the protein, while the OL15 force field was employed for the DNA. The parameters for the nonstandard nucleotides thymine glycol (Tg) and 8-oxoguanine (8-oxoG) were developed using Antechamber. The charges and electrostatic potentials (ESP) were computed at the HF/6-31G(d,p) level of theory, after geometry optimization at the B3LYP/6-31G(d,p) level of theory using Gaussian 16 software118. The enzyme-substrate complex was then placed in a cubic box of 100x100x100 Å3 dimensions. The shortest distance from the edge of the box to the surface of the complex was not shorter than 10 Å. The TIP3P water model was used as the solvent, and Na+ and Cl- ions were added to neutralize the total charge of the system and to achieve a concentration of 0.154 M. More details about the topology of the system are available in Supplementary Table 4.

After the initial minimization, the system was heated up to 300 K over 100 ns at constant volume (NVT), while imposing positional restraints of 100 kcal/(mol Å2) on the heavy atoms. Subsequently, restraints were slowly removed, and to bridge the crystal structure, model building, and ab initio QM/MM simulations, we introduced a 200-ns equilibration stage (Supplementary Fig. 15), performed with classical MD using the isothermal-isobaric ensemble (NPT). The temperature control (300 K) was performed by a Langevin thermostat with a collision frequency of 1 ps−1, and the pressure control (1 atm) was accomplished by a Monte Carlo barostat. The SHAKE algorithm was used to constrain the bonds involving hydrogen atoms, and the particle mesh Ewald method was used to compute the electrostatic interactions, which for both Coulombic and Van der Waals interactions, a 10 Å cutoff distance was maintained. The trajectories were computed with a time step of 1 fs. Cluster analysis was utilized to obtain the most representative structure from three independent simulations.

Quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) molecular dynamics

The QM/MM Born-Oppenheimer MD simulations were performed using the CP2K code119, which combines the QM program QUICKSTEP and the MM driver FIST. The electrostatic coupling between the QM and MM regions was computed using a real-space multigrid technique. The QM region of the system was simulated in a cubic box with sides of 20 Å. For the AfNth1:DNA complex, the side chains of the amino acids D46, K122, Y130, D139, and N177, as well as the nucleotide Tg, were incorporated in the QM layer. In the AfOgg1:DNA complex, the side chains of residues K122, E123, H126, R129, D140, and D185, together with the main chain (C-terminus) of K198 and the nucleotide 8-oxoG, comprise the QM layer. After completion of the MD simulations, water molecules remaining within hydrogen-bonding distance of the nucleotide and the active-site residues defining the QM region were systematically identified and incorporated into the QM model. This procedure resulted in the inclusion of four water molecules in AfNth1 and two water molecules in AfOgg1.

The QM region was treated at the BLYP-D3 level using a dual basis set of Gaussian and plane-waves (GPW). We employed a double zeta (MOLOPT) basis set along with an auxiliary PW basis set with a density cutoff of 320 Ry and Goedecker-Teter-Hutter (GTH) pseudopotentials. The remaining protein, DNA, ions, and solvent atoms were modeled in the MM part of the system, which was described using the same AMBER ff14SB /OL15 force fields parameters employed as in the classical MD simulations. The simulations were performed in a NVT ensemble (300 K), employing a velocity rescaling thermostat. A time step of 0.5 fs was employed during the 50 ps of simulations. Cluster analysis was utilized to obtain the most representative structure from three independent simulations. MD simulation reliability and reproducibility checklist is provided as Supplementary Table 5.

QM/MM metadynamics

The free energy surfaces for each chemical reaction were obtained using metadynamics (MTD) simulations as implemented in the CP2K code. Each MTD simulation was performed utilizing a single collective variable (CV). CV1 is the difference in the distance between the NK atom of the lysine residue and the C1′ atom of the ribose and the distance (length) of the O4′-C1′ bond in the ribose. CV2 is the addition of the distances (lengths) of the NK-HK bond in the lysine residue and the C1′-N1 bond in the nucleotide. CV3 is the difference in the distance between the OW atom of the water molecule and the C1′ atom of the ribose and the distance (length) of the OW-HW bond in the water molecule. CV4 is the difference in the distance between the NK atom of the lysine residue and the C1′ atom of the ribose and the distance of the HW atom (from the OH group bound to the ribose) and the OD atom of the aspartic acid residue. CV5 is the difference in the distance between the H2 atom of the ribose and the OW atom of the water molecule and the distance (length) of the C3′-O3 bond in the phosphate group. Gaussian hills with a height of 0.5 – 1 kcal/mol and a width of 0.1–0.2 Å were placed in the CV space every 100 fs. Quadratic walls were used to restrict the exploration of the free energy surface around the reactive region. The simulation was considered complete after the re-crossing event from the reactants to the products and back occurred.
