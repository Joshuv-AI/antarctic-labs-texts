Visualizing stigma and HIV service engagement: a participatory approach using causal loop diagrams
Authors: Meibin Chen, Stefan Baral, Sarah M. Murray, Samuel M. Jenness, Irah L. Lucas, Travis Sanchez, Kaitlyn Atkins, Carrie Lyons, Yue Yu, Kristen Alicea-Jorgensen, Kalai C. Robinson, Takeru Igusa, Amrita Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHIV stigma continues to undermine efforts to reduce HIV incidence and improve care outcomes in the United States, particularly among communities disproportionately impacted by the epidemic. While stigma is a known barrier to care, how it operates across HIV prevention, testing, and treatment remains difficult to characterize within complex service systems. This study employed a participatory systems science approach to map these dynamics across the HIV care continuum.MethodsWe conducted three virtual group model building sessions (10–15 participants per session) with stakeholders from city and state health departments and community-based organizations across Ending the HIV Epidemic priority jurisdictions to develop causal loop diagrams (CLDs). Literature-informed base diagrams for HIV prevention, testing, and treatment were iteratively expanded during facilitated discussions to identify key factors, causal relationships, and feedback loops. Diagrams were then refined and validated with academic experts; feedback loops were synthesized into core system domains and integrated into a comprehensive CLD.ResultsThe resulting CLDs highlighted interrelated feedback loops across three key domains: (1) personal health attitudes and care navigation, (2) provision of quality services, and (3) community influences on HIV-related health and care. Anticipated and internalized stigma were found to weaken social support and personal resilience, while enacted stigma from providers reduced care quality and compromised health data. Community-level stigma was described as highly context-dependent – reinforced by social isolation or reduced through visibility and inclusive norms in others. Overall, stigma was traced in pathways across individual, social, and structural subsystems.ConclusionsParticipatory systems methods can help visualize how socio-behavioral determinants, such as HIV stigma, dynamically shape engagement across HIV prevention, testing, and treatment. CLDs offer an intuitive framework for identifying leverage points for stigma mitigation, which can inform the design of multi-level interventions to support progress towards EHE goals.Trial registrationClinical trial number: not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28414-w.

Background

Despite advancements in care and prevention, HIV remains a threat to public health in the United States. Over 1.1 million people are currently living with HIV, and more than 39,000 new cases were diagnosed in 2023 [1]. In response to this persistent epidemic, the US Department of Health and Human Services proposed the Ending the HIV epidemic (EHE) initiative in 2019 to prioritize reducing the numbers of incident HIV infections by 90% within ten years. EHE is centered around four key pillars of testing, treatment, prevention, and outbreak detection and response [2]. Despite considerable progress in serving people at risk for and living with HIV during the first phase of EHE, barriers still exist in achieving the goals of the EHE initiative. Among these barriers, stigma, specifically HIV stigma, has been shown to persist as an incompletely understood impediment to engagement in HIV prevention and care services [3–8].

Broadly, stigma can be defined as the negative labeling and marginalization of individuals due to a particular attribute or trait, occurring within an unequal power context [9]. For people at risk for and living with HIV, stigma takes on multiple dimensions, encompassing negative perceptions of HIV itself with prejudices tied to disproportionately affected racial, ethnic, and sexual identities [8, 10]. This reflects intersectionality, the recognition that individuals hold multiple identities that interact to shape experiences of social and structural inequalities [11]. For example, Black gay and bisexual men may face racialized assumptions that pathologize their sexuality and presume HIV infection, while transgender women of color may experience compounded stigma at the intersection of gender identity, personal histories and behaviors, and HIV status [12, 13]. Theories including the Minority Stress Model and fundamental cause theory have been used to frame how co-occurrence of HIV stigma with identity-based stigma, such as homophobia, transphobia, and racism, within these multiple circumstances have been shown to have cascading effects on health outcomes [7, 14]. Hatzenbuehler (2013) highlights how stigmatized characteristics are associated with a broad range of factors from housing, social relationships, psychological and behavioral responses, healthcare treatment, and health – underscoring the importance of social and structural context [7]. Building on this, Turan et al. (2017) proposed a conceptual framework describing how specific types of stigma, i.e. internalized, anticipated, perceived, and enacted stigma, lead to measurable effects on individual engagement in HIV care and related health outcomes through mechanisms like interpersonal factors (disclosure, social support, and other social dynamics), psychological resources (the tools and personal identities used for coping), mental health (disclosure, anxiety, and other conditions), and stress processes (chronic stress) [15, 16]. These individual-level processes operate within a context shaped by both intersectional and structural stigmas.

Despite this progress, there is still need for a more precise understanding of how stigma permeates through and intersects with individual (e.g., knowledge, attitudes, behaviors), network (e.g., social support, social networks), and structural (e.g., access to care, policy environments) risks, uniquely experienced by individuals [17, 18]. These challenges are particularly pronounced among communities disproportionately affected by HIV, including gay men and other men who have sex with men (MSM), and transgender populations [19–22]. As stated by Hatzenbuehler, to fully understand stigma’s impacts on health, researchers “must take into account all stigmatizing circumstances that may be operating” [7]. The knowledge gap that currently exists represents a limitation in how to estimate the benefits of different stigma-related strategies and design effective interventions. Developing a comprehensive understanding of how stigma interacts with HIV prevention, testing, and treatment processes is critical for pinpointing precise areas to target for stigma mitigation and support interventions, as well as for informing the development of relevant social policies.

While limitations in traditional public health methodology restrict the use of a straightforward approach to fill this gap, systems science, an interdisciplinary field with a focus on modeling complex systems, is increasingly being used in public health [23]. Tools within the field have been shown to be useful for implementation studies because of their ability to convey dynamic change, investigate causal pathways, and clarify cascading effects through complex feedback mechanisms [24–28]. Across applications, systems science investigations begin with causal loop diagrams (CLDs) to build visual representations of the directional relationships and interactions between system elements [20]. In the context of stigma, CLDs can provide a visual framework for illustrating the complex interrelationships between stigma, mental health, and engagement in HIV services—highlighting how specific factors influence and are influenced by one another, and how these dynamic relationships evolve over time. One of the central methods to build CLDs is through participatory group modeling [29]. The design of CLDs generally involves key partners responsible for implementing relevant EHE interventions, which is appealing for ensuring that developed models are directly relevant, and therefore applicable, to key issues experienced by local partners and community members in implementation [23, 25, 29–31]

While CLDs have been used in building system dynamics models that depict HIV prevention and care processes and outcomes, the focus on behavior and stigma in system dynamics model building has been limited [32–36]. The objective of this paper is to describe the participatory process of developing CLDs to represent HIV service engagement, shaped by stigma, as understood by key local stakeholders. The paper also explains how CLDs offer an important perspective to understanding the complex system of HIV stigma by identifying feedback loops, potential trends, and possible intervening points for increased uptake of HIV prevention, testing, and treatment.

Methods

Overview of causal loop diagramming

We begin by defining the key components of CLDs. “Factors” are variables that compose the system depicted in the CLD. When a change in a factor triggers a change in another factor, this depicted causal relationship is represented with an arrow pointing in the direction of causality. A positive causality, where an increase in one factor leads to an increase in a downstream factor, is represented with a solid arrow. A negative causality, where an increase in one factor leads to a decrease in a downstream factor, is represented with a dashed arrow. The sign of the causality can be called its “polarity.” In our usage in the results, a direct effect is when a factor is connected to an arrow directly, while an indirect effect is when a factor is separated from another factor by at most two arrows. Feedback loops occur when an upstream factor is affected by a downstream factor through a connected sequence of arrows. A reinforcing loop occurs when an initial increase in a factor leads to an additional increase of the same factor after propagation through the feedback cycle, leading to an amplifying effect. This is in contrast with a balancing loop, which occurs when an initial increase in a factor is counteracted after propagation through the feedback cycle, leading to dampened effect that tends to bring the factor back to its original state [37].

Development of CLDs can be split into two categories: ex-post development and real-time development. In ex-post development, thematic analysis and purposive text analysis are methods used to extract information from primary and secondary sources of data to create CLDs. In real-time development, group model building brings together stakeholders and experts to develop the CLD together [37]. A participatory-based group model building approach for developing CLDs uses a development process that empowers and engages stakeholders – in the case of our study, direct implementers – to create localized visual representations of care pathways, leading to a more accurate depiction of the system and increases the likelihood of successfully adopting findings [25, 29, 31, 37]. The following sections describe our approach to participatory group modeling in detail.

Group model building sessions for mapping stigma in HIV services

We mapped the influence of stigma in HIV service engagement through three group model building sessions. This study was determined to be non-human subjects research by the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health Institutional Review Board (IRB# 00007006). The three sessions, occurring in November 2023, February 2024, and July 2024, involved participants from city health departments and community-based organizations working across six EHE priority jurisdictions including Wayne County, Michigan; East Baton Rouge and Orleans Parish, Louisiana; King County, Washington; Harris County, Texas. One participating jurisdiction requested not to be identified in study materials. Initial meetings with health departments and community-based organizations were conducted to inform relevant stakeholders and potential participants of the study. Subsequently, recruitment emails were sent to attendees of those initial meetings to request participation in the focus group discussions (FGDs). Potential participants who were interested in participating indicated their availability, and discussions were scheduled to maximize attendance. Participation was voluntary, and participants were not compensated for attending the FGDs. Each session consisted of 10–15 participants and was conducted via Zoom using Miro as a diagramming tool [38].

In preparation for the first two focus groups, three simple base diagrams were drafted for each HIV prevention, testing, and treatment, outlining example paths to seeking care. The base diagrams served as a starting point for discussion during the sessions and were developed based on a review of literature on facilitators and barriers of the three pillars and iterated upon with consensus among academic experts [39–42]. Each diagram consisted of around 10–12 factors and followed a similar structure consisting of a factor related to willingness to engage in services, a factor related to actual engagement in services, and a factor downstream of receipt of care. Each diagram also included at least one facilitator to care, one barrier to care, and one stigma factor, such as enacted healthcare stigma. The diagrams can be seen in Fig. 1. An introduction to systems thinking and CLDs was provided to participants as a slideshow presentation that included a review of key concepts such as factors and relationships, polarity, and feedback loops. A guide was developed using Scriptapedia, an online tool containing a repository of standard guides for group model building [40], which served as a reference for facilitators during the sessions (see Supplementary File S1).[43].Fig. 1Base causal loop diagrams shown to workshop participants before group modeling

Each FGD started with the introductory slides before participants broke into smaller groups of five or six individuals. Each smaller group had a facilitator, note taker, and someone who drew the components of the diagram in real-time using Miro. Facilitators guided the discussion by first encouraging the naming of relevant factors, then adding arrows to connect factors and identify feedback loops. Participants were also prompted to elicit ideas of stigma-specific interventions and how the interventions would fit into the causal pathways. Each smaller group focused only on one pillar.

The first session had two breakout rooms, one focused on HIV prevention and the other on HIV treatment. The second session had two breakout rooms, one focused on HIV testing and the other on stigma interventions for care engagement in general. After the two sessions, the expanded diagrams were cleaned, condensed, and validated together with academic experts using recordings and notes from the session. Modifications to the CLDs were limited to drawing out relationships between factors discussed but not finalized during the FGDs and to including obvious connections that were missed during the actual discussion, for example, connecting a negative arrow from social support to social ostracization. After each FGD, the modified diagrams were distributed to original group participants for their approval and feedback. After finalization of the three diagrams for HIV prevention, testing, and treatment, we began identifying feedback loops. Several feedback loops could be grouped together thematically, for example, the feedback loop of knowledge of appropriate care provision to receiving quality service provision to increasing health literacy and the parallel feedback loop of appropriate care provision to receiving quality service provision to increasing client self-advocacy. We coalesced similarly themed feedback loops into domains that were useful labels to organize the system, a structure adopted in several other applications of CLDs [44, 45]. This process and the individual feedback loops are described in Supplementary File S2. The condensed domains were 1) initial engagement with healthcare services, 2) experiences during and after care, 3) personal health attitudes and care navigation, and 4) community influences on HIV-related health and care. For the final session, one group focused on the first two domains and the other group focused on the final two domains. After the final session, the diagrams of domains 1 and 2 and of domains 3 and 4 were integrated into a final combined diagram.

Personal health attitudes and care navigation

Workshop participants frequently brought up the link between personal health attitudes and HIV care navigation. Broadly, this domain captures the relationship between an individual’s internal orientation toward health, such as beliefs, motivations, and perceived agency, and their external, actionable knowledge about engaging with the healthcare system. Internal attitudes are shaped by factors like resilience and empowerment, self-advocacy, and awareness of personal risk. In contrast, external aspects of care navigation include practical knowledge of how to access and move through the healthcare system (labeled as knowledge of care navigation in the diagrams), as well as the ability to recognize what constitutes appropriate and high-quality care (labeled as knowledge of appropriate provision).

Within this domain, stigma was described to have direct effects on willingness to access care and an indirect effect on general personal health attitudes. Participants described direct effects not only through anticipated or perceived concerns of discrimination or poor treatment, but also through internalized stigma and self-worth. One participant (FGD 1) explained that “it’s not that they think that something bad is going to happen, but that they just really don’t think that they deserve maybe health.” Beyond these direct effects on care-seeking, participants also described indirect pathways through which stigma influences broader psychosocial factors related to care engagement. The indirect effect is seen in the negative relationship between anticipated and internalized HIV stigma, social support, and resilience and empowerment. Anticipated and internalized stigma reduce social support, a factor that positively contributes to resilience and empowerment. As a result, increased stigma diminishes an individual’s sense of resilience and empowerment.

Feedback loops across personal health attitudes, quality care provision, and knowledge of care navigation show up multiple times in the full diagram. One example depicts a reinforcing loop that broadly moves from knowledge of HIV care navigation to appropriate awareness of HIV risk to quality of HIV service provision (3b). The primary driver of this reinforcement is through accessing quality care that not only delivers treatment but provides services to support and educate, for example, through quality case management that links clients to medical and support services. As one workshop participant (FGD 1) observed, case management “may be a positive input at multiple points, frankly throughout the entire diagram,” pointing to a role of case management as a key facilitator of care engagement. Similar reinforcing loops exist with other factors like client self-advocacy and resilience that increase engagement with services when care itself establishes a positive relationship with the patient.

When the driver of this reinforcement is absent, the reinforcing loop ceases to exist. During the workshops, participants identified several programmatic efforts aimed at fostering reinforcing patterns. These included initiatives on the quality care side, such as integrated care options and one stop clinics, and initiatives on the individual side, focused on strengthening health literacy, self-advocacy, and care navigation. One participant (FGD 2), for example, described seeking funding for “a peer-led workshop led by people living with HIV for people living with HIV to (…) talk about (…) what does this mean with your new diagnosis (…) how do you interact with a healthcare provider, how do you advocate for yourself when battling with a pharmacy or dealing with a receptionist that may be a little bit stigmatizing.” Other examples included positive speaker bureaus, which offer platforms for individuals to share their personal experiences living with HIV, and Mahogany Blue, a Michigan-based educational campaign promoting PrEP and sexual health resources [46, 47].

Provision of quality services

This domain focuses on dynamics within the health system itself, which have implications on individuals and their receipt of care. Factors within this category include any factors directly within the health system, such as characteristics of healthcare providers and resources within the health system. These factors are colored blue in Fig. 2. Within this domain, stigma was described to have a direct effect on health system factors, specifically reducing quality service provision and accurate and transparent data collection, through enacted HIV stigma.

One balancing loop in the diagram starts with the accessibility of accurate data on informing decision-making for interventions, such as allocating resources for provider training and education (Fig. 3c). Enacted HIV stigma from healthcare staff and providers was described to disrupt the balancing loop by compromising the quality of collected data, ultimately hindering effective resource allocation. Participants described this process through the mechanism of ideology conflict, by which provider, organizational, or community beliefs could conflict with recommended care practices. For example, one participant (FGD 1) noted that some “faith-based hospital settings (…) do not feel like they should have to ask those questions because it goes against their belief system.” Participants further described how discomfort among healthcare workers can lead to data being omitted altogether. As one participant (FGD 3) explained, “many nurses doing intake feel uncomfortable discussing sexual history or sexual health with patients. They are declining on behalf of the patients without even asking.”

During discussions of this feedback loop, participants suggested routinizing provider and staff training within healthcare facilities as a broad intervention to address HIV and sexual stigma. A specific example highlighted was the ShesWell Campaign, which aims to reduce these forms of stigma through education and awareness [48]. Additionally, the Shared HIV Networks (SHiNe) program was mentioned as an example of using data to improve coordination in programming and resource allocation [49]. SHiNe is a Michigan-based program that uses genotype testing data to identify and engage communities experiencing increased HIV transmission and facilitate linkage to care and prevention services.

Community influences on HIV-related health and care

Community influence factors are denoted in gray in Fig. 2. Primarily because community can be defined in different ways – personal community and the broader community environment – community influences are shown to have several different roles in the system.

Personal community refers to the immediate social network and support system surrounding an individual, including their family, friends, and healthcare providers. Positive community influences include increased social support and a desire to protect partners, which can be directly negatively influenced by anticipated and internalized HIV stigma. The community environment refers to the larger social, cultural, and structural context that shapes collective behaviors, norms, and resources available to individuals. Within the context of HIV service engagement, this community broadly includes public health initiatives, community organizations, and the visibility of people living with or at risk for HIV in media, healthcare settings, and care-related spaces (represented in the CLD as “representation”). Participants described the influence of community identity and representation on engagement with HIV services. One participant (FGD 2) noted that a lack of representation could discourage engagement, for example, how some Black cisgender women reported not using PrEP “because they don’t see anything that looks like them surrounding PrEP.” Conversely, another participant (FGD 1) warned of the challenge of in-group, out-group affinities, citing resistance among some individuals attending clinics who “don’t want to go to a place for, you know, gay people, or (…) don’t want to go to a place for white people, (…) not wanting to be affiliated with certain groups, behaviors…”.

One reinforcing loop in this system brought up by participants was the positive relationship between HIV service engagement and knowledge of care navigation at a community level (Fig. 3d). This loop, also described in the earlier domain of personal health attitudes and care navigation, was recognized as similarly operating at the community scale. Participants noted that experiences with HIV services could spread through social networks and influence broader community perceptions. As one participant (FGD 3) explained, “one bad or one poor experience with the staffer can lead to this organization having a poor reputation in a certain community.” The loop was described to have downstream effects on other community-level factors, such as increased representation of people living with HIV, which in turn can reduce stigma and foster more community-driven HIV programming. However, participants also emphasized that when representation is lacking, particularly for highly stigmatized or vulnerable populations, this loop can reverse. In such cases, the absence of visible role models seeking care and the fear of social isolation may reinforce stigma and reduce community engagement, especially among populations already facing intersecting forms of marginalization.

Several community-level interventions were discussed in the sessions relevant to this domain, most of which emphasized the importance of community engagement in programming. Specific initiatives included the Youth Pride Conference, which brings together LGBTQIA + youth for community-building, education, and self-expression, as well as the ShesWell Campaign described above and other peer-led education interventions. Participants also described interventions that aimed to develop community advocates through peer education. One participant (FGD 3) described efforts focused on “training them to be advocates not only for themselves, but for the people in their own community as well,” reflecting an emphasis on sharing knowledge and support through existing community networks.

Discussion

In collaboration with participants from health departments and community-based organizations, we developed a CLD to explore HIV stigma in relation to care engagement. This process took place over the course of three group model-building sessions and was grounded in participants’ perspectives and their experience working to advance EHE goals across the pillars of prevention, testing, and treatment. Compared to traditional static approaches, CLDs offer a different visual perspective for health systems analysis. The final CLD produced details how various subsystems and levels of factors in community, healthcare, individuals, and society interact and influence care engagement. The CLD format allowed us to establish key factors in relation to each other, trace factors together in pathways towards key outcomes, and understand, through feedback loops, the cascading effects of key factors on care engagement indicators.

Mechanisms of stigma

The participatory process elucidated the ways in which stigma permeates the system and influences specific factors related to HIV care. Initially, participants found it challenging to connect stigma to discrete system elements, as it appeared to pervade every aspect of the diagram. As the discussion progressed, however, participants shared phenomena that they had witnessed in their work related to stigma. In describing these examples, participants had to articulate more precisely how stigma contributed to certain processes, resulting in an implicit mapping of stigma onto direct system factors. As individuals shared, others affirmed their insights or expanded upon them. Through this exchange, the CLD process helped surface and prioritize specific factors relevant to participant experiences – those perceived as most directly shaped by, or shaping, stigma.

In terms of the impact of stigma on key factors, anticipated and internalized HIV stigma were shown from discussions to directly affect social support, which in turn shapes personal health attitudes such as resilience and empowerment. Enacted HIV stigma, on the other hand, was said to undermine the quality of service provision and compromise the accuracy and transparency of data collection, with implications for overall care quality. As expected, participants suggested stigma had direct effects on both willingness to access care and quality of the care provided. While these identified relationships may not capture all specific factors affected by stigma, they represent a consensus among participants and fall within the broad mechanisms described to affect engagement in care [6, 7, 10, 50]. For factors influencing stigma, anticipated and internalized HIV stigma are shaped by poor care experiences and prevailing community attitudes, while enacted HIV stigma is influenced by the attitudes of healthcare providers and staff.

The relationships identified through the CLD process are broadly consistent with existing frameworks and empirical studies on stigma and health [7, 15, 18]. For instance, the influence of anticipated and internalized stigma on social support, and subsequently on resilience and empowerment, fits within Turan et al.’s description of stigma operating through interpersonal factors and psychological resources to affect care engagement [15, 18]. Similarly, the impact of enacted stigma on care quality reflects a directional relationship shown in Stangl et al.’s Health and Discrimination Framework between manifestations of stigma towards structural drivers and facilitators of stigma [18]. The CLD approach extends the understanding of stigma and health beyond these accepted frameworks by explicitly mapping these pathways within a broader system of interacting factors, which is discussed in greater detail below. The participatory nature of the process also provides a natural prioritization of relationships: while many factors may be affected by stigma, the diagram reflects those that participants, based on their experience supporting HIV care engagement, perceived as most directly influenced by, or influential in shaping, stigma.

Feedback loops

Personal health attitudes and care navigation

Within this domain, the main feedback loop reveals that accessing services through an empowering or non-stigmatizing experience can build an individual’s confidence, increase their health literacy, and strengthen personal resilience and empowerment. These strengthened attributes, in turn, further reinforce care engagement over time, highlighting an important pathway for sustaining HIV care at the individual level.

Two critical relationships underpin this loop: the association between personal health attitudes, such as resilience, and care engagement, and the association between care engagement and the further development of positive personal health attitudes. The link between resilience resources and engagement in care is well established in the literature; not only are these attributes directly associated with positive health behaviors, but they also buffer against barriers such as stigma, supporting continued care engagement [51]. From a CLD perspective, this dual direct and indirect influence suggests that resilience-related factors may have a nonlinear, compounding positive effect on care engagement. However, the directionality of the relationship between care engagement and personal health attitudes depends heavily on the quality of care received. A positive care experience, one that extends beyond clinical competence to empower individuals and address broader health needs, can amplify protective attitudes. Conversely, experiences of enacted stigma during patient encounters can erode trust, undermine confidence in navigating services, and transform what should be a positive reinforcing loop into a negative one. Sustaining the positive feedback loop thus requires strengthening both components: promoting resilience and ensuring high-quality, non-stigmatizing care.

Participants in our FGDs identified interventions such as case management and integrated care models as promising strategies to bypass stigma-related barriers and promote adherence and retention in care. In the literature, multi-level interventions extending those discussed show promise, including strengths-based case management, which explicitly encourages clients to leverage their own abilities to achieve health goals, and patient navigation models, which provide individualized, peer-supported assistance in accessing and remaining engaged with healthcare and social services [52, 53].

Quality service provision

Within quality service provision, accurate data recording within healthcare services plays a big role specifically in addressing the HIV epidemic. Public health targets, such as those outlined by the EHE initiative, are informed by HIV estimates and projections generated by mathematical models that rely heavily on routine programmatic and surveillance data [54, 55]. Ideally, the allocation of limited resources is guided by this data, allowing for funding to be distributed optimally across interventions, populations, and geographic areas where needs are greatest [56]. Localized interventions and program planning similarly depend on understanding the characteristics of the current HIV epidemic – who is being infected, and what facilitators and barriers may prevent access to services.

Leveraging data thus acts as a feedback mechanism that should, in theory, drive continuous improvement in care delivery. However, stigma significantly disrupts this data-care relationship across multiple levels. At the individual level, anticipated or internalized stigma may deter individuals at risk for or living with HIV from disclosing their status, compromising the accuracy of collected data [57, 58]. This issue may extend beyond HIV-specific stigma to include stigma related to other marginalized identities, further distorting data for key populations. At the provider level, stigmatizing attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors can lead to biased interactions, incomplete assessments, and poor record-keeping, directly impacting the quality and reliability of clinical data [59]. In such cases, the very data intended to inform resource allocation and quality improvement initiatives becomes flawed [52, 59]. Flawed data stemming from stigma not only exacerbates health disparities but also risks reinforcing the social conditions and marginalization that harden into structural stigma [60]. Without strong, accurate data to drive advocacy and system reform, discriminatory structures may persist, undermining both data collection efforts and broader goals of health equity. Interventions raised during our FGDs, such as structural policies like opt-out testing and individual-level strategies like provider-staff training, underscore the need for multi-level approaches to address or bypass stigma. This feedback loop is increasingly important as technological advances make data collection and use more central to public health decision-making; without addressing stigma, better data systems counterintuitively may perpetuate existing inequities.

Community influences on HIV-related health and care

Within this domain, the main feedback loop illustrates how personal health experiences shape collective health outcomes through shifts in social influence and community norms. While this community-level feedback loop parallels similar mechanisms at the individual level, it operates in fundamentally different ways. The translation of individual experiences into broader community-wide change is complicated by characteristics of the network itself, such as the fragmentation of social connections and the absence of supportive ties. Participants during the FGDs also noted the paradox that stronger community identities, i.e. through religious or ethnic ties, were sometimes associated with more entrenched stigma, suggesting that social cohesion can simultaneously facilitate social support and exacerbate social ostracization.

Although participants discussed interventions that leveraged community-driven programming, peer education, and the visible representation of people living with HIV, the literature on these social network-based approaches highlights important caveats [61, 62]. Effective interventions often require complex contagion, where behavioral change is dependent on exposure to multiple sources of influence. The selection of peer influencers is particularly challenging; focusing solely on individuals already inclined toward behavior change risks missing critical populations. Furthermore, individuals with multiple marginalized identities are often embedded within overlapping networks that vary widely in their levels of supportiveness or stigma, adding an additional layer of complexity [10, 32, 61, 63]. These realities underscore the necessity of designing network-based interventions with precision and strong localized community engagement [61, 64].

Despite these complexities, community-level interventions remain particularly powerful because they address stigma at its foundation. Returning to Goffman’s original conception of stigma as a “spoiled identity,” or a deviation from the norm, efforts to shift community norms represent a fundamental strategy for reducing stigma itself [65]. The feedback loops identified in this domain suggest pathways through which such norm change might occur, while the broader literature emphasizes the importance of precision, contextual sensitivity, and localized design to ensure these interventions succeed.

Policy implications

The mechanisms of stigma and service engagement have implications for policymakers. Policy can complement intervention efforts by institutionalizing successful strategies and sustaining the conditions that enable effective interventions to succeed. We highlight several areas where policy may be particularly complementary to the intervention directions revealed through the CLD. Within healthcare systems, policies that support standardized care practices, such as routine opt-out testing and status-neutral care, can help normalize HIV prevention and treatment services and reduce the influence of identity-associated stigma on care engagement. Additionally, the development and incorporation of measures of stigma-related experiences into quality assurance and monitoring systems may provide a means of more precisely evaluating the effects of stigma mitigation efforts [66, 67]. At the community level, policies that support community-led programming and local adaptation may facilitate localized approaches to stigma mitigation responsive to specific social, cultural, and network dynamics operating within a given setting [66]. As jurisdictions advance EHE goals, such policy approaches may strengthen the reinforcing pathways identified in the CLD across personal health attitudes and care navigation, quality service provision, and community influences by sustaining supportive care environments, improving the quality of information used for decision-making, and centralizing community engagement around HIV services.

Limitations

The current study has several limitations. First, the participatory sessions were conducted on Zoom. While this facilitated important geographic diversity within our sample, the virtual format inherently leads to a more structured one-to-many communication style rather than organic person-to-person dialogue, which may have influenced the flow of discussion and potentially shaped how connections within the CLDs were developed. Second, unintentional omissions of factors or relationships may have occurred due to time constraints during the sessions. To mitigate this, additional input was sought from academic experts following the sessions to refine the diagrams. Third, the composition of participants likely limited the range of perspectives brought to the CLD design process. All participants were affiliated with city and state health departments or community-based organizations, and while some individuals living with HIV contributed personal insights, the group was not intentionally structured to represent a diverse range of experiences from people living with or at risk of HIV. As a result, discussions may have been more oriented toward structural and service-level barriers to engagement, potentially overlooking individual-level experiences of stigma. Nonetheless, the CLD methods used here does model how such a participatory approach can be used to highlight important feedback loops and mechanistic relationships that can be used for future intervention design.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the utility of an expressly participatory process, one that centers community stakeholder insights, to develop CLDs that visualize the complex interactions between stigma and HIV-care engagement. CLDs shift our conceptualization of stigma’s relationship to HIV care from linear, one-to-one associations towards layered reinforcing and balancing pathways, a portrayal that more closely mirrors reality. For example, the development of an individual’s self-perception around care-seeking is not solely a product of a static self-concept but is shaped over time by repeated interactions with the health system, which can change through positive and negative care experiences. While these cyclical relationships are difficult to articulate qualitatively, CLDs offer an intuitive visual language for expressing system behavior and sparking further dialogue.

Future research can build on these findings in several ways. Additional participatory work involving people living with HIV and populations at elevated risk of HIV acquisition may reveal mechanisms of stigma and intervention opportunities not fully captured in the current CLD. Future efforts may also benefit from developing more localized CLDs within individual jurisdictions or communities. Localized model-building efforts may identify feedback mechanisms and intervention priorities that differ across settings or are more strongly emphasized in particular jurisdictions. Finally, where deeper analysis is warranted, CLDs provide a foundation for additional quantitative exploration, serving as precursors to system dynamics models that can be used to simulate projected effects of key intervention areas on HIV-related outcomes.

Ultimately, systems science can be used to advance programmatic efforts to mitigate stigma, inform quantitative modeling efforts to evaluate its impact on key HIV service indicators, and serve as a reference for integrating participatory approaches into informing intervention and policy efforts across similar public health contexts.
