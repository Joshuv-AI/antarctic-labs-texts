Minimally invasive repair of pectus excavatum in children: insights from a large cohort at a single institution
Authors: Hyung Joo Park, Gongmin Rim, Seung Keun Yoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe outcomes and potential for recurrence following surgical correction of pectus excavatum (PE) in the pediatric population remain unresolved. We carried out non-delayed PE repair in all patients aged 5 years or older. This study aimed to verify the safety and effectiveness of pediatric PE repair, based on our 10-year research data, focusing on the incidence of recurrence and the advanced strategy of using the crane sternal pre-lifting and multiple pectus bars for anatomical chest wall remodeling.MethodsFrom January 2013 to July 2023, a cohort of 816 pediatric patients aged ≤10 years (range, 3–10 years) underwent PE repair using pectus bars to assess the outcomes of the procedure. Among these, we specifically focused on 173 patients who underwent surgery within the last 3 years (2020–2022), during which our approach shifted to the multiple bar technique for pediatric patients. This subset was divided into the single-bar (Group S, 87 patients) and multiple-bar (Group M, 86 patients) approaches for comparison. The analysis of outcomes included changes in the computed tomography severity index (Haller index), complication rates, length of hospital stay, and reoperation incidence, with a specific focus on recurrence.ResultsThe two groups had similar baseline characteristics, and there was no recurrence in either group in the recent 3-year analysis. In the single- and multiple-bar group comparisons, most Group M patients underwent repair with two pectus bars (2 bars: 84, 3 bars: 2, P=0.045). The length of stay showed no significant difference between the groups (Group S; 4.63 days vs. Group M: 4.40 days, P=0.22). Among complications, there were no life-threatening complications. Similar rates of occurrence of pleural effusion (Group S, 2.3% vs. Group M, 2.3%, P=0.96), pneumothorax (Group S, 2.3% vs. Group M, 3.4%, P=0.75), and wound complications (Group S, 1.2% vs. Group M, 0%, P=0.32) were observed in both groups. However, Group M showed less total intravenous rescue analgesic consumption (Group S, 16.5 mg vs. Group M, 13.92 mg, P<0.01).ConclusionsOur findings showed no significant increase in the risk of recurrence or major complications with the approach of non-delayed PE repair for all patients aged 5 years and older. Our transition from a single-bar to a multiple-bar repair approach has been crucial for achieving a thorough anatomical remodeling of the entire chest wall. This strategic shift has proven to be a safe and effective surgical method, applicable even to pediatric PE patients.Keywords: Chest wall, pectus excavatum (PE), pediatric, anatomical remodeling, multiple pectus bar

Highlight box

Key findings

• Our non-delayed approach to pectus excavatum (PE) repair in children over 5 years of age has been proven to be safe and effective in a 10-year large-cohort study, with recurrence or bar displacement observed only rarely. The transition to a crane-powered multiple-bar technique has contributed to more consistent and comprehensive chest wall remodeling.

What is known and what is new?

• Anecdotal concerns about potential recurrence during puberty have long discouraged childhood repair of PE. Our large cohort study of 816 pediatric patients provides meaningful evidence that early repair can yield favorable outcomes when precise and reliable techniques are applied.

• This study provides compelling long-term evidence that early surgical correction, supported by our technical advancements, can be achieved with virtually no recurrence or bar displacement. These innovations, including crane-powered sternal pre-lifting and the use of multiple pectus bars, contribute to more effective and stable correction. The novel approach—shifting the focus from simple sternal elevation to comprehensive chest wall remodeling with precise anatomical targeting—is described in detail.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Our findings suggest that the timing of PE repair should be redefined. When cardiac distress, asymmetry, or scoliosis progresses, timely intervention is warranted rather than delaying until adolescence without evidence-based support. A durable and successful repair depends on sound techniques for comprehensive anatomical remodeling, not on age as the primary determinant of failure.

Introduction

The optimal age for pectus deformity repair remains a topic without consensus. In our previous publication, we highlighted the advantages of early repair of pectus excavatum (PE) (1). In the present study, we evaluate our approach with no age limitation in PE repair. Given the congenital nature of this deformity, correcting it aligns with the goal of restoring normal anatomical and physiological conditions. Despite concerns about recurrence during the pubertal growth spurt (2,3), we found no evidence supporting these notions.

Our non-delayed repair approach is based on the clinical assumption that achieving thorough and precise correction of the deformity in early childhood establishes a foundation for the chest wall to grow in a normal direction, supporting improved chest configuration through the pubertal growth spurt. In this study, we aimed to share our data comprising 10 years of experience with a non-delayed PE repair strategy, utilizing advanced surgical methods and equipment in the treatment of pediatric pectus deformity. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-24-1199/rc).

Methods

We undertook a comprehensive analysis of our 10-year experience in pediatric pectus deformity repair to ascertain the effectiveness and safety of our approach. The objective was to establish a standard for the most favorable method for PE repair, considering factors such as timing, theories about advanced repair techniques, and overall outcomes.

Indication for surgical repair

The decision regarding the indication and timing of pediatric PE repair is guided by the severity of the deformity and its physical and physiological impacts. The various factors to be considered include the presence of cardiac compression, anticipation of future improvement, and the overall significance of the deformity. Indications for surgical intervention are evident in cases where the deformity is markedly deep, leading to cardiac compression and associated symptoms such as retarded growth, recurrent upper respiratory infections, pneumonia necessitating frequent hospitalization, poor posture, progression of scoliosis, and, in extreme cases, respiratory distress and failure to thrive. Additionally, psychosocial factors are considered following our observations that reveal a notable impact on self-esteem, even in very young children experiencing these deformities.

Timing of repair and age limit

We have adopted a non-restrictive approach toward age limitations, with a policy favoring non-delayed repair, based on 20 years of experience in PE repair across a wide age range extending from 16 months to 54 years in over 3,000 repair cases. Notably, the youngest patient, aged 16 months at the time of repair, presented an extreme anterior chest wall depression, causing respiratory difficulties due to intractable pneumonia with prolonged hospitalization. Successful recovery ensued after pectus bar repair in this patient.

We have advocated that candidates for repair can be identified as early as 3 years of age when the deformity requires correction due to significant cardiac compression. However, a recent policy shifts in pediatric pectus repair, adopting a multiple bar strategy, has established an age of 5 years as the standard indication for multiple bar PE repair. This adjustment reflects an evolving approach to optimize outcomes in pediatric patients with pectus deformities.

Surgical technique

A minimally invasive approach using pectus bars was adopted in all patients by a single surgeon (H.J.P.) in a single institution. All surgeries carried out for PE bar repair did not involve any conversion to open or other correctional methods.

The PE repair technique involves a comprehensive four-step approach described below.

Step 1: crane sternal pre-lifting (Figure 1)

The crane system (the “Easy crane”) utilized to lift the sternum (arrow) before commencing the operation. The sternum is raised to the desired level, and the procedure for pectus bar placement is performed with the crane in place.

After anesthesia induction, the procedure is initiated by full elevation of the depressed sternum using the crane system. This system utilizes sternal wire suturing, or a spiral screw connected to the table-mounted crane (“Easy crane” system, Primemed, Seoul, South Korea). The sternum is gradually lifted beyond the level of the chest wall to achieve correction (sternal overlifting). This creates space, reducing the weight on the chest wall and facilitating the overall procedure (the crane-powered PE repair).

Step 2: pectus bar introduction

The operating surgeon customizes the bar design during the operation to match the specific chest wall contours of the patient, bending the bar accordingly on the operating back table. Bar configuration adjustments are made for best outcomes, such as positive chest wall elevation, achievement of post-repair symmetry, and a full correction of the combined deformities. Pectus bars are introduced by using a pectoscope, a unique device providing visualization, dissection, and a guide placement in a single pass (Figure 2). Depending on the morphological characteristics of the deformity, pectus bars are placed in parallel, cross, or the XI patterns, fulfilling the entire chest wall remodeling strategy. Since 2022, this approach has evolved from a single-bar to a multiple-bar strategy.

The pectoscope is employed for internal visualization, mediastinal dissection, and guide placement in repairing pectus excavatum. (A) The pectoscope features a curved shaft and touch-view capability, enabling examination of the chest wall and heart surface while navigating through them. (B) The pectoscopic internal view clearly shows the interface of the chest wall and the pericardium (dotted line). It facilitates easier examination of the mediastinum and dissection with touch view, especially in situations with limited open space.

Step 3: pectus bar stabilization using claw fixator or bridge plate (Figures 3,4)

The single bar PE repair employs claw fixators for pectus bar stabilization (circles). The hook-shaped blades are anchored to the ribs bilaterally to immobilize the pectus bar. The blades are secured to the bar end using a bolt-nut locking system. PE, pectus excavatum.

The multiple bar PE repair involves using a bridge for pectus bar stabilization (circles). The bridge plates connect the pectus bars bilaterally, preventing rotation of the bars. The bridge plates are secured to the bar ends using a bolt-nut locking system. PE, pectus excavatum.

After positioning the pectus bars and raising the chest wall to the desired height, the bars are secured with claw fixators or bridge plates (4). Single bars are anchored to the ribs using claw fixators. Multiple bars are linked with the bridge plate, securely fastened to the end holes of the pectus bars using a bolt-nut structure. In 2022, with the transition from claw fixators to a bridge plate technique, we abandoned the single-bar approach (5). This represented a significant advancement, offering enhanced stability, reduced complications, and improved success.

Step 4: chest wall contouring

After elevating the excavated chest wall, lower costal flares and focal protuberances emerge in the parasternal cartilages. The modified sandwich technique, involving the “flare-buster” and the “magic string” technique, effectively manages these protrusions (5,6). This final touch smooths out the lower costal flares and bumps on the chest wall and remodels chest wall close to normal anatomy by eliminating residual focal malformations.

Postoperative pain management

The pain management protocol comprises intravenous patient-controlled analgesia, oral medication, and recue analgesics. The local anesthetic agent involving subcutaneous continuous infusion (Painbuster) was changed to serratus anterior plane block with 0.5% bupivacaine local infusion at a rate of 2 cc/h. Oral administration of analgesics included ibuprofen (10–15 mg/kg, every 6 h) and acetaminophen (10–15 mg/kg, every 6 h). Intravenous rescue analgesics were ketorolac (0.5 mg/kg, every 6 h or as required by the patient) and pethidine (0.5 mg/kg, every 6 h or as required by the patient) that were used to manage any breakthrough pain experienced by the patient (7).

Single bar repair strategy (Figure 3)

In the initial phase of our experience, both pediatric and adult patients underwent PE repair utilizing a single bar. During this period, our understanding lacked the ideas of an anatomical remodeling of the deformed chest wall, and the repair was limited to a simple elevation of the depressed sternum, with the sole intent of relieving cardiac compression. The single-bar approach remained unchanged for 20 years, despite its limitations. However, we transitioned to a different approach for adult cases by adopting multiple bars in parallel fashion to address the need for covering a broader area of the deformities as early as the year 2000. The decision to stick with a single bar lasted long owing to a lack of understanding of anatomical remodeling and concerns about using too many bars on children’s bodies.

As our experience evolved, it became evident that even in pediatric cases, a single bar often fell short in providing adequate coverage. Our current perspective, applicable universally regardless of age or other factors, acknowledges the limitations of the single bar strategy (Figure 5). Consequently, we have devised a concept of anatomically intact repair that utilizes multiple bars to comprehensively address the entire chest wall in all repair cases (Figures 6,7).

We introduced the parallel bar technique initially aiming at addressing upper chest wall depression, particularly in extensive deformities of the Grand Canyon type (Park Class 2A3R) or the long/broad flat type (Park Class 1B or 2A2R) (8). As our experience deepened, we recognized that the parallel bars alone fell short of providing comprehensive coverage for the entire chest wall. This limitation was evident in the gap between two bars (inter-bar depression) and the inability to cover the lower lateral chest lower than the xiphoid level (9,10).

To address the need for complete chest wall coverage, the primary author devised the XI bar technique, expanding the crossbar method by adding an extra bar to the upper chest to prevent any potential remaining deficiencies (10).

These advancements were instrumental in systematically restoring the anatomical integrity of the chest wall, marking a significant shift in our approach to PE repair.

In the pediatric patient cohort, while using a single bar approach, the challenge of insufficient repair and the inherent instability of a single bar has been a persistent concern. Despite exploring various bar positions during the repair procedure, including oblique orientations, these endeavors yielded only partial success and often left areas of insufficiency. Post-repair, we frequently observed a gradual regression in the areas unsupported by the pectus bar, signifying an incomplete repair. The instability of a single bar can also cause the pectus bar to slip away from the target or partially dislodge (Figure 5). Typically, it does not cause bar flipping, which would require a reoperation but often compromise the general integrity of the repair.

After enduring years of these challenges, we were finally motivated to adopt a multiple bar approach, extending it to pediatric patients as well. Initially we were cautious about employing multiple bars in children, with a concern of potential overload on small bodies. However, drawing from extensive experience with multiple bars in the teenagers and adult population, where the approach already extended to using 3 or 4 bars, our reservations diminished over the years. We safely expanded the application of the multiple bar strategy to children aged over 5 years. Remarkably, we have not observed any issues related to breathing or the movement of the chest wall when using multiple bars in pediatric situations, although the 3 bars are only employed in two cases (Figure 9).

Originally, the Nuss procedure involved lifting the chest wall depression through the leverage provided by the pectus bar turn-over, pivoting on the chest wall hinge points. However, utilizing the rotational power of the pectus bar to elevate the bottom of the excavated chest wall presented significant drawbacks. Turning the pectus bar at the chest wall entrance points (hinge points), where pressure is applied to lift the chest wall against its weight, carried the risk of intercostal muscle stripping. This process could potentially result in downward shifts in the hinge points, leading to loss of targeting of the desired point—the very bottom of the chest wall depression.

In the crane-powered approach, the lifting force for the chest wall is solely generated by the crane, eliminating the reliance for pivoting on the chest entrance point of the pectus bar. Consequently, no additional pressure is exerted on this entry point. This approach allows for the full lifting of the pectus depression using the crane machinery instead of leveraging the pectus bar against the chest wall. With this method, we can lift the depressed chest wall safely and position the pectus bar accurately at the precise point to support the chest wall. Even in pediatric patients with particularly deep and sharply defined bottomed deformities, the precise positioning of the bar is critical for ensuring accurate deformity repair and the subsequent secure maintenance of the pectus bar at the correct position (4,11).

Initially in 2002, we introduced a crane-powered chest wall lifting approach designed to assist in lifting heavy adult chest walls as a rescue procedure (12). Over time, the role of the crane expanded, progressing from partial lifting to total overlifting. In 2019, based on our accumulated experience and successful outcomes, we decided to implement the total crane approach for all repair cases, including pediatric patients. The rationale behind extending the use of the crane to pediatric cases, particularly those under 5 years old, is rooted in our commitment to achieving precise and efficient deformity repair. This strategic shift reflects our continuous effort to identifying the most effective methods for optimal outcomes in pediatric PE repairs.

Patient enrollment

Among 1,630 cases that underwent pectus deformity repair using pectus bars during the most recent 10 years (from January 2013 to July 2023) of our surgical practice, a pediatric patient population up to the age of 10 years old was included in this study. A total of 816 patients qualified for inclusion in this category and were divided in two groups according to the number of pectus bars used. A single pectus bar was used in 640 patients, while 176 patients were treated with multiple pectus bars.

Among the 816 patients enrolled over a 10-year period, a comparison was made specifically among cases from the most recent 3 years, spanning from January 2020 to July 2022 in order to compare the single versus multiple bar strategy. This timeframe coincided with a transition in our pediatric multiple bar technique (Figure 11), ensuring that the comparison within this cohort is based on similar repair techniques. A single pectus bar was used in 87 patients belonged to Group S (single-bar), while 86 patients were treated with multiple pectus bars and were assigned to Group M (multiple-bar).

The shift in strategy from the single-bar approach (blue area) to the multiple bar repair (pink area) in all patients, including the pediatric population. Multiple bars gradually replace single bars around the year 2021, with the complete transition to the multiple bar-only approach established in 2022.

The operative technique involved using a crane and a pectoscope for both Group M and S. The multiple-bar approach comprised placing parallel bars, cross bars, and cross bars plus upper horizontal bars (the XI configuration). Stabilization methods for pectus bars included using claw fixators for Group S and bridge plate fixation for Group M.

Outcome analysis included pre- and postoperative computed tomography (CT) index [Haller index (HI)], complication rates, reoperation due to bar dislocation, and the length of hospital stay.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 24.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Unpaired t-tests were employed to compare numerical variables between the two group. The Chi-squared test was utilized to analyze complication frequency between the groups. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical consideration

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by institutional Review Board of Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital (No.: KC22RISI0639) and informed consent was obtained from the patients or patients’ parents for the publication of this article and accompanying images.

Results

Among the 816 patients enrolled over a 10-year period, 640 patients underwent single pectus bar repair, while 176 patients received multiple pectus bars for repair. The mean age of the patients was 4.7 years. The gender distribution was predominantly male (n=584, 72%). Among them, 606 were classified as symmetric type (Park Type 1), while 210 (25.7%) were classified as asymmetric type (Park Type 2).

In the 10-year period study, there were two recurrence cases requiring reoperation. While there were no gross bar dislocations, they experienced bar dislodgement as they slipped away from the target a few months after repair (Figure 5B). The recurrence in these cases occurred soon after bar removal because of pectus bar dislodgement and progressed slowly. For this case, three parallel bars were implemented to ensure a secure and complete repair at reoperation.

In the comparative analysis between single bar repair and multiple bar repair groups over the recent 3 years, baseline patient data including age, height, weight, and gender proportion were found to be similar in both groups. Group S exhibited a higher prevalence of symmetric chest wall deformities, whereas Group M showed a higher prevalence of asymmetric deformities (refer to Table 1). Discussion

There has been an invasive repair method such as the Ravitch operation prevailed that involved the resection of costal cartilages to varying extents (14,15) before the advent of the Nuss technique in 1998 (16). Unfortunately, this invasive approach led to significant problems, including chest wall destruction, loss of supporting cartilage structures, and, more critically, the development of acquired constrictive thoracic dystrophy or a huge chest wall defect due to insufficient growth in the extensively resected chest wall (17,18). Consequently, the Ravitch procedure may not be recommended for pediatric patients to avoid the abovementioned destructive complications.

Recently, minimally invasive repair of pectus deformities, using the pectus bars, has become a widely adopted practice over the last two decades (19-21). In contrast to the invasive approach, the Nuss technique emerged with a promising approach that aimed to preserve the chest wall structure, including the costal cartilages, intercostal muscles, and the sternum. Based on these facts, we reasoned that an early repair of the deformity would offer diverse benefits, not only in physiological and social aspects but also by facilitating the surgical procedure for improved outcomes (1).

The current criteria for surgery in PE cases seem too narrow, mainly focusing on cardiac issues, possibly due to insurance regulations. Even when significant cardiac compression is evident in CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans (22,23), standard heart function tests like echocardiograms fail to detect its harm. Nevertheless, the compression on the right heart and its distortion cannot be ignored.

While the heart decompression plays a critical role in PE surgery, it is vital to emphasize that our primary focus is on addressing the chest wall disease rather than cardiac conditions. We aim to correct chest malformations beyond simply relieving pressure on the heart. Our goal is to remodel the entire chest wall to restore patients’ chests close to normal anatomical and physiological standards.

In pediatric pectus deformity repair, the risk of recurrence during pubertal growth spurts seems to be mostly an empirically supported claim based on the inductive perspective (2,3), but there is no definitive evidence or data to back it up. Our research findings, backed by large volumes of data, would help lessen persisting worries regarding recurrence due to early-age repair.

Throughout our experience from 1999 to 2023, bar removal was performed in 2,593 out of a total of 3,687 cases (70.3%) of pectus deformity repair. Over the course of this 25-year experience, there were a total of 9 cases (0.35%) requiring reoperation. During the 10-year study period, we encountered 2 cases of recurrence (0.2%). The recurrences happened soon after the bar was removed, but not later in puberty. This was because the first repair was not good enough because of the complexity of the morphology or the inadequate surgical technique. However, no recurrences identified during the recent 3-year comparison study. Importantly, there were no instances of delayed recurrence during pubertal growth as elusively concerned.

Regarding the pubertal recurrence concept, although some reports address the recurrence rate in patients repaired at an earlier age (2,3), our data, involving thousands of patients, did not show any evidence of recurrence in the pediatric population. Moreover, the recurrence itself was very rare, with less than 0.5% throughout our whole experience. In the current study, which involved over 800 patients over a 10-year period, we encountered 2 recurrent cases out of 800 cases. With no cases of delayed recurrence during pubertal growth observed and only a minimal number of recurrences necessitating reoperations, we believe our evidence suggests that these empirical concerns may not be accurate.

Before attributing the recurrence issue solely on age, it is essential to consider various technical factors that contribute to recurrence after pectus deformity repair. These factors encompass the complexity of the deformity, the adequacy of the repair technique, the thoroughness of the correction, and the precision of pectus bar placement. Each of these elements plays a critical role in ensuring a successful and durable correction of pectus deformities. Our data suggests, when the standard technique is applied correctly, the risk of recurrence is minimal in both adults and children. Of utmost importance, when pectus bars are placed incorrectly, missing the intended target, they fail to effectively elevate the depressed portion of the chest wall with precision. Consequently, these misaligned bars are unable to reshape the chest wall into the desired configuration, ultimately leading to failure (Figures 5,12).

There have been well known advantages for early intervention for the significant pectus deformities. First and foremost, the heart is innocent and deserves liberation from these constraints without undue delay. There is no justification for restricting the heart’s natural freedom for prolonged periods, such as waiting until the arbitrary age threshold of 12 years. The rationale behind this age limit lacks substantial evidence or data to support its validity as a universal rule.

From the surgeons’ perspective, younger individuals have more malleable skeletal structures, making correction easier and generally involving less complicated procedures compared to harder adult skeletons. This characteristic enables a more precise and comprehensive correction, minimizing the likelihood of complications or recurrence.

The PE deformity often becomes asymmetric over time (1). This asymmetry arises from the sternal depression, which compresses the heart and reduces the space within the left chest cavity. The heart, being a dynamic organ, requires adequate space to function optimally. Consequently, the heart exerts pressure on the thoracic skeletal structure, pushing it to the right and causing torsion of the sternum, along with the spine inclining to the right (Figure 13).

The emotional and social struggles associated with pectus deformities are often underestimated. Even in young children, they feel significantly impacted by their poor self-image, feeling unlike their peers. They may avoid social situations because they do not want others to notice their condition. The Figure 14 illustrates how children proudly show off their improved chest after treatment.

We propose a theory that PE presents in two forms: (I) the childhood form, which may be apparent early after birth or in childhood; or (II) the puberty form, which manifests delayed appearance during the teenage years, particularly during periods of rapid body height increase. Based on our observation over 20 years and experience with over 3,500 pectus deformity repair cases, we have developed a theory to explain the early after birth and delayed presentation of PE deformity during adolescence.

We assume that the delayed manifestation is different from the early manifested cases, and more detailed studies focused on particular genetic mutations are warranted. The puberty form cases become apparent in the teenage years when diagnosis and indication of surgery become imperative. However, most deformities that are revealed in early childhood should be dealt with differently from the later developing deformities. We advocate an early repair strategy because we assume that if we repair PE in its developing period, this will direct skeletal growth toward a normal direction. Throughout our experience, which includes over 3,500 cases of PE repair and 2,500 bar removals spanning a 25-year period, we have encountered only a few recurrent cases. Notably, no instances of relapse during the pubertal growth period were observed among clinic follow-up visitors. Therefore, we reason that the early childhood PE repair strategy was effective without an undue risk of recurrence, provided that the repair procedure corrects the deformity without residual deformity.

We have currently implemented our most advanced technique that has been developed over the past two decades (4,11). A significant decision was made to stop using the single-bar repair for all patients after the age of 5 years (4). A feeling of insufficiency that lasted for 20 years finally led us to the conclusion that a single bar was inadequate in resuming normal anatomy and for bar stability to meet our ultimate goal of treating pectus deformities. Moreover, patients nowadays are highly motivated, and their expectations for aesthetic outcomes are more elevated than ever.

In a particular case study highlighting the limitations of single bar repair, a 5-year-old boy with severe depression required immediate correction to alleviate pressure on his compressed heart. We opted for a routine single bar repair using bilateral claw fixator anchoring to the ribs. Initially, the repair appeared successful with precise bar positioning. However, a few months later, the bar slid off the target, resulting in a recurrence of xiphi-sternal depression (Figure 12). Despite a reoperation to reposition the bar, it slid upward, leaving a large residual depression of the sternal tip. In cases with very deep depression, characterized by a sharp-pointed sternal tip forming the bottom of the excavation, placing the bar precisely and maintaining its position until removal can be challenging. We observed slippage, where the bar shifts upward or downward from the target point, rather than flipping entirely. In cases of partial slippage of the single bar, the lateral view of the chest X-ray reveals the sternal tip is visibly not contacting the bar and has slipped away from it (Figure 12). This case highlights the difficulty in achieving stable and long-term maintenance of the bar in deep depressions during single bar repair.

Throughout our experience, the number of bars used has increased incrementally, advancing from parallel to cross bars and eventually to the XI configuration. Inspired by our multiple-bar approach for over 20 years, pediatric pectus repair has evolved towards the adoption of a multiple-bar repair strategy (Figures 7,11). This approach aligns with our commitment to achieving comprehensive anatomical remodeling. We have moved away from the insufficient and insecure single-bar repair approach, aiming to fulfill our entire chest wall remodeling concept (Figure 8) (4,10).

Significantly, our observations highlight that incorporating multiple bars in pediatric patients did not lead to any significant issues concerning chest wall mobility or respiratory hazards. The multiple-bar repair approach demonstrated greater effectiveness in covering a broader area of the chest wall and addressing asymmetry. When comparing the multiple-bar repair group with the single-bar group, no significantly different adverse effects were observed in terms of complications, length of stay, or recurrence, even though the multiple-bar group included cases with slightly more complex deformities.

Furthermore, our current crane-powered approach has resulted in an improved and complete elevation of the chest wall and facilitated pinpoint accuracy in placing the pectus bar on the target (Figures 1,10) (4,11). In the pediatric population, the total crane sternal lifting works well for efficient and accurate correction of the deformity. This is because the crane pre-lifting eliminates the requirement for the pectus bar to simultaneously push the chest wall up and be positioned precisely at the intended spot.

Conclusions

Based on data from our current study, our consistent approach of prioritizing non-delayed repair for significant deformities has been proven to be both safe and effective. Building upon the positive outcomes highlighted in our previous publication advocating for early repair in pediatric patients, the present results further underscore the safety and efficacy of utilizing crane-powered multiple-bar PE repair in the pediatric population. This approach effectively remodels the entire chest wall without encountering undue complications or recurrence.

Our PE repair approach, referred to as non-delayed repair after age 5 years, appears to be effective in treating pediatric patients with PE.
