Evaluating the Feasibility of Methods of Live Data Collection for Sociability Assessments
Authors: Grace E Sigwanz, Gabriela Salazar, Janelle K Bacotti, Samuel L Morris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSociability assessments may be useful for determining the function of social interaction; however, intensive data collection requirements may limit their clinical feasibility. The purpose of the current studies was to evaluate the feasibility of implementing sociability assessments while collecting discontinuous versus continuous data. We compared procedural fidelity and interobserver agreement across sociability assessment sessions during which implementers collected no data, momentary time sampling data, or duration data in role-play (both studies) and clinical (study 2) contexts. Most participants exhibited relatively high procedural fidelity and interobserver agreement scores remained high across conditions requiring live momentary time sampling and duration data collection. These findings support the feasibility and adoptability of sociability assessments in research and clinical practice. Directions for future research related to sociability assessments as well as general methods of evaluating the feasibility of different data collection procedures are discussed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40617-025-01101-9.Keywords: Data collection, Discontinuous measurement, Feasibility, Procedural fidelity, Sociability assessments

Social interactions are ubiquitous in the natural environment and their function or the type and degree of influence they exert on behavior is important to consider. Behavior analysts have developed several methods of evaluating the function of social interaction. Perhaps the most fundamental method is a reinforcer assessment (e.g., Morris et al., 2025; Piazza et al., 1999; Smaby et al., 2007). During reinforcer assessments, we deliver social interactions contingent on an arbitrary target response and evaluate the degree to which social interactions support an increase in response rate to determine whether or not social interactions are reinforcing.

More recently, researchers have developed preference assessments for social interaction (e.g., Clay et al., 2013; Nuernberger et al., 2012; Kelly et al., 2014; Morris & Vollmer, 2019; 2020a; 2020b). During preferences assessments, we deliver social interactions contingent on a simple selection or approach response and evaluate the relative occurrence of selection responses to create a hierarchy of preference. Researchers have demonstrated that the social preference assessment hierarchies often correspond to the results of reinforcer assessments (Morris et al., 2023).

The results of test conditions evaluating whether the presentation (e.g., Iwata et al., 1982; Kodak et al., 2007) or removal (e.g., Cengher et al., 2021; Cengher et al., 2022; Harper et al., 2013; Slocum et al., 2021) of social interaction may be the functional reinforcer for challenging behavior are also a method of evaluating the function of social interaction. If challenging behavior occurs at elevated rates in a condition where it produces the addition of social interaction, then we may conclude that social interaction is reinforcing. In contrast, if challenging behavior occurs at elevated rates in a condition where it results in the termination of social interaction, then we may conclude that social interaction is aversive.

Sociability assessments are another method of assessing the function of social interaction that researchers have recently developed and evaluated (e.g., Call et al., 2013; Morris & Vollmer, 2020c; 2021; 2022a; 2022b; Morris & Pizzuto, 2023). One factor that makes sociability assessments unique relative to the methods discussed above is that they concurrently allow for approach and avoidance to social interaction and, as a result, can efficiently delineate whether social interaction functions as a reinforcing, neutral, or aversive stimulus. During sociability assessments, a room is divided in half with identical leisure activities available on each side. The two halves of the assessment only differ in terms of the availability of social interaction. A therapist who delivers generic social interactions and reinforces clear mands for more individualized types of play or interaction is present on one side of the room, which is designated the social side. In contrast, no people or social interaction are available on the other side of the room, which is designated the independent play side. The side on which social interaction is available is systematically manipulated within each session to evaluate whether and how social interaction influences participant’s behavior. The only way participants can consistently maintain access to or avoid social interaction is by altering their time allocation based on changes in the availability of social interaction. Behavior analysts can determine whether social interaction functions as a reinforcing, neutral, or aversive stimulus based on the way participants allocate their time during sociability assessments (Morris & Vollmer, 2021).

Sociability assessments may serve a few important functions in behavior-analytic research and practice. They may be used to evaluate the need for and efficacy of interventions related to improving sociability. For example, sociability assessments could be routinely conducted at the onset of services to screen for indifference to and avoidance of social interaction. Then, when consistent indifference or avoidance is observed, assessment results could provide a baseline with which to evaluate intervention strategies such as incorporating individualized, preferred types of play and social interactions (e.g., Morris & Vollmer, 2022a; Sivaraman & Fahmie, 2018), teaching new communication (e.g., Mattson et al., 2023; Seaver & Bourret, 2020) or play (e.g., Agana et al., 2025; Pane et al., 2022) skills, or improving the function of social stimuli (e.g., Dudley et al., 2019; Jimenez-Gomez et al., 2024; Sainsbury et al., 2024). Using sociability assessments to screen for indifference and avoidance and evaluate corresponding intervention strategies could also help to prevent the development of social avoidance and challenging behaviors reinforced by the termination of social interaction (e.g., Cengher et al., 2022; Slocum et al., 2021). However, if sociability assessments are not feasible to routinely conduct in research and practice, then behavior analysts may not contact or expand upon this potential utility.

Researchers have recently evaluated methods of reducing the complexity and improving the efficiency of sociability assessments (e.g., Morris & Vollmer, 2020c; 2021; 2022a; Morris & Pizzuto, 2023). However, one unaddressed characteristic that may limit feasibility is the number of personnel required to conduct a sociability assessment. In all previous research, sociability assessments have required one therapist whose sole responsibility was implementing the sociability assessment procedures. In most cases, one or two additional research assistants have been present to video record the session and collect data, respectively (e.g., Call et al., 2013). Although using additional personnel to support or assist with assessment implementation is common in research, doing so may often be infeasible in clinical practice. One potential solution could be to use mounted video equipment to record sessions that are conducted in the absence of additional personnel beyond the therapist who implements sociability assessment procedures, which would allow for data to be collected from video recordings after-the-fact. Although this may circumvent personnel limitations, it may double the duration required to complete a sociability assessment and require additional time in the absence of the participant or client with whom the assessment was conducted, making this strategy infeasible in many clinical contexts. Thus, additional research is needed to evaluate methods of data collection that are feasible to use while implementing sociability assessment procedures, which could circumvent prohibitive personnel or time requirements.

To date, the primary dependent variable during sociability assessments has been the duration of social time allocation (Call et al., 2013; Morris & Vollmer, 2020c; 2021; 2022a; 2022b; Morris & Pizzuto, 2023). The feasibility of conducting sociability assessments while collecting duration data has yet to be evaluated. It is unknown if implementers can reliably collect duration data while maintaining high procedural fidelity. If collecting duration data while implementing sociability assessments degrades procedural fidelity, then methods of discontinuous sampling may provide a more feasible alternative that closely approximates duration data. Partial interval recording and momentary time sampling have both been found to provide close approximations1 to continuous measures such as frequency and duration (Devine et al., 2011; Harrop & Daniels, 1986; LeBlanc et al., 2020; Rapp et al., 2007; Rapp et al., 2008; Schmidt et al., 2013) and to allow for similar conclusions about assessment (e.g., Gardenier et al., 2004) and intervention (e.g., Meany-Daboul et al., 2007) outcomes. Importantly, researchers have found that the degree of correspondence between methods of discontinuous sampling and continuous measurement was inversely related to the size of the interval used for discontinuous sampling: correspondence tends to decrease as intervals become larger (e.g., Alvero et al., 2008; Gunter et al., 2003).

Given the potential for correspondence between methods of discontinuous sampling and continuous measurement, it is important to consider whether methods of discontinuous sampling are feasible to use, in general, and especially while implementing assessment or intervention procedures (Fiske & Delmolino, 2012; LeBlanc et al., 2015). Previous research suggests that methods of discontinuous sampling can yield high levels of interobserver agreement between two data collectors (Hanley et al., 2007) and that methods of discontinuous sampling that are preferred by data collectors can readily be identified (Hanley et al., 2007; Kolt & Rapp, 2014). Data collectors have been found to display higher levels of interobserver agreement while, and a consistent preference for, using methods of discontinuous sampling with larger intervals (Hanley et al., 2007; Kolt & Rapp, 2014). Interobserver agreement and procedural fidelity are essential measures in applied behavior analysis as they (a) allow for opportunities to provide feedback to data collectors and therapists, (b) permit long-term monitoring of performance trends, and (c) elucidate the extent to which accurate clinical decisions can be made from the data (Vollmer et al., 2008).

To our knowledge, researchers have yet to evaluate the feasibility of implementing any form of assessment or intervention procedures while collecting continuous versus discontinuous data. As a result, the degree to which methods of continuous measurement or discontinuous sampling may be feasible to use while conducting sociability assessments also remains unclear. It is important to evaluate how concurrent data collection and implementation requirements may impact the quality of data collected and the fidelity with which procedures are implemented because behavior analysts are often required to serve this dual role in clinical practice (e.g. Fiske & Delmolino, 2012). Therefore, the purpose of the current studies was to evaluate the feasibility of collecting continuous versus discontinuous data while simultaneously conducting sociability assessments in role-play and clinical contexts.

General Method

Below, we present the methods and results for two distinct studies, both of which aimed to evaluate the utility and feasibility of different data collection methods for sociability assessments. We evaluated the feasibility of collecting discontinuous and continuous data while conducting sociability assessments with a role-play partner (both studies) or child (study 2 only). Across both studies, we conducted sociability assessments using the same arrangement, procedures, and response definitions. The next section describes methodological components consistent across both studies.

Arrangement and Procedure

Both studies included sociability assessments conducted in the manner described by Morris and Vollmer (2021). All sociability assessments occurred in a room divided into two identical halves by a line of tape. Tables, chairs, and generic, developmentally appropriate toys were present on each side of the room. We individualized the available toys to meet the needs of the child by removing toys that the child was reported to engage with in an unsafe manner. The implementer did not move the toys during the assessment sessions. However, it is important to note that the child or role-play partner could move toys from one side of the room to the other (see Morris & Vollmer, 2020c for discussion and rationale). Other materials included a timer, device equipped with Countee® for data collection (Slanzi & Fernand, 2024), and a GoPro® to video-record sessions. Countee® is a mobile data collection application that produced a data stream that indicated when the child (study 2) or role-play partner (both studies) was on the social side on a second-by-second basis. We modified the settings of the devices equipped with Countee® to avoid the screen turning off during data collection.

An implementer was always present on one side of the room. The side where the implementer was present was designated as the social side because the implementer provided social interactions whenever the child (study 2) or role-play partner (both studies) was also on that side. The implementer delivered generic, non-individualized social interactions approximately once every 10 s that the child or role-play partner was on the social side (i.e., comparable to a variable interval 10-s schedule). Examples of the generic interactions provided by the implementer included praise-like statements (e.g., “You are playing so nicely!”), descriptions of the child’s or role-play partner’s behavior and environment (e.g., “That’s a blue ball!”), and child- or role-play partner-directed play (e.g., coloring together). Examples of nongeneric interactions that the implementer refrained from providing included therapist-directed play (e.g., “Let’s play with the blocks!”), questions (e.g., “What is your favorite color?”), and any interactions tailored to the child’s preferences that were not clearly requested (e.g., providing tickles in the absence of a mand for tickles). The implementer only delivered preferred, individualized social interactions contingent on clear mands, and did not prompt or direct the child or role-play partner’s communication, play, or movement. The implementer reinforced all mands for individualized play or social interaction emitted on the social side (i.e., a fixed ratio 1 schedule), but this did not alter the schedule of generic social interactions described above. Examples of clear mands included specific vocal requests (e.g., “Up”) and gestures (e.g., hand leading the therapist to hold a ball), whereas examples of unclear mands included all nonspecific or unintelligible vocalizations and gestures (e.g., tapping the therapist). The side where the implementer was not present was designated the independent play side2 because no social interactions were available when the child or role-play partner was present on that side of the room. When the child or role-play partner was on the independent play side, the implementer did not deliver social interactions and maintained a neutral gaze and posture. The child and role-play partners could move freely about the room and could instantly access social interaction on the social side and leisure items on either side of the room (i.e., there was no changeover delay). The implementer switched between the two sides of the room every 2 min during each 8-min session to systematically manipulate the availability of social interactions. The implementer always signaled that they were switching sides by making a statement such as, “I’m going to play over here now.” Therefore, the only way the child or role-play partner could consistently access or avoid social interaction was by altering their social time allocation based on changes in the availability of social interaction3.

Response Definitions

In both studies, the primary dependent measure was the duration of social time allocation, which is consistent with Morris and Vollmer (2021). As described in Morris and Vollmer, a child or role-play partner was recorded as being on the social side when both of their feet were on the same side of the room as the implementer, using a 0-s onset and offset. The child or role-play partner was recorded as being on one side of the room until they met criteria for being on the other side. That is, if the child or role-play partner had one foot on both sides of the line, or both feet on top of the line, then we recorded them as being on the side they were on previously until they met criteria for being on the other side of the room.

Study 1: Evaluating Feasibility in a Role-Play Context

Study 1 evaluated the feasibility of implementing sociability assessments with a role-play partner while collecting no data, momentary time sampling, or duration data. We selected momentary time sampling as the discontinuous measure to be evaluated on the basis of a retrospective analysis of data sets from Morris and Vollmer (2021) comparing partial interval recording, whole interval recording, and momentary time sampling to duration data. Results suggested that momentary time sampling produced the closest approximation to duration on the social side, which replicated the findings of previous research in this area (e.g., Harrop & Daniels, 1986; LeBlanc et al., 2020; Meany-Daboul et al., 2007). Supporting Information A provides a complete description of the procedures and findings of the retrospective reanalysis. Study 1 consisted of two parts. First, we trained undergraduate research assistants to implement and collect data for sociability assessments. Second, we conducted a recording feasibility evaluation which compared implementer’s procedural fidelity and interobserver agreement across conditions with different data collection requirements (i.e., no live data collection, discontinuous measurement [momentary time sampling], and continuous measurement [duration]).

Participants, Setting, & Materials

We recruited seven undergraduate students to participate in this study: Beth, Carol, Ernest, Kennedy, Lonnie, Meghan, Victoria. All participants expressed interest in gaining research experience related to conducting behavioral assessments and interventions with children with developmental disabilities and provided informed consent to participate in this study. All participants were enrolled in a course designed to provide undergraduate students with hands-on experience and training related to conducting psychological research. Five participants (all but Kennedy and Meghan) were also enrolled in an introductory course on applied behavior analysis. One participant (Kennedy) worked as a Registered Behavior Technician at a local clinic and had considerable experience collecting behavioral data. All other participants reported that they had no experience collecting behavioral data and all participants were unfamiliar with sociability assessments. Table 1 includes the participants’ demographic information, major, and undergraduate student status. Participants completed up to three sociability recording feasibility evaluation sessions per day, two to three days per week, with at least a 10-min break between sessions.

We completed behavioral skills training and the sociability recording feasibility evaluation sessions in a 3.45 by 2.68 m room constructed out of moveable room dividers. During the data collection training and the duration condition, participants had access to a device equipped with Countee®, which included a template set to 480 s and included a social side duration key to collect social time allocation data. We implemented momentary time sampling with 30-s intervals. We anticipated this interval duration would be large enough to improve feasibility while remaining small enough to allow for correspondence with duration data (see Supporting Information A). During the momentary time sampling condition, participants had access to a pencil, clipboard, timer, and paper datasheet that provided the start and end time for each 30-s interval across rows and a column for indicating whether the role-play partner was on the social or independent play side at the end of each interval.

Design

In the sociability recording feasibility evaluation, we used a multielement design to evaluate how different data collection requirements influenced the feasibility of conducting sociability assessments while collecting live data. Specifically, we presented the no live data collection, momentary time sampling, and duration conditions in rapid alternation. We evaluated (a) the procedural fidelity with which participants conducted sociability assessments across all conditions and (b) participants’ interobserver agreement with a trained observer during conditions requiring data collection (i.e., momentary time sampling, duration). We rapidly alternated between these three conditions to minimize the likelihood that practice or exposure effects differentially influenced one condition over another and could constitute a confounding explanation for differentiation between conditions. We implemented conditions in a consistent order to facilitate detection of sequence effects (i.e., rather than randomizing condition order, which would obscure such effects; Sidman, 1960). For most participants, we implemented conditions in the following order: no data collection, momentary time sampling, and duration. We selected this order in an effort to facilitate higher levels of performance during the initial exposure to conditions requiring data collection, which we anticipated would be more difficult.

Procedures

Pre-experimental Procedures

We completed multiple training steps before progressing to the sociability recording feasibility evaluation. First, we assigned and discussed the Morris and Vollmer (2021) article. Next, we trained participants to collect duration data until they met 96% interobserver agreement and provided participants with instructions on how to collect momentary time sampling. We selected 96% interobserver agreement as our training criteria because it is a more stringent training criterion than more common requirements of 80% or 90% but still allowed for disagreement on up to two intervals out of the 48 10-s intervals in an 8-min session. Finally, we completed behavioral skills training with participants to practice implementing sociability assessments until they met the mastery criteria of two consecutive rehearsals without error, which signaled that we could commence the study. The exact number of rehearsals required was not documented for participants in study 1, but all participants met mastery criteria in five or fewer rehearsal sessions. We completed these training steps before participants progressed to the sociability recording feasibility evaluation to control for the history of accurate implementation and data collection during sociability assessments. These preliminary steps corresponded with competency-based training standards supported within applied behavior analysis (Behavior Analyst Certification Board, 2018; Parsons et al., 2012) to promote high procedural fidelity and accurate data collection before evaluating the isolated effects of a data collection requirement Supporting Information B provides a more detailed description of the study 1 pre-experimental training procedures that preceded our experimental recording feasibility evaluation described below.

Sociability Recording Feasibility Evaluation

The evaluation consisted of three conditions: no data collection, momentary time sampling, and duration. Across all conditions, participants implemented the sociability assessment with a role-play partner, according to the procedures described in the “General Method” section. During the no data condition, there was no data collection requirement; participants only implemented sociability assessment procedures. During the momentary time sampling condition, the participants collected momentary time sampling data while implementing the sociability assessment procedures. During the duration condition, the participants collected duration data while implementing the sociability assessment procedures. Participants only collected data on social time allocation as this was the dependent measure required to delineate whether social interaction was reinforcing, neutral, or aversive in Morris and Vollmer (2021). Additionally, we conducted different conditions in a consistent sequence for each participant (e.g., no data collection, momentary time sampling, duration). Before each session, we instructed participants that they would collect no data, momentary time sampling, or duration data. For the sessions in the momentary time sampling condition, participants received a paper datasheet and pen to score social time allocation as well as instructions for how to use the data sheet and when to record “social” or “alone” (i.e., independent play) side. Participants also received a timer to signal the end of each 30-s interval. For sessions in the duration condition, participants received a device equipped with Countee® to score social time allocation using duration as well as instructions for how to use the application to record duration on the social side.

Participants completed contrived, 8-min sessions with a role-play partner. We used a role-play partner, instead of a child who may benefit from participating in a sociability assessment, to control the difficulty of implementation. During each session, the role-play partner spent at least 60% of the time on the social side, switched sides at least 20 times (e.g., 2.5 times per min), and emitted at least eight clear mands for specific types of play or social interactions (e.g., “stack the blocks with me”). Although sociability assessment procedures usually specify that the therapist will reinforce mands for movement (e.g., “leave me alone” or “come play over here”), these have rarely been observed in the research conducted to date. As a result, the role play partner did not mand for the participant to switch sides. We completed three or four series of the three conditions with each participant.

Preference and Feasibility Ratings

We assessed social validity by obtaining verbal reports of participants’ preference and feasibility ratings for collecting data and implementing sociability assessments, respectively. After completing the sociability recording feasibility evaluation, we asked participants, “If you routinely conducted assessments of sociability, would you rather (a) not collect data during the assessment and collect data after each assessment from a video recording, (b) collect momentary time sampling data during each assessment, or (c) collect continuous duration data during each assessment?” We also asked participants to rate the feasibility of conducting sociability assessments in each condition (i.e., no data collection, momentary time sampling, duration) on a scale from one to five, stating, “Please rate the difficulty or feasibility of each method of conducting assessments of sociability from one (i.e., very easy/feasible) to five (i.e., very difficult/infeasible).”

Dependent Measures, Interobserver Agreement, and Procedural Fidelity

Participant’s Dependent Measures

Two trained observers collected data during all sessions of the sociability recording feasibility evaluation. The dependent measures included the participant’s procedural fidelity in implementing sociability assessments and interobserver agreement with trained observers. We assessed procedural fidelity by segmenting each session into 10-s intervals and dividing the intervals without an error by the total intervals (48) and multiplying the quotient by 100. This method allowed us to capture moment-to-moment errors in implementation that could occur repeatedly throughout the session and evaluate the degree to which participants consistently implemented sociability assessment procedures with fidelity. Procedural fidelity errors were generally related to the timing or signaling of switches and delivery of social interactions. We recorded an error if the implementer did not switch sides of the room within 5 s of or provide a vocal signal for a scheduled switch. On the social side, we recorded an error if the implementer delivered nongeneric vocal interactions, prompted play that was not initiated by the role-play partner, did not deliver generic interactions during a full interval, did not engage in parallel play when the role-play partner displayed item engagement for a full interval, did not engage in play directed by the role-play partner, or did not reinforce clear mands within the interval that they occurred. We compared the percentage of 10-s intervals with no errors across individual sessions of each condition and the mean across conditions and participants.

We assessed interobserver agreement by using the proportional agreement method for duration and interval (i.e., occurrence, nonoccurrence) agreement method for momentary time sampling data. We compared the participant’s live data collection to that of a trained observer who did not conduct the sociability assessment. We calculated proportional interobserver agreement by dividing the session into 10-s intervals and comparing each observer’s data on an interval-by-interval basis. Within each interval, we divided the smaller number of seconds on the social side by the larger number of seconds on the social side to obtain a proportion. The sum of the proportions for each interval was divided by the total number of intervals and then multiplied by 100. We calculated interval-by-interval interobserver agreement for momentary time sampling by dividing the number of 30-s intervals with agreement (i.e., both observers scoring social side at the end of the interval) by the total intervals and multiplying the resulting quotient by 100. We compared the interobserver agreement across individual sessions that required data collection (i.e. momentary time sampling and duration conditions) and the mean across conditions and participants.

Trained Observers’ Interobserver Agreement

As described above, interobserver agreement was one of the primary dependent measures, which we assessed by comparing data collected by participants and a trained observer. However, because the trained primary observer served as the “standard” or point of comparison for participants, we also assessed interobserver agreement for data that they collected. Therefore, a secondary observer independently collected data on the role-play partner’s duration on the social side from video recordings for one randomly selected session from each condition for each participant (i.e., 25 or 33% of session in each condition). We calculated proportional interobserver agreement for duration on the social side, which had a mean of 98% (range, 96% to 99%) across participants.

Procedural fidelity was another primary dependent measure. As described above, we calculated the percentage of 10-s intervals without a procedural fidelity error for each session. A trained primary observer collected data for each session via a live video feed. In order to evaluate interobserver agreement on procedural fidelity data, a secondary observer independently collected procedural fidelity data from video recordings for one randomly selected session from each condition for each participant (i.e., 25 or 33% of session in each condition). We calculated interval-by-interval interobserver agreement for the occurrence and nonoccurrence of procedural fidelity errors. Each session was divided into 10-s intervals, and we compared the primary and secondary observer’s data on an interval-by-interval basis. For each interval, we scored an agreement if both observers indicated that a procedural fidelity error did or did not occur. The number of intervals with agreement was divided by the number of intervals and multiplied by 100. Mean interobserver agreement was 95% (range, 92% to 98%) for procedural fidelity across participants.

Role-Play Partners’ Procedural Fidelity

In addition to evaluating participants’ procedural fidelity implementing and collecting data during sociability assessments, we evaluated the procedural fidelity of the role-play partner’s social time allocation, social and avoidant switches, and clear mands for play or social interaction during sociability recording feasibility evaluation sessions. We instructed the role-play partner to spend at least 60% of the session on the social side, switch sides at least 20 times for each session, and emit at least eight clear mands for individualized play or interaction for session to control for the difficulty of conducting the assessment and collecting data across conditions. The role-play partner met or exceeded the targeted percentage of session on the social side, number of switches, and number of mands for 75 out of 78 sessions conducted across all conditions and all participants. The exceptions included Ernest’s second momentary time sampling session in which the role-play partner only switched sides 17 times, Victoria’s third no data session in which the role-play partner only emitted seven clear mands, and Lonnie’s first duration session in which the role-play partner only switched sides 18 times.

MTS Momentary time sampling

Study 1 demonstrated that it may be feasible to collect accurate momentary time sampling or duration data while implementing sociability assessment procedures with a high degree of procedural fidelity. However, four limitations should be noted. First, participants received extensive training on how to collect duration data but were not formally trained on how to collect momentary time sampling data prior to the start of the recording feasibility evaluation. Second, the modality of data collection differed between the duration (digital) and momentary time sampling (paper and pen) conditions and, anecdotally, some participants reported that they would have preferred collecting momentary time sampling data using a digital data system. Third, in study 1, we instructed the role-play partner to allocate a minimum amount of time to the social side, display a minimum number of switches, and emit a minimum number of mands. Although the role-play partner was generally successful in meeting these minimum requirements, only specifying minimum values may have allowed unnecessary heterogeneity in the actual amount of time spent on the social side and number of switches or mands. Fourth, we conducted the recording feasibility evaluation in study 1 entirely in a role-play context, so it remains unclear whether and how differences in procedural fidelity and interobserver agreement would generalize to sociability assessments conducted with individuals from clinical populations. Therefore, the goal of study 2 was to replicate and extend study 1 by addressing the limitations described above. Specifically, in study 2, we (a) formally trained participants to collect momentary time sampling and duration data, (b) incorporated a digital data system for both methods of data collection, (c) provided the role-play partner with discreet text-based coaching related to their time allocation, switching, and mands, and (d) conducted recording feasibility evaluation sessions with a child with autism for whom the sociability assessment was clinically indicated as well as a role-play partner.

Study 2: Evaluating Feasibility in a Clinical Context

Participants, Setting, and Materials

We recruited four undergraduate students (Erica, Liz, Margot, Vivian) enrolled for research credits whose schedule aligned with the children enrolled in a clinical research program, all of whom provided informed consent to participate in this study. Participants had no prior coursework in applied behavior analysis, except for a 40-hr Registered Behavior Technician online course. Additionally, participants had limited experience with continuous or discontinuous data collection and no experience using Countee® or implementing sociability assessments. Table 3 includes the participants’ demographics, major, and undergraduate student status. Participants completed sessions with two children enrolled in a clinical research program whose caregivers provided research consent for participating in assessments during their admission. We recruited these children because the onset of their services (i.e., when we would typically conduct an initial sociability assessment) and schedule of weekly appointments were well aligned with the beginning of study 2 and participants availability. These children were 5 and 7 years old, on the autism spectrum, minimally verbal (i.e., limited to no echoics or vocal-verbal behavior), and engaged in aggression (client A) or self-injury (clients A and B). Participants completed up to nine sociability recording feasibility evaluation sessions per day, two to three days per week.

We completed behavioral skills training and sociability recording feasibility evaluation sessions in a 2.9 by 2.3 m room with an observation window. We arranged the sociability assessment room and included the same materials as described in the “General Method” section and study 1, except data collection training and sessions only involved the use of a device with Countee®. The duration Countee® template was preset identical to study 1. The momentary time sampling Countee® template was preset to 510 s (8.5-min) and included “social” or “alone” (i.e., independent play) side frequency keys. Participants had the opportunity to reference the running timer on Countee® to score which side the participant was on at the end of every 30-s interval. As with the timer in study 1, the Countee® session clock served as the sole signal to the participants to collect data at the end of each interval. We instructed participants to preset the momentary time sampling Countee® template to 510 s to maintain a 30-s opportunity to score all intervals within a session (i.e., “adding” 30 s prevented Countee® from ending the session before the participant had the opportunity to score the final interval).

Design

We replicated the multielement design from study 1, with the exception of adding child probes before and after role-play sessions. First, following behavioral skills training, participants completed two series (i.e., six sessions) of the sociability recording feasibility evaluation with a child. We limited these initial child sessions to two series to minimize the child’s potential exposure to low procedural fidelity. Then, participants progressed to completing at least three series with a role-play partner. Finally, participants returned to completing sessions with a child for at least three series to evaluate the effects that contrived, role-play sessions had on procedural fidelity and interobserver agreement when collecting data while implementing a sociability assessment with a child. As in study 1, we also obtained participants’ preference and feasibility ratings after they completed the sociability recording feasibility evaluation.

Procedures

Pre-experimental Procedures

As described for study 1, participants progressed from the pre-experimental procedures (i.e., reading and discussing Morris and Vollmer [2021], data collection training, and behavioral skills training) to the sociability recording feasibility evaluation. As in study 1, the study could commence following behavioral skills training in which participants meet the mastery criteria of two consecutive rehearsals without error. It took participants 3 to 4 rehearsals to meet this criterion. One change for study 2 was that participants received competency-based training for momentary time sampling as well as duration data collection. Supporting Information B provides a detailed description of the study 2 pre-experimental training procedures that preceded the recording feasibility evaluation.

Sociability Recording Feasibility Evaluation

The sociability recording feasibility evaluation with a child and role-play partner constituted the experimental procedures of study 2. As described for study 1, each series involved instructing participants to implement sociability assessment sessions while collecting no data, momentary time sampling, or duration data. Participants completed sociability assessment sessions with a child before and after completing at least three series of sessions with a role-play partner. As described in Study 1, the role-play partner distributed their social time allocation, programmatically switched sides, and emitted clear mands for play or social interaction. To ensure and equate the difficulty of conducting the assessments during role-play partner sessions, the role-play partner received discreet text-based coaching via their mobile device to follow one of three scripts, each of which involved spending at least 60% of the session on the social side, switching sides at least 13 times, and emitting eight clear mands for individualized play or interaction during each session. Each participant completed sociability assessment sessions with one (Liz, Erica) or two (Margot, Vivian) children. Child data and the sociability assessment outcomes during each participant’s sessions are available for review as Supporting Information C.

Preference and Feasibility Ratings

We completed the same questionnaire described in study 1 to assess social validity by obtaining reports of participants’ preference and feasibility ratings for collecting data and implementing sociability assessments.

Dependent Measures, Interobserver Agreement, and Procedural Fidelity

Participant’s Dependent Measures

As described in study 1, we included the same dependent measures: procedural fidelity and interobserver agreement. We calculated the percentage of intervals without procedural fidelity errors for each session of each condition for each participant and summarized these data across conditions and across participants. We also assessed the percentage of procedural fidelity errors that occurred when the child was on the social versus independent play side during the child-probe sessions of the sociability recording feasibility evaluation, which may be found in Supporting Information D.

We calculated interobserver agreement on social time allocation using the same proportional agreement and interval-by-interval agreement methods described in study 1 for duration and momentary time sampling data, respectively. We evaluated interobserver agreement for each session of each condition for each participant, and we summarized these data across conditions and across participants.

Trained Observers’ Interobserver Agreement

Interobserver agreement was one of the primary dependent measures. As described in study 1, we calculated interobserver agreement by comparing data collected by participants to data collected by a trained primary observer. Similar to study 1, an independent secondary observer also collected data on the role-play partner’s and child’s duration on the social side by reviewing 33% of video-recorded sessions that occurred across conditions and phases for each participant. We randomly selected these sessions, with the caveat that we ensured at least one session from each condition and phase was included. We also assessed interobserver agreement by comparing the primary and a secondary observer’s data using the same calculations for proportional agreement described in study 1. We obtained a mean interobserver agreement of 98.75% (range, 87.5% to 100%) for duration on the social side. Procedural fidelity was also a primary dependent measure. We calculated interobserver agreement on procedural fidelity identically to study 1. Across participants, mean interobserver agreement on procedural fidelity was 98.83% (range, 95.83% to 100%) during initial child sessions, 99.13% (range, 95.83% to 100%) during role-play sessions, and 97.75% (range, 87.5% to 100%) during the reversal to child sessions.

Role-Plays Partners’ Procedural Fidelity

As described in study 1, we evaluated the procedural fidelity of the role-play partner’s social time allocation, social and avoidant switches, and clear mands for play or social interaction during sociability recording feasibility evaluation sessions. However, in study 2 the role-play partner received discreet text-based coaching related to their time allocation, switching, and mands rather than simply being given instructions for the target values of each measure as in study 1. The role-play partner met or exceeded the target percentage of session on the social side and number of mands for all sessions conducted across all conditions and participants. However, the role-play partner completed one fewer switch than the minimum (i.e., 12 instead of 13) during four sessions (i.e., 32 out of 36 sessions) but adhered to the scripted switch schedule for all other sessions.

General Discussion

The purpose of the current studies was to evaluate the feasibility of collecting live discontinuous and continuous data while implementing sociability assessments. Specifically, we evaluated the feasibility of collecting momentary time sampling and duration data while implementing sociability assessment procedures with a role-play partner (both studies) or child on the autism spectrum (study 2 only). The results of study 1 suggest that (a) participants could collect accurate momentary time sampling data while implementing the assessment with similar procedural fidelity as when no data collection was required and that (b) requiring implementers to collect duration data may slightly decrease procedural fidelity, although it is unclear if these differences are clinically significant (i.e., would alter sociability assessment outcomes). Interobserver agreement remained relatively high across conditions. We obtained slightly higher scores for momentary time sampling than duration for most participants, but interobserver agreement was higher for duration for two out of seven participants.

Study 2 included child sessions, improved training prior to the evaluation, and digital momentary time sampling. Results of study 2 suggest that high levels of procedural fidelity can be achieved while collecting momentary time sampling or duration data during sessions conducted with a role-play partner or child. For most participants, we obtained similar, high levels of interobserver agreement for momentary time sampling and duration across role-play and child sessions, but one participant obtained much lower interobserver agreement for momentary time sampling. In part, we may have obtained these differences because a single error impacts interobserver agreement more for momentary time sampling (i.e., 16 opportunities for agreement) than for duration (i.e., 48 opportunities for agreement).

Across both studies, participants’ preferred method varied between collecting no data, momentary time sampling, or duration data. Generally, participants rated no data collection as more feasible than momentary time sampling or duration. Participants rated momentary time sampling and duration as similarly feasible, with most assigning both a rating of either 2 or 3 on scale of 1 (i.e., very easy/feasible) to 5 (i.e., very difficult/infeasible). One aspect that may have prevented momentary time sampling from being more feasible than duration is that participants were required to attend to a timer to determine when each 30-s interval elapsed, which may have increased difficulty relative to formats in which the end of the interval is signaled by an auditory or tactile prompt (e.g., Gunter et al., 2003; Seward & Redner, 2024). Overall, results from both studies indicate that it may be feasible to maintain relatively high procedural fidelity and interobserver agreement when collecting live discontinuous or continuous data while concurrently implementing sociability assessment procedures. Additionally, there was correspondence between the data collection method that was most preferred and the data collection method with the highest performance for some participants (e.g., Lonnie and Vivian), but not others (e.g., Victoria). It may be useful for future research to further investigate the relation between preference and correspondence, especially as performance improves with repeated practice (e.g., Bacotti et al., 2021; Thibodeaux et al., 2025).

The current study extends previous research by evaluating the feasibility of different methods of data collection while implementing assessment procedures. Researchers have evaluated the feasibility of methods of data collection when it was the only response requirement (Hanley et al., 2007; Kolt & Rapp, 2014) and even systematically manipulated the difficulty of data collection (e.g., Rolider et al., 2012). However, to our knowledge, the current study is the first to evaluate the feasibility of different methods of data collection while implementing assessment procedures. In clinical practice, behavior analysts may often serve the dual role of implementer and data collector (e.g. Fiske & Delmolino, 2012). Therefore, further evaluating the feasibility of methods of data collection with the concurrent requirements of conducting sociability assessments and expanding this type of evaluation beyond sociability assessments are important directions for future research. Our findings suggest that implementers can conduct sociability assessments with high procedural fidelity while concurrently collecting accurate momentary time sampling or duration data.

Although previous research on sociability assessments suggest they may require additional personnel or non-assessment time to conduct, the current studies demonstrate that, following substantial training, a single individual can readily serve the dual role of implementer and data collector. Therefore, additional personnel or non-assessment time are not required and need not hinder sociability assessments from being routinely conducted in research or clinical practice. Both momentary time sampling and duration may allow for similar conclusions about the function of social interaction (see Supporting Information A), so behavior analysts may prioritize their data collection preference or default to collecting duration data, which may allow for more precise conclusions and could facilitate within-session analyses (Morris & Pizzuto, 2023). However, an interesting direction for future research would be to evaluate methods of collecting data on other clinically relevant participant behaviors during sociability assessments (e.g., Morris & Vollmer, 2022a), which may be more feasible in addition to momentary time sampling than duration data collection.

The results of both studies replicate the findings of previous research evaluating the feasibility of using discontinuous methods of data collection: (a) momentary time sampling can yield high levels of interobserver agreement and (b) methods that are rated as feasible or preferred by data collectors can readily be identified (Hanley et al., 2007; Kolt & Rapp, 2014). Whereas previous research has focused on comparing the feasibility of and preference for different interval sizes (Hanley et al., 2007) or different interval sizes and different methods of discontinuous measurement (Kolt & Rapp, 2014), the current study extends this literature by including a continuous method of data collection as a point of comparison. Many participants obtained high interobserver agreement and provided similar feasibility ratings across the continuous and discontinuous methods evaluated.

The current study has several limitations with corresponding directions for future research. First, our participants were undergraduate students with little to no experience providing behavior analytic services. It remains unclear whether our results would generalize to students with other demographic backgrounds, registered behavior technicians with extensive experience, or practicing, masters-level, board-certified behavior analysts. Replicating the current study with these populations is an important direction for future research.

Second, we somewhat arbitrarily selected 30 s as the interval to be used in the evaluations of momentary time sampling. Our results suggest that momentary time sampling with 30-s intervals may allow for similar conclusions as duration data (see Supporting Information A) and may be feasible to use while implementing sociability assessment procedures. However, smaller intervals could further improve correspondence with duration, whereas larger intervals could further improve feasibility. Future research should parametrically evaluate the effects of different momentary time sampling intervals on correspondence, feasibility, and preference.

Third, aspects of our training, role-play sessions (i.e., rate of switching, type of prompting), and data collection systems varied unsystematically between studies 1 and 2. These unsystematic procedural variations preclude clear conclusions about why slightly different results may have been obtained across studies and the impact of these procedural variations will be important to explore in future research. For example, systematic, within-participant manipulation of the modality of data collection (e.g., digital versus paper and pen) and the characteristics of role-play partner behavior (e.g., high versus low rate of switching) may elucidate variables that influence procedural fidelity, interobserver agreement, and preference.

Fourth, disagreements may have differentially impacted interobserver agreement for momentary time sampling in comparison to duration. As mentioned above, there were only 16 opportunities for agreement with momentary time sampling (i.e., 30-s intervals) whereas there were 48 opportunities for agreement with duration (i.e., 10-s intervals), meaning the extent to which each disagreement decreased interobserver agreement varied across the data collection methods. It is possible that interobserver agreement scores were deflated for momentary time sampling due to the smaller number of opportunities for agreement. We used 10-s intervals in evaluating interobserver agreement for duration data to remain consistent with previous research (e.g., Morris & Vollmer, 2021). However, in future research it may be useful to attempt to equate the interobserver agreement calculations across data collection methods, potentially by using 30-s bins and interval-by-interval agreement in evaluating interobserver agreement for duration data.

Finally, participants’ preference and feasibility ratings often did not correspond to their procedural fidelity and interobserver agreement data. This type of disconnect between preference and efficacy (e.g., Goldman et al., 2025; Morris et al., 2024) or indirect versus direct measures of preference (e.g., Ferguson et al., 2019; Hanley, 2010; Morris & Vollmer, 2022c) is not necessarily surprising, but nevertheless limits the conclusions that can be drawn about preference and feasibility. In future research, it could be useful to incorporate a more formal evaluation of preference and social validity by incorporating a concurrent chains choice phase (e.g., Auten et al., 2024; Linton et al., 2025) in which participants select the method of data collection they will implement in the upcoming session. Additionally, our preference questionnaire described the no data collection condition as collecting retrospective data after sessions occurred. This isolated preference for implementing sociability assessments with or without concurrent data collection requirements and ensured that whether or not any data collection was required could not exert a confounding influence on preference. However, participants were not required to collect data from video recordings following no data collection sessions during the recording feasibility evaluation. Future research on simultaneous data collection and implementation may evaluate the no live data collection condition with a retrospective data collection requirement to further mirror research and clinical practice.

The current studies constitute important steps toward improving the feasibility and adoptability of sociability assessments. Previous research has focused on reducing assessment complexity (e.g., Morris & Vollmer, 2021) as well as the amount of time (e.g., Morris & Vollmer, 2020c) and number of sessions (e.g., Morris & Pizzuto, 2023) required to draw conclusions about the function of social interaction, but the current study is the first to directly evaluate the feasibility of conducting sociability assessments. The trajectory of this line of research parallels the increasing focus on effectiveness, feasibility, and adoptability that can be seen in other areas (e.g., Ghaemmaghami et al., 2021). However, rather than progressing from efficacy to effectiveness, more research is needed in both domains (Morris & Pizzuto, 2023). Future research should continue to explore methods of improving the efficacy and precision with which sociability assessments delineate the function of social interaction while also continuing to improve the effectiveness, feasibility, and adoptability of sociability assessments for routine use in research and practice. Beyond the nascent literature on sociability assessment, the methods employed in the current studies also have the potential to impact other areas of research. Evaluating the feasibility of implementing procedures with concurrent data collection requirements has the potential to improve the adoptability and dissemination of myriad behavior-analytic assessment or intervention strategies and could facilitate progress toward closing the research-to-practice gap.
