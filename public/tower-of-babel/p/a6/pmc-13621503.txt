Occult odontogenic infection as a possible trigger of post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis: a case report and scoping review highlighting a possible oral–systemic association
Authors: Nara Sarmento Macedo Signorelli, Camilla Christian Gomes Moura, Danilo Cassiano Ferraz, Marlene A dos Reis, Juliana Reis Machado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic odontogenic infections may represent overlooked infectious foci in patients with infection-related glomerulonephritis when conventional sources, such as skin or upper respiratory tract infections, are not identified. This manuscript first reports the case of a 36-year-old man who presented with nephritic syndrome and acute kidney injury requiring dialysis. Renal biopsy demonstrated infection-related glomerulonephritis with features compatible with post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, although antistreptococcal serology was not available. Extensive medical investigation did not identify a conventional infectious source. A subsequent dental evaluation revealed an untreated chronic endodontic infection associated with pulpal necrosis, which was considered a plausible odontogenic infectious focus rather than a definitively proven causal source. After endodontic treatment, together with supportive medical care, the patient experienced progressive recovery of renal function and dialysis was discontinued. To contextualize this clinical observation, a scoping review was then conducted according to the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and reported in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines, aiming to map previously published cases in which oral or odontogenic infections were described as possible infectious foci associated with post-streptococcal or infection-related glomerulonephritis. Searches were performed in MEDLINE/PubMed, LILACS, SciELO, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, Open Access Theses and Dissertations, and DANS Easy. Eligible reports were charted descriptively, focusing on patient characteristics, oral infectious source, renal diagnosis, treatment, and clinical outcome. The available evidence remains limited and predominantly descriptive, supporting hypothesis generation rather than causal inference. Nevertheless, both the present case and the mapped literature reinforce the importance of considering occult oral infectious foci in selected cases of infection-related glomerulonephritis of unclear origin. Introduction

Glomerulonephritis (GN) comprises a heterogeneous group of immune-mediated diseases characterized by inflammatory injury to the renal glomeruli, leading to hematuria, proteinuria, and impaired renal function, among which post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN) represents a classic and well-recognized post-infectious subtype [1–3]. Although GN can be triggered by bacterial, viral, parasitic, or fungal infections, infection-related GN is commonly associated with β-hemolytic group A Streptococcus infections originating from the skin or respiratory tract, and occasionally streptococci from groups C or G [3, 4]. These infections may develop after complete resolution of the primary infection and a latent period, or in association with an ongoing infection [3].

Children are predominantly affected by PSGN; however, debilitated elderly individuals and, to a lesser extent, young adults may also be affected [1, 4, 5]. While the development of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) due to PSGN is rare, the overall prognosis is generally worse in adults than in children [1, 6], especially when other risk factors, such as diabetes mellitus, coexist [4].

Streptococcus species have been identified as components of the caries-associated microbiome [7] and, to a lesser extent, of endodontic infections [8]. Despite the well-established association between PSGN and infections of the skin or upper respiratory tract, several clinical series have reported that in a substantial proportion of cases the triggering infectious focus cannot be clearly identified, even after extensive clinical investigation [9, 10]. This diagnostic gap raises the possibility that less obvious or clinically silent infectious sites may act as overlooked sources of streptococcal antigenic stimulation. Because direct microbiological confirmation is not always available in clinical reports, such associations should be interpreted cautiously and framed as biologically plausible rather than definitive. In this context, oral and odontogenic infections—particularly chronic endodontic infections—represent a biologically plausible yet underexplored reservoir of Streptococcus species capable of sustaining systemic immune responses [7, 8].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to report a case of infection-related GN with features compatible with PSGN in which an untreated endodontic infection was identified as a possible infectious trigger. This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE (CAse REport) guidelines [10], and a scoping review of previously published cases was conducted to contextualize the clinical findings. By integrating an individual clinical observation with a synthesis of the available evidence, this study seeks to contribute to a better understanding of the potential role of oral infections in the pathogenesis of post-infectious GN and to emphasize the relevance of oral health in interdisciplinary clinical decision-making. The review was intended to map available evidence and generate hypotheses, not to establish definitive causation.

Case report

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local Research Ethics Committee (CAAE: 44083620.9.0000.5565). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient prior to inclusion in the study, authorizing participation in the research and the publication of clinical data.

A 36-year-old asymptomatic male patient was referred to the Endodontics Clinic of the School of Dentistry, Federal University of Uberlândia, on November 12, 2020, for dental evaluation following a previous episode of acute kidney injury.

Medical history

The patient had been in good general health until October 7, 2020, when he presented to a primary health care center with generalized edema, arterial hypertension, and pleural effusion (Fig. 1). He was subsequently evaluated by several physicians and underwent renal function assessment. All laboratory tests performed at admission, during hospitalization, and after discharge are presented in Table 1. The clinical history suggested GN without an apparent cause, and the patient reported no recent infection of the airways, ears, or skin. He also denied a previous diagnosis of COVID-19. Antistreptolysin O and anti-DNase B titers were not available in the medical records, which limits definitive attribution to a recent streptococcal infection. The patient was hospitalized for 20 days, and his discharge medications included losartan potassium (50 mg), furosemide (40 mg), amlodipine besylate (5 mg), prednisone (60 mg), and hydralazine hydrochloride (100 mg). On October 28, 2020, the patient underwent kidney biopsy.

Renal biopsy

Renal tissue was obtained by percutaneous kidney biopsy and processed for routine renal pathology evaluation, including light microscopy and direct immunofluorescence. For light microscopy, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, Sirius red, and Jones methenamine silver; frozen sections were used for immunofluorescence staining against IgA, IgG, IgM, kappa, lambda, C1q, C3, and fibrinogen. Under light microscopy, nine glomeruli were evaluated (Fig. 2A, B, and C). Hypercellularity was observed in all glomeruli owing to cell proliferation and intense neutrophil infiltration. Rare capillary loops showed duplication of the basement membrane. A mononuclear inflammatory infiltrate permeated by a few scattered neutrophils was observed in the tubulointerstitial compartment. Interstitial fibrosis or tubular atrophy was not observed. The arterioles and interlobular arteries were within the normal range, and the arciform artery was not sampled.

Thirteen glomeruli were analyzed using immunofluorescence (Fig. 2D, E, and F). Anti-IgA serum was negative in the glomeruli. Anti-IgG serum (Fig. 2D) was moderately positive in the mesangial area, capillary loops, and subendothelial and subepithelial areas of the glomeruli. Anti-IgM serum was negative in the glomeruli. Anti-kappa and anti-lambda sera showed staining similar to the heavy chains of immunoglobulins. Anti-C1q serum (Fig. 2E) was moderately positive in the mesangial area, capillary loops, and subendothelial and subepithelial areas of the glomeruli. Anti-C3 serum (Fig. 2F) was strongly positive in the mesangial area, capillary loops, and subendothelial and subepithelial areas of the glomeruli, with positivity also observed in the vascular wall and focal peritubular area. Anti-fibrinogen serum was negative. Renal biopsy under light and fluorescence microscopy revealed acute diffuse global GN (GNDA), likely associated with infection (post-infectious or infectious), with zero out of 22 sclerosed glomeruli and no tubulointerstitial effects (interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy). Considering the absence of documented antistreptococcal serology and the immunofluorescence pattern with positivity for IgG, C3, and C1q, the renal findings were interpreted as infection-related GN with characteristics compatible with PSGN.

Dental history

The patient was asymptomatic at the initial examination and stated that he had pain six months earlier in the upper left first molar area following restorative treatment failure, which disappeared within a few days.

Oral examination and diagnostics tests

A careful clinical evaluation was performed to identify possible infectious foci, and a complete periapical radiographic series was obtained (Fig. 3). The soft tissues of the mouth and pharynx were normal. Because the clinical objective was to identify subtle odontogenic and endodontic infectious foci with high-resolution intraoral imaging, a periapical series was selected. In similar future cases, panoramic radiography/orthopantomography may be considered as an initial screening approach to reduce the number of exposures when clinically appropriate.

Complete dental examination. A Periapical radiographic series. Intraoral photographs: B right lateral view; C left lateral view; D frontal view

Tooth 46 was missing, having been extracted approximately 20 years earlier, and the third molars were impacted, with no exposure to the oral environment and no apparent caries (Fig. 3). During periodontal evaluation, the patient did not show periodontal pockets or probing depths greater than 2 mm, bleeding points, tooth mobility, or gingivitis. Some bacterial plaque foci were observed, without repercussions on the periodontal clinical condition. Figure 4 shows tooth 26, which had an extensive carious lesion and apparent exposure of the pulp chamber to the oral environment, and the tooth did not respond to electrical or thermal testing. These clinical findings, together with the radiographic findings, were indicative of pulpal necrosis requiring endodontic treatment. The probable etiology was extensive caries that had been improperly restored with composite resin; the restoration had become dislodged, exposing the tooth to microbial contamination and consequent necrosis.

The physician responsible for the clinical management of PSGN was contacted and informed of the endodontic treatment plan, which consisted of root canal therapy for tooth 26 followed by restorative treatment. Because there was no evidence of pain, swelling, or elevated temperature, short-term antibiotic coverage with 2 g of amoxicillin 1 h before the first appointment was considered adequate.

Endodontic therapy

The patient received prophylactic antibiotic coverage as described above and underwent prophylaxis and supragingival scaling in all quadrants. Endodontic treatment began on December 11, 2020. All endodontic procedures were performed under local anesthesia with 2% lidocaine and 1:100,000 epinephrine. Routine endodontic access was performed under rubber dam isolation, and all four root canal orifices were located using a dental loupe (Zeiss Pico; Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) at 3.5× magnification. The working lengths were determined using an apex locator (Romiapex A-15; Romidan Dental Solution, Kiryat Ono, Israel) and confirmed radiographically. The canals were enlarged with ProGlider #16.02 (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland), and preparation was completed with WaveOne Gold #25.07 (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland). The apical preparations were enlarged to a #40 K-file (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland). The canals were irrigated with approximately 80 mL of 2,5% sodium hypochlorite, and final irrigation was activated using an EasyClean device (Easy Equipamentos Odontológicos, Belo Horizonte, Minas Gerais, Brazil). Subsequently, calcium hydroxide paste (UltraCal XS; Ultradent Products Inc., South Jordan, UT, USA) was applied as a temporary intracanal dressing for 15 days. The access cavity was temporarily sealed with glass ionomer cement (Ketac Nano; 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA).

The canals were obturated at a second appointment on December 27, 2020, with AH Plus sealer (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland), Tagger’s hybrid technique, and vertically condensed gutta-percha using cold pluggers. The cavity was restored during the same visit with composite resin (Filtek Z350 XT; 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA), and the dental occlusion was checked. The patient was asked to return for endodontic evaluation at 1, 12, 18, and 24 months after treatment completion. The patient returned for follow-up at 1, 18, and 24 months (July 2022) and was completely symptom-free. Clinical examination revealed that the tooth showed no sensitivity to percussion or palpation, no mobility, and probing depths were within the normal range. Radiographic and tomographic assessments revealed a continuous periodontal ligament space with no signs of apical periodontitis (Fig. 5).

Medical status

During the two-year follow-up period, the patient was monitored by a nephrologist and showed complete regression of GN. Blood pressure was controlled at follow-up in January 2021, and antihypertensive medication was discontinued in March 2021. C-reactive protein (CRP), urea, and creatinine levels were within normal ranges in laboratory tests conducted in 2021 (Table 1). New laboratory tests requested during the annual follow-up in 2022 showed even lower levels of these markers (Table 1).

Contextual review of previously reported cases

Contextual review of previously reported cases

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines. The methodological framework was chosen to allow comprehensive mapping of clinical evidence, particularly case reports and case series, which were expected to represent the predominant body of literature addressing the association between PSGN and oral infectious foci. Given the anticipated scarcity and heterogeneity of the evidence, the review was designed as a descriptive and hypothesis-generating synthesis rather than as a confirmatory analysis. The final protocol was retrospectively registered with the Open Science Framework in June 2026 (registration record: https://osf.io/5mx6p/).

Study design and eligibility criteria

The review was guided by a research question structured according to the Population, Concept, and Context (PCC) strategy. The Population consisted of patients diagnosed with PSGN or infection-related GN with features compatible with PSGN; the Concept was the identification of a buccal or odontogenic infectious focus as a possible triggering source; and the Context included clinical case reports and case series. Based on this framework, the guiding question was defined as: “What clinical evidence exists reporting PSGN or infection-related GN temporally associated with infections of oral or odontogenic origin?”.

Case reports, case series, letters to the editor, and expert correspondence describing clinical cases were eligible for inclusion, provided that they reported patients with PSGN or infection-related GN temporally associated with an oral, buccal, or odontogenic infectious focus. Reports describing systemic or infectious diseases in which GN constituted part of the overall clinical presentation were also included when a plausible association with a streptococcal infection of oral origin could be identified. The inclusion of expert correspondence and letters was considered appropriate given the rarity of PSGN associated with oral infectious foci and the consequent scarcity of conventional observational studies.

Eligible studies were required to present sufficient clinical, laboratory, and/or histopathological information to support the diagnosis of GN and to allow assessment of the infectious source. No restrictions were applied regarding patient age, sex, geographic location, year of publication, or language. Studies were excluded if they consisted exclusively of narrative reviews, systematic reviews without original case descriptions, conference abstracts lacking full clinical data, experimental or animal studies, in vitro investigations, or reports of GN unrelated to streptococcal infection or without any reference to an oral or odontogenic infectious focus. Articles in which GN was not clinically or pathologically substantiated were also excluded.

Sources of information, search strategy, and study selection

The electronic databases MEDLINE (via PubMed), LILACS, SciELO, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase were systematically searched. In addition, Open Access Theses and Dissertations (OATD) and Data Archiving and Networked Services (DANS Easy) were consulted to partially capture grey literature.

Search strategies were developed using a combination of controlled vocabulary (MeSH, DeCS, and Emtree terms) and free-text terms related to PSGN, streptococcal infection, and oral or odontogenic infectious sources. Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” were applied, and the strategies were adapted to the syntax and indexing characteristics of each database. No geographic filter was applied to any database search. All retrieved records were imported into EndNote X9 (Thomson Reuters, Toronto, Canada), where duplicates were automatically removed and subsequently checked manually. Records were then exported to Rayyan QCRI (Qatar Computing Research Institute, Doha, Qatar) for screening and selection.

Study selection process

Two reviewers (NSMS and DCF) independently screened titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. Full texts were retrieved for records considered relevant or when eligibility could not be determined based on title and abstract alone. Disagreements at any stage of the selection process were resolved by discussion and, when necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer (CCGM). Reference lists of all included studies were also manually screened to identify additional eligible reports not captured in the electronic searches.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (NSMS and DCF) using a standardized data collection form. Extracted variables included: author, year of publication, country, patient demographics, clinical presentation, diagnostic criteria for PSGN, laboratory and histopathological findings, description of the oral or odontogenic infectious focus, microbiological evidence when available, therapeutic interventions (including dental management), and clinical outcomes (Supplementary Table).

Given the exploratory nature of this scoping review and its objective to map available clinical evidence, no formal critical appraisal of methodological quality or risk of bias was conducted, in accordance with JBI recommendations for scoping reviews.

The findings were synthesized descriptively, with results summarized in tables and narrative form to highlight patterns, gaps, and consistencies in the reported association between PSGN and buccal infectious sources. Because the evidence base consisted of case reports, the synthesis was not intended to quantify risk or confirm causality.

Results

The electronic search across databases retrieved a total of 1,043 records, including PubMed (n = 443), Embase (n = 330), Scopus (n = 146), Web of Science (n = 62), LILACS (n = 47), and SciELO (n = 15). An additional 33 records were identified through grey literature sources, comprising Open Access Theses and Dissertations (OATD; n = 20), the DANS Easy repository (n = 13), and three studies identified through manual reference list screening. After removal of duplicate records (n = 100) and exclusion of studies deemed ineligible by automated screening tools (n = 102), 874 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 866 were excluded for failing to meet the eligibility criteria, primarily because they did not report an association between GN and an oral or odontogenic infectious source.

Eight articles were assessed in full text for eligibility. Following full-text review, one study was excluded due to the absence of a dental or buccal infectious focus, leaving seven studies that fulfilled all inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the scoping review. PRISMA flow diagram

All included studies were case reports, published between 2003 and 2025, and originated from diverse geographic regions, including South America, North America, Asia, and the Middle East. The seven reported patients included six males and one female, with ages ranging from 20 months to 86 years; five of the seven cases involved adults. In all included reports, an oral or odontogenic infectious focus was explicitly identified, such as dental abscesses, infected teeth, or advanced dental caries associated with active infection.

Regarding the infectious etiology, streptococcal species were the most frequently implicated pathogens, reported in all included studies. Four reports identified Granulicatella species, two reported Streptococcus spp., and in one case, streptococcal infection was presumed based on clinical presentation, serological findings, or histopathological features consistent with post-infectious GN, even in the absence of microbiological isolation. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as supportive of a plausible association rather than as proof of direct pathogen concordance.

Renal diagnoses were most commonly described as post-infectious GN [11–13], acute diffuse GN [14, 15], or infection-related GN [16], frequently exhibiting features compatible with PSGN.

Management strategies consistently included systemic antibiotic therapy [12–17] combined with eradication of the oral infectious focus, such as tooth extraction [11, 14, 15, 17] or drainage of dental abscesses [13]. Renal outcomes varied among the reported cases. Four patients experienced partial or complete recovery of renal function following infection control and supportive management [12–15], whereas two progressed to severe renal impairment requiring renal replacement therapy [11, 17] and one experienced a fatal outcome related to systemic infectious complications [16]. Poorer prognoses were more frequently observed in older patients and in those with complex systemic infections, particularly when GN occurred in the context of infective endocarditis.

Discussion

For a physician to suspect PSGN, the patient should have a history of untreated streptococcal infection occurring one to six weeks before the onset of the characteristic clinical manifestations of the disease [1]. Infections commonly associated with PSGN originate in the skin, throat, and ears, and the initial symptoms preceding the clinical manifestation typically include sore throat, swollen tonsils covered with a white exudate, fever, headache, skin rash, and middle ear infection [2]. However, the proportion of cases in which the streptococcal source cannot be identified remains clinically relevant, particularly among adults with atypical presentations.

In the present case, the patient did not report any classical prodromal symptoms and sought medical attention because of peripheral edema, fluid retention, hypertension, and nephrotic syndrome detected on urinalysis. Despite the presence of clinical signs suggestive of GN, renal biopsy remained a determining diagnostic tool for PSGN [1], as performed in this case. However, because ASO and anti-DNase B titers were not available, the diagnosis was more cautiously framed as infection-related GN with features compatible with PSGN. The scoping review revealed heterogeneity in the histopathological findings reported across cases. When available, renal biopsy commonly demonstrated endocapillary proliferative GN, prominent C3-dominant immune deposits on immunofluorescence, and variable immunoglobulin deposition, including IgA, IgM, and IgG [9–18]. Crescent formation was described in a subset of cases [11, 14, 16, 17]. In some reports, IgA predominance was emphasized, raising diagnostic considerations of IgA-dominant infection-related GN rather than primary IgA nephropathy [9, 10, 18].

Several studies have associated poor oral health with an increased risk of complications in patients with chronic kidney disease [19, 20], as well as a lower risk of mortality among dialysis patients who received adequate endodontic treatment [21]. However, the effects of periapical disease on the development of renal alterations are not well understood [22, 23]. In this case report, the endodontic infection was identified as a possible and clinically relevant candidate infectious focus, rather than as a definitively proven causal source. The findings from the literature review further highlight that a substantial proportion of infection-related GN cases are reported with unclear, unknown, or nonspecific infectious origins [9, 18, 24], suggesting that occult sources—such as chronic dental infections—may be overlooked in routine clinical evaluation.

The seven studies included in the scoping review contributed clinically relevant parallels to the present case. Matsukura et al. described biopsy-proven acute PSGN with atypical systemic manifestations in a child [12], whereas Gadi et al. reported PSGN associated with a dental abscess in a 6-year-old boy [15]. Other included reports involved infective endocarditis or systemic infection caused by oral commensals, including Granulicatella or Streptococcus species, with associated glomerular injury [11, 13, 16, 17]. Together, these reports support the concept that oral infectious foci may be overlooked in selected cases of PSGN or infection-related GN; however, they also reinforce the need for cautious interpretation because the evidence remains limited to case-level observations.

Granulicatella adiacens is a fastidious Gram-positive coccus historically classified among the nutritionally variant streptococci and phylogenetically closely related to viridans group streptococci, sharing similar ecological niches and pathogenic behaviors [25, 26]. This organism is part of the normal oral microbiota and exhibits overlapping virulence traits with streptococci, including adherence to host tissues, biofilm formation, and the ability to cause persistent, low-grade bacteremia [11, 16]. Taxonomic reclassification based on 16 S rRNA sequencing distinguished Granulicatella from Streptococcus; however, their clinical profiles remain comparable, particularly regarding infective endocarditis and other systemic immune-mediated complications [26, 27]. Importantly, G. adiacens infections have been reported in association with immune complex–mediated diseases, paralleling mechanisms described for streptococcal infections, including complement activation and sustained inflammatory responses [28], reinforcing its relevance within the spectrum of streptococcus-related systemic disorders.

Indeed, the data in this report deviate in several aspects from what is expected from the medical histories of patients with PSGN. First, it is uncommon for young adults to develop this condition because it is more commonly observed in children and the elderly [5]. Additionally, the infection did not originate from commonly affected sites [4], and the exact latency period between the onset of renal disease symptoms and the onset of endodontic infection could not be determined. However, laboratory tests, clinical findings, and renal biopsy supported a diagnosis of infection-related GN with features compatible with PSGN. Therefore, the physician investigated other possible causes, including oral cavity infection.

Referral of the patient to the dentist was essential to investigate a possible oral infectious source, as the patient had a compatible history of symptomatic pulpitis that progressed to pulp necrosis in tooth 26, with no other sources of infection detected in the oral cavity. Although PSGN is most commonly associated with beta-hemolytic group A Streptococcus, other streptococci may also be involved [4]. A study demonstrated that certain strains of S. mutans found in pulp tissues and adhering to the root canal [29] can invade human endothelial cells and cause bacteremia, contributing to systemic virulence [30]. Because microbiological analysis of the root canal content was not performed in this case, concordance between the endodontic microbiota and the systemic immune-mediated renal process could not be demonstrated. Therefore, a similar mechanism can only be proposed as a biologically plausible hypothesis.

Adults with PSGN can develop renal insufficiency, hypertension, and recurrent proteinuria if the underlying infection is left untreated [3]. However, appropriate treatment of the infection can lead to favorable prognosis, as confirmed by the follow-up test results in this case report, showing complete remission of the patient’s condition, discontinuation of medication, and absence of new disease episodes after endodontic treatment over 2.5 years. This finding is also reflected in the literature review, in which recovery of renal function after infection control was reported in four cases [12–15], including discontinuation of dialysis after removal of the oral infectious focus [14]. Nevertheless, improvement after dental treatment should be interpreted in conjunction with supportive medical care and does not, by itself, prove causality. Therefore, healthcare professionals should be aware of endodontic infections as possible contributors to infection-related GN, particularly when the etiological factor cannot be clearly determined.

Clinical implications

This case highlights the diagnostic challenges encountered in infection-related GN when classical infectious triggers are not identified. While PSGN is traditionally associated with pharyngeal or cutaneous streptococcal infections, persistent odontogenic infections may act as an overlooked source of chronic antigenic exposure capable of triggering immune-mediated renal injury. Awareness of this potential association is clinically relevant, particularly in adults presenting with infection-related GN and no identifiable infectious focus after standard evaluation. Early recognition and elimination of oral infectious foci may contribute to improved renal outcomes and should be considered as part of a comprehensive diagnostic approach. These implications should be viewed as clinical considerations generated by a rare case and a descriptive scoping review, rather than as definitive evidence for routine causal attribution.

Limitations

This report describes a single clinical observation, which limits the ability to establish a definitive causal relationship between the odontogenic infection and GN. In addition, microbiological confirmation of the dental infectious agent was not available. ASO and anti-DNase B titers were not documented, limiting direct confirmation of recent streptococcal infection. The immunofluorescence pattern, including IgG, C3, and C1q positivity, also supports a cautious interpretation within the broader spectrum of infection-related GN. Other potential infectious sources, including subclinical upper respiratory infection, transient bacteremia, or unrecognized skin infection, cannot be completely excluded. The temporal relationship between renal disease onset, dental diagnosis, endodontic intervention, and renal recovery is clinically relevant but remains indirect. Finally, the scoping review included only a small number of case reports; therefore, its findings are descriptive and hypothesis-generating and cannot be used to estimate incidence, risk, or causality. Nevertheless, the temporal association between eradication of the oral focus and renal recovery, together with supportive evidence from previously published cases, strengthens the plausibility of this association.

Patient perspective

The patient reported that he had never anticipated that an oral infectious focus could lead to systemic health problems. He expressed relief at having an infectious source identified after a prolonged period of investigation and remained optimistic throughout the diagnostic process. The patient also expressed satisfaction with the endodontic treatment performed and with the multidisciplinary approach adopted by the healthcare team, which contributed to his full recovery.

Conclusion

This case report and scoping review support the concept that oral and odontogenic infections may represent possible underrecognized contributors to infection-related GN with features compatible with PSGN, particularly in patients in whom conventional infectious sources are not identified. In this context, interdisciplinary collaboration between nephrology and dentistry is essential to improve etiological investigation and guide targeted interventions. The findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Future longitudinal, prospective, and interventional studies are needed to better characterize the contribution of oral infections to the pathogenesis of PSGN and infection-related GN and to define standardized approaches for dental assessment in patients with glomerular disease of uncertain origin.
