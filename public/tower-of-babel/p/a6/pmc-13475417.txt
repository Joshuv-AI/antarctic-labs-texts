Effectiveness of ω-3 Supplementation on Biochemical, Clinical, and Inflammatory Parameters in Patients with Diabetes: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Jessica Paola Loaiza-Giraldo, Rocío Valdivia-Arroyo, Valentina Greve-Quezada, Alonso Quivira-Muñoz, Orlando Silva-Perez, Trinidad Soublette-Tocornal, Alejandro Bruna-Mejias, Pablo Nova-Baeza, Mathias Orellana-Donoso, Maria P Moya, Juan Sanchis-Gimeno, Vitor E Valenti, Maria Piagkou, Marko Konschake, Juan José Valenzuela-Fuenzalida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Omega-3 (n–3) polyunsaturated fatty acids, including marine-derived eicosapentaenoic acid and docosahexaenoic acid, as well as plant-derived formulations rich in α-linolenic acid, have been proposed as adjunctive therapy in diabetes because of their potential effects on lipid metabolism, inflammation, oxidative stress, and neuroprotection. Searches were performed in MEDLINE, EMBASE, SCOPUS, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, and Web of Science through January 2026. Outcomes included lipid parameters, glycemic markers, inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers, and corneal nerve branch density (CNBD). Random-effects models were applied. Eleven trials met the criteria for quantitative synthesis. Pooled analyses showed no significant effects on triglycerides, low-density or high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, glycated hemoglobin, fasting plasma glucose, or inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers, with substantial heterogeneity across several outcomes. In contrast, fasting insulin and homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance were significantly reduced with low heterogeneity, and CNBD showed a consistent improvement with no detectable heterogeneity, suggesting potential benefits for insulin sensitivity and small-fiber nerve integrity. Overall, the certainty of evidence ranged from very low to low, mainly because of heterogeneity, imprecision, and small sample sizes. ω-3 supplementation demonstrated limited effects on most metabolic and inflammatory outcomes in diabetes. Observed improvements in insulin sensitivity indices and CNBD warrant further investigation in adequately powered trials with longer follow-up. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia resulting from impaired insulin secretion, insulin resistance, or a combination of both mechanisms [1]. This metabolic dysregulation affects not only glucose homeostasis but also lipid and protein metabolism, contributing to systemic metabolic dysfunction. When inadequately controlled, diabetes leads to progressive, often asymptomatic, damage to multiple organs and systems [2]. Long-term complications most commonly involve the kidneys (diabetic nephropathy), retina (diabetic retinopathy), peripheral and autonomic nervous systems (diabetic neuropathy), and the cardiovascular system, with cardiovascular disease remaining the leading cause of morbidity and mortality among individuals with diabetes [3].

The global burden of diabetes has increased markedly over the past few decades. By 2022, >800 million individuals worldwide were living with diabetes, compared with ∼200 million in 1990 [4]. During this period, adult prevalence nearly doubled, rising from 7% to 14%, with a disproportionate impact observed in low- and middle-income countries [5]. This rapid increase underscores the urgent need for effective preventive strategies and adjunctive therapeutic approaches applicable across different types of diabetes. The cornerstone of diabetes management remains nonpharmacological intervention, including dietary modification to optimize glycemic control, lipid profiles, and blood pressure, as well as regular physical activity to improve insulin sensitivity, manage body weight, and reduce the risk of complications [6]. When glycemic targets are not achieved, pharmacological therapy is initiated. This includes insulin therapy—essential in type 1 diabetes and advanced type 2 diabetes—as well as oral and injectable glucose-lowering agents, such as metformin, sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, α-glucosidase inhibitors, and incretin-based therapies [7,8]. Treatment selection is guided by individual patient characteristics, diabetes subtype, and comorbidities. Long-chain omega-3 (n–3) PUFAs, specifically EPA and DHA, are predominantly obtained from fatty fish such as salmon, tuna, and mackerel, or from purified fish oil supplements [9]. Upon ingestion, EPA and DHA are incorporated into cell membrane phospholipids and metabolized through cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase pathways into specialized proresolving lipid mediators, including resolvins and protectins. These mediators exert anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cytoprotective effects, modulating inflammatory signaling pathways implicated in the pathogenesis of chronic metabolic diseases [10]. Given the central roles of chronic low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress in the development and progression of diabetes and its associated complications, ω-3 fatty acids have attracted significant interest as a potential adjunctive therapy. In addition to marine-derived EPA and DHA, ω-3 fatty acids also include plant-derived α-linolenic acid (ALA), which is present in sources such as flaxseed and perilla oils. Although the biological effects of ALA may differ from those of EPA and DHA because of limited conversion to long-chain metabolites, these formulations have also been investigated in patients with diabetes and were therefore included in the present review.

International guidelines generally recommend an intake of ∼250 to 500 mg/d of EPA and DHA for the general population to support cardiovascular health, whereas higher pharmacological doses may be used under clinical supervision for specific indications, such as severe hypertriglyceridemia. [11]. Supplementation enables standardized dosing and may reduce exposure to environmental contaminants in certain fish species, provided high-quality preparations are used [12]. In patients with diabetes, particularly those with type 2 diabetes, ω-3 supplementation has been associated with improvements in lipid profiles, and most consistently reductions in triglyceride concentrations, with modest or neutral effects on other lipid fractions [13]. ω-3 fatty acids have also been reported to exert anti-inflammatory effects by downregulating proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, and by enhancing antioxidant capacity, potentially mitigating oxidative stress [14]. However, evidence regarding their effects on glycemic control, including fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), remains inconsistent and appears to vary by diabetes subtype, dosage, formulation, and duration of supplementation [15].

Despite increasing interest, the clinical effects of ω-3 supplementation on biochemical, inflammatory, and functional outcomes across different forms of diabetes remain heterogeneous and incompletely understood. Existing trials differ substantially with respect to diabetes type (type 1, type 2, or gestational diabetes), ω-3 formulation (EPA, DHA, or mixed), dosage, cosupplementation strategies, and follow-up duration, resulting in marked clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Therefore, systematic review and meta-analysis are warranted to synthesize the available evidence regarding the effects of ω-3 supplementation on metabolic, inflammatory, and clinical parameters in patients with diabetes [16]. Given the heterogeneity of populations and interventions, the present synthesis is intended to be exploratory and hypothesis-generating, with the aim of clarifying current evidence and informing the design of adequately powered clinical trials.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [17]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO under the registration number CRD420250655364.

Literature search

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was performed across multiple electronic databases, including MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE, SCOPUS, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, and Web of Science. The search covered all records from database inception through January 2026. Randomized controlled trials and controlled clinical trials published in English or Spanish were considered eligible.

Search terms were applied in various combinations and included: “omega-3 supplementation,” “omega-3 therapy,” “omega-3 fatty acids,” “eicosapentaenoic acid,” “docosahexaenoic acid,” “type 1 diabetes,” “type 2 diabetes,” and “gestational diabetes.” The complete search strategy for each database is provided in Supplemental Table 2. Two reviewers (JJV-F and ML-C) independently screened titles and abstracts of all retrieved records. Full-text articles were obtained for studies considered potentially eligible by either reviewer. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion, and when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (MB-V) adjudicated.

Study selection

Studies were considered eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria:1.Population: adults or pregnant women diagnosed with type 1 diabetes mellitus, type 2 diabetes mellitus, gestational diabetes mellitus, or individuals at high risk of diabetes, according to established diagnostic criteria.2.Intervention: supplementation with ω-3 PUFAs, including EPA, DHA, or combined formulations, administered at any dose or duration.3.Comparator: placebo, standard care, or control intervention without ω-3 supplementation.4.Outcomes: at least one reported biochemical, metabolic, inflammatory, oxidative stress, or functional outcome, including lipid parameters, glycemic markers, insulin resistance indices, inflammatory or oxidative stress biomarkers, or corneal nerve parameters.5.Study design: randomized controlled trials or controlled clinical trials.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: narrative or systematic reviews, meta-analyses, case reports or case series, editorials, letters to the editor, conference abstracts without full data, or nonhuman studies; inclusion of participants without diabetes or with metabolic disorders unrelated to diabetes; interventions not involving ω-3 fatty acids or where the effect of ω-3 supplementation could not be isolated; absence of a comparator group; or insufficient quantitative data to permit extraction.

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers (MB-V and ML-C) independently extracted data from each included study using a standardized data extraction form. Extracted information included author(s) and year of publication; study design; sample size; geographic location; participant characteristics (age, sex distribution, and diabetes type); details of the intervention (ω-3 formulation, dose, duration, and route of administration); comparator characteristics; outcome measures; and corresponding statistical data.

Methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) tool [18], which evaluates the following domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective outcome reporting, and other sources of bias. Each domain was classified as low, unclear, or high risk of bias. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (JPL-G). Interrater agreement was quantified using Cohen’s kappa statistic, indicating substantial agreement (κ = 0.79).

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Data synthesis focused on biochemical, metabolic, inflammatory, and functional outcomes most frequently reported across studies, including triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, VLDL cholesterol, non-HDL cholesterol (mg/dL), FPG (mg/dL), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c, %), fasting insulin (μIU/mL), HOMA-IR, Quantitative Insulin Sensitivity Check Index (QUICKI), inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers [C-reactive protein (CRP)/high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP), malondialdehyde (MDA), total antioxidant capacity (TAC), glutathione (GSH), nitric oxide (NO)], and corneal nerve branch density (CNBD, branches/mm2).

Clinical heterogeneity was anticipated because of differences in diabetes subtype, ω-3 formulation (EPA, DHA, or mixed), dosage (0.5–8 g/d), cosupplementation strategies (e.g., vitamin D, curcumin, aspirin, and vitamin E), and intervention duration (6 wk to 5 y). For outcomes reported in identical units, treatment effects were expressed as mean differences (MDs). The standardized mean difference (SMD) was used only for outcomes measured with different scales, such as CNBD. Pooled estimates were calculated using a random-effects model (DerSimonian–Laird method) to account for expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the χ2 test and quantified with the I2 statistic; values >75% were interpreted as indicating substantial heterogeneity. When 10 or more studies were available, funnel plots were visually inspected to explore potential publication bias. Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted according to intervention duration (short: 1–12 wk; intermediate: 13–26 wk) when data permitted. Exploratory metaregression analyses were performed to assess the potential influence of age, ω-3 dose, intake frequency, and sex distribution; these analyses were explicitly considered hypothesis-generating because of the limited number of studies, potential collinearity among predictors, and absence of correction for multiple testing.

All statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration) and Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software. Given the substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across included trials, all pooled analyses were prespecified as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and results should be interpreted with caution regarding clinical significance and generalizability.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for each meta-analyzed outcome was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [19]. Evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low based on RoB, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Although several trials demonstrated acceptable methodological quality, certainty was frequently downgraded because of extreme inconsistency (I2 >75%), serious imprecision related to small sample sizes and wide confidence intervals (CIs), and indirectness arising from heterogeneous populations, formulations, and intervention durations. Consequently, the overall certainty of evidence for most biochemical and inflammatory outcomes ranged from low to very low, whereas CNBD achieved low certainty with consistent findings across studies. GRADE assessments were conducted using GRADEpro, with results summarized in the “Summary of Findings” table (Supplemental Table 3) [19,20].

Among the 22 included studies, 1 study exclusively enrolled individuals with type 1 diabetes, 10 enrolled individuals with type 2 diabetes, 4 enrolled women with gestational diabetes, 2 included individuals with prediabetes or at high risk of developing type 2 diabetes, and 1 large trial enrolled participants with diabetes irrespective of subtype. The studies were conducted between 1992 and 2022 across several countries, including the United States, Germany, Australia, Iran, the United Kingdom, China, Canada, Brazil, and South Korea. Overall, 20,013 participants were enrolled (experimental, n = 10,013; control, n = 10,000), with mean ages ranging from 30 to 65 y and follow-up durations ranging from 6 wk to 5 y. Interventions included marine-derived EPA/DHA preparations as well as plant-derived ω-3 formulations, including perilla oil and flaxseed oil, administered at doses ranging from 500 mg/d to 8 g/d. Control interventions included olive oil, sunflower oil, liquid paraffin, and cornstarch. Outcomes assessed across studies included glycemic indices, lipid parameters, inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers, vascular and renal outcomes, periodontal outcomes, corneal nerve measures, and patient-reported outcomes. Eleven studies provided sufficient comparable data for quantitative synthesis [23,27,28,30,[33], [34], [35],37,[40], [41], [42]], whereas the remaining studies contributed to the qualitative synthesis.

RoB assessment in individual studies

RoB was assessed using the Cochrane RoB tool and showed mixed methodological quality across domains. Random sequence generation was judged to be of low risk in 10 studies [23,27,28,30,[33], [34], [35],37,41,42], with 1 study unclear [40]. Allocation concealment was low risk in 4 studies [34,35,37,40] and high risk in 7 [23,27,28,30,33,41,42]. Blinding of participants and personnel was low risk in 9 studies [23,28,30,33,35,37,[40], [41], [42]] and high risk in 2 [27,34]. Blinding of outcome assessment was low risk in 4 studies [23,33,35,42] and high risk in 7 [27,28,30,34,37,40,41]. Attrition bias was low risk in 6 studies [27,28,30,35,37,41] and high risk in 5 [23,33,34,40,42]. Selective reporting was low risk in 9 studies [23,27,28,30,33,34,[40], [41], [42]] and high risk in 2 [35,37]. Other bias was low risk in 6 studies [28,30,33,34,40,41] and high risk in 5 [23,27,35,37,42]. Other methodological considerations: placebo selection

Control interventions exhibited considerable variability, encompassing inert agents such as liquid paraffin and cornstarch, as well as vegetable oils, including sunflower and olive oils, which may exert metabolic effects. Multiple trials employed matched placebo capsules to ensure blinding, as seen in the studies conducted by Jamilian et al. [28] Differences in placebo composition and their metabolic neutrality likely contributed to heterogeneity in outcomes, warranting consideration in the interpretation of pooled results.

Synthesis of results

Meta-analyses were performed in RevMan 5.4 using continuous outcome data and the inverse-variance method. When SDs were not reported, they were derived from CIs using SD = √n × (upper limit − lower limit)/3.92. Effects were oriented so that reductions represented benefit where clinically appropriate. Given substantial clinical heterogeneity, pooled estimates were interpreted as exploratory/hypothesis-generating.

Adverse effects

Most trials did not report frequent or serious adverse effects. When reported, adverse events were generally mild and included gastrointestinal symptoms (e.g., nausea and diarrhea), headache, cough, and respiratory infections, observed in small proportions of participants in Mozaffarian et al. [34] and Holman et al. [41].

Exploratory metaregression

An exploratory metaregression assessed whether age, intervention duration, intake frequency, ω-3 dose, or sex distribution moderated metabolic outcomes. Given the limited number of studies per outcome, potential collinearity (dose–duration–frequency), and lack of multiplicity adjustment, results were interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Associations were observed for FPG with weekly intake frequency (β = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.01, 1.07; P = 0.04635); LDL cholesterol with daily dose (β = −0.47; 95% CI: −0.61, −0.33; P < 0.00001); HDL cholesterol with weekly intake frequency (β = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.09, 1.44; P = 0.02668); total cholesterol ratio with daily dose (β = 2.19; 95% CI: 1.25, 3.14; P = 0.00001); triglycerides with daily dose (β = −0.39; 95% CI: −0.48, −0.30; P < 0.00001); HbA1c with weekly intake frequency (β = −1.12; 95% CI: −2.14, −0.09; P = 0.03239); HOMA-IR with age (β = −4.43; 95% CI: −5.28, −3.57; P < 0.00001); and daily dose (β = −0.68; 95% CI: −0.79, −0.56; P < 0.00001). These findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of studies and the exploratory nature of the analyses (Table 2).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized randomized evidence on the effects of ω-3 fatty acid supplementation on metabolic, inflammatory, oxidative stress, and functional outcomes in individuals with diabetes. Overall, the findings indicate that ω-3 supplementation does not consistently improve glycemic control or most lipid fractions, whereas selected outcomes, particularly insulin sensitivity indices and CNBDs, showed directionally favorable or statistically significant effects. However, the certainty of evidence was predominantly low to very low, and the quantitative synthesis should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Metabolic and lipid outcomes

Across pooled analyses, ω-3 supplementation did not significantly improve HbA1c or FPG, suggesting that ω-3 fatty acids should not be considered a glucose-lowering intervention in diabetes management. These neutral findings are consistent with earlier systematic reviews in type 2 diabetes, which reported no meaningful effects on long-term glycemic control despite improvements in lipid concentrations [43,44]. With respect to lipid metabolism, triglyceride concentrations showed a directionally favorable reduction in the overall pooled analysis, but this effect did not reach statistical significance and was accompanied by substantial heterogeneity. Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested that interventions lasting 13 to 26 wk were associated with statistically significant reductions in triglycerides, whereas shorter interventions were not. This temporal pattern is biologically plausible because suppression of hepatic VLDL synthesis and enhanced clearance of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins may require prolonged exposure [43]. Nevertheless, the small number of contributing studies and marked heterogeneity substantially limit confidence in the magnitude and generalizability of this effect. LDL and HDL cholesterol remained unchanged across both global and subgroup analyses, indicating that ω-3 supplementation does not consistently affect these lipid fractions in diabetic populations, in agreement with previous meta-analyses [43,44].

Insulin sensitivity

Despite neutral effects on HbA1c and fasting glucose, ω-3 supplementation was associated with statistically significant reductions in fasting insulin and HOMA-IR, with minimal heterogeneity across studies. These findings suggest a potential improvement in insulin sensitivity, possibly mediated through alterations in membrane lipid composition, modulation of intracellular insulin signaling pathways, or reductions in ectopic lipid accumulation [44]. Similar improvements in insulin resistance indices, without parallel reductions in HbA1c, have been reported in individuals with or at risk of diabetes [44]. However, these results were derived from a limited number of trials and were graded as low to very low certainty due to imprecision and small sample sizes. As such, although biologically plausible, they remain insufficient to support definitive therapeutic recommendations.

Inflammation and oxidative stress

Markers of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, including CRP/hs-CRP, MDA, GSH, NO, and TAC, did not demonstrate statistically significant pooled effects. Most analyses were characterized by extreme heterogeneity, reflecting differences in baseline inflammatory status, biomarker assays, intervention duration, and cosupplementation strategies. Although nonsignificant trends were often directionally consistent with the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties attributed to ω-3 fatty acids, the available evidence does not support reliable conclusions regarding these pathways in diabetes. Prior meta-analyses have similarly reported inconsistent effects on inflammatory biomarkers, with benefits appearing more pronounced in selected subgroups or under specific conditions [45]. Standardized biomarker assessment and adequately powered trials are required to clarify these mechanisms.

Functional outcomes and neuropathy

A notable and comparatively robust finding of this review was the improvement in CNBD with ω-3 supplementation. This outcome demonstrated a moderate effect size, with no detectable heterogeneity, and was assigned a low certainty rating by GRADE. Corneal nerve metrics are increasingly recognized as sensitive indicators of small-fiber neuropathy and early neuronal repair in diabetes. The homogeneous effect observed across studies suggests a potential neuroprotective role for ω-3 fatty acids, possibly related to membrane stabilization, axonal regeneration, or modulation of neuroinflammatory processes [30,33]. However, the limited number of trials and the absence of parallel improvements in neuropathic symptoms, functional impairment, or quality of life limit conclusions regarding clinical relevance. This is consistent with prior work reporting minimal effects of ω-3 supplementation on neuropathic symptoms despite structural nerve changes [33].

Sources of heterogeneity and certainty of evidence

Interpretation of pooled estimates is complicated by substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Included trials varied widely in diabetes subtype (type 1, type 2, or gestational), ω-3 formulation (EPA, DHA, or mixed), dose, duration, baseline metabolic risk, and cosupplementation strategies. Evidence from gestational diabetes trials has shown more consistent improvements in metabolic biomarkers, although without clear effects on clinical outcomes [46,47]. Placebo selection also differed considerably, ranging from inert substances to vegetable oils with potential metabolic activity, further contributing to heterogeneity. These factors, combined with small sample sizes and short intervention durations, led to significant inconsistency, indirectness, and imprecision, which explains the frequent downgrading of certainty in GRADE assessments.

Implications for practice and research

From a clinical perspective, current evidence does not support routine ω-3 supplementation for improving glycemic control or most lipid parameters in people with diabetes. Importantly, the predominance of neutral findings across major metabolic, inflammatory, and oxidative outcomes suggests that ω-3 supplementation should not be considered a disease-modifying intervention for diabetes itself. Although improvements in selected surrogate markers of insulin sensitivity and nerve morphology were observed, these changes occurred in the absence of consistent improvements in major metabolic outcomes and cannot presently be assumed to translate into clinically meaningful long-term benefits. ω-3 supplementation may remain appropriate for indications supported by broader cardiovascular evidence, particularly in populations at high cardiovascular disease risk [48], but diabetes-specific benefits remain uncertain. Future research should prioritize adequately powered randomized trials with clearly defined diabetes subtypes, standardized ω-3 formulations with quantified EPA and DHA content, metabolically neutral placebos, and sufficient duration. Importantly, trials should extend beyond intermediate biomarkers and include clinically meaningful endpoints such as cardiovascular events, progression of diabetic complications, neuropathy-related disability, and patient-reported outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

This systematic review and meta-analysis have several important strengths. First, it followed a prospectively registered protocol and was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines, enhancing transparency and methodological rigor. Second, an extensive literature search across multiple major databases was conducted, minimizing the risk of missing relevant trials. Third, the analysis incorporated a broad range of outcomes, including lipid parameters, glycemic markers, insulin sensitivity indices, inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers, and functional outcomes such as CNBD, providing a comprehensive overview of potential metabolic and neuroprotective effects of ω-3 supplementation in diabetes. Methodologically, the use of MD for outcomes reported in the same units improved clinical interpretability, whereas the SMD was appropriately reserved for outcomes measured on different scales. The application of random-effects models prespecified subgroup analyses, and clearly labeled exploratory metaregression allowed for appropriate handling of anticipated heterogeneity. Importantly, the GRADE framework was systematically applied to assess the certainty of evidence, facilitating transparent interpretation of findings and preventing overstatement of results. Finally, the explicit framing of pooled analyses as exploratory and hypothesis-generating represents a methodological strength, aligning conclusions with the limitations of the underlying evidence.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity was present across most outcomes, driven by variation in diabetes subtypes (type 1, type 2, gestational, and high-risk populations), ω-3 formulation (EPA, DHA, or mixed), dosage, intervention duration, baseline metabolic status, and cosupplementation strategies. This heterogeneity frequently led to very high I2 values and contributed to the downgrading of evidence certainty. Second, many pooled analyses were based on a small number of trials with limited sample sizes and short follow-up periods, leading to imprecision and wide CIs. As a result, several statistically significant findings, particularly for insulin sensitivity indices, were graded as low or very low certainty. Third, placebo selection varied considerably across studies, ranging from inert substances to vegetable oils with potential metabolic effects, which may have attenuated observed differences between intervention and control groups. Fourth, the evidence base relied predominantly on intermediate biomarkers rather than hard clinical endpoints. None of the included trials evaluated outcomes such as cardiovascular events, progression of nephropathy or retinopathy, or neuropathy-related disability, limiting the ability to draw conclusions about long-term clinical benefit. Fifth, although exploratory metaregression analyses identified potential moderators of treatment effects, these analyses were constrained by low study counts, collinearity among predictors, and the lack of adjustment for multiple testing, and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Finally, only studies published in English or Spanish were included, raising the possibility of language bias, and publication bias could not be reliably assessed for several outcomes because of the limited number of available trials.

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis indicate that ω-3 fatty acid supplementation does not consistently improve glycemic control, LDL or HDL cholesterol, or inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers in individuals with diabetes. Directionally favorable effects were observed for triglyceride concentrations, and statistically significant improvements were identified for fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, and CNBD. However, the overall certainty of evidence for most outcomes ranged from low to very low, largely because of substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, small sample sizes, short intervention durations, and imprecision. Importantly, none of the included trials evaluated hard clinical endpoints such as cardiovascular events, progression of diabetic complications, or neuropathy-related disability. As a result, it remains uncertain whether observed changes in intermediate biomarkers translate into meaningful long-term clinical benefits. Given these limitations, the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Future research should prioritize adequately powered randomized controlled trials with clearly defined diabetes subtypes, standardized ω-3 formulations (EPA, DHA, or mixed), sufficient intervention duration, and metabolically neutral placebos. Crucially, such trials should incorporate clinically relevant outcomes alongside mechanistic biomarkers to clarify whether ω-3 supplementation has a meaningful role as an adjunct to diabetes management. Until such evidence is available, ω-3 supplementation should be regarded as a complementary intervention rather than a routine therapeutic recommendation for people with diabetes.
