Dental students’ perceptions, attitudes, and professional approaches toward the use of social media in learning endodontics: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Merve Gökyar, Gözde Yıldırım, Hesna Sazak Öveçoğlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial media has become an increasingly prevalent tool in health professions education, including dentistry. This study aimed to investigate dental students’ perceptions, attitudes, and professional approaches toward the use of social media in learning endodontics, with comparisons across academic years and gender.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was administered to third-, fourth-, and fifth-year dental students at Marmara University during the 2024–2025 academic year. A total of 302 students completed a structured 18-item questionnaire that had been reviewed for content and face validity by field experts. The questionnaire assessed social media usage, engagement with endodontics-related content, students’ perceived educational and clinical benefits, and their ethical views on how dental professionals should behave on social media—particularly regarding patient confidentiality and professional boundaries. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Chi-square, and Fisher’s Exact tests (p < 0.05).ResultsThe majority of students (78.8%) followed endodontic content on platforms such as Instagram and YouTube. Nearly half (49.3%) perceived a contribution to professional development, and 37.7% reported a perceived influence on their clinical thinking or practices. Educational benefit perceptions increased with academic seniority (p < 0.001). While male students emphasized visual quality (p = 0.007), female students were more likely to support strict ethical boundaries (p = 0.043), especially those related to patient confidentiality and professional ethics. Following endodontic content was significantly associated with perceived clinical influence (p < 0.001), whereas students’ ethical attitudes were not significantly associated with their responses to misinformation (p = 0.463).ConclusionsThe findings indicate that social media is widely used by dental students and is perceived to support clinical learning, especially among senior students. These results highlight the need for further research into students’ evaluation of online content and their professional attitudes in digital environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08089-4.Keywords: Dental education, Endodontics, Social media, Student

Introduction

Social media refers to the entirety of content created and shared through digital technologies on internet-based platforms. These platforms enhance interaction among individuals, facilitate information exchange, and promote online collaboration [1]. Moreover, social media is defined as a dynamic communication environment in which individuals engage through digital networks and virtual communities, enabling the production, sharing, and transmission of knowledge, ideas, and experiences [2]. In this context, social media constitutes a significant technological tool that allows for rapid, efficient, and bidirectional communication among users [3].

Social media encompasses a broad range of platforms, from user-generated knowledge sources such as Wikipedia to microblogging applications like Twitter, content-sharing communities such as YouTube, Facebook, and WhatsApp, as well as virtual interaction environments like Second Life. These platforms also function as supportive tools in various domains, including education and vocabulary acquisition [1]. Widely used platforms such as Facebook, Twitter (X), YouTube, and Instagram are particularly preferred in the healthcare sector for both personal and professional purposes. Recent studies indicate that social media usage is highly prevalent among healthcare professionals, with approximately 90% of actively practicing physicians reporting that they use platforms like Facebook for either professional communication or personal sharing [4].

Nowadays, social media platforms are widely and intensively used by university students on a daily basis [5]. Various studies conducted among dental students have identified Facebook, YouTube, and WhatsApp as the most commonly used platforms. Research conducted in the United States and the United Kingdom has shown that students most frequently prefer Facebook, while platforms such as Twitter and LinkedIn are used less frequently [5, 6]. At King Abdulaziz University in Saudi Arabia, one study found that WhatsApp and Facebook were the most frequently preferred platforms among students [7]. However, another cross-sectional study reported that Facebook was the least preferred platform, whereas WhatsApp, Snapchat, and Instagram were more commonly used [8].

In recent years, the rapid advancement of information and communication technologies -including social media- has led to profound transformations in various domains such as business, education, and politics. Dental education has also been influenced by this shift. Particularly in developed countries, the widespread use of computers and the internet has brought about significant changes in teaching and learning processes. Mobile devices have become key indicators of individuals’ digital literacy, while the internet has emerged as the most comprehensive and accessible source of information for students. Digital networks, which provide billions of users worldwide with access to information and interaction, have expanded the boundaries of educational environments and enabled more personalized and flexible learning models.

Although social media platforms were originally designed for social interaction, they are now increasingly being utilized for educational purposes. Indeed, recent studies indicate that approximately 85% of medical students actively use smartphones on a daily basis, underscoring the extent to which digitalization has been integrated into health professional education [9]. The relatively low cost of the internet compared to traditional print sources, along with its capacity to provide instant access on a global scale, has created an effective, accessible, and economical medium of communication for health professionals. These advantages represent significant factors supporting the digitalization of dental education. The rapidly increasing use of social media has transformed not only interpersonal communication but also the strategies employed by healthcare providers for patient education, service promotion, and improved engagement. In this context, the use of social media platforms as institutional and professional communication tools in the healthcare sector is becoming increasingly widespread [10].

With the increasing accessibility of technological resources in recent years, students have been actively using various social media tools to enhance their knowledge. Research indicates that many dental students use platforms such as YouTube and Twitter to support their learning and to communicate with academic staff [11]. Some students report using YouTube to watch dental procedures and improve their clinical skills before engaging in hands-on training [11, 12]. Furthermore, approximately one-third of students have expressed a positive attitude toward integrating social media as a learning tool into the formal curriculum [5]. Similarly, a study conducted by Aboalshamat et al. reported that senior dental students in Saudi Arabia actively use social media platforms in their learning. These students were found to watch videos related to clinical procedures and seek various online resources to access up-to-date dental information [8]. In addition, several international studies have explored the potential effects of incorporating social media into dental curricula. One such study described a flipped classroom approach in which pre-recorded video lectures on clinical periodontology were uploaded to YouTube by instructors, and students were expected to view them before attending class. The findings demonstrated that this preparatory strategy significantly enhanced students’ comprehension of the subject matter compared to traditional lecture-based methods [13]. On the other hand, some researchers have highlighted the possible negative consequences of digital tool usage among students. Excessive and unregulated use of social media may increase the risk of developing addictive behaviors and contribute to cognitive distraction, thereby negatively impacting academic focus and overall learning performance [14].

The easy accessibility and diversity of information shared on social media platforms raise a variety of professional and ethical concerns for healthcare professionals. These platforms often contain content that includes personal information, individual opinions, and subjective evaluations. Presenting such elements within the same medium can blur the boundaries between personal and professional identities. This blurring of roles may pose ethical risks, particularly in the context of the dentist–patient relationship, where trust-based communication is essential [15].

While the role of social media in dental education has been widely acknowledged, most existing studies have addressed this issue in general terms, without focusing on specific disciplines. Endodontics involves complex procedures that often require visual and step-by-step learning, making it a field where students may particularly benefit from supplementary online content. Despite this, little is known about how students engage with such content or how their perceptions differ across academic levels. Therefore, this study aimed to explore dental students’ perceptions, attitudes, and professional approaches toward the use of social media in learning endodontics, with comparisons across year groups.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between March and April 2025 at the Faculty of Dentistry, Marmara University. The target population consisted of third-, fourth-, and fifth-year dental students, who were eligible because they had both theoretical and clinical exposure to endodontics. Participants were then selected through random sampling from these cohorts. According to faculty records, approximately 436 students were enrolled in the third-, fourth-, and fifth-year cohorts (third year: 129; fourth year: 171; fifth year: 136). A total of 302 out of 436 eligible students completed the questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 69.3%. Only fully completed questionnaires were included in the analysis.

Sample size and sampling method

The sample size was calculated using a 95% confidence level and a ± 5% margin of error, assuming maximum variability (p = 0.5). Based on the total number of eligible students (n = 436), the minimum required sample size was calculated as 205. Ultimately, data were collected from 302 students, with approximately equal representation across all academic years.

Participants were selected through random sampling method from among third-, fourth-, and fifth-year dental students who were available during the data collection period. The selection was based on official class rosters to ensure equal representation of students across all academic years. Names were randomly selected from each cohort list, and students who were present during scheduled lectures were invited to participate.

Data collection tool

To recruit participants, in-class announcements were made during scheduled lectures. Students who agreed to participate completed the paper-based questionnaire on-site immediately after the briefing.

A structured questionnaire specifically developed by the researchers was used as the data collection tool. The questionnaire aimed to assess students’ social media usage habits, their exposure to endodontics-related content, their perceptions regarding the usefulness and influence of such content on their professional knowledge and clinical learning, and their ethical evaluations of these materials.

During the development process, the thematic structures and item formats of similar studies by Rajeh et al. (2021) and Farghal et al. (2023) were reviewed to inform the formulation of the current items [16, 17]. However, the present questionnaire was neither adapted, translated, nor directly derived from these instruments. Instead, it was independently developed by the authors as a context-specific and original tool tailored to the objectives of the study. The items developed for this study differ substantially in both content and structure from those presented in the cited studies. The finalized questionnaire is presented in Supplementary Material 1.

Although no formal psychometric validation was conducted and no pilot study was carried out prior to data collection, the questionnaire was reviewed by all three researchers—each with a different level of academic experience in endodontics—to ensure item clarity, relevance, and alignment with the study objectives. This internal expert review process contributed not only to face validity but also served as a basis for supporting the content validity of the instrument within the scope of this exploratory study.

Ethical considerations

Participation in the study was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Responses were kept anonymous and confidential, with no identifying information collected. The study posed no risk and contributed to raising students’ awareness of social media use and professional attitudes. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences, Marmara University, and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (Protocol No:80, Date:30.04.2025). This study was reported in accordance with the CROSS (Consensus-based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies) guideline.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies (n) and percentages (%), were used to summarize the demographic characteristics of the participants and their responses to the survey items. Pearson’s Chi-square test (χ²) was employed to examine associations between categorical variables. In cases where the expected cell frequency was less than 5, Fisher’s Exact Test was used to ensure the validity of the results. This adjustment was particularly necessary for items with low response frequencies, such as TikTok and Twitter usage in Q6, the “Not useful” option in Q9, and small subgroup responses in Tables 4 and 6. The type of test used for each comparison is specified in the corresponding table footnotes. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Significant gender-based differences were observed in the perceived importance of visual quality in endodontic social media content; a higher proportion of male students emphasized this factor compared to female students (35.8% vs. 21.4%; p = 0.007). No significant gender differences were found in students’ perceptions of how social media content influences their professional development or clinical practice, nor in reactions to misinformation. Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the perceptions, attitudes, and professional approaches regarding the role of social media in learning endodontics at Marmara University in Turkey. Participants reported that Instagram and YouTube were the most frequently used platforms. Similarly, a study conducted by Hamid and Jaafar among dental students in Malaysia identified Instagram as one of the most preferred platforms [18]. In contrast, a similar study conducted in Saudi Arabia reported that WhatsApp was the most commonly used social media application among students [16]. These differences may reflect regional communication habits, with WhatsApp commonly adopted for group coordination, whereas Instagram and YouTube are primarily used as visual platforms that support learning from demonstrations. Another study conducted among faculty members aged 50 to 60 reported that Facebook and Skype were the most frequently used platforms, which may be explained by generational differences in digital preferences [19].

A study by Farghal et al. reported that the majority of students used social media regularly and spent an average of 2 to 6 h per day for on this activity [17]. Similarly, other studies have shown that university students spend an average of three to four hours per day on social media platforms [20, 21]. These findings indicate that social media occupies a significant place in the daily lives of young adults. The results obtained in the present study are consistent with this body of literature. Most students reported spending an average of 1 to 5 h per day on social media, while 16.2% indicated that their usage exceeded five hours per day. Such intensive use may be associated with challenges in time management, attention span, and academic performance. It may also reflect the ease of access to mobile technologies and the dual role of social media as both an educational aid and a recreational activity.

According to the findings of the present study, the vast majority of dental students (78.8%) reported following endodontics-related content on social media, with Instagram being the most frequently encountered platform (78.1%). In a study conducted by Al Rababah et al., it was reported that Jordanian dental students predominantly used Facebook to access dental information [22]. However, this finding contrasts with a Saudi study that examined medical students’ use of social media and identified YouTube and Twitter as the preferred platforms for viewing medical procedures [23]. Such a discrepancy may reflect contextual or disciplinary differences in how clinical practices are presented and emphasized, as dental education often relies on visual demonstrations of specific procedures, whereas medical education may prioritize broader procedural explanations and discussions.

Approximately half of the participants (49.3%) stated that the content they encountered on social media was beneficial for their professional development, while 47.4% perceived it as partially beneficial. These findings suggest that social media platforms are used not only for personal interaction and entertainment but also for accessing professional information and enhancing clinical awareness. Indeed, numerous studies have emphasized the effective use of social media for educational purposes and have recommended its integration into dental education [13, 24, 25]. However, it is critically important that students develop a critical perspective regarding the accuracy and reliability of digital content [25]. Factors such as the lack of evidence-based material, insufficient scientific referencing, and the uncontrolled nature of user-generated posts may reduce the quality of information presented on social media [25, 26].

In the present study, 37.7% of the participating students reported that endodontic content encountered on social media was relevant to their clinical practice. Notably, visually oriented platforms such as Instagram and YouTube attracted considerable attention, particularly in the context of clinical case presentations (80.1%) and demonstrations of treatment techniques (61.9%). This highlights the potential role of these platforms in supporting procedural knowledge transfer and strengthening visual memory. Similarly, Farghal et al. emphasized that YouTube has become a frequently utilized supplementary resource alongside traditional dental education [17]. Moreover, in the multicenter study conducted by Burns et al., YouTube was reported to be commonly used for acquiring knowledge about clinical procedures and visualizing clinical concepts [27], which may be explained by the alignment between visual media formats and the procedural, technique-oriented nature of dental training.

In the study conducted by Rajeh et al., it was reported that third-year dental students had lower rates of using social media for learning purposes compared to final-year students, and that the tendency to engage in social media–based learning increased with academic level [16]. In the study by Farghal et al., perceptions regarding the effectiveness of social media for acquiring dental-related knowledge and facilitating professional interaction were found to differ significantly across academic years, with final-year students more frequently selecting the response “strongly agree” (p = 0.028). These findings may indicate that students with greater clinical exposure recognize the potential educational value of social media and show a higher tendency to use it for learning. This trend was also observed in the present study, where senior students demonstrated more favorable attitudes toward its educational use compared to junior students.

The lower levels of agreement observed among junior students may be attributed to a lack of awareness regarding the educational potential of social media or to a more cautious approach in evaluating such content. It is also possible that their limited clinical experience reduces their ability to relate digital content directly to patient care. Additionally, the same study reported that fifth-year students perceived clinical procedure videos on YouTube to be more beneficial than students in other years, with the highest rate of “strongly agree” responses recorded in this group (10.5%) (p = 0.05) [17]. Similarly, in the present study, the perceived contribution of social media content to clinical practice increased with academic level, and this association was found to be statistically significant (p < 0.001). This trend may be attributed to the fact that senior students assume greater clinical responsibilities, actively participate in comprehensive case management, and develop more autonomous learning behaviors.

During this process, students tend to utilize digital resources, including social media, to supplement their existing knowledge and reinforce their clinical competencies. Furthermore, individual research projects integrated into the curriculum may also encourage students to use social media platforms more effectively for literature review, tracking recent advancements, and engaging in professional interactions. In addition, it was observed that fourth- and fifth-year students were more likely than third-year students to perceive social media content as educational and beneficial to clinical applications. However, the criteria used to assess content reliability varied by academic level, with a significant decline in the importance assigned to scientific referencing among senior students (p = 0.004). This finding suggests that, over time, students may adopt a more selective, practice-oriented, or pragmatic approach to evaluating information sources, prioritizing immediate clinical applicability over strict adherence to evidence-based referencing.

In the present study, no statistically significant gender-based differences were found in students’ perceptions regarding the contribution of social media content to professional development or its relevance to clinical practice. Similarly, gender differences were not observed in students’ motivation to consult academic sources. However, male students were found to place greater emphasis on the visual quality of content, and this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.007). Additionally, the proportion of participants who believed that ethical boundaries should be observed in social media posts by healthcare professionals was higher among females (55.6%) than males (50.9%), with this difference also reaching statistical significance (p = 0.043).

In contrast, the study conducted by Farghal et al. reported that male students perceived social media as more effective for acquiring dental-related knowledge compared to their female counterparts, and they expressed greater trust in clinical content available on YouTube (p = 0.038). The same study also found that male students were more inclined to recommend such content to their peers (p = 0.003). These findings contrast with the gender-neutral trends observed in the current study. This discrepancy may be attributed to sample characteristics, differing levels of digital media integration in educational environments, or broader cultural contexts [17].

In the multicenter study conducted by Burns et al., 36% of the participants reported concerns about the lack of scientific foundation in the educational videos they watched [27]. This finding highlights the importance of evaluating the scientific validity of content when using social media platforms as supplementary learning resources. Similarly, in an analysis conducted by Knösel and colleagues, the majority of dental-related YouTube videos lacked reliability, with only 5% of the content originating from academic institutions [28]. In another study, Silva et al. reported that the majority of dental education content available on YouTube lacks reliability [29]. These concerns about content reliability align with the findings of the present study, which suggest that students tend to prioritize expert opinion and scientific referencing when evaluating social media content. Specifically, participants identified the presence of an endodontist as the content creator and the inclusion of scientific sources or references as among the most important criteria for assessing the credibility of endodontic content encountered on social media. This finding indicates that students’ perceptions of the educational potential of social media platforms may be influenced by professional identity and values. However, it should be noted that the present study relied on students’ self-reported perceptions rather than objective evaluations of academic performance or clinical competence. Therefore, any educational or curricular implications should be interpreted with caution.

Various studies have indicated that some dental students lack sufficient awareness of the ethical and professional standards associated with social media use. This lack of awareness may be associated with inappropriate or irresponsible use of digital platforms, which, in turn, could lead to undesirable consequences [5, 8]. Studies conducted particularly in the United Kingdom have reported instances where social media was used in ways that conflicted with professional ethics. Such behaviors have been noted as potentially undermining students’ professional competence and possibly affecting their clinical performance [5, 8, 25]. When the findings of the present study are evaluated in terms of ethical awareness, the vast majority of students expressed the opinion that ethical boundaries should be respected in social media posts made by healthcare professionals. However, no statistically significant association was found between this view and students’ responses when encountering misinformation or incomplete content. This may indicate that, while students demonstrate ethical sensitivity, they tend to refrain from actively correcting misinformation in social media settings. Indeed, the most frequently reported reactions to misinformation were “unfollowing the source” (34.7%) and “ignoring the content” (26.2%), which may reflect a reluctance to engage in potential online conflicts or a lack of confidence in challenging the credibility of digital content.

This study has certain methodological limitations. First, the sample was limited to third-, fourth-, and fifth-year dental students from the Faculty of Dentistry at Marmara University, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to all dental students in Turkey. Social media usage habits, perceptions, and professional attitudes may vary across institutions due to cultural, pedagogical, or organizational differences. Second, the questionnaire used in the study was developed by the researchers. Although it was informed by an extensive literature review and expert input, no formal psychometric validation or pilot testing was conducted. Therefore, its validity and reliability could not be fully established. Moreover, participants’ evaluations of social media content relied on subjective perceptions, which may have been influenced by cognitive biases -such as social desirability- potentially affecting data quality. Given the exploratory nature of the study and its context-specific objectives, the internally reviewed instrument was deemed sufficient for preliminary data collection. Nevertheless, future studies employing multi-institutional samples along with formally validated instruments are recommended to improve methodological rigor, accuracy, and generalizability.

Conclusion

This study reveals that social media is widely used by dental students as a supplementary learning tool in endodontics, with senior students more frequently reporting perceived clinical benefits. The observed trends in students’ reliance on non-academic content and variable sensitivity to ethical concerns underscore the need for enhanced media literacy and digital professionalism within dental education.
