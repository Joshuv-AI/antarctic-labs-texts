The role of preoperative sarcopenia in post-nephrectomy prognosis of renal cell carcinoma: an integrated systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhenlang Guo, Junwei He, Guixing Tang, Jiwan Qiu, Franky Leung Chan, Zhaohui Wang, Zunguang Bai, Zehuai Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough several studies have suggested that sarcopenia is associated with adverse outcomes in kidney cancer patients undergoing nephrectomy, the results have been inconsistent. Therefore, this meta-analysis was conducted to investigate the relationship between sarcopenia and post-nephrectomy survival in kidney cancer patients.MethodsA thorough search was executed across multiple databases, including MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library, to identify pertinent studies up to August, 2025. By employing random/fixed effects models, we calculated multivariate-adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) accompanied by their respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs), in strict compliance with the guidelines delineated in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses statement.ResultsA total of thirteen studies encompassing 3,142 patients who underwent nephrectomy fulfilled the inclusion criteria. Regarding the thirteen aggregated multivariate estimates, sarcopenia was found to be associated with reduced OS (HR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.25–2.01) and CSS (HR = 1.93, 95% CI: 1.40–2.45). Nevertheless, our findings suggest that sarcopenia is not correlated with inferior PFS in kidney cancer patients post-nephrectomy (HR = 1.17; 95% CI: 0.92–1.41). The robustness of these findings is substantiated by sensitivity and meta-regression analyses.ConclusionsSarcopenia has been demonstrated to be independently correlated with inferior OS and CSS in individuals who have undergone nephrectomy for kidney carcinoma. These empirical observations highlight the critical necessity of sarcopenia evaluation in the context of risk stratification, patient advisement, and therapeutic strategy formulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04079-4.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC), ranking as the 7th most prevalent malignancy in the United States, often presents with regional or distant metastases in up to 30% of newly diagnosed cases [1, 2]. For those with localized RCC, the standard approach typically involves either radical or partial nephrectomy. In the context of advanced metastatic RCC (mRCC), cytoreductive radical nephrectomy has long been considered a cornerstone of treatment, despite recent Level I evidence challenging this practice in specific patient populations, particularly in the era of targeted therapies [3–5]. Given the evolving understanding of the role of cytoreductive radical nephrectomy, it is crucial to identify prognostic factors that can aid in risk stratification and enhance management strategies for mRCC [6].

Sarcopenia, a condition characterized by the progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, is a significant health concern [7]. This condition can diminish a tumor patient’s tolerance to treatment, exacerbate the toxicity of anti-neoplastic drugs, extend hospital stays, and increase the incidence of postoperative complications, thereby serving as a marker of poor prognosis following surgery [8]. Beyond these direct impacts, sarcopenia is also associated with reduced mobility, frailty, premature mortality, and chronic illness. Recent studies have suggested that sarcopenia may also predict unfavorable outcomes in patients undergoing major abdominal surgeries [9].

While previous meta-analyses have explored the prognostic role of sarcopenia in RCC, most have included heterogeneous patient populations, including those managed non-surgically. In contrast, the present study focuses exclusively on nephrectomy cohorts, encompassing radical, partial, and cytoreductive procedures—to provide a more specific and clinically relevant assessment of sarcopenia’s impact in the surgical setting. Furthermore, we introduce cancer-specific survival (CSS) as a key endpoint, which has been underreported in prior syntheses. We also perform detailed subgroup analyses stratified by surgical approach (radical vs. cytoreductive nephrectomy), offering nuanced insights that may guide individualized patient management. Moreover, sarcopenia is particularly prevalent among elderly patients, who constitute a significant proportion of those undergoing nephrectomy for RCC. In this population, sarcopenia may exacerbate postoperative morbidity and impair recovery, underscoring the importance of preoperative muscle mass assessment in geriatric oncologic care [10]. Our study aims to contribute to this growing body of evidence by evaluating the prognostic significance of sarcopenia across diverse surgical cohorts, including older adults.

In light of these considerations, we undertook a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the impact of sarcopenia on survival outcomes in patients with renal cell carcinoma post-nephrectomy. This investigation is particularly pertinent given the known association between sarcopenia and adverse clinical outcomes in kidney cancer patients, aiming to provide critical insights for future research and the development of clinical practice guidelines, as well as to inform public health and research priorities.

Materials and methods

A thorough assessment and synthesis of primary research were conducted in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11]. As a result, neither ethical approval nor patient consent is required. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42024627387) and is accessible at: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?RecordID=627387.

Literature search

We conducted a comprehensive search for eligible studies across multiple databases, including MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library, up until August, 2025. The focus of these studies was to investigate the correlation between sarcopenia and post-nephrectomy survival in patients diagnosed with kidney cancer. The database search was executed without imposing any constraints on language, publication type, or geographical region. Our search strategy incorporated a combination of medical subject headings (MeSH) and non-MeSH terms, specifically: (“Renal Cell Carcinoma“[MeSH] OR “Kidney Neoplasms“[MeSH] OR “renal cell carcinoma” OR “kidney cancer” OR “renal cancer”) AND (“Nephrectomy“[MeSH] OR “nephrectomy” OR “nephrectomies” OR “kidney resection” OR “partial nephrectomy” OR “radical nephrectomy” OR “cytoreductive nephrectomy”) AND (“Sarcopenia“[MeSH] OR “sarcopenia” OR “sarcopenic” OR “muscle mass” OR “skeletal muscle index” OR “psoas muscle” OR “muscle wasting” OR “body composition”) AND (“Survival“[MeSH] OR “survival” OR “mortality” OR “prognosis” OR “outcome” OR “overall survival” OR “cancer-specific survival” OR “progression-free survival”). The full, reproducible search strategies for all databases are provided in Supplementary Material 1. Additionally, we performed a manual review of reference lists from prior reviews and other pertinent articles to uncover supplementary reports of interest. Any discrepancies encountered were adjudicated through consensus among the co-investigators.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Initially, two authors independently reviewed the titles and abstracts of the articles to identify studies that were ineligible and to document the reasons for their exclusion. A comprehensive full-text review of potentially eligible studies was undertaken. Furthermore, studies were included if they satisfied the following eligibility criteria: (1) original investigations concerning the association between sarcopenia and survival following nephrectomy (i.e., radical nephrectomy, partial nephrectomy, or cytoreductive nephrectomy) in patients with kidney cancer; (2) studies that reported risk estimates (i.e., odds ratios [ORs], hazard ratios [HRs], and relative risks) accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (CIs); alternatively, sufficient raw data for calculation should be provided if risk estimates with associated 95% CIs were not reported; and (3) observational studies (i.e., retrospective or prospective cohorts, case–control, or cross–sectional studies) published as original articles. In the event of duplicate publications, only the most recent or the higher-quality study was retained. Disagreements were resolved through consensus among the co-investigators.

Data extraction and methodological quality assessment

Two independent researchers meticulously gathered pertinent data from the selected studies. The core attributes were systematically recorded in a uniform Excel (Microsoft Corporation) document, encompassing: first author, year of publication, country of origin, study design, sample size, categories of surgical interventions, participant characteristics (e.g., mean age and follow-up period), survival metrics (e.g., overall survival [OS], cancer-specific survival [CSS], progression-free survival [PFS]) for patients with sarcopenia undergoing nephrectomy, and univariate and/or multivariate risk assessments. Additionally, we reached out to the principal authors to obtain missing data when the eligible studies failed to provide comprehensive information. Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through mutual agreement among the collaborating investigators.

The evaluation of the quality of the eligible studies was performed by two authors utilizing the Newcastle–Ottawa scale (NOS)[12], which encompasses 10 items. Each item was assessed as either “yes” or “no/unclear,” corresponding to scores of “1” and “0,” respectively, derived from the data extracted from the studies. The total score varied from 0 to 10, with the following classifications: scores ranging from 8 to 10 were deemed high quality, scores from 5 to 7 were categorized as moderate quality, and scores of 5 or less were considered low quality. Discrepancies were addressed through collaborative discussion among the co-investigators.

Data synthesis and analysis

The comprehensive risk assessments derived from the studies were calculated employing hazard ratios (HRs) accompanied by their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) via the STATA statistical software (version 15.0; serial number: 10699393; StataCorp Wyb) to evaluate the correlation between sarcopenia and post-nephrectomy survival outcomes in renal cell carcinoma patients. Forest plots were utilized to illustrate the aggregated findings and the heterogeneity across the studies. Additionally, the I2 test was executed to gauge the impact of heterogeneity on the meta-analysis outcomes. The I2 values, categorized as 0%, 25%, 50%, and 75%, respectively denote no, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity. A random effects model was implemented when heterogeneity was indicated by I2≥ 50%, as per the Cochrane review guidelines[13]; otherwise, a fixed effects model was employed. Statistical significance was determined at a P value of < 0.05[13]. Subgroup analyses, stratified by country and study design, were performed to examine the effects of diverse methodological approaches and patient attributes on heterogeneity. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially excluding each study to evaluate the robustness and consistency of the results. Meta-regression analysis was also undertaken to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity across several variables, with the restricted maximum likelihood method applied for the analysis. Ultimately, Egger’s test was performed to assess the presence of publication bias [14].

Results

Study identification and selection

The initial search yielded 185 records. Following the removal of duplicates, 75 studies remained. Subsequently, 55 studies were discarded after screening their titles and abstracts, and 17 articles underwent full-text evaluation. Ultimately, 4 articles were excluded due to the following reasons: three studies failed to report pertinent outcomes, and one study lacked sufficient data for extraction (illustrated in Fig. 1). Consequently, 13 original studies involving 3,142 patients who underwent nephrectomy were selected for the systematic review and meta-analysis, adhering to the established eligibility criteria [15–27]. Methodological quality assessment

The methodological rigor of the incorporated studies was assessed in accordance with the NOS criteria. Of these, ten studies [15–20, 23, 25–27] attained scores ranging from nine to ten, thus being categorized as high quality. Two studies [21, 22] obtained scores within the range of seven to eight, signifying moderate quality. The sole remaining study[24], which garnered a score of five, was adjudged to be of low quality.

Overall survival of patients after nephrectomy

A comprehensive compilation of twelve studies [15–20, 23, 25–27] provided substantial data elucidating the correlation between sarcopenia and OS following nephrectomy in patients with kidney cancer. The manifestation of sarcopenia in these patients was significantly associated with reduced OS, exhibiting a combined HR of 1.63 (95% CI: 1.25–2.01), as illustrated in Fig. 2. While our subgroup analyses provided valuable insights, we observed substantial heterogeneity within certain strata, most notably in the radical nephrectomy subgroup (I² = 90.2%). This degree of heterogeneity, while not uncommon in meta-analyses of observational studies, warrants careful consideration. Despite the consistent direction of effect across studies favoring better outcomes in non-sarcopenic patients, the magnitude of this association varied considerably. This variability likely reflects underlying differences in patient populations, surgical techniques, and perioperative management that were not fully accounted for in our analysis. The consistency in the direction of the effect estimates across studies, however, reinforces the robustness of the main finding that sarcopenia is associated with poorer overall survival.

Seven studies, namely studies [16, 18–20, 24, 25, 27] furnished ample data elucidating the correlation between sarcopenia and CSS post-nephrectomy in patients afflicted with kidney cancer. Discussion

Main findings

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to investigate the correlation between sarcopenia and post-nephrectomy survival in patients diagnosed with kidney cancer. The findings revealed that sarcopenia was significantly associated with inferior OS and CSS, whereas no significant association was detected in PFS. Notably, sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the stability of the results remained unchanged when each study was excluded individually, despite the inability of meta-regression to pinpoint the potential factors influencing the degree of heterogeneity among studies. No evidence of publication bias was detected based on the outcomes of Egger’s test and the funnel plot analysis.

It is noteworthy that while our overall findings demonstrated a consistent association between sarcopenia and survival outcomes, we observed considerable heterogeneity in some subgroup analyses. This was particularly evident in the radical nephrectomy subgroup, where the I² value exceeded 90%. This substantial heterogeneity likely reflects the clinical and methodological diversity inherent in real-world surgical practice across different institutions and time periods. Factors such as variations in patient comorbidities, surgical technique, adjuvant therapy protocols, and postoperative care standards—none of which could be fully adjusted for in this meta-analysis—may contribute to these differences. Nevertheless, the consistent direction of effect across all studies strengthens the validity of our primary conclusion regarding the adverse prognostic impact of sarcopenia.

In the context of RCC, the prognostic significance of sarcopenia continues to be a subject of debate. In 2017, Fukushima et al.[15] elucidated the influence of SMI dynamics on post-cytoreductive nephrectomy survival in patients with mRCC, demonstrating that muscle atrophy is independently correlated with reduced OS. Analogously, Sharma et al.[26] identified sarcopenia as a factor related to OS in patients with mRCC who underwent cytoreductive nephrectomy or received cytokine therapy and targeted agents. Conversely, Peyton et al.[23] observed that sarcopenia was not linked to OS in advanced RCC patients who underwent radical nephrectomy.

Implications for clinical practice

In a retrospective analysis conducted by Psutka et al. examining preoperative sarcopenia in patients with nonmetastatic renal cell carcinoma (RCC), significant associations were identified between sarcopenia and both age (P < 0.001) and male gender (P < 0.001), along with a correlation with elevated tumor grades (P = 0.05).24 Conversely, Watanabe et al.[27] undertook a study encompassing patients undergoing radical nephrectomy with caval thrombectomy, revealing no significant disparity in major complication rates among individuals with sarcopenia, as delineated by sex-specific SMI and BMI criteria as formulated by Martin et al.[28] This divergence in findings could potentially be attributed to the incomplete documentation of postoperative complications, primarily resulting from restricted data exchange among medical institutions. In light of the variability observed in the reporting of surgical complication rates across studies centered on sarcopenic RCC populations, additional research is imperative. Prior investigations have established tumor stage and Fuhrman nuclear grade as pivotal prognostic markers for patients afflicted with localized RCC. Nevertheless, it has been evidenced that the exclusive reliance on TNM staging and Fuhrman nuclear grade is inadequate for precisely forecasting individual patient outcomes [29]. BMI, analogous to sarcopenia, has been demonstrated to impact overall and progression-free survival among RCC patients [30]. Significantly, although BMI exhibits a strong correlation with L3 SMI, numerous overweight individuals (BMI >25 kg/m²) remain classified as sarcopenic, underscoring the criticality of accurate muscle mass evaluation [18]. The mechanisms underlying the accelerated muscle loss in cancer are hypothesized to encompass heightened inflammatory responses, resulting in the disruption of skeletal muscle protein metabolism [31]. Exercise and amino acid supplementation, which are posited to activate anti-inflammatory pathways and enhance myoprotein synthesis, theoretically present potential benefits in the management of sarcopenia [32–34]. However, these interventions frequently exhibit a paucity of robust evidence within sarcopenic cancer populations and may prove impractical owing to cancer-associated symptoms, including anorexia and fatigue. Significantly, a multitude of studies have highlighted the benefits of maintaining skeletal muscle mass throughout cancer treatment [32]. A thorough preoperative evaluation of body composition and inflammation could augment treatment decision-making for urologists and oncologists, facilitating the more accurate identification of patients who would derive greater benefit from radical nephrectomy as opposed to partial nephrectomy or active surveillance, attributable to a more aggressive disease manifestation. Moreover, these biomarkers could aid in the selection of candidates for clinical trials encompassing neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapies.

A notable finding of our analysis was the distinct association pattern of sarcopenia with different survival endpoints. While it was a significant predictor for reduced OS and CSS, its association with PFS was not statistically significant. This discrepancy may be attributed to the multifaceted nature of these endpoints. OS and CSS are influenced by a patient’s overall physiological reserve and ability to withstand the cumulative burden of both the disease and its treatments. Sarcopenia, as a marker of systemic frailty and diminished physiologic resilience, likely exerts a stronger effect on these broader survival metrics. In contrast, PFS is more directly governed by tumor biology and the efficacy of oncological therapies. Our findings align with recent literature suggesting that the impact of body composition may be more pronounced on overall survival than on time to progression in RCC. As noted in a recent analysis, the complex interplay between muscle mass and survival outcomes in kidney cancer warrants careful consideration of the specific endpoint being studied [35]. Furthermore, the non-significant trend for PFS (HR = 1.17) should not be entirely dismissed; it may suggest a subtle relationship that larger, more homogeneously designed prospective studies, with standardized PFS criteria, could potentially clarify in the future.

Beyond establishing a prognostic association, the clinical utility of sarcopenia assessment warrants careful consideration. From a practical standpoint, sarcopenia evaluation can be feasibly integrated into preoperative workflows through opportunistic assessment of routine preoperative cross-sectional imaging (CT/MRI), requiring no additional scans or patient burden. This positions it as a cost-effective biomarker that could enhance existing risk stratification models. In clinical practice, the identification of significant preoperative sarcopenia could inform several key aspects of patient management. For patients with localized disease, it might prompt more comprehensive geriatric assessment, influence surgical planning regarding the extent of lymph node dissection, and guide discussions about anticipated recovery trajectories. In the metastatic setting, where treatment decisions balance cytoreductive nephrectomy against systemic therapy alone, the presence of severe sarcopenia could provide valuable insight into a patient’s physiological reserve, potentially influencing this delicate risk-benefit calculation. A crucial question remains whether this identified risk is modifiable. Currently, evidence regarding the efficacy of specific prehabilitation protocols (targeted exercise and nutritional supplementation) for sarcopenic RCC patients awaiting nephrectomy remains limited. However, emerging data from other surgical oncology populations suggest that such multimodal interventions may improve functional capacity and potentially reduce postoperative morbidity. This represents a promising yet unverified avenue for mitigating sarcopenia-associated risk in RCC patients. Therefore, while preoperative sarcopenia assessment provides valuable prognostic information, its role as a directly actionable target through specific interventions requires validation through dedicated prospective trials.

Comparison with prior Meta-Analyses

Our results align with and substantially advance the evidence from Hu et al.,[36] whose meta-analysis of 771 renal cell carcinoma patients across only 5 studies identified sarcopenia as a predictor of poorer OS (HR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.35–2.31). However, their cohort encompassed heterogeneous populations, including non-surgical patients managed with systemic therapies. In contrast, our analysis, which includes 3,142 patients from 13 studies, exclusively examines nephrectomy cohorts, thereby isolating the prognostic impact of sarcopenia in surgically managed RCC. This focus enhances the clinical applicability of our findings for urologists and surgical oncologists. Additionally, we provide novel insights by stratifying outcomes according to surgical approach (radical vs. cytoreductive nephrectomy), a distinction absent in prior syntheses. Importantly, our inclusion of CSS as a primary endpoint addresses a significant knowledge gap, as Hu et al. did not evaluate cancer-specific mortality. By integrating a larger sample size, rigorous subgroup analyses, and clinically relevant endpoints, our study offers a more nuanced and actionable evidence base for preoperative risk stratification in RCC.

Strength and limitations

In general, the meta-analysis demonstrated several pivotal strengths. Primarily, this meta-analysis represents the inaugural exploration of the correlation between sarcopenia and post-nephrectomy survival among patients diagnosed with kidney cancer. Subgroup analyses were performed across various countries and surgical procedure types to ascertain whether these variables impact the degree of heterogeneity, in adherence to the PRISMA guidelines. Secondly, multivariate-adjusted risk estimates were employed to mitigate confounding factors that could potentially influence the overall outcomes. Lastly, the outcomes of the meta-regression and sensitivity analyses substantiated the reliability and rationality of our research.

Notwithstanding, several limitations inherent to the meta-analysis necessitate recognition. Primarily, we observed substantial heterogeneity in some subgroup analyses, particularly within the radical nephrectomy cohort. This heterogeneity, while explored through meta-regression and sensitivity analyses, remains incompletely explained by the variables available in the original studies. Potential sources include variations in patient selection criteria, differences in surgical expertise and techniques across institutions, evolving perioperative care protocols over the extended study period, and heterogeneous distribution of tumor characteristics within broad surgical categories. The consistent direction of effect across studies despite this methodological diversity strengthens the main conclusion regarding sarcopenia’s adverse prognostic impact, but the high heterogeneity in specific subgroups cautions against over-interpretation of the precise effect sizes in those contexts. Second, the heterogeneity in the operational definition of sarcopenia across the included studies represents a major limitation. The use of different assessment methods (SMI, PMI, TPA) with varying, study-specific cut-off values undoubtedly introduces measurement variability. We rigorously explored the feasibility of a subgroup analysis based on the measurement technique to quantify its influence on our pooled estimates. However, this was not viable due to the disproportionate distribution of studies, with the vast majority (n = 10) employing SMI and only three utilizing PMI or TPA, which precludes a statistically robust comparison. This methodological diversity complicates the direct comparability of individual study results. However, it is noteworthy that despite these differing techniques and thresholds, the direction of the association between sarcopenia and reduced survival was remarkably consistent across nearly all studies. This consistency in the face of methodological heterogeneity may, in fact, strengthen the fundamental conclusion that low muscle mass is a significant prognostic factor, as the association persists across different measurement paradigms. Nevertheless, this limitation underscores the urgent need for future research to adopt consensus-driven, standardized definitions for sarcopenia in oncological populations to facilitate more uniform and directly comparable prognostic assessments. This lack of standardization is a major limitation that likely introduced variability and complicates the direct comparability of results across studies. We attempted to perform a subgroup analysis based on the measurement method (e.g., SMI vs. PMI/TPA) to explore its impact on the pooled estimates; however, this was not feasible due to the insufficient number of studies in each subgroup (e.g., only 2 studies used PMI/TPA) to allow for a statistically robust comparison. This methodological heterogeneity underscores the urgent need for future studies to adopt consensus-based, standardized definitions and cut-offs for sarcopenia to facilitate more reliable and comparable prognostic assessments. As mentioned in the revised text, we sincerely attempted to conduct the suggested subgroup analysis. However, upon detailed re-examination of the included studies, we found that 10 of the 13 studies utilized SMI, while the remaining three used PMI or TPA. This unequal and small distribution across categories would have rendered any subgroup analysis underpowered and potentially misleading. We believe that explicitly acknowledging this constraint and the rationale behind it represents a more scientifically honest approach. Lastly, the inclusion of solely English-language studies may have introduced selection bias. Therefore, a substantial number of multicenter, prospective studies are still requisite to substantiate the correlation between sarcopenia and survival outcomes post-radical nephrectomy.

Conclusion

Our findings unequivocally establish that preoperative sarcopenia serves as an independent prognostic factor for diminished OS and CSS in individuals undergoing nephrectomy for kidney cancer. Consequently, a preoperative assessment of sarcopenia empowers clinicians to formulate more tailored therapeutic strategies, refine post-operative care protocols, and, ultimately, enhance the survival prognosis of affected patients.
