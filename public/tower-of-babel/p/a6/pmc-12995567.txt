Stability studies on Ghanvati formulated from Psidium guajava Linn. leaf decoction
Authors: Poonam Daswani, Sanjeevani Deshkar, Asha Thomas, Nilima Dharkar, Roshani Pagar, Juilee Jagtap, Sarika Chaturvedi, Nerges Mistry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We developed a formulation (tablet, Ghanvati), from Psidium guajava L. leaf decoction and assessed its stability using standard guidelines. The organoleptic and physicochemical analysis of the leaves and prepared extract was undertaken. The tablets were subjected to friability, the dimensions were measured and its stability [hardness, disintegration, and loss on drying (LOD)] tested on 0-day and the end of 3, 6, and 12 months at ambient, intermediate and accelerated conditions as per International Council for Harmonization Q1 (R2) guidelines. Simultaneously, representative phytocompounds (gallic acid, epigallocatechin gallate, ellagic acid, and quercetin) were quantified using high performance liquid chromatography. The organoleptic, physicochemical, and friability were within specified limits. Stability studies indicated significant increase in % LOD values affecting the hardness of the tablet. With respect to phytoconstituents, epigallocatechin gallate and ellagic acid showed increase in concentration at 12 months at ambient (6.524 ± 0.93 and 8.03 ± 0.75 μg/mg, respectively) and intermediate conditions (5.53 ± 0.34 and 8.267 ± 0.87 μg/mg, respectively) compared to 0-day (4.03 ± 0.66 and 0.513 ± 0.08 μg/mg, respectively). In contrast, compared to the 0-day values (16.0 ± 0.14 and 0.8154 ± 0.13 μg/mg, respectively), the concentration of gallic acid and quercetin decreased under ambient (14.44 ± 0.99 and 0.4 ± 0.06 μg/mg, respectively) and intermediate (8.6 ± 0.69 and 0.324 ± 0.3 μg/mg, respectively) conditions. Thus, despite stability in terms of hardness and disintegration time, the hygroscopicity and chemical variability with time of the prepared Ghanvati warrant the need for alternative approaches to improve stability.

Introduction

Psidium guajava Linn. (guava, Myrtaceae family) is a common tropical plant traditionally used as food and folk medicine. The plant possesses several medicinal properties largely attributed to the presence of phytoconstituents such as polyphenolics, flavonoids, and triterpenoids [1]. Previous studies at PD's institute demonstrated guava leaf decoction (GLD) to be a promising antidiarrhoeal plant remedy. Extensive laboratory assays, studies in a mouse model, and a subsequent proof-of-concept clinical trial in adults with acute diarrhoea supported its traditional use for gastrointestinal ailments [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. The encouraging results of the pilot randomized trial documented efficacy as well as safety since no adverse events were reported [5]. Considering the potential therapeutic utility of GLD, undertaking further feasibility studies to translate to clinical applications was imperative. Developing a suitable formulation from GLD is envisaged to overcome issues with shelf life and storage conditions while ensuring uniform efficacy. As in previous studies, herein, an aqueous guava leaf extract (decoction) was used to develop a formulation, Ghanvati, using traditional method, and its stability tested. By amalgamating traditional process with recommended guidelines for estimating shelf life, we aimed to integrate and popularize herbal remedies into the mainstream. The data would be useful in designing an appropriate formulation for administration when indicated for longer times, such as a possible adjunct with extended anti-tuberculosis or anti-diabetes regimens.

Materials & methods

Plant material

Mature guava leaves of the ‘Sardar’ variety, used in previous studies, were collected from Shirwal, Satara, Maharashtra in November 2022 and authenticated by an ethnobotanist.

Preliminary analysis

The organoleptic (color, odor, taste) and physicochemical analysis (foreign matter, pH, loss on drying, total and acid insoluble ash values, water and alcohol soluble extractive values) of raw leaves and the extract prepared were conducted as per guidelines [6]. The hardness, friability, disintegration, and dimensions of the compressed tablets were measured as per Indian Pharmacopeia.

Preparation of bulk extract, granulation, and tablet compression

The formulation was developed under supervision of ND at Sudhatatva Ayurved, an FDA-approved and GMP-certified pharmacy. A decoction was initially prepared by heating 1.5 kg of 36-mesh sieved leaves in 24 L potable water at 70–90°C and reducing to 3 L (1/8) [7]. This was then filtered, reheated at 70–75 °C, and dried at 40–45 °C for 3 h to yield 270 g of solid extract. Next, in a granulator equipped with a 16-mesh sieve, granules were formulated using 5% starch (binder) and dried in a tray dryer at 60°C for 12 h. The dried granules were then blended with 1% magnesium stearate and 1% talc, and compressed into 250 mg tablets (Ghanvati) using an 8 mm punch on a rotary tablet press.

Stability studies

The tablets were stored in wide-mouth high-density polyethylene containers and in addition to ambient conditions, subjected to elevated temperature and humidity conditions of 30 ± 2°C; 65 ± 5% relative humidity (RH) (intermediate conditions) and 40 ± 2°C; 75 ± 5% RH (accelerated conditions) in stability chambers. The tablets were assayed at 0-day and at the end of 3, 6, and 12 months as per the International Council for Harmonization Guidelines [Q1(R2)] [8]. The physical parameters [hardness, disintegration, and loss on drying (LOD)] were undertaken by standard methods. Based on literature, the quantification of representative phytoconstituents: gallic acid (GA), epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), ellagic acid (EA), and quercetin (Q), was conducted using high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Shimadzu (LC2050C3D, i series) system comprising a quaternary pump, an autosampler, and a photodiode array detector. A Kromasil 100 C18 analytical column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) was employed and a mobile phase comprising 0.1 % orthophosphoric acid (A) and methanol (B) was used in gradient mode. Throughout the gradient process (0.01–7 min: A, 70-20%: B 30–80%; 7–11 min: A, 20%: B, 80%; from 11 to 17 min as A, 20–70%: B, 80-30%; and then as A, 70%: B, 30% till 17 min), the flow rate of the mobile phase was maintained at 1 mL/min with UV detection at 254 nm. Each phytoconstituent was appropriately diluted in methanol, and the linearity plots obtained before initiating studies.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of mean. A two-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test using Prism 6.0 (GraphPad, Inc., USA) was conducted. Significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Preliminary analysis

The organoleptic and physicochemical parameters of raw leaves and the prepared extract were comparable and within permissible limits (data not shown). The friability of the compressed tablets was acceptable (<1 %) as per pharmacopeial standards. The average diameter and thickness were 8.00 ± 0.05 and 4.80 ± 0.10 mm, respectively.

As seen from Table 1, the hardness of the tablets varied from 4.47 to 2.33 kg/cm2, the disintegration time ranged from 62 to 70 min, and the LOD% was between 0.06 and 8.18. As compared to 0-day, the increase in disintegration time under ambient conditions was significant at 3 and 12 months; at accelerated conditions significant change was noted between 3 and 6 months (p < 0.05), with a decrease at the 6 month. HPLC studies

Representative chromatograms of each phytocompound, the extract, and the tablet are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. The linearity in concentration ranged from 10 to 100 μg/mL (r2 = 0.998), 0.5–15 μg/mL (r2 = 0.990), 0.5–2.5 μg/mL (r2 = 0.998), and 0.05–3 μg/mL (r2 = 0.998) for GA, EGCG, EA, and Q, respectively.

As for tablet stability (Fig. 1), in comparison to 0-day (16.0 ± 0.14 μg/mg), the concentration of GA decreased (ranging from 5.39 ± 0.77 to 14.44 ± 0.99 μg/mg) under all storage conditions and time intervals; however, at 12 months under ambient conditions, its concentration was higher in comparison to previous intervals (p < 0.0001). For EGCG, under all three conditions, as compared to the 0-day (4.03 ± 0.66 μg/mg), a decrease in concentration was seen at 3 and 6-months (ambient: 0.89 ± 0.20 and 0.77 ± 0.03 μg/mg; intermediate: 0.869 ± 0.08 and 1.16 ± 0.05 μg/mg, respectively) (p < 0.001), and the concentration increased significantly at 12 months (ambient: 6.524 ± 0.93 μg/mg; intermediate: 5.53 ± 0.34 μg/mg). At 3 months under all three conditions, the amount of EA decreased (0.09 ± 0.02 to 0.193 ± 0.07 μg/mg) from 0.5137 ± 0.08 μg/mg (0-day) but increased at 6 and 12 months (ranging from 2.27 ± 0.07 to 8.267 ± 0.87 μg/mg); the difference between intervals (except 0-day vs. 3 months) being highly significant (p < 0.0001). Discussion

In this study, guava leaves of 'Sardar' variety collected from Shirwal, Maharashtra, India, were used. In continuation of our previous proof-of-concept clinical trial [5], the present study aimed conducting stability of a Ghanvati formulated from GLD to assess parameters for developing an appropriate formulation for future studies. The study not being a clinical trial involving humans, the plant material was not analyzed for heavy metals, pesticide, and aflatoxin. A detailed pharmacognostical characterization though not done, we did conduct the organoleptic and physicochemical analysis of the leaves and prepared extract, and found them to be within limits specified in Ayurvedic pharmacopeia.

Stability testing being central to the study, it is worth mentioning that this important aspect reveals the variability of herbal products over time under different conditions such as temperature and moisture stressing appropriate storage conditions and shelf life [9]. We assessed the stability of prepared Ghanvati over a 12-month period. According to Ayurveda classics, Ghanvati is stated to be potent till one year from preparation [10], while contemporary literature mentions 3 years from manufacture [11]. The formulated Ghanvati underwent considerable moisture-related changes (highly hygroscopic) over time, with storage conditions having an important impact on its stability despite storage in air tight high-density polyethylene containers. The concentration of the phytoconstituents included varied at the time intervals tested.

The significant decrease in hardness of Ghanvati after 6 months of storage can be correlated to the higher LOD values. The higher hygroscopicity and moisture content could be attributed to the presence of free and bound water in guava extract due to the high number of hydrophilic groups forming hydrogen bonds with water as documented earlier [12]. A maximum of up to 8–10% moisture content in extracts is considered acceptable as per industry and regulatory guidelines to maintain the stability and reduce the microbial contamination [13]. Ghanvati stored under controlled conditions showed acceptable moisture content. The increase in disintegration time throughout the study period can be attributed to the hydrophilicity of dried GLD which is similar to reports of a significant impact of the extraction and drying process on moisture content in herbal products [14].

Low stability of polyphenols like GA and their high susceptibility to degradation under different conditions is known [15]. Variability in the biological efficacy of EGCG when tested for its in vitro and in vivo effects may be correlated to its decreased stability, thereby lowering the bioavailability and resulting in degradation [16]. Extracts containing EA are stable in dried powder but not in aqueous solution [17]. The variability in EA content over the 6-month is probably due to notable reversible changes associated with the presence of lactone rings, as reported earlier [18]. The reduced levels of EA and Q seen in the present study could be due to their oxidation at an accelerated rate associated with temperature and light [19]. The sudden increase in these compounds after 12 months may be due to increased moisture content. In presence of moisture, the polyphenolic oligosaccharides may convert to phenolic monomers due to hydrolysis process mainly under acidic conditions [20].

Though we had not carried out any pharmacological activity, a brief mention of possible effects of changes in efficacy due to observed levels of phytoconstituents screened in this study need a mention. In light of our previous studies, and literature attributing antidiarrhoeal activity of GLD to the presence of several compounds (including the four used in the present study) [21], the changes seen in the amount of the phytoconstituents, could affect the antidiarrhoeal efficacy of the developed Ghanvati.

The study had some limitations. Microbial load of the collected leaves and the prepared Ghanvati was not estimated. We did not explore other reported stabilizers/additives towards enhancing stability. No representative bioassay was undertaken with Ghanvati to correlate the observed changes with any pharmacological activity.

Conclusion

The developed Ghanvati exhibited stability in terms of hardness and disintegration time; however, the hygroscopicity and chemical variability with time warrant modifications to the procedure used. Minimizing LOD with suitable packaging, preparing the formulation with suitable organic solvents or using hydro-alcoholic guava leaf extracts, and employing novel drug delivery systems such as nano/microparticles, can be explored for future studies.
