Radiomorphometric and texture-based mandibular bone assessment in type 2 diabetes mellitus: correlation with vitamin D, osteocalcin, and glycemic control—an analytical cross-sectional study
Authors: A. Benjamin Rajasekar, C. L. Krithika, Anuradha Ganesan, K.Yesoda Aniyan, Sasidharan Sivakumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This analytical cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate the association between Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and mandibular bone quality by integrating biochemical markers with radiomorphometric and texture based indices. A total of four groups were assessed: healthy males, healthy females, diabetic males, and diabetic females. Biochemical parameters were measured, and mandibular bone quality was quantified using Mental Index (MI), Periosteal Indices (PMI-i, PMI-s), Mandibular Cortical Index (MCI), fractal dimension, trabecular strut analysis, and gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) features. Group differences were analysed using Kruskal–Wallis tests with Dunn’s post hoc comparisons, chi-square analysis for categorical variables, Spearman correlations, and multivariable regression models adjusted for age, sex, and glycemic control. Diabetic participants were characterized by significantly lower vitamin D and osteocalcin levels, reduced MI, PMI-i, and PMI-s values, and higher MCI scores, features consistent with cortical thinning and porosity. Fractal dimension and trabecular strut parameters showed a pattern of deterioration predominantly in diabetic males, while texture homogeneity differed between diabetic males and females. Correlation analysis confirmed strong internal consistency among mandibular indices and positive associations with osteocalcin, whereas vitamin D showed limited relationships. Regression models identified diabetes status as an independent correlate of cortical thinning and higher MCI severity, while HbA1c was strongly associated with osteocalcin reduction. T2DM is associated with a clinically meaningful reduction in mandibular cortical and trabecular bone quality, independent of age, sex, and glycemic control. Conventional radiomorphometric indices (MI, PMI, MCI) proved robust, reproducible, and clinically feasible, whereas advanced texture-based parameters showed limited independent explanatory power. Panoramic radiographs may serve as valuable adjunctive tools for opportunistic screening of skeletal alterations co-occuring within diabetic populations, warranting validation through longitudinal studies incorporating DEXA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41522-2.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus, is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent elevation of blood glucose levels and impaired metabolism of fats and proteins. This results from insufficient insulin production, insulin resistance, or both1. Its global prevalence, driven by shifting lifestyles, stress, and obesity, currently affects approximately 537 million adults which is projected to rise to 643 million by 2030 and potentially to 783 million by 20452. Beyond its well-documented vascular and neurological complications3, Diabetes Mellitus exerts a profound impact on skeletal integrity, predisposing individuals to osteoporosis and fragility fractures.

Chronic hyperglycemia disrupts bone remodelling by impairing osteoblast function and enhancing osteoclast activity, thereby reducing bone formation and increasing resorption. Deficiencies in vitamin D4,5, a key regulator of calcium homeostasis and bone remodelling, together with altered serum osteocalcin levels, a non-collagenous protein secreted by osteoblasts that is essential for bone mineralization and implicated in glucose metabolism further compromise skeletal strength in individuals with diabetes. Research indicates that poor glycemic control is associated with reduced osteocalcin levels6,7. These biochemical alterations manifest radiographically as changes in bone mineral density and microarchitecture, which can be quantified through radiomorphometric indices and texture-based analyses on panoramic radiographs (OPG).

These radiograph offers a non-invasive means of assessing mandibular bone quality through radiomorphometric indices and texture-based parameters such as Fractal dimension (FD), Strut analysis, and Gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM). Evidence suggests that OPG derived bone assessments show good correlation with dual energy Xray absorptiometry (DEXA) measurements, the current gold standard for bone mineral density evaluation8–10. However, their specific relevance in assessing skeletal health among diabetic populations remains underexplored. Evaluating mandibular bone integrity on Panoramic radiographs in T2DM is clinically vital as it can serve as a non-invasive interim for identifying systemic bone health by providing essential data for customising complex dental interventions. Therefore, this study was conducted to evaluate the relationship between biochemical markers with radiographic bone parameters in individuals with and without T2DM. By integrating biochemical profiling with the radiographic bone density assessment, this study aims to establish a comprehensive assessment framework for understanding diabetes-related skeletal degeneration.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a cross-sectional observational design with the inclusion of 132 participants, with all aged 35 years and above. Participants were stratified into four groups, each comprising 33 individuals: diabetic males, diabetic females, healthy control males, and healthy control females.

Group allocation was based on HbA1c levels, ensuring clear distinction between diabetic and non-diabetic controls. Age and sex were carefully matched across groups to minimize confounding. In addition, menopausal status was assessed among female participants and evenly distributed between the control and diabetic female groups to account for its potential influence on bone metabolism.

Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (Approval No. SRMDC/IRB/2022/MDS/No.903). All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a detailed explanation of the study objectives and procedures, in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. To ensure methodological rigor and transparency, the study design, conduct, and reporting strictly adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines11 (Supplementary file S1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria Participants in the diabetic group were required to have an HbA1c value greater than 6.5% along with a confirmed history of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus for at least two years. Individuals in the control group were included if their HbA1c levels were below 5.7%.

Exclusion criteria Participants were excluded if they had a known diagnosis of osteoporosis or osteopenia, thyroid dysfunction, renal disease, or parathyroid disorders. Individuals undergoing treatment with bisphosphonates, systemic corticosteroids, anticonvulsants, or hormonal therapy were also excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included pregnancy, lactation, smoking, alcohol consumption, or unwillingness to provide informed consent.

Sample collection

5 ml venous blood were collected from each participant using multisampling needles from the antecubital region under aseptic conditions. Samples were then transferred into EDTA vacutainers and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15 min in 2–8 °C within 30 min of collection. Serum samples were separated and stored at − 80 °C. Upon completion of the collection phase, all samples were analysed in a single batch. Vitamin D and HbA1c levels were measured in a NABL accredited laboratory. Osteocalcin levels were quantified using a Human Osteocalcin ELISA kit (Invitrogen, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

ELISA procedure

The diluted standards, blanks, and samples were pipetted into wells in duplicate, sealed, and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. Following this, a biotinylated detection antibody was added, and the plate was incubated for 1 h. After thorough washing, horseradish peroxidase (HRP) Conjugate was introduced and incubated for 30 min. The plate was then washed five times, before the addition of substrate reagent, which was allowed to react up to 30 min before adding Stop Solution. Optical density was measured at 450 nm, and duplicate readings were averaged and the final concentrations were calculated using a four-parameter logistic curve with adjustments made for dilution factors.

Radiographic acquisition and ROI selection

Digital OPGs were captured using the XMind Trium (Acteon, France) with exposure parameters set between 70–80 kVp and 8–15 mA. Four Regions of Interest (ROI) were chosen based on criteria proposed by Hwang et al.13. ROI-1 in the central condylar head area, ROI-2 in the central ramus, ROI-3 between the molars, and ROI-4 near the endosteal margin. Image processing and analysis

Regions of interest (ROIs) were cropped using Adobe Photoshop, version 23.0. The cropped images were subsequently processed in ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) with the FracLac plugin for fractal dimension (FD) analysis. Strut analysis and gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) texture evaluation were performed using MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA), while radiomorphometric measurements were facilitated through the Sidexis 4 viewer software (Dentsply Sirona, Bensheim, Germany).

Image enhancement followed the White and Rudolph method14, which involved 400% magnification with bicubic interpolation, application of a Gaussian blur filter, subtraction of blurred image from the original, and normalization by adding a grey value of 128 to each pixel. The enhanced images were then binarized and skeletonized for advanced structural and texture analysis (Fig. 1B).

Fractal dimension

FD was performed to assess the complexity and architectural variation of trabecular bone. The skeletonized binary images of the ROIs were analysed using the box counting method, quantifying trabecular irregularity and bone texture variation.

Grey level co-occurrence matrix

GLCM offers insight into the spatial distribution of pixel intensities. Four key textural parameters were calculated for each ROI: contrast, correlation, energy, and homogeneity, each representing different aspects of trabecular bone structure.

Strut

Strut was conducted to evaluate the structural integrity of high-density trabecular bone networks. High density bone was marked with white pixels, while low density areas were represented by black pixels. Metrics assessed included the High-Density Area (HDA), the number and density of nodes (N, Nd), termini (Tm), and Total Strut Length (TSL). Each parameter was normalized relative to bone area or perimeter to standardize intergroup comparisons, providing insights into bone integrity and potential osteoporotic changes.

Radiomorphometric assessment

Mandibular bone quality was evaluated using radiomorphometric indices. The Mandibular Cortical Index (MCI), categorized as C1, C2, or C3, was employed to assess cortical erosion15. The Mental Index (MI) measures the cortical width at the level of the mental foramen16. Two variants of Panoramic Mandibular Index (PMI) were calculated: PMI-s (from the superior margin of the mental foramen to the mandibular base) and PMI-i (from the inferior margin of the mental foramen to the mandibular base), both expressed as ratios of the mandibular cortical width to the corresponding vertical distances (Fig. 1C)17.

Reliability assessment

A single examiner performed all radiomorphometric assessments. Intra-examiner reliability was evaluated by re-assessing 20% of the radiographs, randomly selected for analysis, after a 10-day interval. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was calculated and yielded a value of 0.87, indicating good agreement and consistent measurements across repeated evaluations.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and JASP, Version 0.19.1 (JASP Team, University of Amsterdam, Netherlands). Descriptive statistics were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables due to non-normal distributions, assessed using Shapiro–Wilk test, and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Group comparisons for continuous variables were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons. The Chi-square (χ2) test was used for categorical variables.

Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to assess the relationships between diabetes status, glycemic control, serum biomarkers, and mandibular bone parameters, adjusting for age and sex as potential confounders. Separate regression models were constructed for each outcome variable, including radiomorphometric indices (MI, PMI-s, PMI-i and MCI) and bone texture parameters (FD, Strut, and GLCM features). Due to non-normal data distribution, all regression analyses will be employed with bias-corrected accelerated bootstrapping with 5000 resamples to obtain robust confidence intervals and p-values. Prior to regression modelling, Spearman correlation matrices were examined to assess multicollinearity and guide variable selection.

Results

Descriptive characteristics

Supplementary Table S1 summarizes the characteristics of study cohort stratified by group (Control vs. Diabetic). The groups were comparable in age (Diabetes group: 50.93 ± 8.316 years, Control group: 50.95 ± 8.738 years). HbA1c levels were higher in the Diabetic group, with the median value of 8.75, compared with 5.6 in the control group. Lower median values for the Mental Index (MI), mean PMI-i, and mean PMI-s, along with higher MCI, were observed in connection with the Diabetic group. In addition, lower levels of both serum vitamin D and osteocalcin levels were noted in the Diabetic group. Differences were also observed in texture and microstructural parameters.

Lower Vitamin D levels were observed in diabetic males compared with control males after adjustment. Serum osteocalcin was lower in both diabetic females and diabetic males when compared with their respective control counterparts, with the strongest effects observed in comparisons involving diabetic males. All mandibular indices (MI, PMI-i, PMI-s) showed lower values in diabetic females and males relative to controls, co-occurring with diabetes-associated cortical bone compromise. Fractal dimension exhibited significant sex- and disease-related differences, with diabetic males differing significantly from control males and diabetic females even after Bonferroni correction. Trabecular number–based parameters (N.Tm and N.Nd per unit area, TSL, and periphery) were lower predominantly in diabetic males compared with diabetic females, aligning with patterns of greater trabecular deterioration in males with diabetes. Texture homogeneity also differed significantly between diabetic females and males, suggesting an association between altered mandibular bone microarchitecture and glycemic status. Overall, the adjusted post-hoc results show pattern of lower bone quality in those with Type 2 diabetes, with a more pronounced differences in males.

Correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis presented in supplementary table S2, revealed that the radiomorphometric indices like MI, mean PMI-i, mean PMI-s showed a strong positive correlation with each other (ρ = 0.73–0.79, p < 0.001), revealing internal consistency in mandibular cortical thickness assessments. They also correlated positively with serum osteocalcin (ρ = 0.42–0.54, p < 0.001), aligning with its biological link between bone turnover markers and radiographic bone density. MCI demonstrated moderate inverse correlations with MI, PMI-i, and PMI-s (ρ ≈ –0.30 to –0.38, p < 0.001), showing that cortical erosion co-occurred with porosity, simultaneously. Though Vitamin D is a globally accepted marker of bone health, this specific cohort shows it is not a direct driver of the structural jaw changes seen on the panoramic radiographs. There was weak or non-association of Vitamin D with serum osteocalcin. This unexpected association could be attributed to the fact that this analysis was not performed with grouping based on HbA1C levels.

Strut analysis parameters (N.Tm, N.Nd ratios) exhibited extremely high intercorrelations (ρ > 0.98, p < 0.001), coinciding with redundancy among trabecular connectivity measures. Representative variables (e.g., N.Tm/sq cm) were included for regression modeling. Among GLCM features, Contrast and Correlation showed a near-perfect inverse relationship (ρ = − 0.91), while Energy and Homogeneity were strongly correlated (ρ = 0.748). Contrast and Energy were the selected features in regression modelling to prevent redundancy.

Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the independent associations between Type 2 diabetes mellitus, sex, age, and glycemic control with biochemical, radiomorphometric, and texture-based mandibular bone parameters (Table 3). All models were fully adjusted for the covariates included in each analysis, and bootstrap-corrected p values were applied to enhance the robustness of inference. Cortical bone assessment revealed that diabetes was strongly and independently associated with lower Mean MI values, paralleling with mandibular cortical thinning irrespective of age, sex, or glycemic level. Similarly, MCI severity was independently higher in diabetic individuals, consistent with increased cortical porosity and endosteal erosion. Trabecular and periosteal indices (PMI-i and PMI-s) were significantly lower in diabetics and females, aligning with diabetes- and sex-related compromise of mandibular bone thickness. HbA1c showed a modest independent association with PMI-i, suggesting a link between chronic hyperglycemia and trabecular architecture. Trabecular number was not independently associated with diabetes, sex, age, or HbA1c. Among texture features, homogeneity was independently associated with sex but not with diabetes status, while energy showed no significant independent associations. Collectively, these adjusted models demonstrate that Type 2 diabetes mellitus is independently associated with variations in mandibular cortical and trabecular bone quality, beyond the patterns seen with age, sex, and glycemic control.

Discussion

The present study investigated the relationship between type 2 diabetes mellitus, biochemical markers, and mandibular bone quality using panoramic radiographic indices and advanced texture-based parameters. Stratifying participants were divided into four groups (healthy males, healthy females, diabetic males, and diabetic females), both disease-related and sex-specific differences were observed in cortical and trabecular bone characteristics. Serum vitamin D levels were consistently lower in diabetic participants compared with controls (Fig. 2A), with post hoc analysis revealing more pronounced reductions in males5. This finding is associated with lower vitamin D levels among men with diabetes, while the absence of significant differences between control and diabetic females co-occurs with the presence of estrogen, its interaction with vitamin D, and favorable fat distribution18. Regression analysis further showed that sex was the primary independent variable associated with vitamin D status, highlighting the relevance of hormonal and metabolic factors alongside diabetes grouping.Fig. 2Rain cloud graph of mean HbA1c, Vit-D, OC, MI, PMI and FD.

Serum osteocalcin levels were lower in diabetic individuals (Fig. 2B), aligning with an inverse relationship with glycemic control7,19. Multivariable regression demonstrated that HbA1c was the dominant negative predictor of osteocalcin, while female sex and age showed positive associations. Importantly, diabetes status itself was not an independent correlate after adjustment, indicating that variations in bone turnover markers match metabolic dysregulation patterns more closely than the clinical diagnosis of diabetes. These findings show a link between glycemic control and markers of bone metabolism.

Radiomorphometric indices derived from panoramic radiographs demonstrated consistent and clinically relevant differences. Mean Mental Index values were lowest in diabetic participants (Fig. 2C), aligning with previous reports of cortical thinning observed in connection with diabetes20,21. Similarly, PMI-i and PMI-s values were noted in diabetic groups (Fig. 2D and E), with regression models confirming diabetes status as an independent negative correlate across all indices. Sex was an additional factor associated with PMI-i and PMI-s, with females showing lower trabecular indices, consistent with prior literature22. These results indicate that diabetes is a robust independent variable related to differences in mandibular cortical and trabecular characteristics.

Fractal dimension values were significantly lower in diabetic groups (Fig. 2F), co-occurring with variations in trabecular architecture. Differences were particularly evident between control and diabetic males, and between diabetic males and females. Although concerns regarding the reliability of fractal dimension analysis persist23, our methodology supports its utility, consistent with previous studies10,24, particularly in identifying osteoporosis-related changes. Moreover prior research has established that fractal dimensions are effective in assessing structural alterations in mandible caused by systemic inflammatory conditions, which further clarifies its precision25. Mandibular Cortical Index analysis further revealed a higher frequency of advanced cortical erosion (C2 and C3) in diabetic participants26, with regression and correlation analyses identifying MCI as an independent qualitative correlate of cortical status.

Trabecular strut parameters revealed limited differences between control and diabetic females, whereas some parameters differed between control and diabetic males. Regression analysis showed that trabecular number (N.Tm/sq cm) was not significantly associated with diabetes status, sex, age, or HbA1c, consistent with a lack of correlation between these variables and trabecular number. The observed sex-specific differences may reflect underlying variations in bone metabolism, although averaging of ROI values is linked to different patterns of localized changes compared with prior reports13.

Texture-based GLCM features did not demonstrate significant differences between control and diabetic individuals of the same sex. However, differences were observed in correlation and homogeneity between diabetic males and females, co-occurring with sex-related variations in trabecular microarchitecture. Strong intercorrelations among GLCM parameters highlight their coherence in texture description but curb their independent discriminative utility.

Collectively, correlation and regression analyses reveal that bone alterations observed in diabetic populations co-occur with multiple factors, including glycemic control, pharmacological influences of commonly prescribed drugs, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, microvascular impairment, and the accumulation of advanced glycation end products, indicating that, in diabetic individuals the bone health is dependant on various factors which could collectively lead to changes in bone health27–29. The discrepancy between the assessed biochemical parameters and bone health necessitiates a more comprehensive investigation.

Panoramic radiographic features, particularly cortical thinning and reduced trabecular quality, are associated with early imaging findings of skeletal involvement in type 2 diabetes. While DEXA remains the gold standard for bone mineral density assessment, its limited accessibility co-occurs with infrequent routine use. Previous work has shown that fractal dimension analysis may correlate with skeletal status30, yet in our study, advanced image-derived parameters such as fractal dimension, strut analysis, and GLCM features demonstrated limited independent association in multivariable models and their role in routine clinical assessment remains exploratory within the framework of this study . Accordingly, conventional radiomorphometric indices (MI, PMI, MCI) exist as simple, reproducible, and clinically feasible tools for the opportunistic screening of skeletal alterations in individuals with diabetes within routine dental practice. Moreover this assessment can provide tailored treatment options for performing complex dental procedures.

Limitations

Commonly prescribed agents that could potentially affect bone health like proton pump inhibitors and vitamin D supplements were not considered in exclusion, as this study employed a cross-sectional design, it precludes causal inference and temporal assessment of bone changes. The absence of DEXA validation limits direct comparison with the gold standard for bone mineral density. Potential undiagnosed comorbidities and lifestyle factors may have influenced biochemical and radiographic outcomes. Finally, averaging ROI values may have reduced robustness to localized trabecular alterations.

Conclusion

This study identifies a significant association between specific radiomorphometric indices on panoramic radiographs with T2DM, with serum osteocalcin showing a biological link with bone turnover. Presence of these objective radiographic markers within routine dental imaging may assist in the identification of at risk patients allowing for precise treatment planning. Given these observations, future longitudinal research incorporating DEXA validation is warranted to confirm these associations and strengthen the translational utility of panoramic radiographic screening in diabetic populations.
