Efficacy of device-measured physical activity on cardiometabolic outcomes among individuals with abnormal glucose tolerance in Ghana: A pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Thomas Hormenu, Ebenezer Oduro Antiri, Stephen Ocansey, Eric Awlime-Ableh, Juliet Elikem Paku, Edward Wilson Ansah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbnormal glucose tolerance (AGT) is surging rapidly in sub-Saharan Africa, yet evidence on effective interventions remain limited. Physical activity improves glycemic control, but self-reported measures are prone to bias. Device-based monitoring enables objective assessment and may enhance adherence. This pilot randomized controlled trial assessed the preliminary efficacy of a structured, device-measured physical activity intervention on cardiometabolic outcomes among Ghanaian adults with AGT.MethodsAn open-label, parallel-group pilot RCT with blinded outcome assessment randomly assigned 28 adults aged ≥25 years with newly diagnosed AGT who owned smartphones to intervention and control groups. The intervention involved a 12-week structured program prescribing ≥150 min of moderate-intensity or ≥ 75 min of vigorous-intensity activity weekly, tracked via smartphone sensors and mobile health applications. Participants received brief training and were instructed to carry their phones during activity. Controls received basic health education materials and kept activity journals. Primary outcomes included glycemic measures and secondary outcomes comprised anthropometric, bioimpedance, and biochemical markers. Generalized estimating equations were used under an intention-to-treat protocol.ResultsAdherence was high, with mean daily moderate-to-vigorous physical activity of 77.4 ± 44.2 min. Group × Time interactions suggested differential changes favouring the intervention group for BMI (β = −2.7 kg/m2, 95% CI: −5.2 to −0.1), alkaline phosphatase (β = −24.0 U/L, 95% CI: −47.9 to −0.1), and total protein (β = −12.6 g/L, 95% CI: −23.2 to −2.0). The intervention group was associated with larger within-group changes in HbA1c (−1.2% vs. -0.6%), weight (−4.7 kg vs −0.8 kg), basal metabolic rate (+92.9 vs +66.7 kcal/day), and total body water (+18.5 L vs +16.5 L).ConclusionsDevice-based physical activity interventions appear feasible and acceptable among Ghanaian adults with AGT. Preliminary findings suggest potential benefits for body composition and glycemic control, supporting progression to larger trials to confirm efficacy and assess scalability in low-resource settings.Trial registrationThis trial is prospectively registered with the Pan African Clinical Trails Registry (PACTR202409492459905).

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has become a global epidemic, with incidence rising sharply in Sub-Saharan Africa [1]. Abnormal glucose tolerance (AGT), encompassing both prediabetes and diabetes [2], is also increasing, with prevalence in Ghana estimated at 18% [3]. This places a significant burden on healthcare systems, driven by cardiometabolic comorbidities and over-reliance on pharmacological treatments [3], [4]. Lifestyle interventions offer potential to reduce disease progression and complications [5], [6], with the prediabetes stage providing a crucial window for intervention [3]. However, few evidence-based interventions have been developed or tested in African settings [7].

Physical activity is a cornerstone of AGT management, improving insulin sensitivity, glucose regulation, lipid profiles, and broader cardiometabolic health. [8], [9], [10], [11]. Clinical markers such as lipid profiles, liver function, and kidney function serve as reliable indicators of AGT progression, and may improve through regular physical activity [12]. Current guidelines recommend at least 150 min of moderate-intensity or 75 min of vigorous-intensity activity weekly [13], [14], yet little is known about the efficacy of structured physical activity interventions in African populations with AGT.

Evidence from Ghana is limited to a few studies that focused primarily on glycemic outcomes in individuals with diagnosed diabetes, overlooking both broader cardiometabolic indicators and persons with diabetes [15], [16]. Critically, these studies relied on self-reported physical activity assessments, which are prone to social desirability effects, recall bias, and over-reporting [17]. Mobile health technology and smartphone-enabled sensors now enable objective, real time tracking of physical activity [17], [18], offering scalable solutions in Africa where smartphone usage is growing quickly [19]. Despite this technological advancement, no studies have evaluated device-measured physical activity interventions targeting individuals with AGT in African populations. This represents a significant gap, as objective monitoring could enhance adherence, reduce measurement error, and provide more accurate evaluation of intervention effects [17].

This pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) addressed this gap by investigating the preliminary efficacy and feasibility of a structured, device-based physical activity intervention on cardiometabolic outcomes among Ghanaians adults newly diagnosed with AGT as determined by glycemic measures of Fasting Plasma Glucose (FPG), Fasting Blood Glucose (FBG), 2-h Postprandial Glucose (2hPG), and Glycated Hemoglobin (HbA1c). Smartphone-based monitoring was used to enable more objective evaluation of intervention adherence and reduce reporting bias.

The findings have implications for future research, public health policy, and clinical practice. Scalable, cost-effective interventions tailored to African contexts could be incorporated into national diabetes prevention programs. This study adds to evidence supporting lifestyle approaches to diabetes prevention, and contributes to the global effort against AGT in low-resource settings.

Methods

Study design

This single-blinded, parallel-group RCT assessed the impact of a 12-week structured physical activity intervention on cardiometabolic outcomes among adults recently diagnosed with AGT in the Cape Coast Metropolis, Ghana. Measurements were taken at baseline (T0) and 12 weeks (T1). The study adhered to an intention-to-treat (ITT) protocol, including all participants in the final analysis regardless of adherence or withdrawal. This study was approved by the University of Cape Coast Institutional Review Board (UCCIRB/EXT/2022/27) and registered with the Pan African Clinical Trials Registry (PACTR202409492459905) on September 16th 2024 (https://pactr.samrc.ac.za/TrialDisplay.aspx?TrialID=31856).

Participants

The study population comprised adults newly diagnosed with AGT through the screening process, encompassing both prediabetes and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM).

Inclusion criteria

Participants aged 25 years or above with glycemic levels indicative of AGT [2-h PG of 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L) or above; FBS of 6.6 mmol/L or above; HbA1c of 5.7% or above; FPG of 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) or above] [20], who owned a smartphone, and who were medication-naive at enrolment were included. Participants diagnosed with overt T2DM during screening who were subsequently prescribed antihyperglycemic medications were eligible, with medication use recorded and adjusted for in the analyses.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded from if they had BMI less than 18.5 kg/m2, pre-existing diagnosis of type 1 or 2 diabetes, were anemic, pregnant or lactating, had physical, cardiovascular or psychiatric conditions contraindicating participation, any medical condition making physical activity unsafe, or were receiving antihyperglycemic medications, antihypertensive agents, or weight modifying medications before enrolment.

Recruitment

Using a multi-stage sampling, 1200 individuals from urban, peri-urban, and rural clusters were screened for undiagnosed AGT using FBS, FPG, HbA1c, and 2hPG. Details of the screening are described elsewhere [21](https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2024.1337895). Sample size

Given an estimated dropout rate of 20% for a pilot RCT in similar settings [22], [23], a minimum of 20 participants was determined based on feasibility considerations in this setting, and was considered sufficient to evaluate retention [24], [25]. To account for the intervention's complexity and resource requirements, a target sample size of 30 was initially planned. However, 28 individuals (14 per arm) provided informed consent and were enrolled in the study. This still exceeded the minimum threshold and remained adequate for the study's objectives. As a pilot study, the primary objective was to assess feasibility and generate preliminary estimates of effect rather than to detect definitive treatment effect. Consequently, no formal power calculation was conducted, consistent with recommendations that pilot studies prioritise feasibility parameters and confidence interval estimation to inform sample size calculations for future definitive trials [26].

Procedures

Randomization

Participants were randomized into intervention (n = 14) and control (n = 14) groups using a web-based system by an independent researcher. Block randomization ensured balanced group allocation stratified by diabetes status. This was an open-label study due to the nature of the intervention, while outcome assessors and data analysts being blinded.

Intervention

The intervention group followed a structured 12-week physical activity regimen, prescribing a minimum of 150 min (18,750 steps) of moderate or 75 min (10,425 steps) of vigorous-intensity activity per week [13], [14]. The physical activity prescription followed standardized weekly targets for all participants, though individual modifications were permitted based on baseline fitness levels, physical limitations, or comorbidities. Core activities included brisk walking and running, with duration increasing every four weeks. Participants were advised not to exceed two consecutive days without physical activity.

Prior to the start of the intervention, participants were given standardized safety instructions on how to progress gradually, stay hydrated, wear proper footwear, and recognize warning signs including dizziness, chest pain, musculoskeletal pain, or hypoglycemic symptoms. If such symptoms occurred, participants were instructed to cease activity and contact the research team.

Physical activity was monitored throughout the intervention using smartphone sensors (GPS, accelerometer, gyroscope) through mobile health applications (Apple Health, Google Fit). Activity data were collected passively and continuously, synchronised automatically, and summarised at daily and weekly intervals. Adherence was assessed based on the proportion of prescribed weekly physical activity targets achieved by participants over the intervention period. Weekly activity summaries were reviewed by the research team, and participants in the intervention group received brief weekly feedback through phone calls and messages to reinforce adherence, clarify targets, and address barriers to participation. Data quality checks were conducted to identify implausible values, non-wear periods, or synchronisation failures.

Participants were advised to progress at their own pace within the structured framework, and those with diabetes received additional guidance regarding exercise timing relative to medication and meals. Participants with diabetes were cautioned against exercising immediately after oral hypoglycemics without eating. Adverse events were reported by participants and reviewed by the research team, with events requiring medical attention being referred to appropriate healthcare services and documented throughout the study.

The controls received health education materials emphasizing the benefits of physical activity but no structured activity prescription. They maintained daily activity journals, meaning that objective physical activity monitoring was implemented only in the intervention group, while the control group relied on subjective self-report. Both groups received standardized nutritional guidance from a dietitian to and were asked to report adverse events. The intervention design was informed by previous physical activity studies in AGT [17], [27].

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was glycemic control assessed using HbA1c, FBS measured by point of care glucometry, FPG measured by laboratory analysis, and 2hPG. Point-of-care glucometry was used to provide immediate feedback to participants during the intervention, while laboratory plasma measurements including FPG and 2hPG were obtained as part of the oral glucose tolerance test. These measures were used to evaluate whether the intervention resulted in meaningful changes in glycemic levels among participants.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes included anthropometric measures, bioimpedance measures, and biochemical measures. Anthropometric measures included weight and height to determine Body Mass Index (BMI), waist-to-hip ratio, blood pressure and bioimpedance measures (total body fat, metabolic age, basal metabolic rate (BMR), total body water). Biochemical measures included lipid profile tests, liver function, and kidney function tests. Physical activity was tracked throughout the entire study period.

Data measures

Data were collected at baseline and 12 weeks at the Cardiometabolic Epidemiology Research Laboratory, University of Cape Coast between November 2024 and February 2025. Fasting Blood Sugar was assessed using a glucometer device, HbA1c was assessed using the HbA1c analyzer, glucose tolerance was assessed using FPG and 2hPG and was analyzed using the blood chemistry analyzer. Additionally, the blood chemistry analyzer was used to analyze results from lipid profile tests [total cholesterol, triglycerides, High-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL)], liver function tests [albumin, total bilirubin, indirect bilirubin, direct bilirubin, Alanine transaminase (ALT), Aspartate aminotransferase (AST), Alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and Gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT)], and kidney function tests (total protein, creatinine, uric acid, urea and globulin). The blood pressure of participants was measured using a digital Sphygmomanometer. Height was measured using a stadiometer and weight was measured using a digital scale.

Physical activity was tracked using mobile health devices interoperable with fitness applications (Google Fit, Samsung Health, Apple Health, Fitbit). Statistical analysis

Distributional assumptions were assessed by examining histograms and conducting Shapiro-Wilk tests for all outcome change scores (Supplementary Material 3). Baseline differences were examined using Chi square tests. Primary and secondary outcomes were analyzed using multivariable models under ITT principles. Consistent with ITT principles, all randomized participants were retained in their assigned groups for analysis. Missing data (n = 4) were handled with multiple imputation generalized estimating equations (MI-GEE) [28], adjusting for group, time, age, sex, marital status, and occupation, and antihyperglycemic medication use among participants with overt diabetes. This approach ensured that all participants contributed to the final estimates regardless of adherence or missing data. Effect sizes of continuous outcomes were expressed as mean difference coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals. Given the number of outcomes examined, a supplementary sensitivity analysis applying a Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure [29] was conducted on the Group × Time interaction estimates (Supplementary file 4). All analyses were done using R software 4.4.3 [30]. Generalized estimating equations were fitted using the geeglm() function from the geepack package [31] with an exchangeable correlation structure.

Adherence to physical activity regimen

Adherence to the physical activity regimen was monitored using a smartphone to assess the minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) per day over the 12-week intervention period (Fig. 3), with all participants meeting the predefined activity targets. Participants maintained a mean daily MVPA of 77.44 ± 44.23 min, exceeding the recommended 30-min threshold. In the GEE analysis (Table 5) the adjusted Group × Time interaction for BMI (β = −2.7 kg/m2, 95% CI: −5.2 to −0.1) suggested a potential differential reduction in adiposity favouring the intervention group. Body weight showed a similar pattern (β = −3.9 kg, 95% CI: −8.4 to 0.6). Total body water (β = 1.95 L, 95% CI: −0.8 to 4.70) showed a small differential change. Group × Time interactions for total body fat (β = −0.7%, 95% CI: −8.03 to 9.5), BMR (β = 26.6 kcal/day, 95% CI: −50.1 to 103.3) and metabolic age (β = −2.5 years, 95% CI: −7.8 to 2.8) were small with confidence intervals.

Biochemical measures

Responses to the intervention were mixed for biochemical measures. Both groups showed increases in HDL (intervention: +11.3 mg/dL, 95% CI: 1.3 to 21.4; controls: +7.7 mg/dL, 95% CI: −2.5 to 17.9), and albumin (intervention: +3.5 g/L, 95% CI: 0.2 to 6.8; control: +7.6 g/L, 95% CI: 0.1 to 15.1), while LDL decreased in the intervention (−2.6 mg/dL, 95% CI: −29.3 to 24.1) but increased in the controls (+16.2 mg/dL, 95% CI: −9.8 to 42.2). Creatinine, uric acid, and urea showed no discernible variation across groups. Discussion

This pilot RCT assessed the preliminary efficacy of a structured, device-measured physical activity intervention on cardiometabolic outcomes among Ghanaian adults newly diagnosed with AGT. The findings suggest possible improvements in BMI, ALP, and total protein based on Group × Time interactions, alongside within-group changes in HbA1c, weight, BMR, and total body water.

Adherence to intervention

Sustaining physical activity is critical to promoting cardiometabolic health in individuals with AGT [32], [33]. Participants in the intervention maintained physical activity levels above current moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) recommendations [34]. Although activity levels fluctuated over the 12-week period, such variation is expected given individual variances in motivation, environmental context, and behavioural adaptation over time [35]. Consistent with previous device-based interventions, physical activity showed sustained improvements across the intervention period [17], [18]. While smartphone-based tracking provides a scalable tool for real-time feedback and improved adherence, the observed variability highlights the need for tailored support strategies [18]. Collectively, these findings support the integration of device-measured physical activity for individuals with AGT. It should be noted, however, that the intervention in the intervention group combined multiple components, including the structured physical activity itself, predefined targets, device-based monitoring, and behavioural reinforcement, while the control group received only general health education and paper-based self-monitoring, Thus, the observed between-group differences reflect the potential combined influence of these components rather than physical activity volume alone and must be considered when interpreting the magnitude and direction of the observed differences.

Glycemic outcomes

Both groups showed reductions in HbA1c and FPG over the 12-week period. The average 1.2% within-group reduction in HbA1c in the intervention group exceeded the clinically meaningful 0.5% threshold and is comparable to improvements from pharmaceutical interventions [36], consistent with evidence that physical activity improves skeletal muscle glucose uptake and insulin sensitivity [10], [11]. This reduction is noteworthy given the 12-week duration, adequate to record physiologic improvements in glycemic markers [17], [18].

However, the Group × Time interaction for HbA1c, FPG, and 2hPG were small with wide confidence intervals, providing no clear evidence of differential change between groups. These findings suggest that while both groups improved over time, the differential effect of the intervention on acute glycemic measures requires further investigation in larger samples. The observed reductions are consistent with evidence that physical activity may stimulate mechanisms that enhance glucose homeostasis and reduce hepatic gluconeogenesis by activating AMPK during aerobic exercise [10].

Anthropometric and bioimpedance outcomes

Within-group changes showed reductions in weight, total body fat, and metabolic age in both groups, alongside increases in BMR and total body water. This is particularly relevant as even the modest weight loss (3–5%) improves insulin sensitivity and lower AGT progression risk [5], [37].

The Group × Time interaction for BMI suggested a potential meaningful reduction in adiposity among intervention participants compared to controls, with body weight showing a similar pattern. This finding provides preliminary evidence that the intervention may have influenced body composition, beyond weight change alone, suggesting that the intervention may have specifically targeted adiposity reduction. The differential effect on BMI, despite the wider weight confidence interval, may reflect changes in body composition favouring lean mass preservation in the intervention group. [38]

The Group × Time interactions for total body fat, BMR, metabolic age, and total body water showed smaller differential changes between groups. The within-group improvements observed for these outcomes, as reflected in the time effects, suggest that both groups may have benefited from participation in the study, potentially due to increased health awareness or the Hawthorne effect [39].

Together, these body composition changes, particularly the possible differential effect on BMI, suggest the intervention may have utility in promoting metabolically beneficial adaptations. Within-group improvements in BMR and total body water in the intervention group suggest possible changes in lean muscle mass and metabolic efficiency, both of which may improve glucose disposal and long-term energy balance [40], [41]. Enhanced cellular hydration may support insulin signaling and glucose transport [42].

Biochemical outcomes

Biochemical changes were mixed but informative. Within-group changes showed increases in HDL and albumin in both groups, while LDL and total cholesterol changes were variable across groups.

The Group × Time interaction for total protein suggested divergent trajectories between groups, with the control group showing increases while the intervention group showed decreases. This pattern suggests potential effects of physical activity on protein metabolism, which may be clinically relevant given the risk of renal complications in AGT [43]. Physical activity may exert nephroprotective effects through improved blood pressure regulation and glycemic control [44].

The Group × Time interaction for ALP suggested a possible differential change between groups, with the control group showing increases while the intervention group showed decreases, suggesting physical activity may enhance hepatic metabolism and reduce hepatic fat accumulation [45].

The remaining biochemical markers showed Group × Time interactions with wide confidence intervals. Total cholesterol, triglycerides, and VLDL showed point estimates favouring the intervention group. These patterns align with evidence linking physical activity to improved lipoprotein metabolism and reduced cardiovascular risk [3], [46]. Albumin, direct bilirubin, and uric acid showed minimal differential changes. No adverse biochemical impacts were noted, supporting the intervention's safety and tolerability in this sample.

With the control group having no predefined activity targets and the intervention group receiving a device-monitored physical activity, these findings still offer important insights for preventing and managing AGT in low-resource settings.

Clinical and public health implications

The study highlights the importance of structured physical activity in AGT management. The Group × Time interactions for BMI, ALP, and total protein provide preliminary evidence for differential intervention effects on body composition, liver function, and protein metabolism. Alongside within-group changes in HbA1c, weight, BMR, and total body water, these findings suggest such interventions may help delay or reduce the need for pharmacological treatment. Clinicians should be trained to use mobile health tools for monitoring adherence, leveraging digital health innovations to support behavior change in resource-limited settings.

From a public health perspective, scalable, cost-effective interventions are needed where AGT prevalence is rising and healthcare resources are limited [47], [48]. Device-measured physical activity programs may be feasible given increasing smartphone penetration, potentially offering motivation, real-time feedback, and reduced self-report bias [49], [50], [51]. Economically, physical activity interventions can reduce hospitalizations and long-term treatment costs, mitigating the AGT burden and promoting equity in care [52].

Limitations

The 12-week duration, although sufficient to observe changes in HbA1c and metabolic markers, may not capture long-term adherence. Longer studies are required to evaluate sustainability. Several outcomes showed wide confidence intervals, reflecting variability in individual responses and the relatively smaller sample size, which may limit the generalizability of estimates. Conducting this study in a single metropolis limits generalizability and device variability or inconsistent wear time may have affected physical activity measurements. Furthermore, because device-based physical activity monitoring was implemented only in the intervention group while the control group relied on self-reported measures, the observed between-group differences may partly be attributable to this asymmetry. Although smartphone ownership is relatively high in Ghana, requiring smartphone ownership may have introduced selection bias and limited generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the inclusion of individuals across the AGT spectrum may have introduced some heterogeneity in responses. Finally, given the exploratory nature of the study and the number of primary and secondary outcomes examined, some findings may reflect chance variation and should be interpreted with caution. These results provide preliminary effect estimates and identify promising outcomes to prioritise in a fully powered confirmatory study.

Directions for future research

This study provides the foundation for larger, multi-site RCTs with extended follow-up to confirm efficacy and sustainability. Future studies should evaluate dose-response relationships, cost-effectiveness of technology-enhanced interventions, and mechanistic research pathways linking physical activity with protein metabolism and liver and kidney function in typical African populations.

Conclusion

This pilot RCT suggests that a structured, device-measured physical activity intervention has the potential to produce clinically meaningful improvements in cardiometabolic health among Ghanaian adults with AGT. The intervention was associated with reductions in HbA1c and changes in BMI, weight, BMR, total body water, while Group × Time interactions suggested possible differential effects on BMI, ALP, and total protein.

The findings offer preliminary evidence that device-measured physical activity interventions are scalable, feasible and culturally relevant in low-resource settings. If confirmed in larger trials, such interventions could potentially supplement or even delay pharmacological treatment, while leveraging growing digital infrastructure in Sub-Saharan Africa to reduce measurement bias and improve patient engagement. Larger, longer-term studies are needed to confirm effectiveness, assess sustainability, and optimize delivery while ensuring equitable access.
