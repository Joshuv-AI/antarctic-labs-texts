Quercetin ameliorates oxidative stress in BMP15-deficient oocytes by regulating the ERK1/2-GSK3β-CypD pathway
Authors: Yafei Jiao, Xinran Li, Jinming Guo, Chang Bei, Xintong Jiang, Houwu Jiang, Bingqian Mi, Tiantuan Jiang, Xiaohong Liu, Yaosheng Chen, Peiqing Cong, Zuyong He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone morphogenetic protein 15 (BMP15) is an oocyte-secreted growth factor, which interacts with ovarian follicular somatic cells and in turn promotes oocyte maturation. Disrupting BMP15 by CRISPR-ctRNP has been found to severely impair in vitro maturation (IVM) of porcine oocytes, accompanied with mitochondrial dysfunction and increased accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). To investigate whether the plant-derived antioxidant quercetin (QUE) is able to rescue the IVM of BMP15-deficient oocytes, porcine oocytes microinjected with CRISPR-ctRNP targeting BMP15 were treated with 10 μM QUE, and we found that QUE can effectively rescue the impaired IVM of BMP15-deficient oocytes by restoring the impaired mitochondrial functions and reducing ROS through activating extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) pathway. Activated ERK1/2 in turn inactivated glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK3β), which subsequently reduced Cyclophilin D (CypD) levels, and probably modulated the status of the permeability transition pore (PTP) of mitochondria, contributing to the reduced oxidative stress and apoptosis in porcine oocytes, and thus the improved oocyte quality and IVM. Our study further revealed the molecular mechanisms of QUE on alleviating oxidative stress of BMP15-deficient oocytes, suggesting that QUE may be a promising candidate for improving quality of oocytes with BMP15 mutations.Keywords: Quercetin, BMP15-Deficiency, Mitochondrial function, ROS, ERK1/2 signaling pathway

Introduction

Bone morphogenetic protein 15 (BMP15) is a key oocyte-secreted member of the transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) superfamily, which is closely associated with female fertility in several mammals, including humans [1]. BMP15 exists as a BMP15:BMP15 homodimer or cumulin heterodimer BMP15: growth differentiation factor 9 (GDF9). These homodimers or heterodimers bind to the unique BMP receptor type 2 (BMPR2)-anaplastic lymphoma kinase 4/5/7 (ALK4/5/7)- anaplastic lymphoma kinase 6 (ALK6) receptor complex to induce divergent suppressor of mothers against decapentaplegic (SMAD) pathways in granulosa cells, thereby regulating their proliferation and differentiation, which in turn promotes oocyte maturation through gap junction intercellular communications (GJICs) [2]. BMP15 deficiency has been shown to significantly affect oocyte maturation [3]. In the ovulated oocytes from Bmp15 knockout mice, significantly fewer progressed to MII, and the resumption of meiosis was significantly delayed [4]. Our previous study has demonstrated that disrupting BMP15 increased the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and impaired the in vitro maturation (IVM) of porcine oocytes, which may be associated with attenuated SMAD1/5 and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) signaling in cumulus granulosa cells (CGCs) and inhibited MOS/ERK1/2 signaling in oocytes [3]. Activation of ERK1/2 has been proved to improve mitochondrial functions through inactivating GSK3β activity to inhibit CypD phosphorylation, favoring mitochondrial membrane permeability transition pore (PTP) kept in a closed conformation, preventing the release of pro-apoptotic substances and cell death [5]. Functional mitochondria thus can counteract ROS production in oocyte, and improve oocyte quality [6,7]. Therefore, we are curious whether the rescue of ERK1/2 signaling by a specific antioxidant could restore the IVM of BMP15-deficient porcine oocytes, through improving mitochondrial functions and reducing oxidative stress.

Quercetin (QUE; 3,3,4,5,7-pentahydroxyflavone) is a potent antioxidant and ROS scavenger that is derived from various fruits and vegetables [[8], [9], [10]]. QUE modulates pathways associated with various mitochondrial functions, including mitochondrial membrane potential, intramitochondrial redox status, and mitochondria-induced apoptosis [10]. An increasing number of studies have shown that QUE can improve the IVM of oocytes of various mammalian species by modulating mitochondrial function and ROS-scavenging capability [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. Furthermore, one study has revealed that QUE protects pancreatic β-cells against oxidative damage induced by hydrogen peroxide by activating the ERK1/2 pathway [16].

Based on the ability of QUE to protect cells from ROS damage via the ERK1/2 pathway, we investigated whether QUE could restore IVM of BMP15-deficient porcine oocytes through rescuing impaired mitochondrial functions. BMP15 was efficiently disrupted in porcine oocytes by cytoplasmic microinjection of CRISPR-ctRNP. The treatment of BMP15-deficient oocytes with QUE was found to effectively reduce the ROS by modulating mitochondrial functions through regulating the ERK1/2-GSK3β-CypD pathway, leading to improved IVM, implicating the potential use of QUE on treating oocytes with BMP15 mutations.

Material and methods

Chemicals

Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH, F2293) and luteinizing hormone (LH, L5269) were purchased from SanSheng Biological Technology (Ningbo, China), and 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, C1002) was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). All other chemicals and reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, United States) unless otherwise indicated.

Porcine oocyte collection and in vitro culture

Sow ovaries were collected from the Guangzhou local slaughterhouse. The ovary tissues, such as non-related membranes, were removed, rinsed with saline, and placed in a saline bath pot maintained at 38 °C. Cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) were aspirated from 3 to 6 mm of the follicles using an 18-gauge needle and syringe. COCs with at least three layers of CGCs were collected, washed three times with Tyrode's lactate-HEPES-polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) medium, and cultured in TCM-199 cell culture medium (with Earle's salts; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) under mineral oil for 42–44 h in a 38.5 °C incubator containing 5 % (v/v) CO2 and 95 % air. The culture medium was supplemented with 3.05 mM d-glucose, 0.91 mM sodium pyruvate, 0.57 mM cysteine, 0.1 % bovine serum albumin (BSA), 10 % porcine follicle fluid, 26.19 mM NaHCO3, 10 ng/mL EGF, 0.5 μg/mL FSH, 0.5 μg/mL LH, 75 μg/mL penicillin G, and 50 μg/mL streptomycin sulfate. After 24–26 h of oocyte culture, the oocytes were transferred to a culture medium without hormones (FSH and LH). Oocytes with the first polar body (PB1) were considered mature. Germinal vesicle (GV)breakdown (GVBD), metaphase I (MI), and metaphase II (MII) stage oocytes were collected after maturation in vitro for 24, 26, and 44 h. QUE (Q4951, Sigma, USA) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and diluted to a final concentration of 10 μM in TCM-199 cell culture mature medium to culture oocytes from the GV stage to the MII stage. The final concentration of DMSO was less than 0.05 %.

Preparation of the ctRNP complex

Oligos of crRNA and tracrRNA (Table S1) were used to assemble ctRNPs, as previously described [3,17]. Briefly, a mixture of 5 μL of 400 ng/μL tracrRNA stock solution and 6 μL of 100 ng/μL crRNA stock solution was annealed in a thermocycler using the following program: heating at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by a 5 °C reduction in temperature per minute until reaching 25 °C. Then, 4 μL of the crRNA–tracrRNA complex was mixed with 1.5 μL of 1 μg/μL Alt-R™ S. p. Cas9 nuclease (IDT, USA), and the mixture was incubated at 22 °C for 5–10 min to allow for the formation of the ctRNP complex for oocyte microinjection (Table S2).

Cytoplasmic microinjection

Approximately 50 porcine COCs in the GV stage from each group were placed in a 10-μL drop of manipulation medium (TCM-199 containing 0.75 mg/mL HEPES, 0.595 mM NaHCO3, 60 mg/mL streptomycin, 50 mg/mL penicillin G, 30 mM NaCl, and 3.0 mg/mL BSA) and covered with mineral oil. Cytoplasmic microinjection was performed using an Eppendorf microoperation system (FemtoJet 4i; Calibre Scientific, Hamburg, Germany). Approximately 50 pL of ctRNPs were injected into the cytoplasm of each oocyte using a previously described method [18]. The injected COCs were then cultured for 42–44 h in a 38.5 °C incubator under 5 % CO2/95 % air. In the rescue experiment, 10 μM QUE was added to the TCM-199 culture medium of oocytes in the ctRNP injection group from the GV to MII stages [12,16]. For investigating the regulatory role of QUE on porcine oocyte maturation by activating ERK1/2 signaling, the cultured COCs were divided into the following groups: DMSO group, COCs supplemented with 0.04 % (v/v) DMSO; U0126 group, COCs supplemented with 50 μM of the ERK1/2 inhibitor U0126 (Selleck, USA) [19]; TBHQ group, COCs supplemented with 20 μM of ERK1/2 activator tert-butylhydroquinone (TBHQ) (MCE, USA) [20]; ctRNP injection group, COCs injected with CRISPR ctRNP with a pair of crRNAs targeting porcine BMP15; ctRNP injection + TBHQ group, BMP15-edited COCs supplemented with 20 μM TBHQ; ctRNP injection + QUE + U0126 group, BMP15-edited COCs supplemented with 10 μM QUE and 50 μM U0126.

Evaluation of COC expansion

After COCs were cultured in vitro for 24 and 44 h, COC expansion was assessed as previously described [21]. COC expansion was characterized using three grades. Grade 1: No significant morphological changes were observed compared with freshly recovered COCs; grade 2: COCs expanded slightly, but cell-to-cell contact was still relatively tight, with clustered cells still observed; grade 3: COCs expanded extensively, and CGCs spread homogeneously. Three replicates per treatment group and approximately 40 COCs were replicated simultaneously. Representative images of the different expansion grades are shown in Fig. S1. Isolation of porcine oocytes and CGCs

COCs were cultured in vitro for 24 h (GVBD), 26 h (MI), and 44 h (MII) to allow oocyte maturation and then digested with 0.1 % hyaluronidase at 37 °C. CGCs were separated from porcine oocytes by passing the COCs through a pipette several times in a PBS solution containing 0.01 % polyvinyl alcohol (PBS-PVA). After removing all denuded oocytes from the dish, the remaining CGCs were transferred to another 1.5-mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 13,000×g. The resulting pellets were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen or temporarily stored at −80 °C in a freezer prior to RNA isolation or protein preparation. Approximately 50 COCs were used for CGC and oocyte collection from each group in each independent experiment.

Polymerase chain reaction amplification in single oocytes

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of genomic DNA from a single oocyte was performed as previously described [22]. Briefly, after oocyte IVM for 42–44 h, the oocytes were collected, washed thrice with 0.01 % hyaluronidase in PBS, and individually transferred into separate 200-μL tubes. Then, 2.5 μL of a genomic DNA extraction solution (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 0.5 % Triton X-100, and 1 mg/mL proteinase K) was added, and the samples were incubated first at 65 °C for 3 h and then at 95 °C for 10 min. The samples were amplified using nested PCR, and the PCR products were subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis and Sanger sequencing. The primers used for PCR were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China; Supplementary Table S3).

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR

The oocytes and CGCs were collected from the NC injection, ctRNP injection, and ctRNP injection supplementation of QUE groups, washed three times with PBS-PVA, and transferred into EP-tubes with 8 μL of cells-to-cDNA™ II Kit (AM1722, Invitrogen, CA, USA). RNA was isolated and reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase Kit (11750150; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the 20 μL of the mixture of reverse transcription system contained the following: 8 μL of cells-to-cDNA™ II Kit, which contained eight oocytes, 1 μL of DNase, 0.2 μL of DNase I buffer, 1 μL of EDTA, 1 μL of dNTP, 2 μL of random primer, 4 μL of 5 × First-Stand Buffer, 0.5 μL of RNase inhibitor, 2 μL of DTT, and 0.3 μL of FS RT (reverse transcriptase). Before qPCR amplification, all mRNA primers (Table S4) were designed using Oligo 7.0. Primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Gene transcripts were quantified using 2 × Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and fluorescence data were acquired using a fluorescence ratio PCR instrument (Applied Biosystems, QuantStudio 7 Flex, Singapore). In each run, 1 μL of cDNA was used as the template for the amplification reaction. The sample was added into 9 μL of the reaction mixture, containing 2 μL of H2O, 5 μL of 2 × Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and 2 μL of 0.5 mM aliquots of both forward and reverse primers (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The thermal cycling profile started at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 10 s at 95 °C and 30 s at 60 °C, 15 s at the primer-specific annealing temperature (95 °C), 60 s at 60 °C, and final step during which fluorescence was acquired. Finally, a melt curve was generated, followed by 15 s at 95 °C, 60 s at 60 °C, and 15 s at 95 °C with fluorescence acquisition after each step. Each sample was repeated three times, and the relative gene expression was calculated with the 2−△△Ct method with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase as the reference gene.

Immunofluorescent staining

Oocytes at the GVBD, MI, and MII stages were collected with or without the removal of surface CGCs. Next, the oocytes and COCs were washed thrice with PBS-PVA. Oocytes were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight, then oocytes and COCs were washed thrice with blocking fluid that included 3 % BSA and 7.5 % glycine in 50 mL of PBS. Oocytes were then permeabilized with 1 % Triton X-100 in PBS at 25 °C for 30 min and blocked with 1 % BSA for 1.5 h at 25 °C. Then, oocytes were washed with 1 % Triton X-100 and 3 % BSA in PBS thrice, and the oocytes and COCs were incubated with a primary antibody at a suitable dilution (Table S5) overnight at 4 °C. Oocytes and COCs were then washed three times in PBS-PVA and incubated with a secondary antibody at a dilution of 1:200 for 1.5 h at 25 °C (Table S5). Oocytes and COCs were stained with 10 ng/mL DAPI for 15 min. Oocytes and COCs were washed three times with PBS-PVA. Finally, images were captured using a LAS-Leica TCS-SP5 microscope (Leica, Germany) with the same scanning settings for all groups, and the fluorescence intensity of oocytes and COCs was calculated using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Western blotting

Oocytes, CGCs, and COCs were collected from the NC, ctRNP, and ctRNP injections supplemented with QUE groups, respectively. They were washed thrice with PBS-PVA and lysed with 120 μL of RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) in Eppendorf tubes. Then, 30 μL of 5 × loading buffer was mixed with each lysed sample. Next, the samples were boiled at 100 °C for 10 min in a metal bath and subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Proteins in the gel were subsequently transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes via Semi-Dry Transfer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The membranes were blocked for 1 h with 4 % BSA. Then, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (Table S5) at a suitable dilution (Table S5) overnight at 4 °C and incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (Table S5) at a dilution of 1:5000 for 1 h. The membrane was washed and covered with 1:1 enhancer solution (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) for 3 min. Membranes were examined using an imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Finally, the band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The relative abundance of proteins in the three groups was compared using glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase as a reference protein.

Transzonal projections (TZPs) assay

The TZPs were visualized using Actin-Tracker Green (C2201S; Beyotime, Shanghai, China), a specialized fluorescent F-actin indicator. After culturing oocytes in vitro for 42–44 h, the COCs were washed thrice, fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and washed three times with PBS. The COCs were loaded with Actin-Tracker (1:100, diluted in immunofluorescence staining secondary antibody dilution buffer) at 38 °C for 30 min and then washed thrice with PBS-PVA. The COCs were then incubated with DAPI for 15 min in the dark. After washing thrice, the COCs were placed on glass slides and mounted with a neutral resin. COCs were observed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM880; Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

ROS level assay

The dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe (S0033S, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was diluted with a serum-free medium at 1:1000 to achieve a final concentration of 10 μM. Porcine oocytes were collected, and the surface CGCs were removed. Oocytes were washed three times with PBS-PVA to remove the serum. Then, 20–30 oocytes were placed in 10 μM DCFH-DA fluorescent probe for 30 min at 38.5 °C. The oocytes were washed thrice with PBS-PVA. Images were captured using a microscope (Eclipse Ti2-E; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with the same scanning settings for all the groups. Oocyte fluorescence intensity was calculated using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) content detection

An ATP assay kit (S0026, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was used to detect ATP content according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, an appropriate amount of ATP detection working solution was prepared based on the ratio of 100 μL of ATP detection working solution for each sample and standard. The reagents were then dissolved in an ice bath. An appropriate amount of the ATP detection reagent was diluted using an ATP detection reagent diluent at a ratio of 1:100. The diluted ATP detection reagent was used as the working liquid for ATP detection in the subsequent experiments. The COCs were removed from the CGCs and washed thrice with PBS-PVA. Thirty oocytes were placed together to crack for 15 min, and the ATP content in 20 μL of the lysate sample was measured using a multifunctional Enzyme Labeling Instrument (Synergy H1; BioTek, Vermont, USA).

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) detection

The MMP was measured using an MMP assay kit (C2006, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. An appropriate amount of 5,5′,6,6′-tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′-tetraethylbenzimi- dazolylcarbocyanine iodide (JC-1; 200 × ) was diluted by adding 8 mL of ultrapure water per 50 μL of JC-1 (200 × ) and mixed vigorously using a vortex mixer. Next, 2 mL of JC-1 staining buffer (5 × ) was added and mixed to obtain the JC-1 staining working solution. COCs were removed from the CGCs and washed thrice with PBS-PVA, and oocytes were collected from one sample (three samples per group) for MMP measurement. Images were captured using a microscope (Eclipse Ti2-E; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with the same scanning settings for all the groups. Oocyte fluorescence intensity was calculated using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Staining of golgi apparatus

In-vitro-matured oocytes that had been stripped of CGCs were washed three times in PBS-PVA and then incubated with a preheated working solution of Golgi-Tracker Red (C1043; Beyotime Biotechnology) for 30 min at 37 °C. The oocytes were washed again with PBS-PVA and observed under a confocal fluorescence microscope (LSM880; Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The fluorescence intensity of the oocytes was determined using ImageJ software.

Annexin V staining

To detect early oocyte apoptosis, an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (A211; Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 400 μL of 1 × Binding Buffer were mixed lightly. The COCs were washed three times with PBS-PVA to remove the cumulus cells. The oocytes were then placed in Annexin V-FITC working solution at room temperature, and oocyte apoptosis was detected within 1 h. Images were captured using a microscope (Eclipse Ti2-E; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with the same scanning settings for all the groups. Oocyte fluorescence intensity was calculated using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Glutathione (GSH) measurement

A GSH detection kit (Invitrogen, USA) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions to evaluate GSH content in porcine oocytes. Briefly, denuded oocytes from different treatment groups were incubated with 10 ng/mL CellTracker Blue 4-chloromethyl-6.8-difluoro-7-hydroxycoumarin for 30 min, protected from light, and then washed three times with 0.1 % PVA-PBS. The fluorescent signal was observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the fluorescence intensity was analyzed using the ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Measurement of E2 using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay

The maturation medium for each group of COCs cultured in vitro for 42–44 h was collected, 55 μL was aliquoted into separate 1.5-mL tubes, and stored at −20 °C temporarily. The E2 concentration in the samples was determined using a commercial kit (RXJ99872; Ruixin Biotechnology, Quanzhou, China). First, 50 μL of culture medium and horseradish peroxidase-labeled E2 were placed on the E2 monoclonal antibody-coated microplate to form an E2 monoclonal antibody-E2 (HRP) complex. The amount of HRP-labeled estradiol bound was inversely proportional to the E2 content in the culture medium. After color was produced in the mixed solution, the absorbance value (OD) was measured using ELISA (ELISA Calc) software. Four-parameter logistic curve fitting (4-pL) was used to create a standard curve equation. The absorbance (OD) of the sample was measured at 450 nm, and the concentration of the sample was calculated.

Molecular docking

The three-dimensional structure of QUE (PubChem CID: 5280343) was downloaded from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Protein structures of Sus scrofa mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)1 (ERK2; UniProt E3UV40) and MAPK3 (ERK1; UniProt AOA287BLM6) were downloaded from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database [23,24]. The “.pdb” files of protein structures were removed of water molecules, non-protein structures, and lone election pairs and preserved only standard residues; after adding all hydrogens, they were changed into “. Pdbqt” files using AutoDock Vina. The protonation state of QUE (“. Sdf” file) was corrected at pH 7.4 and changed into “. Mole 2” using AutoDock Vina [25,26]. Blind docking of ERK1 or ERK2 with QUE was performed using AutoDock Vina and visualized using PyMOL (https://pymol.org).

Cellular thermal shift assay

Cellular thermal shift assay was performed as previously described [27]. Briefly, porcine kidney (PK15) and human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293) cells were pretreated with DMSO or QUE for 48 h. Then, the cells were collected. The cell pellets were washed twice with PBS, resuspended in PBS containing a protease inhibitor, and aliquoted into 10 PCR tubes. Next, the tubes were heated at specified temperatures (37, 40, 43, 46, 49, 52, 55, 58, 61, and 64 °C) for 3 min on a thermal cycler (Thermo Fisher, USA). After completion of the heating process, the tubes were cooled to 25 °C for another 3 min. Finally, after centrifugation at 15,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were collected for further western blotting analysis.

Pull-down assay

Quercetin-Sepharose 4 B beads were prepared as described previously [28,29]. Briefly, CNBr-activated Sepharose™ 4 B beads (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Little Chalfont, USA) were activated by adding 1 mM HCl. Then, the activated Sepharose 4 B beads were mixed with the coupling buffer (0.5 M NaCl, 0.1 M NaHCO3, 35 % DMSO, pH 8.0) containing 2.5 mg QUE or DMSO at 4 °C. After overnight incubation, Tris-HCl buffer (0.1 M, pH 8.0) was added to block excess groups. The beads were finally alternately washed with high (0.1 M Tris-HCl NaCl buffer, pH 8.0) and low pH (0.1 M acetic acid buffer, pH 4.0) buffer three times.

PK15 and HEK293 cells were used for the lysate preparation. The lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 5 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.01 % NP-40, 0.02 mM PMSF, and protease inhibitor cocktail was added to cells, followed by sonication. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation and supplemented with 2 μg/mL BSA, and then incubated with QUE- or control sepharose beads overnight at 4 °C. After overnight incubation, the supernatant was discarded, and the beads were washed thrice with buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 5 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.01 % NP-40, and 0.02 mM PMSF). The beads were finally mixed with 1 × loading buffer to elute proteins bound to the beads by boiling beads at 95 °C for 5 min. The eluates were analyzed using western blotting.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed at least three times. The results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 statistical software, and t-tests were used to compare the statistical significance between the two groups. One-way analysis of variance was used to compare more than two groups, and significance was determined using the Bonferroni multiple-comparison test. Data of PB1 extrusion rates were analyzed using the chi-square test (p < 0.05). Differences with a p-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Efficient disruption of BMP15 in porcine oocytes through cytoplasmic injection of ctRNP

Porcine oocytes were isolated from fresh ovaries and cultured in vitro for cytoplasmic microinjection of CRISPR-ctRNP with scrambled crRNA (NC injection), ctRNP with a pair of crRNAs targeting porcine BMP15 (ctRNP injection) [30], or ctRNP targeting porcine BMP15 supplemented with 10 μM QUE (ctRNP injection + QUE) (Fig. 1A). Red fluorescence derived from CY3-labeled crRNA indicated that all porcine oocytes at the GV stage were successfully injected with ctRNPs (Fig. 1B and C). This finding is consistent with the results of our previous study [3]. The nested PCR analysis of porcine oocytes at the MII stage revealed that 51.85 % of the oocytes presented targeted deletions in BMP15, as reflected by the appearance of truncated PCR products (Fig. 1D). Of these oocytes, 46.29 % were monoallelically edited, and only 5.61 % were biallelically edited (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, Western blot analysis showed that microinjection of BMP15-targeting ctRNPs into porcine oocytes (ctRNP injection) markedly reduced the expression of BMP15 protein compared with that in the control group (NC injection), whereas QUE supplementation did not affect BMP15 editing efficiency (Fig. 2A and B).

QUE improved the meiotic maturation in BMP15-deficient oocytes

CGC expansion in porcine COC was assessed between the GVBD and MII stages (Fig. 2C), and we found that disrupting BMP15 reduced CGC expansion to a small extent, whereas supplementation with QUE significantly promoted CGC expansion in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. 2C and D). Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis showed that disruption of BMP15 did not significantly affect the expression levels of key regulatory genes involved in CGC expansion, although there was a slight decrease in HAS2 and PTX3, while supplementation with QUE significantly increased the expression of these regulatory genes, except for TNFAIP6 (Fig. 2E).

After the removal of CGCs from COCs cultured for 42–44 h, the nuclear maturation of porcine oocytes was distinguished by the presence of PB1 extrusion (Fig. 2F). We found the PB1 extrusion rate in the BMP15-deficient oocytes (39.06 % ± 1.39 %) was much lower than that in the control oocytes (73.73 % ± 1.90 %), whereas the supplementation with QUE significantly improved the PB1 extrusion rate in the BMP15-deficient oocytes (51.2 % ± 1.91 %) (Fig. 2F and G), which suggests that the QUE could rescue the nuclear maturation of BMP15-deficient oocytes.

QUE improved the expression of maturation-promoting factor in BMP15-deficient oocytes

Since QUE improved the maturation of BMP15-deficient porcine oocytes, the key factors involved in the oocyte maturation process were further analyzed. RT-qPCR analysis showed that mRNA expression levels of key regulatory factors of oocyte maturation, such as MOS, CyclinB1, and GDF9 (except for CDK1), were markedly lower in BMP15-deficient oocytes than in control oocytes, whereas supplementation with QUE significantly increased the RNA expression levels of all these factors in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. 3A). Further Western blot analysis showed that protein expression levels of the maturation-promoting factor (MPF) components CyclinB1 and CDK1 were notably lower in the BMP15-deficient oocytes than in control oocytes, while QUE supplementation significantly improved the protein expression levels of these two components of MPF (Fig. 3B and C). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed that QUE increased the protein expression levels of the MPF components CyclinB1 and CDK1 (Fig. 3D, E, and G). Moreover, we observed a significant increase in Y15 phosphorylated CDK1 level, which led to inactivation of MPF in BMP15-deficient oocytes [31], whereas supplementation with QUE significantly decreased the level of Y15 phosphorylated CDK1 (Fig. 3F and G), suggesting that QUE could rescue the inactive MPF in BMP15-deficient oocytes and improved the nuclear maturation.

QUE partially rescued aberrant spindle assembly in BMP15-deficient oocytes

Meiotic failure in oocytes has often been shown to be associated with defective spindle assembly [32,33]. We examined spindle formation in oocytes during the GVBD and MI stages. Approximately 24 and 26 h after culture, oocytes at GVBD and MI stages were immunostained with anti-α-tubulin antibody to label the microtubules in the spindle and counterstained with DAPI to visualize the chromosome alignment. We found typical chromatin condensation, nuclear membrane breakdown, and incorporation of microtubules in control oocytes, whereas there was an increased appearance of diffused chromatin within the intact nuclear membrane, accompanied by the aggregation of microtubules around the nuclear membrane, indicating aberrant GVBD in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. 4A and C). QUE supplementation significantly reduced the occurrence of aberrant GVBD in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. 4A–C). At this stage, disrupting BMP15 did not appear to affect the microtubule assembly, as reflected by no significant differences in the fluorescence intensity of α-tubulin in both BMP15-deficient and control oocytes (Fig. 4A and B). At the MI stage, most control oocytes presented a typical barrel-shaped spindle with well-aligned chromosomes on the equatorial plate, whereas BMP15-deficient oocytes presented various morphologically aberrant spindles with misaligned chromosomes (Fig. 4D–F). QUE supplementation significantly reduced aberrant spindle assembly in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. 4D–F). Additionally, we found that disrupting BMP15 significantly impaired the microtubule assembly as reflected by the significantly weaker fluorescence intensity of α-tubulin in BMP15-deficient oocytes than that in control oocytes, while QUE supplementation attenuated the impaired microtubule assembly in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. 4D and E). These results show that QUE restored the dynamic stability of spindle organization in BMP15-deficient oocytes and improved the nuclear maturation.

QUE improved mitochondrial functions and reduced oxidative stress in BMP15-deficient oocytes

The functional integrity of the mitochondria is critical for oocyte meiosis and is considered as a key indicator of oocyte cytoplasmic maturation [34,35]. Therefore, we measured the MMP (ΔΨm) by using JC-1 fluorescence staining (red/green). We found a notable decrease in ΔΨm, accompanied by a marked reduction in ATP content in BMP15-deficient oocytes relative to the control oocytes (Fig. 5A, B, C), indicating impaired mitochondrial functions in BMP15-deficient oocytes. Supplementation with QUE effectively restored the mitochondrial functions to a certain extent in BMP15-deficient oocytes, as reflected by increased ΔΨm and ATP content (Fig. 5A, B, C). Impaired mitochondrial function could lead to the accumulation of ROS, inducing oxidative stress in oocytes and adversely affecting oocyte quality [36]. Therefore, we measured ROS levels in oocytes using DCFH-DA fluorescent probe and found that ROS levels were markedly higher in BMP15-deficient oocytes than in control oocytes, whereas supplementation with QUE effectively reduced ROS levels to a certain extent in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. 5D and E). Moreover, RT-qPCR analysis of the expression levels of anti-oxidative stress response genes, including CAT, SOD2, NFE2L2, and HMOX1, further supported that QUE reduced the ROS levels in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. 5F). The Golgi apparatus is a key indicator of oocyte cytoplasmic maturation [37]. Therefore, we analyzed the Golgi apparatus content in oocytes using Golgi-Tracker Red staining and found that the Golgi apparatus content was substantially lower in BMP15-deficient oocytes than that in control oocytes, whereas QUE supplementation partially restored the Golgi apparatus content in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. S2A and B).

QUE reduced apoptosis in BMP15-deficient oocytes

Elevated oxidative stress can induce apoptosis in various ways [17]. Therefore, we further examined oocyte apoptosis using Annexin V staining and found that the percentage of early apoptosis in BMP15-deficient oocytes was much higher than that in control oocytes, whereas QUE supplementation effectively reduced the percentage of early apoptosis in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. 6A and B). Furthermore, western blotting results showed that the protein expression levels of the pro-apoptotic factors BAX and Cleaved Caspase3 increased significantly in BMP15-deficient oocytes compared with those in control oocytes, while supplementation with QUE decreased the levels of these two factors in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. 6C and D). Additionally, we detected decreased expression of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 in BMP15-deficient oocytes relative to control oocytes and increased expression of BCL2 in BMP15-deficient oocytes after supplementation with QUE (Fig. 6C and D). Immunofluorescence staining for Caspase3 further confirmed that QUE reduced early apoptosis in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. 6E and F). Oxidative stress can induce DNA damage and trigger cell apoptosis [38,39]. Through the immunofluorescent staining of the DNA damage marker γH2A.X, we found a significantly increased signal of DNA damage in BMP15-deficient oocytes in comparison with the control oocytes, while QUE supplementation effectively decreased the level of DNA damage in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. 6G and H). Taken together, these results demonstrated that QUE can protect BMP15-deficient oocytes from apoptosis caused by oxidative stress.

QUE attenuated the impaired communications between oocyte and CGC in BMP15-deficient oocytes

Gap junction intercellular communications (GJICs) play an important role in oocyte maturation, and TZPs mediate GJICs between cumulus cells and oocytes [40,41]. Using F-actin immunostaining, we observed abundant intact TZPs with fine filaments connecting the inner layer of the cumulus cell to the oocyte in control COCs and a substantially reduced number of intact TZPs in BMP15-deficient oocytes (Fig. 7A and B), indicating impaired GJICs in BMP15-deficient COCs. QUE supplementation increased the number of intact TZPs in BMP15-deficient COCs (Fig. 7A and B). Additionally, the statistical analysis of the length of TZPs revealed that the number of TZPs with a length over 10 μm was much lower in BMP15-deficient COCs than in control oocytes, while QUE supplementation significantly increased the number of TZPs with a length over 10 μm in BMP15-deficient COCs (Fig. 7A–C). Gap junctions are comprised of connexin proteins, including connexin 43 (CX43). Increased CX43 phosphorylation at S368 has been found to inhibit GJICs in mammalian COCs [42,43]. Immunofluorescent staining of phosphorylated CX43 at S368 revealed significantly reduced GJICs in BMP15-deficient COCs compared with control COCs, whereas supplementation with QUE effectively increased GJICs in BMP15-deficient COCs (Fig. 7D and E). Additionally, western blotting confirmed the reduction of S368 phosphorylated CX43 in BMP15-deficient COCs compared with that in control COCs and increased levels of phosphorylated CX43 in BMP15-deficient COCs after QUE supplementation, whereas the level of total CX43 protein was not significantly different among the groups (Fig. 7F and G). Taken together, these results indicate that QUE attenuates impaired GJICs in BMP15-deficient oocytes and CGCs, which would favor the development of both oocyte and granulosa cells.

QUE rescued the maturation of BMP15-deficient oocytes by reducing oxidative stress through activating the ERK1/2 signaling pathway

Our previous study has shown that disrupting BMP15 in porcine oocytes may attenuate SMAD1/5 and EGFR-ERK1/2 signaling in CGCs and inhibit MOS/ERK1/2 signaling in oocytes [3]. Therefore, whether the ability of QUE to rescue the IVM of BMP15-deficient oocytes is associated with the regulation of the aforementioned signaling pathways needs to be investigated. Through molecular docking, we found that QUE predominantly interacts with ERK1 (MAPK3) through hydrogen bonding interactions with the polar amino acids ASP (166), GLN (104), ASP (105), and MET (107), resulting in a binding energy of −8 kcal/mol at the low-energy conformations (Fig. 8A). Similarly, QUE can also bind to ERK2 (MAPK1) by forming hydrogen bonds with the residues ARG (147), THR (180), THR (184), and SER (201) with its hydrogen atoms, resulting in a binding energy of −8.4 kcal/mol at the low-energy conformations (Fig. 8B). To confirm the predicted interaction of QUE and ERK1/2 proteins, we conducted the cellular thermal shift assay and found that incubating the lysate of PK15 or HEK293 cells with QUE under a temperature increasing from 37 °C to 64 °C, showing enhanced stability of both ERK1 and ERK2 proteins by QUE during 43 °C–58 °C (Fig. 8C), which indicates that ERK1 and ERK2 proteins are likely the targets of QUE. To confirm the direct interaction between QUE and ERK1/2, pull-down assays were conducted on both PK15 and HEK293 cells. The results clearly showed that both ERK1 and ERK2 could be pulled down by QUE-Sepharose 4 B beads, and increasing the concentration of free QUE could effectively compete with ERK1/2 proteins for binding to Quercetin-Sepharose 4 B beads (Fig. 8D). These results indicate that QUE directly binds to both ERK1 and ERK2.

To further confirm the regulatory role of QUE in the maturation of BMP15-deficient oocytes, ERK1/2 signaling in differentially treated porcine COCs was inhibited or activated by supplementation with an inhibitor or activator. QUE rescued mitochondrial functions of BMP15-deficient oocytes through ERK1/2 -GSK3β-CypD pathway

Functional mitochondria are essential for counteracting ROS production in oocyte to improve oocyte quality [46]. The permeability transition pore (PTP) is an unselective voltage-dependent mitochondrial channel regulated by CypD, and long-lasting openings of PTP could lead to various modes of mitochondrial dysfunctions [47]. It has been proved that mitochondrial GSK3β can be inactivated by ERK-dependent phosphorylation to inhibit CypD phosphorylation to keep PTP at a closed conformation [5]. Therefore, immunofluorescent staining of GSK3β and CypD was carried out in porcine oocytes with different treatment to confirm whether QUE was able to rescue mitochondrial functions of BMP15-deficient oocytes through activating ERK1/2 signaling. We found inactivating ERK1/2 signaling by U0126 increased the level of GSK3β while decreased the level of GSK3β phosphorylated at serine 9 (p-GSK3β), and increased the level of CypD in porcine oocytes (Fig. 10), which probably did not favor a closed conformation of PTP, leading to impaired mitochondrial functions, as evidenced by the reduced membrane potential (ΔΨm) (Fig. 9C and D), and elevated ROS production (Fig. 9E and F). Activating ERK1/2 signaling by TBHQ decreased the level of GSK3β while markedly increased the level of p-GSK3β, and decreased the expression of CypD in porcine oocytes (Fig. 10), which probably favor a closed conformation of PTP, leading to improved mitochondrial functions, as evidenced by enhanced membrane potential (ΔΨm) (Fig. 9C and D), and reduced ROS production (Fig. 9E and F). These results indicate activated ERK1/2 signaling is capable to regulate GSK3β and CypD to enhance mitochondrial functions in porcine oocytes by favoring a closed conformation of PTP. As expected, the GSK3β activity was significantly enhanced in BMP15-deficient porcine oocytes (ctRNP injection), as reflected by markedly increased GSK3β level and significantly decreased p-GSK3β, which caused significantly increased level of CypD (Fig. 10), and probably favor an open conformation of PTP, leading to impaired mitochondrial functions. Activating ERK1/2 signaling by TBHQ in BMP15-deficient porcine oocytes (ctRNP injection + TBHQ) significantly inhibited the GSK3β activity as reflected by significantly decreased GSK3β level and increased p-GSK3β, which caused significantly decreased level of CypD (Fig. 10), which probably favor a closed conformation of PTP, leading to improved mitochondrial functions. Inactivating ERK1/2 signaling by U0126 attenuated the rescue effect of QUE on impaired mitochondrial functions in BMP15-deficient oocytes (ctRNP injection + QUE + U0126), as reflected by reduced level of p-GSK3β and increased level of CypD as compared to control (Fig. 10). Taken these results together, QUE was able to rescue mitochondrial functions of BMP15-deficient oocytes through ERK1/2-GSK3β-CypD pathway.

Discussion

BMP15 is an oocyte-secreted growth factor critical for regulating ovarian follicular somatic cell growth, differentiation, and oocyte quality [3,48,49]. Mutations in BMP15 have been associated with poor-quality oocytes and impaired fertility in human [50,51]. We have previously shown that disrupting BMP15 severely impairs the quality of porcine oocytes, as evidenced by increased ROS accumulation and inhibited IVM [3], which supports the adverse impact of BMP15 mutations on oocyte quality in human. Therefore, rescuing the oocytes affected by BMP15 deficiency may help to alleviate impaired female fertility. The current study found that the antioxidant chemical compound QUE is a promising agent for rescuing porcine oocytes affected by BMP15 deficiency through modulating the redox signalings.

We found QUE can effectively restored the nuclear maturation of BMP15-deficient porcine oocytes, as reflected by reduced abnormalities in spindle assembly, increased PB1 extrusion, and upregulated MPF expression. Efficient cellular uptake of the more lipophilic flavonol QUE has been well documented [52]. Therefore, we consider that the major effects of QUE in rescuing nuclear maturation of BMP15-deficient porcine oocytes can be attributed to its specific interaction with the intracellular ERK1/2 proteins and the effective activation of ERK1/2 signaling, which was proved by CETSA assay and pull-down assay. As ERK1/2 pathway participates in the regulation of chromosomal morphology and spindle assembly [53,54], the restored activation of ERK1/2 signaling by QUE may contribute to a reduced percentage of abnormalities in spindle assembly in BMP15-deficient porcine oocytes, resulting in increased PB1 extrusion because a properly assembled spindle is required for PB1 extrusion [55,56]. QUE may also exert its biological effects through interaction with specific receptors on cell membrane [57]. Therefore, whether the effects of QUE on rescuing BMP15-deficient oocytes may also be associated with its interaction with any specific receptor of oocytes, and eliciting any signaling pathway crossed with ERK1/2 would be an interesting topic merits for further study.

Furthermore, we found the interaction of QUE with ERK1/2 proteins in CGCs around the BMP15-deficient oocytes also could contribute to the improved oocyte maturation. The inhibited ERK1/2 signaling in the CGCs of BMP15-deficient porcine COCs was mainly caused by the attenuated activation of SMAD1/5/8 signaling by BMP15 [2], owing to the deficiency of BMP15 secreted from CRISPR ctRNP-edited porcine oocytes. Attenuated SMAD1/5/8 signaling thus downregulated its target gene, EGFR, inhibiting the activation of the ERK1/2 signaling pathway [58,59]. The restored ERK1/2 signaling by QUE in CGCs can effectively restore the impaired gap junction intercellular communications (GJICs) by regulating the expression and phosphorylation of the gap junction protein CX43 and the assembly of the actin-containing transzonal projections (TZPs), which is extended from CGCs to an oocyte across the zona pellucida, responsible for the exchange of nutrients and signals critical for the regulation of oocyte maturation [41]. The restored GJICs in BMP15-deficient COCs in turn could favor the proper closure of gap junction channels between CGCs and oocytes, and block the entry of cGMP from CGCs to oocytes, thus activating cAMP-PKA signaling in oocytes, resulting in the activation of MPF and resumption of oocyte meiosis [60,61]. We thus first proved the beneficial effects of QUE on BMP15-deficient oocytes through improving GJICs by activating ERK1/2 signaling in CGCs, which may be extended to other species including human.

Most importantly, we found that QUE was able to restore the impaired mitochondrial functions and significantly reduced ROS levels in BMP15-deficient porcine oocytes. A sufficient number of functional mitochondria is crucial for oocyte maturation, as it produces sufficient ATP for continuous transcription and translation in oocytes [62,63]. Functional mitochondria counteract ROS production, and mitochondrial dysfunction can lead to increased oxidative stress and oocyte apoptosis [6,7]. ERK1/2 signaling inhibits the opening of inner mitochondrial membrane channels and prevents the release of apoptotic substances, including ROS [64]. Therefore, the restored activation of ERK1/2 signaling by QUE may contribute to the partial restoration of mitochondrial functions and reduced ROS levels in BMP15-deficient porcine oocytes, resulting in a reduced occurrence of early apoptosis. We further found the restored mitochondrial functions in BMP15-deficient porcine oocytes by QUE was related to the regulation of the conformation of the permeability transition pore (PTP) of mitochondria, which is a key effector in the mitochondrial pathways to cell death, and regulated by GSK3β and CypD [65]. The activation of ERK1/2 signaling in BMP15-deficient porcine oocytes by QUE significantly inhibited the GSK3β activity and caused significantly decreased level of CypD, probably favor a closed conformation of PTP, preventing the release of apoptotic substances, thus reducing early apoptosis of oocytes. Our study thus indicates the importance of regulating ERK1/2-GSK3β-CypD pathway by QUE for alleviating oxidative stress in oocytes caused by mitochondrial dysfunctions, suggesting QUE as a promising candidate for improving quality of oocytes with BMP15 mutations. Except for as by-products of mitochondrial activity, ROS also can be produced from NADPH oxidases (NOXs), which comprise a family of oxidoreductase enzymes that expressly catalyze the formation of ROS [66]. Moreover, QUE may present pro-oxidant characteristic to produce H2O2 through auto oxidation under certain conditions [67]. Therefore, whether QUE can interact with NOXs to alleviate oxidative stress or whether it can be auto oxidized to produce H2O2 in porcine oocytes merits for future study. This will help to full elucidate the mechanism the antioxidative functions of QUE in oocytes.

Although we suggest QUE as a promising candidate for rescuing IVM of oocytes with BMP15 mutations through redox modulation, its clinical use should be taken in caution, as QUE has shown to be able to cause genome wide epigenetic modifications through modulating the expression of DNA methyltransferase (DNMTs) and histone acetyltransferases (HATs), thus exerting an impact on epigenetic networks [68]. As epigenetic changes induced by QUE may have pleotropic effects, whether this would lead to medium or long-term adverse consequences in the offspring requires for a thorough evaluation in further studies.

Conclusions

The specific interaction of QUE with ERK1/2 proteins can effectively restore impaired ERK1/2 signaling in the CGCs of BMP15-deficient COCs, which could attenuate impaired GJICs by regulating the phosphorylation of CX43 and the assembly of TZPs. This may help to restore the proper activation of MOS and meiosis resumption in oocytes by activating MPF, resulting in improved PB1 extrusion and a reduced occurrence of abnormally assembled spindles. Most importantly, QUE could restore impaired ERK1/2-GSK3β-CypD signaling in oocytes of BMP15-deficient COCs, which could modulate the conformation of PTP, and restored mitochondrial functions, leading to reduced ROS and early apoptosis in oocytes, thus improving oocyte quality (Fig. 11). Our study thus suggests QUE as a promising candidate for rescuing IVM of oocytes with BMP15 mutations.

Summary of the regulatory role of QUE on reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) of BMP15-defficient porcine oocytes. Disrupting BMP15 by cytoplasmic microinjection of CRISRP ctRNP increased the accumulation of ROS and impaired the IVM of porcine oocytes through attenuated SMAD1/5 and EGFR-ERK1/2 signaling in the cumulus granulosa cells (CGCs) and inhibited MOS/ERK1/2 signaling in oocytes. The interaction of QUE with ERK1/2 can effectively restore the impaired ERK1/2 signaling in CGCs of BMP15-deficient COCs, which could attenuate the impaired gap junction intercellular communications (GJICs) by regulating CX43 expression and formation of transzonal projections (TZPs), which may help to restore the proper activation of MOS in oocytes through GJICs. Most importantly, QUE may restore the impaired mitochondrial functions in BMP15-deficient oocytes through activating ERK1/2 signaling, which in turn inhibited the activity of GSK3β through increasing the level of the its phosphorylated form (p-GSK3β), and inhibited the expression of CypD, and modulat the status of the permeability transition pore (PTP) of mitochondria, contributing to the reduced oxidative stress and apoptosis in porcine oocytes, and thus the improved oocyte quality and IVM.
