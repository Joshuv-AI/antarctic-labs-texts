Real-world use of rivaroxaban for primary thromboprophylaxis and cardiac thrombosis treatment in congenital and acquired heart disease: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Neil Derridj, Sophie Malekzadeh-Milani, Dominique Lasne, Sabrina Da Costa, Diala Khraiche, Hugues Ndjoli, Damien Bonnet, Fanny Bajolle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate the incidence of bleeding and thrombotic events in children with congenital or acquired heart disease (CAHD) receiving direct oral anticoagulants by rivaroxaban, and identify associated covariates. This prospective cohort study included children with CAHD treated with rivaroxaban between September 2023 and March 2025, excluding venous thromboembolism. Patients were anticoagulant-naïve or switched from antithrombotic therapy. Serious adverse events (SAE) included significant bleeding events (major bleeding (MB) and clinically relevant non-major bleeding (CRNMB)) and thrombotic events. Cox proportional hazards models were used, with sensitivity analyses excluding patients with prior events under previous antithrombotic therapy. 125 patients were included with: 88(70.4%) patients with Fontan physiology. Seventy-two (58%) patients were switched to rivaroxaban from previous antithrombotic therapy. Median age at initiation was 9.3(IQR:5.5–13.9) years with a median follow-up of 8.5(IQR:3.9–13) months. Twenty SAEs were recorded (3 MB, 13 CRNMB, 4 thrombotic events). The incidences of significant bleeding events and thrombosis event were of 13.9%(95%CI[7,9%–24,4%]) and 4.2%(95%CI[1.6%–11,2%]) per patient year, respectively. At 12 months, 88.9%(95%CI[79.8%–94%]) of patients were free of significant bleeding events. Multivariable analysis identified female gender (HR = 13.2(95%CI[2.8–62.4])) and age > 12 years (HR = 7.1(95%CI[1.9–26.3])) as risk factors for significant bleeding events. Abnormal uterine bleeding accounted for 56.2% of significant bleeding events. Sensitivity analyses yielded similar results. Rivaroxaban therapy appears feasible in children with CAHD, but was associated with higher bleeding and thrombotic events than previously reported in clinical trials. Introduction

Anticoagulants are among the leading medications implicated in serious adverse drug events, with over 21 000 reports of major harm in the U.S. alone, and are classified by WHO as high-alert drugs due to their significant contribution to morbidity and mortality1,2. Beside these warnings, managing traditional anticoagulants, especially vitamin K antagonists (VKAs), is particularly challenging in pediatric populations due to their narrow therapeutic window, variability in dose-response, and frequent drug interactions3. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) offer benefits, including fewer drug interactions, no dietary restrictions, and reduced need for routine monitoring4. Although DOACs are widely used in adults5, their use in children, especially those with congenital or acquired heart disease (CAHD), is still in its early stages. Short-term use of DOACs for conditions like deep vein thrombosis (DVT) has shown promising safety and efficacy in children6. Recent trials have further supported the potential of DOACs in pediatric CAHD. For instance, the UNIVERSE trial, comparing rivaroxaban to aspirin in children with Fontan physiology, showed comparable safety and efficacy7. The SAXOPHONE and ENNOBLE-ATE trials also demonstrated favorable outcomes for apixaban and edoxaban in children with CAHD compared to VKA therapy8,9. However, significant gaps remain in the literature due to the heterogeneity of CAHD and the diversity of clinical indications, some of which require lifelong anticoagulation. These factors raise concerns about the adoption of DOACs in CAHD populations. Long-term anticoagulation in children with CAHD exposes them to cumulative thrombosis and bleeding risks, which are insufficiently documented. Although recent randomized controlled trials report follow-up durations close to 12 months, real-world studies remain essential as they include broader clinical scenarios and patient heterogeneity not fully represented in controlled trials. Consequently, data on DOAC use for mid- to long-term anticoagulation in the broader pediatric CAHD population is lacking. In this context, we conducted a prospective real-world study focusing on rivaroxaban. This study aims to (1) describe the incidence of bleeding and thrombotic events in children with CAHD receiving mid- to long-term rivaroxaban therapy, and (2) identify covariates associated with these events.

Materials and methods

We performed a prospective cohort study of all children (< 18 years of age) with CAHD who received rivaroxaban therapy, following surgery or for medical conditions, for more than two months. All children with CAHD needing anticoagulation for thromboembolism prophylaxis or for the treatment of intracardiac thrombosis between September 2023 and March 2025 were eligible for inclusion. Patients with isolated or associated DVT or pulmonary embolism were excluded. The study was approved by the local ethics committee of our institution and is registered in the Assistance Publique-Hôpitaux de Paris (AP-HP) General Registry of Treatments (N°025 0627151343). All clinical investigational practice methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Data were prospectively collected after obtaining written informed consent from the parents. All children who received rivaroxaban therapy were included into an educational program (EP). More details on the EP are available in Appendix 1. During the study period, all consecutive children with CAHD assessed for anticoagulation were prospectively screened. In addition to the predefined exclusion of venous thromboembolism, patients with acute arterial or venous stroke, acute peripheral arterial thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, or indications requiring long-term VKA therapy (e.g., mechanical valve replacement) were not eligible for rivaroxaban therapy. Other exclusions were based on clinical context or patient preference. A detailed flow chart summarizing patient screening, inclusion, and exclusion criteria is provided in Fig. 1.

Patient management

Medical support was available 24/7 for emergencies, and unplanned contacts regarding rivaroxaban treatment were recorded. Follow-up appointments were conducted at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, with encouragement for families to report any adverse events related to rivaroxaban therapy. All patients received branded rivaroxaban (Xarelto®, Bayer). When antiplatelet therapy was used, it consisted exclusively of acetylsalicylic acid, prescribed at standard pediatric doses (3-5 mg/kg/day). Rivaroxaban was administered with normal feeding, orally or via feeding tube if required; doses were repeated only in case of vomiting within 30 min after intake. Telephone consultations were available for reporting bleeding events or suspected thrombosis. Treatment adherence was supported through the therapeutic education program and reinforced during scheduled visits and telephone follow-up. In routine practice, adherence was assessed indirectly through adverse event reporting and prescription renewal tracking.

Pediatric weight-adjusted dosing for Rivaroxaban followed the Einstein-Jr recommendations10 corresponding to the adult-equivalent dose of 20 mg once daily were delivered. For patients requiring discontinuation of anticoagulation before procedures, French guidelines11 were followed to manage anticoagulation rates, particularly in high-risk scenarios. If major bleeding occurred, prothrombin complex concentrate was used and rivaroxaban plasma levels were assessed using a chromogenic anti-Xa assay based on residual factor Xa activity, with absorbance inversely proportional to drug concentration12. Anti-Xa measurements were performed only in selected clinical situations (e.g., major bleeding), interpreted according to timing of intake (peak or trough), and were not used for systematic dose adjustment. PCC dosing followed adult guideline principles, with 25–50 IU/kg administered, expressed in factor IX units.

At rivaroxaban initiation, patients were either anticoagulant-naïve or switched from a previous antithrombotic therapy, including VKA or aspirin. Patients previously receiving VKA were systematically switched to rivaroxaban during EP sessions. Aspirin was discontinued at rivaroxaban initiation in most patients; however, a limited number of patients remained on concomitant aspirin therapy due to predefined clinical indications, mainly Kawasaki disease with giant coronary aneurysms. Patients with Fontan physiology were initiated on Rivaroxaban post-operatively after chest drain removal (heparin bridge). Children with arrhythmias at thrombotic risk received Rivaroxaban for a variable duration based on their rhythm status13. Cardiomyopathy (CM) patients were treated with Rivaroxaban when left ventricular systolic function was below 30% or if intracardiac thrombosis was diagnosed. Kawasaki patients with giant aneurysms (diameter ≥8 mm or Z-score ≥ 10) were initially treated with intravenous immunoglobulin and aspirin, followed by heparinoids and Rivaroxaban to prevent coronary thrombosis. Initial anticoagulation consisted of low-molecular-weight heparin (enoxaparin) at age-adjusted doses (150 IU/kg twice daily for 0–3 months, 120 IU/kg twice daily for 3 months–2 years, and 100 IU/kg twice daily beyond 2 years). Heparin therapy was generally administered for a short period (a few days) to assess early coronary evolution and ischemic risk, after which patients were switched to rivaroxaban once clinical and imaging stability was confirmed.

Data collection and outcomes definition

Baseline characteristics, including age, weight, height, CAHD subtype, liver and renal function, and the duration of Rivaroxaban therapy, were recorded prospectively. Significant bleeding events were classified according to the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) Subcommittee on Pediatric and Neonatal Thrombosis Guidance into major bleeding (MB) or clinically relevant non-major bleeding (CRNMB). Major bleeding was defined as a new visible bleeding or signs suggestive of bleeding confirmed by imaging, accompanied by ≥ 2 g/dL decrease in hemoglobin or requiring surgical intervention. CRNMB included any significant bleeding requiring medical intervention or hospitalization, while minor bleeding referred to overt but non-severe events14. Menstrual bleeding events were classified as MB or CRNMB when meeting the corresponding ISTH criteria; otherwise, they were considered minor bleeding or patient-important bleeding, no intervention, defined as bleeding prompting medical contact without hospitalization or therapeutic intervention. Minor bleeding and patient-important bleeding, no intervention were not considered as study outcomes.

Thrombotic events, confirmed by imaging, included both arterial and venous events and were categorized as symptomatic or asymptomatic. Imaging was performed in cases of clinical suspicion reported by families or clinicians, as well as during scheduled follow-up or when incidental findings were identified during imaging performed for other clinical indications (e.g., cardiac follow-up, post-operative assessment).

All major bleeding, CRNMB, and thrombotic events were reviewed and validated by two pediatric cardiologists and classified accordingly.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median values with interquartile ranges (25th-75th percentile). Categorical variables were presented as percentages. We used chi-square or Fisher’s tests for normative categorial covariables and Wilcoxon rank sum or Kruskal Wallis tests were used for the skewed and non-normative distributions of some co-variables as appropriate. The incidence was calculated per 100 patient-years for the study period. Time to bleeding and thrombotic events were described using Kaplan–Meier curves. Survival rate estimates were calculated with the use of the Kaplan–Meier method. A Cox proportional-hazards model was used to investigate risk factors of bleeding and thrombotic events in univariable and multivariable analyses using a backward approach. Clinically relevant factors or variables with p values of less than 0.20 were included in the multivariable models. Sensitivity analyses were performed excluding patients who had experienced clinically relevant bleeding or thrombotic events under previous antithrombotic therapy prior to rivaroxaban initiation. The proportional hazards assumption was verified by the Schoenfeld residual method. All tests were two-sided with a p value of less than 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance. All analyses were performed with the use of Stata software version 15.

Results

Among all consecutive children with CAHD assessed for anticoagulation during the study period (n = 205), 125 were included (Fig. 1), with a median treatment duration of 8.5 months (IQR: 3.9–13), corresponding to 36 873 cumulative patient-days of rivaroxaban exposure. Overall, 72 (58%) patients were switched to rivaroxaban from a previous antithrombotic therapy (VKA, n = 38; aspirin, n = 34), while 53 (42%) patients were anticoagulant-naïve at rivaroxaban initiation. Among patients switched to rivaroxaban, CRNMB under previous antithrombotic therapy were observed in three Fontan patients receiving VKA, one Fontan patient receiving aspirin, and one patient with Kawasaki disease receiving both VKA and aspirin. The cohort included 92 (73.6%) children with congenital heart diseases (CHD), 22 (17.6%) with cardiomyopathies, 6 (4.8%) with arrhythmias, and 5 (4%) with giant aneurysms complicating Kawasaki disease. The median age at rivaroxaban initiation was 9.3 years (IQR: 5.5–13.9) with a median height of 132 cm (IQR: 106–156) and a median weight of 27 kg (IQR: 17.7–48.7). Baseline laboratory values were generally within normal pediatric ranges. Baseline coagulation parameters and hepatic enzymes (AST, ALT, GGT) and bilirubin were within acceptable ranges and revealed no contraindications to rivaroxaban initiation. The vast majority of patients (91.2%, n = 114) received rivaroxaban in tablet form; syrup formulation was used in 11 patients (8.8%), allowing weight-adjusted dosing ranging from 0.8 to 3.0 mg three times daily in children under 12 kg (Table 1). Concomitant medications were common at baseline, with 68 patients (54.4%) receiving at least one additional therapy: diuretics (56.0%), heart failure treatments including ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers and sacubitril/valsartan (44.8%), anti-arrhythmic agents (11.2%), and antiplatelet therapy (5.6%) (Table 1). Rivaroxaban therapy was discontinued in 19 patients (15.2%) during follow-up: eight due to improved left ventricular function (primarily in dilated CM), three in the context of mechanical circulatory support or transplantation planning, one due to recurrent thrombosis (antiphospholipid syndrome), and two due to resolution of the initial indication (e.g., surgical mitral annulus or coronary fistula). Three patients discontinued therapy following CRNMB, and two deaths occurred from end-stage heart failure unrelated to rivaroxaban use. One case was a 5-year-old with COL7A1-related epidermolysis bullosa who died of septic shock; the other deceased patient was a 1-year-old with advanced dilated CM.

Bleeding events (MB, CRNMB and minor bleeding events)

A total of 48 bleeding events were reported in 23 (18.4%) patients, with a predominance of minor (n = 22) and patient-important bleeding, no intervention events (n = 10) (epistaxis (n = 18), 5 AUB, 5 hematomas, 3 oral bleeding and 1 conjunctival hemorrhage).

However, 16 events were classified as significant, with 3 MB and 13 CRNMB. The overall incidence of significant bleeding events was 13.9 per 100 patient-years (95% CI [7.9–24.4]). At 12 months, the cumulative probability of remaining free from a significant bleeding event was 88.9% (95% CI [79.8–94.0]). In univariate analysis, female sex was significantly associated with a higher risk of significant bleeding events (HR 9.15; 95% CI: 2.00–41.88; p = 0.004), as was age ≥ 12 years at rivaroxaban initiation (HR 6.1; 95% CI: 2.9–29.3; p = 0.001). Thrombosis at initiation showed a non-significant trend toward increased bleeding risk (HR 3.06; 95% CI: 0.67–14.08; p = 0.15). No significant associations were found between bleeding risk and underlying cardiac diagnoses (e.g., cardiomyopathy, Fontan physiology) or concomitant therapies, including diuretics, antiplatelet agents, and heart failure medications. In multivariable analysis, both female sex (HR 13.2; 95% CI: 2.8–62.4; p = 0.0001) and age ≥ 12 years at treatment initiation (HR 7.1; 95% CI: 1.9–26.3; p = 0.0003) remained independently associated with an increased risk of significant bleeding events (Table 2). Sensitivity analyses excluding switched patients with prior CRNMB (3 Fontan on VKA, 1 Fontan on aspirin, 1 Kawasaki on VKA and aspirin), yielded similar results, with no meaningful change in effect estimates. Among the 3 MB events, 2 involved severe abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB) in adolescent girls with Fontan physiology. Both cases resulted in acute anemia requiring intensive care unit admission and red blood cell transfusion; events occurred after 5 and 12 months of rivaroxaban therapy, respectively. The third MB occurred in a male adolescent with restrictive CM and arrhythmia who experienced major bleeding during an electrophysiology procedure, necessitating surgical intervention and massive transfusion. Thirteen CRNMB events were observed in 11 (8.8%) patients, including up to three separate events in one patient. Seven of these events were AUB episodes occurring in five girls aged 13 to 17 years. These events required intensive hormonal therapy and/or iron supplementation. Detailed individual clinical data are summarized in Tables 3 and 4.

Thrombotic events

Four thrombotic events occurred, yielding an incidence of 4.2% patient-years (95% CI [1.6–11.2]). At 12 months, the cumulative probability of remaining free from a thrombotic event was 94.9% (95% CI: 89.8%–97.9%). Two events involved patients with severe dilated CM: a 17-year-old adolescent with epidermolysis bullosa whose multiple left ventricular thrombi resolved after one month of rivaroxaban therapy (confirmed by MRI) but recurred 51 days after initiation, and a 4-month-old infant with severe CM who developed a left ventricular clot nine days after starting rivaroxaban. Another event occurred in a 12-year-old patient with Kawasaki disease and giant coronary aneurysms. After six years on VKA and 7.2 months on rivaroxaban, he developed a partial thrombosis of the right coronary aneurysm detected on CT angiography and confirmed by coronary angiography, but without signs of myocardial ischemia on ECG, TEE, or scintigraphy. Notably, his VKA adherence had been poor, and rivaroxaban levels measured at 14 h post-dose was within the expected trough range (33.6 ng/mL vs. expected 240 ng/mL at 3 h and 20 ng/mL at 20 h), but lower than anticipated given the dosing regimen, highlighting the challenges of assessing DOAC adherence in clinical practice. The final event involved an adolescent with tetralogy of Fallot who, following right ventricular re-valvulation, developed an intracardiac thrombus post-operatively. Further evaluation revealed antiphospholipid syndrome, explaining the recurrence despite anticoagulation. Detailed individual clinical data are summarized in Table 5.

Serious adverse events: major bleeding, CRNMB, and thrombotic events

A total of 20 SAEs occurred in 16 (12.8%) children, with an incidence of 18.4% (95% CI [8.9–22.2) per patient-year. No deaths occurred because of bleeding or thrombosis events. At 12 months, 81.6% (95% CI [69.9%–89.4%]) of patients remained free of any SAE (Fig. 2).

Discussion

We report the results of a prospective real-world cohort evaluating the safety and efficacy of rivaroxaban in children with CAHD requiring primary thromboprophylaxis or treatment for thrombosis. We reaffirmed the safety and efficacy of these treatments within this population, with no deaths attributable to rivaroxaban occurring during the study. However, we report a higher incidence of thromboses and significant bleeding events than previously reported. Most bleeding episodes occurred during menstruation, suggesting the need for careful monitoring in adolescent females.

In our study, we report an incidence of significant bleeding events of 13.9 per 100 patient-years, a rate notably higher than those observed in clinical trials such as the SAXOPHONE study9, which reported 1.8 per 100 patient-years, or the single real-world retrospective study using apixaban, published by the Boston team, which reported an incidence of 2.9 per 100 patient-years, including 219 patients15. Several hypotheses can explain this difference. Follow-up duration in our study was slightly longer, which may have contributed to the higher number of bleeding events observed; however, it remains broadly comparable to that of many pediatric DOAC trials and is therefore unlikely to be the main explanatory factor. Another important distinction is that the UNIVERSE study administered Rivaroxaban at low doses to Fontan patients, which may have reduced the incidence of bleeding events. This variability in anticoagulation intensity raises the question of the most appropriate dose. However, it is well-established that thromboembolic risk in this population is high, with thrombosis rates reaching up to 25% at 10 years, including strokes and pulmonary embolisms, which may be asymptomatic or symptomatic and associated with a 25% mortality rate16. In light of this risk-benefit balance, we opted for high-intensity anticoagulation, based on well-established dosing profiles from the Einstein Junior study17. Our study presents several characteristics that explain the differences observed compared to other real-world studies. The prospective design of our study avoids recall bias commonly observed in retrospective studies. Furthermore, excluding short-term indications such as DVT helps to reduce a certain “dilution of effect.” Another potential variable could be the difference between rivaroxaban and apixaban, as suggested by a recent study reporting a higher bleeding rate in patients taking rivaroxaban compared to those on apixaban in the context of DVT15. We also specifically identified girls as being at increased risk of significant bleeding events under rivaroxaban therapy, particularly those presenting with AUB. Among the 49 girls included in the cohort, nearly one-third developed AUB, which accounted for more than 50% of all significant bleeding events. These bleeding events can largely be attributed to dysovulation, a phenomenon frequently observed at the onset of menarche and associated with an increased risk of uterine bleeding. Although ovulatory status was not formally assessed by hormonal testing, the clinical context was consistent with dysovulation, which commonly occurs during the period of hypothalamic–ovarian axis maturation that may extend up to 2–6 years after menarche18. Dysovulation affects 40–90% of adolescents19. This issue was not highlighted in other cohorts, likely reflecting important differences in study populations and clinical contexts. Several pediatric DOAC studies included few or no pubertal girls, thereby limiting the ability to capture menstruation-related bleeding events. Conversely, trials such as EINSTEIN-Jr (Extension) included adolescent girls but largely in populations without CAHD, with different baseline hemodynamic conditions, shorter treatment duration, comorbidities, and risk profiles. These differences in age distribution, underlying cardiac disease, and clinical complexity may have influenced bleeding patterns and the reported incidence of AUB. Therefore, the incidence of AUB under DOACs is likely underreported in the literature. Beyond bleeding itself, recurrent AUB may contribute to iron deficiency and anemia, conditions that are already highly prevalent in adolescents with congenital heart disease and Fontan physiology and are associated with impaired functional capacity. In this context, menstrual bleeding under anticoagulation represents a double burden, combining hemorrhagic risk and potential worsening of baseline iron deficiency, which may partly explain the clinical severity observed in some patients. It is essential not to overlook this issue, especially since two girls in our cohort required blood transfusions due to severe bleeding. This phenomenon, already reported in adult women on anticoagulants, can potentially affect treatment adherence, which could have serious consequences, particularly for young girls with Fontan physiology, who are at high thromboembolic risk. Thus, it is crucial to implement a dedicated educational program for this population, in collaboration with pediatric gynecologists, to identify and anticipate the risk of uterine bleeding. The use of tools such as the Adolescent Menstrual Bleeding Questionnaire before initiating rivaroxaban therapy may help identify and monitor AUB while capturing its impact on quality of life, thereby facilitating early gynecological management20. Additionally, the lack of adherence monitoring tools such as the INR used with VKAs makes it more challenging to assess adherence21. Regarding treatment efficacy, while the incidence of thrombosis is higher than that reported in the literature, it remains low at 4.2% per patient-year. In comparison, a thrombotic event rate of approximately 2% was reported in the UNIVERSE trial in children with Fontan physiology treated with rivaroxaban. Importantly, this incidence should be interpreted in light of the clinical context and may not be directly comparable to rates reported in randomized trials, as all thrombotic events occurred in patients with strong, disease-specific prothrombotic conditions. These included a patient in whom antiphospholipid syndrome22 was diagnosed retrospectively, a patient with epidermolysis bullosa who experienced a recurrence of intracardiac thrombosis despite anticoagulation treatment, likely due to chronic inflammation and altered intestinal absorption23, and a patient with Kawasaki disease and giant coronary aneurysms. In this latter condition, thrombotic risk is likely driven by coronary flow abnormalities and rheological factors rather than anticoagulation intensity alone, as previously reported in patients treated with VKA24. These cases illustrate the considerable variability within CAHD and highlight the need to tailor anticoagulation treatment to each patient’s clinical specifics.

This study has some limitations and caveats that should be considered. Firstly, although prospective, the median follow-up duration of less than one year remains relatively short. Therefore, it is possible that the efficacy and safety of DOACs may evolve over the very long term, necessitating future studies to assess long-term outcomes. Additionally, while events were regularly monitored and reported according to the protocol, there is always a risk of unreported events, despite our efforts to maintain rigorous follow-up. Although events were prospectively collected and systematically reviewed by two pediatric cardiologists, the absence of a formal blinded adjudication committee represents a limitation of this real-world study. Another important point is the external validity of our study. Indeed, it was conducted in a tertiary care center, primarily focusing on the most severe CAHD cases, which may limit the generalization of the results to larger or less complex pediatric populations. Moreover, the number of patients in each subgroup remains limited, making it difficult to conduct in-depth analyses of specific pathologies, such as Kawasaki disease or cardiomyopathies, for which homogeneous data are needed to better assess baseline risk probabilities in these subgroups. We did not perform a dedicated analysis of thrombosis risk factors, as only four thrombotic events occurred during follow-up, which was insufficient to yield meaningful statistical conclusions. This question may be addressed in future analyses with a longer observation period and a larger event count. These limitations underscore the need for future larger-scale studies with homogeneous groups and longer follow-up periods to confirm our results and better evaluate the real-world impact of DOACs in this complex patient population.

Conclusion

This prospective real-world study provides valuable insights into the safety and efficacy of rivaroxaban in pediatric CAHD patients requiring mid- to long-term anticoagulation, supporting its use in this clinical context. However, we observed a notably higher incidence of significant bleeding events compared to previous reports, particularly among girls over 12. These findings highlight the importance of considering individual patient characteristics in this heterogeneous population. Specifically, girls with potential dysovulation should be closely monitored to prevent abnormal uterine bleeding.
