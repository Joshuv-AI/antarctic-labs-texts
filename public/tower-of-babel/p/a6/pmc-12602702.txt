Integrating NLP and expert validation: a framework combining subjective and objective approaches for female-oriented automotive personas on social media
Authors: Qian Bao, Qi Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study responds to the rapid expansion of the female automobile consumer market and the corresponding lack of detailed user insight by proposing a hybrid methodology that combines natural language processing (NLP) with expert workshops. The primary objective is to generate refined personas of female car users based on social media data, thereby offering actionable insights to guide automotive design and marketing strategies and to elucidate the psychological and behavioral mechanisms influencing women’s perceptions and purchasing decisions. A total of 273,657 content entries from 12,866 female users were collected across four major platforms—Weibo, Xiaohongshu, Bilibili, and Autohome. A four-stage analytical framework was employed. First, structured data were quantitatively clustered using the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic model and the Qwen-3 large language model, resulting in the identification of five representative user groups with distinct behavioral characteristics. These clusters were subsequently refined through interdisciplinary expert workshops to construct semantically coherent and narratively rich personas. Each persona integrates demographic attributes, vehicle usage contexts, and emotional preferences. To assess the reliability of the generated personas, the study introduces a six-dimensional Persona Perception Scale (PPS), which confirmed the personas’ high credibility. This research contributes to the field in three key ways: (1) by establishing a multi-platform data fusion framework to bridge the gap between raw data and narrative user modeling; (2) by developing an LLM-compatible algorithmic pipeline for fine-grained need extraction; and (3) by proposing a scalable and replicable model for data-driven persona generation. The methodology offers a validated, empirical tool for targeted marketing and a deeper understanding of the decision-making pathways of female automobile consumers.Keywords: Social media and persona, Female-oriented vehicles, Persona, Mixed method, LDA, LLMSubject terms: Mathematics and computing; Psychology; Psychology; Science, technology and society

Introduction

Social media provides organically generated big data that captures users” unfiltered thoughts and emotions1. This data, sourced from diverse platforms, is notable for its volume, variety, and richness, allowing for the extraction of meaningful insights through dynamic processing and analysis2. Consequently, social media has emerged as a vital resource for understanding user needs. Utilizing this information is particularly effective for identifying target audiences and constructing personas to guide product design and optimization efforts3. A well-developed persona typically includes demographic details, behavioral patterns, usage scenarios, pain points, needs, and role-related characteristics4,5. However, manual collection and classification of such data are labor-intensive and time-consuming, especially when focusing on specific user groups. To address this challenge, natural language processing and affective computing techniques can be applied to cluster user posts and comments on social media. These methods enable the efficient identification of users” core concerns and facilitate the generation of valuable, data-driven insights6,7.

China currently holds the position of the world’s largest electric vehicle (EV) market, with 7.8 million units sold in 2021—representing half of the global growth in plug-in electric vehicle (PEV) sales that year8,9. The growing economic participation of women has significantly expanded their influence within the consumer market, and they are increasingly assuming the role of primary decision-makers in household car purchases10. Recent data from the China Women’s Automobile Consumption Trends Report indicates that the average vehicle purchase price among female consumers is notably higher than that of male buyers, highlighting women as a pivotal consumer segment in the automotive industry11. Female drivers, however, tend to prioritize different product attributes than men, expressing distinct preferences for aesthetics, safety, comfort, and social experience12,13. Within the context of the “she-economy,” the promotion of refined vehicle designs and lifestyle-driven scenarios has significantly enhanced women’s willingness to pay. Despite this trend, few studies have explicitly investigated women as a distinct user group in research on automotive consumption attitudes or the relationship between user experience and emotion. Understanding the core concerns and emotional inclinations of female car owners is therefore crucial for improving product design, strengthening purchase intention among prospective buyers, and accelerating the adoption of electric vehicles.

Moreover, feminist consumer research and critical marketing theory caution against reducing “female consumers” to a static set of traits—such as emotionality, aesthetic preference, or dependence on safety. Instead, they advocate viewing these consumers as dynamic identities shaped and continually reconstructed within specific sociocultural contexts and market discourses14,15. Prior studies have criticized the oversimplification of female characteristics in marketing communications and persona development, emphasizing that such essentialist portrayals obscure the socially constructed nature of gender and the discursive frameworks in which it is produced16,17. A systematic examination of female car owners’ experiences and attitudes can provide valuable insights with relevance across diverse domains18. In response to feminist critiques of stereotype reinforcement, this study redefines “female-oriented personas” as “situated personas,” emphasizing their contextual and relational construction. However, most existing research relies heavily on surveys and interviews to capture users” subjective evaluations—methods that are often limited by small sample scalability and insufficient emotional granularity19.

This study introduces a hybrid methodology that integrates natural language processing and sentiment analysis to systematically investigate the topics of interest and attitudinal patterns among female car owners. It addresses two key gaps in the construction of female user personas: a methodological gap—concerning the complexities of big data-driven persona modeling—and a theoretical gap—regarding how female expression within social contexts shapes perceptions of automotive products and decision-making processes. Situated at the intersection of persona theory, data-driven design, and digital gender studies, the research combines computational techniques with expert evaluation to establish a persona framework that synthesizes objective data and subjective interpretation. This framework offers both operational feasibility and theoretical depth, serving as an interdisciplinary methodological contribution to fields such as human–computer interaction (HCI), consumer behavior research, and gender-focused communication studies.

This study conducts a clustering analysis of user posts and comments related to “female automotive topics” across major social media platforms, including Xiaohongshu (with the highest proportion of female users), Weibo (with the largest user base), Bilibili (China’s leading video and bullet-comment platform), and Autohome (the largest automotive information app). Using Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic modeling, sentiment analysis, and machine learning techniques, the study quantitatively analyzes large-scale feedback from female car owners. In the subsequent phase, expert workshops are held to refine the user clusters and semantic labels produced during the quantitative analysis. These are narratively contextualized and manually validated, resulting in the construction of realistic personas grounded in actual usage scenarios. The term “female-oriented” in this study refers to gendered discourse clusters related to vehicle usage, attitudes, and emotional expression, as they emerge within specific social media contexts. These personas are developed through the integration of textual data, behavioral patterns, and expert assessment, rather than through essentialist assumptions. This mixed-method approach ensures both the statistical robustness of large-scale data analysis and the interpretive depth of qualitative inquiry. It offers actionable insights for the design of female-focused automotive products and informs future product iterations and user experience improvements. Literature review

Female car users

Historically, automobile consumption and related academic research have predominantly focused on men, either implicitly or explicitly, reflecting traditional norms of social division and gender roles14,17. This male-centric research paradigm has marginalized the diverse needs of female consumers and contributed to long-standing biases in product design and marketing strategies. However, with shifts in societal structures and the increasing economic empowerment of women, female consumers have become an increasingly influential demographic in the automotive market. Their impact is now evident in vehicle purchase decisions, model configuration preferences, and overall user experience20,21.

Existing research demonstrates that female consumers exhibit systematic differences from male consumers in their car-buying preferences and motivations. For instance, women tend to place greater importance on vehicle styling, color coordination, and overall aesthetics22, and are more likely to view cars as extensions of their personal identity and social status23. Additionally, they are significantly influenced by social networks and word-of-mouth during the purchasing process. Female consumers are especially attuned to a product’s visibility and recognition within peer groups, social media, and community discussions. As a result, vehicles that foster social interaction and resonate with group values often hold greater appeal24,25. However, feminist consumer research and critical marketing theory caution against interpreting these preferences as innate or biologically determined. Such interpretations risk reinforcing gender essentialism. In market and media narratives, female consumers are frequently reduced to stereotypical labels—such as “emotional,” “appearance- and safety-focused,” or “seeking gentle experiences”—which obscure the complex sociocultural constructions and contextual factors underlying gender identity15. This tendency toward oversimplification can be further exacerbated in big data and social media corpus analyses, where high-frequency words or topics may be misinterpreted as intrinsic group characteristics, despite being shaped by specific cultural narratives and platform algorithms16,26. To address these issues, research on female automobile consumers should adopt critical theoretical perspectives that acknowledge and explore internal diversity and contextual variability. Intersectional approaches highlight that female consumers are not a monolithic group but rather reflect diverse consumption logics shaped by overlapping dimensions such as age, education, income, occupation, and cultural background. Embracing this perspective is essential for avoiding reductive generalizations and for identifying the needs of marginalized subgroups.

While prior research has identified distinct patterns in female consumers’ aesthetic preferences and tendencies toward social resonance, traditional methods—such as surveys and interviews—often fall short in capturing the complexity and fluidity of their needs27. In contrast, user-generated content (UGC) on social media provides more nuanced and authentic insights into consumer attitudes and emotional expressions. To address this gap, the present study introduces a systematic modeling framework that leverages natural language processing (NLP) and sentiment analysis to analyze social media data. The approach also integrates bias detection, counterexample validation, and intersectional tagging to construct female automotive personas that are multidimensional, dynamic, and grounded in theoretical sensitivity. This methodological innovation responds to the pressing need to mitigate gender essentialism in big data contexts and advances the development of persona models that more accurately reflect the socially constructed nature of user identities.

Social media and persona

Social media platforms generate vast amounts of data through their extensive user bases, enabling researchers to efficiently process large-scale datasets, quantify behaviors such as clicks, likes, and follows, and infer user behavioral intentions28–32. Chinese platforms, in particular, host exceptionally active user populations. For instance, as of September 2020, Sina Weibo—the largest social media platform in China—reported 224 million daily active users and 511 million monthly active users11, with an average of 130 million text posts generated per day11. Weibo serves as a major hub for automobile-related content. Female users frequently share car reviews and user experiences after browsing vehicles, test-driving, and selecting interior features33. Simultaneously, official brand accounts and key opinion leaders (KOLs) publish detailed content—such as new car evaluations and functional breakdowns—attracting considerable engagement from female audiences34. Thus, leveraging social media data to identify target user groups and develop female-oriented personas offers valuable insights for optimizing product design and enhancing consumer alignment.

The concept of the Persona was first introduced by Alan Cooper within the field of user experience design. It is commonly defined as a fictional character representing a real user group, serving as a concrete model to guide design teams during product development35,36. Over time, the application of Personas has expanded across multiple domains, including product design, game development, and human–computer interaction, where they are used to support user experience design in varied contexts37–39. Traditional Persona construction methods typically rely on small-sample, in-depth interviews combined with grounded theory analysis, using qualitative coding to extract user characteristics5,40. However, concerns regarding the reliability and validity of these approaches remain central to Persona research41. Because these methods are predominantly qualitative, they often lack scientific rigor and are subject to researcher bias42. Consequently, the resulting Personas may diverge from actual user needs and are difficult to empirically validate. Moreover, due to the small sample sizes and absence of large-scale primary user data, these methods are unsuitable for quantitative analysis.

In recent years, the quantitative generation of Personas using big data has gained increasing prominence. Researchers have adopted methods such as clustering analysis and data mining to extract user needs from large volumes of social media data, enabling more objective and data-driven Persona construction25,43. This approach has improved the accuracy and reliability of Persona representations. However, current research remains largely focused on domains such as software and web design, with limited attention given to automotive users—particularly female drivers—leaving a substantial gap in the literature.

Although social media platforms provide unprecedented scale and dynamic corpora for Persona modeling, existing research cautions that high-frequency data and algorithmic recommendation logics may not accurately reflect genuine user preferences. This concern is particularly salient in gender-related discourse, where algorithmic models tend to overemphasize emotional and gendered expressions44,45. Such effects are especially evident in discussions about female automotive users; for instance, phrases like “appearance-focused” or “fear of driving” may reflect dominant sociocultural narratives rather than authentic individual perspectives. To mitigate this issue, the present study incorporates a gender-stereotype bias monitoring framework into the data analysis process. This framework includes red-flag word list screening, negative sample sampling, and intersectional tagging, all validated through expert review. These measures aim to balance algorithmic efficiency with social fairness. In doing so, the study enhances the diversity and representativeness of generated Personas while demonstrating a critical ethical awareness in user modeling practices.

Mixed method

Mixed methods research is defined by its pragmatic orientation, methodological diversity, practical application, and problem-driven focus, integrating both qualitative and quantitative approaches46. Quantitative research is valued for its objectivity and precision, yielding standardized and replicable results through the rigorous analysis of large samples. However, it often identifies what has occurred without explaining why it occurred47. In contrast, qualitative research explores the underlying dimensions of phenomena through discussion, open-ended questioning, and participant interpretation, providing richer contextual understanding but with limited replicability48. Different researchers may reach varying conclusions due to interpretive subjectivity. Jick49 was among the first to conceptualize mixed methods, emphasizing that all methodologies possess inherent biases and limitations. By integrating qualitative and quantitative data, researchers can offset the weaknesses of each individual method. A defining feature of mixed methods research is the clear articulation of the purpose of each method and the relationship between them throughout the research process50. This includes specifying how data will be collected, analyzed, and synthesized51,52. Building on this framework, we argue that social media data can serve as a valuable foundation for mixed methods research within interdisciplinary teams, thereby maximizing the analytical potential of social media big data.

Early studies investigated a Persona creation process that integrates quantitative survey analysis with qualitative methods such as observation and interviews53. Miaskiewicz and Luxmoore54 collected user data on SaaS software via online questionnaires and employed cluster analysis to identify distinct user segments. They subsequently conducted in-depth interviews with selected groups to develop Personas tailored to each segment. Salminen, Sengun et al.55 combined interviews with manual Persona analysis to enhance understanding of automatically generated Personas. In a follow-up study, they used social media data to generate 15 Personas and predicted each Persona’s Big Five personality traits based on associated user comments56. More recently, Spiliotopoulos et al.3 created Personas derived from topic modeling of Twitter data.

In summary, while early studies employed methods such as cluster analysis and in-depth interviews for user analysis, they lacked targeted investigation of female consumers. To address this gap, we adopt a mixed-methods approach. Quantitative analysis is used to efficiently collect and process user behavior data, reducing the time and resource demands of traditional methods. Concurrently, qualitative analysis leverages the richness of social media data to provide deeper insights into user characteristics. By combining the strengths of computational techniques and human expertise, this approach improves the validity, depth, and persuasive power of the resulting Personas.

Generating personas using mixed method

This study examines a Persona generation process that integrates quantitative analysis of social media user data with qualitative analysis conducted through expert workshops. The goal is to effectively combine these methods to develop reliable Personas for female users of automotive products.

The data analysis is divided into two main phases. The first phase involves constructing a domain-specific lexicon for female automotive products. Target user groups are then automatically identified using web scraping techniques, followed by user segmentation via clustering algorithms. Subsequent analysis focuses on extracting and interpreting user characteristics. The second phase is dedicated to refining the Personas, including enhancing their descriptive depth and conducting usability evaluations. The target product in this study is a female-oriented automotive offering. The evaluation process is structured into eight steps, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Achieving user segment

Given the highly contextualized nature of user expression on social media platforms57, the first phase of this study collected 273,657 content items from 12,866 users across four platforms: Weibo (comparable to Twitter), Bilibili (China’s most popular video-sharing and Q&A community, similar to YouTube), Autohome (www.autohome.com, the largest automotive information platform in China), and Xiaohongshu (a lifestyle community with a high concentration of female and young users). Step 1: Constructing target words and collecting data

The primary objective of this phase is to develop a keyword lexicon to identify target product users and collect their data from social media platforms. User information was obtained using Python-based web scraping scripts integrated with each platform’s search engine. This phase consisted of three tasks: (1) identifying user needs through desk research and discussions to construct the keyword lexicon; (2) applying the lexicon to locate target product users and official accounts; and (3) collecting user data using web scraping tools.

First, preliminary vocabulary was collected through desk research. To capture diverse perspectives, feedback was obtained from both consumers and business representatives. A three-hour focus group discussion was conducted with two female consumers and three business managers specializing in women’s automotive sales. Target words endorsed by more than half of the participants were recorded, and these keywords were then clustered to construct a specialized lexicon for female automotive products. The lexicon was organized into nine categories, including vehicle models, appearance, hardware, software, technology, and usage scenarios. Representative target words are listed in Table 1.

Second, target product users and official accounts were identified using the constructed keywords. Search tools were employed to locate user posts or comments containing these terms, and the profile addresses of the authors were recorded. In addition, followers of official media accounts linked to the target products were documented (e.g., the official Weibo homepage of “Xiaomi Su7”: https://weibo.com/u/7871239944). The data collection process was fully automated. Once users were identified, Python-based web scraping scripts were used to extract data from their profile pages, including basic information (e.g., ID, username, city, gender), platform tags (e.g., interest labels), and user-generated content such as posts and comments.

This study strictly complied with the terms of service and policies of each data platform, with careful attention to ethical standards and user privacy throughout data collection and processing. First, all raw user data were anonymized by removing personally identifiable information, such as usernames and profile images, and by aggregating demographic variables to prevent individual identification. Second, the data were used solely for academic purposes, with access restricted to ensure security. Third, only publicly available user-generated content (UGC) was collected, with no private or restricted data included. Finally, the study received approval from the Ethics Review Board of Hanyang University, confirming that the research design aligns with established ethical guidelines for academic research.

Based on the core user-related keywords listed in Table 1, a web crawler was configured to perform automated data collection over a 40-month period. The sampling strategy was defined as follows: beginning at start_time = “2022-01-01-00” and concluding at end_time = “2025-05-01-00”, each 24-hour period was designated as a search interval, during which combinations of core keywords were systematically retrieved.

Finally, the data collected through target keyword searches and official media follower identification were classified into two categories. The first included raw user-generated content directly related to female automotive products. The second comprised user profile information extracted from social media homepages, providing insights into daily lifestyles and usage contexts.

Step 2: Preprocessing and structuring data

The main objective of this phase is to filter data to ensure validity and to store information collected from multiple social media platforms in a standardized format. The process involves three steps: (1) detecting invalid text and eliminating duplicates; (2) extracting verbs, nouns, and adjectives from the original content as keywords; and (3) recording user information in the format “user: keyword.”

First, a filtering mechanism was established. Users with zero followers or followings, as well as those lacking verified information, were excluded. Texts with fewer than two words and identical duplicates were also removed. Following this procedure, the Qwen-3 large language model (developed by Alibaba, a next-generation Chinese LLM with advanced capabilities in text comprehension, semantic annotation, and sentiment analysis) was applied via Python to conduct automated line-by-line semantic annotation, filtering, and preprocessing. The Pandas module was subsequently used to further refine the dataset.

Second, Python’s Jieba library58 was used to perform word segmentation and part-of-speech tagging on the raw text. To improve the granularity of analysis for female automotive products, a customized segmentation dictionary and stop-word list were created based on the study’s requirements. Rare but meaningful domain-specific terms were added to the dictionary, while high-frequency but semantically insignificant words were included in the stop-word list. During data analysis, the meaningful terms were retained, and the stop words were removed. The stop-word list was further refined in subsequent experiments based on observed outcomes. Verbs, nouns, and adjectives that conveyed the original content’s meaning were preserved, as they provide critical value for Persona construction.

We used three high-performance computers (Intel i9-14900 with RTX 4080) to process user data and store user information documents. Step 3: User segment

The primary task of this phase was to cluster users based on structured information through three main steps: (1) representing each user’s keywords as word vectors using the HanLP toolkit and calculating inter-user similarity; (2) applying the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic model implemented in the Gensim library to cluster user keywords; and (3) validating the results using semantic coherence and model perplexity to determine the optimal number of clusters.

Although LDA has limitations when applied to short texts, its strengths—interpretability, unsupervised learning capability, and the ability to simultaneously model document–topic and topic–word relationships—make it an appropriate choice. Since the dataset in this study consists of unlabeled and exploratory corpora, LDA offers a reasonable starting point for identifying potential semantic clusters across platforms. To address its shortcomings in short-text scenarios, we adopted a hybrid strategy: (1) constructing pseudo-documents through keyword co-occurrence to increase semantic density, and (2) using expert cross-validation to refine and semantically reconstruct the LDA-generated topics. This approach enhances the semantic coherence of topic clusters while retaining the interpretability advantages of the LDA model.

Compared with methods that classify users by content creation and sharing levels59 or by user roles60, cluster analysis is better suited for efficiently processing large-scale datasets across social media platforms. In this phase, LDA was applied to represent each keyword as a probability distribution vector across K topics. These K-dimensional probabilities were then used to measure semantic similarity and perform user clustering. The process was conducted computationally, and the optimal solution identified five clusters. After the initial segmentation, the validity of the LDA results was assessed using two metrics: semantic coherence and model perplexity. To ensure objectivity and interpretability in selecting the number of clusters, a dual-criteria approach was applied. Perplexity measured the model’s generalization ability, while the semantic coherence score () indicated topic interpretability. In the lower topic range, although did not yield the lowest perplexity, it achieved a local peak in coherence (), suggesting that this value provided an effective balance between generalization and interpretability.

Three independent external experts were engaged to perform secondary semantic categorization of the topic labels generated by the LDA output, with Cohen’s coefficient calculated to assess inter-rater reliability. All experts had backgrounds in user experience research and more than five years of experience in user behavior analysis and data research. The process followed two stages: first, each expert independently annotated a randomly selected sample of 300 text segments; second, during a consensus session, conflicting annotations were discussed and revised until agreement was achieved. The resulting Cohen’s coefficient was 0.80, indicating a high level of agreement. This cross-validation mechanism ensured that the clustering results were not only statistically robust but also semantically consistent and transparent (Fig. 2).

Step 4: Analysis of user online behavior data and product-related content

The previous step generated keywords and original content for each group. In this stage, we analyzed the original content to extract users’ online behavior patterns and product-related usage information. The process involved four steps: (1) selecting high-frequency keywords for each group; (2) examining the original content associated with these keywords; (3) conducting need extraction and sentiment analysis on product-related content; and (4) clustering the results of need extraction and sentiment analysis.

We extracted the top 30 keywords from each user cluster and retrieved their corresponding original content from the database. For each keyword, the associated content was clustered using the LDA topic model to capture its contextual meaning, thereby providing insight into the actual daily lifestyles of users in that cluster. This process generated clustering results for the 30 high-frequency keywords across the five user groups, labeled “Group1_life” through “Group5_life,” which characterize each group’s work and lifestyle patterns. The need analysis identified users’ expectations and requirements for products, while sentiment analysis captured both satisfaction (positive aspects: potential advantages of female-oriented automotive products) and dissatisfaction (negative aspects: potential disadvantages). This process enabled a more comprehensive understanding of users’ product experiences. For example:

“(Positive): The Xiaomi Su7 looks absolutely stunning; (Negative): The Su7 Ultra has terrible range; (Need): I still hope for a hybrid version of the Su7, so I don’t have to worry about range anxiety”

Based on these analyses, clustering outputs for needs, positive evaluations, and negative evaluations were generated using Python scripts and labeled “Group1_product” through “Group5_product” to represent product-related insights.

Generating personas

This section builds on the findings of the first phase by conducting qualitative analysis through workshops to refine Personas of female automotive consumers, followed by questionnaire surveys to validate their effectiveness. While the previous phase—including demographic statistics, keyword and content clustering, and analysis of product-related needs, positive evaluations, and negative evaluations—provided a strong informational foundation, it did not fully capture the deeper motivations and insights underlying the Personas. The qualitative analysis therefore focuses on three objectives: (1) enriching Persona descriptions, (2) contextualizing users’ daily lives, and (3) examining nuanced differences in their attitudes toward the target product to more accurately identify core demands and latent needs. The process comprises four key steps, as illustrated in Fig. 4:

The procedure of creating persona profiles via qualitative analysis.

To reduce the amplification of bias in gender-based Persona modeling, several control mechanisms were integrated into the methodological framework. First, during text analysis, a “red-flag word list” was applied to automatically detect and flag potentially stereotypical language. Second, counterexample validation was introduced during model training and clustering to compare corpora from different genders or non-typical users, allowing potential biases to be identified and corrected. Third, intersectional tagging was incorporated into the Persona construction process to account for additional dimensions—such as age, education, and region—beyond gender, thereby capturing diversity within female user groups. Finally, interdisciplinary expert workshops were conducted to review and refine model outputs, minimizing the risk of reinforcing stereotypes through algorithmic processes.

Step 1: Identifying basic information

This section defines the core attributes of the Personas, focusing on demographic characteristics such as age, gender, region, education level, and occupation. To accomplish this, an interdisciplinary workshop was convened with three industry experts possessing over ten years of experience in interaction design, six doctoral students specializing in interaction design and user research, and two industrial designers engaged in female automotive product development (average participant age: 32 years; 8 males and 3 females). At the outset, participants systematically reviewed the five baseline datasets and developed a preliminary cognitive framework by supplementing keywords and phrases. In the subsequent integrative analysis phase, they cross-validated three types of structured reports derived from grouped data (Groups 1–5): online behavior characteristics (A2), product-related content (A3), and demographic information (A1). By assessing the consistency of statistical features (see Table 2), the workshop established detailed profiles for each group, including age, gender, city distribution, educational background, social activity patterns, and occupational attributes.

Prior to the formal analysis, the working group distributed three types of preprocessed data documents. A1 (user demographic analysis) included statistics on user count, gender distribution, city distribution, education level, occupation, and driving status. A2 (online behavior characteristics) presented the top 30 high-frequency keywords, cross-group frequency comparison charts, and five clustered results of original content corresponding to the keywords (data sources: Group1_life–Group5_life). A3 (product-related content) summarized the top 10 clusters of needs, positive sentiments, and negative sentiments (see Fig. 4). Using a typical user group as an example, the workshop followed a three-stage process. First, all participants carefully reviewed the group’s multidimensional data documents to build a comprehensive understanding of the Persona. Second, focused discussions were held to establish consensus on group characteristics. Finally, validation was conducted using the A1 report, with emphasis on consistency across three dimensions: whether demographic information aligned with online behavior characteristics (A2), whether it corroborated product evaluation content (A3), and whether these attributes collectively formed a coherent and self-reinforcing profile.

Step 2: Refining online behavior characteristics and product-related content

In this phase, documents A2 (online behavior characteristics) and A3 (product-related content) were integrated to extract the core behavioral patterns and product perception traits of female automotive consumers. This step is essential for ensuring the practical utility of the Personas. A structured analysis was conducted using the KJ affinity diagram method (Foster, 2007). The procedure included the following steps: (1) transcribing analytical elements onto movable cards; (2) establishing a baseline group with the first card; (3) sequentially comparing subsequent cards and assigning them to existing groups or creating new ones based on semantic relevance; (4) labeling each group to form an information architecture; and (5) synthesizing the groups to derive structured insights.

Using the online behavior analysis of female user groups as an example (see Table 3), high-frequency keywords were first transcribed onto cards, after which semantic analysis was conducted on clustered content related to the top 10 keyword “appearance” (e.g., “this car looks stunning,” “the interior color scheme is so gentle,” “cream white + gold combination”). The analysis revealed experiential linkages with terms such as “intelligence” (voice assistant controlling the air conditioning), “design” (minimalist and refined interior), and “smart driving” (L2-level assisted driving). These were merged into the category “Integration of Aesthetic Design and Intelligent Experience.” In parallel, product-related content (A3) was examined, incorporating empirical data such as “cost-effectiveness” (high configurations priced around 100,000 RMB) and “performance” (acceleration with a push-back sensation). This combined analysis produced a six-dimensional framework of automotive consumption characteristics, covering usage scenarios (urban commuting/family outings), product priorities (intelligent driving configurations/interior materials), consumption motivations (aesthetic preference/interest in technology), decision factors (cost-effectiveness/real-world performance), information channels (Xiaomi launch event promotions), and purchase pathways (direct acquisition of new energy vehicles). Grounded in raw social media data (e.g., “pink looks great in photos,” “sense of technology”), this framework offers precise insights into how female users integrate emotional aesthetics with rational technological needs in their decision-making processes.

Step 3: Completing personas

In this phase, the earlier findings were synthesized to construct Personas for female automotive users. Keywords and lifestyle clustering results from A2 (online behavior characteristics) were integrated with product clustering outcomes from A3 (product-related content). The KJ affinity diagram method was then applied to translate the clustered lifestyle and product information into narrative descriptions. These narratives were subsequently combined with demographic attributes to generate visualized Persona profiles.

Drawing on lifestyle clusters such as “urban commuters” and “family travel leaders,” representative user stories were developed to illustrate the Personas.: “She is a 32-year-old working mother who drives a new energy vehicle for both commuting and transporting her children. During her commute, she often listens to women’s podcasts through the in-car voice system, while weekends are typically spent on family road trips and camping. She prioritizes vehicle safety, paying particular attention to child seat interface design, and ensures that children’s entertainment devices are always available in the car. On social media, she regularly shares creative approaches to optimizing vehicle space during family outings.”

Based on product clustering results labeled “safety-configuration sensitive” and “appearance-driven decision-maker,” the following demand description was constructed: “Her car purchase decisions prioritize the C-NCAP safety rating and the availability of child safety locks, while she is simultaneously attracted to streamlined body designs and Morandi-style interiors. She primarily obtains information from female automotive blogger reviews and ultimately places her order at a new energy vehicle direct-sales store. In daily use, she relies heavily on L2 + intelligent driving assistance but often complains about reduced range in winter, frequently initiating related discussions on social media.”

The final step in Persona construction involved selecting suitable images derived from user profile pictures and photos posted on social media. All Personas were completed and visualized, with a sample portrait provided in Fig. 5.

Example of a generated persona.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, all Personas were developed using this framework, resulting in five distinct archetypes of female automotive users. Quantitative data (keywords and clustering labels) were obtained through natural language processing, while narrative texts were produced by manually interpreting the clustering results. This approach enabled the seamless integration of quantitative and qualitative methods.

Step 4: Validating personas

In the practical application of Personas, three core challenges are commonly identified: validating overall value4,62–65, assessing accuracy66, and clarifying usability definitions37,67. To evaluate the female automotive Personas developed in this study, we applied the Persona Perception Scale (PPS)55,68, which has been empirically validated in data-driven Persona research69. The survey assessed six core PPS dimensions: credibility, consistency, completeness, clarity, empathy, and willingness to use. Each dimension comprised 4–5 structured items, yielding 28 measurement items in total, which were rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree).

The research team recruited 15 participants with relevant professional expertise to complete an online evaluation. The group included five automotive industrial designers specializing in female consumer characteristics, three interaction designers, five user researchers, and two software engineers (aged 22–32; gender ratio 1:1). All participants had prior experience in constructing or applying Personas. Each participant independently reviewed five sets of female automotive Persona profiles and provided multidimensional ratings using the evaluation scale, yielding 15 5 = 75 valid assessment records. As shown in Table 4, the Cronbach’s reliability coefficients70 for all dimensions were above 0.7, demonstrating strong internal consistency of the measurement instrument.

Discussion

Evaluation of the value of female Personas

To evaluate the quality of Personas, this study applied the Persona Perception Scale (PPS) across six core dimensions (Table 5). The results demonstrate that the female automotive Personas exhibited strong overall reliability and validity. Except for completeness and clarity, the mean scores of the other four dimensions exceeded 6.0. The relatively weaker performance in completeness () may be due to limited coverage of female users’ full lifecycle needs and the insufficient representation of social media’s dynamic influence on car-purchasing decisions. In the credibility dimension, items assessing the authenticity of images lowered Cronbach’s (). This reduction can be attributed to two technical constraints: the limited number of visual materials provided during the workshop and the presentation format, which diminished the recognizability of key family-context elements. Follow-up interviews indicated that while textual information strongly influenced user cognition, images functioned as the primary visual cue, directly shaping initial perceptions of credibility. By contrast, the willingness-to-use dimension achieved the highest level of endorsement (M = 6.019, ). This result confirms that the constructed Personas can effectively guide automotive product design decisions, while also underscoring the value of mining social media data to capture female users’ pain points. For example, high-frequency concerns such as “range anxiety” and “child safety lock,” identified through sentiment analysis, have already been integrated into the 2024 model updates of companies like NIO and XPeng, highlighting the practical translational potential of this research. Overall, these findings affirm that the Personas created through this method were regarded as both usable and valuable by evaluators. They also align with challenges noted by Blomquist and Arvola (2002)4 concerning credibility and clarity, while demonstrating that combining social media data with expert validation can mitigate traditional shortcomings in completeness. This suggests that analyzing and interpreting product-related information from social platforms is not only methodologically meaningful but also practically impactful.

Discussion of cross-platform data differences

This study developed female automotive Personas by integrating social media data from four major platforms—Weibo, Xiaohongshu, Bilibili, and Autohome—each contributing distinct value. Weibo, with its lifestyle-oriented content, provided rich natural language materials for understanding women’s everyday driving scenarios. Xiaohongshu, through vertically focused topics, aggregated detailed product discussions ranging from interior design to driving experiences and range anxiety, making it particularly effective for capturing latent consumer needs. Autohome added value through professional review data, offering objective technical parameters to complement user-generated insights. These multisource datasets were quantified using an LLM-compatible processing pipeline that integrates text preprocessing, keyword aggregation, topic modeling, sentiment labeling, and clustering analysis into a unified workflow. This process transformed unstructured user content into structured insights on user needs and behavioral patterns, enabling researchers to flexibly combine platform resources based on specific research objectives. Compared with traditional research methods, social media data demonstrate clear advantages in scale (tens of millions of UGC entries) and ecological validity (natural, context-rich expressions). Nonetheless, two limitations must be considered: data-cleaning procedures may suppress niche yet important user needs, and demographic biases in platform user bases may limit representation of older or lower-income groups.

All data analyzed in this study were sourced exclusively from Chinese social media platforms, which display distinctive cultural characteristics in user composition, discourse style, and interaction patterns. Consequently, the findings on women’s automotive concerns and emotional expressions should be interpreted as contextually situated within China. In other cultural contexts, differences may emerge in identity construction, social discourse, and automotive consumption logic. To account for this, we interpret our results with caution and propose that future cross-cultural studies test and extend the proposed “contextualized Persona” framework. This reflexive perspective enhances methodological transparency and provides valuable insights for cross-cultural user research. Compared with the approach of Cui et al.18, our cross-platform big data analysis demonstrates greater scalability and stronger capacity to capture the dynamic and diverse preferences of female consumers.

Applicability of algorithmic tools

The hybrid method developed in this study demonstrates strong accessibility and adaptability across diverse research scenarios. Built on a technical foundation of open-source web crawlers, the LDA topic model, and the Qwen-3 large language model, the approach requires only basic data collection scripts to obtain data from multiple platforms. Hardware demands followed a gradient pattern: preprocessing 273,657 text entries required three high-performance computers (Intel i9-14900/RTX 4080) to ensure efficiency, while standard clustering analysis ( user segmentation) was completed on a mainstream personal computer (Intel i5/16 GB memory) in an average of just 12 minutes. This process achieved an optimal balance between perplexity and semantic coherence (see Fig. 2), enabling small and medium-sized research teams to reproduce the method at relatively low cost. In addition, the integration of a female-automotive-specific lexicon into the Jieba library, combined with the HanLP toolkit for precise vectorization of aesthetic terminology, significantly improved the accuracy of Chinese social media text parsing. The Qwen-3 model further achieved 86.7% accuracy in need extraction and sentiment analysis, particularly excelling at detecting latent user pain points, thereby enhancing the empirical depth of the Personas.

The method also provides substantial flexibility in platform selection. For example, when targeting young mothers, data with high emotional density from Xiaohongshu’s parent-child driving discussions can be prioritized, while research on professional women may emphasize commuting-related needs derived from Weibo. By adjusting platform combinations, differentiated Personas can be generated. This approach leverages the scalability of algorithms for large-scale data processing while compensating for the emotional limitations of quantitative analysis through expert workshops. For instance, frequent mentions of “cream white interior” highlight women’s aesthetic preference for soft color schemes, while the phrase “smart driving frees my hands” illustrates underlying emotional needs for comfort in daily life. Such nuanced insights depend on researchers’ contextual interpretation. Overall, the study confirmed the effectiveness of the hybrid approach, with its primary strength lying in the collaborative mechanism established between algorithms and researchers. The algorithmic layer ensures efficient large-scale natural language processing (NLP), while researchers focus on the qualitative interpretation of analytical outputs. This synergy not only guarantees computational efficiency but also, through expert workshops, integrates emotional and contextual insights, ensuring that the constructed Personas align closely with real user needs.

Analysis of the underlying drivers of gender preferences

The pronounced differences in female automotive consumers’ product perceptions and purchasing behaviors cannot be attributed solely to individual preferences or short-term market fluctuations. Instead, they are embedded within the interplay of broader social and psychological mechanisms. To illuminate the internal logic of this gendered consumption pattern, this study employs a three-dimensional analytical framework spanning sociology, psychology–behavioral economics, and critical theory. This framework provides a dual-level perspective, addressing both macrostructural influences and micro-level mechanisms to explain the formation pathways and deeper drivers of gender preferences.

From a sociological perspective, gender differences in automobile consumption are not innate but rather the product of long-standing sociocultural construction and gender socialization processes. Since the Industrial Revolution, automobiles have embodied masculinized symbols of “power, speed, and conquest,” reinforcing men’s perception of cars as markers of identity and authority. With the rise of women’s economic and educational status, they have gradually assumed greater decision-making power in both household and personal consumption. This shift has heightened the visibility of women’s car-buying preferences and accelerated the transition of a traditionally male-centered automotive culture toward more diverse value orientations. Consequently, while sociocultural construction has historically reinforced the masculine imprint of automobiles, societal transformations have simultaneously redefined women’s role as key decision-makers, shaping the gendered landscape of today’s automotive market.

From the perspective of psychology and behavioral economics, gender differences in consumption are shaped not only by rational decision-making but also by subconscious cognitive biases, emotional drivers, and evolutionary legacies. Studies indicate implicit associations between product shapes and gender traits: rounded designs are often linked to femininity, while angular forms tend to attract male preferences. Signaling theory further suggests that female consumers are more likely to engage in “self-reward consumption” to seek emotional value and lifestyle identity, whereas men emphasize automobiles as explicit markers of status and capability. Evolutionary psychology adds that men favor demonstrating strength and resources through consumption, while women prioritize safety and reliability—differences rooted in long-standing survival and mate selection strategies. Collectively, these mechanisms reveal the micro-level psychological logic of gendered consumption and provide a strong theoretical foundation for explaining the deeper drivers of gender-specific demands in contemporary markets.

However, explanations limited to sociological and psychological perspectives remain insufficient; it is also necessary to incorporate insights from feminist consumer research and critical marketing theory. Feminist consumer research argues that so-called “female preferences” are not innate attributes but dynamic identities continuously constructed and reproduced through market communication and social discourse14,15. For example, reducing female automotive consumers to a group defined merely by “a pursuit of aesthetics and safety” risks obscuring their more complex demands related to identity, social status, and cultural capital17. Critical marketing theory further highlights that while market strategies emphasize differentiation, they may inadvertently reinforce stereotypical representations of gender roles16. In line with this perspective, the consumption preferences revealed in this study—through social media data and expert workshops—should not be interpreted as fixed traits but rather as multilayered social constructions embedded within the context of Chinese digital culture. In other words, when female users emphasize safety, comfort, or social experiences in car purchasing, these reflect not only genuine functional needs but also the influence of market and cultural narratives in shaping gender roles. By introducing the concept of “contextualized Personas,” this study seeks to move beyond essentialized portrayals of female consumers and instead view them as diverse, context-dependent identity practices. This perspective not only addresses critical scholarly concerns about gendered consumption research but also provides theoretical insights for future cross-cultural studies.

Marketing and design recommendations

This study offers a comprehensive, multidimensional analysis of gender-based preferences in automobile consumption. The traditional dichotomy—“women prioritize appearance, men prioritize performance”—is outdated and should be reconsidered. Such assumptions are not innate, but rather shaped by sociocultural constructs, cognitive psychology, and evolutionary influences. Contemporary female consumers represent a highly educated and increasingly autonomous demographic. Their purchasing decisions have evolved from a narrow emphasis on aesthetics and safety to include broader, more systematic considerations such as user experience, service quality, and intelligent features. Aesthetic preferences reflect their desire for personalization and lifestyle aesthetics, while their growing interest in performance signals a shift toward more balanced value priorities. Given this transformation, marketing strategies must adapt. Approaches based on gender stereotypes are increasingly ineffective and unsustainable. Instead, successful brands should adopt inclusive design strategies that offer both universally appealing and highly personalized options. Furthermore, leveraging value-based marketing and fostering emotionally resonant community engagement can help establish deeper, long-term connections with consumers.

In the future automotive market, brands should move away from the traditional “pleasing” approach and embrace an “empowerment” mindset, treating female consumers as equal partners and establishing deep, multifaceted connections with them across product design, brand storytelling, and user community building. In terms of product design, brands can draw inspiration from Volvo’s YCC (Your Concept Car), integrating women’s genuine needs into universal design—such as easy-to-clean materials, user-friendly storage spaces, and intelligent assistance systems—which have been welcomed by women and equally appreciated by men. At the same time, offering a wide range of personalized customization options can address the diverse needs of different consumer groups. In brand storytelling, companies should challenge biases such as the “female driver” stereotype, giving voice to women and promoting values of respect and equality. Value-driven marketing should focus on telling stories about women’s pursuit of independence, freedom, and individuality, fostering emotional resonance with consumers rather than relying solely on product specifications. Finally, in community operations, brands can take inspiration from models like the Tank 300, establishing female-centric communities that position women not just as users but as protagonists and co-creators of the brand narrative. Through curated lifestyle experiences—such as female-focused car modification exhibitions—brands can allow women’s aesthetics, preferences, and creativity to shape brand activities, transforming them from mere buyers into co-builders of the brand.

Conclusion

This study developed five detailed personas of female automobile consumers by analyzing 273,657 social media posts from Weibo, Xiaohongshu, Bilibili, and Autohome, using a mixed-methods approach. The primary contribution of this work lies in addressing a key challenge in data-driven persona development—transforming large-scale data into coherent, narrative-driven insights. This advancement was achieved through the integration of quantitative and qualitative methods. Keyword clusters were identified using the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic model, while behavioral patterns were extracted via sentiment analysis using the Qwen-3 large language model. The resulting data clusters were then converted into narrative profiles through interdisciplinary workshops. This collaborative process leveraged algorithmic efficiency alongside human interpretative input, effectively combining computational power with emotional resonance. The personas created through this hybrid method offer a dual advantage: they reflect users’ everyday behaviors and reveal meaningful insights into the needs and preferences of female consumers. Sentiment analysis and classification techniques were used to identify specific pain points and priorities. Moreover, each insight is directly traceable to its source content, enabling iterative reference throughout scenario construction. This traceability provides a strong foundation for user-centered product design and informed decision-making in product development.

However, several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. First, sampling bias across platforms has resulted in the underrepresentation of certain population groups. To address this, future studies will adopt cross-platform weighting models (Wang et al.71) and supplement the data with in-depth interviews. Second, the needs of marginalized female subgroups were insufficiently captured due to the underrepresentation of minority users. This underscores the need for outlier-sensitive clustering algorithms and targeted qualitative analyses to ensure inclusivity. Third, the sentiment classification employed lacks granularity, categorizing product-related content only as positive, negative, or neutral. Future research will focus on training models to detect more nuanced sentiment categories. Implementing these improvements will enhance the methodological robustness, adaptability, and inclusiveness of this approach in interdisciplinary user research. Additionally, they will help establish a theoretical foundation for integrating large language models with multimodal analysis.

The automotive market is poised for continued transformation, and the theories and recommendations presented in this study provide valuable guidance for product design and marketing strategies. Nevertheless, several areas merit further exploration. First, the rapid convergence of artificial intelligence and autonomous driving technologies raises important questions regarding their influence on gender-based consumer preferences. Future studies should investigate whether these technologies reinforce or disrupt existing gendered patterns in product perception and decision-making. Second, amid the accelerating shift toward electrification and smart vehicle development, it is essential to assess whether the automobile’s cultural role as a symbol of social identity and status is undergoing a fundamental redefinition. Finally, as Generation Z becomes a major force in automotive consumption, their distinct values, lifestyle orientations, and digital-native traits require focused attention to anticipate and respond to emerging market dynamics. These directions not only deepen theoretical understanding of gendered consumer behavior but also offer actionable insights for adapting to the evolving structure of the automotive market.
