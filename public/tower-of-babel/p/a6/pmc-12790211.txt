Background

Methamphetamine use, and its associated cardiotoxicities, is increasing both in the United States and worldwide.1 It works by increasing catecholamine release and sympathetic agonism and causes significant morbidity and mortality including through its effects on the cardiovascular system. Methamphetamine use leads to methamphetamine-associated heart failure (MAHF) through both direct myocardial toxicity and sympathetic overstimulation.2,3

•A multidisciplinary, trauma-informed, and patient-centered approach to patients with methamphetamine-associated heart failure leads to improved engagement in outpatient heart failure care and better long-term outcomes.

MAHF is increasing in prevalence, with a study of the US National Inpatient Sample showing a 12-fold increase in hospitalizations for MAHF from 2002 to 2014.4 There is a spectrum of phenotypic presentations of MAHF from diastolic dysfunction with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) to severe forms of cardiomyopathy with severe left ventricle dilation and dysfunction.2 The demographics and comorbidities of patients with MAHF tend to be distinct from standard heart failure. Patients with MAHF tend to be younger; have higher rates of psychiatric diagnoses, polysubstance use, and homelessness; and are more often insured by Medicaid.2,4 This complex psychosocial milieu presents therapeutic challenges for treating patients with MAHF as they often have mistrust of the medical system, financial barriers to care, and competing interests that take priority in their day-to-day lives. Patient-specific care barriers and medical complexity likely also contribute to patients with MAHF being hospitalized at higher rates and having longer lengths of stay than their non-MAHF counterparts.5 Overall, MAHF is a cause of significant morbidity, mortality, and economic burden.6

Treatment of MAHF centers around the initiation of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) and decreased use of methamphetamine. GDMT initiation with or without abstaining from methamphetamine can lead to recovery of left ventricular ejection fraction and improvement in left ventricular size.7,8 We present the case of a patient with methamphetamine use and debilitating heart failure who had a significant improvement of left ventricle function and quality of life with meth cessation and GDMT. This case demonstrates the importance of understanding a patient's individual barriers to seeking treatment, the impact of GDMT on MAHF, and the role of substance-use counseling to encourage further research and advocacy for patients with MAHF.

Case Presentation

A 31-year-old female with past medical history of hypertension, pulmonary embolism on rivaroxaban, and methamphetamine use disorder presented to the emergency department complaining of progressive dyspnea and lower-extremity edema over the preceding several months. She reported three-pillow orthopnea and orthostasis. Her symptoms were severe enough that she was unable to work, and this led her to seek emergency care.

On arrival, the patient was afebrile, tachycardic with a heart rate of 121, hypertensive with a blood pressure of 162/130, tachypneic with 22 respirations per minute, and saturating 100 on room air. ER work-up was notable for an elevated B-type natriuretic peptide at 608 pg/mL (reference range 0-100 normal) and an ECG showing sinus tachycardia and signs of left atrial enlargement without ST changes (Figure 1). Computed tomography pulmonary angiogram was negative for pulmonary embolism but did demonstrate small bilateral pleural effusions, cardiomegaly, and a pericardial effusion. The patient was admitted to cardiology and underwent a transthoracic echocardiogram which demonstrated an LVEF of 22% due to severe global hypokinesis with a severely dilated left ventricle, mild increased wall thickening, severe functional mitral regurgitation, moderate dilation of the right ventricle with mildly reduced systolic function, and moderate functional tricuspid regurgitation, with a small-to-moderate pericardial effusion (Video 1). She was admitted for acute decompensated heart failure where she was started on diuresis and then GDMT. After 6 days as an inpatient, her weight decreased from 106 kg to 95 kg, and she was discharged once stable on 40 mg furosemide daily and GDMT including 25 mg losartan, 25 mg spironolactone, 25 mg metoprolol, and 10 mg empagliflozin daily. On further chart review, she had been originally diagnosed with systolic heart failure approximately 1 year prior to this admission with etiology presumed to be due to methamphetamine use but did not have follow-up or GDMT initiated. She underwent a patient-directed discharge after 48 hours in the hospital due to needing to take care of her young children, and as a result, she did not have follow-up arranged or medications prescribed.

Electrocardiogram From Admission Demonstrating Sinus Tachycardia and Left Atrial Enlargement

During her hospitalization, the cardiology team met with the patient and invited her to visit the outpatient clinic so she could become familiar with the setting that would serve as her primary site for ongoing cardiovascular care. The patient was seen in clinic 3 days after discharge, her B-type natriuretic peptide had decreased to 101, and her symptoms were NYHA functional class II. Six months after her hospital admission, she had NYHA functional class I symptoms and returned to work. She was proud to share that she had abstained from methamphetamine use since her hospital admission. In addition to ongoing cardiology follow-up, she was connected with a primary care provider and referred to social services for housing support and substance use counseling. The cardiology team also engaged her in harm-reduction strategies, including education on triggers and relapse prevention, and discussed the importance of continued follow-up to monitor cardiac function recovery.

She underwent a repeat transthoracic echocardiography earlier that month which showed recovery of left ventricular function with an LVEF of 59% but persistent left ventricular dilation with an left ventricular end diastolic volume index of 83 mL/m2 (Video 2). Both the patient and physician were very happy with her improvement in health and cardiac function.

Discussion

The case presented demonstrates some of the unique challenges of caring for patients with MAHF. Our patient's care, like many others with MAHF, was complicated by her initial low engagement in outpatient cardiology because of her psychosocial instability. These challenges were made more difficult by failure of the care team to arrange for GDMT initiation and follow-up after patient-directed discharge. There is data to support both methamphetamine cessation and GDMT initiation for MAHF, but a clinical challenge that remains is how to ensure patient engagement in outpatient care.9,10 Many patients with MAHF have suffered medical trauma during prior interactions with the healthcare system, and garnering patient trust is imperative. Different groups have piloted a variety of innovative care models for MAHF across the country that aim to address barriers to engagement in outpatient cardiology care, which we will review in detail.

First, Leyde et al11 developed an addiction medicine and cardiology co-management clinic to facilitate engagement in both outpatient cardiology and addiction medicine services. Contingency management, an evidence-based behavioral treatment that reinforces target behaviors (ie, clinic attendance) with rewards (ie, gift cards), was the emphasis of the co-managed clinic. Twelve participants with stimulant-associated cardiomyopathy and a mean LVEF of 29% were enrolled in the pilot program and attended over 80% of clinic visits. In a qualitative assessment of the program, many patients identified mistrust in the medical system as an initial barrier to engaging with heart failure care but credited the trusting relationships they built in the co-managed clinic as helping to overcome this barrier. This mixed-methods study identified homelessness, racism, trauma, and poverty as barriers to engaging in outpatient care. While many of these barriers need to be remedied at the systems level, prior medical trauma can be addressed with trauma-informed care.

Another novel model of care delivery has been developed by the Harborview Medical Center Community Heart Failure Program (CHFP) in Seattle, WA.8 CHFP is a mobile team of medical providers who provide outpatient cardiology care in the community, meeting patients on the street, in homeless shelters, or at their residences. CHFP has demonstrated success with patient retention, improvement in LVEF, and mitigation of heart failure hospitalization. While one key element of their success is eliminating the barrier of transportation to clinic visits, the team also provides trauma-informed care which helps them gain the trust of their patients who allow the medical team to enter the privacy of their homes and see the patients in vulnerable moments.

This case illustrates the complexities of caring for patients with MAHF and the need for novel, patient-centered models of heart failure care delivery such as the ones referenced earlier. While these programs can be complex with a multidisciplinary team and care model, they have been shown to engage patients with outpatient cardiology at higher rates and decrease HF hospitalizations.12 Even in settings where there are no resources to dedicate to one of the aforementioned systems of care for MAHF, the principles of trauma-informed and patient-centered care build patient trust in the medical system and improve patient engagement in heart failure care. In our patient, engaging the patient with her outpatient cardiology providers during her hospitalization was integral to establishing a therapeutic relationship that could be further developed in the outpatient setting. Trauma-informed and patient-centered care are the foundation on which outpatient cardiology care for patients with MAHF is built.
