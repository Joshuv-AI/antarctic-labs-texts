Assessment of health-related quality of life in transfusion dependent beta thalassemia
Authors: Sokiyna Ababneh, Ali A Abu Siyam, Majdi M Alzoubi, Nour A Al-Sawalha, Farah F Al Sukhni, Tahani Al Rahbeni, Khalid Al-Mugheed, Sally Mohammed Farghaly Abdelaliem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractResearch on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among thalassemia patients in Jordan remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate HRQoL outcomes in patients with thalassemia and examine associations with psychosocial, medical, and clinical factors. A cross-sectional study was conducted among β-thalassemia major patients attending the Thalassemia Unit at Princess Rahma Educational Hospital for Children. HRQoL was assessed using a validated Arabic version of the Medical Outcomes Study Short Form (SF-36). A total of 285 patients were enrolled, with a mean age of 20.23 years; 55.1% were male. Lower mental health scores were associated with male gender, higher education, and non-smoking status. Poor physical functioning was linked to male gender, smoking, and not receiving anticoagulant therapy. Lower pain scores were observed among males and those not on anticoagulants. Vitality was significantly lower in males. Absence of comorbidities was linked to better role functioning, while non-smoking and higher income were associated with improved general health perception. Male gender, lower income, absence of splenectomy, and not using anticoagulant therapy were associated with poorer HRQoL outcomes. Overall, male patients reported lower HRQoL than females. Targeted national healthcare strategies are essential to enhance HRQoL among thalassemia patients in Jordan.Keywords: Quality of life, Beta thalassemia, Health-related quality of life, JordanSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Beta-thalassemia is a common inherited blood disorder caused by mutations in the β-globin gene, leading to reduced (β⁺) or absent (βº) synthesis of β-globin chains1,2. While advances in treatment—especially blood transfusion and iron chelation therapy—have significantly improved life expectancy, patients continue to experience long-term physical, emotional, and social challenges that impact their overall well-being.

Globally, over 40,000 infants are born with β-thalassemia each year, with approximately 25,500 requiring regular transfusions3. In the Eastern Mediterranean region alone, an estimated 9,914 newborns are affected annually3. In Jordan, the carrier rate for β-thalassemia ranges between 3.0 and 5.9%, largely attributed to the high prevalence of consanguineous marriage, which accounts for 25.6–52.9% of unions4,5.

Although blood transfusion therapy effectively alleviates anemia and prevents growth abnormalities, it also increases the risk of iron overload, which can lead to further complications and a decline in quality of life (QoL)6. The burden of β-thalassemia is not limited to physical symptoms; it includes psychological, cognitive, social, and economic challenges that collectively interfere with daily functioning7–15.

Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) is a multidimensional construct that reflects an individual’s perceived physical, mental, emotional, and social functioning in relation to their health status16,17. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), QoL is defined as "an individual’s perception of their position in life in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live, and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards and concerns"18. HRQoL further focuses on the impact of disease and treatment on an individual’s ability to lead a fulfilling life19.

Studies from Egypt, Iraq, Saudi Arabia, Iran, and Italy have demonstrated that thalassemia negatively affects HRQoL, with key influencing factors including age, comorbidities, gender, and hemoglobin levels14,20–24. A recent study in Indonesia found that thalassemia patients had low QoL in school and career domains, while a study in Malaysia reported better outcomes among younger patients who received early medical care and had fewer comorbidities25,26.

Despite the high prevalence of thalassemia in Jordan, few studies have evaluated its impact on HRQoL. This gap highlights the need for local research to inform patient-centered care and guide health policy. Therefore, this cross-sectional study aims to assess HRQoL in Jordanian patients with β-thalassemia using the SF-36 instrument and to identify socio-demographic, clinical, and medical factors associated with different HRQoL domains. The findings aim to support improved disease management and enhance the quality of care for this population.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A volunteer-based, single-center cross-sectional study was performed from 20 October 2021 to 20 November 2021 among Jordan inhabitants who were diagnosed with thalassemia, regardless of their nationalities, occupations, and ethnicities. The questionnaire was circulated by the thalassemia unit in Princess Rahma Educational Hospital for Children and approached all patients diagnosed with thalassemia who had regularly scheduled visits for blood transfusion in these units. The inclusion criteria included children and adolescents with a diagnosis of transfusion-dependent thalassemia, understand the Arabic language sufficiently to complete the survey and accepted to participate in the study. Of note, patients who were physically or mentally unable or unwilling to participate were excluded from the study. The study design, objectives, and statement about data confidentiality were clearly stated on the first page of the questionnaire and followed by an informed consent form that was obtained from the patients or their parents.

Data collection procedure

Participants who had routine follow-ups at the Princess Rahma Educational Hospital for Children were approached. Written informed consent was taken before participating in this study. Research assistant was hired to be in charge of recruitment and was briefed about the aims of study, and trained on the recruitment process. Once written consent was obtained, participants were approved to complete the questionnaire. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained from the Jadara University (Memo No: JU/ERC/DC/21/17/264). The research ethical committee in Jordan Ministry of Health also granted for data collection permission (Memo No: MOH/REC/2021/152). The thalassemia unit in Princess Rahma Educational Hospital for Children granted the data collection permission. The data collection procedure followed tenets of the Helsinki Declaration.

Background survey

The background survey collected data across three main domains: socio-demographic characteristics, clinical status, and medical history. Socio-demographic information included age, gender, place of residence, educational level of both the patient and their guardian, employment status of the patient and guardian, and the presence of medical insurance. Clinical status data encompassed smoking history (including cigarette and arghile/waterpipe use), medical diagnosis, source of medical information, type of iron chelation therapy, average number of blood transfusions per month, types of vitamins taken, use of anticoagulant medication, and the date of the last blood transfusion. Regarding medical history, participants were asked about any past surgical procedures, the number of affected siblings or relatives diagnosed with the same condition, and the presence of any chronic illnesses.

Health-related quality of life research instrument

36-Item short form health survey 1.0 (SF-36) was used to assess health-related quality of life of children and adult patients with transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia (TDT). The questionnaire had been used in recently published HRQOL studies among TDT patients14,22. A well-structured Arabic version were developed by researchers based on the previous studies27,28. Also, it was tested for internal consistency, validity, and reliability in a sample of Saudi Arabian citizens. The median Cronbach a for the Arabic RAND-36 in multiple subgroups exceeded 0.70 for most of the scales29,30.

In this study, the sum of the scores on all relevant items was used to determine an individual’s level of health-related quality of life in eight different domains, including physical functioning, bodily pain, social functioning, general mental health, vitality, role limitation physical, role limitation emotional, and general health perception. The total score for each domain of health-related quality of life was calculated, and then according to the minimum and maximum score for each scale, it was categorized into the low and high according to the midpoint of the scale. This was due to the fact that different numbers of items were related to each domain of health-related quality of life31.

SF-36 is a well-documented self-assessment tool of quality of life that evaluates eight major domains in health status by reporting 36 questions. The eight health concepts include the following: Physical functioning (PF), physical role limitation (PRL), bodily pain(BP), general mental health (GMH), emotional role limitation (ERL), social functioning (SF), vitality, and general health perception (GHP). Also, a single item for measuring health transition over the past year was included. Data were collected using the SF-36 questionnaire distributed to patients. Self-reporting of the questionnaire is performed by Adult patients (≥ 14 years), whereas pediatric patients (< 14 years) filled in the questionnaire in conjugate with their parents or a trained data collector.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the Raosoft online sample size calculator (Raosoft Inc., Seattle, WA, USA). Assuming a 5% margin of error, a 95% confidence level, and a 50% response distribution, and based on the total number of registered beta-thalassemia patients in Jordan at the time of the study (approximately 1,200, according to the Ministry of Health, Amman, Jordan), the minimum recommended sample size was estimated to be 292 participants. In the present study, 285 patients with beta-thalassemia were successfully enrolled, with an additional 27 patients declining to participate, resulting in a response rate of 91.3%. Although the final sample was slightly below the recommended threshold, the high response rate and small margin of difference were considered acceptable for maintaining statistical power.

The study employed a convenience sampling method. Participants were recruited from major thalassemia treatment centers and hematology outpatient clinics across selected hospitals in Jordan. Patients were invited to participate consecutively as they attended routine follow-up appointments. This method was chosen due to the practical challenges of accessing a complete patient registry and the logistical feasibility of recruiting from clinical settings where the target population regularly receives care. Inclusion criteria included a confirmed diagnosis of beta-thalassemia and age ≥ 18 years, while patients with comorbid conditions affecting cognitive function or who were unwilling to consent were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25.0. Initially, descriptive statistics were used to summarize respondents’ socio-demographic characteristics. Prior to applying inferential statistics to address the study objectives, exploratory data analysis (EDA) was performed to assess the distribution and suitability of the data for parametric testing. As recommended by Cramer (1997), EDA ensures that assumptions are met before applying statistical models.

To assess normality, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests were employed. Results indicated that all quantitative variables significantly deviated from a normal distribution (p < 0.05); therefore, non-parametric statistical methods were used. Specifically, Spearman’s rank correlation, Mann–Whitney U test, and Kruskal–Wallis test were used to examine associations between socio-demographic, clinical, and medical variables and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) domains.

Each of the eight domains of the SF-36 health survey—physical functioning, bodily pain, social functioning, general mental health, vitality, role limitations due to physical and emotional problems, and general health perception—was treated as a continuous variable. These domains were scored on a 0 to 100 scale, where higher scores represent better perceived health status or functioning.

To assess the contribution of socio-demographic, clinical, and medical characteristics to the various HRQoL domains, a generalized linear model (GLM) was applied. Each HRQoL domain (physical functioning, bodily pain, social functioning, general mental health, vitality, role limitations due to physical and emotional problems, and general health perception) was treated as a dependent variable measured on an interval scale but not normally distributed.

Given the non-normality of the dependent variables, the GLM was specified using a Gamma distribution with a log link function, which is appropriate for continuous, positively skewed data. Independent variables included all socio-demographic, clinical, and medical factors that showed a univariate association with any HRQoL domain at p < 0.25.

Discussion

The high rates of thalassemia32 Worldwide demand the need to conduct more scientific research to evaluate the effect of disease severity on HRQoL outcomes. This study focused on assessing the HRQoL outcomes in thalassemia patients (TP) in Jordan. Thalassemia patients reported low scores on general mental health, social functioning, physical functioning, bodily pain, and vitality subscales. The significant findings of this study are the HRQoL of male patients and those who do not take anticoagulant therapy are more vulnerable. However, our observation about gender-related effect on HRQoL was echoed by a few earlier studies33–35. Differences in the disease severity could be one of the reasons for the gender-related variations in HRQoL outcomes among thalassemia patients. Males with thalassemia more commonly experience severe symptoms and need transfusions more frequently than females. This might have a more critical effect on their health and well-being36,37. Furthermore, the risks of not taking anticoagulants for patients with thalassemia can significantly impact their QoL. Thalassemia patients, particularly those who are transfusion-dependent, face increased risks of thromboembolic events due to factors such as iron overload and atrial fibrillation. The absence of anticoagulants can lead to complications such as stroke or pulmonary embolism, which can severely diminish QoL38,39. The fact that 27% of our patients take anticoagulants while only 2.1% suffer from thromboembolic events can be attributed that anticoagulants were not solely prescribed for thromboembolic events. Many of the patients were prescribed anticoagulants for thromboprophylaxis or had atrial fibrillation. Given the known bleeding risks and medication burden associated with anticoagulant therapy, we think the possible explanation is the Selection Bias in which patients prescribed anticoagulants were generally healthier or had fewer comorbidities (71.6% of the subjects did not report having any other chronic disease) compared to those not on anticoagulation therapy. These patients may have been more actively engaged in their healthcare, which could contribute to better HRQoL outcomes. Patients may adapt to the demands of anticoagulant therapy may also foster a greater sense of control and well-being. Other variables, such as underlying conditions, demographic and clinical factors and overall healthcare management, may have confounded the relationship between anticoagulant use and HRQoL. These factors could influence HRQoL independently of anticoagulant therapy.

To our best knowledge, this is the first study in Jordan that investigated the HRQOL in different age groups including children, adolescents, and adults with thalassemia whereas previous studies in Jordan or Middle Eastern countries either only included pediatric40–44 or adults patients45. The majority of participants in this study are adults (average age = 20). However, the majority of our thalassemia patients had limited education (below high school level) due to the required regular blood transfusions every two weeks, painful complications (may cause hospitalization), fatigue (as a result of the disease), and assigned or urgent doctor appointments which lead to absence from school or work for several times each month. We found that most of the thalassemia patients in Jordan are males. The male patients could experience more difficulty in education than the female patients because they have more responsibilities to take care of their family members in Jordanian culture. Also, a limited education could lead to low family monthly income as we reported that the majority of thalassemia patients were from low-income families. Previous studies reported the same findings in Syria and south Turkey10,46.

In our sample, the majority of thalassemia patients were diagnosed with beta-thalassemia, non-smokers , and had no other co-morbidities. This may indicate that the affected persons had a relatively healthy lifestyle and appropriate early intervention in receiving adequate medical treatment. As per the pre-transfused hemoglobin level (≥ 10g/dl) , 2 units of packed RBCs per transfusion may be required by adult patients47. Moreover, we reported that the vast majority of the patients received two blood units per month and two blood units in the last hospital administration at the time of the study conducted which is another factor that can impact the HRQoL of TP. Despite the importance of regular blood transfusions in correcting anemia in thalassemia patients, it also leads to clinical complications such as iron overload, organ dysfunction, and poor physical health status33. Splenectomy is a clinical operation performed to prolong the red blood cells survival in circulation and thus minimize the need for blood transfusion and its negative consequences. However, splenectomy could cause complications including venous thromboembolism, iron overload, and severe post-splenectomy infections. In this study, most patients did not undergo splenectomy since they have adequate pre‐transfusion hemoglobin levels and adequate intervals between transfusions which could be a good indication of receiving the appropriate medical treatment in early diagnosis48.

Deferasirox was the most used iron chelator in our study sample. This may be because it is orally administered and more efficacious and tolerable among thalassemia patients49,50. It’s also documented that beta thalassemia patients who receive deferasirox are more satisfied and adherence with iron chelation therapy and have an improved HRQOL51. In addition, folic acid and vitamin D were the most consumed vitamin supplements by our TP, which could impact QoL. Folic acid is important in erythropoiesis and decreasing bone pain complaints ,and muscle weakness in beta-thalassemia patients. Also, vitamin D is essential in preventing or minimizing the incidence of osteoporotic fractures, which are highly prevalent in beta-thalassemic patients52–54. Interestingly, we found that zinc supplementation recorded the lowest frequency among our patients which could adversely impact the QoL. In literature, up to 30% of beta-thalassemia patients are suffering from zinc deficiency ,and this leads to decreased insulin secretion and thus diabetes occurrence55.

Only 28.4% of the patients had comorbidities in this study, where orthopedic disease and type 2 diabetes were recorded with the highest frequency. Similarly, a recent study showed that 26% of the thalassemic patients had comorbidities33. Comorbidities contribute to reducing HRQOL by increasing medical visits, medical costs, the side effects of the medicines, and worsening of symptoms of thalassemia. This ultimately increases the physical and economic burden on the patients33.

Thalassemia patients in our study reported low scores in five HRQOl domains: general mental health, social functioning, physical functioning, bodily pain, and vitality, in agreement to what was found in previous reports in some Middle Eastern countries22,23,56. A study involving 130 pediatric thalassemia patients in Egypt reported significantly lower HRQoL scores in all domains compared to healthy controls44, similar to a study in Saudi Arabia of thalassemic patients57. In Iran, a study found that thalassemia patients had an HRQoL score of 75.05, significantly lower than the control group score of 93.4 in physical, social, and mental domains23, in agreement with our study findings. Additionally, Italian thalassemic patients reported lower HRQoL compared with the general population in all SF-36 domains (except for emotional well-being and role limitations due to emotional problems)14. This offers valuable assess that Jordanian thalassemic patients experience a bad quality of life (except for heath perception and role limitations due to emotional and physical problems) similar to the previously mentioned studies.

This study has shown that poor mental health in thalassemic patients has been significantly associated with the male population, non-smokers, and holders of higher degree of education. A study in Iranian thalassemic patients revealed that these individuals suffer from severe anxiety and depression which negatively affect mental well-being58. A previous study had stated a similar observation where male patients reported more severe depression symptoms than females by 6 times. This could be explained by the burden focused on males in terms of marriage, economic status, and responsibility for the family in this population of male patients59.

Few studies have focused on the relationship between mental HRQoL outcomes and patients’ sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. We found that patients with higher education had low mental HRQoL score and this may be due to the Exam and coursework pressure and a lack of engagement with learning activities, in addition to the chronicity of thalassemia, the requirement for constant adherence to treatment, and the need for regular leave to continue therapy throughout their education. According to the literature, mental health in thalassemia patients is generally ignored because the patient and physician’s first concern is to keep the disease course under control60. This addresses the emerging need to provide mental health support as an essential component of health care to individuals living with thalassemia.

Our Research indicates that thalassemia patients exhibit lower physical functioning. This result was similar to that reported by Qayyum et al.61. We found that males, smokers, and patients who do not take anticoagulants are facing problems in daily activities. The relationship between these patients’ anticoagulant levels and physical functioning is significant, as deficiencies in natural anticoagulants contribute to a hypercoagulable state and thus interrupt physical functioning62. Also, thalassemia is a chronic disease with severe anemia and smoking can exacerbate anemia and thus lead to disfiguring bone deformities and interrupt daily activities63,64. Despite these challenges, we found that improved management strategies including an increased number of transfused blood units monthly and managing potassium levels can enhance physical functioning. These results support the findings of the study done by Chordiya et.al, who found the positive impact of blood transfusions on the physical well-being of Transfusion-dependent thalassemic children65. We found that severe and extremely limiting pain is influenced by the following factors: male gender and not taking anticoagulants. The findings of the current study were inconsistent with previously published articles regarding gender; males scored higher than women on a scale for bodily pain34,66. In the present paper, we report a low vitality, in agreement with the observations of Sobota et al.67. It was associated with male gender. A study involving 356 thalassemia patients in Bangladesh found that factors such as comorbidities, and financial burdens correlate with lower vitality scores33. This was the opposite in our study due to the potential biases in self-reporting.

Moreover, the findings of the current study were consistent with those of Hossain and colleagues regarding affected individuals with no comorbidities experienced low role limitations due to physical and emotional problems because comorbidities increase the disease burden on the patient33. Additionally, low-income and older individuals experienced more role limitations due to physical and emotional problems. Also, patients without splenectomy had severe physical-related role limitations. Evidence from a previous study showed that low income was associated with lower scores for physical health and mental health outcomes, as well as a total SF-36 score68. This highlights the role of splenectomy in improving the quality of life of affected individuals by decreasing the frequency of blood transfusions, ameliorating hemoglobin levels, and alleviating symptoms associated with splenomegaly69. Also, Low-income patients may endure social and emotional stress, which could negatively impact their general well-being, mental and physical health. These results are consistent with the findings of Hossain et al.33. We found that both non-smoking and higher earnings play a critical role in good general health perceptions among thalassemia patients. The possible explanation for this finding is that Non-smokers perceive themselves as healthier and recognize the health benefits of abstaining from smoking, which reinforces their positive health perceptions. Additionally, Higher-income individuals often have better access to healthcare services, which enhances their health outcomes70.

Our study limitations include the following: the study sample did not include the patients’ caregivers, which could be a factor that affects the patient’s QoL. There is a potential of having a bias in patient reporting. In addition, we only included thalassemia patients from a single center in Jordan, this was not nationally representative data. However, Comprehensive management models should ideally be developed to fulfill the patient and their caregiver’s needs by giving financial and social support, in which facilitates the social integration of TP. The care strategies that address both physical and mental health should be taken into consideration by clinicians and policymakers to improve HRQoL outcomes for TP in Jordan.

Conclusions

This study assessed the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among Jordanian patients with β-thalassemia and identified key factors that negatively influence various QoL domains. The findings revealed that patients without a history of splenectomy, those with poor adherence to chelation therapy, and individuals with lower income levels experienced significantly worse HRQoL outcomes. Domains most affected included general mental health, social functioning, physical functioning, bodily pain, and vitality, reflecting the multidimensional impact of the disease and its treatment. Additionally, male patients appeared to be disproportionately affected, particularly in mental and social domains, suggesting the need for gender-sensitive interventions. These results underscore the importance of improving current thalassemia management practices in Jordan. Establishing comprehensive care centers and integrating psychosocial and counseling services may help mitigate the disease burden and enhance patients’ overall quality of life.
