Arthralgia in Patients with Chronic Plaque Psoriasis: Practical Insights for Dermatologists
Authors: Paolo Gisondi, Francesco Bellinato, Enzo Errichetti, Luca Idolazzi, Alen Zabotti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionArthralgia is a frequent but often overlooked symptom in patients with psoriasis (PsO) and may represent an early manifestation of psoriatic arthritis (PsA).ObjectiveTo provide a practical overview aiming to support dermatologists in the identification and management of arthralgia in PsO.MethodsAn expert panel comprising three dermatologists and two rheumatologists convened to discuss data from a literature search of PubMed/MEDLINE and EMBASE conducted using the terms “arthralgia” and “psoriasis.” Relevant studies were qualitatively analyzed based on clinical relevance and available data on epidemiology, pathophysiology, diagnosis, and management.ResultsPredictors of PsA development and distinctions between inflammatory and noninflammatory joint pain are presented, and the main differential diagnoses such as osteoarthritis and fibromyalgia are discussed. The importance of validated screening tools, early imaging, and timely rheumatology referral is emphasized, along with recommendations for patient education and risk factor management.ConclusionsDermatologists play a central role in the early detection of arthralgia in patients with PsO. Timely rheumatology referral is essential to facilitating early intervention, preventing irreversible joint damage, and improving long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Psoriasis (PsO) is a chronic inflammatory skin disease affecting approximately 2–3% of the global population, with up to 7.5 million cases in the United States and similar or higher prevalence reported in various European countries, including Denmark, Italy, France, and Norway 1, 2. PsO is frequently associated with inflammatory comorbidities, including psoriatic arthritis (PsA). Approximately 15–42% of patients with PsO may develop PsA, with variability influenced by methodological differences and geographic factors 1, 3. PsA can present as peripheral arthritis, enthesitis, dactylitis, or axial disease. Notably, skin symptoms precede joint involvement in about 70% of these cases 1, 3, 4.

Arthralgia, defined as articular symptoms in the absence of clinical arthritis 5, is commonly reported by patients with PsO and may represent an early manifestation of PsA, underscoring the importance of prompt recognition to improve early PsA diagnosis and prevent significant joint damage 6. However, differentiating arthralgia as a warning symptom of PsA from arthralgia due to other causes (e.g., osteoarthritis) can be challenging. This difficulty particularly concerns the threshold for further investigation or referral to rheumatologists.

Objectives

This review aimed to provide dermatologists with practical, evidence-based strategies for identifying and assessing arthralgia in PsO patients, evaluating potential joint involvement, determining appropriate referral to rheumatology, and implementing management strategies that help prevent disease progression and enhance patient outcomes.

Methods

An expert panel, comprising three dermatologists and two rheumatologists from Italy with experience in managing patients with psoriatic disease, was convened. The panel gathered for two virtual meetings. The first meeting focused on the literature search strategy and outlined key clinical issues to be evaluated; the second focused on reviewing, discussing, and refining the material to be included in the review. From the output of the second meeting, all the panelists drafted a manuscript that was eventually approved by the whole panel.

To identify papers to be included in the panel discussion, a literature search was conducted between December 2024 and February 2025 in PubMed/MEDLINE and EMBASE for studies published in the preceding ten years addressing arthralgia in patients with PsO. Papers were identified and qualitatively analyzed using the key search terms “arthralgia” and “psoriasis.” Manuscripts were chosen based on their clinical relevance, inclusion of data on prevalence, pathophysiology, diagnostic criteria, and management strategies for PsO-related arthralgia. The key points of discussion are presented in this narrative review, which does not provide new data, but rather offers a critical overview of the topic.

Results

Arthralgia in PsO: Quantifying the Risk of Progression to Psoriatic Arthritis

The rates of patients with PsO without joint symptoms who subsequently develop PsA varies according to study setting, follow-up length, and case identification methods. A prospective cohort found an annual incidence of 2.7 cases per 100 PsO patients (95% confidence interval (CI): 2.1–3.6) over eight years of follow-up 7, while population-based PsO cohorts report annual incidence rates of PsA between 0.3% and 3.7% 8. In a clinical cohort used to derive the PRESTO risk tool, 51 of 635 PsO patients with no clinical musculoskeletal sign developed PsA within one year and 71 of 635 within five years 9. In a nationwide Swedish cohort of 123,814 adults with incident PsO and no PsA signs, about 1.69 per 100 patient-years developed PsA, rising with PsO severity from 1.48 (mild) to 3.00 (moderate) and 5.49 (severe) per 100 patient-years 10.

Several studies have shown that individuals with PsO who develop arthralgia have a significantly higher risk of progressing to PsA than do those without arthralgia 3, 6, 7, 11–13. The incidence rate of PsA in PsO patients without arthralgia varies from about 1.34 [0.0–7.5] to 17.4 [5.6–40.5] per 100 patient-years, whereas in PsO patients with arthralgia it ranges from 10.9 [3.5–25.4] to 34.3 [13.8–70.7] per 100 patient-years 14. A recent meta-analysis showed that the presence of arthralgia doubles the risk of developing PsA (relative risk (RR): 2.15; 95% CI: 1.16–3.99) 14. In line with this, a very recent prospective study demonstrated that psoriatic patients reporting arthralgia are at an almost twelve-fold higher risk of progressing to PsA compared with those without arthralgia (hazard ratio (HR): 11.7; 95% CI: 1.57–86.7) 8. These findings are clinically highly relevant as they highlight arthralgia as an early warning sign of imminent disease transition. Indeed, in a cohort of patients with PsO and subclinical PsA, defined as arthralgia in the absence of clinical synovitis, the cumulative probability of developing PsA reached 9.4% at 12 months, 18.0% at 24 months, and 22.7% at 36 months 8. This temporal gradient illustrates how musculoskeletal symptoms may conceal a substantial risk of progression, underscoring the need for vigilant monitoring and timely preventive strategies. Supporting this view was another study of patients with PsO who presented with arthralgia that showed that the probability of developing PsA within one year was 29% 15.

Characterization of Arthralgia: the Key Short-Term Predictive Sign of PsA

Arthralgia has primarily been used as an enrichment strategy to design prospective studies aimed at identifying PsO populations at higher risk of PsA rather than for direct clinical or diagnostic purposes. Consequently, the definitions adopted across studies are heterogeneous and not yet applicable in routine clinical practice. Still, these investigations provide important insights with potential clinical relevance, although they require validation. Faustini et al. 1 described arthralgia as the presence of at least one tender joint; Zabotti et al. characterized it as recent-onset, noninflammatory joint and entheseal pain in patients not meeting CASPAR criteria 16; Simon et al. refined the concept using visual analog scales and tender joint counts 17.

Several longitudinal cohorts, including those by Eder 7 and colleagues, have consistently demonstrated that many PsO patients experience a prodromal period before formal PsA diagnosis, often characterized by arthralgia or other musculoskeletal symptoms. During this phase, pain and stiffness typically intensify in the months leading up to disease onset, with studies showing a progressive rise in pain scores over the year preceding diagnosis and a marked acceleration in the final three to six months. Unlike the widespread arthralgia often observed in fibromyalgia, prodromal PsA tends to involve a limited number of joints or entheseal sites, most commonly in an oligoarticular distribution affecting structures such as knees, ankles, distal interphalangeal joints, or focal entheseal insertions. Interestingly, this distribution pattern is frequently preserved at the time of overt PsA onset, when the disease also typically presents as an oligoarticular phenotype 13, 18. Importantly, the risk of developing PsA is not constant over time but tends to cluster in the period immediately preceding diagnosis. For example, Eder et al. reported that the incidence rate in the six months before diagnosis was up to five times higher than during the preceding year 7. Another characteristic of this prodromal phase is the absence, in some patients, of classical inflammatory features. In some patients, pain appears mechanical or intermittent, lacking prolonged morning stiffness, which likely reflects the heterogeneous and multifaceted nature of PsA 8. This atypical and often noninflammatory presentation can complicate early recognition while also highlighting the potential value of imaging in detecting subclinical inflammation that may not be evident on clinical examination. Imaging, and particularly musculoskeletal ultrasound with power Doppler, plays a crucial role in characterizing arthralgia and in differentiating potentially prodromal PsA from nonspecific musculoskeletal pain. Ultrasound represents the most accessible and practical tool in routine clinical settings for the detection of peripheral inflammation. However, for the assessment of axial involvement, especially in patients with inflammatory back pain, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) remains the gold standard for detecting early inflammatory changes at the level of the sacroiliac joints and spine 19. When subclinical arthritis or suspected early PsA is considered, referral to a rheumatologist remains essential to ensuring timely evaluation, accurate diagnosis, and appropriate management 12.

Other Predictors of PsA: Long-Term Risk Factors

Beyond arthralgia, several features confer an increased long-term risk of PsA in patients with PsO. Long-term predictors of PsA include severe and/or long-standing PsO, particularly when exceeding a decade and characterized by elevated Psoriasis Area Severity Index (PASI) scores 12, 20. Nail involvement represents another strong risk factor, as histological and imaging studies have shown that inflammation in the nail unit is closely linked to inflammation in adjacent joint structures 12, 21, 22. A family history of PsA, especially in association with specific HLA alleles such as HLA-B27, further amplifies this susceptibility 12, 14, 22. Obesity is also a well-established modifiable risk factor, contributing to systemic inflammation and significantly increasing the risk of PsA 14, 23. In addition, obesity may promote chronic joint trauma, acting as a form of “deep Koebnerization,” where mechanical stress or continuous microtrauma at the level of the enthesis organ can trigger local innate immune activation. Experimental and translational studies suggest that biomechanical stress at the enthesis promotes the release of pro-inflammatory mediators and the activation of entheseal-resident immune cells, with subsequent upregulation of the IL-23/IL-17 axis, contributing to the transition from subclinical inflammation to the onset of arthritis in predisposed joints or anatomical regions 24, 25 (Figure 1). Importantly, observational studies have demonstrated that a reduction in body mass index (BMI) is associated with a lower risk of developing PsA, suggesting that weight loss may act as a protective factor 13. In this regard, it will be crucial to evaluate whether novel pharmacological strategies for weight reduction such as tirzepatide and other GLP-1 receptor agonists can further modify the long-term risk of PsA in psoriatic patients.

Diagnostic Approaches to PsA

Currently, no diagnostic criteria for PsA are available; only classification criteria exist. The CASPAR criteria for PsA classification require evidence of inflammatory articular disease (arthritis, enthesitis, or spondylitis) together with specific features such as skin PsO, nail lesions, dactylitis, negative rheumatoid factor status, and juxta-articular new bone formation on plain radiography 26. It is important to underline that CASPAR are classification criteria, and the lack of universally accepted diagnostic criteria for PsA remains a major unmet need. Despite their utility, early identification of PsA is still challenging due to the broad heterogeneity of musculoskeletal manifestations, the absence of a definitive biomarker, and the overlap with other conditions such as osteoarthritis, gout, fibromyalgia, and even rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

In clinical practice, the diagnosis of peripheral arthritis generally requires unequivocal evidence of peripheral joint swelling, while current PsA treatment recommendations state that enthesitis should be clinically evident and, when suspected, confirmed by imaging. For axial involvement, rheumatologists often apply the ASAS recommendations 19 as an analogy, although dedicated PsA-specific criteria are expected in the future.

Observational studies suggest that PsA at onset is predominantly characterized by peripheral arthritis, most often presenting with an oligoarticular pattern involving the small joints of the hands and feet as well as the knees 18.

Other clinical features such as dactylitis or enthesitis may occur, but they are less common at disease onset compared with peripheral joint involvement, which represents the principal marker of early PsA 18.

Notably, radiographic evidence of erosions is already detectable in approximately 20–30% of patients at disease onset, underscoring that structural damage may occur very early in the disease course 18.

Taken together, these findings emphasize that the absence of validated diagnostic criteria represents a crucial limitation in both research and clinical practice. While the CASPAR criteria serve as a robust tool for case classification in established disease, they do not provide sufficient guidance for the diagnosis of early or very early PsA. The development of shared and validated diagnostic criteria capable of integrating clinical and imaging findings remains an urgent unmet need to enable timely identification and early treatment, which would ultimately favor the prevention of irreversible joint damage in PsA.

In the dermatology setting, the recognition could be even more complex. The prompt identification of suspicious features of PsA could be facilitated by the use of validated screening questionnaires such as the Psoriatic Arthritis Screening Evaluation (PASE), the Psoriasis Epidemiological Screening Trial (PEST), the Early ARthritis Psoriatic questionnaire (EARP), and the Psoriatic Arthritis Screening (Si-PAS) tool 27, 28. Although these tools are intended only for screening and cannot establish a definitive diagnosis, their systematic use can facilitate recognition and timely referral to rheumatology for further evaluation. These tools show good sensitivity and specificity in initial validations, but their performance can be lower in routine or primary-care dermatology practice, especially for patients with mild or oligoarticular PsA presentations 6, 27.

Standard serum inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP, ESR) may not always be elevated in early PsA. Consequently, conventional laboratory tests often have limited sensitivity or specificity for PsA screening 27.

Musculoskeletal symptoms in PsO patients should be carefully considered as a substantial proportion of these cases are due to conditions other than PsA. In some cohorts, as many as 50% of suspected PsA cases are eventually reclassified as alternative diagnoses 6. The conditions most commonly misdiagnosed as PsA include osteoarthritis (OA), fibromyalgia, RA, gout, and other crystalline arthropathies 27, 29, 30. Adding to the complexity, PsA can also coexist with these conditions, further complicating clinical evaluation 6.

One of the primary clinical tasks is to discriminate between inflammatory, typical of PsA, and noninflammatory symptoms. Noninflammatory arthropathies such as OA often worsen with prolonged use of the joint and are usually associated with less pronounced morning stiffness 22. However, when PsA presents in early or mild forms, or when inflammatory markers remain within normal ranges, as occurs in up to 50% of cases, it can be difficult to distinguish it from noninflammatory conditions such as OA 22.

Clinicians should be aware that OA typically features cartilage degeneration, bone enlargement, and a pattern of pain that worsens throughout the day. Although OA and inflammatory conditions should be readily distinguishable, both can involve the distal interphalangeal joints and exhibit enthesophytes. Moreover, ultrasound may reveal overlapping abnormalities at entheseal sites, further complicating the differentiation 21, 31. Findings such as active power Doppler signal, erosions at the entheses, and marked inflammatory changes are more typical of PsA or rheumatoid processes than of primary OA 21.

Fibromyalgia, characterized by widespread, longstanding musculoskeletal pain, fatigue, sleep disturbances, and tenderness at soft tissue sites, including entheses, often poses a different but equally significant challenge 32, 33. As PsA can also feature pain at tendon insertions, clinicians must look for additional hallmarks of inflammation such as swelling, redness, or power Doppler signal on ultrasound to distinguish enthesitis from the diffuse tenderness typical of fibromyalgia 21. Furthermore, fibromyalgia can coexist with PsA, compounding the diagnostic complexity 6, 34. Both conditions are also frequently associated with depression, which can further amplify pain perception and symptom burden 35, 36.

Other conditions mimicking PsA include gout and RA. Gout usually presents as acute attacks of severe joint pain (classically, first metatarsophalangeal joint) associated with elevated uric acid or characteristic crystals in joint fluid. Patients with PsO are at a higher risk of developing hyperuricemia, and concomitant gout may also occur 22, 37.

Multiple arthropathies may coexist in the same patient, requiring dermatologists to consider both concurrent diseases and overlap syndromes 6. In addition, musculoskeletal pain in PsO patients can be nonspecific, especially in early disease 12, 38. Management Perspective of Arthralgia in PsO Patients

Effective management of arthralgia in patients with PsO focuses on controlling joint pain and improving daily function, thereby reducing the negative impact on overall well-being.

In PsO patients with intermittent pain or stiffness and no objective sign of dactylitis or enthesitis, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) can provide symptomatic relief of musculoskeletal signs, although they do not prevent joint damage 40. In patients presenting clear signs of articular involvement, systemic therapy should be considered. Conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) such as methotrexate are useful in cases of moderate-to-severe cutaneous PsO with peripheral active joint involvement 40, 41.

Biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs) offer additional options and should be adopted in patients with peripheral arthritis and an inadequate response to at least one csDMARD 40. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) inhibitors are extensively used in patients with established PsA and may potentially reduce progression in those with arthralgia “at-risk” 8. There are now data indicating that treating PsO patients with targeted drugs reduces systemic inflammation and could have the potential to intercept the development of PsA 42. Interleukin-17 (IL-17) inhibitors benefit both cutaneous and articular manifestations of psoriatic disease and can be considered at earlier stages showing inflammatory changes in entheses or joints 6. Interleukin-23 (IL-23) inhibitors are gaining attention for their potential role in lowering the risk of disease progression, given their effectiveness in controlling skin lesions and underlying inflammation 43, 44. IL-23 is a key driver of the Th17 pathway, inducing overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines involved in both skin and joint inflammation, thus establishing a mechanistic link between PsO and PsA 45. Emerging evidence indicates that IL-23-activated cells may migrate to sites such as the joints and entheses in PsA, where they produce cytokines, including IL-17 and TNF, that amplify the local inflammatory response 46. Observational studies suggest that early IL-23 blockade in PsO may be associated with a reduced incidence of new-onset PsA. For example, patients treated with IL-23 inhibitors for PsO were found to be less likely to develop PsA or inflammatory arthritis when compared with patients treated with IL-17 inhibitors or anti-TNF 47–50. However, these findings are derived from non-randomized data and do not allow definitive conclusions to be drawn. Ongoing prospective studies are designed to test whether IL-23 inhibitors can potentially prevent or delay PsA and reduce subclinical musculoskeletal inflammation in patients with PsO at increased risk of transitioning to PsA 51, and conclusive evidence is not yet available.

Newer agents, such as phosphodiesterase-4 (PDE4) inhibitors (e.g., apremilast), are effective for cutaneous lesions and mild joint symptoms, serving as an intermediate option prior to more potent biological therapies. Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitor use in established PsA has shown improved peripheral arthritis, dactylitis, and enthesitis 52, 53; they are currently indicated after inadequate response to at least one bDMARD 40, 54.

Dietary and lifestyle interventions should always be used in conjunction with standard medical therapies for PsO and PsA and might contribute to reducing disease progression. Weight management is suggested, as obesity is a significant risk factor for PsA 14. Moderate exercise and weight reduction can improve inflammatory control, alleviate mechanical stress on the joints, and support better pain control.

Given that BMI is a modifiable parameter through both lifestyle interventions and pharmacological treatments, weight reduction may represent a key target for improving outcomes in patients with PsA. Interestingly, statin use has also been linked to a reduced risk of both PsA and psoriasis, potentially due to its anti-inflammatory properties 55.

Emerging weight loss therapies, including glucagon-like peptide-1 agonists and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, may offer benefits when used in combination with disease-modifying treatments in patients with PsA and metabolic syndrome and warrant investigation as potential adjunctive options 56, 57.

Finally, patient education is essential: individuals with PsO should be informed about the risk of developing PsA when experiencing persistent joint pain, stiffness, or swelling (even mild) and should be encouraged to report any changes promptly. Early recognition of symptoms and proactive patient engagement in communication with healthcare providers can enhance outcomes 6.

Limitations

This review is based on a narrative, expert-driven approach and does not follow a systematic review methodology. The literature search was conducted using a set of keywords and was intended to support the panel discussion and identify publications considered relevant from a clinical perspective rather than to provide an exhaustive overview of all available evidence. As such, the selection of studies may reflect a degree of expert judgment. Nevertheless, this approach was chosen to align with the aim of offering a pragmatic and clinically oriented synthesis to support dermatologists in routine practice.

Conclusions

Arthralgia in patients with psoriasis frequently represents an early warning sign of PsA or other musculoskeletal conditions. Dermatologists are often the first to encounter these patients and play a pivotal role in timely recognition by distinguishing inflammatory from noninflammatory joint pain and, when appropriate, by integrating imaging modalities such as ultrasound or MRI. Early referral to rheumatology is essential as prompt initiation of appropriate therapy, ranging from NSAIDs and conventional synthetic DMARDs to biologics and targeted synthetic DMARDs, can reduce progression and prevent irreversible joint damage. In parallel, lifestyle interventions, particularly weight reduction, provide an additional protective effect and enhance long-term outcomes. Importantly, close dermatology–rheumatology collaboration not only improves prevention strategies but also enables the selection of therapies effective for both skin and joint disease, ensuring more comprehensive and streamlined patient care. Ultimately, multidisciplinary collaboration and effective patient education remain essential to optimizing long-term outcomes.
