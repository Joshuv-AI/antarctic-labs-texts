Published Evidence on Peripheral Atherectomy: A Meta-analysis and Systematic Literature Review of More Than 300 Original Investigations
Authors: Jeffrey G. Carr, Ralf Langhoff, Brian G. DeRubertis, Kristin L. Hood, Prakash Krishnan, Vikram Puttaswamy, Thomas Zeller, Eric A. Secemsky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough atherectomy for peripheral interventions has been studied for over 35 years, recent criticisms suggest it lacks supportive evidence. This analysis provides a comprehensive overview of the quality and outcomes in the published atherectomy literature.MethodsA systematic review of original research published in MEDLINE, Embase, and PubMed through November 2024 identified prospective and retrospective studies on atherectomy for infrainguinal peripheral artery interventions. Case studies and meta-analyses were included in the systematic review but excluded from the quantitative meta-analysis. Risk of bias was assessed using validated scales. Study design, device class, patient/lesion characteristics, provisional stenting, distal embolization, and 12-month outcomes (patency, major amputation, target lesion revascularization, and mortality) were captured.ResultsThe systematic review included 322 published atherectomy papers (121 directional, 44 laser, 30 orbital, 72 rotational, 55 mixed atherectomy classes). Designs were meta-analyses in 3.7% (12 papers), randomized controlled trials in 5.9% (19 papers), prospective observational studies in 29.8% (96 papers), retrospective observational studies in 45.7% (147 papers), and case studies in 14.9% (48 papers). Adjunctive therapies were used in 91.5%, and 29.2% included a comparator arm. Among 190 papers included in the meta-analysis, the 12-month patency, target lesion revascularization, major amputation, and mortality rates were 75.4% (51 studies), 15.6% (67 studies), 1.7% (71 studies), and 2.8% (63 studies), respectively. The distal embolization rate was 2.2% (159 studies), and the provisional stenting rate was 9.3% (131 studies). Considerable heterogeneity was observed.ConclusionsExtensive published literature exists for peripheral atherectomy, including high levels of evidence. Clinical outcomes were overall favorable with low rates of amputation, mortality, provisional stenting, and distal embolization. This intentionally broad review was associated with considerable heterogeneity and should be confirmed with appropriate comparative studies.

Introduction

Atherectomy is a vessel preparation procedure that removes atheromatous plaque by cutting, drilling, shaving, pulverizing, lasing, or sanding. Atherectomy has evolved as an adjunct during peripheral endovascular intervention since the first publication describing outcomes with directional atherectomy in 1988.1 Initially, there were a limited number of approved devices, but in recent years, there are now many atherectomy choices with varying mechanisms of action. Atherectomy has the ability to improve the compliance of the vessel wall, reduce plaque burden, and enlarge the treated lumen, and may be of particular benefit in occlusive and calcified lesions.2 Postprocedural residual stenosis is a known predictor of patency failure.3 By achieving enhanced lumen gain and reducing barotrauma that leads to dissections, atherectomy can decrease residual stenosis and reduce the need for stent scaffolding,4, 5, 6 which has been associated with high restenosis rates.7 Plaque modification to increase drug absorption into a calcified vessel wall may also improve outcomes.8,9

Atherectomy use has increased over the past 15 years due to advances in the devices, training, unmet clinical needs, and supportive reimbursement.10,11 This increase in use has led to criticism, with some suggesting atherectomy should have a limited role in clinical practice due to a lack of supportive data.12 Although prior meta-analyses have been published on atherectomy, they fail to represent the totality of published literature. Additionally, a recent proliferation of published studies has not yet been fully captured in published meta-analyses. Materials and methods

Data sharing

Data may be made available to qualified researchers upon reasonable request after publication of the primary and planned secondary papers of the meta-analysis.

Eligibility criteria, information sources, and search strategy

A PRISMA-compliant13 and MOOSE-compliant14 systematic review of MEDLINE, Embase, and PubMed identified original research articles published on the use of atherectomy (with or without adjunctive procedures) in human peripheral artery interventions through November 30, 2024 (Supplemental Appendix S1). Manual searches were also conducted using Google Scholar and the references listed in included papers and other meta-analyses. Review articles, letters, conference abstracts, books, trade journals, non-English language studies, and nonhuman interventions were excluded. Papers on intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) were included in the search, but were out of the scope of the current atherectomy analysis. Randomized and nonrandomized controlled studies, single-arm prospective studies, retrospective series, and case studies were included in the search. Meta-analyses and case studies (defined as single-patient case reports or case series with fewer than 10 patients with no aggregate data presented) were included in the systematic literature review but excluded from the quantitative meta-analysis.

Selection process and data collection

Titles and abstracts were initially screened by 2 reviewers. Articles that evaluated atherectomy for endovascular treatment of occlusive or stenotic disease in native, infrainguinal arteries (including all arteries from the common femoral artery and below) were included. Both de novo and restenotic lesions were included, including in-stent restenosis. Iliac artery interventions were included only if combined with infrainguinal interventions; articles that evaluated the iliac artery alone were excluded, as no atherectomy device is currently approved for this vasculature. Articles that evaluated atherectomy devices only for thrombus removal were also excluded. The full text of each manuscript was reviewed if it either passed initial screening criteria or had insufficient information in the title or abstract to determine eligibility. Articles that did not present any clinical data specifically related to atherectomy use, or studies using atherectomy in fewer than 95% of patients (and not specifically reporting outcomes in an atherectomy subgroup) were excluded. Articles presenting overlapping or sequential follow-up results of the same study were included in the systematic literature review but excluded from the meta-analysis to avoid data redundancy. In cases of redundant publication, only the paper with the most complete reporting of the predefined outcomes was selected for the meta-analysis. Database and claims analyses (eg, National Inpatient Sample, Vascular Quality Initiative [VQI], Medicare) on overlapping patient cohorts were excluded from the quantitative meta-analysis to prevent redundant reporting; in such cases, the most complete and current analysis was selected.

An Excel (Microsoft Corp) worksheet was developed, tested, and refined to serve as the source for data extraction with predefined end points. Full text articles meeting the inclusion criteria were each read by 2 reviewers, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Original authors were not contacted.

Data items and definitions

The following baseline datapoints were collected: study design; country of the first author’s affiliation; types and brands of atherectomy devices used; adjunctive therapies used (if applicable); comparator (if applicable); core laboratory use; artery levels/locations; number of patients; number of lesions; follow-up timepoints; lesion length; proportion of severe calcification and any calcification; proportion of total occlusions; TASC II C/D proportion15; proportion of patients with chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI); proportion of cases using embolic protection. The prespecified primary outcomes of interest were the rates of 12-month primary patency, 12-month target lesion revascularization (TLR), 12-month major amputation, 12-month all-cause mortality, distal embolization, and provisional (unplanned) stenting.

The definition of primary patency varied widely across studies. Of the papers reporting 12-month patency, the most common definitions were “freedom from restenosis >50% and without TLR,” “peak systolic velocity ratio (PSVR) <2.4 by duplex ultrasound and without TLR,” “PSVR <2.4 by duplex ultrasound,” “freedom from restenosis >50%,” and “freedom from TLR.” For the purposes of this meta-analysis, primary patency was defined as angiographic or duplex ultrasound patency (PSVR ≤2.4 or angiographic stenosis <50%) and/or freedom from TLR. Papers using other definitions were excluded from the patency analysis.

Adjunctive therapies included any therapy (eg, plain old balloon angioplasty [POBA], drug-coated balloons [DCB], stents, specialty balloons) used after atherectomy and intended for definitive treatment of the stenosis or occlusion. Planned stenting for definitive treatment was considered adjunctive therapy, whereas unplanned stenting for suboptimal results (eg, residual stenosis >30%, flow-limiting dissection, or perforation) was considered provisional/bailout stenting. Studies that did not define the reasons for stenting were excluded from the provisional stenting analysis. Baseline CLTI was defined as Rutherford categories 4 to 6 or as reported in the original paper. Severe calcification was defined as reported in the original paper, or as Peripheral Artery Calcification Scoring System (PACSS) grade 4 if reported.16 Distal embolization was defined as clinically significant (as reported by the original authors) or requiring treatment; filter capture not requiring treatment was not considered distal embolization for the purpose of the meta-analysis. Other datapoints were captured as defined in the original papers.

Statistical methods

Baseline patient, lesion, and procedural characteristics are described using descriptive statistics, including means, proportions, and weighted averages across studies. A random effects model was used for the meta-analysis of the predefined outcomes (patency, TLR, major amputation, mortality, distal embolization, and provisional stenting) among studies reporting each outcome.17 Meta-analysis rates and 95% confidence intervals were reported. Normal approximation confidence intervals were used in cases when at least 1 value for the number of events contained a noninteger value. Studies reporting outcomes as Kaplan-Meier survival estimates or cumulative incidences were treated as if they reported proportions by multiplying the survival estimate (or cumulative incidence) by the sample size to approximate the number of events. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q-statistic and I2 test; heterogeneity was deemed “substantial” for I2 values ≥50% and “considerable” for I2 values ≥75%.18 Subgroup analyses based on study design, patient and lesion characteristics, and atherectomy class (directional, laser, orbital, rotational) were conducted to assess potential sources of heterogeneity. The class-specific analysis was conducted only for the years 2014 to 2024 to restrict the analysis to currently available devices and contemporary practice techniques. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 and the meta package version 7.0-0.17,19

Risk of bias and quality assessments were conducted for studies contributing data to the quantitative meta-analysis of the predefined outcomes for this analysis. The Cochrane RoB 2 tool was used to evaluate methodological quality and internal validity for randomized studies and is reported as low, some, or high risk of bias.20,21 For observational studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used for quality assessments.22

Results

Systematic literature review and qualitative analysis

A total of 819 nonduplicative records were screened; of those, 323 records that did not report on the use of atherectomy or IVL for percutaneous, infrainguinal, native, human, peripheral artery interventions were excluded during initial screening. The full text was assessed in 496 records, and 148 were excluded for the reasons stated in Figure 1. An additional 26 papers reporting on IVL outcomes were excluded. Twelve were meta-analysis papers (4 including only randomized controlled trials [RCT] and 8 including both RCT and observational studies; Supplemental Table S1). There were 18 unique RCT reported in 19 papers (1 RCT was published in 2 papers), of which 11 were multicenter. In addition, there were 96 prospective observational studies (53 multicenter), 147 retrospective observational studies (41 multicenter), and 48 case reports. Twenty-one of the 147 retrospective observational studies were claims or centralized database analyses (2 MAUDE, 5 Medicare, 2 National Inpatient Sample, and 12 VQI). Thirty-four papers (10.6%) reported the use of at least 1 angiographic or duplex ultrasound core laboratory. First authors’ affiliations included 21 different countries, the most common being the United States (158 papers), Germany (46 papers), China (30 papers), Italy (16 papers), Japan (13 papers), and Turkey (12 papers). The affiliation of the first author was an office-based setting in fewer than 3% of papers, with the remainder in hospital settings.

Meta-analysis of periprocedural and 12-month outcomes

Of the 322 atherectomy papers included in the systematic literature review, 190 papers (59.0%) contributed data to the quantitative meta-analysis of periprocedural or 12-month outcomes (see Supplemental Appendix S2). Papers excluded from the quantitative meta-analysis were 12 meta-analyses, 48 case reports, 17 claims or database papers with overlapping patient cohorts, 30 papers reporting subgroup analyses or sequential follow-up of an already included study, and 25 papers not reporting any of the periprocedural or 12-month outcomes predefined for this analysis (patency, TLR, major amputation, all-cause mortality, distal embolization, or provisional stenting).

Twelve-month outcomes, distal embolization, and provisional stenting are shown in Figure 3. Across all published atherectomy studies, primary patency, TLR, major amputation, and mortality rates were 75.4% (51 studies with 4806 patients), 15.6% (67 studies with 4783 patients), 1.7% (71 studies with 65,160 patients), and 2.8% (63 studies with 63,568 patients), respectively. Both patency and TLR rates tended to be lower in RCT vs observational studies. Major amputation rates were 0.9% in RCT (11 studies with 443 patients) compared to 1.7% in observational studies (59 studies with 4651 patients). Mortality rates also trended lower in RCT (2.2% vs 2.8%). Clinically significant distal embolization was reported in 2.2% of patients overall (159 studies comprising 24,114 patients). Distal embolization was 2.0% (95% CI, 1.3-2.7) in studies using embolic protection devices in <50% of patients and 2.9% (95% CI, 1.9-4.2) in studies using embolic protection devices in 50% of patients or more. Considerable heterogeneity (I2 ≥75%, P < .001) was observed for all of the outcomes of interest (Supplemental Figures S1-S6). Heterogeneity in 12-month outcomes was substantial for RCT (I2 values, 53.4%-73.0%) and considerable (I2 values, 75.7%-88.7%) for observational studies. Risk of bias and quality were assessed in the 190 papers contributing data to the quantitative meta-analysis. Of the 16 RCT contributing data to the quantitative meta-analysis, risk of bias was judged as low in 11 studies and moderate in 5 studies (Supplemental Figure S7). For nonrandomized studies (Supplemental Figure S8), quality assessments as evaluated by the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale were better for comparative studies (average score of 8.5/9.0) than for single-arm studies (range, 7.1-7.4). Funnel plots for the outcomes of interest are provided in Supplemental Figure S9.

Subgroup analyses were conducted for 12-month and periprocedural outcomes (Table 2). Patency and TLR rates following atherectomy use were similar for studies with <50% CLTI patients vs studies with ≥50% CLTI patients; however, major amputation (0.9% vs 4.7%) and mortality (1.9% vs 6.0%) were higher in studies with a greater proportion of CLTI patients. Outcomes were worse for below-the-knee (BTK) interventions vs ATK interventions: TLR (15.1% ATK vs 18.2% BTK), major amputation (0.6% ATK vs 5.7% BTK), and mortality (2.3% ATK vs 5.6% BTK). TLR rates were lower in studies reporting severe calcification in ≥50% of patients or lesions (7.5% in 9 studies with 373 patients) vs studies reporting severe calcification in <50% of patients (12.6% across 24 studies with 2415 patients). Twelve-month outcomes were relatively unaffected by the percentage of patients with total occlusions. The TLR rates were higher in cohorts with a higher proportion of TASC C/D lesions (11.2% vs 19.8%). Provisional stenting rates were lower in BTK data sets (3.4% in 13 studies with 966 patients) than in ATK data sets (10.2% in 86 studies with 6362 patients). Provisional stenting rates more than doubled in patient cohorts with ≥50% total occlusions vs those with <50% total occlusions (15.6% vs 6.5%) and more than tripled in cohorts with a higher proportion Discussion

Since the advent of drug-coated technologies, reintervention rates in randomized trials have dropped to as low as 25.5% at 5 years postprocedure,23 with statistically significant reductions in long-term revascularization rates compared to POBA in both ATK23 and BTK interventions.24 However, because most endovascular treatments modify rather than remove atheroma, vessel recoil, dissection, suboptimal luminal gain, and inadequate stent expansion can impact outcomes. Calcium burden is known to reduce the efficacy of drug-coated technologies,25 and suboptimal residual stenosis is a proven risk factor for increased restenosis risk and patency loss after endovascular interventions.3 Although stents can be used to treat suboptimal results, they carry risks of stent fracture26 and in-stent restenosis.27, 28, 29, 30 In-stent restenosis is particularly difficult to treat and is associated with both a higher restenosis risk than other common risk factors7 and high treatment costs.31 Atherectomy is, therefore, an attractive option to prepare the vessel for definitive treatment by modifying or removing atheroma or calcified plaque, thereby providing better procedural outcomes, reducing the risk of dissection, and reducing the need for provisional stenting.2

This scoping review identified a total of 322 infrainguinal atherectomy publications over a 37-year period, with more than 130,000 unique patients reported. A high level of evidence is represented, with 12 meta-analyses, 19 RCT, and 96 prospective observational papers. Among published atherectomy studies (excluding meta-analyses and case reports), 92.7% were observational and 7.3% were RCT. This ratio is aligned with other interventional practices reported in past meta-analyses, including biomimetic stents (95% observational, 5% RCT),32 coronary drug-eluting stents (89% observational, 11% RCT),33 and coronary artery bypass grafting (92% observational, 8% RCT).34

Heterogeneity in reporting, differing selection criteria, and choice of adjunctive therapies all hinder comparison to meta-analyses of other established technologies; nonetheless, the outcomes observed for atherectomy appear aligned to standards of care and, in some cases, are better than reported outcomes for POBA. The 12-month patency rate for atherectomy in this meta-analysis (75.4%) is higher than reported rates for POBA, both ATK (47.4% in 10 RCT)35 and BTK (63.1% in 19 RCT and observational studies),36 as well as for DCB ATK (67.6% in observational studies and 67.9% in RCT).35 The meta-analyzed 12-month TLR rate in the current study (15.6%) is also lower than previously observed for POBA ATK (26.0% in 13 RCT)35 but similar to BTK rates for POBA (18.5%, 3 RCT, 200 patients total).35 Amputation rates in the atherectomy dataset are low at 12 months (1.7% overall) compared to 1 meta-analysis of 37 RCT and observational studies on POBA (14.9%). The atherectomy RCT amputation rate of 0.9% is aligned with the <1% amputation rates typically reported in RCT of DCB and POBA.35 Mortality rates are also low and aligned with prior literature, ranging from 2.1% for DCB ATK to 15.1% for POBA BTK.35, 36, 37

The provisional stenting rates for atherectomy in the current meta-analysis are lower than reported meta-analysis rates for POBA (15.2% ATK and 9.1% BTK),35,36 and DCB without atherectomy (9.7%-20.2%).35 This meta-analysis therefore supports the use of atherectomy as an adjunctive vessel preparation tool prior to definitive treatment in order to achieve better procedural results and thereby reduce the need for a permanent scaffold.35

Subgroup analyses based on study design, patient and lesion characteristics, and device class were conducted to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity. Considerable heterogeneity was observed for all of the outcomes of interest, with expectedly higher heterogeneity in observational studies than in RCT. Effectiveness outcomes were relatively well-balanced across a variety of higher-risk patient and lesion profiles whereas safety outcomes showed greater variability. Studies enrolling more CLTI patients reported expectedly higher rates of major amputation and mortality. BTK cohorts also had higher rates of major amputation and mortality than ATK cohorts, but low rates overall (5.7% major amputation, 5.6% mortality) given the high risk profile of the BTK population. Surprisingly, higher proportions of severe calcification did not have a major impact on outcomes, and in fact, 12-month TLR was lower in cohorts with a greater prevalence of severe calcification (12.6% vs 7.5%). Disease severity (CLTI vs claudication) and location (BTK vs ATK) likely explain variations across studies, with a lesser impact of calcification severity. This might suggest that atherectomy is effective in modifying calcium to improve procedural outcomes, although the number of patients in the highly calcified cohort was relatively small (373 patients across 9 studies).

Choice of atherectomy device also likely contributed to heterogeneity across studies. Although the current analysis focused on atherectomy as a whole, an examination of class-specific results may help to inform current practice. Directional atherectomy was the most commonly studied device overall, although rotational atherectomy publications have increased in recent years, along with an increase in laser atherectomy publications. Current practice utilization patterns favor specific devices and the drivers for device choice may include economic, speed, ease of use, access, familiarity, and plaque morphology. Given these factors, as well as variations in imaging use (eg, intravascular ultrasound) and adjunctive therapies (POBA or DCB), comparison of results between atherectomy classes across inherently noncomparative studies must be interpreted with caution. In general, outcomes were similar across the 4 classes of devices. There were no safety signals for any class and provisional stenting rates were low. Rotational atherectomy had the highest patency rate, whereas TLR, amputation, and mortality rates trended lower for directional atherectomy. The major amputation rate trended lower for directional atherectomy. Outcomes for laser and orbital atherectomy trended higher than directional and rotational, although small sample sizes limit the interpretation of these results. Directional atherectomy had a lower rate of provisional stenting compared to laser, orbital, and rotational atherectomy.

Few studies have directly compared atherectomy classes. In separate VQI analyses, Bai et al38 and Sharma et al39 observed no statistically significant differences in procedural outcomes between directional, laser, and orbital atherectomy for tibial disease; however, long-term patency, reintervention, and major amputation rates were lower for directional atherectomy. Bai et al38 further examined isolated femoropopliteal disease and reported higher perforation rates and lower embolization rates for directional atherectomy compared to laser and orbital, with no differences at 1 year. Noory et al40 retrospectively compared directional atherectomy to front-cutting (rotational or excimer laser) atherectomy and reported 1-year reintervention rates of 19.5% and 29.4%, respectively (P = .023), possibly related to the more common use of front-cutting devices for restenosis and in-stent restenosis. This significant difference was maintained at 2 years.40 Only 1 randomized study to date has directly compared atherectomy devices. Babaev et al41 in 2022 compared directional and orbital atherectomy, both followed by DCB, for symptomatic femoropopliteal lesions and reported a greater reduction in plaque volume and reduced postprocedural angiographic stenosis for the directional arm vs the orbital group. The 3-year results showed lower rates of restenosis and revascularization in the directional arm, particularly in nonstented patients.42 Future studies will need to examine which atherectomy strategy optimizes lumen gain and drug penetration while minimizing permanent scaffolding. The observed heterogeneity in outcomes between atherectomy device types in the current study also emphasizes the need to evaluate them separately.

Although the findings of the current meta-analysis are encouraging, there is always a role for more prospective comparative evidence. The largest prospective, core laboratory adjudicated study to date, DEFINITIVE LE (Determination of EFfectiveness of the SilverHawk PerIpheral Plaque ExcisioN System [SIlverHawk Device] for the Treatment of Infrainguinal VEssels/Lower Extremities), enrolled 800 patients treated with directional atherectomy but did not include a comparator arm.43 The 18 unique RCT included in this systematic review enrolled fewer than 800 atherectomy patients combined, with the largest (EXCITE ISR [EXCImer Laser Randomized Controlled Study for Treatment of FemoropopliTEal In-Stent Restenosis]) enrolling 169 atherectomy patients,44 emphasizing the need for more large-scale RCT. Nevertheless, of the 15 published RCT that compared a commercially available atherectomy device to a nonatherectomy control, 11 (73.3%) demonstrated improved outcomes for atherectomy on at least 1 key outcome, including superior technical or procedural success,8,44 fewer periprocedural complications,44 lower dissection rates,8,45 reduced need for bailout stenting,46, 47, 48 improved patency,45,49, 50, 51, 52, 53 and reduced TLR.44,48,50,51 Thus, this suggestive pattern will need to be validated in large-scale RCT. The cost of atherectomy must also be taken into consideration, especially when the added clinical benefit of atherectomy might appear minimal compared to definitive therapy alone. However, although the incremental cost of procedures is higher when atherectomy is used, several studies have shown that atherectomy is associated with cost savings over time because of the less frequent use of stents, and reduced need for repeat interventions, amputation, and end-of-life care.54,55

Future directions

Future trials should address the impact of plaque modification with atherectomy in the context of evolving drug delivery solutions with DCB, drug-eluting stents, and bioabsorbable devices. Although disease manifestation throughout the lower extremity vascular beds is pleomorphic and heterogeneous, there is a need for comparative evaluations to identify and define lesion subsets in which specific atherectomy modalities are best suited for plaque modification and lumen gain. As atherectomy devices continue to evolve, it is important to support newer technology with trials, registries, and other evidence-generation means. Evaluations of the cost-effectiveness and accretive value of atherectomy outcomes with longer-term studies will also better inform value-based decision-making.

Limitations

Nonclinically relevant embolism was not captured as it was inconsistently reported across studies. Longer-term follow-up is desirable; however, due to the smaller number of studies that published outcomes between 2 and 5 years, there were not enough data for the meta-analysis to allow an assessment of longer-term results. This analysis of 12-month outcomes, however, does allow comparisons within and across interventional specialty care, with cumulatively high levels of evidence (RCT and prospective multicenter studies) to guide care choices. This review was also limited in its ability to compare classes of devices across studies, with few prospective comparative atherectomy device trials available. Device selection is often based on lesion morphologic features, which are difficult to assess outside of randomized studies that include core laboratory description and adjudication of lesion characteristics. Iterations of legacy atherectomy devices and newer technologies (more recently available for use) make evidence-based decisions challenging for the operator as devices and the ability to treat more complex lesions and territories evolve. This analysis is unable to provide firm conclusions regarding the outcomes following atherectomy as a standalone procedure, given that the majority of included studies used adjunctive therapies. Finally, although observational studies tend to represent more real-world lesion and patient types, heterogeneous patient selection and outcome reporting make conclusions across these diverse studies challenging.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis is the first to provide a comprehensive overview of the atherectomy literature, demonstrating an absence of safety signals and efficacy rates that are within accepted standards. With over 300 published papers including the highest levels of evidence, this literature review supports the use of atherectomy in appropriately selected patients as part of the endovascular treatment algorithm for peripheral artery disease.
