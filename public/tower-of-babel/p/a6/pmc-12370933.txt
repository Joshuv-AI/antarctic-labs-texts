Disaster treatment and multisource monitoring of rockbursts in isolated island coal face under hard roof conditions
Authors: Mengchen Yun, Jianxi Ren, Liang Zhang, Fengjing Guo, Xu Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRockbursts are common dynamic phenomena in coal mining and have become a prominent topic in the mining industry. This study examines the isolated island coal face in an extra-thick, fully mechanized top-coal caving operation in deep burial conditions, using it as a representative case. A comprehensive pressure relief strategy was employed at the coal face, incorporating directional roof blasting and loose roof blasting techniques. These techniques facilitated the pre-cracking and fracturing of the hard roof, addressing the issue of excessive suspended roof areas and high rock pressure affecting the roadway. Furthermore, a multi-source monitoring and evaluation method was developed to assess the effectiveness of the pressure relief measures. This method was based on the evolution of the rock loosening zone, stress distribution, displacement, and microseismic events. The study demonstrates that this approach can accurately evaluate the conditions at the coal mining face, providing valuable data to inform decision-making and enabling timely implementation of prevention and control measures.Keywords: Isolated island coal face, Pressure relief blasting, Mine pressure, Multi-source monitoring, Safety evaluation

Introduction

Rockbursts is a dynamic phenomenon that occur during mining and represent one of the major global hazards in mining1. Their occurrence is closely associated with the sudden and vigorous release of elastic energy. This substantial energy release is often accompanied by intense mine tremors and, in severe cases, can result in the destruction of coal face tunnels and cause injuries or fatalities to underground workers2–5.

Deep burial, hard roof strata, and isolated island mining are often considered the primary contributing factors which trigger rockbursts6–11. Most previous studies have primarily focused on the mechanisms, prediction methods, and prevention of rockbursts. These studies typically employ theoretical and experimental approaches12–16, including mechanism analysis17–20, laboratory experiments21–25, and numerical simulations26–29. The absence of field engineering applications in these theories has led coal mine support designs continuing to rely largely on empirical or semi-empirical methods. These methods often fail to provide precise guidance for on-site disaster control measures, presenting a significant challenge to rockburst management. Therefore, further refined research and optimization based on on-site practice are necessary. At sites where rockbursts have already occurred, the common approach involves a combination of active and passive pressure relief methods. Active pressure relief methods include ‘intensive support’ and ‘strong pressure relief’ measures30–34. Techniques such as borehole imaging and seismic monitoring are used to determine the extent and pattern of roof disintegration in highly stressed mining roadways35,36. Huang37 and Konicek38 employed hydraulic fracturing and deep hole blasting techniques to optimize the stress distribution in the surrounding rock. Additionally, passive pressure relief is typically achieved by adjusting mining and roadway layouts. For example, Mo et al.39 addressed the floor heave phenomenon under the longwall mining conditions by increasing the mining height and reinforcing the floor, achieving favorable field test results. Bai40 investigated the deformation mechanism of the roadway roof under the influence of overrun and lateral support pressure, while Jiang41 studied the optimization effect of different coal pillar widths on surrounding rock stress in roadway. Previous studies have primarily focused on the mechanism, prediction, and prevention of rockbursts, leading to certain achievements that have been successfully applied in mining practice42,43. Nevertheless, rock burst disasters remain a highly complex and technically demanding challenge. Although theoretical research has progressed, practical engineering applications remain limited. As a result, rock burst disaster management still largely relies on empirical and semi-empirical approaches, lacking a robust scientific foundation and comprehensive assessment systems.

In view of this, it is urgent need to establish an evaluation system for disaster management effectiveness. Comprehensive monitoring and timely feedback on-site disasters will create a vital link between theoretical research and field operations. This approach supports effective management and mitigation of rockburst disasters. This study focuses on the N.4206 isolated island coal face at Jianxin Coal Mine in northwest China characterized by a high burial depth and frequent rockburst occurrences. Two blasting-based pressure relief measures were proposed, and a multi-source monitoring and evaluation method was developed. The method’s effectiveness was validated through engineering practice, successfully addressing the encountered problems. The findings offer technical support for managing similar disasters in the region.

Fundamental information and disaster introduction in the study area

Fundamental information

Jianxin Coal Mine, located in the Huangling mining area of Northwest China, has an annual production capacity of 4 million tons. It commenced operations in October 2012 and has since played a significant role in regional coal production. The N.4206 coal face is the first isolated island working face in the Huangling mine area. Successful extraction of this coal face will not only significantly enhance the production efficiency of Jianxin Coal Mine but also offer valuable insights for the exploitation of other isolated coal seams in the region.

The study area is located in the eastern section of the No. 4–2 panel. It is bounded by the LW4204 goaf, is the LW4208 goaf to the east, the northern boundary of the 4–2 panel to the north, and the coal pillar for the auxiliary transport roadway to the south (Fig. 1a). The area has a strike length of 1,791.5 m, a panel face length of 200 m, and a maximum mining depth of 738 m (Fig. 1b). The parameters of the coal seam are listed in Table 1. The overlying roof of the coal seam consists of fine sandstone, medium-grained sandstone, and siltstone, while the floor mainly consists of carbonaceous mudstone and siltstone (Fig. 1c). The basic physico-mechanical properties of rocks from different layers in the borehole were tested, and their stress–strain curves are presented in Fig. 1d. The strength of the rock layers near the bottom ranges from 23.63 MPa to 48.29 MPa, with the fine sandstone-1 layer in the roof exhibiting the highest strength of 48.29 MPa. The basic properties of the rock layers are summarized in Table 2. The geological conditions of the coal mining face are more complex than those at the surface. The coal seam exhibits significant variations in deposition, a hard roof, and is affected by structural features, making it highly susceptible to rockbursts.

The specific manifestations of the hazards in the study area are presented in Fig. 2. The cross-sectional design dimensions for the transport roadway at the coal face are 5800 mm × 4400 mm (width × height), while those of the air roadway are 5200 mm × 4400 mm (width × height). During mining operations at a depth of 500 m, slight mining pressure was observed, resulting in minor floor heaving. This floor uplift was minimal and had a negligible impact on production activities. However, at a depth of 800 m, pronounced and persistent ground pressure was observed. The surrounding rock of the roadway exhibited significant stress, leading to severe convergence deformation. The roof and sidewalls underwent significant deformation, accompanied by severe floor heave and frequent rockburst occurrences. Due to intense ground pressure, the roadway underwent substantia deformation and required the combination support using single hydraulic props with steel beams. However, this support method significantly reduced the available workspace for on-site personnel. As shown in Fig. 2a, the narrowest section of the transport roadway measured approximately 2800 mm by 2200 mm (width × height). The lowest height of the return air roadway was approximately 1700 mm (Fig. 2b), reducing the cross-sectional area by more than 50%. Figure 2c shows the floor heave, which reached up to 850 mm. Figure 2d shows ongoing large deformations in the rock walls, accompanied by anchor cable failure and detachment. In certain areas, pillar spalling and localized roof falls were observed, posing a serious threat to production operations and worker safety.

Analysis of disaster factors

The disaster records and fundamental data indicate that the primary mechanisms driving large deformation and strong mining pressure in the surrounding rock can be divided categorized as bearing capacity failure, buckling, and swelling44–46. The retreat mining speed at the coal face was slow, with significant burial depth and high roof and mining pressures. The roadway exhibited considerable deformation along both sidewalls, noticeable roof subsidence, floor heave, anchor cable breakage, and a substantial reduction in cross-sectional area. During the retreat mining of the coal face, the roof rock layer structure exhibited an 'O-X' shaped fracture pattern along the horizontal strata. Both sides of the isolated working face are abandoned areas. The overlying rock strata form a vertical bridging structure above the working face (red area in Fig. 3). After the retreat mining of the adjacent N.4208 and N.4204 coal faces, a large goaf area formed, with the supporting pressure from the overlying rock layers gradually transferring to the coal seam. This led to the development of a plastic zone in the coal pillars, resulting in a larger influence range and higher stress concentration at the coal face. Under the influence of secondary mining, the strong mining pressure at the coal face became more pronounced.

Disaster countermeasures

Based on the overlying rock conditions and mining pressure characteristics at the coal face, directional and loose roof blasting were employed for presplitting. The objective was to sever the hanging roof and alleviate stress concentration within the coal body. Simultaneously, a set of integrated monitoring techniques was implemented, including digital borehole imaging, roadway convergence measurements, stress monitoring, and microseismic surveillance. The countermeasures process is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Countermeasure flowchart and blasting parameters.

The multi-source monitoring approach includes mine pressure monitoring, surrounding rock characteristic monitoring, and microseismic monitoring. The monitoring equipment is equipped with automatic data collection and wireless transmission capabilities. The monitoring measures are shown in Fig. 5, and number of perimeter rock deformation monitoring sensors is listed in Table 3. This includes the support pressure monitoring area located on the hydraulic support, the surrounding rock characteristic monitoring area, and the microseismic monitoring area situated on both sides of the roadway. Microseismic monitoring is primarily carried out by arranging four sensors at both the transport and return air declines. The sensors are spaced 150 m apart and positioned at both the top and bottom plates of the roadway. Surrounding rock monitoring is divided into six sections, spaced 200 m apart, categorized into force monitoring, deformation monitoring, and borehole inspection. Each monitoring section is equipped with eight anchor force monitors, four anchor cable force monitors, one displacement monitoring section for surrounding rock, and one borehole inspection section, as illustrated in Fig. 5.

Results and discussion

Analysis of digital imaging in observation holes before and after blasting

The digital borehole images before and after blasting are shown in Fig. 6. The total depth of the borehole was 26 m. New fractures were observed at depths of 7.1 ~ 7.9 m, and 13.6 m, with minor seepage noted at the fracture at 16.7 m after the blasting. This indicated that a continuous fractured zone had formed in the roof, with the surrounding rock acting as a buffering layer and creating a pressure relief zone. It transferred the support peak pressure deeper into the surrounding rock and cut off the stress transmission path between the coal and rock mass.

Digital borehole histogram before and after blasting.

Variations in rock pressure

The 108 hydraulic supports along the coal face were divided into three monitoring areas. Continuous tracking and monitoring were conducted over 90 days, spanning both the pre- and post-blasting periods (Fig. 7). Before blasting, support pressures were primarily distributed between 10 and 40 MPa, with several instances exceeding the warning threshold of 42 MPa. After blasting, support pressure significantly decreased and was mainly distributed between 10 and 28 MPa. Only two instances exceeding 30 MPa, with values of 34 MPa and 32 MPa, both remaining well below the high-pressure warning threshold of 42 MPa.

Variation law of support force in 4206 coal face.

Variations in surrounding rock

The roadway convergence monitoring results are shown in Fig. 8. As the step distance of blasting pressure relief decreased, the range of both roadways affected by advancing mining also decreased, from approximately 200 m to 80 m. Roadway convergence gradually decreased. Roof and floor movement decreased by 42.3%, from 1300 to 750 mm. The movement speed decreased from 120 mm/d to 17 mm/d. Lateral movement decreased by 35.9%, from 780 to 500 mm, with the movement speed reduced from 80 mm/d to 11.5 mm/d.

By comparing and analyzing the roadway convergence and surrounding rock stress monitoring at the coal face during pressure relief blasting, it was evident that the range of advance impact on the coal face caused by blasting pressure relief significantly decreased as the step distance for pressure relief was reduced. As a result, the difficulty of advancing maintenance in the two roadways was substantially alleviated.

Microseismic monitoring variation patterns

The seismometers were installed at 150 m intervals in two roadways at the coal face, arranged to form a spatial envelope covering the entire monitoring area. Data were collected over a 60-day period during the blasting pressure relief operation, as shown in Table 4 and Fig. 10. Before blasting pressure relief, there were 3783 microseismic events including 1590 micro events (E < 103 J), 1403 small events (103 J ≤ E < 104 J), 778 medium events (104 J ≤ E < 105 J), and 10 large events (E ≥ 105 J). After blasting pressure relief, there were 2377 microseismic events including 1212 micro events, 703 small events, 456 medium events, and 6 or more large events. Prior to blasting pressure relief, the average frequency of microseismic events was 122 events per day. After blasting pressure relief, this frequency decreased to 76.7 events per day, representing a 37.1% reduction. Specifically, the frequency of larger microseismic events decreased from 25.4 events per day to 14.9 events per day, a reduction of 41.3%. Additionally, the average energy of medium and larger microseismic events decreased by 35%, from 1.27 × 106 J to 8.25 × 105 J.

This further confirmed that the blasting measures adopted were highly effective in relieving pressure.

Discussion

As a globally significant energy resource, coal mining depths are increasing worldwide at a rate of 10–20 m per year, advancing toward greater depths and consequently causing a series of dynamic disasters in deep mining environments. Classic theories explaining rock burst mechanisms include the strength theory, stiffness theory, energy theory, and rock burst tendency theory, originally proposed by researchers from Germany, Poland, the Soviet Union, and other countries47–51. These theories have been instrumental in the prevention and control of rock burst disasters. Nevertheless, some phenomena observed in practice remain unexplained. For instance, stress concentration zones frequently develop during coal mining, with intensities often meeting criteria for rock burst occurrence; however, significant rock deformation may occur without triggering rock bursts. Similarly, coal seams with identical geological conditions and rock burst propensity experience rock bursts only in localized areas. Consequently, recent studies categorize the primary factors contributing to rock burst disasters into internal factors (the inherent rock burst tendency of the coal-rock mass) and external factors (force sources such as high static loads and strong disturbances, as well as geological structures like abrupt slip planes)52–54. A preliminary integrated regional and local technical system for rock burst prediction, monitoring, and early warning has been established, demonstrating promising disaster prediction capabilities. However, there remains a shortage of comprehensive engineering practice cases to further validate these systems.

This study primarily employed blasting methods in mine engineering practice. The results demonstrate that combining directional roof blasting with loose roof blasting effectively alleviates mine pressure and significantly reduces the frequency of intense vibrations and rock bursts within the mining area. Employing multi-source monitoring methods enhances the accuracy of disaster-related data, thereby improving the timeliness and precision of disaster response.

Although this study provides a theoretical foundation and practical insights for rockburst disaster management, further optimization is required. For instance, hydraulic fracturing, another pressure relief method, warrants further investigation. Additionally, future research should focus on enhancing the multi-source monitoring system under complex geological conditions. Furthermore, a more refined disaster assessment system should be developed by integrating additional mine data, aiming to provide more scientific and accurate disaster prevention and control strategies for various mining environments, thereby advancing the development of intelligent mines.

Conclusions

Due to the deep burial, hard roof conditions, and isolated coal faces, high mining pressure and large deformations occurred in the roadways during the development process, leading to rockburst disasters. The combination of directional roof blasting and loose roof blasting pressure relief at the coal face effectively fractures the hard roof and creates a reliable fractured zone. This disrupts the transmission of high stress to the coal mining face, solving the problem of strong mining pressure in the isolated island coal face. The detailed conclusions are as follows:

Compared to traditional single blasting methods, directional roof blasting and loose roof blasting offer better pressure relief effects and more significant advantages, minimizing the need for tunnel repairs to the greatest extent.
