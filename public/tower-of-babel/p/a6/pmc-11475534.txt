Detecting spatial clusters of human scabies in Tigray, Ethiopia from 2018 to 2023
Authors: Akeza Awealom Asgedom, Micheale Hagos Debesay, Chigozie Louisa J Ugwu, Woldegebriel Assefa Woldegerima
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScabies is one of public health concerns among communicable disease in Ethiopia, especially among disadvantaged and the poor. This current study aims to detect the spatiotemporal patterns of scabies in Tigray from 2018 to 2023 using scabies data aggregated at the zonal level. The study also examined the persistent patterns in the spatial variation of scabies incidence across the administrative regions during the study period.MethodWe collected scabies data using a weekly disease surveillance reporting format of the country from 2018 to 2023 across all accessible district health facilities in Tigray region, Ethiopia. We conducted retrospective analyses using both purely spatial and spatiotemporal scan statistic approaches, employing a discrete Poisson probability model to identify statistically significant clusters of high scabies rates throughout the Tigray regional zones in Ethiopia. Our methodology involved the use of Kulldorff’s spatial scan statistic software (SaTScan v10.1.3), R programming software version 4.3.1, and ArcGIS Pro for all analyses.ResultsA total of 101,116 cases of scabies were reported from 2018 to 2023. Our study indicated a spatial heterogeneity in the pattern of scabies across Tigray region as well as its localization among geographically contiguous zones across space, except for the Western zone of Tigray where no data was collected. The detected statistically significant spatial clusters persisted mainly in the Central, Eastern and Northwestern zones of Tigray over the six years of the study period. The highest relative risk (RR) was recorded in year 2021 (. The central zone had the major clusters of scabies at district level from 2018 to 2023. The heterogeneous distribution of scabies across Tigray could be due to the spatial variations in the determinants of scabies (such as socioeconomic status, demographics, and material deprivation) across the region.ConclusionAn enormous burden of scabies was reported over a period of six years. The present study found localized clusters of high scabies rates at district and zonal levels in Tigray, Ethiopia, possibly due to differences in various determinants of scabies such as access to WASH services. The findings could help the government and health authorities to develop and implement scabies control strategies in Tigray, with a focus on high-risk districts and zones to ensure optimal resource allocation.Keywords: Scabies, Tigray Ethiopia, Cluster analysis, Spatial scan statistics, Relative risk

Introduction

Human scabies is an infestation of the skin by a tiny mite called Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis, characterized by itching and a rash [1]. This condition often leads to a secondary bacterial skin infection known as impetigo and it is transmitted through close personal contact [1, 2]. Even though it is easily treatable disease, its prevalence is high and continuous as neglected tropical disease of resource-poor settings, and mostly affects children, adolescents and old age populations [2, 3].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), scabies affect over 190 million people at any time and more than 390 million people annually [1, 4]. Scabies predominantly impacts the world’s most vulnerable populations, particularly those in crowded and impoverished environments, including Pacific island communities, Latin American favelas, remote rural areas in Africa and Australia, and displaced populations in camps [1, 5]. In these resource-limited settings, the treatment of scabies and its complications places a substantial financial burden on healthcare systems [5, 6]. In wealthier nations, although cases are infrequent, outbreaks in healthcare facilities and among vulnerable communities can lead to considerable economic costs for national health services [7, 8].

Human scabies has a significant worldwide impact, thus the WHO included it in their 2020 roadmap for neglected tropical diseases [9]. This integration is a strategic attempt to solve the Sustainable Development Goals’ neglect of such conditions by 2030 [9]. The roadmap aims to achieve these worldwide goals by concentrating on an integrated strategy to control and manage neglected tropical diseases associated to the skin [9, 10].

Among communicable diseases in Ethiopia, scabies is a major public health concern that especially affects vulnerable populations including the poor, migrants, and street people [11]. According to epidemiological reports, Ethiopia has been experiencing scabies outbreaks since 2015, and with contradictory reports, the prevalence of scabies infestation in the nation ranges greatly from 2.5 to 78% [11, 12]. Ethiopia has reacted to the increasing burden of human scabies by putting into place a number of legislative initiatives aimed at lowering the scabies burden in high risk areas [11]. In Ethiopia, areas vulnerable to drought, human scabies has been recognized as a notifiable disease and its elimination is also the goal of the national strategic plan for Neglected Tropical Diseases (NTDs) for the years 2021–2025 [13]. Furthermore, scabies is among the weekly reportable diseases in Ethiopia.

Research on factors associated with scabies has somewhat largely been conducted among in Ethiopia [14–16], while fewer studies have been conducted in hospital settings [15, 17]. Many of these studies did not employ a robust design. Literature on spatial analysis of human scabies in Tigray Region is also lacking [18, 19]. The goal of this study was to detect the significant spatial clusters of scabies across the Tigray regional states using spatiotemporal analysis.

Spatial epidemiology has been widely used in infectious diseases in recent years to analyze association between distribution of a disease and change in different regions based on monitoring data [20]. Studies from Iran [21], Syria [22–24], Spain [25] and South Korea [26] have indicated a spatial clustering of human scabies at the national and county levels. This spatial clustering helps to apply context based interventions.

Thus far, there is no published study on the spatial and spatiotemporal analysis of human scabies in Tigray regional state of Ethiopia. Therefore, we conducted a spatial, and spatiotemporal analysis of human scabies incidence at the zonal-level in Tigray region of Ethiopia from 2018 to 2023, to provide more useful epidemiological information for policymaking towards reducing human scabies in Tigray regional districts, particularly in high-risk areas for SDG 2030 Agenda [1, 9].

Materials and methods

Study area

Tigray regional state (Fig. 1) is in the northernmost part of Ethiopia with a latitude and longitude of about 14° 08’ 11.67” N, and 38° 18’ 33.57” E, respectively and a total area of 41,410 km² [27]. The region has a total population of approximately 5.98 million as at the time of Census 2016 [28, 29]. In Tigray more than 84% of the population live in the rural areas and over 80% are farmers [27, 30]. This study was in all the zones of Tigray regional state, except in western Tigray due to security reasons (Fig. 1).

Geographic location of the study area, Tigray; Tigray is in the Northern part of Ethiopia

Prior to the war Tigray had a well-founded health system and the region was one of the best performers among the regions and have shown a remarkable improvement in the health system performance [31]. The region is prone to drought and access to basic water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) services is also not promising [32].

Data source and processing

The number of human scabies cases was obtained retrospectively from surveillance system of district health offices in Tigray Ethiopia over the period of 2018 to 2023 (6 years). From 2018 to 2023, scabies data were reported by health institutions to the district health offices monthly. The health facilities/institutions recorded scabies cases based complete structured weekly disease reporting format. Collected data was checked for completeness before the analysis.

Ethiopia administrative level 0–3 boundary shapefiles were downloaded from the database of the National Basic Geographic Information System [27]. We calculated the incidence rate of scabies in the six out of the seven zones of Tigray due to inconsistent data from the western zone mainly from 2021 to 2023 due to inaccessibility as a result of insecurity. The ArcGIS Pro software [33], was used to construct a geographic information database of scabies incidence rate (per 100, 000 population based on the 2016 census population) in each study area, including the name, code, latitude, and longitude, with the administrative division code as the matching field associated with the vector map (boundary shapefile).

Scabies rate and trends

The incidence rate of scabies was calculated as the total number of scabies cases per 100, 000 population (2016 census population for each year) and were plotted at the zonal level and geographically to observe yearly trend of scabies distribution in the study area (except in the war western zone of Tigray due to inconsistent and unavailability of data). Subsequently, we imported the scabies incidence data into SaTScan software, which was pre-processed to include geographic coordinates and case counts for each location within Tigray, Ethiopia. We applied the purely spatial scan statistic, configuring the analysis to scan the study area using circular windows of variable sizes, which covered different geographical regions.

Spatial and spatiotemporal clusters of scabies in Tigray

Spatial scan statistics (purely spatial statistic), a key method for detecting disease clusters, identifies and assesses the statistical significance of localized clusters in space that cannot be accounted for by the null hypothesis of complete spatial randomness defined by a classical underlying probability model [34]. The spatial scan statistics [35], is a maximum likelihood ratio test statistic based on the numerous variable-size circular windows that scans distribution of diseases in the entire study region. Spatial scan statistics methods have been widely used in the field of epidemiological studies, demography and disease surveillance for cluster detection [36]. Each window was evaluated using a maximum likelihood ratio test, comparing the observed number of scabies cases within the window to the expected number under the null hypothesis of complete spatial randomness.

The relative risk (RR) is the estimated scabies risk within a given location divided by the scabies risk outside a given location (that is, in all other areas). For instance, if a location has a RR of 2.7, then the population within that specific location or district are 2.7 times more likely to be vulnerable to scabies infection. The relative risk of a given cluster is estimated as (observed scabies cases/expected cases) divided by the risk of scabies outside the cluster. All the spatial analyses were implemented in SaTScan v10.1.3 [37] and the spatial maps were plotted using the ArcGIS Pro [33].

Data quality control

The collected data was checked for completeness before the actual analysis.

Spatial clusters of scabies from 2018 to 2023 using Kulldorff’s spatial scan statistic

We utilized SaTScan to identify statistically significant clusters of human scabies within the zonal-level regions of Tigray. Our analysis pinpointed significant clusters where the observed cases of scabies exceeded the expected numbers. This method serves as a local indicator of spatial associations across districts within each zone, highlighting statistically significant outliers. The neighborhoods within these clusters should be specifically targeted for outreach programs, including scabies screening and resource distribution. Such targeted efforts are crucial for the control, prevention, and management of skin-related neglected tropical diseases, aligning with the WHO’s Sustainable Development Goals agenda for 2030 [9].

The number of statistically significant high-rate clusters of scabies detected by spatial scan statistics in 2018 was two (Fig. 5), including Aksum, Maychew, Alamata, Mekelle, Shire, and Adigrat districts across Central, Southern, Mekelle, North west and Eastern zones of Tigray.

Discussion

The present study applied purely spatial scan statistic to evaluate the spatial patterns of scabies from 2018 to 2023 at the district and zonal level in Tigray Ethiopia. The result of the study indicated statistically significant local clustering of high rates of scabies across the zones. The result agree with some studies that human scabies has a heterogeneous distribution in local districts [18, 43, 44], and may have district/local-specific influence [13, 14, 45].

From the result of the spatial analysis using Kulldorff’s purely spatial scan statistic, statistically significant scabies cluster was found to vary in size and location over the years (2018–2023). The clustering was evident across the Central, Northwestern, Eastern, Mekelle, Southeastern and Southern zones, except for the Western zone that was not included in the study due to inconsistent and no data (occupied by non-Tigray forces). The most spatially correlated clusters were concentrated in Central zone.

The most likely clusters (that is the primary clusters) indicated a shift in the scabies spatial pattern throughout the study period, with high scabies correlated clusters located across the Northwestern and Eastern in 2023, Eastern in 2021, Central in 2020, Central in 2019, Central and Southern in 2018 influenced by the local districts in each zone. Notably year 2019 recorded the highest risk ratio of scabies rates, while no spatially correlated cluster was observed in 2022 across the zones, although we detected statistically significant high rates of scabies in the zones. In this study, certain districts were part of top scabies clustering in the zones within the study period such as Mekelle, Samre, Asgede, Fireweyni, Adigrat, Hintalo, Adwa and Maychew districts. The observed discrepancy in scabies in different zones of Tigray could be due to the different WASH services as indicated in a previous study [32].

According to several studies, poverty and household environmental factors contribute to scabies occurrence [15, 19, 46], which is consistent to the fact that local factors may have huge contribution to scabies high risk in Tigray region. Scabies as widespread contagious skin disease is mostly prevalent in humanitarian crises and armed conflicts [47]. This is due to the reduced access for water, sanitation and hygiene services as a result of the extensive damage of WASH facilities during the war in Tigray [32]. In addition, many people were displaced and living in overcrowded makeshift settlements with limited access to food, health, water and sanitation services [48]. This could lead to a high scabies burden [49], especially among children, adolescents and the elderly [4]. Furthermore, Crisis-related problems such as lack of hygienic, soap and water, overcrowding and sharing of clothing could exacerbate the ease spread of scabies mostly in the displaced IDP families share beds and clothing, which is a crucial factor in the spread of the disease [50–52]. Studies have also indicated that regions with similar system input in disease prevention and control strategies but differ geographically in different environmental and socioeconomic indicators also different in scabies control and resource allocation [44, 53, 54]. The zonal variation in socioeconomic, demographic, behavioral, environmental, and cultural characteristics may as well influence spatial distribution of scabies cases in Tigray. However, no clear relationship was established regarding the spatial distribution and possible influential factors across Tigray zones in this study. Therefore, there is need for further studies focusing on understanding and detecting factors that contribute to localized clustering of high rates of scabies at district or zonal levels in Tigray, Ethiopia.

Strength and limitations

This study leveraged spatial analysis to investigate the clustering of scabies across Tigray. It appears to be the first to use spatial scan statistics for studying the spatial epidemiology of scabies in the region from 2018 to 2023. The insights gained are crucial for supporting the Sustainable Development Goals Agenda 2030. Nonetheless, the study has several limitations that should be acknowledged: [1] potential shortcomings due to limited and possibly underreported scabies data in Ethiopia [2], the exclusion of the Western zone of Tigray from the analysis (2021–2023 data not available) due to the ongoing conflict [3], the absence of risk factor analysis and causal inferences regarding scabies risk [4], due to lack of population data, the study utilized the 2016 Ethiopian calendar population projection data as the most correct population of each zone and regions in Ethiopia. Another possible limitation is the use of circular scanning windows, which may not effectively detect irregular scabies clusters. It is important to note that this paper exclusively examined the spatial patterns of scabies from 2018 to 2023, without considering contributing factors. In future research, we plan to incorporate multiple factors to develop a comprehensive model of their impact on the incidence rate of scabies in Tigray, Ethiopia. Despite these challenges, the findings offer important information on the regional spatial distribution of scabies in Tigray, Ethiopia, which could inform effective control and prevention strategies.

Conclusion

The present study detected localized spatial clusters of high rates of scabies in Tigray, Ethiopia at district and zonal levels. The study also detected the spatial patterns of scabies across the zones of Tigray and identified high risk areas. The detected spatial patterns were heterogeneous and local clusters were majorly spread across districts in Central, Northwestern, Eastern, Mekelle, Southeastern and Southern zones. The spatial heterogeneity distribution of scabies in Tigray could be due to zonal variation in different determinants of scabies such as access to WASH services.

Therefore, the identification of local high-risk districts for scabies is invaluable for understanding the drivers of scabies at the regional level. The results might help the government and health authorities to develop and implement scabies control strategies in Tigray, with a focus on high-risk districts and zones to ensure optimal resource allocation.
