Deep Learning–Based Quantification of Vitreous Hyperreflective Foci as a Biomarker for Intraocular Inflammation
Authors: Yaniv Cohen, Maxime Usdin, Matthew McLeod, Yusuke Kikuchi, Miao Zhang, Qi Yang, Marina Mesquida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop and validate an artificial intelligence (AI)-driven pipeline to quantify vitreous hyperreflective foci (vHRF) from OCT images and assess their association with intraocular inflammation (IOI).DesignA retrospective analysis of a multicenter double-masked placebo-controlled clinical trial cohort.SubjectsA clinical analysis cohort of 369 patients from the GALLEGO clinical trial (Galegenimab vs. placebo in patients with geographic atrophy, clinical trial ID: NCT03972709).MethodsWe trained a deep learning segmentation model, U-Net Transformer (UNETR) with a Vision Transformer backbone on 491 OCT B-scans with expert vHRF annotations from the GALLEGO, BURGUNDY (neovascular age-related macular degeneration; NCT04567303), and DOVETAIL (uveitic macular edema; NCT06771271) clinical trials. We applied the optimized model to a clinical analysis cohort from the GALLEGO clinical trial, comprising 1049 OCT volumes (34 images of IOI-positive cases), and evaluated the association between the resulting quantitative vHRF metrics and clinically diagnosed IOI.Main Outcome MeasuresSegmentation performance and the association of quantitative vHRF metrics with clinically diagnosed concurrent IOI, including receiver operating characteristic, precision–recall, predictive value, and eye-clustered generalized estimating equation analyses.ResultsThe UNETR model demonstrated strong segmentation performance. In the clinical cohort, IOI-positive eyes showed significantly elevated vHRF metrics (P < 0.001), and vHRF volume density was the strongest biomarker for concurrent IOI detection with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.84. In eye-clustered logistic generalized estimating equation models, 5 filtered biomarkers were significantly associated with inflammation, with the strongest association for filtered vHRF density [vHRF/μm3] (odds ratio per standard deviation 1.62, 95% confidence interval 1.26–2.07, P < 0.001). At the optimal threshold for vHRF density, sensitivity was 67.7%, specificity 89.0%, and positive predictive value was 16.9% despite an IOI prevalence of 3.2%.ConclusionsOur AI-driven pipeline accurately quantified vHRF from OCT images, and the resulting metrics, particularly vHRF volume density, were significantly associated with concurrent IOI. These findings support automated vHRF quantification as a promising imaging biomarker for inflammation assessment, although further validation in larger and more diverse datasets is needed.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.

Drug-induced intraocular inflammation (IOI)1 is a significant adverse event commonly associated with emerging ophthalmic therapies,2 particularly intravitreal injections, which are widely used for conditions such as neovascular age-related macular degeneration,3 diabetic macular edema,4 and geographic atrophy.5 Drug-induced IOI is characterized by inflammation that is sterile in nature,6 and the underlying pathophysiology remains incompletely understood. The proposed mechanisms include immune reactions to the drug molecule, its formulation, or impurities within the injected compounds.7 If unaddressed, IOI can progress to more severe forms such as retinal vasculitis, resulting in occlusive retinal vasculopathy and permanent vision loss,8 obscuring the tested drug's clinical relevance.9 The inflammatory response disrupts the immune-privileged environment of the eye. Persistent inflammation compromises the integrity of the blood–retinal barrier, allowing systemic circulating immune cells to infiltrate the vitreous cavity and potentially cause structural damage to ocular tissues.10 Early and accurate diagnosis of IOI is therefore critical to prevent these adverse outcomes and initiate timely therapeutic interventions, such as corticosteroids, which are often employed to suppress the inflammatory reaction and mitigate permanent tissue damage.11

The diagnosis of IOI currently relies on a combination of clinical examination, patient-reported symptoms, and various imaging modalities. Clinicians assess hallmark signs, including anterior uveitis, intermediate or posterior uveitis, using slit-lamp biomicroscopy and indirect ophthalmoscopy. OCT has become a key modality in IOI diagnostics,12 enabling high-resolution cross-sectional imaging of the retina, vitreoretinal interface, and the posterior vitreous immediately anterior to the retina. Notably, OCT facilitates the identification of vitreous hyperreflective foci (vHRF), which are thought to represent infiltrating immune cells or inflammatory aggregates present in the vitreous humor or depositing in the vitreo-retinal interface.12 These vHRF are increasingly recognized as potential noninvasive biomarkers of IOI,13 offering clinicians a novel approach to monitor disease progression and therapeutic response.

Despite its utility, OCT-based diagnosis faces limitations as manual quantification of vHRF in OCT images remains labor-intensive. The development of artificial intelligence (AI)–driven diagnostic algorithms poses a significant hurdle due to the generally low incidence of drug-induced IOI in ophthalmology clinical trials,14, 15, 16 which makes it challenging to collect a sufficiently large dataset of positive cases for traditional deep learning methods.17 Recent advancements in foundation models, trained on large datasets and fine-tuned for specific tasks, offer a promising solution.18,19 We propose an AI-driven pipeline that leverages a pretrained model to automatically detect and segment vHRF from OCT images. The primary goal is to address the limitations of existing methods by providing a scalable and objective tool that can significantly reduce the time and variability associated with vHRF quantification. We intend to validate this approach using a curated dataset and apply it to a clinical trial cohort to investigate the potential associations between vHRF and confirmed IOI diagnoses. This work aims to pave the way for earlier and more accurate IOI detection, enabling timely therapeutic intervention and improving patient outcomes.

Methods

Study Dataset and Preprocessing

The training dataset for the vHRF segmentation model comprised OCT B-scans from 3 clinical trials. Images of IOI diagnosed cases were drawn from the GALLEGO (geographic atrophy; NCT03972709), BURGUNDY (neovascular age-related macular degeneration; NCT04567303), and DOVETAIL (uveitic macular edema; NCT06771271) clinical trials. B-scans displaying vHRF were manually curated and annotated to generate binary pixel-wise vHRF masks. Vitreous hyperreflective foci were defined as discrete punctate hyperreflective structures with limited spatial extent, exhibiting reflectivity greater than the surrounding vitreous background signal and an estimated physical size ranging from approximately 10 to 100 μm2. These structures were required to be located within the vitreous compartment anterior to the internal limiting membrane (ILM), including foci freely suspended within the vitreous cavity as well as those adherent to the ILM.

Annotations were performed on the V7 lab platform (www.v7labs.com/darwin) using centroid-point labeling with instance segmentation masks. Segmentation assistance, SAM 219 was used to propose candidate masks, which were manually reviewed and corrected. Annotations were performed by a single trained annotator and independently reviewed post hoc by a second grader. A representative annotated B-scan is provided in Supplemental Figure 1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org.

The resulting dataset for the segmentation task consisted of 491 B-scans from 42 OCT volumes containing vHRF instances, representing all B-scans with vHRF from the selected volumes, with a total of 4201 segmented vHRF instances. This dataset was divided at the patient level into a training set (309 B-scans), a validation set (98 B-scans), and a test set (84 B-scans). The source OCT volumes images (Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering), contained 49 or 97 B-scans with a resolution of 496 × 512 or 496 × 1024 pixels, respectively. For model input, individual B-scans were resized to a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels. All volumetric and density-based metrics were calculated using the original Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine-derived axial and lateral pixel spacing to preserve physical calibration and account for anisotropic resolution.

Definition and Identification of Intraocular Inflammation (IOI)

The clinical association analysis was conducted exclusively on data from the GALLEGO trial and its open-label extension. Intraocular inflammation events were extracted from prospectively recorded adverse events in the trial database. Intraocular inflammation events were identified using a predefined basket of Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities preferred terms corresponding to IOI. These included anterior segment inflammation (e.g., iritis), posterior segment inflammation (e.g., vitritis), combined anterior and posterior involvement (iridocyclitis and panuveitis), and IOI-associated retinal vasculitis.

Clinical diagnosis was based on comprehensive ocular examination and protocol-mandated imaging standards, including slit-lamp examination with grading of anterior chamber cells and flare, assessment of vitreous cells and haze, dilated fundus examination, spectral-domain OCT imaging, color fundus photography, and fluorescein angiography when posterior segment inflammation or vascular involvement was suspected. Inflammatory severity was graded using the Standardization of Uveitis Nomenclature criteria.

Deep Learning for vHRF Segmentation

We fine-tuned a U-Net Transformer (UNETR) model, a transformer-based architecture employing a Vision Transformer (ViT-Base-16) as the encoder backbone within a U-Net-style decoder20 by conducting Bayesian hyperparameter sweeps designed to maximize the mean cross-validation Dice score for the vHRF class. UNETR Segmentation Model Hyperparameters

Models were trained to segment vHRF using a Generalized Dice Focal Loss function,21 with the background class excluded from the calculation. Training was conducted for a maximum of 250 epochs using 16-bit mixed-precision22 and a gradient clipping threshold23 of 1.0. An early stopping protocol was employed with a patience of 50 epochs, monitored on the validation set to prevent overfitting. All training was conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with 8 CPU cores per task. A layer-wise optimizer24 was used with distinct learning rates for the encoder and decoder. The final model was retrained on a combined dataset of the original training and validation sets using the optimal hyperparameters in Table 1.

OCT Volume Segmentation Pipeline

A comprehensive segmentation pipeline was implemented to process clinical OCT images and generate detailed vHRF segmentation masks.

UNETR-Based Segmentation

The pipeline applied the trained UNETR model to individual B-scan slices from the clinical OCT images. The model generated pixel-level vHRF segmentation masks with high spatial accuracy, using a threshold of 0.5. Consolidated Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine volume masks were then created by combining individual slice results, facilitating three-dimensional analysis.

Vitreous Region Delineation

Retinal layer segmentation maps were generated by a pretrained model, Eyenotate.25 The ILM, segmented by Eyenotate, served as the inferior anatomical boundary. Postprocessing and Quantitative Analysis

We observed that the model was oversegmenting hyperreflective foci (HRF), particularly on images with high speckle noise. We applied a postprocessing threshold of a minimum circularity of 0.9. This threshold was defined a priori, without evaluation of test-set performance and in alignment with previous literature,26,27 and serves to filter out speckle noise. The results from the segmentation model before and after applying the postprocessing filter are shown in Figure 2, including an example with more speckle noise, where the model continued to misclassify noise artifacts and segment them as HRF. Quality Control and Image Exclusion

A quality control step was applied to the entire clinical analysis cohort to remove volumes with excessive noise. A signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)28 was computed for the entire vitreous area of each B-scan and used as an exclusion criterion. The selection of the SNR threshold was guided by a data-driven analysis to ensure the cutoff did not inadvertently exclude images with high, nonartifactual vHRF counts.

In the absence of fully annotated volumes, direct empirical measurement of the normative range for total vitreoretinal hyperreflective foci (vHRF) counts was not feasible. Therefore, to establish a statistical threshold for an anomalously high vHRF burden, a model was developed from our ground truth dataset of individually annotated B-scans.

The negative binomial distribution was selected for this analysis, as it is a conventional and robust model for overdispersed count data frequently observed in biological systems. This approach was predicated on the assumption of a uniform spatial distribution of vHRF across all B-scans within a volume. From the fitted distribution, the 95th percentile was calculated to establish a threshold of 543 instances, with counts exceeding this value considered statistically significant outliers. Using this analysis, we determined the highest SNR value (1.14) at which 95% of the volumes in the complete dataset fell below this vHRF count threshold (Supplemental Figure 2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). This analysis provided a statistical justification for selecting a more conservative final SNR cutoff of 1.0.

In the final analysis pipeline, vHRF instances were separated into free-floating vHRF (fvHRF) and membrane-associated (maHRF) subtypes based on their spatial relationship to the ILM. Membrane-associated hyperreflective foci were defined as foci located within a small axial distance (±2 pixels) of the ILM. In contrast, fvHRF were defined as foci located beyond this distance threshold within the vitreous space.

We computed counts for maHRF, fvHRF, and total vHRF. Volumetric metrics (μm3) and density (normalized by the vitreous volume captured in the OCT volume) were calculated using high-precision spatial calibration derived from Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine metadata to ensure physically correct measurements. The vitreous volume captured varied between OCT volumes and was impacted by image registration artifacts. We applied a noise filter in addition to the ILM segmentation to accurately segment this vitreous volume, as shown in Figure 1.

The protocols for the GALLEGO (NCT03972709), BURGUNDY (NCT04567303), and DOVETAIL (NCT06771271) clinical trials were approved by the institutional review boards or ethics committees at each participating site. All research adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent.

Filtered vHRF density demonstrated the strongest discrimination, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.84 (95% CI 0.75–0.92), followed closely by filtered vHRF volume (AUROC 0.84, 95% CI 0.75–0.92) and filtered total vHRF count (AUROC 0.82, 95% CI 0.74–0.91). Filtered maHRF count achieved an AUROC of 0.80 (95% CI 0.70–0.88), while filtered fvHRF count demonstrated an AUROC of 0.76 (95% CI 0.64–0.87).

Optimal operating thresholds were determined using the Youden J statistic33 (maximizing sensitivity + specificity – 1). The optimal thresholds were: 3 maHRF per OCT volume, 6 fvHRF per OCT volume, 9 total vHRF per volume, 4.04 × 10-5 vHRF/μm3 for density, and 1.15 × 106 μm3 for total vHRF volume. These thresholds indicate that even modest elevations in vHRF burden are associated with increased likelihood of concurrent IOI in this cohort.

Predictive Performance in a Low-Prevalence Setting

Intraocular inflammation was a rare event in this cohort (prevalence 3.2%), and predictive values were evaluated at the optimal Youden J threshold for vHRF density from the precision–recall curves (Supplemental Figure 4, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). At this operating point (sensitivity 67.7%, specificity 89.0%), the positive predictive value was 16.9%. Relative to the baseline IOI prevalence of 3.2%, the positive predictive value represents a 5.3-fold enrichment in post-test probability. Conversely, the residual probability of IOI following a negative test result was reduced from 3.2% to 1.2%. These findings indicate that, although positive predictive value remains modest due to low event prevalence, vHRF metrics provide strong rule-out capability and substantial enrichment of IOI risk when positive.

In logistic generalized estimating equation models adjusted for visit and clustered at the eye level (216 eyes, 1049 volumes, and 34 inflammatory events), all 5 filtered biomarkers were significantly associated with inflammation. The strongest association was observed for filtered vHRF density (odds ratio [OR] per standard deviation 1.62, 95% CI 1.26–2.07, P < 0.001), followed by filtered vHRF volume (OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.20–2.1, P = 0.001). Associations for maHRF count (OR 1.51, 95% CI 1.30–1.74, P < 0.001), total count (OR 1.38, 95% CI 1.12–1.72, P = 0.003), and fvHRF count (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.08–1.53, P = 0.005) were also statistically significant.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that an AI-driven pipeline can quantify vHRF from OCT images, suggesting them as biomarkers for detecting IOI. The performance of postprocessed metrics, particularly filtered vHRF volume density for concurrent IOI detection (AUROC 0.84) underscores the potential for clinical application of this automated approach. The consistently improved performance of filtered metrics validates our robust quality control pipeline, highlighting that managing image noise and applying morphological constraints are critical steps to isolate the true biological signal from imaging artifacts.

Current Evidence and Clinical Context

Our findings align with a growing body of evidence supporting the use of OCT for objective inflammation assessment, which has gained traction in recent years. Quantification of vitreous cell density from OCT images shows a strong correlation with standardized vitreous haze scores,34 solidifying the link between OCT findings and clinical inflammation, supporting the utility of vHRF as a biomarker for IOI.

Limitations

Despite these promising findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. The primary limitation is the low prevalence of IOI in our clinical analysis cohort (34 IOI+ visits). This imbalance can lead to statistical models that are highly specific but have modest sensitivity, a trade-off that was evident in our results. While appropriate for a screening tool where minimizing false positives is crucial, the derived thresholds may not be perfectly generalizable to populations with a higher incidence of inflammation. Furthermore, our data-driven filtering thresholds, for both SNR and vHRF morphology, were optimized on this specific dataset. While statistically justified, these filters could inadvertently exclude true, albeit small or irregularly shaped, inflammatory foci. Consequently, these thresholds may require validation and recalibration on external datasets, especially those acquired with different OCT devices or imaging protocols. It is additionally hypothesized that the risk of IOI may be higher in patients with a predisposition to inflammatory responses.35 Future work should be performed to evaluate whether baseline HRF has predictive utility for IOI in larger datasets.

Future Directions and Remaining Unknowns

This work also highlights several unanswered questions in our understanding of vHRF. While we establish a strong statistical link between vHRF and IOI, the precise biological identity of these foci remains elusive. It remains unclear whether these structures are composed of individual immune cells, cellular aggregates, or protein clumps. Future studies correlating OCT findings with cellular and molecular analyses of aqueous or vitreous humor are needed to answer this fundamental question.

Additionally, the dynamic behavior of vHRF in response to anti-inflammatory treatment is a critical area for future investigation. Longitudinally tracking changes in vHRF burden could clarify whether these metrics track treatment response and longitudinal inflammatory activity.

Finally, while our model performed well, its generalizability is yet to be determined. Validating this pipeline on a larger, more diverse dataset (encompassing different OCT manufacturers, patient populations, and etiologies of uveitis) is the essential next step toward translating this powerful research tool into a reliable clinical asset for managing inflammatory eye disease.
