Nitric oxide interacts with brassinosteroid signalling to regulate plant growth and development in an organ-specific manner
Authors: Jorge Rubio-Heras, Laura Huebra-Montero, Antonio de la Torre, Inmaculada Sánchez-Vicente, Chao Zhang, Jana Okleštková, Karel Doležal, Ondřej Novák, Brigitte Poppenberger, Oscar Lorenzo, Pablo Albertos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNitric oxide (NO) is a plant gasotransmitter that regulates plant growth and development by interacting with different regulatory pathways. Among these, brassinosteroids (BRs) have become candidate phytohormones that interact antagonistically and closely with NO to regulate plant growth. Seedling growth in NO-deficient mutants is enhanced and hyper-responds to BR treatments, while NO overaccumulator mutants show some developmental defects. Interestingly, this hyper-response was also observed in mutants that had both constitutively activated BR signalling and reduced NO levels. BR signalling mutants exhibited different responses to the repressive role of NO in seedlings. The phenotypes observed were attributed to the induction of BR-repressed biosynthetic genes and the repression of BR-induced growth-promoting genes detected in NO-overaccumulating plants, with opposite gene expression patterns detected in NO-deficient plants. Furthermore, the activation of BR signalling has an impact on NO accumulation, as BR-treated plants or mutants with activated BR signalling showed hyperaccumulation of NO. Finally, elevated endogenous levels of brassinolide, the most bioactive BR, were found in NO-deficient plants, which could explain the growth promotion observed in this mutant. These findings contribute to a better understanding of the growth-repressive role of NO during seedling development through its interaction with BR biosynthesis, signalling, and responses.

Introduction

Post-germinative growth is the developmental phase that begins immediately after radicle emergence and continues until the seedling fully establishes autotrophic growth (Kitajima and Fenner, 2000). It involves the mobilization of seed reserves, hypocotyl elongation, root development, cotyledon expansion, initiation of photosynthesis, and the establishment of responsiveness to environmental signals (Bewley et al., 2013). This period is crucial for successful adaptation and survival of the plant. Therefore, the germination and post-germinative growth must be precisely regulated by external environmental cues, phytohormones, and other endogenous signalling molecules. Among them, nitric oxide (NO) has emerged as a major signalling molecule that modulates multiple aspects of plant cycle life, especially germination and post-germinative growth. This gasotransmitter is known to regulate key physiological processes such as seed germination (Arc et al., 2013; Albertos et al., 2015), hypocotyl elongation (Beligni and Lamattina, 2000; Lozano-Juste and León, 2011), root growth and development (Fernández-Marcos et al., 2011; Sanz et al., 2014), and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses (Delledonne et al., 1998; Neill et al., 2002; Romero-Puertas et al., 2004; Lai et al., 2011). Its action is tightly integrated with phytohormonal pathways, including abscisic acid, gibberellins, and auxins, orchestrating a coordinated regulation of early seedling development (Freschi, 2013; Sanz et al., 2015).

NO biosynthesis in plants can occur through two main pathways: enzymatic and non-enzymatic, both depending on redox processes. In the enzymatic pathway, NO is produced as a secondary product of the oxidation of L-arginine by the product of NITRIC OXIDE SYNTHASE-LIKE 1/NITRIC OXIDE ASSOCIATED 1 (NOS1/NOA1), or the reduction of nitrate (NO3−) to nitrite (NO2−) by the proteins encoded in the two homologous NITRATE REDUCTASE NADH1 and 2 (NIA1 and NIA2) genes (Guo et al., 2003; Gupta et al., 2011; Asgher et al., 2017). Furthermore, loss-of-function mutations in the CHLOROPHYLL A/B BINDING PROTEIN UNDEREXPRESSED 1/NO OVERPRODUCER (CUE1/NOX1) gene cause an increase in endogenous L-arginine levels, leading to an overproduction of NO (He et al., 2004; Lechón et al., 2020). The non-enzymatic pathway is based on the production of NO from NO2− at acidic pH, or through the action of carotenoids or phenolic compounds (Sanz et al., 2015; Asgher et al., 2017). The complexity of NO production requires the use of mutant plants with altered genetic backgrounds in these enzymes, resulting in altered endogenous levels of NO. As mutants that overaccumulate NO, different alleles of CUE1/NOX1 are widely used, with nox1-1/cue1-1 being the strongest and cue1-5/cue1-6 the weakest alleles in levels of NO overaccumulation (Lechón et al., 2020). NO deficiency is found in the nia1nia2noa1 triple mutant, which is defective at both nitrate reductase loci NIA1 and NIA2 as well as the NOA1 locus (Lozano-Juste and León, 2010). NO can be stored as S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO) and S-nitrosothiols (SNOs) in plants and animals, and the balance between free NO and GSNO determines the protein S-nitrosylation levels in the cell (Gupta et al., 2011). This post-translational modification could occur by free NO or through an enzymatic pathway—S-transnitrosylation—and it is one of the principal mechanisms of NO signalling processes to regulate growth and stress responses in plants (Sánchez-Vicente et al., 2019).

Among all the phytohormones implicated in growth and developmental responses, brassinosteroids (BRs) play a crucial role in regulating cell elongation, flowering, senescence, and response to abiotic and biotic stresses (Anwar et al., 2018; Garrido-Auñón et al., 2024). BRs are a group of steroid hormones that includes >70 compounds, with brassinolide (BL) being the principal bioactive form (Aldukhi et al., 2020; Bajguz et al., 2020).

BR perception occurs through a leucine-rich repeat kinase in the plasma membrane, BR INSENSITIVE 1 (BRI1) (Li and Chory, 1997). For the activation of the receptor–hormone complex, a co-receptor called BRI1-ASSOCIATED KINASE 1 (BAK1) is essential (Nam and Li, 2002; Sun et al., 2013). After the complex is formed, a downstream signalling cascade starts, leading to the dephosphorylation and thus inactivation of a GSK3/shaggy-like kinase, BR INSENSITIVE 2 (BIN2) (Li et al., 2001). This kinase is a major negative regulator of the BR signalling pathway because its role is to phosphorylate the two major transcription factors (TFs) in this pathway, BRI1-EMS-SUPRESSOR1 (BES1), also known as BRASSINAZOLE RESISTANT 2 (Li and Nam, 2002; Yin et al., 2002), and BRASSINAZOLE RESISTANT 1 (BZR1) (Wang et al., 2002). These TFs become inactive when phosphorylated and are consequently targeted to degradation, suppressing the BR developmental responses (Wang et al., 2002; Yin et al., 2002).

After BR signalling activation, the BR-controlled TFs will act as repressors or activators of the expression of BR-regulated genes. Some of the BR direct target genes have sequence-specific BES1/BZR1-binding sites, such as the E-boxes (5′-CANNTG-3′), G-boxes (5′-CAGGTG-3′), or BR response elements (BRREs) (5′-CGTGT/CG-3′), in their promoters (Yin et al., 2005; Sun et al., 2010). Among the most studied BR-repressed genes are a set of BR biosynthetic genes such as CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENESIS AND DWARFISM (CPD), ROTUNDFOLIA3 (ROT3), and BR-6-OXIDASE 1 and 2 (BR6ox1 and BR6ox2), which control the conversion of campesterol to castasterone (CS), which is the main precursor of the most bioactive BR molecule, BL (Szekeres et al., 1996; Kim et al., 1998; Shimada et al., 2003). Following BR signalling activation, the expression of these genes appears to be down-regulated through a negative feedback mechanism to turn off BR responses and adjust plant growth (Martínez et al., 2018). Likewise, the activation of BR signalling also causes transcriptional activation of a set of growth-promoting genes. Some of them are involved in cell elongation through the biosynthesis of auxins and gibberellins, such as SMALL AUXIN UPREGULATED-ARABIDOPSIS THALIANA ECOTYPE COLUMBIA 1 (SAUR-AC1), INDOLE-3-ACETIC ACID INDUCIBLE 5 (IAA5), GIBBERELLIN 3-OXIDASE 1 (GA3ox1), GIBBERELLIN 20-OXIDASE 1 (GA20ox1), and INDOLE-3-ACETIC ACID INDUCIBLE 19 (IAA19) (Yin et al., 2005; Unterholzner et al., 2015). Other BR-induced genes are cell wall biosynthesis related, such as EXPANSIN 1 (EXP1), XYLOGLUCAN ENDOTRANSGLUCOSYLASE/HYDROLASE 17 (XTH17), XYLOGLUCAN ENDOTRANSGLYCOSYLASE-RELATED PROTEIN 7 (XTR7), and PACLOBUTRAZOL RESISTANCE 5 (PRE5) (Nakamura et al., 2003a, b; Martínez et al., 2018). Therefore, the activation of BR signalling and responses can be analysed by the expression pattern of these two groups of BR marker genes.

Although NO and BRs display antagonistic roles in the regulation of post-germinative growth and development, little is known about how this is achieved. During seed germination, both regulators promote the emergence of the root tip through the testa and endosperm layers (Albertos et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2019). Once the seed has germinated, BRs and NO can have different effects: while NO inhibits, BRs promote hypocotyl elongation, and elevated levels of both regulators inhibit root growth by reducing the size of the root meristem and suppressing cell elongation (Li and Chory, 1997; Beligni and Lamattina, 2000; Choe et al., 2001; Fernández-Marcos et al., 2011; Jantapo et al., 2021). Interestingly, in vitro experiments reported that BIN2 S-nitrosylation inhibits the kinase activity, but no physiological effect has yet been shown (Wang et al., 2015).

In this study, the NO and BR crosstalk at the biosynthesis and signalling levels during post-germinative growth and development is investigated using pharmacological, genetic, transcriptional, and biochemical approaches. Evidence is provided that NO represses post-germinative growth responses promoted by BRs by modifying the expression pattern of BR marker genes and inhibiting BR accumulation. Additionally, enhancing BR signalling induces the accumulation of NO in roots that may be used in a negative feedback fashion for the plant to turn down BR responses and balance growth.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh ecotype Columbia (Col-0) is the genetic background for all wild-type and mutant plants used in this work. The mutant lines used have been described: bin2-1 (Li et al., 2001), bes1-D (Yin et al., 2002) utilizing a version that has been introgressed into Col-0 (Ibanes et al., 2009), bes1-1 (He et al., 2005), bzr1-D (Wang et al., 2002), bzr1-2 (Lachowiec et al., 2018), cue1-6 (Streatfield et al., 1999), nox1-1 (He et al., 2004), nia1nia2noa1-2 (Lozano-Juste and León, 2010), cue1-6;bes1-D (this study), and nia1nia2noa1-2;bes1-D (this study). Arabidopsis plants were grown in a growth chamber or greenhouse under 50–60% humidity, 22 °C, and long-day (LD) conditions (16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod) at 70 µmol m–2 s–1 for plates.

For in vitro culture, Arabidopsis seeds were surface-sterilized in 75% (v/v) commercial bleach (4–5% sodium hypochlorite) and 0.01% (w/v) Triton X-100 for 5 min and washed three times in sterile water before sowing. Seeds were stratified for 3 d at 4 °C and then sown on 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) (Murashige and Skoog, 1962) solid medium with 1% (w/v) sucrose and 0.6% (w/v) agar, and the pH was adjusted to 5.7 with KOH before autoclaving. Seeds were sown in different treatments, and plates were sealed and incubated in a controlled-environment growth chamber. For NO and hormone treatments, Arabidopsis seeds were sown on 1/2 MS medium supplemented with 1.5 mM GSNO or 0.01, 0.1, or 1 µM epibrassinolide (eBL). All the chemical reagents and genetic lines are given in Supplementary Table S1.

Nitric oxide visualization by DAF-FM assays

Seven-day-old seedlings of the wild type, bin2-1, bes1-D, bes1-1, bzr1-D, bzr1-2, cue1-6, nox1-1, and nia1nia2noa1-2 which had been grown in 1/2 MS were incubated in 200 µl of solution containing 5 µM 4-amino-5-methylamino-2′,7′-difluorofluorescein diacetate (DAF-FM) in 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4 for 40 min at 21 °C in the dark, and washed three times for 10 min after DAF-FM incubation. For treatment with BR, the wild type, cue1-6, nox1-1, and nia1nia2noa1-2 were treated for 2 h with 1 µM eBL, and the same concentration of eBL was maintained during incubation with DAF-FM and washing. The NO-derived fluorescence in roots emitted by DAF-FM was detected using a Leica DM LB or Thunder Microscope by excitation at 480–495 nm and emission at 515 nm. ImageJ was used for the fluorescence quantification analysis, shown in arbitrary units (AU), and each genotype or treatment was normalized to the wild-type fluorescence signal.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription–PCR analysis

Total RNA for real-time quantitative reverse transcription–PCR (RT–qPCR) was extracted from a set of 15–20 seven-day-old 1/2 MS-grown seedlings of the wild type, the wild type treated with GSNO, nox1-1, and nia1nia2noa1-2, using five biological replicates for each genotype and treatment. Plant tissue was harvested into 2 ml safe-lock Eppendorf tubes, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground in the tubes by glass beads using two pulses of 1 min in a Mixer Mill MM 400 (Retsch). Plant powder was used for total RNA extraction using the E.Z.N.A ® plant RNA kit (Omega) according to the instructions of the manufacturer and as previously described (Albertos et al., 2022). RNA quality and concentration were evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis and an ND-1000 (NanoDrop) spectrophotometer. DNA was eliminated using the RNase-free DNase I recombinant kit (Roche), incubating the RNA samples with the enzyme for 20 min at 25 °C, and then the samples were placed under 75 °C for 10 min for DNase I inactivation. cDNA was synthesized using the iScript® cDNA Synthesis kit (BioRad) employing 2 µg of RNA samples as follows: 5 min at 25 °C for oligonucleotide annealing, 20 min at 46 °C for reverse transcriptase activity, and 1 min at 95 °C for enzyme inactivation. Three technical replicates of 2 µl of cDNA from each biological replicate were used for the qPCR amplification using the FastStart Universal SYBR® Green Master kit for the RT–qPCR following the instructions of the manufacturer and as previously described (Sánchez-Vicente et al., 2024). The thermal cycles for SYBR Green real-time PCR were 95 °C for 10 min followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. qRT–PCRs were performed using the ABI PRISM 7000 Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems, Framingham, MA, USA). Relative expression analysis for the selected marker genes was performed using the Ct (cycle threshold) obtained from the software of the ABI PRISM 7000 Sequence Detection System. The Ct value for each gene analysed is the specific cycle number at which the fluorescence signal from the reaction reaches this threshold, and it is inversely proportional to the initial amount of target nucleic acid. UBIQUITIN-CONJUGATING ENZYME 21 (UBC21) and ELONGATION FACTOR 1 ALPHA (EFalpha) were used as reference genes for normalizing relative expression values (Czechowski et al., 2005; Eremina et al., 2016; Soucek et al., 2017; Albertos et al., 2022; Škiljaica et al., 2022; Sánchez-Vicente et al., 2024). The ΔΔCt method was used to calculate relative gene expression (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). First, ΔCt was calculated for each sample by subtracting the Ct value of the reference gene (UBC21 or EFalpha) from the Ct value of the gene of interest. Next, the average ΔCt for the control group is calculated, and then the ΔΔCt for each sample is calculated by subtracting the average ΔCt of the control group from the ΔCt of each individual sample. Finally, the changes in the relative expression levels were calculated using the formula 2−ΔΔCt.

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization-mass spectrometry imaging assays

Unlabelled BL standards were purchased from OlChemIm Ltd (Olomouc, Czech Republic) and used for peak assignment, mass calibration, and quantification of endogenous phytohormones. The MS and MS/MS spectra of all standards were acquired using a Synapt G2-Si MS instrument equipped with the matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) ion source (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The optimized collision energies for analyte fragmentation were between 25 eV and 30 eV, accordingly. Precursors and major fragment ions of the standard compounds were identified using MassLynx™ software (v4.1, Waters), after which the FooDB Database (V 1.0) were consulted to confirm the molecular formulas (Giacomoni et al., 2014).

MALDI-MSI for in situ phytohormone visualization was performed on the Synapt G2-Si MS instrument equipped with the MALDI ion source (Waters) (Zhang et al., 2021). Briefly, freshly harvested plant samples (n=3) were placed on Superfrost glass slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using non-conductive double-sided tape (Plano GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) and vacuum dried in a desiccator. The 1,5-diaminonaphthalene (1,5-DAN) matrix (Merck, Germany) was overlaid onto the sample slides using a SunCollect MALDI Sprayer (SunChrom Wissenschaftliche Geräte GmbH, Germany) and then submitted to MSI analysis. MALDI-MS was used with the following settings: 1 kHz with a 150 shot laser, m/z 100–600 detected in the negative sensitive mode, with 50 μm spatial resolution acquired every second. MALDI-MS/MS was performed on plant samples with a 25 eV collision energy scanning of the regions of interest (ROIs). The acquired MS/MS spectra were processed using MassLynx™ software (v4.1) and then identified by their masses acquired from the standard solution.

The acquired MSI spectra were processed into an imzML format using HD imaging software (Waters, UK). Subsequently, they were analysed using msIQuant 2.x (Uppsala University, Sweden), where the data were processed for ion intensity map creation. ROIs containing hypocotyls and roots of the A. thaliana samples (n=3) were selected to determine the average signal intensities for each studied compound with a 0.1 Da binning window in every comparison group. Differences in the mean intensities of the studied compounds between different ROI mutants were analysed using GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software, MA, USA), utilizing an in-house R script, which provided the mean and SD in violin plots.

Image processing and analysis

Seedling images of the phenotypical experiments were taken using a Nikon D90 Digital Camera and Epson Perfection V600 Photo Scanner. All image measurements were made using ImageJ. GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software), Microsoft Office LTSC Professional Plus 2021, and Rstudio (PBC, Boston, MA, USA) were used to write the manuscript, generate the graphs and figures, and perform the statistical analysis.

Results

Plants with deficient nitric oxide levels have enhanced brassinosteroid growth responses

To determine whether the effects of changes in endogenous NO levels during post-germinative growth are related to BR responses, three NO mutants with altered NO levels were studied and compared with wild-type plants in BR dose–response treatments in darkness and under LD conditions (Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. S1). The NO mutants utilized were two NO overaccumulator alleles nox1-1 and cue1-6 (Streatfield et al., 1999; He et al., 2004) and the triple NO-deficient nia1nia2noa1 (Lozano-Juste and León, 2010). Under control conditions, nia1nia2noa1-2 plants exhibit NO deficiency and larger hypocotyls under LDs (Fig. 1A, C) and under dark conditions (Fig. 1B, D) compared with wild-type plants. The opposite result was observed in nox1-1 and cue1-6 NO overproducers, as hypocotyls were shorter in both LD and dark conditions. Therefore, plants with reduced levels of endogenous NO showed some growth-promoting characteristics that have been described in relation to BRs. The sensitivity of nox1-1, cue1-6, and nia1nia2noa1-2 plants to treatments with 0.01, 0.1, and 1 µM eBL was tested, and the response was compared with that of untreated plants. Treatments with 0.01 µM and 0.1 µM eBL caused shorter hypocotyls in wild-type plants in the dark and larger hypocotyls in the light, as previously shown (Chen et al., 2017). However, the NO impaired mutants exhibited different sensitivity to eBL treatments under both conditions. Under light conditions, NO deficiency in the nia1nia2noa1-2 mutant caused a hyper-response to eBL at concentrations of ≥0.1 µM, resulting in the longest hypocotyls of all genotypes under study. Hypocotyls of cue1-6 developed under LDs at different concentrations of eBL remained shorter than those of the wild type, a response that was also observed for nox1-1 hypocotyls, except at 0.1 µM eBL, although without statistical significance (Fig. 1A, C). This hyper-response to eBL in the NO-deficient background was dose dependent in seedlings grown in darkness, as nia1nia2noa1-2 hypocotyls were the most reduced hypocotyls under treatment with 0.01 µM and 0.1 µM eBL compared with the other genotypes (Fig. 1B, D). Interestingly, cue1-6 and nox1-1 plants showed a lower response to eBL compared with wild-type seedlings, especially at 0.01 µM and 0.1 µM eBL. Regarding roots under LD conditions, alterations in NO were observed to cause defects in root elongation compared with the wild type, with the roots of nia1nia2noa1-2 seedlings being the most inhibited under mock conditions and with 0.01 µM eBL, and cue1-6 roots showing the greatest inhibition with 0.1 µM and 1 µM eBL (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Regarding dark-grown seedlings, primary roots were larger in cue1-6 and nox1-1 and shorter in nia1nia2noa1-2 (Supplementary Fig. S1B). cue1-6 responded more strongly than the wild type to the increase in eBL levels that caused root inhibition, although this phenotype was also observed in nox1-1 and nia1nia2noa1-2 plants, albeit to a lesser extent. The percentage inhibition or elongation of the hypocotyl or root for each genotype and/or treatment is represented in the bars of each graph. These results revealed that NO may have antagonistic effects on eBL-induced hypocotyl elongation but synergistic effects on eBL-induced primary root length inhibition, as plants with reduced endogenous NO levels, such as nia1nia2noa1-2, showed enhanced BR-induced growth responses.

Nitric oxide suppresses brassinosteroid-induced hypocotyl elongation

To examine the effects of NO on BR signalling, selected BR mutants were treated with the NO donor GSNO to increase the levels of NO present in the plants (Fig. 2). Among the mutants analysed, bin2-1 represents a dominant gain of function of BIN2, a GSK3-like protein kinase, resulting in a constitutively hyperactive kinase that fails to be inactivated upon BR perception (Li et al., 2001). bin2-1 backgrounds are insensitive to eBL, and BR-regulated TFs remain phosphorylated and inactive, unable to regulate gene expression. Two other dominant gain-of-function mutants, bes1-D and bzr1-D, were used, which have been described as constitutively activating responses to BRs (Wang et al., 2002; Yin et al., 2002). To complement the genetic background, the loss-of-function knockout mutant alleles bes1-1 and bzr1-2 of the BES1 and BZR1 genes, respectively, with reduced eBL sensitivity, were also introduced into the experiments (He et al., 2005; Sandhu et al., 2013). Therefore, three mutants with no or reduced sensitivity to BRs, bin2-1, bes1-1, and bzr1-2, respectively, and two mutants with constitutive activation of BR signalling and responses were used to evaluate NO-promoted inhibition of hypocotyl elongation and primary root growth under dark and LD conditions (Beligni and Lamattina, 2000; Castillo et al., 2018). The bin2-1 mutant was found to be insensitive to NO treatment under both LD and dark conditions, as no inhibition of hypocotyl elongation was detected (Fig. 2). This insensitivity to NO during hypocotyl elongation under LD and dark conditions was also observed in the bes1-1 and bzr1-2 mutants, in which BR signalling is reduced, although to a much lesser extent (Fig. 2). In contrast, the bes1-D and bzr1-D mutants with BR gain of function showed inhibition of hypocotyl elongation by NO, as did wild-type plants. Regarding primary root elongation phenotypes, no significant differences in the response to NO were observed between mutants under LD or dark conditions (Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). However, the bin2-1 mutant again showed some characteristics of NO insensitivity under dark conditions, as root inhibition in this BR-insensitive background was not as strong as in wild-type plants. The percentage inhibition or elongation of the hypocotyl or root for each genotype and/or treatment is represented in the bars of each graph. Taken together, these results indicate that NO repression of post-germinative growth and development is less pronounced in BR-insensitive backgrounds, where BZR1, and most probabely BES1, is totally or partially inactive.

To provide genetic evidence of the effect of BRs and NO during post-germinative growth responses, the bes1-D gain-of-function mutation was introgressed into cue1-6 and nia1nia2noa1-2 backgrounds. cue1-6;bes1-D and nia1nia2noa1-2;bes1-D plants with NO overaccumulation or deficiency in constitutively BR-activated backgrounds were used to study hypocotyl and root length and compared with wild-type, cue1-6, nia1nia2noa1-2, and bes1-D plants (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. S3). The hypocotyls of the double mutant cue1-6;bes1-D were larger than those of cue1-6, but did not reach the length of the hypocotyl of bes1-D, providing genetic evidence that NO overaccumulation repressed BR-induced hypocotyl elongation (Fig. 3A, B). Consequently, the quadruple nia1nia2noa1-2;bes1-D mutant hypocotyls were larger than bes1-D hypocotyls, meaning that NO deficiency promotes BR-mediated hypocotyl elongation (Fig. 3C, D). Regarding roots, primary root lengths from all mutants were all reduced compared with the wild type, and cue1-6;bes1-D and nia1nia2noa1-2;bes1-D mutants showed slightly larger roots than the corresponding NO parental mutants. The percentage inhibition or elongation of the hypocotyl or root for each genotype is represented in the bars of each graph. This genetic evidence supports the results obtained in Fig. 1, where single mutants were treated with eBL, and highlights the relevant relationship between NO homeostasis and BR signalling to regulate post-germinative growth, especially at the level of hypocotyl elongation.

Nitric oxide induces brassinosteroid biosynthetic genes and represses brassinosteroid growth-promoting genes

To understand whether the growth phenotypes observed by altering NO levels depend on BR-controlled transcriptional activity, the expression levels of the BR-regulated genes were quantified by RT–qPCR (Fig. 4; Supplementary Fig. S4). To normalize the RT–qPCR results, two validated reference genes were used, UBC21 and EFalpha (Czechowski et al., 2005; Eremina et al., 2016; Soucek et al., 2017; Albertos et al., 2022; Škiljaica et al., 2022; Sánchez-Vicente et al., 2024), and the results are shown in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S4, respectively. Upon BR activation, BR biosynthetic genes CPD, ROT3, and BR6ox2 have been shown to be regulated in a feedback manner (Fujioka and Yokota, 2003; Martínez et al, 2018). This response aims to switch off BR signalling after activation and maintain the correct BR levels in plants, since BR overaccumulation can lead to negative effects on plant growth and development (Wei and Li, 2020). Therefore, CPD, ROT3, and BR6ox2 were quantified in wild-type plants after GSNO treatment and in NO mutants. This showed that after NO treatment and in the NO overproducer mutant background nox1-1, the expression of these BR biosynthetic genes is significantly increased (Fig. 4A, B; Supplementary Fig. S4A, B). Opposite changes were observed when CPD and BR6ox2 expression was checked in the triple NO-deficient mutant nia1nia2noa1-2 (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Fig. S4B). nia1nia2noa1-2 plants show constitutive down-regulation of these genes, as occurs in plants that have activated the BR signalling pathway. Additionally, another set of growth-promoting genes up-regulated by BRs, namely SAUR-AC1, IAA5, GA3ox1, GA20ox1, IAA19, EXP1, XTH17, XTR7, and PRE5 (Nakamura et al., 2003a, b; Yin et al., 2005; Unterholzner et al., 2015; Martínez et al, 2018), were tested in plants with altered NO content (Fig. 4C, D; Supplementary Fig. S4C–F). NO treatment showed significant down-regulation of these genes, with SAUR-AC1 and GA20ox1 (Fig. 4D; Supplementary Fig. S4D) and XTH17 and PRE5 (Supplementary Fig. S4E, F) being most affected. In nox1-1 plants, GA3ox1 was constitutively repressed (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Fig. S4C, D). An opposite result was observed in the triple NO-deficient nia1nia2noa1-2 mutant where IAA5, GA3ox1, and GA20ox1 were constitutively up-regulated (Fig. 4D; Supplementary Fig. S4D). These results indicated that increased NO levels in plants caused repression of BR-induced growth-promoting genes, while plants deficient in NO have enhanced expression of these BR-controlled genes that promote growth. Taken together, there is evidence that NO represses BR transcriptional responses, with clear effects on a set of BR-regulated genes.

Nitric oxide content in roots is promoted by brassinosteroid signalling activation

NO modulates seed physiology, alleviating seed dormancy and promoting germination (Albertos et al., 2015; Sanz et al., 2015), and inhibits root development and hypocotyl elongation (Beligni and Lamattina, 2000; Fernández-Marcos et al., 2011). To test whether mutations in the BR signalling pathway alter the accumulation of NO, the fluorescence dye DAF-FM was used to detect changes in the endogenous levels of this gasotransmitter. DAF-FM dye reacts with NO to form a fluorescent derivative that has an excitation wavelength of ∼495 nm and an emission wavelength of ∼515 nm that can be detected by fluorescence microscopy. Seven-day-old bin2-1, bes1-D, bes1-1, bzr1-D, and bzr1-2 mutant and wild-type plants were treated with DAF-FM and the fluorescence signals of roots were detected and quantified, using wild-type plants without dye as the negative control (Fig. 5A, B; Supplementary Fig. S5). When BR signalling is strongly interrupted as in the bin2-1 dominant mutant, no significant changes were observed in NO accumulation (Fig. 5A, B). Regarding the impact of the BR-regulated TFs BES1 and BZR1 on NO accumulation, major changes were detected in BES1 mutant backgrounds that did not appear in plants with an altered BZR1 locus. bes1-D mutant plants overaccumulate NO, while knockout bes1-1 plants showed the lowest NO content (Fig. 5A, B). Therefore, there is an impact on the homeostasis of NO in roots affected by alterations in the BR signalling pathway. It can be speculated that NO modulation of the BR signalling pathway mainly takes place through the TF BES1. Plants with hyperactive BES1 forms, as in bes1-D, or plants lacking BES1, as in bes1-1, accumulated higher or lower levels of NO. This may be established as a regulatory feedback mechanism that plants activate to finely balance growth promotion by BR and growth repression by NO.

To support these results, and in a similar manner, the accumulation of NO by DAF-FM in wild-type, cue1-6, nox1-1, and nia1nia2noa1-2 untreated or 1 µM eBL-treated plants was analysed (Fig. 5C, D). Compared with the wild-type signal, the fluorescence signal derived from NO detection after DAF-FM dye incubation was higher in the roots of cue1-6 and nox1-1 mutants and very low in the nia1nia2noa1-2 mutant, which served as positive and negative controls of DAF-FM, respectively. It should be noted that treatment with 1 µM eBL significantly increased the fluorescence signal derived from NO in all genotypes, with the strongest increase in the cue1-6 and nox1-1 mutants. Combined, these results indicate that the fluorescence levels observed in bes1-D are similar to the values obtained in the cue1-6 and nox1-1 backgrounds under control (mock) conditions, and that the promotion of BR signalling following treatment with eBL also leads to higher NO accumulation.

Nitric oxide-deficient plants hyperaccumulate brassinolide, the most bioactive brassinosteroid molecule

To further explain the phenotypical and transcriptional responses observed, a novel technique based on MALDI-MSI for in situ phytohormone visualization was performed on wild-type, nox1-1, and nia1nia2noa1-2 seedlings (n=3 for each genotype) (Boughton et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024) (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. S6). This technique allows detection in situ of the endogenous levels of two BR molecules, CS and BL, that have been shown to bind to the BR receptor family BRI1 and BRI1-like (Li and Chory, 1997). Of these two BR bioactive molecules, BL was found to be the most active in plants, with the highest capacity to bind BR receptors and activate BR signalling and responses (Aldukhi et al., 2020). Therefore, wild-type, nox1-1, and nia1nia2noa1-2 plants were used to establish the ion intensity maps, which show the in situ spatial distribution of CS and BL in representative samples (Fig. 6A, B). Images of other plant samples are shown in Supplementary Fig. S6A and B. In triplicate samples, all peaks were detected within the 0.1 Da range of the targeted theoretical deprotonated mass m/z 479.3378 assigned to BL ions and m/z 463.3429 assigned to CS ions. The established ion intensity maps highlighted the hypocotyl and primary root tips from wild-type and mutant samples and especially hypocotyls, whose BL intensities are significantly stronger than those of other tissues. The images obtained were evaluated for the in situ distribution of CS and BL ion intensities found in wild-type, nox1-1, and nia1nia2noa1-2 plants (n=3), shown in Fig. 6A and B via violin plots for whole seedlings (Fig. 6C, D) or just hypocotyls (Supplementary Fig. S6C, D) or roots (Supplementary Fig. S6E, F). Although the detected BL peaks in the boxplots demonstrated significant intensity variations due to tissue heterogeneity among different samples, a relatively higher BL signal in nia1nia2noa1-2 in whole plants, and in hypocotyls and roots was reported in their average ion intensity level in comparison with wild-type plants. Additionally, MALDI-MS/MS was used to validate CS and BL precursor ions and fragmentation ions with the molecular formulas of the identified fragments (Fig. 6E, F). These results highlighted that the growth-promoting phenotypes, and the eBL sensitivity observed in NO-deficient nia1nia2noa1-2 plants, can be a consequence of the hyperaccumulation of BL. With these data, it can be hypothesized that plants with reduced NO levels resemble plants with constitutively high levels of bioactive BR molecules that activate BR signalling to regulate BR target genes and promote post-germinative growth.

Discussion

The efficiency of integrating environmental signals and endogenous growth regulators determines plant growth. The gasotransmitter NO has been shown to impact hormonal signalling cascades to regulate different developmental stages such as germination, root and shoot development, and flowering (Albertos et al., 2015; Sánchez-Corrionero et al., 2023; Sánchez-Vicente et al., 2024). Plants with higher NO levels acquired through mutations in the CUE1 gene involved in NO production, such as the cue1-6 and nox1-1 alleles, or induced by pharmacological treatments with NO donors, show some phenotypes that resemble BR deficiency, such as shorter hypocotyls, in both light and dark conditions (Figs 1–3). In contrast, the hypocotyls of NO-deficient nia1nia2noa1-2 plants are much larger than wild-type hypocotyls under both light and dark conditions, and show an exaggerated response to eBL treatments >0.1 µM (Fig. 1). Furthermore, cue1-6 and nox1-1 show a lower response to the plant steroid hormone under light and dark conditions compared with the wild type. Therefore, NO represses some post-germination growth responses attributed mainly to BRs.

When testing the NO growth-inhibitory responses of mutants in which BR signalling is dampened, such as bin2-1, bes1-1, or bzr1-2, it was observed that NO treatments had minor effects on the repression of hypocotyl elongation compared with wild-type plants (Fig. 2) (Li et al., 2001; He et al., 2005; Sandhu et al., 2013). Accordingly, the dominant mutants bes1-D and bzr1-D, with constitutively active BR signalling and responses (Yin et al., 2002; Wang et al., 2002), responded to NO more than the knockout and bin2-1 mutant plants. These results indicated that NO may be affecting the BR signalling cascade at the level of TFs to control hypocotyl elongation. Given that NO also has an impact on other hormonal pathways that regulate growth, such as the suppression of auxin signalling by NO, it is possible that a complex network formed by NO and various phytohormones is regulating post-germination growth in plants (Nakamura et al., 2003a, b; Shi et al., 2015; Castillo et al., 2018). To strengthen the results obtained on activating BR responses under conditions of NO overaccumulation or deficiency, double cue1-6;bes1-D and quadruple nia1nia2noa1-2;bes1-D mutant plants were generated (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. S3). cue1-6;bes1-D plants with NO overaccumulation and hyperactive forms of BES1, and therefore with constitutively active BR responses, showed shorter hypocotyls than the single bes1-D mutant, implying that NO represses BR-induced hypocotyl elongation. This result is supported by nia1nia2noa1-2;bes1-D plants with NO deficiency and BES1 hyperactive forms, which showed larger hypocotyls than those of bes1-D (Fig. 3C, D). Therefore, there is phenotypic and genetic evidence that NO represses BR-induced hypocotyl growth during post-germinative development, while the elongation of the primary root can be inhibited by high doses of both NO and BRs.

The NO-inhibitory effects during post-germinative growth and development in seedlings were correlated with changes in the expression of a set of BR-regulated genes. Plants with increased NO levels by pharmacological or genetic means showed constitutive induction of CPD, ROT3, and BR6ox2 (Fig. 4; Supplementary Fig. S4). This group of BR biosynthetic genes are strongly inhibited upon BR signalling activation as a negative feedback mechanism to turn off the signalling of the hormone and maintain BRs at suitable levels for plant growth (Martínez et al., 2018). Since BRs promote and NO represses growth, based on results in Figs 1–3, and supported by previously reported results (Beligni and Lamattina, 2000; Lozano-Juste and León, 2011; Castillo et al., 2018), wild-type plants treated with GSNO or nox1-1 plants showed up-regulation of this set of BR biosynthetic genes, as happens in BR-deficient or insensitive plants with reduced growth capacities (Noguchi et al., 1999). Correspondingly, in NO-deficient backgrounds, such as nia1nia2noa1-2, expression levels of CPD and BR6ox2 are constitutively reduced, as previously reported in plants after BR treatment or in genetic backgrounds where BR signalling is constitutively activated (Shimada et al., 2003; Castillo et al., 2018).

Regarding the BR-induced growth-promoting genes, increasing NO levels, mostly by pharmacological means, produced constitutive down-regulation of this set of genes, with SAUR-AC1, IAA5, GA3ox1, and GA20ox1 being among the most strongly reduced (Fig. 4; Supplementary Fig. S4). Remarkably, these genes were found to be up-regulated in the NO-deficient nia1nia2noa1-2 mutant. This would explain the growth-promoting phenotypes observed in this mutant background, since these genes are induced after BR treatments or in genetic backgrounds where BR signalling is constitutively activated (Nakamura et al., 2003a; Unterholzner et al., 2015). These results indicate that NO has some impact on the transcriptional regulation of genes regulated by BRs, and the interaction established with other growth-promoting hormones, such as auxins and gibberellins, for the regulation of post-germination growth (Sanz et al., 2015; Castillo et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2024).

The NO accumulation in BR mutant roots was tested using the DAF-FM probe, resulting in NO overaccumulation in the gain-of-function bes1-D mutant and lower NO levels in the loss-of-function bes1-1 mutant (Fig. 5). Furthermore, NO accumulation levels in bes1-D roots were similar to those found in NO-overproducing mutants cue1-6 and nox1-1. This could explain why bes1-D, cue1-6, and nox1-1 all have shorter primary roots, as shown in Figs 1–3. Likewise, wild-type plants treated with eBL showed extremely high levels of NO accumulation (Fig. 5). It has been reported that BR treatments cause NO accumulation in the root, and that this effect occurs downstream of the BR receptor BRI1 (Tossi et al., 2013). It has also been described in other plant species that BR treatment leads to higher NIA and NOS-like activity (Li et al., 2020); nevertheless, there is no evidence of this activity increment in A. thaliana. This reported NO accumulation seems to depend on BES1, since only BES1 mutant backgrounds showed significant differences in the accumulation of NO-dependent fluorescence. BR phenotypes are also dose dependent, as high doses of BR reduce plant root development and plant growth (Chaiwanon and Wang, 2015; Jantapo et al., 2021). Since BRs and NO display antagonistic roles during post-germinative growth, it can be suggested that plants with enhanced BR signalling may activate NO production as a negative feedback mechanism to finely adjust growth and development (Wei and Li, 2020). In bes1-D plants, the higher NO accumulation detected could be a part of this regulatory loop to maintain BR outcomes at control levels, and thus facilitate plant growth. In addition to the results presented herein, a transcriptional down-regulation of BES1 by NO has been shown (Castillo et al., 2018). This would also support the precise regulatory mechanisms that plants have developed, where BR increases NO accumulation, which in turn affects BR signalling and responses.

The presence of BR bioactive molecules is essential for normal plant development (Nolan et al., 2020). The accumulation of BL and CS was determined by a novel technique based on MALDI-MSI for in situ phytohormone visualization (Giacomoni et al., 2014; Boughton et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2021) (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. S6). MALDI-MSI results revealed that the NO-deficient background nia1nia2noa1-2 hyperaccumulated the most bioactive BR molecule, BL. Furthermore, in nia1nia2noa1-2, the set of BR marker genes analysed responded as in plants treated with eBL (Shimada et al., 2003). The hyperaccumulation of BL in plants with NO deficiency may explain the growth-promoting phenotypes exhibited by the triple deficient mutant during post-germinative growth. In contrast, the NO overaccumulator nox1-1 mutant plants display defects in growth, and a lower content of BR molecules was detected. It has been reported that BR null or weak signalling alleles accumulate higher levels of BRs on adult plants as a mechanism to flood the system to try to activate BR signalling and BR outcomes (Noguchi et al., 1999). However, in nox1-1 mutants, with presumably a functional BR signalling pathway, the excess NO may be altering BR homeostasis and/or responses to accumulate fewer bioactive BRs that results in the growth deficiency phenotypes of this genotype. Moreover, nox1-1 plants showed increased levels of CPD and BR6ox2 gene expression, characteristically reported for plants with some BR deficiency (Bancos et al., 2002; Tanaka et al., 2005). It may be beneficial to consider in future scientific approaches whether NO has any effect on BR conjugation and catabolism, that also control activity, transport, and storage of BR bioactive forms (Wei and Li, 2020). Hence, NO accumulation can affect the presence of bioactive BRs that control plant growth.

All the phenotypical, transcriptional, and biochemical results obtained are presented in an organ-specific model of crosstalk between NO and BRs to regulate plant growth and development (Fig. 7). In hypocotyls and roots, NO promotes the expression of BR biosynthetic genes as a counteracting mechanism to compensate the growth defects of NO overaccumulator plants. However, NO reduces the accumulation of bioactive BR molecules, implying that NO may be inducing BR conjugation and/or catabolism. Once BRs bind to the receptor and the signalling is activated, NO represses BR signalling outcomes to inhibit post-germinative growth and development in the hypocotyl, but could be contributing to repressing primary root elongation depending on high BR doses. Moreover, the BR-induced accumulation of NO in roots may contribute to balance the activation of BR signalling and, therefore, the BR-regulated responses during post-germinative growth and development.

Proposed model for NO and BR interplay to regulate post-germinative growth. NO and BRs display antagonistic roles in the regulation of hypocotyl elongation, but both regulatory molecules have an impact on root growth and development. In hypocotyls and roots, NO induces the expression of BR biosynthetic genes and down-regulates the accumulation of bioactive BR molecules, probably by promoting BR conjugation and/or BR catabolism. Once active BRs bind to the receptor and the BR signalling and responses are activated, NO inhibits some of the BR signalling outcomes to repress hypocotyl elongation. In roots, activated BR signalling leads to inhibition of root elongation and growth, responses also attributed to unbalanced NO accumulation, by either high or low NO content. Therefore, altered NO content in roots may further contribute to BR regulation of root growth and development. Moreover, BR signalling activation promotes the accumulation of NO in roots, and this can be observed as a negative feedback mechanism to balance BR-induced responses.
