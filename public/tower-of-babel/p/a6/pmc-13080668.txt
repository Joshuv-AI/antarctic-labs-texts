Background

Anomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery (AAOCA) is a rare congenital condition, affecting approximately 0.1% to 0.3% of the population.1 Though it is rare and often asymptomatic for a long time, AAOCA can increase the likelihood of severe adverse cardiac events, such as sudden cardiac death (SCD) and myocardial infarction. These risks are especially relevant in young, active individuals, as physical exertion can exacerbate their symptoms and increase their risk of SCD.2

Anomalous origin of the right coronary artery (AAORCA) is more common than its left variant. In most cases of AAORCA, the right coronary artery (RCA) originates from the left sinus of Valsalva. The abnormal origin of the artery can restrict blood flow to the myocardium and conduction system of the right ventricle, potentially leading to tissue hypoperfusion and ischemic pain. This condition may remain asymptomatic and is often diagnosed incidentally.3 In symptomatic cases and in patients with high SCD risk, surgery is recommended.4 Several theories exist to explain the pathophysiology of ischemia that results from AAORCA: compression of both the interarterial segment of the RCA by the aorta and pulmonary artery and of the intramural segment via systolic tension increase in the aortic wall itself; kinking or narrowing of the vessel as a result of a sharp origin angle of the RCA, especially during physical activity; and increased risk of spasms or atherosclerosis due to a longer RCA course.5

Multiple imaging modalities are often required to highlight specific features of this anatomical anomaly and aid in the design of a personalized surgical approach. Whereas transthoracic echocardiography is frequently used for initial assessment, multimodality imaging is often necessary.4 During invasive coronary angiography, the anomalous origin of the RCA make it difficult to engage the artery for proper dye injection. Consequently, computed tomography angiography (CTA) has become an increasingly valuable, noninvasive tool, providing detailed coronary anatomy for accurate diagnosis and surgical planning. CTA, with its high spatial resolution, aids in the identification of AAORCA besides enabling detection of coronary artery disease.6

Surgical options to address AAORCA include unroofing of the intramural segment, coronary artery bypass graft (CABG), relocation of the RCA into the right sinus of Valsalva, and neo-ostium creation.7,8 Relocation and unroofing are the most common approaches. Their use depends on whether the course of the RCA is predominantly interarterial or intramural.1 The length of the intramural segment and the proximity of the RCA ostium to the left main orifice also influence the surgical strategy. CABG surgery creates a new pathway for blood flow to bypass the abnormal segment of the RCA, while ligating the native RCA near the abnormal origin. However, high rates of graft failure after CABG surgery have led to a shift toward unroofing and relocation procedures, and CABG is recommended only when distal coronary artery disease is also present, requiring revascularization.2 Here, we report a case of AAORCA, exploring the clinical presentation, diagnosis, and tailored surgical solution of a specific anatomy.

Case Summary

A 55-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with an episode of emesis and several episodes of severe palpitations that had worsened over time. At the time of the emergency department visit, the palpitations were accompanied by spasmodic chest pain and pressure sensations. Her symptoms were exacerbated by physical exertion, including light activities such as walking up stairs. Additionally, her troponin levels were elevated, peaking at 105 ng/mL with no electrocardiogram changes, constituting the diagnosis of non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.

She had significant risk factors for coronary artery disease, including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and a body mass index of 30.8. Her family medical history included heart attacks in both parents. She consumed 6 units of alcohol per week and had a history of breast surgery, colonoscopy, partial thyroidectomy, and tubal ligation.

Because her symptoms indicated possible coronary artery disease, her cardiologist recommended left heart catheterization. This revealed an anomalous origin of the RCA, but the ostium could not be engaged. The diagnosis of AAORCA was established, and follow-up CTA confirmed the RCA originating from the left sinus of Valsalva and visualized a slitlike ostium with a narrow intramural course (Figure 1A). The patient was referred to surgery for resolution of the anomaly.

Preoperative and Postoperative CTA

(A) Preoperative computed tomography angiography (CTA) reveals a normal-sized right coronary artery (RCA) that arises abnormally from the left sinus of Valsalva where it tracks between the aorta (AO) and pulmonary artery (PA). No significant atherosclerotic plaque is present; however, there is severe lumen narrowing at approximately 10 mm from the ostium (asterisk). The RCA shows a long intramural course. (B) Postoperative CTA confirmed that the RCA had been successfully reimplanted into its correct anatomical position, now originating from the right coronary sinus of Valsalva.

The procedure began with median sternotomy, followed by longitudinal opening of the pericardium and aorto-atrial cannulation (Video 1). The patient was then placed on cardiopulmonary bypass, and an aortic cross-clamp was applied. Cardioplegia was delivered into the aortic root. The aorta was transected, revealing that the RCA originated from the left sinus of Valsalva, proximal to the left main coronary artery. The RCA showed a 1.5-cm intramural course behind the commissure between the left coronary cusp (LCC) and right coronary cusp (RCC).

The commissure between the LCC and RCC was excised from the aorta, allowing for unroofing of the intramural segment of the RCA. At the end of the unroofing procedure, the RCA and left main coronary artery were sufficiently distant from each other, and the space between them provided an opportunity for the creation of an RCA-surrounding button. The gap in the aorta was closed with a pericardial patch. The commissure between the LCC and RCC was reinserted on the pericardial patch using 5-0 polypropylene pledgeted sutures. An anastomosis was performed between the aortic root and the ascending aorta to visualize the site of reimplantation of the RCA. Finally, the RCA was reimplanted to its anatomical site and was secured with a 5-0 polypropylene suture.

After careful de-airing, the aortic clamp was released, and the heart resumed spontaneous sinus rhythm. The RCA demonstrated good position without any stenosis, and the patient was weaned from bypass without difficulty. RCA transient time flow was measured with a Medistim probe (Medistim), showing satisfactory results. Decannulation was performed without complications. The patient tolerated the procedure well and was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit.

Postoperative transesophageal echocardiography showed no aortic regurgitation and normal biventricular function. The patient's postoperative course was uneventful. Postoperative CTA confirmed proper, anatomical RCA origin in the right coronary sinus of Valsalva, and good distal contrast filling signified proximal patency (Figure 1B). The patient was discharged on postoperative day 6, has been doing well postoperatively, and continues to follow up with her cardiology specialists.

Discussion

This case demonstrates some of the important, everyday challenges clinicians face when diagnostically evaluating and designing treatment plans for patients with AAORCA. In contrast to anomalous origin of a left coronary artery, an AAORCA diagnosis is not an absolute indication for surgery.1 This patient presented with non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, a slitlike RCA ostium, and a severely narrowed intramural coronary segment. Therefore, surgical repositioning of the RCA was indicated.

As in this patient, the anomalously positioned RCA orifice is often difficult or not possible to engage with preoperative coronary catheterization; therefore the surgeon is challenged to rely on cardiac CTA for preoperative planning. Certain important details, such as the exact course of the intramural RCA segment, are revealed only during surgery, necessitating a dynamic approach to surgical planning. Quick adaptation skills enable the interventionist to incorporate newly discovered information into the plan for the remainder of the procedure.

In this case, unroofing the intramural segment was necessary because of the proximity of the left and right coronary orifices. Unroofing created a greater distance between the 2 ostia. The intramural course of the RCA, however, was not sufficiently long. Consequently, unroofing did not reposition the RCA orifice all the way into the right Valsalva sinus. In fact, unroofing juxtaposed the RCA ostium to the commissural pillar, increasing the possibility of ostium obstruction by the commissure. Therefore, commissural pillar takedown followed by button creation and transposition were necessary elements of the resolution procedure.

Commissural pillar takedown and reinsertion may result in aortic valve insufficiency. This was, however, ruled out in this patient with postoperative imaging. Unroofing exposes subendothelial structures to intra-aortic pressures normally not encountered by these structures, thereby increasing the risk of wall dissection.4 Postoperative CTA concluded an intact aortic wall in this patient. Long-term follow-up is indicated to rule out subacute and chronic complications.

Conclusions

Overall, our case demonstrates the complexity of challenges cardiologists and cardiac surgeons face when treating patients with AAORCA. Surgical indications for these right-sided anomalies are not as clearly outlined as for their left-sided variants. Operative risks should be weighed against the risk of disease complications, and psychological elements such as fear of SCD and athletic aspirations in young patients also need to be incorporated in the detailed planning process. Although guidelines for the management of AAORCA are increasingly available, publications on surgical technique are still an area of opportunity. This highlights the importance of multicenter collaboration among cardiac surgeons experienced in AAORCA repair.
