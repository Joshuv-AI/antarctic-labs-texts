Alteration of gut microbial ecology by the direct activation of the brain: Inverse gut-microbiome-brain dynamics
Authors: I-Ching Wang, Jessica L. Swanson, Jillian L. Waters, Mikhail Y. Kochukov, Shelly A. Buffington, Sean W. Dooling, Benjamin R. Arenkiel, Mauro Costa-Mattioli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe gut-microbiome-brain axis is a bidirectional communication system influencing host physiology and overall fitness. While “bottom-up” effects—where gut microbes influence brain function and behavior—are well established, direct evidence for “top-down” modulation—where the brain shapes the gut microbial ecology—remains elusive. Here, we show that the selective expression of the bacterial ion channel mNaChBac in glutamatergic lateral habenula (LHb) neurons increases their bursting activity and leads to endophentypes associated with depression. Importantly, this chronic activation leads to changes in the gut microbiome composition over time, as reflected by shifts in alpha-diversity and alterations in specific microbial taxa. These findings provide direct evidence that the persistent activation of the brain acts as a selective pressure that affects the stability and diversity of the gut microbiome, providing a new dimension by which gut-microbiome-brain interactions may cooperate to modulate host physiology and health.

Introduction

Gut microbial communities exhibit remarkable diversity and complexity, which is the result of complex co-evolution spanning millions of years with their host organisms.1,2 Multiple selective pressures can shape the structure and function of the gut microbial community.3 For instance, in response to changes in environmental conditions (e.g., nutrients), competition for resources is established. The resulting selective pressure can suppress the growth of some microbes, while simultaneously favoring the growth of others. This ultimately results in a change in the dynamics and structure of the microbial population.

Diet is arguably the best studied selective pressure that rapidly and profoundly alters the gut microbiome of both humans and animals.1,2,3,4,5 Several mechanisms have been proposed by which diet influences the gut microbial ecology. First, distinct nutrients differentially change the overall metabolism of the gut microbiome (e.g., fiber-rich diets promote the growth of fiber-degrading bacteria, while sugar-rich diets promote the growth of carbohydrate-degrading bacteria).2,6,7,8,9 Second, diet can alter the physiology of the gut itself by changing its pH or oxygen levels, conditions that will select for the growth of certain microbial species over others.2,10,11 Moreover, diet can change the mucosal integrity as well as intestinal permeability, allowing some microbes to cross the gut barrier and thus affect the microbial ecology.12 Third, some dietary components exhibit antimicrobial properties that can directly inhibit the growth of some microorganisms.13 Fourth, diet-induced changes in the immune system can also regulate microbial ecology.14,15 Consequently, by impacting such diverse factors, diet serves as a potent selective pressure that ultimately impacts microbial ecology.2

Changes in microbial ecology could have a profound effect on host physiology and behavior. For instance, we previously found that a maternal high-fat diet leads to changes in the gut microbial ecology that is causally linked with autism-like behaviors in the offspring.16 Of interest, a high-fat diet selects against the commensal bacterial species Limosilactobacillus (L.) reuteri (formerly known as Lactobacillus reuteri)16,17 and its reintroduction selectively reversed the social deficits in a wide range of models of autism spectrum disorder (ASD).16,18,19,20,21,22 Mechanistically, L. reuteri promotes social behavior by increasing biopterin levels in the gut, and signals to the brain via the vagus nerve.18,20 In the central nervous system, L. reuteri promotes social reward by targeting the oxytocin-dopaminergic circuits,18,20 and these effects occur independent of the adaptive immune system.22 Finally, and likely of high clinical relevance, two recent clinical trial studies showed that L. reuteri improves social functioning in children with autism, consistent with preclinical findings.23,24

This body of work exemplifies the dominant focus of the field to date: a “bottom-up approach” aimed at understanding how the gut microbiome modulates brain function and behavior. In contrast, little is known whether the brain itself can impose selective pressures and directly shape the composition and function of the gut microbiome—a concept we refer to here as “top-down modulation. The idea that the brain can modulate the gut microbiome is supported by correlational, rather than direct causal evidence, including studies showing that the autonomic nervous system is able to alter gut physiology (e.g., promoting gut motility, secretion, and blood flow).25 Additionally, patients with neuropsychiatric disorders (e.g., including depression) exhibit changes in gut microbiome composition.26,27 However, it remains unclear whether specific brain regions or temporal dynamics of that activity can modulate the gut microbial ecology.

Many mouse models for neuropsychiatric disorders also feature changes in the gut microbiome composition,28,29,30 but these models often rely on constitutive genetic mutations (e.g., knockout, knock-in) or exposure to environmental stressors. While such mutations and stressors affect the brain, they may also impact peripheral systems, including the immune system, the enteric nervous system, and feeding behavior. Therefore, it is difficult to determine whether microbiome alterations arise directly from changes in brain activity or indirectly through these other system effects.

Here, by using a selective brain-centered approach, we provide direct evidence that the persistent activation of a brain region implicated in depression is sufficient to alter the gut microbiome composition over time. These findings support the concept of top-down modulation and highlight a new dimension of the gut-microbiome-brain axis. Investigating how the brain shapes microbial ecology offers critical insight into the mechanisms by which neural activity can influence host physiology, behavior, and overall health.

Results

mNaChBac expression in lateral habenula neurons drives their bursting activity

To directly test whether the brain can modulate the gut microbiome, we first aimed to identify a suitable model. Changes in the microbiome have been identified in several neurological and neuropsychiatric disorders,31,32 among which depression is one of the most prevalent, affecting more than 300 million people worldwide.33,34 Numerous brain regions are implicated in depression, including the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, the nucleus accumbens, and the lateral habenula (LHb).35,36,37,38 Notably, the direct stimulation of select brain regions has been sufficient to induce depression-like behaviors in mice.37,39

We focused on the LHb because it is thought to modulate depression through its interactions with various brain regions implicated in mediating negative emotional states.40,41 LHb hyperactivity is observed across animal models of depression (from zebrafish to rodents) as well as individuals suffering from depression.42,43,44,45 More importantly, the direct stimulation of LHb via optogenetics is sufficient to induce depression-like behaviors in mice.46 In addition, the rapid and robust antidepressant ketamine acts by attenuating LHb neuronal burst firing. These results demonstrate that LHb neuron hyperactivity underlies depression-like behaviors.46,47

Given that the gut microbiome is altered in both animal models of depression and humans with depression,26,27,48,49,50 and that LHb hyperactivation leads to depression, we hypothesized that the persistent activation of LHb neurons would lead to changes in the gut microbial ecology over time. We predict that persistent LHb activation would exert sustained neural and physiological effects that create a lasting selective pressure on the gut microbiota, potentially driving stable shifts in the gut microbial composition.

Several methods could be used to test this hypothesis and selectively activate LHb neurons. For example, techniques such as optogenetics allow for the acute stimulation of specific brain regions. While optogenetics offers precise, acute control of neuronal activity, it is less suited for chronic stimulation due to the need for continuous light delivery and repeated handling or restraint, which could independently influence stress levels and confound results. In fact, we initially attempted to adapt an acute optogenetic stimulation protocol of the LHb, similar to that described by Hu and colleagues,46 for chronic use. However, prolonged stimulation under this paradigm resulted in neuronal toxicity (data not shown), further supporting the need for a different approach to achieve sustained LHb activation.

As an alternative, we employed a method to chronically and selectively enhance the excitability of LHb neurons by expressing mNaChBac, a bacterial voltage gated sodium channel51 previously shown to increase spontaneous firing and prolong action potential duration in basal forebrain neurons.52 Unlike optogenetics, mNaChBac does not require external light for activation or repeated restraint. To achieve LHb-specific expression, we bilaterally injected an adeno-associated virus (AAV) encoding mNaChBac52 in a FLEx (flip-excision) configuration (AAV-Flex-eGFP-p2a-mNaChBac) into the LHb of vGlut2-Cre mice (Figures 1A and 1B). In this vector, mNaChBac and eGFP are expressed in an inverted, antisense orientation and are flanked by two loxP sites in opposite directions. In Cre-expressing neurons, Cre recombinase inverts the sequence, enabling the co-expression of eGFP-p2a-mNaChBac via the self-cleaving p2a peptide. This approach ensures that expression is restricted to glutamatergic LHb neurons. As expected, GFP expression was confined to the LHb (Figure 1B). These mice are referred to as mNaChBac mice. We first examined whether the expression of mNaChBac in LHb vGlut2 glutamatergic neurons changed their electrophysiological properties. To assess intrinsic excitability, we performed both cell-attached and whole-cell patch-clamp recordings on LHb glutamatergic neurons expressing either mNaChBac-GFP or GFP. Compared to GFP control neurons, mNaChBac-expressing neurons exhibited prolonged action potentials (APs) followed by current injection (Figure S1A). Moreover, we found a decrease in the mean membrane voltage between spontaneous APs (Figure S1B), and lowered AP thresholds (Figure S1C) in mNaChBac-expressing neurons. We did not observe any differences in cell capacitance, input resistance, or membrane time constants between groups (Figures S1D–S1F). We then categorized LHb neurons based on their spontaneous firing patterns [non-firing (silent), single AP firing (Figures 1C and S1G), and bursting firing (Figure 1D)]. Notably, we found an increased proportion of neurons with burst firing among the mNaChBac-expressing neurons compared to control-GFP neurons (Figure 1E, p = 0.003, Chi-Square test). Taken together, these data indicate that mNaChBac-expressing LHb neurons have increased overall excitability as well as a greater probability of spontaneous bursting, which has been shown to underlie depression-like behavior.41,46,47,53,54,55,56,57

Persistent hyperactivation of lateral habenula neurons leads to depression-like behaviors

To assess whether mNaChBac-mediated LHb hyperactivation could drive depression-like behaviors, we first used the forced swim test (FST). In this test, mice are placed in a container of water. Since they cannot escape, the time the mice spend immobile (i.e., cessation of swimming) is taken as a measure of despair or depression-like behavior (Figure 1F). We found that compared to control GFP mice, mNaChBac mice showed increased immobile time, indicating that mNaChBac expression in the LHb elicits depression-like behavior. To confirm these results, we employed the tail suspension test (TST) as an orthogonal depression-like behavior test (Figure 1G).58 In this test, mice are suspended by their tails, with the measured time spent immobile reflecting despair or depression-like behavior. Consistent with the FST findings, in the TST test, we observed increased immobile time in mNaChBac mice compared to controls (Figure 1G). Thus, persistent and selective hyperactivation of LHb neurons triggers depression-like behaviors.

The clinical prevalence of depression is higher in women33 and sex differences were observed in other mouse models of depression.59 When we assessed depression-like behaviors in male and female mice, we found no differences between sexes (Figures S2A and S2B). Importantly, locomotor activity was not altered in mNaChBac mice compared to control mice, as determined by the total distance traveled in the open field test (OFT; Figures S2C and S2D). Thus, the depression-associated immobility observed in the mNaChBac mice is not likely due to changes in overall mobility.

Finally, we tested whether mNaChBac expression in the LHb leads to anxiety-like behavior, as determined by the elevated plus maze (EPM; Figure S2E). The EPM consists of two open arms and two closed arms. Mice are naturally averse to open and elevated spaces; thus, mice exhibiting anxiety-like behavior typically spend less time in the open arms than in the closed arms.60 We found no differences in the time spent in the EPM open arms between the control and mNaChBac mice, regardless of sex (Figure S2F), indicating that mNaChBac mice do not exhibit anxiety-like behaviors. Collectively, we concluded that the chronic hyperactivation of the LHb neurons by the expression of mNaChBac leads to selective depression-like behaviors.

Persistent hyperactivation of lateral habenula neurons alters the gut microbiome composition over time

Several studies have shown alterations in the gut microbiome composition in animal models of depression, as well as humans with depression,26,27,49,61 but whether these changes are induced by the brain directly is unclear. Having found that persistent LHb activation using mNaChBac results in depression-like behaviors, we next examined whether this brain-centered paradigm could perturb the gut microbial ecology over time. To this end, we first performed 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing on fecal samples collected at baseline, as well as four, eight-, and thirteen-week post-stimulation. Notably, we found a significant difference in beta-diversity (overall gut microbiome composition, or between sample diversity) between groups over time (Figure 2A). We next measured alpha-diversity (within sample diversity) by two different metrics: i) observed operational taxonomic units (OTUs), which describes the richness, and ii) Shannon diversity, which reflects both the evenness and richness. Both observed OTUs and Shannon diversity showed a trend to increase in mNaChBac mice (but not in GFP mice) over time (Figure 2B). To increase statistical power, we also expanded our sample size by including an additional cohort of animals. Consistent with the 16S results, WGS data revealed a significant increase in alpha-diversity over time in the mNaChBac group, as measured by the Shannon diversity index (Figure 3B). Additionally, beta-diversity analyses showed distinct microbial community trajectories between mNaChBac and GFP mice over time (Figure 3A). As expected, given its higher taxonomic resolution, WGS-based differential abundance analysis using MaAsLin2 identified 23 microbial species that significantly differed between groups over time (Figures 3C, 3D, and S4), compared to only 4 species detected using 16S sequencing. Interestingly, the relative abundances of microbial taxa in mNaChBac mice fluctuated over time, whereas those in GFP controls remained more stable (Figure S4). These data suggest that persistent brain-induced modulation exerts a dynamic and selective pressure on gut microbial communities. Since diet is a major determinant of gut microbiota composition, we monitored body weight and food intake throughout the study. No significant differences were observed in body weight (Figure S3A) or feeding behavior between groups (Figures S3B and S3C). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the chronic stimulation of the LHb induces selective and dynamic changes in gut microbial ecology over time.

Discussion

Over the past decade, there has been an increased interest in the bidirectional communication between the gut microbiota and the brain, recognizing this interaction as a central effector of overall health.62,63 Much of the work in this field has focused on the “bottom-up” signaling—how gut microbes influence the brain—elucidating the mechanism(s) through which the gut microbiome (or specific microbial species) modulates behavior and synaptic function in various neurological disorders.18,20,64,65 In contrast, relatively little is known about the “top down” pathway(s)—whether, and how, the brain, particularly specific brain regions, can directly influence the composition and function of the gut microbiome.

In this study, we focused on the LHb, a brain region known to drive depression-like behaviors when hyperactive.46 While changes in the gut microbiome composition have been observed in animal models and human patients with depression,26,49,66,67,68,69 these studies often rely on chronic stress paradigms that broadly affect the animal physiology (e.g., feeding behavior, metabolism). Therefore, it remains unclear whether the gut microbiome changes are a direct consequence of brain activity or secondary to systemic effects.70

To address this, we employed the targeted stimulation of LHb neurons while longitudinally monitoring the gut microbial community. Importantly, our manipulation did not induce significant differences in body weight or feeding behavior, supporting the conclusion that the observed gut microbiome alterations were driven by brain-specific activity rather than confounding physiological factors (Figure S3).

Persistent perturbations are more likely than acute ones to alter a system’s ecological state by imposing sustained selective pressures. Consistent with this, the acute optogenetic stimulation of the LHb—while sufficient to induce depressive-like behaviors as previously reported46—did not produce detectable changes in the gut microbiome (data not shown). We then employed chronic optogenetic stimulation, but unfortunately led to neuronal toxicity (data not shown), consistent with previous reports.71,72 Therefore, we employed the mNaChBac system to achieve the persistent and non-toxic activation of LHb neurons. This chronic-stimulation paradigm effectively led to depression-like behaviors, evidenced by the decreased mobility in both the forced swim and tail suspension tests (Figures 1F and 1G). More importantly, mNaChBac-mediated chronic stimulation of the LHb was sufficient to induce selective changes in the gut microbial ecology (Figure 2). We observed a progressive increase in alpha-diversity over time in mNaChBac mice compared to GFP-controls, as measured by both 16S and WGS sequencing (Figures 2B and 3B). WGS-based differential abundance analysis further identified 23 microbial species that were significantly altered in mNaChBac mice over time (Figure 3D). Among these were Prevotella, Parabacteroides goldsteinii and Phocaeicola dorei (formerly Bacteroides dorei), all of which have been previously reported to be altered in animal models of depression.30,73,74 These findings support the notion that, similar to diet, the chronic stimulation of a specific brain region can act as a selective pressure that reshapes gut microbial ecology. However, unlike dietary intervention, persistent LHb brain stimulation results in more subtle and selective changes (Figures 2 and 3). It is possible that broader or more pronounced effects on the gut microbiome may require persistent stimulation of additional brain regions or coordinated activity across neural circuits. Indeed, several other brain areas have been implicated in the pathophysiology of depression,75,76,77 suggesting the interesting possibility that the gut microbiota may be differentially influenced depending on the neural networks engaged. Interestingly, two recent studies published during the submission of our article support this claim. For example, inhibition (via chronic stress or chemogenetics) of the amygdala, a brain region implicated in stress responses and depression, leads to decreases in Lactobacillus species.78 Moreover, acute activity changes (via chemogenetics) of hunger-related neurons of the hypothalamus, another brain region implicated in depression, can induce rapid changes in the abundance of select microbes in specific regions of the gut of fasted mice.79

The mechanism by which persistent activation of the LHb modulates the gut microbiome remains to be fully elucidated. One potential route involves indirect signaling through the vagus nerve, a well-established conduit of bidirectional gut-brain communication.80,81 While the LHb does not project directly to the vagus nerve, it sends inputs to the locus coeruleus, which in turn modulates key vagal-regulatory centers such as the nucleus tractus solitarius82 and dorsal motor nucleus.83 The vagus nerve has been implicated in depression, and vagus nerve stimulation is known to alleviate depression-like behaviors through the activation of 5-HT1A receptor-expressing neurons in the dorsal raphe nucleus—a known downstream target of the LHb and a common site of antidepressant action.84,85

A second potential mechanism involves modulations of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which is frequently dysregulated in patients with depression.39 Chronic LHb stimulation could lead to prolonged HPA axis engagement, thereby influencing gut physiology and microbial composition through stress hormones. Thus, measuring circulating corticosterone levels could help clarify whether this neuroendocrine axis contributes to the observed microbial changes.

Third, LHb activity plays a role in regulating sleep and circadian rhythms.86,87 Disruption in these processes has been shown to influence gut microbial dynamics,88,89 suggesting that alterations in circadian biology or sleep architecture may provide another route by which chronic LHb stimulation affects gut microbial ecology.

Another intriguing possibility involves the intestinal epithelium, which serves as a critical interface between host and microbiota. In chronic restraint stress (CRS) models of depression, where LHb hyperactivation has been observed,41,46 increased production of indole-3-acetate by Lactobacillus murinus was found to impair intestinal stem cell lineage commitment, resulting in shortened villi and crypts.90 Such structural changes to the gut epithelium could alter the microbial niche and contribute to shifts in community composition.

Finally, how brain-induced changes in the gut microbiome impact brain function and overall organismal health remains to be determined. It is unlikely that the microbiome alterations resulting from the mNaChBac-mediated chronic stimulation of the LHb are the primary drivers of depressive-like behaviors, as acute LHb activation alone is sufficient to induce these behavioral phenotypes. Accordingly, transfer of microbiota from the mNaChBac mice did not lead to depression-like behaviors in the germ-free recipient mice, supporting that the LHb activation causes depression-like behaviors independent of the changes in the microbiome (Figure S5). Instead, we hypothesize that the microbiome changes may play a role in the development or persistence of depression-related comorbidities. Given that depression frequently co-occurs with conditions such as eating disorders, anxiety, and substance use disorders,91 future studies should investigate whether chronic LHb-driven alterations in the gut microbiome contribute to these overlapping pathologies.

Together, our findings provide direct evidence that persistent activity in a specific brain region is sufficient to reshape the gut microbiome, supporting the notion of the top-down modulation of the gut-brain axis and opening new avenues for understanding how brain states influence systemic physiology and health.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we demonstrate that the chronic activation of LHb using mNaChBac leads to selective changes in the gut microbial ecology. However, several limitations and directions for future research must be acknowledged. First, the mechanisms by which LHb stimulation influences the gut microbiome remain unclear. Further work is needed to determine whether this effect is mediated through neural, hormonal, or immune pathways. Second, while we identified specific microbial shifts, the functional consequences of these changes—on both brain function and overall organismal health—have yet to be fully explored. Future studies should investigate whether the microbial changes could predispose individuals to comorbidities associated with depression, such as addiction or obesity.

Experimental model and study participant details

Male and female V-Glut2-IRES-Cre mice (JAX#016963)100,101 were used in this study. Mice were kept on a 12-hour light/dark cycle and had ad libitum access to food and water. Animal care and experimental procedures were approved by Baylor College of Medicine’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee in accordance with all guidelines set forth by the U.S. National Institute of Health.

Method details

Stereotaxic surgery

Surgical procedures were performed as previously described.52,100 Briefly, prior to surgery, mice were injected with the analgesics Meloxicam (5 mg/kg) and Buprenorphine (1 mg/kg). At the time of surgery, mice were anesthetized with 1-3% vaporized isoflurane with oxygen. Stereotaxic injections were performed using a stereotaxic instrument connected to Angle Two software (Leica) for coordinate guidelines to target regions of the brain. For behavioral experiments, mNaChBac mice were injected with AAV-Ef1a-Flex-eGFP-p2a-mNaChBac-WPRE-hGHpA (serotype DJ8), a plasmid subcloned from a gift from Dr. Mingshan Xue (mxue@bcm.edu) and packaged by the Neuroconnectivity Core at the Jan and Dan Duncan Neurological Research Institute.52 Control mice were injected with AAV-Ef1a-Flex-eGFP (serotype DJ8), a plasmid subcloned from Addgene #28304 and packaged by the Neuroconnectivity Core at the Jan and Dan Duncan Neurological Research Institute. For electrophysiology experiments, AAV-Ef1a-Flex-eGFP-p2a-mNaChBac-WPRE-hGHpA (serotype DJ8), AAV-Ef1a-Flex-eGFP (control virus) or AAV-Ef1a-Flex-mRuby (control virus) were injected unilaterally or bilaterally into the LHb at the same coordinates as described below.

Stereotaxic coordinates for lateral habenula are AP-1.3 mm, ML ±0.4 mm, DV -2.85 mm relative to Bregma. The coordinates were determined empirically and verified with dil injections prior stereotaxic surgery. Bilateral injections into the lateral habenula were performed, using pulled glass pipettes to introduce 41.4 nl of the viral construct at a speed of 46 nl/s. The injection pipette was retracted 0.03 mm above the injection site following the injection and kept stationary for three minutes prior to being fully withdrawn. The incision was closed using 6-0 suture and the animals were post-operatively monitored for 3 days with daily Meloxicam (5mg/kg) injections.

Behavior assays

All behavior experiments were performed on mice aged 12-25 weeks. Prior to the start of behavior assays, mice were handled for 5 minutes per day for 3 days leading up to the time of experiment. Experimenters were blind to the experimental group during behavior tests and were unblinded after data analysis. All experiments were performed during the light cycle.

Open field

The open field test for locomotor behaviors was performed as previously described.18 Briefly, mice were placed in an open white arena (40 x 40 x 20 cm) and allowed to freely explore for 10 minutes, and their position was continually monitored using the tracking software ANY-Maze. Total distance traveled was recorded and analyzed using the automated behavior software, ANY-Maze (Stoelting).

Elevated plus maze

The elevated plus maze for anxiety-related behavior was performed as previously described.60,102 Briefly, mice were placed at the intersection of open and closed arm and allowed to freely explore the apparatus, which is in the shape of a plus sign with two open arms 35 cm-long, 5 cm-wide and two closed arms of identical dimensions with 16cm-high walls, raised 50cm from the floor. Data were acquired using a high-fps camera suspended above the apparatus and time spent in open arms (time open arm) was analyzed using the automated behavior software, ANY-Maze.

Forced swim test

The forced swim test for depression-related behavior was performed as previously described.46 Briefly, mice were allowed to swim in a transparent cylinder 20 cm diameter x 47 cm height for 6 minutes, with the first two minutes considered the habituation phase and the final four minutes considered the test phase. The cylinder was filled with tap water to 20 cm in depth and the water temperature was adjusted to 23.5-24°C. A high-fps camera was used to acquire video from the side. Behavior was recorded using ANY-Maze software. Immobile time (i.e., when the animal remained motionless with only minimal movements for balance) was scored manually by an experimenter blind to the treatment group. Animals were gently dried and transferred to a clean cage for recovery following the test.

Tail suspension test

The tail suspension test was performed as previously described.103 Briefly, mice were suspended from their tails, with tape applied 1 cm from tip, 30 cm above the bottom of the device for 6 minutes. Video was captured from the side and behavior was analyzed using ANY-Maze software. Immobile time (i.e., when the subject mouse was not moving) was manually recorded throughout the full 6-minute test by an experimenter blind to the treatment group.

Fecal sample collection and sequencing analysis

Mice were placed in an autoclaved box for fecal microbiota collection. Once at least two to three pellets of fecal pellets were collected, they were snap-frozen on dry ice and stored at –80°C until prepared for further processing.

16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene amplicon sequencing

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing was performed as previously described.16 Methods were adapted from protocols provided for the NIH-Human Microbiome Project104,105 and Earth Microbiome Project.106 Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted using MO BIO PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (MO BIO Laboratories). The 16S rRNA V4-V5 region was amplified by PCR using the original Caporaso et al. primers92 (515F: GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA; 806R: GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT) and sequenced on the MiSeq platform (Illumina) using the 2 x 250 bp paired-end protocol.

The 16S rRNA amplicon read pairs were demultiplexed and reads were merged using USEARCH v7.0.1090,107 allowing zero mismatches and a minimum overlap of 50 bases. Merged reads were trimmed at first base with Q5. A quality filter was applied to the resulting merged reads. Reads containing above 0.05 expected errors were discarded. 16S rRNA gene sequences were clustered into Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) at a similarity cutoff value of 97% using the UPARSE algorithm.108 OTUs were mapped to an optimized version of the SILVA Database,109 containing only the 16S V4 region to determine taxonomies. Abundances were recovered by mapping the demultiplexed reads to the UPARSE OTUs. A custom script constructed a rarefied OTU table from the output files generated in the previous two steps for downstream analyses of alpha-diversity, beta-diversity, and unrarefied data were used for phylogenetic trends as described in the next section.

Whole genome shotgun sequencing

Individual libraries constructed from each sample were loaded onto the HiSeq platform (Illumina) and sequenced using the 2 x 100 base pair (bp) pair-end read protocol. Illumina paired-end libraries were constructed from total genomic DNA isolated from each sample. The DNA was sheared into approximately 400–600 bp fragments followed by ligation of Illumina adaptors containing molecular barcodes for downstream de-multiplexing. These products were then amplified through ligation-mediated PCR (LM-PCR) using KAPA HiFi DNA Polymerase (Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA). Following bead purification with Agencourt AMPure XP (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), quantification and size distribution of the LM-PCR product was determined using the LabChip GX electrophoresis system (PerkinElmer, Akron, OH, USA). Libraries were pooled in equimolar amounts at six samples per pool and prepared for sequencing with TruSeq PE Cluster Generation Kit (Illumina). Each library pool was loaded onto one lane of a HiSeq 2000 flow cell spiked with 1% PhiX control library. Sequencing files were de-multiplexed with CASAVA version 1.8.3 (Illumina).

For whole genome shotgun (WGS) sequencing analysis, quality filtering, trimming and de-multiplexing was carried out by a custom pipeline containing trimmomatic94 v0.39 and mouse reads were removed in silico after mapping to the GRCm39 reference genome using Bowtie295 v2.5.0. Filtered reads were then taxonomically profiled using Kraken296 v2.1.2 (with confidence parameter set to 0.5) and Bracken97 v2.8 using the Struo293 generated GTDB-r207 Kraken2 and Bracken databases.

Results were imported into R v4.2.2 and pre-processing was performed using Phyloseq98 v1.42.0. The final table was filtered to include taxa that were present in > 20% of samples. For alpha-diversity analyses, a rarefaction depth of 6206725 and 7877 was selected for the WGS and 16S datasets, respectively. For beta-diversity analyses, a two-way PERMANOVA was performed using vegan v2.6-4. Beta-diversity plots were generated using the ATIMA website from Baylor College of Medicine (https://atima.research.bcm.edu/). Differential abundance analyses were performed using MaAsLin299 v1.12.0 with the default parameters. An adjusted P-value (Padj) < 0.05 was considered significant for all analyses and represents the time∗treatment interaction term unless otherwise noted. ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001, n.s. = not significant.

Electrophysiology

Electrophysiology experiments were performed as previously described.100 Briefly, for acute brain slice preparation, animals were anesthetized with isoflurane and perfused with cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) solution, pH 7.35, 310 mOsm/l, containing (in mM): 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 2 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 10 Glucose, 25 NaHCO3. Brains were rapidly removed and transferred into sucrose-based cutting solution pH 7.35 containing (in mM): 87 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 0.5 CaCl2, 7 MgCl2·6H2O, 13 Ascorbic Acid, 75 Sucrose, 10 Glucose, 25 NaHCO3 equilibrated with 5% CO2–95% O2 gas mixture. 300μm-thick coronal brain slices were prepared using a Leica VT1200 vibratome and incubated for at least 15 min at 37°C in 5% CO2–95% O2 bubbled ACSF solution. They were then gradually lowered to room temperature (25°C) and allowed to acclimate for at least 15 min before recording. For recording, slices were transferred into a recording chamber continuously perfused with ACSF at 1-2 ml/min at 25°C. Neurons were identified by transmitted light DIC (BX50WI, Olympus) and EGFP fluorescence using Thorlabs DC4100 470 nm or 565-nm LED illumination with 49002-ET-EGFP (FITC/Cy2) or HcRed1 emission filters (Chroma Technology) respectively, and an optiMOS camera (QImaging) controlled by Micro-Manager version 1.4.22 software (University of California–San Francisco). Recordings were obtained using an Axon MultiClamp 700B amplifier digitized at 10 kHz (Axon Digidata 1440A). Recording electrodes (3-5 MOhm) were fabricated from borosilicate glass microcapillaries (outer diameter: 1.5 mm) with a micropipette puller (Sutter Instruments). The internal solution contained (in mM) 110 K-gluconate, 10 KCl, 4 ATP-Mg, 0.5 GTP-Na, 10 Phosphocreatine-di-Na, 1 EGTA, 10 HEPES, pH was adjusted to 7.3 with KOH, the osmolarity was adjusted to 305 mOsm/l with K-gluconate. After obtaining a GOhm seal, the baseline firing activity was first monitored in the cell-attached mode for 10 minutes and then for another 10 minutes in the whole-cell current clamp configuration without current injection. After assessing the baseline activity, a series of hyperpolarizing and depolarizing current injection steps/ramps were applied to analyze the firing mode and threshold. The access resistance was monitored throughout the experiment and was typically in a range of 10-20 MOhm, considered to be acceptable up to 30 MOhm.

Fecal microbiota transplant (FMT)

FMT was performed as previously described.16 Briefly, 4-week-old C57BL/6NTac germ-free recipient mice were removed from the gnotobiotic isolator and immediately gavaged with donor fecal microbiota. Depression-like behaviors (FST and TST) were assessed at 4 weeks post-colonization. For preparation of the transplant material, previously collected fecal samples from animals with 13 weeks chronic LHb stimulation with mNaChBac or control GFP were thawed from –80°C. Samples were pooled from the same treatment groups (about 4-5 pellets) homogenized with electronic homogenizer on ice in 1ml sterile PBS under sterile conditions. The slurry was centrifuged at 1000g for 3 min at 4oC and the supernatant was retained. The supernatant was then diluted to 5x109 CFU/ml with sterile PBS and 0.2ml of the resulting transplant material was gavaged into recipient mice within 15 minutes of preparation using 24-gauge curved ball tip needle. FMT recipients were housed according to donor group.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SEM with individual data points also shown. GraphPad’s Prism 10.2.0 (La Jolla, CA) software was used for statistical analyses and graphical data generation of behavioral, food consumption, body weight, and electrophysiological experiments. Behavioral (i.e., open field test, elevated plus maze, forced swim test, tail suspension test, and food consumption data) and electrophysiological (i.e., membrane potential, action potential threshold, capacitance, membrane resistance, and time constant) data were first checked for normal distribution of the data using Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Significance (P-values) between the treatment groups was calculated by unpaired Student’s t-test with or without Welch’s correction or Mann-Whitney U-test. All tests were performed with statistical significance considered at P < 0.05. LHb neuron firing pattern percentage comparisons were analyzed using the Chi-Squared test with alpha = 0.05. Body weight data was analyzed by two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc analysis to correct for multiple comparisons between treatment groups at each time point, and statistical significance was considered when P < 0.05. U, F, P values and sample size (n) number were indicated in the figure legends.
