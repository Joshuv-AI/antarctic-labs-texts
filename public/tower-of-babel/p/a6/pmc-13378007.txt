Concurrent Burkholderia cepacia detected by plasma mcfDNA sequencing in a child with MRSA liver abscess and chronic granulomatous disease: a case report
Authors: Maha Mohammed, Laura Tarantino Lang, Sara Robertson, Chinky Patel, Amanda M Green
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic granulomatous disease (CGD) is a primary immunodeficiency marked by defective phagocyte function, predisposing patients to recurrent infections, granuloma formation, and hyperinflammation. Initial diagnosis can be delayed due to nonspecific clinical features, incomplete pathogen detection, and common outpatient antimicrobial use. Plasma microbial cell-free DNA next-generation sequencing (mcfDNA-Seq) is an emerging non-invasive diagnostic tool, but its sensitivity and specificity vary by infection type and host. We report a case of CGD first presenting with concurrent Staphylococcus aureus liver abscess and disseminated Burkholderia cepacia infection, where mcfDNA-Seq contributed to diagnosis and management.Case presentationAn 11-month-old male presented with 17 days of fever, irritability, and bulky cervical lymphadenopathy unresponsive to outpatient antibiotics. Physical exam demonstrated extremity rash, oral ulcers, and bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy. Initial infectious workup was negative including blood and urine cultures, chest radiograph, CMV and EBV serology. Abdominal imaging demonstrated a hepatic abscess, and multiple splenic and hepatic lesions interpreted as granulomas. Culture of liver abscess drainage grew methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA). Fevers and lymphadenopathy continued despite vancomycin, with no evidence of spread or recurrence of MRSA. Rapid resolution of symptoms was achieved after initiating intravenous trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and plasma mcfDNA-Seq confirmed presence of Burkholderia cepacia. Subsequent genetic and dihydrorhodamine flow cytometric testing confirmed the suspected diagnosis of CGD.ConclusionsThis case highlights the diagnostic challenges associated with CGD and the potential utility of plasma mcfDNA-Seq in this immunocompromised population. It may serve as a less invasive alternative to tissue specimens, particularly when conventional diagnostics are unrevealing. Introduction

Chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) is a primary immunodeficiency characterized by impaired phagocyte function, leaving patients susceptible to invasive infections, granulomas, and hyperinflammatory syndromes [1]. Around 70% of CGD cases are due to mutations in the CYBB gene on the X-chromosome [2], and X-linked CGD is typically more severe and diagnosed earlier in childhood compared to other forms [1]. Antimicrobial prophylaxis is the mainstay of management, with interferon-gamma, corticosteroids, and immunomodulators used in select situations. While gene therapy is on the horizon, bone marrow transplant remains the only curative option [1].

Patients with CGD are particularly susceptible to infections by catalase-producing organisms [3]. In North America, patients with CGD most commonly experience invasive infections with Aspergillus spp., followed by Staphylococcus aureus, Burkholderia, Serratia, and Nocardia species [3]. Reported cases of Burkholderia cepacia in CGD usually describe pulmonary involvement, severe and recurrent infections, and tended to cause hyperinflammation and chronic granulomas [3, 4]. They often require invasive specimens such as lung or lymph node biopsy for diagnosis; bacteremia rarely occurs [3].

Prompt diagnosis and targeted treatment of invasive infections is critical to the care of patients with CGD. Plasma microbial cell-free DNA next generation sequencing (mcfDNA-Seq) is an emerging diagnostic test which amplifies extracellular genetic material in an unbiased manner to detect the presence of microbes. Optimal patient populations and infection sites for mcfDNA-Seq are still being investigated. mcfDNA-Seq has been shown to be only moderately sensitive and specific in evaluation of fever of unknown origin and non-blood stream infections (including lymphadenopathy) [5], but has been suggested to have good diagnostic value for culture-negative endocarditis, difficult to culture pathogens, and immunocompromised adults with pneumonia [6–8]. In patients with CGD, next generation sequencing (NGS) has been useful in identifying pathogens from lymph node tissue and bronchoalveolar lavage specimens [9, 10]. However, to our knowledge, plasma mcfDNA-Seq has not been specifically examined in CGD, or in immunocompromised patients without bacteremia or pneumonia. We report the case of an infant diagnosed with X-linked CGD following the identification of simultaneous invasive infections, underscoring the value of broad diagnostics, including advanced molecular techniques in this immunocompromised population.

Case presentation

An 11-month-old fully immunized male presented to the emergency department (ED) with 17 days of fever, malaise, and tender bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy. His past medical history was significant for unilateral S. aureus cervical lymphadenitis at four months of age, which resolved after surgical drainage and oral antibiotics. On day 2 of illness he was prescribed amoxicillin-clavulanate by his primary care provider for fever with unclear source. At 10 days of persistent fever and irritability he presented to the ED with multiple bilateral, non-erythematous, enlarged cervical lymph nodes. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated leukocytosis and an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) of 158.9 mg/dL. Neck computed tomography (CT) showed lymphadenopathy without abscess or associated pathology. He was otherwise well-appearing, and he was discharged home to complete the 10 days of amoxicillin-clavulanate, with the addition of 5 days of azithromycin. Blood and urine cultures were negative.

He returned to the ED on day 17 of illness with persistent fever, worsening bulky lymphadenopathy, a fine erythematous papular extremity rash, and several oral mucosal ulcers. On exam he had mild hepatomegaly without abdominal tenderness, was irritable and tachycardic when febrile, but was otherwise well appearing with normal blood pressures, no respiratory distress, supplemental oxygen requirement or abnormalities on lung auscultation. He had no abdominal pain, gastrointestinal or upper or lower respiratory symptoms. History was significant for exposure to an adult cat, with no gardening or travel history. He was admitted to hospital for evaluation of fever of unknown origin. Pertinent laboratory findings on admission included leukocytosis (15.5 thousand/mcL (15.5 × 10⁹/L)), anemia (hemoglobin 8.5 g/dL), elevated CRP (213.8 mg/dL) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (39 mm/hr) (Table 1). He had normal hepatic and renal function. Ultrasound of the neck again showed bulky bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy without abscess (Fig. 1).

At this time concern was high for S. aureus or other pyogenic bacteria causing his liver abscess, but the bulky lymphadenopathy and disseminated lesions on diagnostic imaging without matching clinical severity raised concerns for an alternative or concurrent disseminated granulomatous infection (most commonly caused in this region by Bartonella henselae, Histoplasma capsulatum, and nontuberculous mycobacteria). Underlying immunodeficiency was suspected. Given negative blood cultures, a 3 mL blood specimen was collected in a plasma preparation tube (PPT), separated from cells in < 6 h, and sent fresh (unfrozen) for plasma mcfDNA-Seq (Karius Laboratory, Redwood City, CA, USA) on day 19 of illness. The patient was initiated on empiric ciprofloxacin (15 mg/kg orally (PO) twice daily) and doxycycline (2 mg/kg PO twice daily) to provide coverage for common Gram negative organisms, S. aureus (based on prior episode susceptibilities), and bartonellosis progressing despite azithromycin therapy (Fig. 2). Percutaneous needle drainage of the liver abscess was obtained on day 20. Gram stain showed many Gram positive cocci; doxycycline was discontinued and intravenous (IV) vancomycin (20 mg/kg loading dose, and 15 mg/kg every 6 h thereafter) was started. Culture of the liver abscess yielded methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), susceptible to ciprofloxacin, clindamycin, tetracyclines, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) and vancomycin). Despite 3 days of doxycycline or vancomycin with therapeutic levels he continued to have high fever more than 24 h after abscess drainage, cervical lymphadenopathy became larger and more erythematous, CRP remained unchanged (Fig. 2), and he developed worsening anemia (Hgb 6.0 g/dL), with high ferritin (554 ng/mL) and continued mild thrombocytopenia (140 K/mcL). Repeat abdominal ultrasound, blood cultures, echocardiogram, and whole body Technetium-99 m (99mTc) nuclear medicine bone scan showed no evidence of re-accumulation of liver abscess or spread of the MRSA infection. With disseminated imaging findings and a second confirmed invasive S. aureus infection, underlying CGD was suspected. Due to worsening clinical status, intravenous TMP-SMX (6 mg/kg trimethoprim IV twice daily) was added empirically out of concern for B. cepacia or other atypical bacterial infection. The patient showed quick clinical improvement, including resolution of bulky lymphadenopathy and fever. On day 23, plasma mcfDNA-Seq identified a single organism, B. cepacia at 288 molecules/microliter (threshold < 10 molecules/microliter, Supplementary Materials). He was discharged to home on day 26 (hospital day 10) on oral clindamycin (10 mg/kg PO thrice daily), TMP-SMX (6 mg/kg PO twice daily), and itraconazole prophylaxis (5 mg/kg PO daily) for presumed CGD. Subsequently, dihydrorhodamine (DHR) flow cytometric testing (Mayo Clinic) showed pathologically low neutrophil oxidative burst activity. A primary immunodeficiency exome sequencing panel (Veritas) revealed a hemizygous pathogenic variant in the CYBB gene, confirming the diagnosis of X-linked CGD (Table 1, ClinVar accession number: SCV006303272).

After three weeks of treatment with clindamycin plus TMP-SMX, repeat ultrasound showed resolution of liver abscess. Spleen and liver granulomas were reduced in size, but persisted. Although the patient was otherwise asymptomatic, a course of corticosteroids was initiated at day 34 of illness (14-day tapered oral prednisolone course (with 7 days at maximum dose of 1 mg/kg/day)), and his antibiotic course was prolonged due to concern for continued heavy granuloma versus active infection burden. Clindamycin was discontinued after 4 total weeks, at completion of steroid taper and normalization of CRP. TMP-SMX was continued for 6 total weeks for presumed disseminated B. cepacia with lymphadenopathy and splenic granulomas. Repeat CT at 3 months showed resolution of liver and spleen granulomas, and no re-accumulation of liver abscess. Daily TMP-SMX and itraconazole prophylaxis were maintained after completion of treatment. The patient was started on thrice-weekly subcutaneous interferon-gamma therapy at 14-months of age, and underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation at 20-months of age due to continued frequent infections.

Discussion

In the United States, patients with X-linked CGD present with infections from a wide spectrum of pathogens [1, 2], with cases reported of multiple concomitant infections [9]. Although S. aureus is a common pathogen among immunocompetent children, recurrent or simultaneous bacterial or fungal infections should raise suspicion for CGD or other immunodeficiencies. Invasive B. cepacia infections are rare in immunocompetent children and should trigger evaluation for CGD or other underlying immunodeficiencies. Delayed diagnosis is associated with high morbidity and mortality, especially during the first episodes of infection [1–3]. Confirmation of a second large S. aureus abscess in this young child raised suspicions of underlying CGD, and the poor clinical response to vancomycin therapy coupled with atypical, disseminated granulomas and lymphadenopathy led to concern for concurrent infection. Due to the high relative risk of infection, if patients with confirmed or suspected CGD do not show expected clinical improvement despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy, concurrent infection should be considered.

The sensitivity and specificity of mcfDNA-Seq has been evaluated across many host populations and infection sites, with widely varying results. Accumulated evidence suggests that plasma mcfDNA-Seq has advantages of high sensitivity and moderate to high specificity with bloodstream infection, can identify multiple pathogens at one time, and may be especially useful for pathogens with low success rates with traditional microbiological testing methods [7, 8, 11]. General limitations are high cost, limited sensitivity from plasma testing in cases of tissue-based infections such as lymphadenitis or liver abscess, and the uncertain clinical significance of identified microbes [8, 11–13]. In a study of adults with presumed infection tested with plasma mcfDNA-Seq, only 25% were deemed clinically relevant, and a smaller proportion added benefit to microbiological culture methods [8]. Poor sensitivity and specificity were seen in fever of unknown origin evaluations, with the biggest impact on management in suspected cardiovascular infections such as endocarditis [8, 11, 12, 14]. An additional study of adults and children with heterogenous infections and immune status suggested limited clinical impact of mcfDNA-Seq, with the majority of patients (92.7%) receiving no management change related to the test [14]. However, in patients with fever undergoing treatment for hematologic malignancy or after bone marrow transplant, plasma mcfDNA-Seq demonstrated high sensitivity compared to conventional methods, with potential for prediction of bacteremia [7, 12]. Prior case reports involving NGS in CGD populations have been successful in identifying pathogens, but limited to invasive specimen types such as lymph node biopsy or bronchoalveolar lavage. One case reported NGS of lymph node aspirate and BAL fluid to diagnose concurrent Nocardia farcinica Aspergillus fumigatus infections in a young child [10]. While our patient did not have the attributes most highly associated with successful pathogen detection previously reported (sepsis, pneumonia, endocarditis), his delayed improvement, multi-focal granulomas not entirely consistent with S. aureus infection alone, and suspicion of underlying CGD prompted expansion of the diagnostic investigation to include plasma mcfDNA-Seq to evaluate for concurrent infection. The disseminated nature of his lymphadenopathy and granulomas suggest hematogenous spread, and possible sub-acute or chronic infection, which are possible explanations for the success of mcfDNA-Seq in this case.

Detection of B. cepacia by plasma mcfDNA-Seq can be difficult to interpret. It is usually considered a commensal (non-pathogenic) organism when isolated from immunocompetent individuals, and is a common laboratory contaminant [3, 8, 15]. However, Burkholderia spp. are common pathogens in CGD, and can cause invasive infections in a wide range of immunocompromising conditions, including iatrogenic infections. For example, B. cepacia culture-negative endocarditis was identified by plasma mcfDNA-Seq culture-negative endocarditis in a patient with risk factors of prolonged steroid use, prosthetic heart valve, and ICU admission for viral pneumonia [16]. Our patient’s plasma mcfDNA-Seq results alone do not definitively confirm the diagnosis, but the rapid improvement after initiation of appropriate therapy, the presence of pulmonary and other granulomas, and the fact that B. cepacia is one of the most common pathogens in CGD was consistent with the presence of a disseminated B. cepacia infection. B. cepacia is infrequently detected by plasma mcfDNA-Seq; it is outside the top 50 most identified taxa by this method [17]. While its role remains uncertain in fever of unknown origin or lymphadenitis in immunocompetent patients, mcfDNA-Seq provided timely confirmation of a suspected diagnosis of a likely simultaneous, symptomatic B. cepacia infection, and helped avoid the need for an invasive lymph node biopsy procedure. This case supports the growing role of mcfDNA-Seq as a second-line diagnostic test, for use in conjunction with concordant imaging, microbiological and biomarker data in immunocompromised patients with complex infections.

A lengthy antibiotic course and a short course of corticosteroids were utilized to achieve clinical and radiological resolution of the abscess and granulomas. While the exact mechanism of chronic inflammation in CGD remains unclear, it is thought to be associated with intracellular microbial persistence and immune dysregulation, which can limit the effectiveness of antimicrobials alone [1]. Over time, chronic granuloma formation can lead to cumulative, non-infectious obstructive complications, even after adequate source control and prolonged antibiotic therapy [18]. Corticosteroids reduce immune cell activation, proliferation, and differentiation, and may play a role in modulating inflammation in CGD-related granulomas. Retrospective data and case series suggest a potential benefit of adjunctive corticosteroids compared to surgical and antibiotic interventions alone in reducing time to resolution and likelihood of recurrence of CGD-associated hepatic abscess, at doses similar to those used in this case (1 mg/kg prednisolone) [19–21].

The propensity for prolonged infection eradication with CGD is compounded here by the pathogen B. cepacia, which has high rates of intrinsic and acquired antimicrobial resistance. Combination therapy with multiple agents is often utilized for severe and recurrent infections, classically with pneumonia in patients with cystic fibrosis (CF). An important limitation to the mcfDNA-Seq results in this case was the lack of antibiotic susceptibility information. Current data from B. cepacia complex isolates in the United States showed 96% of all CF and non-CF isolates combined were susceptible (minimum inhibitory concentration of ≤ 2/38 µg/mL) to TMP-SMX [15]. Combined with our patient’s rapid improvement and relatively mild severity, the decision was made to continue with monotherapy with close follow-up.

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of early recognition of CGD in infants, and the associated risk for recurrent, persistent, and multiple concomitant infections. Plasma mcfDNA-Seq non-invasively identified a likely disseminated Burkholderia cepacia infection in this patient with concurrent MRSA infection, leading to improved medical management. Further research is needed to determine the optimal use of plasma mcfDNA-Seq in the evaluation of complex infections in patients with known or suspected CGD.
