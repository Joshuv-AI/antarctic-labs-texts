Mapping malignancy: Multicenter study addressing topographic challenges in biliary stricture artificial intelligence analysis

ABSTRACT
Background and study aimsCholangiocarcinoma (CCa) is a complex malignancy of the biliary tract, classified as intrahepatic, perihilar, or distal. Digital single-operator cholangioscopy (D-SOC) enhances evaluation of biliary strictures, although it remains limited by suboptimal biopsy yield and technical constraints. Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has emerged as a promising adjunct. However, performance across anatomical subtypes is not well defined. This study evaluated diagnostic performance of an AI-based model in detecting CCa lesions by location.Patients and methodsA YOLOv8-based CNN was trained and validated using 315,993 D-SOC images from 183 patients across six international high-volume centers. Images were labeled as benign or malignant based on expert consensus. Frame-based analysis assessed diagnostic performance using macro-average F1-score, precision, and recall. Subgroup analysis explored anatomical site-specific performance.ResultsAmong the included patients (mean age 66.1±12.1 years; 64.5% male), 43.2% had perihilar, 37.2% intrahepatic, and 19.7% distal biliary strictures. The model demonstrated high overall performance: F1 score 95.3%, precision 95.5%, and recall 95.1%. Site-specific analysis revealed an F1 score of 89.6% for distal strictures and 91.1% for perihilar strictures. Receiver operating characteristic curves showed areas under the curve of 0.980 for intrahepatic strictures and 0.990 for perihilar and distal strictures.ConclusionsThis is the first study to demonstrate AI-based diagnostic performance across CCa topography using a large, multicenter D-SOC dataset. Although anatomical complexity affects detection, the model’s high precision and generalizability suggest potential for clinical utility. These results support application of an AI-enhanced algorithm decision algorithm for cholangioscopy.

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma is a rare but highly aggressive malignancy arising from the bile duct epithelium, representing approximately 3% of all gastrointestinal cancers 1 . The disease typically presents at advanced stages due to its insidious onset and nonspecific symptoms, contributing to its notoriously poor prognosis 2 . Anatomically, these tumors are classified into three main subtypes: intrahepatic (iCCa), originating within the liver parenchyma; perihilar (pCCa), occurring at the hepatic duct bifurcation, and distal (dCCA), located in the common bile duct 3 .

Management depends critically on disease stage and anatomical location, with complete surgical resection offering the only potential for cure in localized cases. However, most patients present with unresectable disease at diagnosis and require palliative therapies.

Cholangiocarcinoma is often diagnosed during workup of indeterminate biliary strictures. Most of these lesions are malignant and prognosis is intricately dependent on timely diagnosis. However, diagnosis in pre-symptomatic stages is often difficult. Indeed, imaging techniques are non-specific and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) and its traditional ancillary techniques (e.g. brush cytology) show limited diagnostic sensitivity and accuracy 4 . Digital single-operator cholangioscopy (D-SOC) has significantly improved diagnostic evaluation of indeterminate biliary strictures. In fact, D-SOC enables direct visual characterization of biliary strictures and performance of visually guided biopsies, which significantly improve diagnostic accuracy 4 . Nevertheless, the diagnostic yield of D-SOC-guided tissue sampling remains suboptimal, with false-negative results further complicating diagnosis and delaying treatment initiation 4 5 .

Artificial intelligence (AI) has become a transformative tool in modern endoscopy and radiology, enhancing diagnostic accuracy and workflow efficiency. Previous studies have assessed the potential impact of AI on diagnostic performance of D-SOC for the evaluation of biliary strictures with promising results 6 . In a recent D-SOC study analyzing over 4,000 images, AI demonstrated sensitivity and specificity exceeding 99% for detecting tumor vessels, optimizing diagnostic workup of patients with indeterminate biliary strictures 7 . Despite these advances in diagnostic performance, we are still far from clinical application and there is still a lack of comparative analyses assessing the impact of stenosis anatomical locations on diagnostic performance 8 . This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of a CNN model in classifying cholangiocarcinoma subtypes using real-world, multicenter imaging data, addressing potential variability in model performance across anatomical sites.

Patients and methods

Study design

This was a retrospective, multicenter study involving six tertiary referral hospitals across three continents: Centro Hospitalar Universitário de São João (CHUSJ, Porto, Portugal), Hospital Universitário Puerta de Hierro Majadahonda (HUPHM, Madrid, Spain), New York University Langone Hospital - Long Island (NYULH-LI, New York, New York, United States), Hospital das Clínicas da Faculdade de Medicina da Universidade de São Paulo (HCFMUSP, São Paulo, Brazil), University of Chicago Medical Center (UChicago, Chicago, Illinois, United States), and Hospital Universitário de Marqués de Valdecilla (HUMV, Santander, Spain). All participating institutions had extensive experience in advanced biliary endoscopy and routinely performed D-SOC as part of diagnostic workup for indeterminate biliary strictures, and procedures were performed by endoscopists with expertise in ERCP and D-SOC (having performed over 1,000 ERCP and 150 D-SOC exams).

This study was approved by the ethics committees of all participating centers: Centro Hospitalar Universitário de São João (Ethics approval: CE41/2021), Hospital das Clínicas da Faculdade de Medicina de São Paulo (Ethics approval: 6.965.460), Hospital Marqués de Valdecilla (Ethics approval: 2024.101), New York University Langone Hospital (Ethics Approval: 03845978/2023), Hospital Universitário Puerta de Hierro (Ethics approval: PI153/22), and University of Chicago Medicine (Internal Review Board approval: DUAO000643).

Data sources and patient selection

Patients who underwent D-SOC between August 2017 and April 2025 were eligible for inclusion. Only those with confirmed malignant or benign strictures, based on histological results were considered. For benign strictures, in addition to no evidence of malignancy on histopathological analysis, a follow-up period of at least 6 months compatible with a benign etiology was required. Exclusion criteria included inadequate image quality, incomplete clinical metadata, and cases in which final diagnosis could not be confidently established

Image acquisition and annotation

D-SOC procedures were performed using three different devices: SpyGlass DS II (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Massachusetts, United States), EyeMax (Micro-Tech, Nanjing, China), and Briview (Seegen, Shanghai, China). Video procedures were reviewed independently by two experienced endoscopists at each center, who extracted high-quality frames and labeled them according to stricture status (benign or malignant). Discrepancies were resolved through consensus. To minimize label noise, only images with confirmed diagnosis and full agreement between reviewers were retained. Image labelling was performed on the web platform Encord (Cord Technologies, London, United Kingdom).

Each stricture was further categorized by anatomical location of the stricture into one of three subtypes ( Fig. 1 ). Malignant lesions were characterized as iCCa, pCCa, or dCCa. Intrahepatic lesions were defined as those occurring within the bile ducts inside the liver parenchyma. Perihilar strictures involved the hepatic duct bifurcation and adjacent segments. Distal strictures referred to strictures located in the common bile duct, distal to insertion of the cystic duct. This stratification enabled location-specific evaluation of the AI model diagnostic performance.

Topography of biliary strictures. For malignant cases, the terminology iCCa, pCCa and dCCa for intrahepatic, perihilar, and distal cholangiocarcinoma, respectively, was applied.

Image preprocessing

All images were standardized to a uniform resolution and underwent color normalization to mitigate device-related variability. Data augmentation techniques—including horizontal flipping, brightness adjustment, and Gaussian noise—were selectively applied to the training set to improve generalizability. The dataset was divided on a per-patient basis into training (70%), validation (20%), and test sets (10%), ensuring no image leakage between groups.

AI model development

A mainly CNN based on a YOLOv8 backbone was employed to classify the images. The model was implemented using Ultralytics deep learning framework. Training was conducted with a batch size of 32, a learning rate of 0.01, and early stopping criteria of 20 epochs to prevent overfitting. A composite loss function for detection tasks was used to fit this model and its three components are the distribution focal loss, varifocal loss, and complete intersection over union. Model optimization was performed using the Adam Optimizer, on NVIDIA Quadro RTX 4000 Graphic Processing Units.

Performance evaluation and statistical analysis

Model performance was assessed on the independent test set using metrics including sensitivity, precision, F1 score, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), and mean average precision at intersection over union (IoU) of 0.5. These classification metrics are reported only for frames for which IoU ≥ 0.5. A higher probability correlated with a greater confidence in CNN prediction. The category with the highest probability score was outputted as the CNN predicted classification. In addition, subgroup post-hoc analyses were performed to evaluate diagnostic performance by anatomical location of the stricture: iCCa, pCCa, or dCCa. Statistical analysis was performed using Sci-Kit learn v0.22.216 9

Results

Patient and image dataset

A total of 196 patients were considered for inclusion in this study. Thirteen patients were excluded due to missed information on stricture topography and 183 patients were ultimately included in the final dataset. A total of 315,993 frames were collected and labelled.

Data acquisition was conducted across six academic medical centers spanning three continents: CHUSJ (Porto Portugal, n = 128), NYULH-LI (New York, New York, United States, n = 28), HCFMUSP (São Paulo, Brazil, n = 12), HUPHM (Madrid, Spain, n = 7), HUMV (Santander, Spain, n = 6), University of Chicago (Chicago, Illinois, United States, n = 2). Three D-SOC devices were employed: SpyGlass DS II (n = 175), EyeMax (n = 7), and SeeGen (n = 1). Among these patients, 118 (64.5%) were male, and had a mean age of 66.1 ± 12.1 years. Model performance and subtype-based performance comparison

Model performance was assessed on a frame-level basis. The model achieved a mAP50 value of 0.980. Fig. 2 shows examples of correct predictions by the model. The algorithm achieved a macro-average F1 score of 95.3% (95% confidence interval [CI] 95.2%–95.3%), with a precision of 95.5% (95% CI 95.4%–95.5%) and a sensitivity of 95.1% (95% CI 95.0%-95.3%). For perihilar strictures, the model identified pCCa with a mean sensitivity of 86.7% (95% CI 85.8%–87.6%), a mean precision of 97.3% (95% CI 97.2%–97.4%), and an F1 score of 91.1% (95% CI 90.5%–91.6%). For intrahepatic strictures, the model differentiated iCCa from benign stricture with a sensitivity of 76.0% (95% CI 74.7%–77.4%). For distal lesions, these values were 84.8% (95%CI 83.5%–86.0%) and 82.9% (95% CI 81.9%–83.9%), respectively. Performance metrics are summarized in Table 2 .

Error analysis and case illustrations

Misclassified cases were retrospectively reviewed. Representative false-positive and false-negative examples are shown in Fig. 4 . Contributing factors included poor image resolution, inflammation-induced distortion, and atypical lesion morphology. For example, images depicting a more evident vascular pattern, blood contamination, or a villous pattern could have been erroneously identified as malignant, versus images with increased blurriness, whitish biliary mucosa or lighting artifacts. These cases provide critical insights for future algorithm optimization and highlight limitations under real-world clinical conditions.

Discussion

This multicenter study represents the first investigation to evaluate performance of a CNN for diagnosing cholangiocarcinoma across distinct anatomical locations of biliary strictures. In recent years, D-SOC has gained relevance in assessment of indeterminate biliary strictures and this technology has been suggested for the initial approach to these patients, particularly in the case of primary sclerosing cholangitis 10 . However, the diagnostic yield of D-SOC-guided tissue sampling remains suboptimal, which can result in delayed diagnosis 11 12 . Therefore, recent recommendations have suggested use of AI to optimize D-SOC diagnostic capacity 10 13 . Nevertheless, data remain scarce and of low quality, and the impact of technical variables such as stricture topography has not yet been systematically explored. Although D-SOC is a valuable diagnostic tool, the influence of lesion topography on its diagnostic yield remains incompletely understood. A recent study by Njei and colleagues suggested that application of deep learning algorithms across biliary strictures from different topographies may produce distinct levels of performance, which suggests that anatomical complexity and limited endoscopic maneuverability may compromise visualization and interpretation 14 .

Against this backdrop, our CNN model demonstrated strong and consistent diagnostic performance across all anatomical subtypes of cholangiocarcinoma. On a frame-based analysis, macro-average F1 scores remained high—0.896 for distal, 0.911 for perihilar, and 0.829 for intrahepatic lesions—with precision exceeding 0.96 across all regions. These results highlight the model’s reliable discrimination between benign and malignant frames, with notably low false-positive rates. However, the F1 score was lower in the case of iCCa and dCCa, compared with cases of pCCa. These results may reflect inherent challenges related to the D-SOC technique in these locations, namely limited visual access and ductal complexity for iCCa and D-SOC scope instability typical when assessing distal strictures. Although differences reached statistical significance, the high performance metrics and relatively narrow variation across locations support the potential of the model to provide accurate predictions despite anatomical variability. However small, the observed differences may raise questions about whether AI can ever be fully immune to topography-related diagnostic limitations, warranting further study.

Beyond anatomical considerations, other factors may contribute to heterogeneity in diagnostic yield of cholangioscopy. Conditions such as primary sclerosing cholangitis are known to produce inflammatory changes and multifocal strictures that complicate visual interpretation and biopsy targeting 15 . Similarly, prior biliary stent placement can introduce mucosal changes or artifacts that obscure malignant features 16 . Diagnostic variability may also stem from the intrinsic growth pattern of cholangiocarcinoma—whether infiltrative, mass-forming, or periductal—each posing unique visualization challenges. In this context, the stable performance of our CNN across stricture locations suggests potential for broader applicability: With appropriate training data, such models could help normalize interpretation across diverse clinical scenarios by recognizing morphological features regardless of underlying distortion, inflammation, or access limitations.

Across the full dataset, the algorithm delivered a macro-average F1 score of 0.953 and mAP50 value of 0.980, reflecting its strong ability to detect and classify subtle morphological patterns. Even in more challenging areas (such as the hepatic hilum and intrahepatic ducts) the model maintained high precision and consistent performance. These results suggest a potential role for the algorithm as a reliable diagnostic support tool, particularly valuable in the context of D-SOC, where interpretation is often subjective and can vary between operators.

Clinical implications are substantial. Integrating computational tools like this CNN could help reduce diagnostic subjectivity, enabling more confident and consistent clinical decisions. This approach aligns with emerging frameworks for trustworthy and ethically grounded AI in medical imaging, which emphasize transparency, clinician oversight, and robust data infrastructures to support real-world applicability. In this context, our model contributes to a paradigm in which AI augments (rather than replaces) expert reasoning. Maintaining the clinician as an active decision-maker—the so-called human in the loop—ensures that AI acts as a support system integrated within medical judgment, not detached from it 17 18 .

Several features of this study enhance external validity. The model was trained and tested on a large dataset sourced from six high-volume centers across Europe, South America, and North America, using three different devices (SpyGlass DS II, EyeMax and Briview). These institutions manage a substantial number of patients with biliary strictures and maintain a consistent focus on advanced biliopancreatic endoscopy, contributing to a dataset that is both clinically relevant and reflective of routine practice in specialized settings. This diversity improves model generalizability and supports adherence to FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable) principles for AI development 19 . Moreover, the model’s inclusion of explainability tools—specifically bounding boxes over detected lesions—marks an important step toward clinical integration, facilitating transparency and user trust. However, these findings require ultimate validation in subsequent prospective clinical studies with evaluation of performance metrics on an external dataset.

A noteworthy strength of this study lies in synergistic integration of lesion detection and characterization within a single AI model. Rather than functioning as separate tasks, these capabilities complement each other—allowing the algorithm not only to classify lesions as benign or malignant, but also to spatially localize areas of concern within the visual field. This dual capacity enhances the model’s clinical relevance, potentially informing not just diagnostic decisions, but also guiding therapeutic planning. In scenarios in which surgery is feasible, such functionality may have potential to contribute to a more precise understanding of tumor extension, supporting tailored resection strategies.

Nonetheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective nature of the study may carry inherent biases. Importantly, the imbalance between different cholangioscope devices, although reflecting routine clinical practice, does not allow for a conclusive comparison of model performance across different manufacturers. In addition, the analysis was conducted on a frame-by-frame basis, reflecting the structure of the available dataset and annotation strategy. This strategy focused on high-quality consensual frames, which differs from what is found in routine clinical practice. Although this approach supports detailed evaluation of algorithm performance, future studies should aim to complement it with patient-level assessments, in a totally independent external set of patients, to broaden clinical applicability. Furthermore, although this study provides an introductory overview of the impact of important variables on the performance of AI for D-SOC, these initial analyses should be assessed against standard-of-care practice before adoption of these technologies.

Looking ahead, prospective validation will be important to confirm the model's performance in clinical settings and to evaluate its contribution to diagnostic workflows. Future developments may benefit from incorporating histological confirmation and relevant clinical parameters, allowing for creation of multimodal AI models. These models bring together different types of input (imaging data, pathology reports, and laboratory values) to support a more complete and clinically informed analysis. Furthermore, harmonizing D-SOC image acquisition protocols across centers may contribute to greater consistency in training datasets, improving model robustness and facilitating wider adoption.

This study provides meaningful evidence that AI can contribute to evaluation of malignant biliary strictures across a range of anatomical locations. By maintaining high and consistent diagnostic performance in intrahepatic, perihilar, and distal lesions, the model addresses a known source of variability in D-SOC—an area in which interpretation is often constrained by anatomical complexity and operator experience.

A key strength lies in integration of detection and classification capabilities, which allows the model not only to differentiate malignant from benign frames but also to localize areas of concern. This dual functionality may assist in both diagnostic precision and procedure planning, particularly in challenging or surgically relevant cases.

Importantly, the model’s stability across diverse topographies raises broader questions about the role of algorithmic support in standardizing cholangioscopic evaluation. As AI becomes more embedded in clinical practice, tools like this one may help reduce subjectivity, bridge inter-operator gaps, and support more consistent decision-making. Although further validation is necessary, these findings represent a step toward more reproducible and equitable diagnostic strategies in biliary disease.
