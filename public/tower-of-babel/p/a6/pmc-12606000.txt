Attenuation of blood-brain barrier dysfunction by functionalized gold nanoparticles against amyloid-β peptide in an Alzheimer's disease-on-a-chip model
Authors: Andreas Arellano, Sujey Palma-Florez, Pablo Cabrera, Elizabeth Cortés-Adasme, Karen Bolaños, Freddy Celis, Aurora J. Araya-Vergara, Melina Pérez, Andrés Crespo, Maycol Huerta Matus, Eyleen Araya, Rebeca Aldunate, Marcelo J. Kogan, Josep Samitier, Anna Lagunas, Mònica Mir, Natalia Hassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gold nanoparticles (GNP) are highly valuable in nanotechnology due to their biocompatibility and unique physicochemical properties, which make them attractive as nanocarriers for targeted drug delivery. In the context of neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs) such as Alzheimer's disease (AD), GNP hold promise for reducing the toxicity of Amyloid-β peptide (Aβ) aggregates. However, a major challenge in developing new therapies for NDDs lies in the limited reliance on animal models and the difficulty of crossing the blood-brain barrier (BBB). This study investigates the effects of GNP on Aβ toxicity using a human-based BBB-organ-on-a-chip model (BBB-oC), mimicking the 3D cellular architecture of the BBB under both normal and pathological conditions. We rationally designed a novel nanosystem functionalized with the peptide D3, which functions both as a selective Aβ toxicity inhibitor and a BBB-targeting agent. The results show that GNP can cross the BBB, reduce the Aβ-induced cytotoxicity, and promote the maintenance of the BBB integrity. Moreover, controlling the shape of GNP further enhanced their protective effect. Overall, this work highlights the feasibility of rationally designed GNP as a promising therapeutic strategy for AD, evaluated through a more reliable and predictive human-relevant model.

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common neurodegenerative disease (NDDs), accounting for approximately 75 % of all diagnosed dementia cases [1]. Increased life expectancy and improvements in public health systems have contributed to a growing prevalence of AD, with the number of affected individuals expected to double every 20 years because longevity may increase dementia risk [2,3]. This has made AD one of the costliest conditions for the public health system. AD is characterized by a gradual deterioration of the central nervous system (CNS) attributed to neuronal death [4] conducting loss of cognitive functions and behavioral alterations [5]. One of its hallmarks is the pathological protein aggregation of amyloid-β peptide (Aβ) [6], where intermediate species of the process such as Aβ oligomers (AβO) demonstrated the most toxic character leading to neuronal dysfunction [7,8], and detrimental effects on the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [9,10]. Consequently, several therapeutical strategies have been focused on inhibiting the aggregation process or increasing the Aβ clearance, nevertheless these are mostly palliative and have failed to pause or even reverse this disease [11].

Gold nanoparticles (GNP) are outstanding nanomaterials thanks to their versatile properties such as high compatibility and stability in biofluids, tunable shape, and interesting plasmonic properties to be used as optical sensing probes, contrast agent for imaging, and photothermal therapy, altogether displaying their theragnostic purposes [12]. Also, they possess a large surface area that allows them to be functionalized [13,14]. Different kinds of molecules can be attached or adsorbed onto the surface of GNP, working as drug carriers and allowing to target specific organs, cells, even crossing biological membranes, enhancing their capability of delivery compared to other nanomaterials and reducing drug side effects [13,14]. Additionally, GNP take advantage of the common drug administration by increasing the local drug concentration, the drug solubility, and their pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamics [15,16]. Regarding AD, the design of an effective nanotherapeutic agent presents two main challenges to overcome. First, nanosystem needs to be functionalized with a molecule to inhibit or disrupt specifically the Aβ aggregation process, and second, it is necessary another functionalization with a molecule to improve their transport through the BBB [17]. In this scenario, the peptide D3 appears as a potential candidate to functionalize the GNP due to its promising effects over AβO [18,19], decreasing the Aβ toxicity both in vitro and in vivo models, and crossing the BBB [20]. Thus, the peptide D3 has decreased the amyloid load and neuroinflammation of transgenic murine models [21,22]. Furthermore, its D-enantiomeric configuration enhances its degradation resistance and avoids recognition by the immune system, thereby increasing its half-life. However, it has been observed a partially saturated transport through D3 peptide internalization via [20,23]. It means that although its plasma concentration is increased, the brain concentration may not necessarily be elevated accordingly. Notably, a similar beta-sheet breaker from the same family, the peptide D1, has been functionalized successfully onto different GNP [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. These nanosystems has exhibited several advantages such as surface enhanced fluorescence to detect amyloid aggregates in in vivo models, micro computed tomography detection, and decreasing the toxicity of Aβ peptide in a C. elegans model of Alzheimer's disease. However, the peptide D1 is limited to detect aggregates of Aβ peptide and not the Aβ oligomers AβO, which the peptide D3 can recognize. Thus, peptide D3 becomes an interesting strategy for functionalizing onto the GNP, to exploit the properties and advantages of GNP to generate a promising nanosystem as a potential therapy for AD. In this scenario, several clinical trials provide promising examples of GNP-based therapies as potential treatments [[28], [29], [30]]. For instance, GNP functionalized with siRNA for glioblastoma have demonstrated the ability to cross the BBB, induce tumor cell apoptosis, promote tumor shrinkage, and produce only tolerable side effects [29,30]. These findings reinforce the feasibility of translating GNP-based therapies into clinical applications.

Interestingly, nowadays one of the main issues related to clinical research is the translation from bench to bedside. Even though animal studies have demonstrated to be a useful approach to determine molecular mechanism of diseases, develop vaccines, and antibiotics, its predictive power in drug discovery remains limited in NDDs [31]. The use of animal models as preclinical studies is being questioned due to several discordances observed in human research [32,33]. In fact, around 8 % of CNS drugs can pass the phase I and less than 3 % the phase III of clinical trial, generating huge losses of invested capital and resources [34]. In recent years, organ-on-a-chip (OoC) technology has emerged as an innovative platform in biomedical research to mimicking the 3D architecture and microenvironment of human organs that allow 3D cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions, and dynamic conditions [35,36]. Indeed, OoC was officially categorized as a specific alternative to animal testing after the signed FDA Modernization Act 2.0 in 2022 [37], offering novel tools for physiological and disease modelling, bridging the gap for translational science success [[38], [39], [40]]. Specifically, Palma-Florez successfully developed a BBB-organ-on-a-chip (BBB-oC) model using human cells that demonstrated high cell viability and a proper development of the endothelial barrier, recreating a physiological context. Notably, this in vitro platform was employed to evaluate the capacity of functionalized GNP to reach brain parenchyma as well as their influence on the viability and integrity of the BBB after administration [41]. Moreover, the use of OoC and disease modelling has had a rapid development, making them interesting platforms to generate 3D models for NDDs [35]. For instance, Shin et al. assembled a complex system which incorporates neurons that express the Aβ peptide, which showed to damage the endothelial cells (ECs) contained in another chamber with the characteristic BBB dysfunction previously observed in AD [42]. These models opened avenues to provide closer features to the human brain, as those observed in BBB disruption at the early stages of AD. Importantly, they utilize inexpensive and reproducible animal-free approach, providing more predictable methods for the assessment of the permeability, toxicity and efficacy of promising new compounds [43,44]. Indeed, most of the developed BBB-oc models have been employed to analyze the permeability of different nanomaterials [45,46], but to the best of our knowledge no studies have evaluated the effect of GNP on Aβ toxicity in OoC. Thus, the performance of GNP on the Aβ peptide aggregation process is poorly understood in a close biological context, and the BBB-oC emerges as an appropriate tool to provide valuable models for disease modelling.

Notably, it has been described that the GNP shape can influence the population of Aβ species in the Aβ aggregation process modifying the toxicity of the sample [47,48]. At the nanoscale, the shape of the nanoparticle becomes an important variable which can promote or alter the molecular interaction between nanoparticles with proteins in the nano-bio interface [47,49,50]. This observation has been determined experimentally using different-shaped GNP which can induce different morphologies of Aβ aggregates [47,51]. Furthermore, different studies have reported that flat surfaces, e.g. gold nanoprisms (GNPr), showed a higher cell uptake compared to other shapes in different cell models [52,53] such as ECs [54,55]. For these reasons, we developed a rational design of a novel GNP-based drug delivery system for reducing the Aβ toxicity in the brain, which was tested into a BBB-oC to recreate disease modelling for AD. In this study, we designed and analyzed two different shapes of GNP which were functionalized with thiol-polyethylene glycol-carboxylic acid (PEG) to increase their stability and biocompatibility and to attach the peptide D3 to target AβO and inhibiting the aggregation of Aβ. We investigated the impact of each nanosystem on the Aβ toxicity over the BBB endothelium using a BBB-oC model (Fig.1). As far as we know, this work is the first to analyze the influence of GNP on the Aβ peptide effect into a BBB-oC, allowing a better understanding of the GNP performance in preclinical study. Altogether, this approach proposes an alternative for disease modelling to study the potential of nanomedicine in a more reliable, predictable and closer physiological context.

Results and discussion

Design of different shapes of functionalized GNP

In this study, two different shapes were designed and their effects compared on the Aβ toxicity in the BBB-oC: the flat GNPr and the curved gold nanospheres (GNS). Both nanosystems were functionalized with PEG which has two roles: first, stabilizing and enhancing the biocompatibility of GNP due to the presence of a terminal thiol group in the PEG, which attaches the PEG onto the GNP surface; and second, working as a linker by the presence of a carboxylic group in the another terminal of PEG to attach the peptide D3 (hereinafter referred to as GNPr-PEG or GNS-PEG, respectively, Fig. 2A and B). Then, they were functionalized with the peptide D3 using the EDC/NHS chemistry (the characterization of peptide D3 is referred to Fig. S1), forming an amide bond between the carboxylic group of PEG and the amine group of peptide D3 (hereinafter referred to as GNPr-PEG-D3 and GNS-PEG-D3, respectively). A complete physicochemical characterization of all nanosystems was done using SEM, UV–Vis–NIR spectroscopy, DLS, ζ potential (Fig. 2 and Table 1, Fig. S2). Based on the SEM images, the size of GNPr-PEG-D3 is 49 ± 15 nm and GNS-PEG-D3 is 39 ± 6 nm, exhibiting a similar mean surface area (4284 ± 1951 nm2 and 4776 ± 1472 nm2, respectively) (Fig. 2C and D, Table 1). As expected, even though the hydrodynamic diameter changed from the naked GNP to the PEG functionalization, it was not considerably modified with the functionalization with peptide D3, because the peptide length (around 3 nm) is close to the detection limit of the technique [56].

Regarding ζ potential, the GNPr from the synthesis exhibited a positive value, +38 mV, attributed to the presence of Cetyltrimethylammonium chloride molecules (CTAC) on the nanoparticle surface. On the other hand, GNS from the synthesis had a negative value, −39 mV, because the synthesis is performed in the presence of citrate. When both shapes were functionalized with PEG, they modified their ζ potential towards more negative values due to the presence of PEG's carboxylic group (+38 to −50 mV for GNPr-PEG, and −39 to −44 mV for GNS-PEG). Finally, both GNPr-PEG and GNS-PEG increased their ζ potential (Δ = +30 mV) after the functionalization with the peptide D3, reaching values nearly −19 to −14 mV, respectively. These changes correlated to the surface modification with the peptide D3 because it has 5 positive arginine in its sequence (pI 12.6). Hence, thanks to this characterization, we determined the proper functionalization with PEG and the peptide D3 on two different GNP shapes, the flat GNPr-PEG-D3 and the curved GNS-PEG-D3, with similar physicochemical properties. Additionally, it is worth noting that the ζ potential values obtained for both nanosystems are similar to those reported with low toxicity and considered adequate for biological studies [57,58]. Since GNP are focused on biological applications, their cytotoxicity was analyzed in the SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cell line (Fig. 2G and H, Fig. S2), widely used in neurodegenerative models [59]. The GNP concentration was calculated through Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) (Fig. S3), and different concentrations were incubated for 24 h with neuroblastoma cells and analyzed GNP toxicity by the MTS assay. Whereas GNPr-PEG-D3 do not exhibit a cytotoxic effect at the range analyzed; GNPr-PEG, GNS-PEG-D3, and GNS-PEG exhibited a statistically significant decrease in the cell viability at the highest concentration (0.4 nM), correlating with previous reports where high concentrations of GNP induce cytotoxic effects [60]. For this reason, the following analyses were conducted using the safe concentration of 0.2 nM for all nanosystems evaluated.

Additionally, to thoroughly analyze the interaction of peptide D3 onto the GNP surface, amino acid analysis and Raman spectroscopy were performed. Using the GNP concentration obtained by NTA and the amino acid analysis (Fig. S4), the number of peptides per nanoparticle was calculated, being 2276 ± 264 and 5700 ± 2260 molecules onto GNPr-PEG-D3 and GNS-PEG-D3, respectively. Interestingly, even though both nanosystems present similar physicochemical properties, the functionalization degree of D3 was clearly different. Similar results have been observed in previous studies using another enantiomer peptide from the same family [61], where they suggested a differential disposition of peptide D3 onto the surface of each GNP. To support this finding, Raman spectroscopy was performed to study how the peptide D3 interacts with the surface of either GNPr or GNS. The Raman spectrum of peptide D3 is presented in Fig. 3A which is characterized by medium-to-low relative intensity of the signals and the observed assignments are detailed in Table 2. Eight signals at 481, 686, 1197, 1220, 1310, 1340, 1454, and 1534 cm−1 dominated the Raman profile and were shown in both nanoparticles. The first (481 cm−1) is attributed to a deformation vibration from the peptide backbone (Fig. 3B), as well as the signal that appeared around 400 cm−1. On the other hand, the vibrations with variable relative intensity observed at 960, 1139, and 1197 cm−1 are associated with the amino acids Arg, Thr, and Pro, respectively. As detailed in Table 2, many of the observed spectral signals correspond to the coupled vibrations of two or three amino acids, with His and Arg being the most prevalent. The Au–S signal is present in both nanosystems; in GNS–PEG–D3 it appears at 274 cm−1 (inset spectrum in Fig. 3A), whereas in GNPr–PEG–D3 the same interaction is observed at 262 cm−1. SERS spectra of the peptide D3 onto GNPr displayed limited vibrational information, due to the low relative intensity of the signals that was distinguished at 772, 1004, 1182, 1293, 1400, 1456 and 1514 cm−1. Signals observed at 1456, 1293 and 1182 cm−1 were attributed to coupled vibrations of His and Arg, while the signal that appeared at 1004 cm−1 was ascribed to an in-plane deformation of His. The spectral data suggests the peptide's middle and COOH-terminal regions preferentially interact with the surface, while the N-terminal remains distant from the GNPr surface (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the SERS bands of D3 attached to the GNS surface displayed many bands observed at 501, 516, 861, 1005, 1040, 1400, 1445, 1457, 1574, and 1617 cm−1 indicating orientation of peptide D3 on GNS surface. In particular, the three signals observed at 1005, 1040 and 1617 cm−1 inferred a tendency of His to interact with the GNS surface. This evidence supported that the GNS surface interacts preferentially with the fragment Arg-Pro-Arg-Thr-Arg-Leu-His. For these reasons, signals associated with both Pro were visible at 861 and 1457 cm−1 and Arg coupled with Pro at 861 and 1400 cm−1; and thus, the N-terminal of the peptide D3 is close to the surface while the middle section and COOH-terminal extend farther away (Fig. 3D). This assignment allows us to establish the preferred orientation of peptide D3 related to the GNPr and GNS surface, according to the Raman assignment and SERS selection rules [62]. Specifically, the portion of D3 that interacts with the flat surface of GNPr is larger, while the portion that contacts the curved surface of GNS is smaller. In vivo evaluation of functionalized GNP on Aβ toxicity

To evaluate the potential of GNPr-PEG-D3 and GNS-PEG-D3 as candidates to inhibit Aβ toxicity, their effects were analyzed using an in vivo Caenorhabditis elegans model for AD. We used the transgenic strain GMC101, which expresses full-length Aβ (1–42 amino acids) human peptide in its body wall muscle cells. The expression of this peptide leads to the formation of oligomers that aggregates, resulting in a paralysis phenotype [63]. C. elegans, as an in vivo model for AD, provides a suitable and powerful platform for the rapid screening of potential therapeutic candidates. This is attributed to its short lifespan, ease of maintenance, and well-characterized genetics [64]. The GMC101 is a thermosensitive strain; worms were grown at 16 °C until reaching the last larval stage (L4) and subsequently, the culture temperature was increased to 25 °C to induce the expression of the Aβ peptide. For the treatment, worms were placed in Petri dish containing food supplemented with different concentrations (0.2; 0.05; and 0.001 nM) of each functionalized GNP for 24 h. The worms were then monitored for movement until paralysis occurred (see scheme in Fig. 4A). The concentration range selected was based on the cytotoxic studies performed above in the SH-SY5Y cells. Fig. 4 shows the results of monitoring worm paralysis during the 16–24 h of treatment. The number of paralyzed worms was recorded hourly for each GNP treatment. GNPr-PEG-D3 significantly improved motor function, reducing the number of paralyzed worms at concentrations of 0.2 nM and 0.05 nM (Fig. 4B and F). This reduction was statistically significant, demonstrating an approximate 40 % improvement relative to the untreated control group (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, GNPr-PEG-D3 enhanced the C. elegans phenotype compared to peptide D3 at 0.5 μM, equivalent to the peptide concentration present in 0.2 nM GNPr-PEG-D3 (Fig. S5A and S5B). On the other hand, all the GNS-PEG-D3 concentrations evaluated were not able to avoid the paralyzed phenotype (Fig. 4D and H), even compared to peptide D3 at a concentration of 1.0 μM, which is the equivalent of the peptide at 0.2 nM GNS-PEG-D3 (Fig. S5C and S5D). Remarkably, comparison of the peptide D3 concentration in each nanosystem with free peptide D3, expressed as the Relative Efficiency Index (REI), revealed that GNPr-PEG-D3 exhibited markedly higher efficiency, even at the low peptide concentrations present on the GNPr surface, an effect not observed with GNS (Table S1).Fig. 4In vivo analysis of GNP in the C. elegans GMC101 model of AD. Thus, from the Raman analysis we can surmise a possible explanation for these results. Even though GNS present a higher amount of functionalized peptide D3 compared to GNPr, its orientation and disposition are completely different. Olubiyi et al. studied the interaction between peptide D3 with the Aβ peptide through computational studies and explicit solvent MD simulations [19]. This study found that the N-terminus of the peptide D3 strongly interact with the Aβ peptide, where this interaction is driven by the electrostatic interactions between the presence of 3 arginine of the peptide D3 with the residues Glu11, Glu22, and Asp23 of Aβ peptide. Conversely, the C-terminal end of peptide D3 only possesses 2 arginines and their interaction is not strong with the Aβ peptide. Thus, our Raman analysis supports the paralysis assay and in consistent with previous reports, reinforcing our hypothesis that the N-terminus is exposed and available for interaction with peptide D3 on gold nanoprisms while it is buried on the surface of gold nanospheres. Finally, the controls without the peptide D3: GNPr-PEG (Fig. 4C and G) and GNS-PEG (Fig. 4E and I) did not show any effect on the C. elegans and did not decrease the paralyzed phenotype. Previous studies have shown comparable effects using different types of molecules on this Aβ worm model [65,66]. A notably reduction in the paralyzed phenotype has been associated with modifications on the Aβ aggregation process; and thus, altering and decreasing the Aβ toxicity. Altogether, the rational design of our nanosystem GNPr-PEG-D3 suggests a potential reduction of the toxicity related to Aβ aggregation process by avoiding the paralyzed C. elegans phenotype.

To dilucidated the aggregation state of the Aβ peptide in the C. elegans at the endpoint of the paralysis assay, we conducted additional experiments by staining the worms using the NIAD-4 probe, which enables live imaging compared to other stains [67,68]. Interestingly, treatment with GNPr-PEG-D3 significantly increased the staining intensity compared with the control and other treatments (Fig. 5). This result is consistent with previous reports [65,69,70]. For instance, Limbocker et al. [65] showed that the molecule trodusquemine produced a similar effect when tested with the same probe and worm strain, as evidenced by increased NIAD-4 the fluorescence intensity and a greater amount of aggregates. Also, they observed this molecule reduced the paralyzed phenotype. In agreement, our findings indicate that GNPr-PEG-D3 decrease Aβ peptide toxicity by shifting the equilibrium toward fibril formation, and therefore, reducing the amount of toxic intermediate species. Additionally, to evaluate GNP permeability in C. elegans, the gold content was measured after each treatment in the paralysis assay. Worms were incubated with 0.2 nM of each nanosystem for 24 h and compared with untreated controls. For quantification, 100 worms per condition were washed three times to remove the excess surface-bound GNP, dissolved in aqua regia, and analyzed by absorption spectroscopy. In this sense, although all samples showed the presence of gold inside the worms, the concentration was higher in those treated with GNPr-PEG-D3 compared with both the control and the other nanosystems (Table S2). This finding may indicate that this nanosystem has greater permeability or a slower clearance rate than the others, possibly due to the presence of peptide D3, which may either enhance permeability or promote strong interactions with aggregates inside the worms. Interestingly, these data are consistent with the previous C. elegans results and the Raman analysis, which revealed a differential orientation of peptide D3 on each nanosystem, with GNPr-PEG-D3 showing the most promising effect.

However, even though this in vivo AD model is an attractive approach, the complex networks and cell interactions found in humans are not mimicked, limiting their relevance in capturing the progression of age-related disorders [71]. Thus, the findings obtained in this model serve as a preliminary step toward validation in more complex models to recreate neurodegenerative conditions. In this scenario, OoC emerges as an interesting technology to emulate human organ structures and function in a physiological context [72]. Moreover, OoC has demonstrated to reproduce pathophysiological environment, generating platforms to develop disease modelling and drug screening [38,39]. The BBB is the primary barrier that every therapeutic agent needs to overcome to reach the CNS. Furthermore, studies have reported that BBB lost its integrity and function at the early stages of AD leading to a faster progression of the disease [73]. For these reasons, a BBB-oC in the presence of Aβ peptide becomes an interesting platform to evaluate the permeability and effect of the GNP over the endothelium in an AD pathophysiological context.

Interestingly, only GNS-PEG incubated on the ECs did exhibit a significant decrease in cell viability at all range of concentrations analyzed, reaching 75 % of cell viability (Fig. S6F). The defined ISO 10993-5 standard defines a material as not in vitro cytotoxic if the cell viability is above 70 % after at least 24 h of incubation [74]. To evaluate the GNP permeability, GNP were functionalized with a fluorescent probe to monitor their crossing from the blood channel to the central chamber in the BBB-oC. GNPr-PEG-D3 and GNPr-PEG were functionalized with the fluorescent probe Alexa-488 while GNS-PEG-D3 and GNS-PEG with Alexa-647. Different Alexa probes were employed to avoid the quenching generated by the overlap of the fluorophore's emission with the absorption of each GNP shape [75]. On day 7 of the BBB-oC, 0.4 nM of each nanosystem was injected into the blood channel of independent microdevices and incubated for 1 h. The fluorescence emitted by the GNP in the chip was obtained at 0 h, to discard autofluorescence, and every 15 min to calculate the permeability coefficient (P coef) (Fig. S7). Interestingly, all nanosystems exhibited the ability to cross the barrier but with slight differences. Comparing the nanoparticles without the peptide D3, GNPr-PEG-Alexa-488 showed a statistical difference compared to GNS-PEG-Alexa-647, which correlates with previous literature where flat surfaces present higher degree of cell internalization [52,53]. However, although the nanoparticles with the peptide D3, GNPr-PEG-D3-Alexa-488 and GNS-PEG-D3-Alexa-647, showed a higher internalization they were not statistically different compared to GNPr-PEG-Alexa-488. This result may be attributable to the Alexa functionalization was carried out onto the peptide D3, masking its crossing effect. Notably, GNS-PEG-D3-Alexa-647 exhibited a slightly high permeability compared to the GNPr, which may be attributable to the higher presence of D3 onto GNS-PEG-D3, as was above analyzed by amino acid analysis.

Once the ability of the synthesized nanovehicles to cross the BBB was confirmed, the cytotoxic effect of Aβ on the BBB-oC and the influence of GNP on the impact of Aβ were evaluated, recreating a pathophysiology context. A live/dead assay was used to analyze the cytotoxic effect of Aβ into the BBB-oC. For this purpose, on day 7 of the BBB-oC, 5 μM of Aβ peptide was injected into the brain channel to reach the hydrogel zone and produce toxic Aβ species. Based on the above cytotoxic results, 0.2 nM of every GNP was injected into the blood channel of independent microdevices and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C and 5 % pCO2. The cytotoxic effect of Aβ peptide was determined by viable cells (green stained) and death cells (red stained) in the area where pericytes and astrocytes were cultured in the hydrogel and in the area where ECs have generated the barrier. A non-cytotoxic effect from Aβ peptide was observed in the hydrogel zone (Fig. S8), as no damage was observed in pericytes and astrocytes under treatments with Aβ peptide for 24 h [76,77]. However, a clear toxic effect was observed on the barrier zone that was decreased by the presence of GNPr-PEG-D3 but not for GNPr-PEG (Fig. 7B). This toxicity over the ECs correlates with studies that show that these cells are the most affected on the BBB of patients with AD [78]. Nevertheless, these data are qualitative and obtaining a quantitative value in 3D culture is challenging. One approach that was employed by Ong et al., 2020 consisted of using the ratio of dead cells (stained for ethidium) to total cells (stained nuclei with DAPI) in 2D cultures [79], but for 3D culture it is difficult to segment and count individually. Instead, since both stains do not compete each other [80,81], the ratio of stained area was used to perform a semiquantitative analysis (Fig. 7B). From this analysis, Aβ peptide generated an increment in the ratio Ethidium/DAPI in the barrier area, that was decreased by the presence of GNPr-PEG-D3 but not for GNPr-PEG (Fig. 7C). However, the cytotoxic effect of Aβ was not suppressed in the presence of GNS-PEG-D3 (Fig. 7E and Fig. S9). Meanwhile, the cytotoxic effects of Aβ on the hydrogel zone with both GNP are negligible (Fig. 7D and F, S8 and Fig. S10). Additionally, the Aβ cytotoxic effect was analyzed in presence of 0.5 μM D3 (concentration of D3 determined on the GNP surface) to compare with the nanosystem. Notably, an ANOVA test showed that the reduction in Aβ cytotoxicity in the presence of GNPr-PEG-D3 was comparable to that observed with 0.5 μM and 1.0 μM of the free peptide D3 (Fig. 7C and Fig. S11), indicating that GNPr-PEG-D3 provide a similar protective effect against cytotoxicity.

As control, every nanosystem was incubated in absence of Aβ into the BBB-oC for 24 h, confirming their non-cytotoxic effect in the barrier nor hydrogel zone (Figs. S12, S13, S14, S15), as was observed in the 2D cultures. These findings correlate with those obtained from the C. elegans model for AD, in which the greatest inhibition of Aβ cytotoxicity was demonstrated for GNPr-PEG-D3 while no effect was observed with GNS-PEG-D3. The in vitro BBB-oC model was able to replicate the in vivo results obtained for both types of GNP, and provided more information on the cell type most affected by Aβ toxicity and on the ability of GNP to cross the barrier, making the BBB-oC a useful platform to assess potential nanosystems in a cheaper and high-throughput manner for developing new treatments against AD. Finally, since only GNPr-PEG-D3 demonstrated a suppressive effect on both in vivo and in vitro Aβ cytotoxicity, further experiments were focused exclusively on analyzing GNPr-PEG-D3.

Based on these results, we further analyzed our BBB-oC after treatment with Aβ peptide and the different nanosystems. For this purpose, we collected the supernatant from each microchip after 24 h of treatment. The supernatants were analyzed by western blot using the antibody 6E10, a widely used antibody to detect different sizes of Aβ aggregates. As shown in Fig. S16, high-molecular weight Aβ species were detected in the presence of GNPr-PEG-D3, accompanied by a decrease in low-molecular weight species. Conversely, high-molecular weight were not observed in the presence of the other nanosystems or peptide D3. Instead, treatments with GNPr-PEG, GNS-PEG-D3, GNS-PEG, or peptide D3 resulted in the presence of various small Aβ species, consistent with oligomeric species. Thus, the results obtained from the BBB-oC agree with those obtained from the C. elegans (Fig. 5), demonstrating an enhanced aggregation state and a concomitant reduction in Aβ toxicity.

Influence of GNP-PEG-D3 on tight junctions

Severe alterations in BBB functions such as microvascular permeability and disruption of cerebral vascular ECs tight junctions (TJs) have been associated with Aβ peptide [9,10], triggering the loss of BBB integrity and function. In fact, ECs damage is considered one of the earliest symptoms in AD [82], showing a close relationship between Aβ peptide and barrier dysfunction. The integrity of the endothelium barrier into the BBB-oC after the presence of Aβ was determined using the standard fluorescent tracer dextran70-FITC (D70, 70.000 Da) which is widely used to evaluate the permeability performance of the BBB in vitro [83]. On day 8 of BBB-oC, D70 100 μM was injected into the blood channel after the treatment with Aβ peptide for 24 h alone and, co-administered with GNPr-PEG-D3 or GNPr-PEG. As a live control, the BBB-oC was incubated with only fresh cell medium. Then, images were captured at different times to determine the permeability of D70 towards the brain area and calculate the P coef. As shown in Fig. 8, live control exhibited a P coef 0.64 × 10−6 cm/s (Fig. 8A and E), which correlates with previous works reported in the literature for microfabricated 3D models [84,85]. In the BBB-oC subjected to Aβ peptide alone, an increment of the fluorescent dextran permeability was observed with a P coef of 2.4 × 10−6 cm/s (Fig. 8B and E). This value suggests that the Aβ peptide not only damage the ECs but also disrupt the barrier functionality, probably due to the impairment of the TJs of the barrier [86]. Conversely, treatment with GNPr-PEG-D3 decreased the fluorescence in the central chamber and presented a P coef 1.4 × 10−6 cm/s (Fig. 8C and E). This lower P coef was statistically significant to the Aβ peptide alone while the control GNPr-PEG did not show a significant difference (P coef 1.8 × 10−6 cm/s) (Fig. 8D and E). Thus, Aβ peptide increases the permeability of the BBB in the BBB-oC indicating a potential neurovascular problem in the presence of Aβ. On the other hand, VE-cadherin is one of the most important endothelial-specific AJs playing a key role for the maintenance and control of ECs contacts, vascular development, vascular permeability, and the architecture of BBB [88]. Thus, these two proteins are main components of the BBB and under pathological conditions their disruption has been reported, compromising their integrity and being associated with early stages of the AD [73]. Effect of GNPr-PEG-D3 on early molecular changes for AD

Finally, to further understand the behavior of BBB in presence of Aβ peptide and the protective role showed by GNPr-PEG-D3, we assessed the gene expression by RT-qPCR of characteristic proteins present in the BBB and related to TJs, AJs, carriers, and transporters (Fig. 11). These proteins are commonly described as diminished in different models of AD [87]; however, in our BBB-oC model affected by Aβ peptide these proteins showed upregulation for almost all of them, except for the transferrin receptor (TFR). This apparent discrepancy between literature and our work could be explained by the course of AD progression. One of the main molecular mechanisms of Aβ peptide toxicity is to promote an inflammatory process altering the correct function and integrity of BBB [95,96]. However, this effect is not necessarily the same throughout the process and may change over time [97]. Previous evidence has described an upregulation of different genes when the BBB exhibits early damage which triggers the activation of compensatory molecular mechanism to reverse the BBB impairment [98]. For example, VE-cad expression was upregulated [99] on ECs culture in response to the tumor necrosis factor (TNFα), a key pro-inflammatory cytokine, but finally its level was depleted overtime, and the BBB integrity being compromised when the inflammation is sustained. Interestingly, this early upregulation was not accompanied by an increase in the protein level [99]. A similar behavior was observed for Claudin-5 (Cl5) under lipopolysaccharide (LPS) treatments (an inflammation inductor) on microglial cultures [100]. Regarding our findings, this phenomenon is comparable to those observed for ZO-1 and VE-cad. Since in BBB-oC, in response to the presence of Aβ, BBB gene expression was upregulated, as indicated by the results with RT-qPCR (Fig. 11B and D), but the protein signal in immunofluorescence was lower (Fig. 9E and F). Thus, we hypothesized that the upregulation observed for TJs and AJs could be associated with an attempt to restore the BBB integrity under the Aβ toxic effect (Fig. 11A, B, 11C, and 11D), but it was not produced at the time. Additionally, low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) and P-glycoprotein, which have been described as responsible to Aβ clearance from the brain, exhibited high levels of expression in presence of Aβ peptide in our BBB-oC (Fig. 11E and G). Moreover, the same upregulation was observed for glucose transporter 1 (GLUT-1), which mediates the glucose transport into the brain (Fig. 11H). Markedly, different studies have shown that these proteins are upregulated under inflammatory treatments with TNFα [[101], [102], [103]], suggesting the BBB may be triggering compensatory mechanisms to clear Aβ peptides and maintain adequate brain glucose supply under an inflammatory response. This information allied with our findings, revealing a similar behavior of our BBB-oC where probably compensatory responses against Aβ toxicity were conducted. Regarding TFR, a key receptor in the transport of iron into the brain, there was not a significant difference comparing the live control to the incubation with Aβ peptide (Fig. 11F), which correlates with literature [104]. Finally, when the BBB-oC were treated with GNPr-PEG-D3 in presence of Aβ peptide, it was no observed this significant upregulation of all proteins induced by Aβ peptide (Fig. 11), obtaining no significant differences with the live control. These results offered further evidence of the protective effect of GNPr-PEG-D3 in the BBB-oC and supported their capability of preventing several endothelium impairments triggered by Aβ peptide. Conclusion

In this study, we developed a novel protective nanosystem that combines the properties of the peptide D3 with the advantages of nanotechnology through the rational design of nanocarriers. We found that the shape of GNP is a critical parameter influencing therapeutic effectiveness, as observed with better performance of GNPr compared to GNS, being more effective GNPr on the inhibition of Aβ peptide toxicity, while GNS cannot reduce its detrimental effect. Interestingly, although GNPr possess a lower number of peptide D3 than GNS (2276 and 5700 molecules per nanoparticle, respectively), they exhibited a distinct spatial arrangement and orientation of peptide D3 on each nanoparticle. This finding highlights the crucial role of nanoparticle shape in determining therapeutic efficacy. The protective effects of GNPr was further assessed both in vivo and in vitro models, showing consistent outcomes across models and reinforcing the relevance of nanoparticles geometry in modulating Aβ toxicity. Notably, both models revealed an increase in Aβ aggregation accompanied by reduce toxicity, supporting the concept that GNPr-PEG-D3 redirects aggregation toward less harmful off-pathway species. This mechanistic insight is consistent with previous reports of small molecules and peptides that modulate Aβ assembly, and it strengthens the rationale for designing nanomaterials that influence aggregation equilibria. Importantly, this work underscores the translational potential of GNPr as a drug delivery system while contributing mechanistic insights into the rational design of nanoparticle-based therapies. Our findings are supported by previous studies showing that nanoparticle shape, particularly flat surfaces such as GNPr, enhances cellular uptake, including by endothelial cells. This effect is further facilitated by the adsorptive-mediated transcytosis of arginine-rich peptides like D3, which exploit favorable electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged surface of the BBB. Moreover, PEGylation improves nanoparticle stability and circulation time, while optimization of particle size prevents rapid clearance. Together, these parameters, shape, charge, size, and surface chemistry, synergistically contribute to the enhanced therapeutic performance of GNPr-PEG-D3 in vitro and in vivo. In fact, the correlation between in vivo and in vitro outcomes further validates the BBB-oC as a reliable human-relevant platform for preclinical evaluation. By exposing the BBB-oC to Aβ peptide, we successfully recapitulated key early events of AD pathology, including barrier disruption, cytotoxicity, and alterations in junctional proteins and gene expression. Treatment with GNPr-PEG-D3 attenuated these detrimental effects, preserving tight and adherent junctions, maintaining BBB-associated proteins, and protecting cellular integrity. Altogether, GNPr-PEG-D3 emerges as a promising therapeutic candidate for inhibiting Aβ toxicity, while BBB-oC offers a more reliable, precise, and predictive tool for preclinical drug screening, toxicity test, and disease modelling in the context of neurodegeneration.

Methods

Synthesis of GNPr

GNPr were synthesized by a modification in the method reported by Chen et al., [105]. The synthesis was realized in a water bath (27 °C), in which a flask with 0.1 M Cetyltrimethylammonium chloride (CTAC, Sigma Aldrich) is first diluted with ultrapure water (Milli-Q degree) and followed by the addition of 0.01 M KI (Sigma Aldrich) under mild stirring for 2 min. Meanwhile, a mix of 29.4 mM HAuCl4 (Sigma Aldrich) and 0.1 M NaOH (Sigma Aldrich) were poured to the flask. The solution was left to mix for 3 min, turning yellowish color. Then, a 0.064 M ascorbic acid solution (Sigma Aldrich) was injected into the solution with mild stirring for 1 min. Next, the solution was kept undisturbed for 1 min, where yellowish solution gradually becomes colorless, and finally, 0.1M NaOH was added to the flask, quickly shaken for 20 seg, and kept undisturbed for 10 min. The colorless solution turned red, purple, and finally blue. Since GNPr are dissolved in an organic detergent (CTAC), it is needed to exchange the buffer for a biocompatible solution to use them in biological systems [106]. GNPr were centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min at room temperature. The precipitate was resuspended in Poly(sodium 4-styrenesulfonate) solution (0.5 % w/v, Sigma Aldrich) and stirred for 2 h. Then, the solution is centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min at room temperature, the supernatant discarded, and the precipitate resuspended in a 38,7 mM Sodium Citrate solution (Sigma Aldrich). The solution was kept under mild stirring for 12 h at room temperature. The next day, the resultant solution was centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min at room temperature, the supernatant discarded, and the precipitate again resuspended in a 38,7 mM Sodium Citrate solution.

Synthesis of GNS

For the synthesis of GNS a modified Turkevich protocol [107] was utilized. An aqueous solution of 0.294 mM HAuCl4 (Sigma Aldrich) was heated at 194 °C with continuous stirring for 10 min. After that, a citrate solution (1 % w/v, Sigma Aldrich) was injected to the gold solution under continuous stirring for 20 min at 194 °C. Then, the solution was cooled down to room temperature, adjusted to pH 8.0, and filtered (pore size 0.22 μM).

Functionalization of gold nanosystems with polyethylene glycol

To stabilize and functionalize the gold nanosystems with the linker to bound the inhibitor peptide, it is necessary to functionalize with thiol-polyethylene glycol-carboxylic acid (PEG) that possesses two reactive groups: the thiol group to bind the PEG onto the gold surface and the carboxylic acid to react with the peptide D3. Either GNPr or GNS from the synthesis were centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min. Each pellet was resuspended in a solution of 5 mg/mL PEG (HS-PEG-COOH, Mw = 5000, JenKem, USA), stirred for 3 h at room temperature, and finally centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min to eliminate the excess of PEG. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate resuspended in ultrapure water (Milli-Q degree).

Synthesis of peptide D3

The synthesis of peptide D3 (Arg-Pro-Arg-Thr-Arg-Leu-His-Thr-His-Arg-Asn-Arg) was carried following a solid phase synthesis protocol using 2-chlorotrityl chloride (2-CTC) resin as a solid support and using the fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl (Fmoc) and Dimethylformamide Dichloromethane methodology. All amino acids were purchased from Iris Biotech GmbH. The first coupling was done in dichloromethane and diisopropylethylamine for 2 h 160 μl of methanol was added and stirred for another hour. Before coupling the second amino acid, the Fmoc protecting group was removed with piperidine. Subsequently, Oxyma bromide was added, and the second amino acid was added in dimethylformamide and diisopropylcarbodimide (DIC). For the coupling of the third to the last amino acid was followed the same protocol as the second amino acid and was repeated consecutively until the amino acid sequence is finished. For the cleavage has been used 95 % TFA (19 mL), 2.5 % H2O (0.5 mL), and 2.5 % triisopropylmethylsilane (TIS) (0.5 mL). It was left to stir for 1 h and an additional hour was added for each arginine that was present in the peptide. After this time, the resulting mixture was precipitated into a falcon tube with cold ether. It was centrifuged at 6000 rpm until all the peptide sedimented, the supernatant was removed. The peptide was dissolved in water, frozen and subsequently lyophilized for 48 h. Once lyophilized, it was stored at 4 °C for subsequent purification and characterization. The Peptide purification was performed on a C18 reversed phase column with water/acetonitrile (ACN) gradient as mobile phase. Purity 98 %. Yield 88 %.

Functionalization of gold nanosystems with peptide D3

The functionalization with the peptide D3 was using the EDC/NHS chemistry (1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide/N-Hydroxysuccinimide, respectively). The pegylated gold nanosystems were centrifuged at 8000 g, and the pellets were resuspended in 1 mg EDC and 2,5 mg NHS dissolved in 500 μL buffer MES 0,1 M and pH 5,5, kept in an ultrasonic bath at 4 °C for 15 min. Then, samples were centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min at room temperature, the supernatant discarded, and pellets were resuspended in a solution of peptide D3 (1 mg/mL). The reaction was left overnight at 300 rpm (room temperature). Next day, the mix was centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min at room temperature, the supernatant discarded, and the precipitate resuspended in ultrapure water (Milli-Q degree).

Characterization of gold nanosystems

All gold nanosystems were characterized by UV–Vis Spectroscopy using a LAMBDA 25 UV/VIS (Perkin Elmer). Dynamic Light Scattering and zeta potential were performed using a disposable polycarbonate cell (DTS 1070, Malvern, UK) and measured in a Malvern ZetaSizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern, UK). Scanning electron microscope images were acquired using a Zeiss GeminiSEM 360, 5 kV (Carl Zeiss).

Quantification of gold nanosystems

The different gold nanosystems were diluted and measured by Nano Tracking Analysis (NTA) using an NS300 instrument (Malvern Instruments). For the analysis, particle movement under brownian motion was recorded using a camera level 11, 30-sec of video recording, 25 frames per second, a detection threshold of 6, and a blue laser (488 nm). Each gold nanosystem records were obtained by the average of three replicates for three different samples and analyzed by the software NTA 3.2 Dev Build 3.2.16.

Peptide D3 quantification

Amino acid analysis was used to determine the number of peptide D3 molecules onto the GNP surface. All samples were centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min at RT, dissolved in a solution 6.0 M HCl, added a solution of 2.5 mM α-Aminobutyric acid (AABA) as internal standard, and hydrolyzed during 48 h at 110 °C. The evaporated samples were derivatized by adding 6-aminoquinolyl-n-hydroxysuccinimide-activated heterocyclic carbamate (Waters AccQ·Fluor Reagent). Then, they were allowed to react for 1 min at RT followed by 10 min at 55 °C. Finally, 20 μL of sample was injected into a HPLC-PDA instrument with a AccQ-Tag column and run for 50 min to determine the concentration of peptide D3.

The number of peptide D3 per GNP was calculated by:N=peptideD3(μM)GNP(nM)∗100

Raman spectroscopy

All Raman measurements were carried out using a WITec SNOM Raman spectrometer, coupled to a microscope. The system was equipped with a 785 nm laser line and an electrically cooled CCD camera. The calibration was performed using the silicon Raman band at 520 cm−1. To minimize intrinsic fluorescence, the samples were deposited on a thin gold substrate. The laser power on the sample was carefully adjusted to less than 1 % of the total power, to avoid thermal damage to the sample. Each spectrum was acquired with an accumulation time of 10 s, achieving a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1. Spectra were recorded within the range of 200–1800 cm−1. The samples were prepared and maintained in their solvent without any exposure to light prior to measurement.

Paralysis assay in C. elegans

C. elegans were cultured under standard conditions described by Stiernagle (2006) [108]. The GMC101 strain was obtained from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center and maintained at 16 °C on nematode growth medium (NGM) agar plates seeded with Escherichia coli (OP50). For the assays, age synchronization of C. elegans individuals was performed according to Stiernagle's protocol. Briefly, eggs were resuspended in M9 buffer (3 g/L KH2PO4, 6 g/L Na2HPO4, 5 g/L NaCl, 1 μM MgSO4), seeded onto agar plates to allow hatching, and incubated at 16 °C for 72 h until reaching the L4 larval stage. Subsequently, 30–40 worms were transferred to NGM plates pre-treated with a mixture of E. coli OP50 and various concentrations of GNP (GNPr-PEG-D3, GNPr-PEG, GNS-PEG-D3, GNS-PEG) or peptide D3. Worms were then incubated at 25 °C for 24 h, and the number of paralyzed animals was recorded hourly.

Live C. elegans stained with NIAD-4

L4-stage GMC101 C. elegans, after 15 h of treatment with or without GNP, were incubated with 3 μM NIAD-4 (0.01 % DMSO in M9 buffer) for 2 h at 25 °C. Worms were then washed three times with M9 buffer to remove excess dye and allowed to recover on NGM plates for 3 h at 25 °C. Fluorescence indicative of β-amyloid fibrils/plaques was assessed by microscopic image analysis of the nematode head region. For imaging, nematodes were immobilized with 40 mM sodium azide and mounted on 2 % agarose pads. Samples were examined using a BX51 microscope (Olympus, Japan) equipped with a Micropublisher 3.3 RTV digital camera (JH Technologies, USA). Experiments were performed in triplicate, and images were captured from at least 30 nematodes per experimental condition. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, MD, USA) and normalized as corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF).

Atomic adsorption spectroscopy

L4-stage GMC101 C. elegans, after 24 h of treatment with or without GNP were collected and washed three times with M9 buffer to remove excess of GNP and bacteria. Then worms were digested in aqua regia for 48 h, diluted with milli-Q water, and introduced into a graphite furnace. Samples were pyrolyzed under an argon flame, and their absorption was measured at 242.8 nm. The final gold concentration was determined using Graphite Furnace Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (GFAAS, ContrAA 800G-Analytic Jena, Germany).

cell culture of SH-SY5Y cells, astrocytes, pericytes, and endothelial cells

Human SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cell line (ATCC #CRL-2266) was maintained as an adherent culture in standard conditions: humid atmosphere at 37 °C and 5 % CO2, in DMEM/F12 medium (Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium/Ham's Nutrient Mixture F12) supplemented with fetal bovine serum 10 % and 1 % penicilin/streptomicyn.

Human primary Astrocytes (Sciencell #1830) and human brain-vascular pericytes (Sciencell #1200) were independently cultured in a T-75 flask previously coated with poly-L-Lysine solution (PLL) (2 μg/cm2). The cell medium used was commercial astrocyte medium (AM) supplemented with 1 % of astrocyte growth supplement (AGS), while the pericyte medium (PM) with 1 % of pericyte growth supplement (PGS). Both media were enriched with 2 % of fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % of penicillin/streptomycin (P/E) solution.

Human brain endothelial cell line (hCMEC/D3) (Merck #SCC066) was cultured in a T-75 flask coated with collagen Type I (Sigma-Aldrich). The endothelial cell media (EM) used was endoGRO™ medium enriched with the provided supplements: 0,2 % of EndoGRO-LS supplement, 5 ng/mL of rhEGF, 10 nM L-glutamine, 1 μg/mL of hydrocortisone hemisuccinate, 0,75 U/mL of heparin Sulfate, 50 μg/mL of ascorbic acid and 5 % of FBS. In addition, endothelial cell media was boosted with 0,001 % of basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) (0,1 mg/mL).

Viability assay

SH-SY5Y cells (5 × 104 cells/well), Astrocytes (1.2 × 104 cells/well), Pericytes (1.0 × 104 cells/well), or Endothelial cells (1.0 × 104 cells/well) were separately seeded in pretreated 96 wells plates and allowed to attach for 24 h in standard conditions. Then, the medium for each cell culture was replaced by its fresh medium containing the treatments at different concentrations and allowed to incubate for 24 h. After this time, the medium was replaced by fresh medium containing MTS (CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay, Promega G3582) and incubated by 2 h. Subsequently, the absorbance of the culture medium at 490 nm was recorded using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite M200 PRO) and the cell viability was calculated compared to a non-treatment control as live control.

Design and fabrication of microchips

The methodology was reported by Palma-Florez et al. [41]. Microdevices were made of PDMS (Sylgard 184, Dow Corning) from masters fabricated over a silicon wafer with the photoresist SU-8 (GM1075, Gersteltec Sarl) using standard lithography and soft lithography techniques. The PDMS replicas will be obtained by mixing a base/crosslinking agent in a ratio 10:1. Then, the mix will be poured into the mold and cured for at least 2 h at 65 °C. Afterwards, every device will be cut and carefully drilled to carve the central chamber using a biopsy punch (Ø = 1 mm), and the side wells will be carve using a punch of Ø = 5 mm. Finally, the PDMS microchip will be bonded with a glass slide by a plasma treatment for 30 s at high mode (10.5 W) and left at 85 °C overnight.

Cell seeding protocol into the BBB-oC

As depicted in Fig. 1, the protocol of seeding was described by Palma-Florez et al. [41]. Pericytes (40,000 cells) and astrocytes (40,000 cells) in their corresponding cell media were mixed, centrifuged at 1.000 g for 5 min and the pellet was maintained at 4 °C. Next, a fibrin hydrogel solution as follows: a fibrinogen solution 3 mg/mL in PBS was filtered (0,22 μm), and 50 μL of this solution was used to resuspend and homogenize the cell pellet. Then, the mix was supplemented with 1 μL of thrombin 100 U/mL in PBS (2 U/mL final concentration), quickly homogenized and immediately the mixture was injected into the central chamber of the chip and the microdevice was incubated for 15 min at 37 °C to allow the polymerization of the hydrogel. After time, both lateral channels of the chip were supplied with a mixture of Endothelial medium (EM) and astrocyte medium (AM) in a 1:1 ratio (EM/AM medium). Chips were maintained in an incubator at 37 °C and 5 % pCO2. After two days, one of the lateral channels was coated with collagen Type I from rat tail (25 μg/mL) in PBS for 1 h at 37 °C and 5 % pCO2. Endothelial cells were centrifuged and resuspended in 30 μL of EM/AM medium and injected into the collagen-coated channel. Subsequently, chips were flipped and incubated for 1.5 h at 37 °C and 5 % pCO2 to allow the attachment of the cells to the hydrogel wall by gravity. Finally, non-adhered cells were removed, and EM/AM medium was added in both channels of the microdevices and maintained in the incubator at 37 °C and 5 % pCO2 for 5 days before conducting any experiment. Cell medium was changed daily to avoid evaporation.

Preparation of Aβ samples for aggregation assays

Aβ42 was purchased from r-Peptide (GA 30622, USA). It is necessary to carry out the studies with the Aβ peptide in its monomeric stage to avoid the presence of small aggregates that could influence the results. Aliquots of β-amyloid were treated with 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFIP) (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min to produce the monomeric Aβ form [27]. These samples were aliquoted, lyophilized, and stored at −20 °C.

Aβ42 aggregation assay into the BBB-oC

On day 7 of the BBB-oC, one aliquot of Aβ42 was resuspended in PBS to generate a stock solution, diluted in EM/AM medium to obtain the desired concentration of 5 μM of Aβ42, and injected into the brain channel (which is in contact with the hydrogel zone), mimicking the disease environment. On the other hand, 0.2 nM of each gold nanosystem was injected into the blood channel (barrier zone). The BBB-oC were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C and 5 % CO2.

Live/dead assay

The cytotoxic effect of gold nanosystems was analyzed on the cells in the BBB-oC microdevice using the live/dead assay. The EM/AM medium in each microdevice was replaced, and the cells stained with ethidium homodimer-1 (1:500) and calcein-AM (1:1000) solutions. BBB-oC microdevice was incubated for 1 h at RT, washed twice, and nuclei were labeled with Hoechst (1:1000) for 15 min at RT. Finally, BBB-oC microdevice was washed twice, and the images were captured in the barrier and hydrogel zone at 10x using a fluorescent microscopy (IX71, Olympus). The image processing was performed using Fiji/ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Permeability assay

To evaluate the permeability of GNP into the BBB-oC, GNPr-PEG-D3 and GNPr-PEG were functionalized with Alexa Fluor 488 Hydrazide (Thermo Fisher Scientific) through the EDC-NHS chemistry (as was detailed in the section functionalization with peptide D3) while GNS-PEG-D3 and GNS-PEG were functionalized with Alexa Fluor 647 Hydrazide (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Before using, GNP were washed three times in milli-Q water to eliminate the excess of fluorophore. Finally, the different GNP stocks were diluted in EM/AM medium and injected into the blood channel.

To evaluate the permeability after treatment for 24 h in the BBB-oC, cells were washed with PBS to remove cell debris, and a fluorescent solution of 100 μM FITC-Dextran 70 kDa (Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in EM/AM medium and injected into the blood channel.

For both permeability assays, images were captured at different times using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti2). The fluorescence in the hydrogel zone was measured and used to calculate the permeability coefficient (P coef). Images were treated by the Fiji/ImageJ® software (NIH, USA) and the following equation [109]:P=(1(ICht1−ITt1)∗(ITt2−ITt1Δt)∗VTAcm/sWhere ICht1 and ITt1 are the fluorescence intensity at the initial time in the blood channel and brain chamber, respectively (u.a.). ITt2 is intensity in the brain chamber at final time (A.U.), Δt is the difference between the initial and the final time (sec). VT is the volume of the brain chamber (cm3) and A is the surface area of the endothelial wall (cm2) that are 1.28 × 10−3 cm3 and 0,078 cm2, respectively.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

To evaluate the TJs and AJs in the BBB-oC, cells were fixed to preserve their structural characteristics over time. For all samples, cells in the BBB-oC on day 8 were washed with PBS, fixed in 4 % formaldehyde for 15 min at RT. Fixed cells were rinsed three times with 1.5 mg/mL of glycine in PBS (PBS-gly) and permeabilized using 0.1 % TritonX-100 in PBS-gly for 10 min at RT. After that, the cells were rinsed three times with 0.1 % TritonX-100 in PBS (PBS-T) and blocked in 3 % BSA in PBS-T for 60 min at RT. Then, cells were incubated with primary antibodies (Table S3) in 3 % BSA in PBS-T overnight at 4 °C. Next day, cells were washed three times with PBS-T and subsequently incubated with the secondary antibody for 2 h at RT. Then, the cells were washed with PBS-T and nuclei were labeled with Hoechst for 15 min at RT. Finally, the cells were rinsed three times with PBS and the microdevices were inspected by confocal microscopy at 100x (Leica STELLARIS Confocal Microscope). The image processing was performed using Fiji/ImageJ software (NIH, USA). The list of antibodies used for immunostaining are shown in Table S3.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

On day 8, the RNA of the BBB-oC was isolated for each treatment using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). The concentration and purity were determined by Nanodrop 2000. The reverse transcription reaction was using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (BioRad) following the manufacturer instructions and carried out into the thermal cycler (T100 BioRad). The thermal cycling program for RT-qPCR reaction consisted of hot start, initial denaturation, denaturation, annealing, and elongation using the PowerUp SyBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cycle was repeated 40 times. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are shown in Table S4.

Western blot

The supernatant samples from the BBB-oC were collected, mixed with 4 × Laemmli buffer (LB), and 50 μL of the resulting solution were loaded for Western blot analysis. Proteins were separated using the Mini-PROTEAN TetraCell system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) on 1.0-mm, 4–15 % glycerol-polyacrylamide gels at 80 V. Electrophoresed proteins were then transferred to 0.22-μm nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad) at 100 V for 80 min at 4 °C. Membranes were washed twice for 5 min with TBS (Bio-Rad) containing 0.1 % Tween-20 (TBS-T) and blocked for 1 h at 4 °C in 3 % BSA (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.1 % TBS-T. Primary antibody 6E10 (BioLegend) (1:1000 in 3 % BSA, 0.1 % TBS-T) was incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, membranes were washed three times for 10 min each with TBS-T, and then incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology) (1:3000 in 3 % BSA, 0.1 % TBS-T). Detection was performed using Clarity Western ECL substrate (Bio-Rad). Finally, membranes were washed three times for 5 min with 3 % BSA in 0.1 % TBS-T before imaging.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained were expressed as mean ± SD. All the analyses were performed by using one-way Anova to compare the different conditions or groups followed by a Tukey post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. P value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
