Trends and inequalities in early breastfeeding initiation in Ghana
Authors: Khadijat Adeleye, Augustus Osborne, Umaru Sesay, Bright Opoku Ahinkorah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly breastfeeding initiation is a critical practice for improving neonatal health outcomes, yet little is known about its inequalities. This study examines trends and inequalities in early breastfeeding initiation among women in Ghana.MethodsWe employed an ecological time-trends study design among women of reproductive age, using six waves data from the Ghana Demographic and Health Surveys conducted in 1993, 1998, 2003, 2008, 2014, and 2022. The World Health Organization’s Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT) was used to compute inequality measures difference (D), prevalence ratio (PR), population attributable fraction (PAF), and population attributable risk (PAR) across six stratifiers: maternal age, child’s sex, household wealth, maternal education, place of residence, and subnational regions.ResultsNationally, the prevalence of early breastfeeding initiation rose significantly from 15.2% in 1993 to 58.1% in 2022. All subgroups recorded improvements, with marginalized populations showing the largest gains. For instance, women in the lowest wealth quintile increased from 13.4% in 1993 to 60.2% in 2022, compared with women in the highest quintile who increased from 16.5% to 49.7% within the same period. Regionally, Bono East recorded the highest early breastfeeding initiation prevalence in 2022 (70.3%), while Greater Accra had the lowest (45%). Inequality assessments showed mixed patterns: wealth- and education-related disparities narrowed over time, with a 2022 prevalence ratio of 0.9, suggesting disadvantaged groups were more likely to adopt early breastfeeding initiation. Similarly, place of residence and child’s sex also narrowed overtime. However, age-related inequalities widened. Regional inequalities also persisted, with a prevalence ratio of 1.6 in 2022, indicating women in underserved regions were still more likely to practice early breastfeeding initiation compared with women in more advanced regions.ConclusionEarly breastfeeding initiation rates in Ghana have improved steadily over the past three decades, yet socioeconomic and regional disparities remain. Targeted strategies are needed to sustain gains and close gaps, including policy reforms to expand maternity leave and workplace breastfeeding support, as well as community-based initiatives such as awareness campaigns to promote equitable breastfeeding practices. Addressing these inequities will be vital for advancing maternal and child health outcomes in Ghana.

Background

The practice of early breastfeeding initiation (EIBF) is endorsed globally due to its critical role in enhancing maternal and infant health. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that breastfeeding commence within the first hour after birth. This practice improves infant survival rates and promotes maternal well-being by fostering a close bond between mother and child [1]. In Ghana, early breastfeeding initiation rates have fluctuated over the years [2–4], impacting healthcare policy development. This also correlate with changes in morbidity and mortality, and changes in breastfeeding practices reflecting evolving cultural norms and attitudes towards infant feeding. Despite its recognized benefits, significant disparities exist in the rates of early breastfeeding initiation across different socio-economic and cultural contexts.

In Ghana, the significance of early breastfeeding initiation resonates profoundly, considering the country’s unique socio-cultural dynamics and historical context regarding maternal and child health. Ghana’s population totals approximately 32 million, characterized by diverse ethnic groups, each with distinct cultural beliefs and practices affecting breastfeeding behaviors [5]. To enhance early breastfeeding initiation practice, the Government of Ghana, with support from partners, has implemented several breastfeeding policies and initiatives. These policies and initiatives include: the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI), the Ghana Breastfeeding Promotion Regulation 2000, and the National Breastfeeding Week celebrations [1]. Despite these efforts, challenges remain in translating policies into practice and addressing the socio-economic factors that influence breastfeeding behaviors. Consequently, these challenges have contributed to the suboptimal early breastfeeding initiation, with significant disparities across different population groups. Studies have identified several factors influencing EIBF in Ghana: Maternal education [6], partner support, exposure to breastfeeding information during pregnancy [7], and assisted delivery [2]. Studies conducted across other sub-Saharan African countries have also demonstrated disparities in early breastfeeding initiation regarding socioeconomic quintiles [8, 9], and place of residence (rural and urban populations) in early breastfeeding initiation [4, 10, 11], disproportionately affecting the marginalized population. Although these findings are crucial in providing evidence-based information on the patterns of early breastfeeding initiation inequalities to inform policy, it is therefore critical for a study to examine these disparities in the context of Ghana comprehensively.

Several compelling reasons exist for conducting a new study on early breastfeeding initiation inequalities in Ghana. Previous studies have focused on specific factors, focusing on the determinants and trends of early breastfeeding initiation [2–4, 10]. For instance, the study by Apanga et al. [2–4, 10] examined the percentage and predictors of early breastfeeding initiation in Ghana over two years; Mohamed et al. examined trends and determinants of breastfeeding among children aged 0–23 months between 2003 and 2017 [2–4, 10]. Furthermore, Tawiah-Agyemang examines barriers and facilitators to early breastfeeding initiation in Ghana [12] and Haile et al. reported findings on the moderating effect of urban versus rural residence based on the relationship between attendant and early breastfeeding initiation [2–4, 10]. While these findings provided valuable insights into early breastfeeding initiation in Ghana, information on inequalities is lacking, which is critical for evaluating public health interventions. This study was conducted to address this knowledge gap by providing a nuanced understanding of inequalities of early breastfeeding initiation over three decades. The temporal scope of 1993–2022 was chosen to capture trends over three decades marked by the introduction and evolution of major breastfeeding initiatives (e.g., Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative), and growing attention to socioeconomic disparities in maternal and child health. Ultimately, this study will provide insights into the long-term patterns and changes of inequalities of early breastfeeding initiation in Ghana resulting in a more holistic view between various socio-demographic, and health system factors influencing early breastfeeding initiation in Ghana.

Methods

Study design, period, and data source

This study employed an ecological time-trends study design among women of reproductive age (15–49 years), using six waves of data from the Ghana Demographic and Health Surveys conducted between 1993 and 2022. The Ghana Demographic and Health Surveys (GDHS) are nationally representative cross-sectional surveys conducted periodically to collect data on various health indicators, including maternal and child health [13]. These surveys employ a multi-stage cluster sampling methodology, where the country is divided into primary sampling units (PSUs). A subset of PSUs is randomly selected, followed by the selection of secondary sampling units (SSUs) within each PSU, and finally, households are randomly sampled from the selected SSUs [13]. Table 1 illustrates the details of the sampling units, clusters, and sample size used during survey administration. Trained interviewers conducted in-person interviews with women of reproductive age and their households, collecting data on demographic characteristics, reproductive health, child health, and family planning. The data cleaning process for each survey involved excluding incomplete records, conducting consistency checks to ensure valid responses, and applying specific criteria to arrive at the final sample used for analysis. This study accessed data through the World Health Organization’s Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT), an online platform designed to assess, analyze, and document health disparities [14]. HEAT provides access to selected datasets relevant to health equity assessments, enabling the use of disaggregated GDHS data to explore trends and inequalities in early initiation of breastfeeding among women in Ghana.

Outcome measure and dimensions of inequality

The main outcome measure for this study was early breastfeeding initiation, defined as the proportion of women who initiated breastfeeding within the first hour of childbirth. Data on breastfeeding initiation were collected through maternal recall during survey interviews. To explore disparities in early breastfeeding initiation, the study examined several dimensions of inequality, including maternal age, economic status, maternal education, place of residence, child’s gender, and subnational regions. The selection of variables for this study was primarily guided by the availability of data in the WHO HEAT database. While a specific framework could enhance the justification for our choices, the constraints of the dataset limited our options.

Maternal Age: Categorized into two groups: 15–19 years and 20–49 years.

Economic Status: Determined using wealth quintiles, which classified households into five groups (lowest, poorer, middle, richer, and wealthiest) based on household asset indices such as ownership of real estate, livestock, and durable goods. The “poorest” (lowest quintile) and “wealthiest” (highest quintile) groups do not correspond to specific income thresholds (e.g., $1 or $1,000,000) but rather represent the bottom 20% and top 20% of households within the study population according to their relative asset scores. Thus, “poorest” refers to households with the lowest asset index scores, while “wealthiest” refers to those with the highest asset index scores.

Maternal Education: Grouped into three categories: no education, primary education, and secondary or higher education.

Place of Residence: Classified as urban or rural.

Child’s Gender: Recorded as male or female. We defined the variable ‘child’ to include individuals aged 0 to 24 months.

Subnational Regions: Reflecting Ghana’s administrative boundaries, which have undergone changes over time due to administrative reforms aimed at addressing demographic and governance needs.

Data management

The datasets were harmonized by standardizing variables across the different survey years to ensure consistency in the analysis. Missing data were handled using appropriate imputation techniques where feasible, and we also conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of missing data on our findings. The samples were weighed to account for the complex survey design and ensure that the results are representative of the population.

Data analysis

The online version of the World Health Organization’s Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT) was used to analyze disparities in early breastfeeding initiation among women in Ghana [14]. HEAT is a software tool designed to facilitate the analysis of health inequality data. It includes indicators of interest categorized by six dimensions of inequality, enabling a detailed examination of early breastfeeding initiation. The software provides estimates, confidence intervals, and summary metrics of inequality, allowing for a comprehensive assessment of disparities and informed conclusions based on the data.

Four key metrics were used to assess inequality: Difference (D), Prevalence ratio (PR), Population Attributable Fraction (PAF), and Population Attributable Risk (PAR). These metrics provide insights into both absolute and relative disparities:

The prevalence ratio is calculated as the prevalence in the most advantaged group divided by the prevalence in the most disadvantaged group and is commonly used in epidemiological studies to compare the likelihood of an outcome between groups. A prevalence ratio greater than 1 indicates a higher prevalence in the advantaged group, while a PR less than 1 indicates a higher prevalence in the disadvantaged group.Population Attributable Fraction (PAF): Indicates the percentage of the total health impact (in this case, early breastfeeding initiation) that it would be eliminated if the inequality factor were removed.Population Attributable Risk (PAR): Measures the proportion of early breastfeeding initiation within the population that is associated with a specific inequality factor.

Metrics D and PAR serve as absolute measures, offering precise values that indicate the exact difference in breastfeeding initiation, or the proportion of health outcomes linked to a specific risk factor. On the other hand, prevalence ratio and PAF are relative measures that contextualize disparities within the broader population dynamics, allowing for comparisons across different factors. This distinction is important, as absolute measures such as D and PAR provide clear insights into the magnitude of inequality, while relative measures like prevalence ratio and PAF highlight the disparities in relation to overall population trends. By combining both absolute and relative metrics, this study ensures a comprehensive understanding of the inequalities in early breastfeeding initiation. The World Health Organization emphasizes the importance of presenting summary metrics in both absolute and relative formats to support the development of policy-relevant conclusions. Complex metrics, such as those provided by HEAT, evaluate the significance of inequalities within specific population subgroups rather than relying on basic measurements. Detailed explanations of the calculations and applications of these metrics are available in the relevant literature, providing further guidance on their interpretation and use in health equity assessments [15, 16].

Results

Trends in early breastfeeding initiation among women in Ghana, 1993–2022

Between 1993 and 2022, the percentage of early breastfeeding initiation in Ghana increased by 42.9% points, from 15.2 to 58.1%. This increase could be attributed to government initiatives implemented over the years to enhance early breastfeeding initiation in the country (Fig. 1).

Age groups

Among women aged 15–19 years, the percentage of early breastfeeding initiation rose steadily from 14.4% in 1993 to 55.6% in 2022, reflecting considerable progress in breastfeeding practices among younger mothers. Similarly, women aged 20–49 years experienced a substantial increase in percentage, from 15.7% in 1993 to 58.5% in 2022, indicating the widespread adoption of early breastfeeding initiation in this age group.

Economic status (quintiles)

In terms of wealth quintiles, the poorest women (Quintile 1) consistently demonstrated higher early breastfeeding initiation rates compared to other wealth groups, with the percentage increasing from 13.4% in 1993 to 60.2% in 2022. Women in the richest quintile (Quintile 5), however, showed slower progress, with rates rising from 16.5% in 1993 to 49.7% in 2022. Middle-income groups (Quintiles 2-4) exhibited varying trends, with Quintile 2 achieving the highest percentage of 62.8% in 2022, while Quintile 4 saw an increase from 20.0% in 1993 to 56.1% in 2022. These trends highlight socioeconomic disparities, with poorer women adopting early breastfeeding initiation at higher rates over time.

Education level

Educational attainment also played a significant role in early breastfeeding initiation trends. Women with no formal education had the highest percentage, increasing from 14.9% in 1993 to 61.3% in 2022. Those with primary education saw a rise from 16.1% in 1993 to 57.3% in 2022, while secondary-educated women experienced an increase from 17.8% in 1993 to 57.8% in 2022. Women with higher education showed a decrease in trends, with rates declining from 55.4% in 2008 to 54.5% in 2022. This suggests that traditional practices and community-based interventions may have had a more substantial impact on women with lower levels of education.

Residence

The rural-urban divide in early breastfeeding initiation percentage remained evident throughout the period. Rural women consistently had higher rates, with the percentage increasing from 14.7% in 1993 to 59.5% in 2022. Urban women also showed progress, with rates rising from 17.7% in 1993 to 56.7% in 2022, but they remained slightly behind their rural counterparts.

Sex of the child

The sex of the child did not significantly influence early breastfeeding initiation trends, as the percentage for female children increased from 17.1% in 1993 to 58.8% in 2022, while for male children, it rose from 14.1% in 1993 to 57.5% in 2022. This indicates that breastfeeding practices were applied consistently regardless of the child’s gender.

Region

Regional trends revealed significant variations in early breastfeeding initiation percentage across Ghana. In 1993, regions like Upper East (25.5%) and Central (30.7%) had higher rates compared to others, such as Northern (4.8%) and Volta (11.5%). By 2022, regions such as Bono East (70.3%), Volta (67.4%), and Upper East (66.3%) had the highest percentage, while Greater Accra (45.0%), Ahafo (44.5%), and Eastern (46.4%) lagged. Regions like Ashanti and Northern showed significant improvements, with Ashanti increasing from 12.5% in 1993 to 62.0% in 2022, and Northern from 4.8% in 1993 to 56.0% in 2022 (Table 2).

Age (2 groups: 15–19, and 20–49 Years)

Between 1993 and 2022, inequality in early breastfeeding initiation by age remained low and stable. In 1993, the difference (D) was 1.3, indicating a negligible disparity between the two age groups (15–19 and 20–49 years). Over the years, the difference fluctuated slightly, peaking at 9.7 in 2003 before declining and stabilizing at 2.9 in 2022. The prevalence ratio of women between the ages of 20–49 years compared with those aged 15–19 years remained constant at 1.1 between 1993 and 2022, suggesting minimal relative inequality between age groups. The population-attributable fraction (PAF) and population-attributable risk (PAR) values were consistently low, indicating that age did not significantly contribute to inequality in breastfeeding practices.

Economic status (wealth quintile)

Inequality by economic status showed significant variation over time. In 1993, the difference (D) was 3.1, indicating modest disparities among wealth quintiles. This inequality peaked in 1998 with a D of 19.5, highlighting a more significant economic disparity in breastfeeding practices. However, by 2022, the difference had reversed to -10.5, suggesting that poorer women were more likely to initiate breastfeeding early than wealthier women. Similar patterns were observed in the economic status, with the prevalence ratio of wealthier women compared with those poorest quintiles decreasing from 1.2 in 1993 to 0.8 in 2022. The PAF and PAR values, higher in earlier years, suggest that economic disparities were more pronounced in the late 1990s but diminished significantly over time.

Education (4 groups)

Educational disparities in breastfeeding practices were moderate but fluctuated over the years. In 1993, the difference (D) was 3.4, indicating low inequality among women with different levels of education. This disparity increased in 1998 (D = 19.5) but gradually declined to -6.8 in 2022, suggesting that women with lower education levels were more likely to initiate breastfeeding early by the end of the period. The prevalence ratio between women with higher education compared with those with no education remained close to 1.0 throughout, indicating that relative inequality by education was minimal. The PAF and PAR values were consistently low, showing that education had a limited impact on overall inequality in breastfeeding practices.

Place of residence (urban vs. rural)

Disparities between urban and rural areas were evident but declined over time. In 1993, the difference (D) was 3.0, reflecting slight inequality, which increased to 18.1 in 1998 before decreasing to -2.8 in 2022. The negative value in 2022 suggests that rural women were more likely to practice early breastfeeding initiation than their urban counterparts. The prevalence ratio between women who resided in urban areas compared with those in rural areas remained close to 1.0 throughout, indicating minimal relative inequality between urban and rural areas. The PAF and PAR values, which were higher in earlier years, suggest that rural-urban disparities were more pronounced in the 1990s but became negligible by 2022.

Sex (male vs. female children)

Inequality in breastfeeding practices based on the sex of the child was minimal throughout the period. In 1993, the difference (D) was − 3.0, indicating a slight bias favoring female children, but this difference diminished over time, reaching − 1.3 in 2022. The prevalence ratio between male children compared with female children remained close to 1.0 across all years, showing no significant relative inequality between male and female children. The PAF and PAR values were consistently negligible, indicating that the sex of the child did not drive inequality in the early breastfeeding initiation.

Subnational regions

Regional disparities in breastfeeding practices were the most pronounced and persistent throughout the study period. In 1993, the difference (D) was 25.9, reflecting substantial inequality across regions, and this increased dramatically to 72.4 in 2003. Although the disparity decreased slightly in subsequent years, it remained high at 25.8 in 2022. The prevalence ratio between the Greater Accra region and other regions followed a similar trend, peaking at 6.4 in 1993 and stabilizing at 1.6 in 2022. The PAF and PAR values were consistently high, particularly in earlier years, indicating that regional differences contributed to inequality in breastfeeding practices. Despite some improvements, regional disparities remain a significant challenge.

Discussion

Our study on the trends and inequalities of early breastfeeding initiation among Ghanaian breastfeeding women highlights significant trends and disparities in early breastfeeding initiation practices across various demographic factors, including age, education, socio-economic status, child gender, and geographical location.​ These trends can be understood within several key national health policy interventions implemented during the study period. Ghana’s adoption of the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative from 1995 to date [17]), the nationwide expansion of Community-Based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) from 2000 onward ( [18]), and the implementation of the National Newborn Strategy and Action Plan (2014–2018) since 2018 to date ( [19]) all contributed to improving EIBF rates. The Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) strategy introduced in 1999 to date was particularly significant, including breastfeeding promotion components, and was progressively scaled up in rural areas [20]. These policy initiatives, emphasizing community-based approaches and focusing on underserved populations, help explain the differential improvement observed across demographic groups. Our findings indicate minimal age-related disparities in early breastfeeding initiation, with younger and older mothers exhibiting similar improvements in their breastfeeding practices over time.​ While this trend is encouraging, other studies conducted within sub-Saharan Africa have revealed varying degrees of disparity in breastfeeding initiation rates among different maternal age groups. For instance, a study in Ghana reported that older maternal age was associated with a longer time to initiate breastfeeding, highlighting potential barriers younger mothers face in the early hours after delivery [3]. Similarly, research in Tanzania [21], Morocco [22], and Nigeria [23] have indicated that socio-economic factors and limited access to healthcare services contribute significantly to disparities in breastfeeding practices between young and older mothers. These studies emphasized the importance of addressing the challenges younger mothers face to ensure they receive adequate support for early breastfeeding initiation. Hence, by understanding these contextual factors and the nuances of maternal age, health programs can be tailored to support young mothers better, thereby enhancing EIBF rates across all demographics.

Our findings indicate a notable increase in early breastfeeding initiation among less-educated women in Ghana, while more highly educated women exhibited stagnant progress.​ Existing literature supports the notion that maternal education strongly influences early breastfeeding initiation [26, 27]. For instance, a cross-sectional study across multiple sub-Saharan countries showed that mothers who had completed primary education were significantly more likely to initiate breastfeeding than those without. However, similar studies noted that mothers with secondary and higher education did not statistically differ from uneducated mothers regarding their early breastfeeding initiation rates, highlighting a divergence in findings related to educational impact [28]. This discrepancy highlights the need for targeted interventions considering educational attainment and cultural beliefs to enhance breastfeeding initiation rates across all academic backgrounds.

Our findings revealed that poorer women exhibited higher early breastfeeding initiation rates than their wealthier counterparts. In particular, the percentage among poorer women increased from 13.4% in 1993 to 60.2% in 2022, contrasting with the wealthiest group, whose rates only rose from 16.5 to 49.7%. This trend is notable as it challenges previous studies that suggested wealthier women typically have better access to healthcare and breastfeeding support, leading to higher initiation rates [2, 29]. This wealth-based disparity’s narrowing and eventual reversal stems from several factors: First, targeted community-based interventions may have effectively reached poorer communities through grassroot health workers and peer support networks. Second, traditional breastfeeding practices may be more culturally embedded in lower-income neighborhoods. In contrast, wealthier women might face different barriers, including earlier return to formal employment and greater exposure to formula marketing. Third, the differential rate of improvement suggests policy interventions may have failed to address specific obstacles facing wealthier women, including potentially inadequate maternity leave protections in formal sector employment. Fourth, this finding can be attributed to the limited financial resources of poorer women, making breastfeeding a cost-effective way to feed infants, as it eliminates the need to buy formula, which can be prohibitively expensive compared to their wealthier counterparts.

Our findings suggest that child gender did not significantly affect early breastfeeding initiation trends. These findings differ from various studies conducted in different contexts that address gender disparities in breastfeeding. For instance, a multi-country analysis of Demographic and Health Surveys involving women from 24 sub-Saharan African countries revealed higher odds of breastfeeding initiation within one hour for female children than male children [30]. Similarly, a trend analysis in Bangladesh highlighted significant gender inequalities related to EIBF. The study conducted by [31] found that cultural attitudes and maternal education were key factors affecting breastfeeding initiation rates, leading to a historical preference for male children. Research in Vietnam by [32] emphasized the benefits of skin-to-skin contact in promoting early breastfeeding, demonstrating that regardless of a child’s gender, such practices positively influenced early breastfeeding initiation rates. Conversely, a study conducted in Saudi Arabia revealed that the sex of the newborn was not a significant factor associated with early breastfeeding initiation; this reflects a shift towards more egalitarian breastfeeding practices [33]. The evidence of increasingly equitable breastfeeding practices underscores the critical need to foster supportive environments for all new mothers, irrespective of their child’s gender. Efforts to strengthen community support systems, nurture peer support networks, and enhance maternal education about the advantages of breastfeeding can significantly contribute to improving early breastfeeding initiation rates.

The rural-urban divide remains significant, with rural women consistently reporting higher early breastfeeding initiation rates over the study period. This is consistent with previous studies conducted in Ghana [10] and systematic review findings of higher breastfeeding initiatives in rural areas [34], similar to a study conducted in Ethiopia by [11]. However, this pattern challenges the expectation that urbanization leads to improved health behaviors through greater access to healthcare services and information. One explanation is that although urban women may have better access to medical resources, they might also encounter competing factors such as formal employment demands, changing social norms, and greater exposure to infant formula marketing that discourage early breastfeeding. In contrast, rural communities may uphold stronger traditional norms and community-driven support for breastfeeding, reinforcing early initiation practices despite limited formal healthcare access. This suggests that structural and cultural factors, beyond mere access to services, are crucial in sustaining breastfeeding behaviors over time. Furthermore, the limited access to commercial infant formula, exacerbated by the high cost of purchasing and the long distance to accessing this formula, encourages mothers to rely on breastfeeding.

Significant regional variations in early breastfeeding initiation across Ghana further underscore the complexity of breastfeeding initiation practices. These findings resonate with existing literature emphasizing the importance of localized health programs and interventions to accommodate regional cultural practices [9, 10]. The impressive increase in regions like Ashanti and Northern suggests that targeted interventions may effectively address regional disparities in breastfeeding practices. This echoes findings from studies in other countries where regional health initiatives improved the breastfeeding rate.

Policy and practice implications

The study emphasizes the critical need for policymakers to develop targeted educational programs that address the unique challenges different demographic groups face. Given that wealthier urban women in Ghana show lower early breastfeeding initiation rates despite better healthcare access, we recommend expanding and promoting peer-led support groups, especially in urban communities, to provide practical guidance and emotional encouragement for breastfeeding mothers. The government should implement and enforce policies requiring private breastfeeding rooms, and flexible schedules for nursing mothers. The government should also increase statutory maternity leave from the current 3 months to at least 6 months to support continued breastfeeding among working women These recommendations align with Ghana’s Universal Health Coverage Roadmap and directly address the specific socioeconomic and geographical disparities identified in our analysis.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths, including using nationally representative DHS datasets spanning three decades, allowing for robust trend analysis of early breastfeeding initiation practices in Ghana. The application of WHO’s HEAT Plus software enhanced our equity analysis across multiple dimensions. However, critical methodological limitations warrant consideration. First, recall bias may have affected the self-reported breastfeeding data, particularly for mothers interviewed several months after childbirth. Second, the cross-sectional nature of the surveys prevents establishing causal relationships between observed factors and early breastfeeding initiation practices. Third, changes in DHS sampling methodology across survey rounds may have introduced comparability challenges despite our standardization efforts. Fourth, our analysis was constrained by the variables available in the DHS datasets, potentially omitting important unmeasured confounders such as hospital protocols, cultural beliefs, and family influence on breastfeeding decisions. Fifth, while we analyzed regional disparities, the administrative boundaries in Ghana changed during the study period, potentially affecting geographical comparisons. Finally, this study acknowledges that the place of birth and the type of facility are essential factors influencing early breastfeeding initiation. However, due to the aggregated nature of the WHO HEAT Equity dataset, we could not analyze these variables, which may limit the comprehensiveness of our findings. Future research should address these factors for a more thorough understanding.

Conclusion

The analysis of early breastfeeding initiation trends in Ghana demonstrates remarkable progress over three decades, particularly among disadvantaged populations, yet persistent inequalities demand targeted policy action. Our findings challenge conventional assumptions by revealing higher early breastfeeding initiation rates among less educated and poorer women while highlighting gaps in urban areas and among formally employed mothers. These results underscore the urgency for policymakers to: (1) Strengthen the Ghana Health Service’s capacity to deliver specialized breastfeeding support in urban facilities; (2) Expand community-based education program through peer support groups where experienced mothers can share their breastfeeding experiences and provide encouragement, enhance training for healthcare providers on breastfeeding promotion and support, especially in urban regions. (3) Develop a comprehensive prenatal curriculum and ensure prenatal classes offered in hospitals or community centers include extensive content on the importance of breastfeeding, and advocating for maternity leave policies that support breastfeeding, including flexible work hours and a designated breastfeeding period (encompassing exclusive and continued) at least six weeks is recommended. By implementing these evidence-based recommendations and systematically monitoring equity dimensions in early breastfeeding initiation practices, Ghana can build on its progress, ensure that all mothers and newborns benefit equally from early breastfeeding initiation, and advance a more equity-sensitive maternal and child health agenda.
