Pharmacovigilance evaluation of somatrogon-associated adverse events in real-world databases
Authors: Mengyuan Cai, Qian Guo, Huqiang Dong, Yabo Wang, Zhongyu Han, Ping Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSomatrogon, a long-acting recombinant human growth hormone, is used in children with growth hormone deficiency. While clinical trials have shown its safety, real-world post-marketing data are limited, and uncommon or delayed adverse events (AEs) may not be fully recognized.MethodsWe analyzed somatrogon-related AEs from the US FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) (Q1 2004–Q2 2025) and the WHO global safety database via VigiAccess (up to Dec 2024). Disproportionality analyses were performed using four signal detection methods: reporting odds ratio, proportional reporting ratio, Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN), and multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS). AEs were assessed at both system organ class and preferred term (PT) levels. In FAERS, time to onset was examined to characterize the temporal pattern.ResultsWe identified 1,001 somatrogon-related reports in FAERS and 863 in VigiAccess. Most cases occurred in pediatric patients, with slightly more reports in males. At the SOC level, most AEs involved general disorders, administration site conditions, injuries, and product issues. At the PT level, recurring signals included injection-site reactions, device-related problems, and insulin-like growth factor abnormalities. Among 168 FAERS cases with valid onset data, 79.76% occurred within 30 days, with a median onset of 0 days.ConclusionSomatrogon’s real-world AE pattern shows early-onset injection-site reactions, device-related issues, and endocrine signals, highlighting the need for early treatment monitoring and ongoing post-marketing surveillance as its use grows globally.Key pointsThis dual-database pharmacovigilance study analyzed somatrogon-associated AE reports from FAERS and VigiAccess, providing a real-world overview of its post-marketing safety profile.Across both databases, the most consistent signal clusters were injection-site reactions, device- and product-related problems, and endocrine/laboratory abnormalities, particularly insulin-like growth factor disturbances.Among FAERS cases with valid onset data, 79.76% of reported events occurred within 30 days, with a median onset of 0 days, indicating that AEs tend to emerge early after treatment initiation.Serious outcomes were relatively uncommon, supporting the importance of early follow-up, injection-site assessment, device-use training, and IGF-1 monitoring in children receiving somatrogon.

Introduction

Somatrogon is a long-acting recombinant human growth hormone (GH) analog in which the amino acid sequence of human GH is fused to three copies of the C-terminal peptide of human chorionic gonadotropin, prolonging its half-life and allowing once-weekly subcutaneous administration (1, 2). It is approved under the brand name Ngenla for children from 3 years of age with growth failure due to inadequate secretion of endogenous GH and has now been introduced in multiple regions, including Australia, the European Union, and the United States (3, 4). A global phase 3 trial demonstrated non-inferior 12-month height velocity compared with once-daily somatropin, with similar safety and tolerability (5), and a parallel Japanese study reported comparable findings (6). Longer-term extension data suggest that growth responses are sustained over several years without new major safety concerns (7, 8). Patient-reported outcomes also indicate that once-weekly dosing is perceived as less burdensome and may support better adherence in routine practice (9, 10).

Regulatory reviews and clinical reports consistently identify injection-site reactions, such as erythema, pain, pruritus, and swelling, as the most frequent adverse events (AEs) with somatrogon, together with class typical symptoms including headache and pyrexia (11, 12). Experience with long-acting GH formulations has prompted discussion about their distinct pharmacodynamic characteristics, particularly the higher and more sustained insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) exposure observed with weekly dosing, and the potential implications for glucose metabolism, immunogenicity, and local tissue reactions, such as lipoatrophy or nodules (13, 14, 15). Somatrogon also carries the established class warnings associated with daily GH therapy, including risks of intracranial hypertension, slipped capital femoral epiphysis, scoliosis progression, and possible effects in patients with a history of neoplasia or predisposition to glucose intolerance (16). Post-authorization risk management plans further emphasize the need for continued monitoring of neoplasms, type 2 diabetes mellitus, immunogenicity, and medication-use errors related to the weekly dosing schedule, and several observational follow-up studies are in progress (17).

Although these findings outline the expected safety profile of somatrogon, they are based largely on pre-approval trials and early post-marketing cohorts, each involving relatively limited follow-up and selected patient groups. Rare, delayed, or geography-specific AEs may therefore remain under-recognized, particularly as the drug gains wider use across heterogeneous clinical settings. Spontaneous reporting systems provide an opportunity to capture such signals, yet no study to date has systematically evaluated somatrogon using a comparative pharmacovigilance framework across both the US FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) and the WHO global database accessed via VigiAccess. Given the expanding international uptake of somatrogon, the relatively modest number of patients included in clinical trials, and the outstanding questions regarding rare or unexpected events, a structured assessment of its real-world AE reporting is warranted.

Materials and methods

Participants and data mining in the FAERS and VigiAccess databases

This pharmacovigilance study drew on data from both the FAERS and WHO VigiAccess databases to characterize the real-world AE reporting patterns associated with somatrogon. FAERS reports from the first quarter of 2004 through the second quarter of 2025 were downloaded to ensure complete database coverage and to avoid missing early, delayed, or inconsistently coded reports. Data cleaning followed the FDA’s recommended deduplication procedures (18). When multiple entries shared the same case identifier, only the latest version was retained. Reports identifying somatrogon as the primary suspect (PS) drug were included in the analysis. To complement the FAERS findings, data were also retrieved from VigiAccess (https://www.vigiaccess.org), the public interface of the WHO global safety database (19), with access completed in December 2024. Because VigiAccess provides aggregated public outputs rather than individual case identifiers, direct case-level matching between FAERS and VigiAccess was not possible. Therefore, the potential overlap between the two databases could not be completely excluded, and cross-database comparisons were interpreted as supportive evidence of similar reporting patterns rather than as analyses of two fully independent case sets. Available information included age group, sex, reporting geography, reporting year, and AE frequencies at both the system organ class (SOC) and preferred term (PT) levels (20). In both datasets, AE terms were standardized according to the MedDRA terminology to ensure consistent classification (21).

Using these two international reporting systems enabled a broader assessment of somatrogon’s post-marketing safety profile, allowing comparison of the US-based signals with patterns observed globally. All analyses were exploratory and interpreted with careful consideration of the known limitations and reporting biases inherent to spontaneous surveillance systems. Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis

In FAERS, descriptive analyses were performed to outline the clinical characteristics of all somatrogon-related reports. Patient age was grouped into predefined categories, and sex, reporting country, and reporting year were summarized to illustrate the overall distribution of submitted cases. Time to onset was calculated as the interval between the documented start of somatrogon treatment and the reported occurrence of the AE. Reports with incomplete treatment start dates, missing event-onset dates, or implausible temporal sequences, such as events recorded before treatment initiation, were excluded from the time-to-onset analysis. To capture the clinical seriousness of events, outcomes were classified into five mutually exclusive categories: death (DE), life-threatening event (LT), disability (DS), hospitalization (HO), and other serious outcomes (OT). When multiple outcomes were reported for a single case, the most severe was retained according to the hierarchy DE > LT > DS > HO > OT. For VigiAccess, available descriptive variables included age, sex, reporting continent, and reporting year.

Disproportional analysis of a pharmacovigilance study

Disproportionality analyses were carried out to identify potential safety signals associated with somatrogon. Four established pharmacovigilance algorithms were applied: the reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN, expressed as the information component, IC), and multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS, expressed as the empirical Bayes geometric mean, EBGM) (22). Analyses were conducted at both the SOC and PT levels. A signal was considered present when the number of reports was ≥3, PRR ≥ 2 with χ2 ≥ 4, the lower limit of the 95% confidence interval (CI) for ROR > 1, IC025 > 0, or EBGM05 > 2 (23, 24). In accordance with current pharmacovigilance practice, primary emphasis in signal interpretation was prioritized on ROR and IC025. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2).

Results

Descriptive analysis

The FAERS dataset spanning from the first quarter of 2004 to the second quarter of 2025 contained a total of 23,168,942 records. After duplicate removal, 1,001 reports identified somatrogon as the primary suspect drug, encompassing 1,879 AEs.

Demographic information was available for most reports, although many lacked complete age or weight data. Males accounted for 45.75% of cases, and females accounted for 26.27%, with the remainder unspecified. The age distribution reflected the pediatric indication of somatrogon: 61.14% of reports involved patients younger than 18 years, and the median age was 10 years, with an interquartile range of 7–12 years and a range of 2–88 years. Only a small proportion of reports concerned adults. Most submissions were recorded between 2023 and 2025, with 2024 contributing the largest share (52.75%). Physicians were the most frequent reporters (44.26%), followed by consumers (30.47%) and pharmacists (25.27%). To further examine whether reporting patterns differed by reporter type, we summarized the main SOC and PT categories reported by physicians, consumers, and pharmacists. The comparison was descriptive because spontaneous reports are affected by reporting behavior, missing information, and clinical context. Overall, the major reported categories remained broadly consistent across reporter groups, with injection-site reactions, device-related issues, and endocrine or laboratory abnormalities forming the main clusters. Detailed reporter-stratified results are provided in Supplementary Table S6 (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article), which summarizes the major SOC-level findings and selected PT-level signals by reporter type. Geographic patterns could not be reliably assessed due to missing location information. Serious outcomes were uncommon. Hospitalization occurred in 3.10% of cases, life-threatening events occurred in 0.50%, disability occurred in 0.40%, and death occurred in 0.20%. An additional 12.69% were classified as other medically important conditions. Time-to-onset data were available for 168 cases. Among these, 79.76% of AEs occurred within 30 days of treatment initiation, although a small number of delayed events were reported beyond one year. The median onset time was 0 days (IQR: 0–18 days), reflecting many events recorded on the day of administration, with a maximum interval of 677 days. In VigiAccess, 863 somatrogon-related reports were identified. The overall demographic patterns closely mirrored those in FAERS: males were the most frequently reported group, and most cases involved children or adolescents, with very few adult reports. Annual reporting was concentrated in 2023–2024. The main difference was geographical distribution, as VigiAccess captured a broader international profile, with most submissions originating from Europe and Asia. Further details regarding the analysis of these reports are presented in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. S1. Overall, both databases showed consistent reporting characteristics, reflecting the predominantly pediatric use of somatrogon in real-world settings.

Distribution of adverse events at the system organ class (SOC) level

At the SOC level, most somatrogon-related AEs in FAERS fell under general disorders and administration site conditions (54.92%, 1,032 reports), followed by injury, poisoning, and procedural complications (15.49%, 291 reports) and product issues (8.41%, 158 reports), which together constituted the majority of submitted events. Using ROR and IC025 as the primary criteria, three SOCs demonstrated notable disproportionality. General disorders and administration site conditions showed the clearest signal (ROR: 5.77; IC025: 1.54), followed by product issues (ROR: 5.44; IC025: 2.06) and injury, poisoning, and procedural complications (ROR: 1.56; IC025: 0.37). All other SOC categories showed no notable disproportionality, with the lower limit of the 95% CI for ROR values below 1 and IC025 estimates below zero. Several SOCs recorded only a small number of cases, limiting further interpretation. In the VigiAccess database, the SOC distribution of somatrogon-related AEs was broadly similar to that observed in FAERS. General disorders and administration site conditions were again the most frequently reported category (50.62%, 813 reports), followed by injury, poisoning, and procedural complications and several nervous system- and skin-related SOCs (Supplementary Fig. S2). Using the lower limit of the 95% CI for ROR > 1 and IC025 > 0 as the primary criteria, the same three SOCs identified in FAERS also showed disproportional reporting in VigiAccess. All other SOCs fell below the predefined thresholds. Overall, the VigiAccess results were consistent with the FAERS findings, underscoring administration site reactions, injury-related events, and product issues as the main contributors at the SOC level (Supplementary Table S2).

Analysis of preferred terms associated with somatrogon-related adverse events

At the PT level in FAERS, disproportionality signals were largely clustered within the three SOCs already identified at the system level: general disorders and administration site conditions, injury, poisoning, and procedural complications, and product issues. Local injection-site reactions formed the most prominent group. Injection-site pain was the single most frequently reported term (709 cases) and showed marked disproportionality (ROR: 126.84, IC025: 6.03), accompanied by consistent signals for injection-site swelling, erythema, mass, bruising, hemorrhage, pruritus, discomfort, rash, discharge, inflammation, induration, and atrophy. A second major cluster comprised device- and product-related problems. Strong signals were observed for wrong technique in device usage process, incorrect dose administered by device, drug dose omission by device, product communication issue, and multiple device-specific terms, including device mechanical issue, device delivery system issue, device leakage, poor-quality device used, defective device, device occlusion, device physical property issue, and patient–device incompatibility, many with very high ROR and IC025 estimates.

Beyond these predominant domains, several less frequent but clinically notable PTs also met the predefined thresholds. These included lipodystrophy acquired and injection-site atrophy within skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders, skeletal events such as epiphysiolysis and growth retardation in the musculoskeletal SOC, and terms compatible with raised intracranial pressure (intracranial pressure increased and papilledema). Additional signals, such as insulin-like growth factor decreased, increased appetite, and fear of injection, were observed but with relatively small case numbers, warranting cautious interpretation (Table 2; full FAERS PT-level metrics in Supplementary Table S5).

In VigiAccess, PT-level results showed a pattern that closely aligned with the findings from FAERS. Injection-site reactions remained the most prominent cluster, with clear signals for injection-site pain (ROR: 31.86; IC025: 4.39), erythema, swelling, pruritus, mass, bruising, and several related local reactions. Device- and product-related terms also demonstrated notable disproportionality, including incorrect dose administered by device, wrong technique in device usage process, device mechanical issue, device delivery system issue, and patient–device incompatibility, which showed one of the highest values observed (ROR: 435.33). Endocrine- and investigation-related terms again produced the strongest overall signals. Insulin-like growth factor increased (ROR: 824.40), insulin-like growth factor decreased (ROR: 738.10), and insulin-like growth factor abnormal (ROR: 2,970.26) all exceeded every predefined threshold with substantial margins. Additional PTs with meaningful signals included lipoatrophy, lipodystrophy acquired, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, glycosylated hemoglobin increased, and increased appetite.

Overall, the VigiAccess results support the FAERS findings, highlighting three consistent clusters at the PT level: injection-site reactions, device- and product-related issues, and endocrine or metabolic changes (Supplementary Table S3).

Time-to-onset analysis

Time to onset (TTO) was assessed in 168 reports with complete onset information. Most events occurred shortly after treatment initiation: 79.76% were reported within the first 0–30 days, while only a small proportion appeared after 90 days, and very late-onset events (>360 days) were uncommon (<3%). As shown in Fig. 3A, the onset distribution was strongly concentrated in the early treatment period. The cumulative curve (Fig. 3B) demonstrated a steep rise immediately after the first dose, with more than 70% of events occurring within the first month. The median TTO was 0 days, with an interquartile range of 0–18 days and a range of 0–677 days. Weibull distribution testing further supported this early-onset pattern. The shape parameter (β = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.58–0.83) was <1, indicating an early-failure type, while the scale parameter (α = 61.12, 95% CI: 42.62–87.64) reflected moderate variability in onset timing. Overall, these findings suggest that somatrogon-related AEs tend to occur promptly following treatment initiation, with relatively few delayed cases.

Discussion

In this comparative analysis of FAERS and the WHO global safety database, three reproducible clusters dominated the real-world AE reporting for somatrogon: local injection-site reactions, device- and product-related problems, and endocrine or laboratory abnormalities, particularly disturbances in insulin-like growth factor measurements. Most reports involved pediatric patients, and serious outcomes were relatively uncommon. The overall pattern of AEs was highly consistent across both databases, strengthening the reliability of these observations. Among cases with valid onset information, approximately 80% of events occurred within the first month of treatment, and the Weibull β value below 1 supported an early-failure pattern. Overall, these findings indicate that somatrogon-associated AEs in routine practice tend to occur early, are mainly local or technical in origin, and align closely with the safety profile described in clinical trials and regulatory assessments (25).

The prominence of injection-site reactions in both FAERS and VigiAccess is in line with phase 3 trials and post-authorization information for somatrogon, in which local reactions, such as pain, erythema, swelling, and pruritus, were the most frequently reported AEs and usually mild to moderate in severity (26, 27). Local lipoatrophy and lipodystrophy signals observed in our analysis also mirror concerns raised for other long-acting GH formulations, where unpredictable injection-site lipoatrophy has been described and sometimes prompted discontinuation (28). Although only a small number of such cases were captured in spontaneous reports, their appearance across databases underscores the importance of strict site rotation and inspection of injection areas in children receiving weekly GH. The large cluster of device-related problems, including incorrect dose administered by device, wrong technique in device usage process, and various mechanical or delivery-system issues, fits with the broader experience that GH pen devices are prone to user errors, especially during the initial learning phase (29, 30). These findings reinforce current recommendations that initiation of somatrogon should be accompanied by structured training for caregivers and patients, as device design and usability have been shown to influence adherence and safety in pediatric GH therapy (31).

Endocrine and laboratory terms formed the third main signal cluster, with very high disproportionality estimates for insulin-like growth factor abnormalities. This pattern is biologically plausible given the pharmacodynamic profile of somatrogon, which produces higher and more sustained IGF-1 exposure over the weekly dosing interval compared with daily somatropin (13, 32). Clinical trials and extension studies have generally not linked these IGF-1 excursions to clear excesses in serious metabolic events, but they have prompted careful monitoring and individualized dose adjustments to maintain IGF-1 within target ranges. It should be noted that FAERS and VigiAccess record IGF-1-related events as coded AE terms, such as insulin-like growth factor increased, decreased, or abnormal, but do not provide reliable numeric IGF-1 concentrations, assay reference ranges, sampling time points, or dose adjustment information. Therefore, we could not determine the absolute IGF-1 levels or assess whether the reported IGF-1 abnormalities were directly associated with specific clinical AEs at the individual level. These signals should therefore be interpreted as laboratory reporting signals that require clinical correlation and further evaluation in studies with standardized biochemical measurements. In our data, signals compatible with intracranial hypertension and skeletal complications (for example, papilledema, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, and epiphysiolysis) were present but infrequent, echoing long-standing class warnings for GH therapy rather than pointing to a new, somatrogon-specific toxicity (33, 34). Notably, we did not observe marked disproportionality for neoplasm-related or overt diabetes-related terms at the SOC level, despite these outcomes being central components of risk management plans for long-acting GH preparations. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because current post-marketing experience with somatrogon remains relatively short, and spontaneous reporting systems cannot define individual treatment duration or long-term follow-up. Therefore, the absence of strong signals in the present analysis should not be considered evidence of absence of risk. Continued long-term surveillance through prospective cohorts, registries, and post-authorization safety studies is required to evaluate rare or delayed outcomes, including neoplasms and glucose metabolism disorders.

Building on these observations, the real-world reporting patterns also provide insights into how somatrogon is implemented during routine clinical care. The early clustering of events in our time-to-onset analysis suggests that the initial phase of treatment remains the most critical period for clinical monitoring, a finding that is consistent with recent real-world evaluations of long-acting GH therapy (35). In these cohorts, most local and administration-related issues, including insufficient site rotation, partial dose delivery, or early device-handling difficulties, were reported within the first weeks of treatment and tended to diminish as patients and caregivers became more familiar with weekly injections (36). Similar observations have been made in other GH programs, where structured injection-technique review and early follow-up were shown to reduce preventable device-related events and improve overall tolerability (37). These findings are in line with current endocrine practice recommendations, which emphasize the importance of careful site rotation, clear guidance on injection-site inspection, and hands-on device education during the initial treatment period. As real-world experience with weekly GH formulations expands, incorporating early usability review and practical technique assessment into routine clinical visits may help reduce avoidable AEs, improve tolerability, and support safe long-term adherence to somatrogon therapy.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It represents one of the most comprehensive post-marketing evaluations of somatrogon to date, integrating data from both the FAERS and WHO VigiAccess systems to capture regional and global reporting patterns. The use of multiple signal detection algorithms, including ROR, PRR, BCPNN, and MGPS, enhances the robustness of the findings and reduces the likelihood of spurious signal generation. Cross-database consistency further strengthens confidence in the observed signal clusters, particularly those involving endocrine, hepatobiliary, and injection-site events. In addition, the incorporation of time-to-onset analyses provides important insights into the temporal behavior of AEs, highlighting the predominance of early-onset reactions following treatment initiation.

However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. As with all spontaneous reporting data, underreporting, missing information, and reporting bias limit the capacity to infer causality or estimate true incidence rates. Detailed clinical variables, such as comorbid conditions, concomitant therapies, laboratory results, and exact dosing history, were frequently unavailable, restricting interpretation of individual reports. Time-to-onset analyses were constrained by incomplete or inconsistent date information, potentially affecting the precision of temporal patterns. Because FAERS and VigiAccess are spontaneous reporting systems rather than longitudinal follow-up cohorts, individual post-treatment follow-up duration could not be reliably determined. Moreover, because VigiAccess does not provide individual case identifiers in its public interface, potential overlap between FAERS and VigiAccess reports could not be fully excluded. Numeric IGF-1 concentrations and their temporal relationship with specific clinical events were also unavailable, limiting interpretation of IGF-1-related reporting signals. The absence of denominator data precludes the estimation of population-level risk, and confounding by indication, particularly regarding reports with neoplasm-related terms, remains a potential source of bias. Furthermore, adult data remain limited given the predominantly pediatric use of somatrogon in current clinical practice. Future studies linking pharmacovigilance data to prescription or registry databases, as well as long-term observational cohorts, will be essential to further clarify the safety profile of somatrogon and to identify rare or delayed AEs.

Conclusion

In summary, this dual-database pharmacovigilance evaluation provides a broad real-world overview of AEs associated with somatrogon. The predominant signals involve endocrine and laboratory fluctuations, injection-site reactions, and device-related issues, which are consistent with the drug’s pharmacological characteristics and established clinical experience. Early-onset reactions were common, reinforcing the importance of regular biochemical monitoring and close clinical follow-up during the initial treatment period. Hepatobiliary and neoplasm-related signals appear biologically plausible but not indicative of causal toxicity. Continued pharmacovigilance, structured patient education, and large-scale prospective studies will be crucial for refining the long-term safety assessment of somatrogon as its global use expands.
