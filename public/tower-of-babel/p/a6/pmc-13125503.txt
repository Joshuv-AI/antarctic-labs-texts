Temporal synchrony and spatial similarity of interbrain subnetworks predict dyadic social interaction
Authors: Yuqin Li, Senqi Li, Yanggong Li, Xiaoya Pang, Chanlin Yi, Lin Jiang, Dezhong Yao, Wei Wu, Fali Li, Peng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman social behaviors involve complex interactions between individuals, and understanding how interbrain neural activity reflects and predicts these interactions is critical for advancing social cognitive neuroscience. While electroencephalography (EEG) hyperscanning has been widely used to explore interpersonal neural dynamics, most studies focus on pairwise regional coupling, overlooking the brain’s intrinsic network-level organization. Here, we propose a spatiotemporal network analysis framework that combines Bayesian non-negative matrix factorization with EEG source imaging to identify interpretable subnetworks with spatiotemporal information. Applying this framework to dyadic EEG datasets from interactive decision-making tasks identifies eight task-relevant subnetworks, including the default mode network (DMN), somatosensory-motor network (SMN), and visual network (VN). Effective interpersonal coordination was associated with enhanced network-level time-domain interbrain synchrony and spatial-domain inter-subject similarity, and the fusion of these metrics reliably predicted interactive behaviors. Notably, synchrony and similarity involving DMN, VN, and SMN emerge as robust predictors of interactive behaviors, with spatiotemporal coupling most prominent within these subnetworks. These findings reveal spatiotemporal network signatures underlying interpersonal neural synchronization and demonstrate the importance of distributed subnetworks and their temporal and spatial alignment in achieving effective social interactions. This framework provides a useful computational tool for probing the neurobiological basis of social behaviors.Subject terms: Cooperation, Network models

Introduction

In social decision-making, uncertainty about the outcomes of our choices depends on others’ decisions. Successfully navigating such interactions requires information exchange between our brains and the brains of others, enabling continuous, reciprocal updates on partners’ internal states and actions. A paradigmatic example is the iterated ultimatum game (UG), which captures the ongoing and adaptive proposal-response behaviors between partners1. While previous studies have highlighted the importance of integrative processes and dynamic interplay among various subnetworks for decision-making2,3, most have focused on individual brains, overlooking the inherently interactive nature of social cognition. To address this gap, we adopt a multi-brain framework and introduce a network-level approach to capture interbrain neural coupling during social interactive decision-making.

Hyperscanning, particularly using electroencephalography (EEG), enables simultaneous recording of brain activity from multiple individuals4–6, allowing investigation of neural processes during real-time social interaction7,8. By measuring interbrain synchrony (IBS), the extent to which two individuals exhibit coordinated neural fluctuations during interaction can be captured9,10. Importantly, although the behavioral or social events that link the two individuals unfold at relatively low frequencies, they elicit shared cognitive and affective processes operating at faster neural timescales, producing measurable IBS. As a statistical measure of dependence between neural signals, IBS serves as an empirical marker of shared neural dynamics, reflecting coordinated engagement in task-relevant cognitive processes rather than direct neural transmission. IBS is typically stronger when partners share mutual intentions or goals, or when they seek to build emotional closeness and align cognitions to facilitate cooperation11–13. A growing body of hyperscanning research has shown that IBS frequently emerges in regions such as the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), and temporoparietal junction (TPJ), which have been identified as crucial hubs in social cognitive processing14,15. Nevertheless, most EEG hyperscanning studies estimate IBS through pairwise comparisons between homologous or distinct brain regions16,17, an approach that implicitly assumes synchrony arises primarily within isolated regions and neglects the inherently network-organized nature of the brain18. In the context of social interaction, two primary IBS networks have been proposed: the mirror neuron system and the mentalizing system. These systems complement each other, facilitating cooperation, empathy, and synchronization, or counteracting such processes19. Along these lines, mentalizing in more natural social interactions may elicit activity in a broader network than the activity elicited in response to static, isolated stimuli. Especially, social interactive decision-making involves mutual prediction, coordination, social comparison, and shared attention processes20, making the information processing complex and likely engaging multiple interacting brain networks in both individuals. Consequently, examining synchrony solely in a pairwise, region-by-region manner may not fully capture the complexity of interbrain dynamics, potentially impeding the extraction of detailed network-level information.

A growing consensus suggests that social behaviors rely on common neurocognitive processes engaging large-scale distributed brain networks21,22. Analyzing integration among these subnetworks enables investigation of brain information processing and adaptive cognitive adjustments at a high hierarchical level23,24. Although functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has been widely used to study large-scale networks due to its high spatial resolution, it lacks the temporal precision to capture rapid cognitive dynamics. To address this, recent studies have combined EEG inverse solutions with spatial independent component analysis (sICA) to derive large-scale networks from scalp EEG. For example, Sockeel et al. successfully identified spatial maps of resting-state networks25, and Li et al. demonstrated their potential for cognitive processing by linking P300 variability to large-scale network dynamics26. However, traditional methods like ICA may generate negative components that violate the physiologically positive EEG source activations in the brain. Bayesian non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) has been proposed as a more physiologically plausible and noise-resilient alternative, producing subnetworks with superior spatial distributions and positive activations3. Despite these advances, fully capturing the neural basis of social interaction requires shifting from single-brain to dual-brain large-scale network analyses that capture fine-grained spatiotemporal dynamics underlying interpersonal coordination. In essence, intrinsic coupling constitutes a key feature of ongoing brain activity, which exhibits rich spatiotemporal patterning and contains information that influences cognitive processing27,28. We propose that interbrain coupling may extend beyond temporal synchrony, with spatial and spatiotemporal interactions jointly supporting interpersonal coordination, and that examining network-level couplings across spatiotemporal domains may reveal interbrain dynamics overlooked by conventional EEG hyperscanning analyses.

To address these issues, we introduce a computational framework combining Bayesian non-negative matrix factorization with EEG source imaging to extract interpretable subnetworks with spatiotemporal information. We apply this to dyadic EEG data from an iterated UG. Based on prior research29, we hypothesize that dyadic decision-making engages multiple structured, non-negative subnetworks exhibiting distinct spatiotemporal dynamics. From these, we derive time-domain interbrain large-scale networks, spatial-domain network-level inter-subject similarity (ISS), and spatiotemporal couplings to investigate neural underpinnings of social decision-making and interpersonal coordination. The time-domain interbrain coupling was hypothesized to occur primarily in mentalizing regions, such as the default mode network, as they were most frequently reported in previous findings29,30. In contrast, no clear hypothesis is proposed for spatial-domain ISS and spatiotemporal couplings due to the limited existing evidence; however, any discovery would promote our understanding of the neural substrates of social interaction. Finally, we compare our approach with traditional scalp- and atlas-based interbrain networks, as well as power spectral ISS, to evaluate its effectiveness and advantages.

Results

Extraction of non-negative activation subnetworks

EEG data were collected from 74 proposer–responder dyads performing an iterated UG. In each round, the proposer determined how to split a specified amount, and the responder decided whether to accept or reject this division. If accepted, both participants received the specified amounts; if rejected, neither received anything (Fig. 1). To characterize the neural dynamics underlying UG decision-making, we implemented a data-driven framework (Fig. 2) that integrates Bayesian non-negative matrix factorization with EEG source imaging, enabling the extraction of spatially and temporally resolved functional subnetworks. An eight-component solution optimally captured task-related neural activity. These subnetworks include the central executive network (CEN), left primary visual network (lPVN), anterior default mode network (aDMN), visual network (VN), self-referential network (SRN), somatosensory-motor network (SMN), right primary visual network (rPVN), and posterior default mode-related network (pDMN). Importantly, the identification of these subnetworks was driven entirely by the Bayesian NMF decomposition of task-related data, without imposing a priori network definitions. While several subnetworks show close correspondence to canonical functional networks (e.g., CEN, aDMN, SMN, PVN), others, such as the pDMN, exhibit partial overlap with visual regions, reflecting task-specific recruitment rather than classical resting-state organization. Figure 3 presents the spatial distribution of the eight subnetworks. Detailed regional composition, cluster sizes, peak activations, and MNI coordinates are reported in Supplementary Table 1, with orthogonal slice views provided in Supplementary Fig. 1 for comprehensive spatial characterization.

Interbrain synchronization in the time domain

Interbrain neural coupling occurs when the behaviors of two individuals dynamically influence and constrain each other to optimize information sharing. The behavioral results indicated that the two players exhibited increasing mutual coordination across these experimental blocks (Supplementary Fig. 2). Accordingly, we next constructed time-domain interbrain large-scale networks using phase-locking value (PLV) to examine the statistical differences in time-domain IBS across three experimental blocks. Figure 4A–C showed that Blocks 2 and 3 had significantly higher network-level IBS compared to Block 1 (pFDR < 0.05), while no differences occurred concerning Blocks 2 vs. 3. After calculating and comparing the nodal degree centrality of the differential interbrain network across the three blocks, the most notable differences were found in the connections between CEN, SMN, SRN, and aDMN of the proposer and SMN, SRN, aDMN, and pDMN of the responder (Fig. 4D).

Network properties, such as characteristic path length (L), clustering coefficient (C), global efficiency (GE), and local efficiency (LE), serve as lower-dimensional quantitative measures of complex networks, reflecting the degree of information exchange and neural alignment between interacting brains. These properties were computed and compared across the three task blocks using a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). A significant main effect of block was observed for all network metrics (C: F(2,146) = 4.747, p = 0.012, ηp² = 0.061; GE: F(2,146) = 5.060, p = 0.010, ηp² = 0.065; LE: F(2,146) = 4.875, p = 0.011, ηp² = 0.063; L: F(2,146) = 5.042, p = 0.010, ηp² = 0.065). Post hoc paired-sample t-tests revealed significantly enhanced interbrain network properties in Block 2 compared to Block 1 (Fig. 4E), including larger C (t = 3.183, p = 0.002, Cohen’s d = 0.370, 95% CI = [0.142, 0.621]), GE (t = 3.409, p = 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.396, 95% CI = [0.167, 0.649]), LE (t = 3.264, p = 0.002, Cohen’s d = 0.379, 95% CI = [0.151, 0.631]), as well as a shorter L (t = −3.393, p = 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.394, 95% CI = [−0.647, −0.166]). No significant differences were observed between Blocks 2 and 3 or between Blocks 1 and 3.

The mutual coordination and enhanced reciprocity in decision-making behaviors between both partners as the experiment progressed were observed (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Therefore, the relationships between the decision-making behaviors (i.e., fair offer rate, acceptance rate, and total revenue) and interbrain network metrics (i.e., network topologies and properties) were further explored to reveal the shared neural representations underlying the interactive decision-making process. As depicted in Fig. 4F–H, interbrain connections were significantly positively correlated with the fair offer rate, acceptance rate, and total revenue, particularly the IBS of aDMNpro-aDMNrsp, aDMNpro-SMNrsp, pDMNpro-aDMNrsp, pDMNpro-SMNrsp, VNpro-aDMNrsp, and VNpro-SMNrsp (pFDR < 0.05). No correlations between interbrain network properties and decision-making behaviors were identified.

Inter-subject similarity in the spatial domain

To comprehensively examine the changes in interbrain interaction patterns as the experimental blocking progresses, we further calculated spatial-domain ISS, a factor often overlooked in previous research. Given the high spatial resolution of the decomposed functional subnetworks by Bayesian NMF, Pearson’s correlation was employed to identify the similarity of spatial distribution between the proposer’s and responder’s subnetworks. A one-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of block on spatial ISS in SRN (F(2,146) = 3.481, p = 0.033, ηp² = 0.046) and SMN (F(2,146) = 4.014, p = 0.020, ηp² = 0.052), as well as a marginal main effect in rPVN (F(2,146) = 2.778, p = 0.065, ηp² = 0.037). No significant block effects were observed in the remaining networks. Post hoc paired-sample t-tests (Fig. 5A) further showed that, relative to Block 1, ISS was significantly higher in Block 2 within SRN (t = 2.171, p = 0.033, Cohen’s d = 0.252, 95% CI = [0.021, 0.484]), and significantly higher in Blocks 2 and 3 within SMN (Block 1 vs. 2: t = 2.023, p = 0.047, Cohen’s d = 0.235, 95% CI = [0.004, 0.467]; Block 1 vs. 3: t = 2.451, p = 0.017, Cohen’s d = 0.285, 95% CI = [0.053, 0.517]). Consistent with the trend-level ANOVA effect, exploratory post hoc comparisons indicated lower ISS in rPVN for Blocks 2 and 3 relative to Block 1 (Blocks 1 vs. 2: t = −2.134, p = 0.036, Cohen’s d = −0.248, 95% CI = [−0.480, −0.016]; Blocks 1 vs. 3: t = −2.009, p = 0.048, Cohen’s d = −0.234, 95% CI = [−0.465, −0.002]) during decision-making interaction. No such differences were detected in other networks.

The relationship between the ISS of each subnetwork and decision-making behaviors was then examined, as depicted in Fig. 5B. To be specific, ISS in SRN was significantly positively (pFDR < 0.05) correlated with the fair offer rate (r = 0.175), acceptance rate (r = 0.172) and total revenue (r = 0.172), while ISS in lPVN, VN, rPVN, and pDMN was significantly negatively correlated with the fair offer rate (lPVN: r = −0.329; VN: r = −0.168; rPVN: r = −0.231; pDMN: r = −0.276), acceptance rate (lPVN: r = −0.269; VN: r = −0.168; rPVN: r = −0.198; pDMN: r = −0.216) and total revenue (lPVN: r = −0.269; VN: r = −0.168; rPVN: r = −0.198; pDMN: r = −0.216). No such relation was detected in any of the other networks (pFDR > 0.05).

Relationship between time-domain IBS and spatial-domain ISS

Next, we sought to identify the relationship between time-domain IBS and spatial-domain ISS across different subnetworks during the interactive decision-making process using Pearson correlation analysis. We found that the time-domain IBS and spatial-domain ISS within the same subnetwork are significantly and positively correlated (pFDR < 0.05) during the decision-making process, as depicted in Fig. 6. We define the correlations between the network-level time-domain IBS and spatial-domain ISS as the interbrain spatiotemporal coupling strength, a measure reflecting the integrative dynamics of information across both temporal and spatial domains between interacting brains. Notably, the subnetworks demonstrating the most pronounced spatiotemporal coupling (r > 0.500, pFDR < 0.001) included lPVN, aDMN, VN, SRN, and SMN.

Prediction of decision-making behaviors

To explore whether time-domain IBS and spatial-domain ISS could predict decision-making behaviors, we applied a LASSO regression model with the independent input parameters of interbrain network features to estimate the behaviors. Specifically, we predicted the behaviors by fusing both types of interbrain features (i.e., IBS and ISS). Because an unconstrained regression framework was used, predicted behavioral values were not restricted to the empirical range; model performance was assessed based on the correspondence between predicted and observed values. As such, predicted values represent regression-based behavioral scores rather than probabilities. As depicted in Fig. 7A, predicted behavioral outcomes were significantly correlated with actual outcomes (fair offer rate: r = 0.374, p = 8.62 × 10−9, root mean square error (RMSE) = 0.277, ppermute < 0.001; acceptance rate: r = 0.364, p = 2.26 × 10−8, RMSE = 0.233, ppermute < 0.001; total revenue: r = 0.363, p = 2.66 × 10−8, RMSE = 70.398, ppermute < 0.001), indicating robust prediction performance. As shown in Fig. 7B, the most frequently selected predictors across leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) included IBS connections between aDMN–aDMN, pDMN–aDMN, and pDMN–SMN, as well as the ISS of PVN and pDMN. Notably, these subnetworks overlapped with those exhibiting the strongest interbrain spatiotemporal coupling, including DMN, PVN, and SMN. Furthermore, models using only IBS or ISS features yielded significantly lower predictive performance compared to those fusing both feature types (Supplementary Fig. 3). Together, these findings suggest that integrating time-domain and spatial-domain interbrain features provides a more comprehensive neural representation of social interactive behaviors, supporting the complementary roles of temporal and spatial coupling in interbrain information exchange. Additional sensitivity analyses excluding dyads associated with extreme predicted values yielded highly consistent results (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5).

Validation analyses

To validate the effectiveness of the proposed approach, we first evaluated the robustness of the Bayesian NMF by examining the consistency of the extracted subnetworks across different initializations. The extracted subnetwork exhibited high replicability, with strong correlations in spatial distribution (r = 0.968 ± 0.029) and temporal dynamics (r = 0.972 ± 0.032), as shown in Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7. We then constructed scalp-level time-domain interbrain networks using PLV and observed consistent patterns of change with the source-level interbrain networks (Supplementary Fig. 8), replicating our main findings. Moreover, based on Power31 and Yan32 (according to Yeo’s seven networks33) parcellation atlas, we applied canonical correlation analysis (CCA) to construct atlas-based time-domain interbrain networks. Compared to our data-driven subnetworks, these atlas-based networks failed to capture the dynamic changes in interbrain interaction patterns effectively (Supplementary Figs. 9–12). From the spatial-domain validation perspective, we also computed ISS based on whole-brain (1 × 6239) power spectral density (PSD) for each proposer–responder pair and found no significant differences in PSD-based ISS among the three experimental blocks (Supplementary Fig. 13). These results demonstrate the superiority of our proposed data-driven method, demonstrating that interbrain interactions between data-driven decomposed subnetworks more effectively characterize the intrinsic dynamic changes during interactive decision-making. Additional methodological details are provided in the Supplementary Notes 3–6.

Discussion

Moving beyond traditional pairwise analyses of brain regions, we proposed a comprehensive analytical framework for both inference and prediction grounded in bipartite interbrain networks. In this study, EEG hyperscanning was employed to simultaneously record brain activity from dyads engaged in real-time interactive decision-making. Through EEG source reconstruction and Bayesian NMF, we identified eight best-fit subnetworks related to the social decision-making process. Interpersonal coordination was characterized by increased time-domain IBS and spatial-domain ISS, with these interbrain metrics significantly predicting higher levels of decision-making interactive behavior. Crucially, IBS and ISS involving DMN, VN, and SMN emerged as reliable predictors, with strong spatiotemporal coupling also observed within these subnetworks. Validation through scalp-level networks, atlas-based comparisons, and PSD-based ISS confirmed the robustness and effectiveness of our data-driven approach. This analysis characterizes network-level interbrain couplings across temporal and spatial domains during interpersonal social interactions, providing a critical perspective in the burgeoning field of hyperscanning.

Recent studies have demonstrated that different decision performances recruit distinct IBS patterns associated with high-level cognitive processing during interactive decision-making10,34,35. Although IBS has been widely employed to investigate interbrain interactions, most hyperscanning studies remain limited to pairwise analyses between specific brain regions of interacting individuals36,37. To move beyond this conventional approach, we introduce an analytical framework that captures interbrain dynamics from the perspective of large-scale network integration. By combining Bayesian NMF with EEG source imaging, we identified eight best-fit subnetworks implicated in decision-making: CEN, lPVN, aDMN, VN, SRN, SMN, rPVN, and pDMN. These subnetworks covered key cortical regions critical to social cognition and decision-making, including the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), mPFC, ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC), and DLPFC38–44. Specifically, PVN and VN are primarily involved in visual information processing45,46, while DMN supports self-referential integration and internally directed cognition in social contexts47. The anterior DMN has been linked to self-referential processing, associating external stimuli with oneself48–50, whereas the posterior DMN is involved in coding and retrieving autobiographical memory, perceptual processing, and information integration50,51. Furthermore, SRN has been implicated in self-referential processes52,53, CEN in working memory and executive control22,54, and SMN in both motion functions55 and decision-making42,56. Hence, interbrain interactions among these subnetworks may reflect high-level social cognitive processing in interactive decision-making.

Human social behaviors can be better understood as shaped and sustained by interactions across distributed brain networks57,58. Along these lines, the time-domain interbrain large-scale network constructed from the eight best-fit subnetworks provides a more macroscopic perspective on interpersonal neural mechanisms. Coordinated decision-making was associated with significantly increased time-domain IBS, particularly involving the proposer’s CEN, SMN, aDMN, and SRN and the responder’s SMN, aDMN, SRN, and pDMN. Among these subnetworks, DMN exhibited the most pronounced enhancement, which aligns with its frequently reported involvement in social cognition—such as mentalizing, attribution, and emotional communication—supported by core regions including the mPFC and TPJ22,59. Given that the UG involves fairness evaluation and active self-referential processing60–62, the observed SRN-related IBS patterns may reflect these shared cognitive demands between interacting partners. SMN showed extensive IBS with CEN, aDMN, SRN, and VN, consistent with its broad involvement in perceptual-motor processes and sustained task engagement33,63. In social contexts, CEN activity has been associated with the regulation of behavior based on others’ mental states and with strategy selection64,65, which may help explain its pronounced IBS. Moreover, the significantly higher C, GE, LE, and lower L in Block 2 suggest more efficient interbrain information exchange and shared cognitive alignment in this phase66–68. The absence of significant differences between Blocks 2 and 3 may indicate that, as the interaction progresses, interacting partners achieve stable coordination without requiring continuous adjustment of cognitive control. Finally, correlation analyses revealed that increased IBS across the DMN, SMN, and VN was associated with more reciprocal behavioral patterns, suggesting that time-domain large-scale IBS captures shared neural processes that support coordinated behavior, social perception, and affective engagement during interpersonal decision-making.

The spatial correlation of interbrain subnetworks may afford objective insights into the similarity of partners’ mental processes as they experience and react to the world around them69. Over the course of the experiment, we observed a progressive increase in the spatial-domain ISS of SRN, SMN, and aDMN, alongside a decline in PVN and pDMN. Further analyses revealed that the ISS in SRN was positively related to reciprocal behaviors, whereas the ISS in PVN, VN, and pDMN exhibited the opposite trend. SRN encompasses core regions including the mPFC, OFC, and ACC, which are central to social cognition44,70; thus, the increasing ISS in SRN may correspond to greater similarity in social-cognitive processing between partners. This interpretation is consistent with recent findings showing that dyads who maintain joint attention tend to exhibit more similar neural representations as they monitor each other’s actions or external cues71. In this context, ISS patterns in the SRN and SMN may reflect neural alignment that co-occurs with reciprocal and coordinated behaviors. In contrast, decreasing ISS in visual-related networks may indicate reduced similarity in externally oriented processing and a shift toward more internally driven evaluative or mentalizing processes as social interaction progresses. These dynamic changes in spatial-domain ISS underscore that highly reciprocal behavior is accompanied by more similar sensory and cognitive processes across multiple levels of the cortical information-processing hierarchy.

Most interestingly, we found a significant positive correlation between network-level time-domain IBS and spatial-domain ISS, particularly within subnetworks such as DMN, SMN, SRN, VN, and PVN. Notably, IBS patterns involving aDMN-SMN and aDMN-aDMN showed strong associations with reciprocal decision-making. In contrast, although ISS in SMN and DMN increased as the interaction unfolded, these changes were not directly related to behavioral reciprocity. This pattern suggests that spatial resonance may be associated with conditions that coincide with more efficient temporal information exchange between the two brains. These findings highlight the involvement of shared neural mechanisms across both temporal and spatial domains between interacting brains when processing evolving decision cues. Predictive modeling further showed that combining IBS and ISS features yielded more accurate estimates of reciprocal behavior than either feature alone, indicating that the complex social behavior may be better characterized when considering synchronized processing of network-level temporal and spatial information across interacting brains. Feature importance analyses identified key predictors, including IBS between aDMNpro-aDMNrsp, pDMNpro-aDMNrsp, aDMNpro-SMNrsp, and pDMNpro-SMNrsp, alongside ISS in PVN and pDMN. These predictive networks also exhibited strong spatiotemporal coupling, underscoring the significance of intricate neural alignment across both temporal and spatial domains in effective social interaction. The consistent prominence of the DMN in these analyses aligns with extensive evidence linking this network to self-referential and social-cognitive processing, particularly during inferences about others’ intentions29,72.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that the proposed heuristic framework effectively captures interbrain coupling at the network level across both temporal and spatial domains. Crucially, the nature of social interactions can potentially be embedded in brain subnetworks. This process is modulated not only by the time-domain synchrony of interbrain large-scale networks but also through the spatial couplings of hub subnetworks. This study further confirms the possibility of probing the interpersonal neural mechanisms of social interaction at a very high temporal and spatial resolution level based on EEG hyperscanning. In addition, interbrain spatiotemporal network couplings may offer a promising direction in the flexible assessment of psychiatric disorders related to social functioning.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although our findings suggest that spatial resonance may facilitate efficient temporal information transfer between interacting brains, future work should incorporate causal modeling approaches (e.g., dynamic causal modeling, Granger-based methods) and perturbation-based paradigms (e.g., dual-brain stimulation) to establish its mechanistic role more clearly in shaping interbrain dynamics. Second, previous studies have shown that differences in interpersonal relationships12,73,74 and gender composition75,76 can influence behavioral coordination and interbrain synchrony between interacting partners. Future studies should systematically examine how factors such as social relationships, gender composition, and personality traits shape neural dynamics during decision-making interactions. Considering these factors in experimental design and analysis will help distinguish the effects of social familiarity and individual variability from task-related neural synchronization. Third, EEG-based IBS has limited spatial resolution and is susceptible to volume conduction and common-source effects, which may lead to spurious synchronization. Although source localization was applied to mitigate these influences, IBS estimates involving subcortical regions should still be interpreted with caution. Future studies may combine EEG hyperscanning with neuroimaging techniques that provide higher spatial resolution, such as fMRI or intracranial recordings, to further corroborate the source-level interpretation of IBS. Finally, while our spatial similarity analysis captures convergence of neural activity patterns across participants, it does not fully characterize the representational geometry underlying different task blocks. Future work may benefit from applying representational similarity analysis (RSA) to examine whether the structure of neural representations is aligned across interacting individuals.

Methods

Participants

This study was approved by the research ethics board of the University of Electronic Science and Technology of China (Approval ID: 1061423091127369). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. We recruited 148 right-handed healthy university students (74 dyads), ensuring a relatively homogeneous age range. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, no color-vision deficiency, no history of neurological disorders, and no current psychiatric diagnosis or psychotropic medication use. Participants were randomly assigned to dyads to avoid potential confounds associated with pre-existing social relationships. The final sample included 74 proposers (42 males, aged 18–26 years, mean ± SD = 21.56 ± 1.64) and 74 responders (46 males, aged 18–28 years, mean ± SD = 21.58 ± 1.80). The gender composition of the dyads consisted of 29 male–male pairs, 13 male–female pairs, 17 female–male pairs, and 15 female–female pairs. All participants were strangers before the experiment, and remuneration was provided based on their performance in the tasks.

Experimental design

Relying on the iterated UG, paired participants were presented with offers to divide a sum of money with their partners. The proposer determined how to split a specified amount, while the responder decided whether to accept or reject this division. If accepted, both participants received the specified amounts; if rejected, neither participant received anything. Participants completed the tasks on separate computers placed back-to-back. Throughout the entire task, participants were instructed to keep their gaze fixed on their own screens, minimizing visual contact and non-verbal communication. Notably, the game involved interactions with strangers. Before the experiment, participants were informed about the task and instructed to maximize benefits, with two participants randomly assigned fixed roles. Participants performed the UG task for about 10 min, and EEG data were recorded simultaneously.

The task timeline is depicted in Fig. 2. Each trial started with a 500 ms fixation crosshair, followed by the total allocated amount displayed on both screens. The proposer selected an allocation scheme by pressing a corresponding number key (1 for “proposer gets ¥9, and responder gets ¥1”; 3 for “proposer gets ¥7, responder gets ¥3”; 5 for “proposer gets ¥5, responder gets ¥5”) on the keyboard, and then the proposed offer was presented on both screens. The responder decided to accept (key 2) or reject (key 4) the offer. Feedback indicating the responder’s decision and both participants’ cumulative earnings were presented for 2000 ms. Each trial ended with a 2000 ms black screen, after which the next trial began. Particularly, participants were required to respond within 3 s in each decision-making phase (both proposer and responder); otherwise, the trial was voided, and no earnings were awarded. A total of 90 trials were completed by each dyad, divided evenly across three blocks, with a 30-s rest following every 30 trials. Furthermore, to assess participants’ cognitive and psychological processes, questionnaire data from the interpersonal trust scale (ITS)77 and self-report altruism scale (SAS)78 were collected from all participants before and after the task, with questions in a different order. Finally, participants were informed that the experiment was over and received their payment.

Dual-EEG acquisition

EEG data for proposers and responders were collected using two separate 64-channel ASA-Lab amplifiers (ANT Neuro, Berlin, Germany), each with a sampling rate of 500 Hz. Synchronization between the two EEG recordings was ensured through triggers received from the parallel port of a server computer, guaranteeing millisecond-level synchrony. Electrode caps were positioned according to the 10-10 system, with electrodes CPz and AFz serving as the reference and ground, respectively. A predefined online filter band of [0.3–100] Hz was applied during recording. Throughout the tasks, electrode impedances were maintained below 5 kilohms, and participants were instructed to relax and minimize eye movement, as well as refrain from excessive head or body motion.

Performance of decision-making behavior and scale

Paired sample t-tests were conducted to assess pre- to post-task changes in scale scores separately for proposers and responders. To evaluate decision-making behavior across the three blocks of the interactive task, three behavioral indices were computed: (a) fair offer rate: percentage of fair offers made by the proposer, (b) acceptance rate: percentage of acceptance feedback made by the responder, and (c) total revenue: combined benefits for both proposer and responder. Notably, these behavioral indices should be interpreted at the dyad level rather than the individual level, as each participant’s decisions are dynamically influenced by their partner’s previous choices throughout the iterated UG task. Additionally, to evaluate temporal changes in responsiveness during the interactive decision-making process, response times were calculated for both roles across blocks. Pearson’s correlation analysis was further conducted to examine the association between response times and decision-making performance metrics.

EEG preprocessing

EEG Preprocessing was conducted using EEGLAB and custom MATLAB scripts (v2018b; MathWorks, Inc., USA). Raw EEG signals were first re-referenced using the reference electrode standardization technique (REST)79. Independent component analysis (ICA) was then applied to remove artifacts such as blinks, eye movements, and myoelectricity80, using the Infomax algorithm implemented in EEGLAB’s “runica” function. Artifact-related components were identified through visual inspection of each component’s topography, time course, and power spectrum, in combination with MARA (p < 0.5)81 and IClabel diagnoses (probability ≥0.80 for non-neural artifacts)82,83. The remaining components were subsequently back-projected onto the scalp EEG channels to reconstruct ICA-cleaned EEG signals. On average, 1–2 components were removed per participant. Bad channels were removed or interpolated using spherical spline interpolation. Given the known role of beta-band activity in social cognitive functions and interactive behaviors71,84,85, EEG data were bandpass filtered offline between 13–30 Hz using a zero-phase sinc FIR filter (order 200, Hamming window) implemented via the “pop_eegfiltnew” function in EEGLAB. EEG epochs corresponding to the feedback stage were extracted based on event markers for both proposers and responders, using a 2-s time window. Artifact-trial removal by a threshold of ±120 μV was applied. To maintain trial correspondence between partners, any trial rejected for one participant resulted in the removal of the corresponding trial from the paired participant.

Bayesian NMF-based functional subnetworks extraction

EEG source reconstruction was performed using standardized low-resolution brain electromagnetic tomography (sLORETA)86 to estimate cortical source activity from scalp-recorded EEG. Detailed procedures for source reconstruction are provided in the Supplementary Note 7. This process yielded cortical activities represented as a 2D matrix with dimensions of 1000 × 6239, capturing voxel-level data for each participant. To improve computational efficiency in subsequent analyses, the time dimension was down-sampled to 200, resulting in a matrix of 200 × 6239 per participant. To construct group-level data for subnetwork analysis, source-reconstructed time series from each proposer–responder pair were concatenated along the temporal axis. Specifically, data from 74 proposers and 74 responders were processed separately, generating two matrices of size 14,800 × 6239 (i.e., 200-time points × 74 participants) per role. These matrices were then merged to form a combined dataset X with dimensions of 29,600 × 6239. For block-level functional network identification, data from all three blocks were further concatenated along the temporal dimension, resulting in a final matrix of 88,800 × 6239. Temporal-spatial decomposition was subsequently conducted on this group-level matrix using Bayesian NMF87. This approach factorizes the input matrix into a set of non-negative components, each was interpreted as a unique functional subnetwork, encompassing multiple cortical regions that exhibit synchronous activation88. Further methodological details regarding the implementation of Bayesian NMF and its application to subnetwork detection are provided in the Supplementary Notes 8 and 9.

Construction of the interbrain large-scale network

For each experimental block, we extracted the time courses of the selected functional subnetworks for both proposers and responders, yielding two matrices of size M × 200, where M denotes the number of task-related subnetworks. In contrast to traditional pairwise analyses based on individual electrodes or ROIs, we treat each functional subnetwork as a network node, capturing coordinated activity across multiple cortical regions. Interbrain large-scale networks were then constructed using the phase-locking value method89, which quantifies phase synchronization between two time series. PLV captures shared neural processing elicited by the same interactive events, rather than direct alignment of overt behavior, and is a well-established measure of IBS90–93. Importantly, we focus on the feedback phase because it represents a period when both the proposer and responder are processing the same information simultaneously—evaluating fairness, anticipating outcomes, and updating expectations. PLV values range from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating perfect phase synchrony. For each dyad, we computed PLVs between every pair of subnetworks between interacting brains, resulting in an M × M weighted connectivity matrix that captures interbrain temporal interactions. In this matrix, nodes represent functionally meaningful subnetworks, and edges capture synchronized activity between these subnetworks. This network-level representation allows examination of coordinated dynamics across multiple brain regions, rather than isolated pairwise interactions.

As the lower-dimensional quantitative indices of complex interbrain large-scale networks, interbrain network properties can assess the efficiency of information interaction between brains66. We calculated multiple interbrain network properties, such as clustering coefficient, characteristic path length, global efficiency, and local efficiency, using the brain connectivity toolbox94. To assess dynamic changes across the experiment, paired sample t-tests were conducted to compare interbrain network topology and properties across the three task blocks. Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine associations between decision-making behaviors and interbrain network features. Considering that the interbrain networks contain many edges, multiple comparisons were performed. To control for Type I errors, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied at a significance level of 0.05 using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. All p-values from the edge-wise correlations were ranked, and a threshold was determined such that the expected proportion of false positives among significant edges was controlled. Edges with p-values below this threshold were considered statistically significant after FDR correction. This procedure ensures robust control of false positives in the context of multiple network comparisons.

Inter-subject similarity

Considering the high spatial resolution of the decomposed subnetworks after source reconstruction, we extracted the spatial distribution of each subnetwork at the individual level. Specifically, we first computed the pseudo-inverse (M × 200) matrix of the time-dimensional 2D matrix derived from the decomposition. This matrix was then multiplied by the transpose matrix (200 × 6239) of the original cortical activity 2D matrix for each participant, resulting in a 2D matrix (M × 6239) representing the spatial distribution of each participant’s subnetwork. For each proposer–responder pair, ISS was quantified as the Pearson correlation between their corresponding spatial subnetwork representations. To examine the temporal dynamics of ISS across task blocks, paired sample t-tests were conducted. In addition, Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess associations between ISS values and behavioral decision-making metrics, and FDR correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons.

Predicting the decision-making behaviors

Leveraging the time-domain IBS features, spatial-domain ISS features, and the fusion of ISS and IBS features, we separately built machine learning models by combining interbrain connectome predictive modeling (CPM) and LASSO regression to predict interactive decision-making behavior, including fair offer rate, acceptance rate, and total revenue. In the CPM step, network features showing significant correlations to the decision-making behaviors were selected as informative features for the prediction model training. Furthermore, LASSO regression, a typical machine learning technique with a sparsity constraint on the feature dimension, was applied to refine the feature set and build a robust prediction model. To ensure the generalizability of the predictive model, the LOOCV strategy was employed. For N dyads, N−1 dyads were selected for training, while the remaining one was used for testing. Regression coefficients were computed based on the training data and subsequently used to predict the decision-making behaviors of the remaining 1 dyad. This process was repeated N times until each dyad was served as the test case once. Additionally, Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed between the actual scores and the predicted scores to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the model, and the RMSE95 was calculated to quantify the prediction error.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB (v2018b; MathWorks, Inc., USA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Behavioral indices and interbrain network measures were analyzed across three task blocks using one-way repeated-measures ANOVA, with Greenhouse–Geisser correction applied when the assumption of sphericity was violated. For questionnaire data, pre- and post-task differences were assessed using paired-sample t-tests, and Pearson’s correlation was used to examine associations between response time and behavioral measures. For time-domain interbrain functional connectivity, block-wise differences at the edge level were evaluated using paired-sample t-tests, with multiple comparisons controlled by FDR correction. In contrast, network-level properties and spatial-domain ISS were treated as scalar measures and compared across blocks using repeated-measures ANOVA, followed by post hoc paired-sample t-tests when appropriate. Effect sizes and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for all significant results. Specifically, F statistics and partial eta squared (ηp²) were reported for ANOVA effects, and Cohen’s d for dependent samples was reported for pairwise comparisons, together with corresponding 95% CIs.

Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine the associations between decision-making behavior and IBS, decision-making behavior and ISS, as well as between predicted and observed behavioral accuracy. To account for potential multivariate outliers, the Mahalanobis distance96 was applied. One block-level observation from a single dyad was identified as an outlier in the ISS–behavior correlation analysis and the IBS–ISS correlation analysis was excluded accordingly. All other analyses were performed using the full dataset.

For predictive analyses, permutation tests were conducted to evaluate whether the model outperformed chance. In each permutation, the behavioral scores were randomly shuffled, and the entire prediction procedure was repeated to obtain a permuted correlation coefficient (rpermute). This process was repeated 1000 times, and the permutation p-value (ppermute) value was then computed by as the proportion of permutations in which rpermute exceeded the observed correlation, providing a non-parametric estimate of statistical significance.

Regarding reproducibility, we first assessed the robustness of the Bayesian NMF by evaluating the consistency of the extracted subnetworks across different random initializations. Correlations between each repeated run and the main results were computed for both spatial distributions and temporal dynamics. To further validate the findings, scalp-level time-domain interbrain networks were constructed using PLV. These analyses revealed patterns of block-wise changes consistent with those observed in the source-level interbrain networks, thereby replicating the main results.
