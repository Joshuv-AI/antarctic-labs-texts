Community-based family planning resources and modern contraceptive use among young women living with HIV in semi-rural northern Uganda: a mixed-method study
Authors: Edward Kumakech, Deo Benyumiza, Marvin Musinguzi, Wilfred Inzama, Ebong Doryn, James Okello, Lydia Kabiri, Jasper Watson Ogwal-Okeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescent girls and young women living with HIV (AGYWLHIV) face heightened risks of unintended pregnancy and often encounter barriers to accessing appropriate family planning (FP) services, particularly in Uganda. Village Health Teams (VHTs) are expected to promote the uptake of modern contraceptives (MCs), yet limited evidence exists regarding their effectiveness among AGYWLHIV in semi-rural areas. This study aimed to determine the prevalence, associated factors, and predictors of MC use among AGYWLHIV accessing HIV care in semi-rural northern Uganda, with a focus on the role of VHTs and other individual, sociocultural, and structural influences.MethodsA convergent mixed-methods study was conducted in Lira district and city northern Uganda among a stratified sample of AGYWLHIV aged 15–24 accessing antiretroviral therapy (ART) at public health facilities. Quantitative data were collected using interviewer-administered questionnaires while qualitative data were gathered through individual in-depth interviews with a purposively selected subsample reporting non-use of MCs. Participants were asked about their awareness and use of FP services, current MC use, and reasons for non-use. Statistical analysis involved descriptive statistics and multivariable modified Poisson regression at a 5% significance level. Manual thematic analysis was used for qualitative data and integration was performed at interpretation to provide a comprehensive understanding.ResultsWe recruited 423 AGYWLHIV aged 21.6 ± 2.5. The study found 62.4% (95% CI 57.8–67.0) MC use prevalence. The MC use was significantly associated with the woman being a catholic by religion (p = 0.001, APR 1.262 (95% CI 1.096–1.452), having no fertility desires (p = 0.034, APR 0.728, 95% CI 0.543–0.976), being single (P < 0.001, APR 0.751, 95% CI 0.651–0.866), alcohol use (p = 0.001, APR 1.288 (95% CI 1.117–1.485), being pregnant (p = 0.004, APR 0.306, 95% CI 0.137–0.684) and having an HIV negative male partner (p = 0.009, APR 1.225, 95% CI 1.052–1.425). Qualitative findings highlighted barriers including male partner disapproval, stigma, misinformation, pregnancy or postpartum status and sociocultural norms.ConclusionWhile MC use prevalence among AGYWLHIV in semi-rural northern Uganda exceeds national, East African and SSA averages, uptake is influenced by multifaceted socio-cultural, individual and systemic factors. The lack of significant associations between the women’s awareness of the various community-based FP resources or providers and their use of MCs but religious affiliation, marital status, alcohol use, fertility desires and male partner’s HIV status implies that socio-cultural, individual and systemic barriers to MC use should be addressed alongside awareness creation to improve the MC use in the AGYWLHIV population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04434-x.

Study background

High prevalence of HIV among adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) aged 15–24 years in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), early and unintended pregnancy, unsafe abortion and maternal mortality remain too common in this age group. Besides the burden of HIV infection and antiretroviral therapy (ART), unintended pregnancy rates are staggeringly high among the adolescent girls and young women living with HIV (AGYWLHIV) in East Africa with pooled crude prevalence at 41% [1]. The magnitude of unintended pregnancy is significantly higher (66%) among the HIV-positive women compared to 34% among their HIV-negative counterparts as demonstrated in a cross sectional study conducted in Zimbabwe [2]. The high unintended pregnancy is also an issue in semi-rural Uganda where 41% of unintended pregnancy rate was reported among the pregnant women living with HIV, with rates being even higher in rural areas [3]. The high rates of unintended pregnancies among the AGYWLHIV underscores the persistent unmet need for contraception among this population.

In addition to the adverse obstetrics and neonatal outcomes of adolescent pregnancy including maternal and neonatal mortality [4], unintended pregnancy in AGYWLHIV can have even more severe consequences including increased risk of internalized stigma [5], late antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation [6], late antenatal care (ANC) initiation [6] and lower likelihood of maternal viral suppression at delivery [6, 7]. In Uganda, the maternal mortality attributable to adolescent pregnancy can be as high as 28% [8].

The use of modern contraceptives (MCs) is a critical intervention to prevent or reduce on the high rates of unintended pregnancy and its associated consequences more so for the AGYWLHIV who carry the additional risk of HIV transmission to their male partners and children. Accordingly, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that AGYWLHIV should be offered a full range of safe and effective contraceptive options including oral contraceptive pills, emergency contraceptive pills, injectable contraceptives, implant contraceptives, intrauterine contraceptive devices, female sterilization and condoms. They should also be counselled on the risk of unplanned pregnancy and the associated consequences so as to make informed contraceptive choices [9]. Earlier studies that contraindicated certain MCs for women living with HIV (WLHIV) have since been updated with new evidence demonstrating the safety and appropriateness of these methods for this population including those receiving antiretroviral therapy [10].

Uganda and other SSA countries are implementing effective interventions to improve access and use of MCs among the AGYWLHIV. These interventions include enhancing the family planning (FP) services [11], integrated delivery of FP and HIV care [11–13] and training healthcare providers on counselling on the MCs options [11].

Community-based FP resources are also critical in improving awareness, access and the use of MCs among highly stigmatized populations such as the AGYWLHIV. The community-based FP resources include the mass media such as radios, television, posters and the billboards. It also encompasses the availability of methods and providers at the community-based health service delivery outlets such as the mobile community health workers (CHWs) also known as the village health teams (VHTs) in Uganda, private clinics or medical centres, community pharmacies or drug shops, government or private health centres or hospitals and other providers such as community-based organizations (CBOs) or non-governmental organizations (NGOs). These community-based FP resources contribute to awareness creation, FP method availability, access and the last mile distribution of the MCs to women who need them in many SSA settings including Uganda. Previous studies indicate that community-based FP resources particularly the CHWs have been effectively used in the last mile distribution of oral contraceptive pills, condoms, injectable and implant contraceptives in many SSA settings [14–16]. Previous studies have demonstrated that women’s physical visits or contacts with the CHWs in particular significantly improves their MCs use among the visited or contacted women compared to their counterparts who did not receive the CHW’s visits or contacts [17–19].

Despite the aforementioned community-based FP resources to improve the availability and access to FP services, the MC use prevalence among the AGYWLHIV in SSA including Uganda has generally remained low. Specific previous studies on MC use among the AGYWLHIV aged 15–24 years in Uganda and other SSA settings are generally inaccessible. Nevertheless, three previous cross-sectional studies conducted among WLHIV aged 16–49 years in central, western and southwestern regions of Uganda reported low MC use prevalence rates of 38%, 40% and 45% respectively [20–22]. Condoms and injectable contraceptives were among the most commonly used methods in both studies [20, 21]. This 38–45% MC use prevalence among the WLHIV in Uganda were unacceptably low given the frequent visits this population makes to health facilities particularly the ART clinics to obtain ARV drug refills, care and treatment. Even though specific previous studies on the overall MC use prevalence among AGYWLHIV in the East Africa and SSA were unavailable for comparisons, the 38–45% MC use prevalence among the Ugandan AGYWLHIV aged 16–49 years were higher than the previously reported 27% rate among the AGYW aged 15–49 years in Uganda, 25% rate among the AGYW aged 15–24 years and 34% rate among women aged 15–49 years in East Africa [23, 24], the 25% rate among the AGYW aged 15–24 years in SSA [25, 26] and 22–26% rate among the AGYW aged 15–49 years in SSA [27, 28] respectively. These conflicting literature call for further investigations into the factors influencing MC use among the AGYWLHIV in the Ugandan setting.

The above conflicting literature on MC use prevalence among the AGYWLHIV is coupled with high unmet needs for contraception and child spacing, estimated at 33% and 24% respectively [2] among the WLHIV population in western Uganda. The key positive predictors for MCs use in this population were found to include high parity, receipt of education or counseling on FP, integrated delivery of HIV care and FP services, and male involvement in contraceptive decision-making [20, 21]. More so, the lack of fertility desires was identified as the only significant predictor of MCs use in this population [3].

While the aforementioned previous studies provide valuable though conflicting insights into the MCs use prevalence rates and its predictors among a broader population of WLHIV, they do not offer a detailed examination of MC use among the AGYWLHIV especially those aged 15–24 years residing in semi-rural settings of SSA. Moreover, they did not explore the associations between awareness of the various community-based FP resources including the mass media and their impact on MCs use in this population.

To address the above knowledge gaps, this study sought to determine the MC use prevalence, its association with the women’s awareness of the available community-based FP resources or providers, predictors of MC use and explore AGYWLHIV’s perspectives and reasons for non-use of the MCs in semi-rural Uganda. The study findings may inform the messages or contents of community health educational and counselling materials for improving the community awareness about the influential community-based FP resources and other factors that facilitate the use of MCs. The educational resources may also be used by the health workers to address barriers to MC use including MC knowledge gaps, myths and misconceptions in this population.

Theoretical framework

This study used the socio-ecological model (SEM) which posits that health behaviour such as the use of MCs by the AGYWLHIV may be influenced by factors at multiple levels including intrapersonal, interpersonal, health institutional, and community/societal factors [29]. These factors informed the potential predictors variables for MC use included in the data collection tools and the analysis.

At the intrapersonal level, age is considered important, as younger women may fear unintended pregnancy, which could increase their likelihood of adopting MCs as found in a previous study [30]. Marital status is also a factor whereby married women would be more likely to use MCs to delay or space their children which is consistent with a previous study [31]. Education is another critical intrapersonal factor. The women with higher levels of education are more likely to understand the benefits and use MCs effectively consistent with previous studies [30, 32–36]. More so, the place of residence matters. Women living in semi-urban areas where more health educational resources such as radios, television and newspapers are available, are better positioned to accept and use the MCs [32]. Consistent with a previous study, the women’s socioeconomic status matters. The women from wealthier families where more financial resources are available, may be better positioned to access and use the MCs [30]. Additionally, distance from the health facility can affect MCs use. Women who perceive the distance from the health facility is not a problem may adopt MCs compared to their counterparts who perceive the distance is a problem which is consistent with a previous study [30]. Furthermore, knowledge and perceptions about the benefits of MCs can strongly influence adoption. The AGYWLHIV who have correct knowledge and perceive the benefits of MCs correctly are likely to value its use and thus adopt it more consistently [37]. Lastly, contraceptive value, preferences and subsequent use maybe influenced by the contraceptive availability, accessibility and convenience, perceived effectiveness, safety and tolerability. The women who favourably evaluate these factors on a particular contraceptive will be motivated to use it [38].

At the interpersonal level, factors such as contraceptive information sharing can significantly influence MCs use. Consistent with the previous studies, discussion of the MCs with partners or health workers can facilitate its adoption [32, 34, 36, 39, 40]. Also consistent with the previous studies, awareness of the HIV status of the sexual partners especially the discordant partners can also encourage the use of MCs such as condoms to minimize the risk of sexual transmission of HIV [33]. Parity, family size and reproductive goals play an important role as well. The AGYWLHIV who do not desire children or whose partners do not wish to have children, may be more inclined to use MCs to avoid unintended pregnancies which is also consistent with the previous studies [20, 28–30].

Health institutional factors also play a critical role in the adoption of MCs. The availability and dissemination of FP information or services through the various resources including the mass media such as posters, billboards, radio and television are other significant factors. The AGYWLHIV who have awareness and access to such FP information or services from the various mass media are more likely to adopt the MCs as indicated in the previous studies [36, 40]. Additionally, ensuring availability and delivery of FP methods at the health centres or hospitals including the ART clinic where the AGYWLHIV routinely obtain their ARV drug refill can increase the opportunity for FP information, counselling and method uptake and minimizes the risk of women failing to get methods when they need it to prevent, avoid or delay unintended pregnancies [12, 13, 20, 36, 41].

At the community or societal level, rumours, myths and misconceptions about contraceptives such as the fears that they may cause infertility, cancer or harmful side effects, often discourage their use [42]. More so, cultural beliefs whereby societal norms discourage premarital sex and put childbearing pressure on married women creating an environment where the use of contraceptives by the unmarried women is socially frowned upon is also factor that influence contraceptive use [43]. Conflicting religious beliefs whereby certain religion advocates for abstinence-only approaches and oppose the use of MCs altogether do influence contraceptive use [43]. Far distances from the health facility and thus high transportation cost in a given community or society may impede access to contraceptive providers and consequently the contraceptive use [44].

The above SEM model has been used before in previous studies on the utilization of sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services including MCs among AGYW in SSA and other developing countries [43, 45].

Methods

The study methodology has been previously described by Kumakech et al. (2023). The summary is provided below.

Study design

This study employed a convergent mixed-methods design [46]. This allowed for the simultaneous investigation of the contraceptive prevalence, its associated factors and the reasons for non-use among the study participants. The quantitative and the qualitative studies converge at the result interpretation or discussion phase.

Study area and setting

The research was conducted in the antiretroviral therapy (ART) clinics of six public health facilities in Lira District and Lira City, Northern Uganda. Lira is located approximately 342 km from Kampala, the capital city of Uganda.

Study population

The study population comprised AGYWLHIV, aged 15–24 years, who were receiving ART services. Eligible participants were those who resided in Lira City or Lira District and had been sexually active within the past year. Individuals who were severely ill and thus unable to withstand the study procedures were excluded.

Sample size

Sample size determination formula and calculation

Determining the sample size for a convergent mixed-methods study like this requires a two-part strategy: one for the quantitative survey and another for the qualitative interview subsample.

The design is sequential (survey first, then interviews), even if the data integration is convergent.

Part 1: Sample size for the initial quantitative survey

The primary objective of the survey was to estimate the prevalence of modern contraceptive use and identify its predictors among the AGYWLHIV in Lira district and city northern Uganda.

The Formula for a Single Population Proportion which is the most appropriate and widely used formula for cross-sectional surveys was considered.

The sample was proportionally distributed across three healthcare facility levels (regional referral hospital, health centre IV, and health centre III) following the principles of proportionate to size stratified random sampling.

Since we planned to use multivariable regression analysis to identify predictors of current use of MCs and we anticipated to include about 11 predictor variables. We confirmed the 423 divided by 11 predictor variables would give at least 38 events (current users) per predictor variable to be included in the regression model. The 38 events per predictor variable is more than the recommended 10–15 events per predictor variable. Therefore, the calculated sample of 423 was regarded sufficient for this study if the current use of MC prevalence is around 50%.

Part 2: Sample size for the follow up qualitative interviews (Subsample)

The goal was not statistical representativeness but thematic saturation which is the point where new interviews would no longer yield new insights or themes regarding reasons for non-use.

Purposive sampling guided by the quantitative survey results

We selected a subsample of quantitative survey participants who reported not using modern contraceptives. The selection was purposive to ensure diversity of perspectives. We considered stratifying the purposive sampling by various factors that were reported from previous studies to influence reasons for non-use of MCs such as (a) achievement of fertility goal; (b) age (adolescent versus young adult); (c) marital status (single versus married); (d) parity (childless versus the ones with 1 + children and (e) type of HIV care facility where referral hospital, health centre IV and health centre III. There is no fixed formula but based on empirical research in qualitative methodology a typical range is 15–25 in-depth interviews (IDIs). The initial target was set at 25 participants. After conducting about 15 interviews, we started analyzing the qualitative data concurrently. We continued interviewing and analyzing. After each new interview, assess if new salient themes were emerging. We stopped when we have conducted 2–3 consecutive interviews that did not provide any new substantive information related to the research question (reasons for non-use of MCs) which we regarded as the thematic saturation point. The saturation was reached at 25 interviews.

Participant’s recruitment and sampling

Participants (AGYWLHIV) were recruited from the six public ART clinics where they regularly attend and received their ARV drug refills. A stratified random sampling approach [47] was used to sample and ensure representation across the different levels of healthcare facilities. The sampling frame was developed using the ART clinic registers, which contained demographic and contact details of potential participants. The list was stratified into three groups based on the healthcare facility level. Thereafter, participants were randomly selected from each stratum to ensure proportional representation.

Participant identification strategies

The selected participants from the ART registers were identified with the assistance of the healthcare providers and community health workers at the ART clinics. Most of the selected participants were found and recruited into the study from the ART clinic when they came for medical reviews and drug refills. Some of the selected participants were contacted via phone calls to come to the clinic to participate in the study. Other selected participants especially the ones without telephone contacts were visited at home by the community health worker to facilitate their inclusion and bring them to the ART clinic to participate in the study, especially if they were not scheduled for a clinic visit at the time of the data collection. These various participant identification strategies ensured all the selected participants were identified and brought the ART clinic facility to participate in the data collection interviews at the ART clinic facility.

Data collection tool

Data were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire consisting of both closed-ended and open-ended questions. The closed-ended questions captured data on the current use of modern contraceptives, exposure factors, and covariates that are more likely to influence its use, while the open-ended questions explored the reasons for non-use. The questionnaire was developed based on the insights from previous studies on modern contraceptive use among the AGYWLHIV conducted in Uganda [20], Ethiopia [31, 36], and Nigeria [35]. To enhance clarity and relevance, the questionnaire was pretested among ten AGYWLHIV at Lira University Hospital. Minimal variations in feedback indicated that the pre-test sample size was sufficient.

Data collection procedure

Data collection took place from November 20, 2022, to April 30, 2023. Six research assistants with midwifery backgrounds conducted the data collection after receiving comprehensive training on the study procedures and ethical considerations. Individual interviews were conducted in either the local language Langi, or English, depending on participants’ preferences. Each interview lasted approximately one hour. The six research assistants altogether interviewed 10–12 participants per day. To ensure privacy and confidentiality, the individual interviews were conducted in private rooms within the ART clinic facility. The responses were recorded in a standardized manner on pre-coded questionnaire, minimizing variations in data collection.

Measures of variables

Outcome variables

The primary outcome variable was the current use of MCs by the participants. To measure this, participants were asked whether they were currently using a MC and to specify the method they were using. Women who reported to be currently using a MC and correctly specified the name of the method were regarded as current users while those who reported not be currently using any modern contraceptive method were regarded as non-users.

Specifically, current users of MCs were defined as women who reported to be using at least one of the following modern contraceptive methods: female condoms, male condoms, 3-monthly injectable contraceptives (Depo-Provera), intrauterine devices (IUDs), oral contraceptive pills whether combined oral contraceptive (COC) or progestin only oral contraceptive (POC) pills, emergency contraceptive pills, implants, tubal ligation, cervical caps, diaphragms or spermicides. Any woman who correctly specified that she was currently using any of these MCs were regarded to be current users of MCs.

In this study, women who reported to be using the natural FP methods such as the use of safe days, coitus interruptus, cervical mucus monitoring, basal body temperature monitoring, moon beads and lactational amenorrhea method (LAM) were not considered as MC users. This ensured consistency with the WHO and previous research definition of modern contraceptive method users.

The non-users of MCs were further individually interviewed to provide reasons for not using the MCs. This provided insights into the barriers to the adoption and use of MCs among this population.

Predictor variables

The explanatory variables considered in this study included participants’ socio-demographic and SRH characteristics. Specifically, data were collected on age, ethnicity, type of residence (rural or urban), current marital status, religion, level of education, source of income, monthly income, distance to the nearest health facility, and substance use. Urban residence was defined as living in a trading centre, town, municipality, or city.

Participants were also asked about their SRH history. This included age at sexual debut, gravidity, parity, history of abortions, number of living children, current pregnancy status, partner’s HIV status disclosure, HIV discordance status, and both the woman’s and her male partner’s fertility desires. Additionally, participants were asked whether they had ever experienced an unintended pregnancy or had ever needed a modern contraceptive method to prevent or delay pregnancy but were unable to access it.

Other factors explored included recent exposure to unprotected sexual intercourse, daily pill burden, and concerns about the side effects of MCs. Participants were also asked about their fear of myths, rumors, and misconceptions related to modern contraceptive methods.

A key focus of the study was to examine the association between participants’ awareness of and access to community-based FP resources and their use of MCs. To this end, participants were asked whether the specific community-based FP resources that provide FP information, education or methods are available in their residential area and whether they have ever personally received any FP information, education, or services from them. A participant who reports awareness of or access to a specified community-based FP resource and its services was regarded to have awareness of and access to the specified community-based FP resources. The opposite was true to the participants who reported being neither aware nor accessing FP information, education or methods from the specified community-based FP resources. This served as the definition of awareness of and access to community-based FP resources in this study.

The community-based FP resources considered included community health workers (also known as Village Health Teams or VHTs in Uganda), private medical centres or clinics, health centres or hospitals, the antiretroviral therapy (ART) clinic where participants usually receive their ARV refills, pharmacies or drug shops, community-based organizations (CBOs), non-governmental organizations (NGOs), radio, television, posters, and billboards. These data allowed for the comparisons between young women who reported access to such community-based FP resources and those who did not, in relation to their use of MCs.

Data management and analysis

Data were double entered into EpiData 3.1 and underwent a double-entry validation process to minimize data entry errors. The validated data were exported to the IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0 for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to determine prevalence rates, while Chi-square tests were conducted to identify factors associated with MC use. Measures of associations from Chi-square tests are reported as Pearson Chi-square value, degree of freedom and p-value. Multi-collinearity analysis between the factors that were significantly associated with current use of MCs were conducted. These allowed for identification and inclusion of only factors without severe multi-collinearity as defined by tolerance value of < 0.2, variance inflation factor (VIF) value of < 5 and conditional index value of < 30 into the multivariable regression analysis for identification of significant independent predictors. Thereafter, bivariate and multivariable analysis were performed using modified Poisson regression with a robust estimator to identify the associated factors and predictors of MC use. For references, we selected the categories of the variables that have the largest proportions for stable comparisons. Measure of association from the modified Poisson regression are reported as p-value, crude and adjusted prevalence ratios plus their corresponding 95% confidence intervals. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered for all hypothesis tests.

Our main reason for the choice and use of Modified Poisson regression was to obtain unbiased, direct estimates of the risk ratios (RR) also called relative risks such as odds ratios when they cannot be accurately obtained from a standard logistic regression model. The problem is that in cross-sectional studies (especially with common outcome), the odds ratio (OR) from logistic regression overestimates the RR when the outcome is common (typically > 10%) [48]. The rarer the outcome, the closer the OR approximates the RR. The Modified Poisson regression uses a Poisson model (typically for count data like the number of current users of MC versus non-current users) with a robust variance estimator (the “modified” part) to directly model the log risk and provide a consistent estimate of the RR, even for common outcomes. Therefore, our choice of the Modified Poisson regression model is appropriate in this study because of the count (binary) nature of the outcome variable and also the fact that the prevalence of current use of MCs among the study participants was very high at 62.4% which was > 10 cut off prevalence for the use of binary logistic regression model.

Qualitative data analysis

Qualitative responses obtained from the open-ended questions about reasons for non-use of the MCs were manually analysed using thematic analysis approach [49]. The process began with familiarization with the data, which involved transcribing the participants’ responses and reading the transcripts several times to gain a deep understanding of the content and to begin noticing recurring patterns. Following this, initial codes were generated by identifying and labelling segments of the text that appeared meaningful or relevant to the research objective. These codes were then examined and grouped into broader categories during the process of searching for themes, with the goal of capturing the key ideas emerging from the data. After identifying the potential themes, they were reviewed and refined to ensure they were coherent, accurately reflected the coded data, and were relevant to the research objective. The themes were then clearly defined and named, with careful attention paid to articulating the essence and significance of each theme in relation to the research objective. The findings were then reported by presenting each theme alongside illustrative quotations from the participants’ responses and linking the interpretations to the research questions guiding the study.

Results

Response rate

The flow diagram (Fig. 1) illustrates that 423 AGYWLHIV representing 93.2% of the targeted sample size of 454 were successfully recruited into the study. A total of 31 potential participants (6.8%) declined to participate, primarily due to poor health conditions that rendered them unable to endure the study procedures. There were no statistically significant differences in the socio-demographic characteristics between those who participated and those who declined. The use of interviewer-administered questionnaires ensured complete data collection, with all relevant questions answered by each participant and no missing data recorded.

Family planning prevalence

The study found that the prevalence of FP (both traditional and modern FP method) among the AGYWLHIV was 68.6% (95% CI 64.2–73.0). The FP method specific prevalence was 3(0.7%) for coitus interruptus, 7(1.7%) for safe days, 8(1.9%) for Sayana press, 11(2.6%) for IUDs, 16(3.8%) for Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM), 17 (4.6%) for emergency contraceptive pills, 36 (8.5%) for implants, 57(13.5%) for oral contraceptive pills, 61(14.4%) for condoms and 74(17.5%) for Depo Provera 3-monthly injections. None of the participant reported using the permanent irreversible contraceptive method such as bilateral tubal ligation (BTL).

Modern contraceptive use prevalence

After excluding the traditional FP methods, the study found that the prevalence of modern contraceptive use among the AGYWLHIV was 62.4% (95% CI 57.8–67.0).

Short-acting reversible contraceptive use prevalence

The overall short-acting reversible contraceptive prevalence stood at 51.3 (95% CI 46.5–56.1). This included 8 (1.9%) for Sayana Press, 17 (4.0%) for emergency contraceptive pills, 57 (13.5%) for oral contraceptive pills, 61 (14.4%) for male condoms, and 74 (17.5%) for Depo Provera 3-monthly injections. The short-acting reversible contraceptive use was significantly lower among the AGYWLHIV who were not aware of the VHTs providing FP methods compared to their counterparts who were aware (χ² [1] = 8.518, p = 0.004, OR 0.559, 95% CI 0.377–0.827).

Long-term reversible contraceptive use prevalence

The long-term reversible contraceptive prevalence among the AGYWLHIV in semi-rural northern Uganda stood at 11.1% (95% CI 8.1–14.1). Of this, 11 (2.6%) were IUD users, and the remaining 36 (8.5%) were implant users.

Regarding religious affiliation, a significant independent association was found for Catholic women. In the bivariate analysis, Catholic women had a significant 26% higher prevalence of MC use compared to the Anglican/Protestant women (CPR: 1.262, 95% CI: 1.077–1.479, p = 0.004). This association remained virtually unchanged and highly significant in the multivariable model (APR: 1.262, 95% CI: 1.096–1.452, p = 0.001). Therefore, being Catholic is an independent predictor of higher contraceptive use. In contrast, there was no significant difference in the MC use between Muslim women and Anglican/Protestant women in either the bivariate (CPR: 1.080, 95% CI: 0.833–1.479, p = 0.561) or multivariable analysis (APR: 1.101, 95% CI: 0.843–1.438, p = 0.478).

Marital status was a strong independent predictor of MC use. In the bivariate analysis, single women had a significant 13% lower prevalence of MC use compared to married women (CPR: 0.866, 95% CI: 0.790–0.949, p = 0.002). This association strengthened in the multivariable model, where single women were 25% less likely to use MCs than their married counterparts (APR: 0.751, 95% CI: 0.651–0.866, p < 0.001).

The HIV status of a woman’s male partner was another critical independent factor. Women with HIV-negative partners had a 38% higher prevalence of MC use in the bivariate analysis (CPR: 1.377, 95% CI: 1.174–1.614, p < 0.001). After adjustment, this effect remained significant, though slightly attenuated, indicating that women with HIV-negative partners had a 23% higher prevalence of MC use than those with HIV-positive partners (APR: 1.225, 95% CI: 1.052–1.425, p = 0.009).

Current pregnancy demonstrated the most substantial negative association. Pregnant women were 73% less likely to use MCs in the bivariate analysis (CPR: 0.272, 95% CI: 0.123–0.605, p = 0.001). This strong association persisted in the multivariable model, with pregnant women being 69% less likely to use MCs (APR: 0.306, 95% CI: 0.137–0.684, p = 0.004).

A counterintuitive yet significant finding was related to fertility desires. Women who reported no desire for more children had a 31% lower prevalence of MC use in the bivariate analysis (CPR: 0.691, 95% CI: 0.499–0.959, p = 0.027). This paradoxical association held in the multivariable model, showing that women with no fertility desires had a 27% lower prevalence of MC use than those who desired children (APR: 0.728, 95% CI: 0.543–0.976, p = 0.034).

Alcohol use was associated with increased contraceptive use. Users of alcohol had a 38% higher prevalence of MC use in the initial analysis (CPR: 1.380, 95% CI: 1.185–1.607, p < 0.001), which remained significant after adjustment, with a 29% higher prevalence (APR: 1.288, 95% CI: 1.117–1.485, p = 0.001).

Conversely, several factors lost their significance after the multivariable adjustment. The initial protective effect of a later age at sexual debut (20–24 years) became non-significant after adjustment (APR: 0.961, 95% CI: 0.758–1.218, p = 0.742). Furthermore, all variables related to the awareness of FP resources or providers (e.g., from VHTs, government health centres, NGOs, or ART facilities), which were significant in the bivariate analyses, became non-significant in the final multivariable model, suggesting their effects were mediated by other more proximal factors.

More so, several variables were excluded from the multivariable model due to their marginal associations or severe multi-collinearity. These included recent unintended pregnancy, history of failed access to contraceptives, recent unprotected sex and fear of MC side effects. While these factors showed significant bivariate associations, their independent effects could not be reliably estimated.

Table 4 shows the results for multi-collinearity analysis between the factors that were significantly associated with the outcome variable (current modern contraceptive use). Other significant associations between variables among the participants

Bivariate analyses for associations between variables indicated that most of the catholic AGYWLHIV in the study were significantly lower income earners (χ² (1) = 8.914, p= 0.003) and also lowly educated (χ² (2) =9.436, p= 0.009) compared to the Anglican and protestant AGYWLHIV in the study. Furthermore, the AGYWLHIV who were alcohol drinkers were also found to be more likely to have frequent sexual activity (χ² (1) = 6.009, p= 0.014), have a positive history of unintended pregnancy (χ² (1) = 17.003, p<0.001) and have met their reproductive goals and thus did not desire anymore children (χ² (1) = 7.290, p= 0.007) compared to their alcohol non-drinker counterparts. Lastly, the result from the bivariate analysis for association found that AGYWLHIV with fertility desires tended to have infrequent sexual intercourse compared those with fertility desires (χ² (1) = 271.272, p<0.001).

Reasons for non-use of modern contraceptives including the male condoms

Thematic analysis of the qualitative responses from the AGYWLHIV aged 15–24 years in north-eastern Uganda revealed seven major themes explaining their non-use of modern contraceptive methods, including the male condoms. These themes reflect a complex interplay of interpersonal, structural, and sociocultural barriers in modern contraceptive use decision-making. Each theme is illustrated with direct participant’s quotations to contextualize the findings.

Partner disapproval of modern contraceptives

A dominant theme across the data was the influence of the male partners in contraceptive decision-making. Many AGYW reported that their male partners or husbands refused or discouraged the use of the modern FP methods, including the male condoms. This was often linked to cultural expectations that men should control the reproductive decisions, as well as fear of relationship consequences such as violence or divorce.

One participant explained, “My husband does not want; he threatens divorce if I ever get any modern FP method” (24-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal). Another echoed this concern, noting, “My husband says only prostitutes want men to use the male condoms” (24-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal). In other cases, the male partners refused the male condom use outright or claimed modern contraceptive use was unnecessary. As one young woman shared, “My male partner is not willing or uninterested in using the male condoms” (21-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal), while another added, “My husband always tells me that there is no need for him to use the male condom because I have IUD already” (22-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal).

Lack of FP knowledge and misinformation

Limited exposure to accurate information about modern contraceptive methods also emerged as a significant barrier. Some AGYW had never heard of modern FP methods, while others expressed confusion or misconceptions about their use and purpose.

One young participant admitted, “I didn’t know of the modern contraceptives” (15-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal), and another stated simply, “I don’t know about any family planning method” (15-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal). Others described having never received any FP information: “No one told me about them – the modern FP methods” (19-year-old, rural, unmet fertility goal). This lack of awareness extended to misunderstandings about when and how modern contraceptives should be used, with one woman explaining, “I was not yet aware about modern family planning methods” (18-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal). Another participant justified their failure to use the male condoms to the fact that both of them are concordant HIV positive, “He is also HIV positive and so more need to use the condoms” (24-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal).

Fear of MC side effects and health concerns

Fear of modern contraceptive side effects—whether based on personal experiences or hearsay—was a common deterrent. Concerns included excessive bleeding, infertility, cancer, and other health complications, often exacerbated by the community rumours, misconceptions and limited access to factual information or counselling.

One participant recounted her experience: “I used injectaplan once and had severe side effects” (22-year-old, urban, met fertility goal), while another stated categorically, “Modern contraceptives bring infertility” (21-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal). Another even associated condoms to the most feared disease cancer, “Condoms cause cancer” (24-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal). Still under this them, a participant categorically stated her fears of allergic reactions from the condoms, “I fear the allergic reactions from the condom lubricants” (24-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal). Other participants were influenced by their family member’s experiences, such as the woman who said, “I heard of bad experience from the sister—she had heavy bleeding from the modern FP method she was using” (21-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal).

Desire for pregnancy and children

A number of participants expressed a desire to conceive in the present or near future, which shaped their decision to avoid modern contraceptive use. Motivations included planning for more children, responding to partner desires or meeting societal expectations to bear children, especially soon after marriage.

As one woman stated, “I wanted to conceive the 3rd born” (24-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal), while another shared, “We want to have a baby and both of us are on ART” (22-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal). For others, the choice was straightforward: “I want to get pregnant” (22-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal).

Infrequent sex from the absence or distance of the male sexual partner

Some AGYW reported of having infrequent sex from having their male partners absent or stays at a far distance, making contraceptive use seem unnecessary. In such cases, the non-use was situational rather than ideologically or medically motivated.

One woman noted, “I am separated from my husband” (24-year-old, urban, met fertility goal), while another explained, “I have no male sexual partner currently” (24-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal). A third participant added, “My male sexual partner isn’t around; he stays far from me” (19-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal).

Pregnancy or postpartum status

Being pregnant or in the postpartum period were cited as reasons for not using modern contraceptives. Some participants believed they did not need any FP method during breastfeeding or while they were still not menstruating after childbirth (i.e., lactational amenorrhea).

As one woman put it, “I am currently pregnant” (22-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal). Others referred to breastfeeding as a contraceptive in itself, with one saying, “My baby is still breastfeeding” (24-year-old, urban, met fertility goal), and another stating, “I have not yet seen my periods since childbirth so no need to use modern contraceptives” (24-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal).

Contraceptive stigma and sociocultural barriers

Stigma associated with FP use—both perceived and internalized—was another major deterrent. Participants also feared the negative judgment from the community or their families, and some internalized beliefs that FP was inappropriate before childbirth or indicative of promiscuity.

One participant confided, “I am afraid of what people will say about me going to start family planning” (20-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal). Another shared familial pressure: “My mother told me I have first to produce a child before I start using family planning” (19-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal). An internalized belief that the male condoms reduces the enjoyment of sexual intercourse was also shared: “The male condom reduces the sweetness of sexual intercourse and so we don’t want to use it” (24-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal). Stigma surrounding the male condom use was also highlighted, as echoed in the statement, “My husband says only prostitutes want men to use condoms” (24-year-old, urban, unmet fertility goal).

Discussion

This study found a moderately high prevalence of modern contraceptive use at 62.4% among the AGYWLHIV in semi-rural northern Uganda. This moderate level of MC uptake is consistent with the 64.1% -75% rates reported from Ethiopia [31, 50]. Notably, the 62.4% MC prevalence rate in northern Uganda is much higher than the 45% and 39.8% MC prevalence rates reported from a similar study conducted in south-western and Western Uganda among the HIV-positive women aged 16–49 attending HIV care services respectively [20, 51]. The 62.4% MC use prevalence found among the AGYWLHIV in Uganda is also higher than the 51.2% rate reported from Malawi [52], 24.7% rate reported from Ghana [53] and The observed difference in modern contraceptive use rates between the two studies may be largely attributed to the differences in the age composition of the study populations. While the present study focused exclusively on a younger cohort of adolescent girls and young women aged 15–24, the south-western Uganda study included older women up to the age of 40. The AGYWLHIV may have a greater demand for modern contraceptives to prevent unintended pregnancies, space births or delay childbearing, especially in the context of their HIV status, socioeconomic vulnerability and high rates of unemployment. In contrast, older women may perceive a reduced need for contraception due to their declining fertility or having already achieved their desired family size. The moderate level of uptake of modern contraceptives found in the quantitative study is further corroborated by the qualitative results, which revealed that modern contraceptive use among these young women was influenced by several key barriers. These included the male partner disapproval and dominance, pregnancy and postpartum status, desire for pregnancy and children, lack of knowledge and misinformation, absence or distance of male sexual partners, as well as stigma and sociocultural norms. These barriers should be targets for future interventions aimed at improving the uptake of modern contraceptive methods in this population.

In examining the relationship between participants’ awareness of the various community-based FP service resources or providers and their use of MCs, none of the resources showed a statistically significant association after adjustments in the multivariable regression model. This finding concurs with a previous study conducted in Malawi among the AGYWLHIV where they also found no associations between women’s access to the FP information from the mass media such as radio, television, newspapers or visits by the FP health workers and their MC use [52]. The insignificant association between women’s awareness of FP service providers and use of MCs indicate that the decision to use contraception is not driven by mere awareness of FP service availability or providers. The implication is that interventions to improve MC use among this population should not merely focus on raising awareness about the availability of the FP services or the providers but also tackle other drivers of MC uptake.

We found that Catholic religious’ believers were more likely to use MCs than the Anglican and Protestant believers. This finding contradicts the previous study that conflicting religious beliefs especially among the Catholic faith advocates for abstinence-only approaches and oppose the use of MCs altogether [43]. Our finding is attributable to the fact that most of the catholic AGYWLHIV in the study were significantly lower income earners and also lowly educated compared to the Anglican and protestant AGYWLHIV in the study that could have contributed to their higher MC use. The finding implies that interventions aimed at improving MC use among the AGYWLHIV population should not only target the Catholic believers but also address the low income and low educational attainment issues among them. Single AGYWLHIV in semi-rural northern Uganda were found to be 23.1% less likely to use MCs compared to their married women counterparts. The association between marital status and MC use is conflicting with the literature with some studies concurring with the current study of single (not married) women being less likely to use MCs [31, 54–56] while others showing the opposite association [57, 58]. The conflicting research findings highlight a significant disparity in contraceptive uptake based on marital status. Several factors may explain this difference as they emerged from this study’s qualitative data on non-use of MCs. First, single AGYWLHIV whether unmarried, separated, divorced or widowed may face greater social stigma or judgment when seeking modern contraceptive services due to the prevailing cultural and religious norms that disapprove of premarital sexual activity. This stigma can discourage them from accessing FP services, especially in semi-rural settings where privacy is limited and community ties are strong consistent with a previous study [43]. Second, single AGYW may lack consistent sexual partnerships, experience infrequent sex or perceive themselves to be at lower risk of unintended pregnancy, which can reduce their motivation to use MCs regularly. This explanation is consistent with a previous study conducted in Ethiopia where they also found lower unintended pregnancy risk perception among the AGYWLHIV was also associated with lower uptake of MCs [31]. The qualitative findings from this study further revealed that AGYWLHIV regardless of their marital status experience barriers such as lack of knowledge and misinformation, fear of modern contraceptive side effects and health concerns or fear of being judged (stigma) which further suppress MC use. Overall, this finding underscores the need for more inclusive, youth- and single women-friendly contraceptive services that reduce stigma and actively engage single AGYWLHIV.

In this AGYWLHIV population in semi-rural northern Uganda, alcohol users were more likely to use the MCs than their non-user counterparts. No specific previous study among AGYWLHIV is accessible to compare this association between alcohol use and the increased likelihood of MC use. Nevertheless, the 82.4% level of MC use among the alcohol drinkers in this Ugandan AGYWLHIV population is much higher than the 20% MC use level reported among non-pregnant sexually active New Zealand women 18–35 [59]. The positive association between alcohol use and increased likelihood of use of MCs concurs with a study conducted in Nigeria although not among the AGYWLHIV that also found that a unit increase in the intake of alcohol increased contraceptive use by 33% [60]. It however contradicts with the finding of the New Zealand study which found no significant association between binge drinking and use of effective contraceptive [59]. In this study, the AGYWLHIV who were alcohol drinkers were also found to be more likely to have frequent sexual activity, have a positive history of unintended pregnancy and have met their reproductive goals and thus did not desire anymore children. These factors underlying alcohol use could therefore be drivers for the MCs use to avert the risks associated with their alcohol use and high sexual activity. Others could be using the MCs to prevent repeat unintended pregnancy or prevent going beyond their reproductive goals. These findings imply that when AGYWLHIV who consume alcohol are identified through for example social history taking, interventions should be targeted at helping make effective MC choices and also reduce their sexual activities that may put them at risk of unintended pregnancies and violation of their reproductive goals.

We also found that AGYWLHIV in semi-rural northern Uganda who had HIV-negative male partners were more likely to use the MCs compared to their counterparts with HIV-positive male partners. This finding concurs with the previous studies among the AGYWLHIV aged 15–49 in Ethiopia and Ghana that also found that AGYWLHIV who knew their male partners were HIV-negative (discordant) were more likely to use MCs particularly the barriers methods such as the male condoms [50, 53]. These high rates of MC use particularly the male condoms among women in discordant relationships were probably undertaken to prevent sexual transmission of HIV to their HIV-negative partners as cited among the reasons for dual protection use in the qualitative component of this study. This finding implies that HIV care and FP programs should incorporate HIV testing for partners to identify the discordant partners and counsel them to use MCs particularly the barrier methods like the male condoms. The use of the barrier MCs like the male condom can also help in the prevention of sexual transmission of HIV and sexual transmitted infections (STIs) to the discordant partners.

We further found that AGYWLHIV in semi-rural northern Uganda with no fertility or reproductive desires were less likely to use the MCs. This finding contradicts a previous study in western Uganda and northern Uganda where they found lack of spouse fertility desires was associated with higher MC use [20, 34]. It however concurs with a previous study conducted in Ghana were they also found lower rates of MC use among AGYWLHIV with no desire to produce more children [52]. The low MC use among the AGYWLHIV with no fertility or reproductive desires can be partly explained by our study finding that they tended to have infrequent sex compared those with fertility desires and therefore saw no need to use the MCs. The implication is that HIV care and FP programs should ask AGYWLHIV for their fertility or reproductive goals and counsel those with no fertility or reproductive goals and yet sexually active even though infrequent to select an effective MCs including emergency contraceptive pills to prevent unplanned or unintended pregnancy.

Lastly, the qualitative findings in this study provide further insight into the socio-cultural barriers to MC use. Several AGYWLHIV who were not using modern contraceptives explained that they felt no need for it due to factors such as postpartum status, breastfeeding, lack of menstruation, or infrequent sexual activity from the absence or distance of a sexual partner circumstances under which they might still report having their needs “met.” Others expressed fears of side effects, male partner disapproval, stigma, or lack of accurate information (knowledge), which may lead them to discontinue use even after having initiated a method. These barriers concurs with the findings of similar previous studies conducted in Ethiopia and Ghana [50, 53]. These narratives suggest that some young women may perceive having a good access to modern contraceptives, while in practice, their contraceptive behavior does not align with sustained protection against unintended pregnancy. This finding underscores the need for context-sensitive approach to defining and measuring contraceptive access among the AGYWLHIV. It also emphasizes the importance of ongoing contraceptive health education, counselling, follow-up and support through integrated HIV and reproductive health services, to ensure that young women not only get aware but initiate using appropriate modern contraceptives in line with their reproductive intentions and health status.

Study strengths and limitations

This study demonstrates several strengths that contribute to the credibility and relevance of its findings. One of the main strengths lies in its use of a convergent mixed-methods design. This approach enabled the researchers to collect and analyze both quantitative and qualitative data concurrently, thereby providing a more holistic understanding of the modern contraceptive use among the AGYWLHIV in semi-rural northern Uganda. The quantitative component allowed for the measurement of prevalence and associated factors, while the qualitative component provided deeper insight into the personal, social, and structural reasons for non-use of modern contraceptives. The integration of both data types strengthened the overall validity of the findings.

Another notable strength of the study is its well-defined and specific target population. By focusing on AGYWLHIV who were accessing antiretroviral therapy (ART) services, the study addressed a particularly vulnerable and underserved group. These young women face heightened risks of unintended pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections, yet often remain neglected in reproductive health programming.

The stratified random sampling method used to recruit participants across different levels of public healthcare facilities namely a regional referral hospital, health centre IV, and health centre III further enhanced the representativeness of the sample. This sampling method choice reduced potential sampling bias and ensured that the perspectives of women receiving care at varying facility levels were included.

Despite these strengths, the study also had several limitations. As a cross-sectional study, it was unable to establish causal relationships between the identified factors and modern contraceptive use. The observed associations should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, all data were self-reported, which raises the possibility of social desirability bias, especially given the sensitive nature of the topics discussed, such as sexual behavior and modern contraceptive use. Participants may have overstated socially acceptable behaviors or understated stigmatized ones. This was minimized through use of structured questionnaire, use of research assistants with health background to collect the data, and training of the research assistants in deep probing techniques.

Lastly, data from the perspectives of the male sexual partners was not part of the current study. This affected the availability of data on male sexual partners and limiting their ability to be analyzed as predictors or mediators for modern contraceptive use. Future studies should include the perspectives of the male sexual partners on modern contraceptive use by their adolescent and youthful female partners living with HIV.

Conclusion

The modern contraceptive use among AGYWLHIV in semi-rural northern Uganda was found to be moderately high with a prevalence rate of 62.4%. This modern contraceptive prevalence level is significantly higher than the rates reported from similar studies in other regions of Uganda and SSA. However, despite this relatively high uptake, MC use is unevenly distributed among subgroups and were influenced by factors such as the woman’s religious affiliation, marital status, alcohol use, male partner’s HIV status and the woman’s fertility desires.

Furthermore, the study findings that AGYWLHIV’s socio-cultural circumstances and behaviours such as having a catholic faith, being single, lacking fertility desires, having an HIV negative male partner and using alcohol were the key influencers of their MC use implies that efforts to improve MC use in this population should engage the catholic church, target the single women, include reproductive goal planning and HIV prevention services for their discordant male partners.

The barriers to MC use identified through the qualitative component of this study such as partner disapproval, lack of FP knowledge or information, fear of MC side effects and health concerns, infrequent sexual activity from the absence or distance of the male partner, postpartum status, contraceptive stigma and discouraging sociocultural beliefs and practices calls for health education and empowerment of the AGYWLHIV and their male partners not only on MCs but the entire spectrum of SRH matters. Integrated HIV care and other SRH services may provide good avenue for addressing these barriers to MC use.

The study finding on the sociocultural barriers also calls for the engagement of community leaders, women leaders, extended family members including mothers’ in-law and cultural leaders to challenge sociocultural beliefs and practices that discourage women from family planning using MCs and accessing sexual reproductive health services. The community resources explored in this study including the mass media and the community health extension workers though not significant independent predictors are cheap, easily accessible and readily available media or channels that may use to engage the community leaders on HIV care, MCs and SRH services.

Lastly, the study found that AGYWLHIV’s mere awareness about the availability of the community-based resources or providers of FP services were not sufficient to influence their use of the modern contraceptives. While consistent with previous studies, this finding implies that efforts to improve MC uptake in this AGYWLHIV population should be targeted at other key barriers to MC use identified from the qualitative aspect of the study such as contraceptive stigma, misinformation, male partner disapproval and discouraging sociocultural norms.
