Engineering novel ceramic metal oxides with residual carbon for efficient sequestration of Auramine O from wastewater
Authors: Reem M. Alghanmi, Ehab A. Abdelrahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Auramine O is a hazardous cationic dye that threatens aquatic ecosystems by suppressing photosynthesis, and it poses health risks to humans through toxicity and bioaccumulation. Its chemical persistence allows it to remain in water bodies and enter food chains, which amplifies environmental and medical concerns. Here, we report a facile Pechini sol–gel route to synthesize CuO/MgCr2O4/C and CuO/MgCr2O4/MgO/C at 600 and 800 °C nanohybrids labeled MCC600 and MCC800, respectively, for efficient Auramine O removal. XRD confirms monoclinic CuO and cubic MgCr2O4 in MCC600 and reveals additional cubic MgO in MCC800 with average crystallite sizes of 60.97 nm and 75.35 nm, respectively, which reflects thermally driven grain growth. EDX verifies C, O, Mg, Cr, and Cu, with carbon decreasing and chromium increasing at higher temperature due to enhanced organic burnout and phase development. FE-SEM shows cauliflower-like agglomerates with fine nanospheres for MCC600 and a more uniform granular texture for MCC800, while HR-TEM reveals ultrafine quasi-spherical clusters for MCC600 and larger faceted polyhedral grains for MCC800. The capacities reach 442.48 mg/g for MCC600 and 298.51 mg/g for MCC800. Kinetic and thermodynamic analyses demonstrate pseudo-first-order behavior with exothermic spontaneous and physical adsorption that follows the Langmuir isotherm. Nearly complete acid-driven regeneration and stable multicycle reuse are achieved, and high performance is retained in real wastewater, which confirms suitability for practical treatment.

Introduction

Water contamination by organic dyes arises mainly from rapid growth of textile printing, paper, leather, cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and laboratory activities that release colored effluents into surface and ground waters1–4. These dyes are designed to be chemically stable and highly visible, which means that even small discharges can cause intense coloration and long-term persistence in aquatic systems. Poor wastewater treatment infrastructure and uncontrolled disposal further accelerate the spread of dye pollutants in natural water bodies. Organic dyes present significant risks to ecosystems as well as human health because they block light penetration, inhibit photosynthesis, and disturb oxygen balance in water5–7. Several dyes along with their degradation byproducts display toxic, mutagenic, or carcinogenic behavior, which makes long-term exposure dangerous for living organisms. Contaminated water can also affect food chains when dyes accumulate in plants, fish, and sediments8–10. Auramine O is a synthetic cationic dye widely utilized for coloring and as a biological stain and it is known to be particularly harmful. It can cause irritation to skin and eyes and prolonged exposure is associated with liver and kidney damage. In aquatic environments Auramine O is toxic to microorganisms and aquatic life and it resists natural degradation which leads to persistent pollution11. Several technologies have been developed to remove organic dyes from water, including membrane filtration12, adsorption13, coagulation/flocculation14, electrodialysis15, bioremediation16, and photocatalytic degradation17,18. Each method has its own advantages, but many suffer from high operational cost, generation of secondary waste, low selectivity, or slow treatment rates. Photocatalysis and membrane processes often require complex equipment19,20, while biological treatments are limited by the toxicity of dyes to microorganisms21. Conventional biological treatment approaches such as anaerobic digestion have been widely employed for organic wastewater remediation; however, their effectiveness toward synthetic dyes such as Auramine O remains limited. Many dyes exhibit strong chemical stability and resistance to biodegradation, which reduces microbial conversion efficiency and prolongs treatment time. Anaerobic treatments typically demand tight operational control and long retention times, and they can be hindered by dye toxicity, which suppresses microbial activity and reduces process efficiency22,23. Catalytic oxidation/reductive degradation routes have also been explored for persistent dyes; for example, noble-metal-modified semiconductors (e.g., Sn3O4@Au) can accelerate dye degradation by enhancing interfacial charge transfer24. However, such catalytic approaches often require added reagents and/or tight process control, which can increase operational complexity and cost. Among these methods, adsorption has gained wide attention because it is simple, efficient, and adaptable to different water matrices. It can remove dyes without producing harmful byproducts, and it allows recovery and reuse of the adsorbent. Adsorption also works effectively at low dye concentrations where many other methods become inefficient25. Nanotechnology provides materials with unique surface and structural features that enhance interaction with pollutants26–28. Metal oxide nanoparticles and their composites are especially attractive for dye adsorption because they combine chemical stability, high surface area, and tunable surface chemistry29–34. Composites formed with polymers or other metal oxides further improve adsorption by introducing multiple functional sites and by creating synergistic interactions that strengthen dye binding35–39. A wide range of synthesis strategies has been developed for preparing nanostructured metal oxides and ceramic-based materials, including hydrothermal and solvothermal methods40,41, co-precipitation42, solid-state reactions43, carbothermal reduction44, high-energy ball milling45, spark plasma sintering46, combustion synthesis47, and microwave-assisted heating48. Although these techniques can successfully produce crystalline nanomaterials, many of them suffer from drawbacks such as poor compositional homogeneity, broad particle-size distribution, high energy consumption, prolonged processing time, limited control over phase purity, or difficulties in scaling up. Microwave-assisted synthesis, for example, has been reported to accelerate phase formation through rapid electromagnetic heating49. However, challenges related to non-uniform heat distribution, hot-spot formation, and restricted control over defect chemistry may arise, particularly in complex multicomponent systems. In contrast, the Pechini sol–gel method provides molecular-level mixing of metal precursors via chelation and polymeric network formation, which enables precise stoichiometric control, homogeneous elemental distribution, reduced diffusion distances during calcination, and improved phase integration in multiphase nanohybrids. These features make the Pechini approach especially suitable for constructing well-dispersed oxide/carbon nanostructures with controlled composition and enhanced functional performance50. The Pechini sol–gel technique offers a potent route for producing uniform metal oxide nanostructures with controlled composition and high purity. It allows intimate mixing of metal ions at the molecular level, which leads to well-dispersed phases after calcination. This method also produces materials with high surface area and homogeneous distribution of active sites, which are essential for efficient adsorption51–53. Various adsorbents have been reported for Auramine O removal, including non-imprinted polymer54, activated carbon55, ZnS/Cu/activated carbon composite56, hydroxyapatite57, Fe3O458, crosslinked chitosan-kaolin composite59, and cellulose60. Although some of these materials show moderate capacities, such as non-imprinted polymers (203.99 mg/g) and hydroxyapatite (132.57 mg/g), many others, such as activated carbon, Fe3O4, and cellulose, display very low uptake. These limitations arise from low surface reactivity, limited porosity, weak dye affinity, and poor stability, which highlights the need for new advanced adsorbents with higher efficiency and durability. This work introduces novel CuO/MgCr2O4/C and CuO/MgCr2O4/MgO/C nanohybrids synthesized by the Pechini-assisted sol gel route with annealing at 600 and 800 °C for effective Auramine O removal. The combination of multiple oxide phases in asingle structure produces a synergistic interaction that enhances the number as well as diversity of active binding centers. This design leads to stronger dye interaction and higher adsorption performance compared with conventional single-phase materials. The selection of each component in the present nanohybrids was strategically designed to maximize adsorption efficiency through complementary physicochemical properties. CuO was selected due to its strong surface reactivity, abundance of surface hydroxyl groups, and well-documented affinity toward cationic organic pollutants through electrostatic interaction and surface complexation. MgCr2O4 spinel was incorporated because of its high structural stability, chemical durability, and resistance to dissolution under alkaline conditions, which ensures long-term adsorption performance and preserves active sites during repeated operation. In the material calcined at 800 °C, MgO was intentionally generated as an additional basic oxide phase capable of enhancing surface alkalinity and strengthening electrostatic attraction toward the cationic Auramine O molecules at pH values above pHPZC. Carbon, originating from controlled partial organic burnout during the Pechini process, was retained within the structure to increase electrical conductivity, provide π–π interaction domains for aromatic dye binding, and contribute to improved surface area and porosity. The integration of these phases into a single multiphase nanohybrid was therefore not arbitrary but was guided by the objective of combining oxide-based electrostatic adsorption, spinel structural robustness, basic surface enhancement, and carbon-assisted interfacial interactions within one synergistic framework. Beyond dye adsorption, multiphase oxide/carbon nanohybrids of this type are relevant to a broad spectrum of environmental and functional applications. Mixed metal oxides such as CuO and Mg-based spinels have been widely investigated for heavy metal ion sequestration, photocatalytic degradation of organic contaminants, antibacterial activity, and catalytic oxidation reactions due to their redox activity and surface hydroxyl functionality. The presence of carbon domains further extends potential applications to electrochemical energy storage, electrocatalysis, and sensing platforms by improving electrical conductivity and interfacial charge transfer. In addition, spinel-type oxides are known for their thermal stability and mechanical robustness, making them suitable for high-temperature catalytic systems and multifunctional ceramic coatings. Therefore, although the present study focuses on Auramine O removal, the designed CuO/MgCr2O4/C and CuO/MgCr2O4/MgO/C nanohybrids possess structural and physicochemical features that could enable their utilization in wastewater treatment, pollutant immobilization, heterogeneous catalysis, and advanced environmental remediation technologies. Future work will investigate additional applications of the prepared nanohybrids, particularly their catalytic activity and energy-related functionalities such as electrocatalysis and energy storage behavior, and the results will be presented in subsequent publications.

Materials and methods

Materials

Tartaric acid (C₄H₆O₆), chromium nitrate nonahydrate (Cr(NO3)3.9H2O), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), polyethylene glycol 400 (H(OCH2CH2)nOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl), magnesium nitrate hexahydrate (Mg(NO3)2.6H2O), potassium chloride (KCl), copper acetate monohydrate (Cu(CH3COO)2.H2O), and Auramine O dye (C17H21ClN3) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and employed directly without additional purification.

Synthesis of CuO/MgCr2O4/C and CuO/MgCr2O4/MgO/C nanohybrids

The synthesis route depicted in Fig. 1 was performed through a Pechini sol–gel strategy based on organic complexation and polymeric network formation. A chelating solution was first prepared through dissolving 35 g of tartaric acid in about 100 mL of deionized water. Separately, 20 g of Mg(NO3)2.6H2O, 20 g of Cr(NO3)3.9H2O, as well as 20 g of Cu(CH3COO)2.H2O were solubilized in 200 mL of deionized water to obtain a uniform mixed precursor solution. After that, the tartaric acid solution was then combined with the metal precursor solution with constant agitation for a duration of 30 min to ensure effective coordination between the metal ions and the organic ligand. After this step, 15 mL of polyethylene glycol 400 was introduced as a polycondensation facilitator, and the blend was heated at 200 °C under constant stirring until the solvents were fully removed and a consolidated gel-like mass was formed. The resulting dried material was ground into a powder and calcined for 3 h at 600 °C or 800 °C to generate the targeted nanohybrids. The product obtained at 600 °C was labeled MCC600 and corresponds to the CuO/MgCr2O4/C nanohybrid, while the material produced at 800 °C was labeled MCC800 and corresponds to the CuO/MgCr2O4/MgO/C nanohybrid.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the Pechini sol gel procedure utilized to produce CuO/MgCr2O4/C and CuO/MgCr2O4/MgO/C nanohybrids.

Schematic diagram of the Pechini sol gel procedure utilized to produce CuO/MgCr2O4/C and CuO/MgCr2O4/MgO/C nanohybrids.

Instrumentation

Crystal phase identification for all prepared samples was carried out applying an X-ray diffraction diffractometer (XRD, D8 Discover, Bruker, USA). Microstructural features as well as elemental signals were obtained applying a field emission scanning electron microscope equipped with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (FE-SEM/EDX, Quanta 250 FEG, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). High-resolution imaging was performed with a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HR-TEM, JEM 2100Plus, JEOL Ltd, Japan) to assess the fine nanostructure and particle characteristics. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded using a Nicolet IS10 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in the range of 400–4000 cm⁻1 to identify surface functional groups and to monitor changes before and after dye adsorption. Quantification of Auramine O in the treated solutions was conducted applying a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Cintra 3030, GBC, Australia).

Results and discussion

Characterization results

XRD

As shown in Fig. 2A, the XRD pattern of the MCC600 nanohybrid confirms the formation of monoclinic CuO that matches card No. JCPDS-00-005-0661 and cubic MgCr2O4 that matches card No. JCPDS-00-010-0351. The characteristic CuO reflections appear at 2θ = 32.56, 38.91, 46.35, 48.84, 58.23, 61.68, 66.37, 68.16, 72.45, and 75.20 and they are indexed to (110), (200), (− 1 1 2), (− 2 0 2), (202), (− 1 1 3), (− 3 1 1), (220), (311), and (004), respectively. The MgCr2O4 reflections occur at 2θ = 18.48, 30.34, 35.60, 37.11, 43.31, 53.68, 57.39, and 63.06 and they are indexed to (111), (220), (311), (222), (400), (422), (511), and (440), respectively. In Fig. 2B, the MCC800 nanohybrid exhibits the same CuO phase with monoclinic symmetry and card No. JCPDS-00-005-0661 together with the MgCr2O4 spinel phase with cubic symmetry and card No. JCPDS-00-010-0351 and it additionally reveals cubic MgO corresponding to card No. JCPDS-01-078-0430. The MgO peaks are observed at 2θ = 62.23 and 78.65 and they are indexed to (220) and (222), respectively. The crystallite size was calculated using the Debye–Scherrer equation68 employing all clearly resolved reflections belonging to the detected crystalline phases in each sample, and the final reported value represents the arithmetic average obtained from all selected peaks. Although EDX confirms the presence of elemental carbon, it does not define its bonding state; therefore, we do not claim carbon incorporation (doping) into the CuO or MgCr2O4 lattices. Instead, the carbon signal is attributed to a residual carbonaceous phase originating from incomplete decomposition of the Pechini polymeric matrix at 600 °C, while the lower carbon fraction at 800 °C reflects more effective organic burnout at higher temperature. This interpretation is consistent with Pechini-derived oxide/carbon composites reported in the literature69, where carbon-rich products form at 600 °C due to incomplete thermal decomposition of organic precursors, whereas 800 °C promotes more extensive removal of organic residues and a carbon-reduced composite.

Figure 4 presents the elemental mapping images of MCC600 (Fig. 4A) and MCC800 (Fig. 4B) nanohybrids, which provide clear evidence of the spatial distribution of the constituent elements within the synthesized structures. The carbon mapping further demonstrates that the carbon phase derived from the Pechini sol–gel process is not localized but rather well-dispersed throughout the oxide framework, which is essential for providing π–π interaction domains and enhancing interfacial contact with dye molecules. FE-SEM

As shown in Fig. 5A, the MCC600 nanohybrid is composed of strongly agglomerated particles that appear as irregular compact clusters with a cauliflower-like texture, and the surface is decorated with fine quasi-spherical nanoparticles. Figure 5B shows that MCC800 exhibits a more homogeneous granular morphology that is dominated by densely packed quasi-spherical nanoparticles with clearer interparticle necks and small intergranular voids. The difference between the two structures is mainly attributed to the higher thermal treatment for MCC800, which enhances the decomposition of the residual organic matrix from the Pechini sol–gel route and promotes improved crystallization and particle rearrangement, which produces a more uniform nanoparticle assembly, while MCC600 retains a less matured framework where incomplete burnout and limited diffusion favor the formation of coarser, irregular aggregates and stronger agglomeration.Fig. 5FE-SEM morphological features of (A) MCC600 and (B) MCC800 nanohybrids.

HR-TEM

Figure 6A shows MCC600 as an assembly of ultrafine quasi-spherical nanocrystallites that form highly agglomerated porous clusters. The gradual contrast and the blurred particle boundaries indicate a high fraction of very small particles, which is consistent with restrained crystal growth at the lower treatment condition. Figure 6B shows MCC800 with larger faceted polyhedral particles together with plate-like and ellipsoidal grains that are joined into dense aggregates. The clearer faceting and the larger particle size are attributed to enhanced atomic diffusion at the higher temperature, which promotes coalescence and sintering of neighboring nanocrystallites and converts the initially fine nuclei into more mature grains. The observed difference therefore reflects temperature-driven structural evolution of the Pechini-derived network, where more organic removal and improved crystallinity at the higher condition yield coarser, well-defined particles compared with the finer nanosphere-rich texture in MCC600.Fig. 6HR-TEM morphological features of (A) MCC600 and (B) MCC800 nanohybrids.

Optimization

Effect of solution pH on removal efficiency

Figure 8 shows that Auramine O removal is very low at pH 2, with MCC600 achieving 2.35% and MCC800 achieving 1.68%, while it becomes much higher at pH 10, where MCC600 reaches 96.77% and MCC800 reaches 64.58%.Fig. 8Influence of pH on the uptake efficacy of Auramine O utilizing MCC600 and MCC800 nanohybrids.

Influence of pH on the uptake efficacy of Auramine O utilizing MCC600 and MCC800 nanohybrids.

Mechanism of adsorption Figure 9 indicates pHPZC values of 7.32 for MCC600 and 8.55 for MCC800, which means that at pH of 2 the dye solution is below pHPZC and the surfaces are mainly positively charged. Under these conditions, electrostatic repulsion between the positively charged surface as well as the cationic dye suppresses adsorption. At pH 10, the solution is above pHPZC, and the surfaces become negatively charged, which promotes electrostatic attraction and a sharp increase in removal efficiency. Figure 10 summarizes these two regimes, and the higher efficiency of MCC600 at pH 10 suggests a stronger affinity and greater availability of effective adsorption sites under alkaline conditions.Fig. 9pHPZC of MCC600 and MCC800 nanohybrids.Fig. 10Removal mechanism of Auramine O using MCC600 and MCC800 nanohybrids.

pHPZC of MCC600 and MCC800 nanohybrids.

Removal mechanism of Auramine O using MCC600 and MCC800 nanohybrids.

FTIR analysis was performed before (Fig. 11A) and after (Fig. 11B) Auramine O adsorption on MCC600 as a representative example to elucidate the underlying adsorption mechanism. Figure 11 provides direct molecular-level insight into the adsorption mechanism of Auramine O onto MCC600 through simultaneous preservation of oxide lattice vibrations and emergence of dye-specific bands. The bands at 405, 474, 633, and 757 cm⁻1, retained before and after adsorption, correspond respectively to M–O lattice bending, Cu–O stretching of monoclinic CuO, and Cr–O and Mg–O vibrations of the MgCr2O4 spinel framework. The stability of these vibrations confirms that the crystalline oxide matrix remains structurally intact, indicating that dye removal proceeds via surface interaction rather than structural transformation or phase change. The slight shift from 888 to 881 cm⁻1, assigned to surface oxygenated/carbonate-type species, reflects modification of surface electronic density after adsorption, suggesting electrostatic interaction between negatively charged surface sites and the cationic Auramine O molecules. Similarly, the displacement of the C–O stretching band from 1116 to 1136 cm⁻1 indicates direct involvement of surface oxygen-containing functional groups in binding, most likely through hydrogen bonding or electrostatic attraction. The shift from 1440 to 1482 cm⁻1 is tentatively assigned to surface carbonate-related vibrations; however, this region can also overlap with contributions from other surface species such as CH3 bending modes and/or carboxylate (COO⁻) vibrations from oxygenated surface residues or adsorbed dye fragments. Therefore, the band displacement is interpreted more generally as evidence of surface functional-group perturbation after adsorption, consistent with electrostatic interaction under alkaline conditions (pH > pHPZC). More importantly, the appearance of new bands at 819 and 943 cm⁻1, assigned to aromatic C–H bending vibrations, and the strong aromatic skeletal vibration at 1592 cm⁻1, confirm immobilization of the dye’s conjugated chromophore onto the adsorbent surface. This indicates π–π stacking interactions between the aromatic rings of Auramine O and the carbon domains within MCC600. Additionally, the emergence of 1040 and 1323 cm⁻1 bands, corresponding to C–N stretching vibrations of the dye’s amine groups, together with the 1392 cm⁻1 CH₃ deformation band, confirms that the amine-containing moieties participate in surface binding. These interactions likely occur via electrostatic attraction and possible hydrogen bonding with surface hydroxyl groups64,66. Collectively, the preserved oxide lattice bands, the adsorption-induced shifts of surface functional-group vibrations, and the emergence of aromatic and amine-related dye bands in Fig. 11 demonstrate that Auramine O removal occurs through a synergistic mechanism dominated by electrostatic attraction between negatively charged oxide surfaces and the cationic dye, reinforced by π–π stacking interactions with carbon domains and hydrogen bonding with surface oxygen functionalities, without altering the crystalline structure of MCC600.Fig. 11FTIR spectra of MCC600 (A) before and (B) after adsorption of Auramine O dye.

FTIR spectra of MCC600 (A) before and (B) after adsorption of Auramine O dye.

Role of contact time in Auramine O uptake

Figure 12 indicates that Auramine O uptake increases quickly at the beginning and then slows as equilibrium is approached, which is consistent with rapid binding on readily available surface sites followed by a diffusion-limited stage as the number of vacant sites decreases. Mechanistically, the acidic eluent protonates surface oxygenated adsorption sites (M–O⁻/M–OH), converting them into neutral or positively charged groups (M–OH and M–OH₂⁺). This protonation competes directly with the cationic Auramine O molecules for the same binding domains, weakens electrostatic attraction, and promotes desorption through charge neutralization or charge reversal when pH becomes far below pHPZC. Therefore, acid-driven regeneration is primarily governed by competitive proton adsorption and disruption of electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions rather than chemical degradation of the dye. To support this interpretation, FTIR spectra were collected after the desorption step (figure omitted for brevity); the post-desorption spectra retain the characteristic M–O lattice vibrations, confirming preservation of the oxide framework and indicating that adsorption–desorption proceeds via largely reversible surface interactions.

Evaluation of Auramine O elimination from wastewater

Practical applicability was examined by treating actual laboratory wastewater collected from the students’ chemistry facilities at the College of Science, Imam Mohammad Ibn Saud Islamic University in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The sample was fortified with Auramine O to reach an initial concentration of 300 mg/L to mirror the single solute tests while keeping the native ionic background of the real environment unchanged. The characterized wastewater contained K⁺ (12.7 mg/L), Na⁺ (58.4 mg/L), Ca2⁺ (24.3 mg/L), Mg2⁺ (7.9 mg/L), NO₃⁻ (21.6 mg/L), Cl⁻ (71.5 mg/L), HCO₃⁻ (118.0 mg/L), SO₄2⁻ (46.2 mg/L), and PO₄3⁻ (4.6 mg/L), along with trace Cu (0.06 mg/L), Fe (0.23 mg/L), and Zn (0.11 mg/L), and no other detectable dyes besides the added Auramine O. Under the optimized conditions, MCC600 achieved an uptake capacity of 418.12 mg/g, and MCC800 reached 274.13 mg/g, which are marginally below the capacities measured in deionized water at the same starting concentration, 442.48 mg/g and 298.51 mg/g, respectively. The modest reduction is consistent with competition from dissolved ions and partial occupation of surface binding domains by matrix constituents, yet both nanohybrids remained highly effective, and MCC600 consistently delivered higher uptake, which supports their use for polishing dye-contaminated student laboratory effluents.

Conclusions

Despite the large number of adsorbents previously reported for Auramine O removal, most conventional materials suffer from limited adsorption capacity, insufficient structural stability, or single-phase compositions that provide a restricted variety of binding sites. Moreover, many studies focus primarily on adsorption performance without integrating controlled multiphase engineering to enhance synergistic surface interactions. Therefore, a clear research gap exists in the rational design of structurally integrated oxide/carbon nanohybrids capable of delivering both high adsorption capacity and long-term operational stability. The present work addresses this gap by constructing a multiphase CuO/MgCr2O4/C (MCC600) and CuO/MgCr2O4/MgO/C (MCC800) framework through a controlled Pechini sol–gel route, enabling synergistic electrostatic, hydrogen bonding, π–π, and surface coordination interactions within a single architecture. The structural analyses confirmed that thermal treatment controls phase development and particle growth, which in turn governs surface accessibility and adsorption efficiency. The superior performance of MCC600 arises from its finer nanostructure and higher density of exposed active sites compared with the more coarsened MCC800. The optimum adsorption conditions were achieved under an alkaline medium at pH 10 using 0.1 g nanohybrid in 150 mL dye solution with a preliminary Auramine O concentration of 300 mg/L at 298 K and equilibrium times of 60 min for MCC600 and 80 min for MCC800. Adsorption proceeds through a physical mechanism driven by favorable surface interactions and is well described by monolayer coverage and pseudo-first-order uptake behavior. The materials also show strong tolerance to competing ions and retain most of their capacity after repeated regeneration, which highlights their durability. Successful operation in real laboratory wastewater demonstrates that these nanohybrids are not limited to ideal conditions but can function in complex aqueous matrices. These outcomes indicate that the designed multiphase oxide/carbon framework offers a reliable and scalable option for treating dye-contaminated effluents and provides a foundation for further development of advanced nanostructured adsorbents. From a broader perspective, the findings of this study open new opportunities for tailoring multiphase ceramic–carbon nanostructures for advanced environmental remediation. Future investigations may focus on scaling-up synthesis, continuous-flow system evaluation, selective removal of mixed organic pollutants, and exploring catalytic or energy-related functionalities of these materials. Such developments could further expand the practical impact of the designed nanohybrids beyond batch adsorption systems.
