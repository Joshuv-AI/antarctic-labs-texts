The association between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases in middle-aged and older population in China: mediating role of depression
Authors: Xing Ming, Huiyi Zhang, Kaige Xue, Zhenghao Cui, Daoxin Wang, Zefan Wei, Shengli Gao, Kunbin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCurrent studies have not sufficiently explored the connection between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases, particularly the potential mediating effect of depression in this relationship. This study aims to clarify the interrelationships among the three variables.MethodsThis study utilized the 2018 CHARLS (China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study) dataset to examine the associations among nighttime sleep duration, depression, and digestive diseases. The analysis incorporated binary logistic regression, Spearman correlation analysis, sensitivity analysis, the restricted cubic spline method, subgroup analysis, and mediation effect analysis based on Baron and Kenny’s causal steps approach and the Karlson/Holm/Breen (KHB) method.ResultsBinary logistic regression analysis revealed that short sleep (< 6 h) was significantly associated with higher odds of digestive diseases compared to optimal sleep (6–8 h) (OR = 1.409, 95%CI: 1.179–1.683, P < 0.001). Restricted cubic spline analysis indicated that the lowest odds of digestive diseases occurred at approximately 7.8 h of nighttime sleep among middle-aged and older adults. In subgroup analyses, longer nighttime sleep duration was not significantly associated with lower odds of digestive diseases among individuals with hypertension, diabetes, or depression. Depression accounted for 20.98% of the association between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases. After full adjustment, depression still explained 17.26% of this association.ConclusionThis paper suggests a significant association between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases. Specifically, insufficient sleep time is associated with a higher prevalence of digestive diseases among middle-aged and elderly people. Furthermore, depression partially mediates the relationship between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases. The results are based on association analyses and do not imply causality. Therefore, the observed mediating effect should be interpreted as a statistical decomposition of associations rather than evidence of a causal pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27164-z.Keywords: nighttime sleep duration, digestive diseases, mediating role, depression, CHARLS

Introduction

Digestive diseases have become a critical issue in international public health, accounting for 13.5% of global hospitalizations [1, 2]. From 1990 to 2019, digestive disease incidence increased in China, mirroring trends in Western countries [3]. In China, the prevalence of constipation is reported to exceed 30% among the elderly population [4]. A recent study conducted in New Zealand found that 20% of the elderly participants suffered from lower digestive tract diseases [5]. Certain digestive diseases, such as infections and chronic gastritis, may lead to malignant tumors of the digestive system [6, 7]. At the same time, the enormous burden caused by these conditions is also evident in mortality data. In 2019 alone, 100,317 people in the United States died from non-malignant digestive system diseases [8]. Early intervention could reduce digestive disease incidence, lower progression risk to malignancy, and alleviate associated economic burdens. However, in this context, modifiable risk factors for the prevention of digestive diseases remain understudied.

Evidence is increasingly suggesting a complex association between nighttime sleep duration and the occurrence of specific digestive disorders [9]. At the mechanistic level, a rat animal experiment confirmed that sleep deprivation directly damages the integrity of the gastric mucosa [10]. This may explain epidemiological associations between short sleep or poor sleep quality and digestive diseases in humans. As is well known, crcadian rhythms regulate digestive functions including enzyme secretion, nutrient absorption, and gastrointestinal motility [11], providing a biological basis for the sleep-digestive disease link. A Taiwanese cohort study found that > 7 h/day sleep was associated with lower gastric ulcer incidence compared to < 7 h/day [12]. Another study indicated that long sleep (≥ 9 h) was associated with an increased risk of colorectal and gastric cancers compared to 7 h of sleep [13]. These studies highlight the need to examine the association between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases and identify optimal sleep duration.

Depression is a major health challenge for middle-aged and older adults globally [14–16]. The majority of evidence indicates that sleep duration is correlated with the presence of depressive symptoms. A cohort study found that short sleep (< 6 h/day) increases depression risk [17]. Another study reported a U-shaped relationship: both short and long sleep increase depression risk [18]. Furthermore, a previous review study showed a bidirectional association between sleep and depression: poor sleep quality exacerbates depressive symptoms, and depression also disrupts sleep [19]. There is also ample evidence suggesting that depression is involved in the pathogenesis of gastrointestinal diseases. A recent Mendelian randomization analysis found that genetically predicted major depressive disorder is linked to an elevated risk of gastroesophageal reflux disease, irritable bowel syndrome, and peptic ulcer disease [20]. A meta-analysis further supports this, indicating that individuals with depressive symptoms are more susceptible to inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) compared to control [21]. Another cohort study reported higher gastroesophageal reflux disease incidence among depressed individuals [22]. A recent study found that shorter nighttime sleep duration significantly mediates 8.76% of the depression-chronic digestive disease relationship [23]. Thus, this study will explore the association between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases, as well as the mediating role of depression.

To date, population-based evidence regarding the indirect effect of nighttime sleep duration on digestive diseases remains limited. Accordingly, this study aims to examine the relationship between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases, as well as whether this association is mediated by depressive symptoms. These findings may inform digestive health strategies for middle-aged and elderly population in China.

Methods

Study population

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a nationally representative survey of middle-aged and older adults in China. A multistage probability sampling design selected participants from 450 communities across 150 counties in 28 provinces [24]. All participants provided informed consent by signing the appropriate forms, and copies of these documents were retained by both the participants and the research team. The study collected comprehensive data through standardized questionnaires covering sociodemographic characteristics, psychological status, and health conditions. These reliable measurement indicators enable the study to thoroughly explore the association between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases in this population [25].

The data employed in this study were obtained from the 2018 CHARLS database. The criteria for including respondents were as follows: (1) complete data on nighttime sleep duration, stomach or other digestive diseases and aged 45 years or older; (2) comprehensive information regarding sex, marital status, residency, education, annual income, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, diabetes, depression, and Activity of Daily Living (ADL) disability. Ultimately, 5,160 respondents were included in the analysis, as illustrated in detail in Fig. 1.

Flow diagram of participants included in the study

Main variables

Nighttime sleep duration

Nighttime sleep duration was evaluated through self-reported responses to the following inquiry: during the past month, how many hours of actual sleep did you get at night (average hours for one night)? Participants nighttime sleep duration was categorized into three groups: short (< 6 h), moderate (6–8 h), and long (> 8 h). This classification aligns with that used in prior studies of similar populations [26]. While alternative cut-offs (e.g., ≤ 5 / 6–8 / ≥9 h) exist in relevant meta-analyses [27], but given intermediate values (e.g., 5.5, 8.5 h) in data, thresholds at 5–9 h would introduce misclassification bias.

Digestive diseases

Digestive diseases were identified by self-reported physician diagnosis. Participants were asked: “Have you been diagnosed with stomach or other digestive diseases (except for tumor or cancer) by a doctor?” Those who responded “yes” were classified as having digestive diseases. In this study, digestive diseases was operationalized as a binary variable, categorized as either “no” or “yes” [28].

Depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CES-D) scale. Scores on the scale range from 0 to 30. Items are scored from 0 to 3 based on frequency, with response options including “rarely or none of the time”, “some or a little of the time”, “occasionally or a moderate amount of the time”, and “most or all of the time”. Two items “I felt hopeful about the future” and “I was happy” are opposite assignments. A cutoff score of 10 was used, whereby participants with a total score ≥ 10 were classified as depressed, whereas those scoring below this threshold were deemed non-depressed. This measure has demonstrated reliability and appropriateness for assessing depression in China’s aging population [29].

Covariates

The covariates in the present study were selected based on previous literature. Specifically, the present study adjusted for age (< 65 years and ≥ 65 years), gender (male and female), marital status (married and others), residency (urban and rural), education level (primary school or below, middle school, high school or above), and annual income (in five tiers from ≤ 5,000 to > 50,000 yuan). The smoking and drinking status were categorized into three distinct groups. Hypertension, diabetes, depression, and activities of daily living (ADL) disability were all dichotomized into yes or no. Data were collected by uniformly trained investigators using a standardized questionnaire.

Participants who were separated, divorced, widowed, or never married were classified as the “others” group. In contrast, those who were married with their spouse present or temporarily living apart from their spouse due to reasons such as work commitments were categorized as the “married” group [30]. The educational attainment was determined by the question, “What is the highest level of education you have now (not including adult education)?”. Educational attainment was stratified into three distinct tiers: (1) Primary school and below, which includes individuals with no formal education, those who have incomplete primary schooling but possess literacy skills, as well as those who have undergone home schooling or completed elementary school; (2) Middle school; and (3) High school or above, a category that encompasses high school diplomas, vocational training certificates, associate degrees, bachelor’s degrees, master’s degrees, and doctoral degrees. According to the classification put forth by Jiang et al. [31], annual income was categorized into five groups: ≤ 5,000 yuan, 5,001–10,000 yuan, 10,001–30,000 yuan, 30,001–50,000 yuan, and > 50,000 yuan. Hypertension or diabetes was defined as self-reported hypertension or diabetes diagnosed by a doctor. The assessment of Activities of Daily Living (ADL) disability was evaluated using combined metrics from both the Basic (BADL) and Instrumental (IADL) activities of daily living scales. This validated approach effectively identifies functional impairment in older adults [32, 33]. BADL evaluated six essential functions (bathing dressing, indoor mobility, toileting, eating, and using the toilet), while IADL measured five complex tasks (doing housework, cooking, shopping, managing money, and taking medication). Each activity was scored 0 (“independent”) or 1 (“difficulty/worse”), with total scores (0–11). A threshold score of 1 was applied for classifying ADL disability (ADL ≥ 1: ADL disability; ADL = 0: no ADL disability) [34].

Statistical analysis

Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages. For comparative analyses, the chi-square test or the Mantel-Haenszel test was employed. Collinearity among explanatory variables was examined using Spearman correlation analysis, while multicollinearity was assessed via the variance inflation factor (VIF), respectively (Table S1 and S2 in supplementary). Binary logistic regression models were constructed to assess the association between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases. The first model (Model 1) was unadjusted. The second (Model 2) was progressively adjusted for sociodemographic factors: age, sex, marital status, residency, education, and income. And Model 3 further adjusted for behavioral and health-related factors: smoking, drinking, hypertension, diabetes and ADL disability based on the adjustments made in Model 2. Depression was not included in main models as it was hypothesized as a mediator, not confounder. Adjusting for a mediator in the outcome model could introduce collider stratification bias and underestimate the total effect of sleep on digestive diseases. Therefore, Model 3 in this study refers to adjustment for all covariates except depression. Additionally, an additional model adjusting for all covariates including depression was constructed, as presented in Supplementary Table S3. To model the potential nonlinear relationship between continuous nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were employed. To determine the optimal model specification, RCS models with 3, 4, and 5 knots placed at Harrell’s recommended percentiles were compared. Model fit was assessed by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The 4-knot model demonstrated the best fit (AIC = 3788.56). Subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by age, sex, marital status, residency, hypertension, diabetes, depression, and activities of daily living (ADL) disability to investigate the association between nighttime sleep and the risk of digestive diseases across different populations. Additionally, an interaction test was performed to further explore these relationships. Mediation analysis was performed to evaluate the role of depression in the nighttime sleep duration-digestive disease relationship using Baron and Kenny’s causal steps approach and the KHB method. To estimate the total effect of nighttime sleep duration on digestive diseases and the indirect effect mediated by depression, a directed acyclic graph (DAG) was constructed based on prior knowledge and descriptive statistics (Supplementary Fig. 1). The minimal sufficient adjustment set for estimating the total effect included age, sex, marital status, residency, education, and annual income, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, diabetes and ADL disability. The set of covariates adjusted for in the mediation analysis to identify the indirect effect was consistent with the total effect, as both required controlling for all measured common causes of the exposure, mediator, and outcome. To visually illustrate the hypothesized relationships among nighttime sleep duration, depressive symptoms, and digestive diseases, we constructed a conceptual framework (Supplementary Fig. 2). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), R (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and Stata (version 17.0; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). The threshold for statistical significance was set at P values less than 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 showed the characteristics of the study participants. The study included 5,160 middle-aged and older adults, of whom 12.2% reported digestive diseases in Table 1. Participants with digestive diseases were younger, more often female, less educated, and had lower income. Evidently, compared with participants who without digestive diseases, those with digestive diseases exhibited significantly higher prevalence rates of depression (10.0% vs. 15.5%). Sleep duration patterns also differed markedly. Short sleep (< 6 h) showed higher digestive disease prevalence (14.9%) compared to optimal sleep (6–8 h: 10.9%) or long sleep (> 8 h: 10.4%) (P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed for sex, marital status, residency, annual income, smoking status, hypertension, or diabetes (all P > 0.05). These findings may highlight the potential roles of depression, and short sleep in digestive disorders.

This analytical sample (n = 5,160) represented only 26% of the original CHARLS 2018 participants. Compared to excluded individuals, those included were slightly older, more likely female, rural, less educated, and had higher depression prevalence (Supplementary Table S4). This suggests potential selection bias that may limit the generalizability of prevalence estimates. Inverse-probability-of-selection weighting (IPSW) was performed to evaluate potential selection bias. The weighted odds ratios (sleep < 6 h: OR = 1.441, 95% CI 1.210–1.717, P < 0.001; sleep > 8 h: OR = 0.978, 95% CI 0.705–1.355, P = 0.892) were nearly unchanged from the unweighted estimates, supporting the robustness of the findings.

Binary logistic regression analysis

In Table 2, Binary logistic regression analyses revealed the association between nighttime sleep duration (continuous) and digestive diseases among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. In unadjusted model (Model 1), longer nighttime sleep duration was associated with lower odds of digestive diseases (OR = 0.904, 95%CI: 0.865–0.944, P < 0.001). This association remained robust after adjusting for sociodemographic factors (Model 2: OR = 0.903, 95%CI: 0.865–0.944, P < 0.001) and full adjustment (Model 3: OR = 0.910, 95%CI: 0.871–0.951, P < 0.001).

When analyzing sleep duration categorically using optimal sleep (6–8 h) as the reference, short sleep (< 6 h) was consistently associated with a higher odds ofdigestive diseases across all models. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), the association of short sleep (< 6 h) and digestive diseases statistically significant (OR = 1.409, 95%CI: 1.179–1.683, P < 0.001). For long sleep (> 8 h), no significant association was observed (OR = 0.954, 95%CI: 0.687–1.324, P = 0.777). It is important to note that the analysis for the long sleep group (n = 452) had limited statistical power (approximately 42% to detect a moderate effect). Therefore, this result should be interpreted as inconclusive. rather than evidence of no association.

Sensitivity analysis

To address the potential confounding influence of distinct pathological entities within the broad “digestive diseases” category, a sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding participants who reported a diagnosis of cancer or liver disease from the original sample. Results were consistent with primary analysis. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), short sleep duration continued to show a significant association with digestive diseases compared to optimal sleep duration (6–8 h) (< 6 h) (OR = 1.358, 95% CI: 1.127–1.636, P = 0.001). The association for long sleep duration (> 8 h) remained non-significant (OR = 0.938, 95% CI: 0.669–1.317, P = 0.714) (Supplementary Table S5). This suggests the association is robust and not driven by cancer or liver disease.

Nonlinear analysis

Restricted cubic splines were employed to examine the potential nonlinear association between continuous nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases. After comparing models with 3, 4, and 5 knots, the 4-knot model (AIC = 3788.56) provided the best fit and was used for the final analysis (Fig. 2). This analysis confirmed overall association was significant (P for overall = 0.002); nonlinearity was not significant (P for nonlinear = 0.147). The odds of digestive diseases decreased with increasing sleep duration, reaching a nadir (point of minimum risk) at approximately 7.8 h of nighttime sleep.

Subgroup analyses and interaction test

Subgroup analyses and interaction test were further conducted to explore the relationship between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases, with detailed results visualized in Fig. 3. The subgroup analyses were stratified by age, sex, marital status, residency, hypertension, diabetes, depression and ADL disability. The P values for interaction presented here test for heterogeneity of the odds ratio on the multiplicative scale.

Associations varied across subgroups. Obviously in adults aged ≥ 65 years, longer sleep showed a clearer inverse association (OR = 0.877, 95%CI: 0.817–0.941, P < 0.001) compared to < 65 years individuals (P for interaction = 0.054). Meanwhile, women showed stronger association (OR = 0.914, 95%CI: 0.863–0.969, P = 0.002) than men. Non-depression individuals were linked to lower odds of digestive diseases (OR = 0.904, 95%CI: 0.844–0.968, P < 0.004), whereas no clear pattern emerged among those with depression. Similarly, no significant associations were observed in participants with hypertension (P = 0.099) and diabetes (P = 0.798). Married individuals (OR = 0.938, P = 0.009) and rural residents (OR = 0.935, P = 0.005) showed stronger association. These findings probably highlight the importance of age, sex, psychological factors, and metabolic health in modulating the sleep-digestive disease relationship. For readers to quantitatively assess the heterogeneity of effects across subgroups, the stratum-specific odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals are provided numerically in Supplementary Table S6.

Mediation analysis of depression

To further explore whether depression mediates the association between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases, two mediation models (Model 4 and 5) were constructed. These models specified digestive diseases as the outcome, nighttime sleep duration as the independent variable, and depression as the mediator. Model 4 included only sleep duration and depression (Table 3). This model demonstrated a significant total effect of nighttime sleep duration on digestive diseases (β = -0.099, P < 0.001). Decomposition of this effect revealed a direct effect of -0.078 (P < 0.001) and an indirect effect through depression of -0.020 (P < 0.001), indicating that depression mediated 20.98% of this association. Model 5 expanded upon Model 4 by incorporating all control variables. Following adjustment, the total effect was reduced to -0.090 (P < 0.001), consisting of a direct effect of -0.074 (P = 0.001) and an indirect effect of -0.015 (P < 0.001). The proportion mediated by depression was found to be 17.26%. The adjusted mediation pathways are illustrated in Fig. 4, where the total effect (path c) is recorded as -0.090. Nighttime sleep duration negatively predicted depression (path a: β = -0.180), which subsequently served as a positive predictor for digestive diseases (path b: β = 0.394). The direct impact of sleep on digestive diseases, after accounting for the mediator, was measured at -0.074 (path c’). The significance observed in both paths a and b, along with the remaining significant direct effect (c’), confirms that depression acts as a partial mediator.

Discussion

This study is the first to utilize the nationally representative data from the 2018 CHARLS to systematically investigate the relationship between nighttime sleep duration, depressive symptoms, and digestive diseases among middle-aged and older adults in China. The findings suggest that a nighttime sleep duration of 7.8 h is associated with the lowest estimated odds of digestive diseases. Subgroup analyses showed that long sleep duration was not clearly associated with lower odds of digestive diseases among participants with hypertension, diabetes, or depression. Mediational analyses further indicated that depression partially mediated the association between sleep duration and digestive diseases, accounting for 17.26% of the total effect even after fully adjusting for covariates.

The results showed that 12.20% of participants reported digestive diseases, with over half of these also exhibiting depressive symptoms, aligning with prior epidemiological data [35–38]. In the adjusted model 3, sleep duration of < 6 h was significantly associated with higher odds of digestive diseases compared to 6–8 h. This finding is consistent with research linking short sleep to altered gut microbiota composition, such as reduced Sutterella abundance, which may negatively impact digestive function [39]. While Ananthakrishnan et al. suggested that both short sleep (< 6 h) and long sleep (> 9 h) increase the risk of ulcerative colitis [40], in the present study, sleep duration > 8 h did not show a significant correlation. This may be due to limited statistical power (approximately 42%) for the long-sleep group (n = 452) or the aggregation of > 8 h into a single category, potentially attenuating effects specific to very long sleep (e.g., > 9 h) [41]. Therefore, this null finding for long sleep should be interpreted cautiously.

The relationship between sleep duration and digestive diseases appears to be modified by several factors. The association was more pronounced in adults aged ≥ 65 years and in women. In older adults, this may relate to age-related reductions in slow-wave sleep [42] and increased gastrointestinal sensitivity [43]. For women, the observed results might be partially explained by the modulatory role of estrogen on intestinal physiology, including its analgesic or anti-inflammatory properties [44]. Notably, the association was not significant in individuals with hypertension, diabetes, or depression. These conditions are characterized by interconnected biological mechanisms, such as chronic inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and gut microbiota dysbiosis [45–48], which may overshadow or interact with the effects of sleep duration on digestive health. These findings have important clinical implications, as while sleep optimization is generally beneficial for digestive health, patients with these diseases may require comprehensive interventions to address both underlying conditions and digestive diseases.

When interpreting these findings, it is also important to acknowledge that nighttime sleep duration is not an isolated behavior but is influenced by a broader social and health context. Factors such as marital status and place of residence can significantly shape sleep patterns. For instance, as highlighted in research on health disparities [49], unmarried older adults, particularly those living alone in rural areas, may experience poorer health outcomes, potentially due to a lack of social support and increased stress. Such circumstances could disrupt sleep and indirectly affect digestive health. Similarly, functional limitations (ADL disability) and physical pain or discomfort, which are more prevalent in aging populations, are well-documented disruptors of nighttime sleep [29, 50, 51]. While our analyses adjusted for marital status, residency, and ADL disability, the potential role of unmeasured factors like chronic pain as a common cause of sleep disturbance, depressed mood, and gastrointestinal symptoms [51] underscores the complexity of these relationships and warrants further investigation.

Mechanistically, the link between sleep and digestive diseases appears both direct and indirect. Sleep duration is associated with depression, with a meta-analysis confirming a U-shaped relationship [52]. Sleep disorders can heighten inflammatory responses [53] and dysregulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to increased cortisol and pro-inflammatory cytokines, which may exacerbate mood disturbances and impair gut function [54]. Inappropriate sleep patterns may increase depression risk, which in turn can elevate the incidence of digestive diseases. Depression is known to be correlated with a higher risk of aggressive inflammatory bowel disease [55] and may lead to autonomic nervous system dysfunction (e.g., vagus nerve abnormalities) or gut microbiota imbalance, thereby triggering digestive pathologies [56, 57]. The findings suggest that depression mediates the relationship between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases. Collectively, these results underscore the potential importance of addressing sleep and psychological health in efforts aimed at reducing the burden of digestive system diseases.

Limitation

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. we categorize them into three areas: analytical, data-related, and survey design limitations.

Analytical limitations

A primary analytical constraint stems from the cross-sectional design, which precludes any causal inference or definitive conclusions about directionality. While the Baron and Kenny steps and KHB method are widely used to examine mediating mechanisms in cross-sectional data, they cannot establish the temporal precedence required for causal mediation. The hypothesized pathway—where sleep duration influences digestive diseases partly through depression—is plausible based on longitudinal mechanisms [58, 59]; however, in clinical practice, reverse causation is equally possible. Chronic digestive symptoms such as nighttime pain or reflux are known to interfere with sleep quality and are frequently associated with depressed mood [50, 60]. Furthermore, these mediation methods are both assumed no unmeasured exposure-outcome, exposure-mediator and mediator-outcome confounding.

Despite DAG-informed covariate adjustment, residual confounding remains a concern. For example, chronic pain is a common factor of sleep disturbance, depressed mood, and gastrointestinal symptoms [29, 50, 51]. Chronic pain can directly disrupt nighttime sleep and induce depressive symptoms, while also being a symptom of digestive diseases, which may open a backdoor path from depression to digestive diseases, inflating the b-path (depression→digestive diseases) and consequently the estimated proportion mediated. Previous studies suggest body mass index (BMI) is related to sleep-disordered, gastroesophageal reflux, and depressive symptoms [61, 62]. It likely confounds both the exposure–mediator and mediator–outcome relationships. Dietary habits—particularly low-carbohydrate diets and overall dietary patterns involving fiber intake, meal regularity, and ultra-processed food consumption—have been implicated in sleep regulation, depressive symptoms, and systemic inflammation, pathways also central to digestive pathology [63, 64]. These unmeasured confounders suggest that the 17.26% mediated proportion is likely an upper-bound estimate; the true effect may be smaller under fuller confounding control. Therefore, studies with richer phenotyping and causal mediation methods are needed to robustly test the directionality and causal structure of this mediated relationship in future.

Data-related limitations

Data-related limitations primarily concern measurement and variable definitions. Nighttime sleep duration was assessed using a single self-reported item, a common approach in large-scale studies like CHARLS [65]. However, self-reports are subject to recall bias and may not fully capture sleep architecture or quality (e.g., sleep latency, wake-after-sleep-onset, daytime napping, chronotype or sleep apnoea). Validation studies show that single-item self-reports overestimate objective short sleep by ≈ 40% [66]. This non-differential misclassification would likely bias the observed associations between sleep duration and digestive diseases toward the null, meaning the reported odds ratios might be conservative estimates of the true effects. In mediation analysis, such measurement error could also bias the decomposition of total effects, potentially inflating the depression-mediated path. Therefore, readers should interpret the findings of this study with caution.

Similarly, digestive diseases were identified based on self-reported physician diagnoses, a method commonly used in CHARLS-based studies [28, 67], but still subject to recall bias. A more substantive constraint arises from the breadth of the outcome definition. The category “stomach or other digestive diseases (except for tumor or cancer)” without distinguishing between subtypes such as peptic ulcer disease, inflammatory bowel disease, or functional gastrointestinal disorders [67]. Consequently, this broad outcome category may mask differential associations between sleep duration and specific digestive disorders. Future research employing clinically verified and subclassified endpoints is warranted to explore these potential heterogeneities.

Survey design limitations

Finally, limitations inherent to the survey design must be acknowledged. Our analytical sample (n = 5,160) represented only 26% of the original CHARLS 2018 participants. Compared to excluded individuals, those included were more likely to be female, rural, less educated, and had a higher prevalence of depression (Supplementary Table S4), indicating potential selection bias that could limit the generalizability of prevalence estimates. However, inverse-probability-of-selection weighting (IPSW) yielded nearly unchanged estimates for the sleep-digestive disease association, supporting the robustness of this core finding. Furthermore, the study lacked data on several important factors that could influence both sleep and digestive health, such as specific medication use (e.g., proton pump inhibitors, antidepressants), detailed chronotype information, and objective measures of gastrointestinal pathology. Future research employing longitudinal designs, clinically verified and subclassified disease endpoints, and objective sleep measures is warranted to confirm and extend these findings.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides empirical evidence for an association between nighttime sleep duration and digestive diseases among middle-aged and older adults in China, and suggests that depression may partly explain this relationship. Despite certain limitations, it offers a useful direction for future research. Subsequent studies with improved designs and more detailed measurements are needed to further clarify these relationships. Future research could explore whether interventions targeting sleep and mental health contribute to better digestive health outcomes in this population.
