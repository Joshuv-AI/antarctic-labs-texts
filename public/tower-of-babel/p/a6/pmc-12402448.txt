Pharmacokinetic analysis of selective TRPV2 inhibitor SET2 in rats
Authors: Linda Bartosova, Gabriel Doka, Eva Kralova, Peter Balis, Ulrika Dulova, Kristina Ferenczyova, Andrej Kovac, Juraj Piestansky, Tomas Rajtik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The TRPV2 channel, which regulates calcium transients, has emerged as a potential therapeutic target in various diseases, including cardiovascular injury, neurodegeneration, and cancer. However, the absence of selective inhibitors has limited functional studies. Here, we report the in vivo pharmacokinetic profile of SET2, a selective TRPV2 inhibitor. Wistar rats received a single intraperitoneal dose of 25 mg/kg, and plasma and urine samples were analyzed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry. SET2 reached a peak plasma concentration of 1428 ± 270 ng/ml (≈ 3.55 ± 0.67 µM) within 2 min, followed by a short mean residence time (70.5 ± 5.7 min). Approximately 4.24% of the administered dose was excreted unchanged in urine over 48 h, with renal clearance of 0.0097 ml/min. SET2 caused no significant changes in heart rate, blood pressure, or ECG parameters during peak levels and up to 2 h post-injection. Routine plasma markers remained stable, although a transient increase in plasma troponin I was observed. Our data suggest that SET2 has high plasma bioavailability, limited tissue distribution, and is rapidly cleared. While acute cardiovascular effects were minimal, the troponin I elevation warrants further safety evaluation in chronic studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-13891-7.

Introduction

Transient receptor potential vanilloid 2 (TRPV2) channels are members of transient receptor potential (TRP) family of non-selective ion channels with permeability for a wide range of cations. TRPV2 has particularly high calcium conductance and can be activated upon a number of cellular and environmental stimuli – mechanical stretch, noxious heat (> 52 °C), hypoosmolarity-induced cell stretch, and endogenous/exogenous chemical compounds1–4. TRPV2 is distributed across a wide range of tissues and organs, including the myocardium, lungs, the brain, or immune cells. The predominant location of TRPV2 is in the cytoplasm where TRPV2 resides on the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum5,6 but upon stimulation, a significant portion of TRPV2 translocates into the plasma membrane, which has been linked to pathological conditions7,8. At the physiological level, TRPV2 interplays with several signalling pathways to maintain homeostasis. TRPV2 is known to regulate the structure and function of the cardiovascular system and is likely critical for heart development, as TRPV2-deficient hearts showed impaired cardiac function and structural changes9. TRPV2 has been shown to play a significant role in the immune system, particularly in the differentiation, migration, and phagocytosis of macrophages10–12cytokine production and T-cell activation13.

The role of TRPV2 has been implicated in several diseases, highlighting it as a promising therapeutic target for drug development. In the cardiovascular system, TRPV2 has been initially investigated in relation to dilated cardiomyopathy associated with Duchenne muscular dystrophy14; Since then, multiple studies have emerged describing its role in myocardial infarction, ischemia/reperfusion injury or diabetic cardiomyopathy15–18. More recently, TRPV2 was identified as a potential oncogenic protein with studies investigating its role in tumorigenesis. TRPV2 has been linked to tumor invasiveness and metastasis by promoting cell migration and mediating cell adhesion, contributing to the progression of multiple types of cancer, including breast cancer, melanoma, hepatocarcinoma, and gastric cancer19–22. Mounting evidence supports the status of TRPV2 as a potential marker of malignancy. Moreover, TRPV2 is important for neuroinflammation. It has been shown that oxygen-glucose deprivation enhanced TRPV2 expression in activated microglia whose overactivation may contribute to the pathology of ischemic stroke23,24. In lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammation of BV-2 microglia and middle cerebral artery occlusion and reperfusion injury, plumbagin, a TRPV2 inhibitor, suppressed the inflammation and ameliorated brain injury25.

For a better understanding of TRPV2 and its exact role in the pathogenesis of diseases, the use of selective agonist and antagonist in experimental settings is crucial. However, experimental interventions that are used in the vast majority of studies lack selectivity or have affinity for multiple off-target receptors. 2-aminoethoxydiphenyl borate (2-APB), cannabidiol (CBD), or probenecid are among the non-selective agonists of TRPV2 that exhibited multiple effects regarding TRPV2 potentiation26–28. On the other hand, ruthenium red, tranilast, SKF96365, valdecoxid, murraxocin, or piperlongumine are experimental TRPV2 antagonists that have been used in a number of studies striving for targeted TRPV2 inhibition27,29–32. However, a common characteristic of these compounds is their low affinity, requiring very high concentrations to inhibit TRPV2. To date, selective inhibition of TRPV2 is severely limited, though increasing number of new potential drug probes like SET2 33, IV2-1 12 or plumbagin25 have been identified. SET2 has been among the first reported selective inhibitors of TRPV2 33 with an IC50 of 0.46 µM (Fig. 1). It is important that SET2 exhibits no sensitivity to TRPV1 which is structurally the most similar to TRPV2 (TRPV1 possesses up to 43% amino acid sequence identity in the transmembrane domains with TRPV2) as well as TRPV3 and TRPV4 33. In vitro, SET2 inhibited EPEC-1 induced cell death by targeting TRPV2, which is critical for early calcium influx that leads to caspase-4 activation in EPEC-1-infected SNU-C5 cells33. Since the discovery of SET2, another potentially selective molecule has emerged. Dithiolane IV2-1 has been reported to inhibit TRPV2-mediated calcium influx in macrophages, as well as their phagocytosis and migration, without affecting TRPV1, TRPV3 or TRPV4 12. The most recently published molecule is plumbagin, an allosteric modulator of TRPV2, which exhibited > 14-fold selectivity over TRPV1, TRPV3 and TRPV4, with IC50 of 0.085 µM25. In the current study, we aimed to describe the pharmacokinetics of SET2, a selective TRPV2 inhibitor which holds the biggest potential for the orphan drug target. TRPV2 pharmacology remains elusive and scarce at best, largely due to the challenges of precise targeting in tissues where it coexists with other isoforms.

Methods

Animals and study design

Male Wistar rats (250 g ± 20 g; 8-week-old) were obtained from Dobra Voda, Slovak Republic and housed under stable conditions (temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, humidity of 45–65%) with access to drinking water ad libitum and fed with regular chow KZ-P/M (Dobra Voda, Slovak Republic). Animals were randomly divided into groups as displayed in the Table 1, based on the type of collected sample (blood/urine) and collection times. All animals were fasted for 24 h prior to the treatment which was administered as a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 25 mg/kg SET2 (molecular weight: 402.44 g/mol) (Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK) dissolved in DMSO according to the manufacturer ’s instructions (100 mM stock solution). All animal experiments (Fig. 2) were reviewed and approved by the Animal Research and Care Committee of the Centre of Experimental Medicine SAS (Project No. 6009-3/2022 − 220) and conducted in accordance with the European Directive 2010/63/EU and ARRIVE guidelines.

Plasma and urine collection

To facilitate efficient plasma sampling, animals underwent cannulation of the left carotid artery one day prior to the SET2 application, as described in detail in Bartosova et al., 2024. Briefly, the procedure was performed under general anesthesia using Isoflurin (Vetpharma Animal Health, Barcelona, Spain) with 5% isoflurane for induction and 2% isoflurane for maintenance. After ligation of the left carotid artery, accessed via an incision in the neck/thoracic area, a cannula was inserted and secured in the artery. To prevent blood clotting, the cannula was filled with heparinized saline solution (250 IU/ml) and sealed with a plastic cap. For postoperative analgesia, metamizole (100 mg/kg) was added into the drinking water for 24 h34. After this period, SET2 was administered as a single 25 mg/kg dose via i.p. injection. A staggered sampling design was employed to minimize blood volume loss per animal while ensuring a complete plasma concentration-time profile. Different subgroups of rats (n = 5 per time point) were sampled at predefined intervals after administration (0, 2, 10, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 180, and 240 min post-dose). Blood samples (5–6 per rat) were collected from the cannulated left carotid artery using a heparinized syringe, allowing for the construction of individual and composite plasma concentration-time profiles. Samples were immediately transferred into heparinised collection tubes and centrifuged at 2000 rpm, 4 °C for 10 min. The plasma was then aliquoted and stored at -80 °C until analysis.

To assess the urinary elimination of SET2, Wistar rats were fasted for 24 h prior to the administration of a single i.p. injection of 25 mg/kg SET2. Following administration, they were housed in metabolic cages for 48 h. Urine samples were collected at the following time intervals: 0–4, 4–8, 8–24 and 24–48 h post-administration, and total urine output was recorded throughout the collection period.

Determination of SET2 plasma and urine concentration by UHPLC-MS

To analyse SET2 plasma concentration, we developed a UHPLC-MS/MS method, as previously described34. The determination of SET2 in rat plasma and urine samples was based on a previously published LC-MS method, with the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) set at 59.27 pg/ml34. This limit was later used for NCA analysis, where samples with concentrations lower than the LLOQ were marked as BLQ (below the limit of quantification). However, some changes were made to the LC-MS method for urine, especially in the detection technique. Here, a simple single quadrupole detector, QDa (Waters, Milford, MA, USA), was used for detection of analytes. The QDa detector was operated under the following conditions: positive ionization electrospray polarity (ESI+), capillary voltage of 0.8 kV, source temperature 120 °C and probe temperature 600 °C. The detection was performed in the selected ion monitoring (SIM) MS scanning mode, where following m/z values were set for SET2 (m/z = 403.07) and its IS (m/z = 452.2). The separation was performed on the Acquity UPLC CSH C18, 2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm column (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) under gradient elution. Mobile phase A was composed of 0.1% formic acid in LC water, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile. The samples were prepared by diluting the collected rat urine LC/MS water (10-fold dilution), adding 10 µL of the internal standard 2-({4-chloro-6-[4-(2,2-dimethylpropanoyl)piperazin-1-yl]pyrimidin-2-yl}sulfanyl)-N-[(furan-2-yl)methyl]acetamide (Comgenex, Budapest, Hungary) and 15 µL of the precipitating agent (2 M trichloroacetic acid). The mixture was gently vortexed and centrifuged at 17,500 rpm for 10 min. After centrifugation, 100 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a vial, and 5 µL was directly injected into the LC system.

Electrocardiography and blood pressure assessment

Electrocardiographic (ECG) and systolic blood pressure (BP) recordings were obtained as part of a single protocol using the ADInstruments setup and analysed with LabChart 8 software (ADInstruments, Dunedin, New Zealand). Wistar rats were induced into anesthesia with isoflurane and maintained under a 2% concentration throughout the procedure. Following a 5-minute stabilization period, a 30-minute continuous ECG recording was performed. Subcutaneous needle electrodes were used for ECG signal acquisition, connected through a Bio Amp (ADInstruments, Dunedin, New Zealand) to ensure high-quality signal recording. Key ECG parameters (PR interval duration (ms), QRS complex duration (ms), ST height amplitude (mV), QT interval duration (ms) and RR interval duration (ms)) were evaluated using the LabChart 8 ECG analysis module. Non-sinus beats were excluded, and 10 consecutive sinus beats per recording were analysed and averaged. The heart rate was evaluated based on the ECG cycle length (RR interval). Frequency-corrected QT intervals (QTc) were calculated using the modified Bazett’s formula: QTcn-B = QT/(RR150)1/2 as described by Kmecová and Klimas (2010)35.

Subsequently, systolic BP was measured continuously under anesthesia using MLT125/R tail cuff transducer plethysmography and recorded with NIBP Controller and PowerLab 4/30 (ADInstruments, Dunedin, New Zealand). BP data were obtained from an average of 3–5 measurements per animal at a single time point. This method was outlined by Erken et al.36 .

Plasma and urine markers

Cardiac and liver markers, including cardiac troponin I, creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), urea, and uric acid, were measured in the plasma from control and SET2 treated rats, collected 2 h post-administration. Analyses were performed commercially by Laboklin (Laboklin GmbH & CO. KG., Bad Kissingen, Germany).

Creatinine concentration in urine was measured using enzymatic method with spectrophotometric detection on the Siemens Healthineers Atellica Solution analytical system (Forchheim, Germany) using Siemens Atellica reagent kits and calibration standards traceable to NIST-SRM967. Analysis was performed in an ISO 15,189 accredited laboratory, and standard analytical quality assessment procedures were used to evaluate and maintain desired method precision, systemic bias, and overall accuracy (Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute Protocols EP05A3 and EP15A3).

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Pharmacokinetic parameters of SET2 were processed by noncompartmental analysis (NCA) using R software (v4.3.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) utilizing the packages PK (version 1.3-6) and boot. Estimates of pharmacokinetic parameter were presented as means with standard errors (SE). Nonparametric bootstrapping with 1000 resampling iterations was used to calculate 95% confidence intervals (CI). Data points below the lower limit of quantification (BLQ) were treated as missing data. BLQ values occurring after the last quantifiable concentration were excluded from the analysis, while those appearing between two measurable concentrations were not interpolated and were also treated as missing.

For the pharmacokinetic analysis following intraperitoneal administration, Cmax and Tmax (time to reach Cmax) were determined directly from observed plasma concentration-time data. The area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) was calculated using the linear trapezoidal method, with AUC0 − last representing the AUC from time zero to the last measurable concentration. The total AUC from zero to infinity (AUC0 − inf) was estimated as the sum of AUC0 − last and the extrapolated portion beyond the last measured time point, using the equation AUC0 − inf = AUC0 − last + Clast/λz, where Clast is the final quantifiable plasma concentration and λz is the apparent terminal elimination rate constant, derived from the linear regression of the log-transformed terminal concentration-time data. The apparent clearance (CL/F) was determined by dividing the administered dose by AUC0 − inf expressed as CL/F = Dose/AUC0 − inf. The apparent volume of distribution during the terminal phase (Vz/F) was obtained by dividing CL/F by the elimination rate constant (λz). Bioavailability (F) was assumed to be equivalent to that of intravenous administration, set at 100%. The mean residence time (MRT) was computed as the ratio of the area under the first moment curve extrapolated to infinity (AUMC0 − inf) to AUC0 − inf. The steady-state volume of distribution (Vss/F) was derived from the product of CL/F and MRT.

For the urinary excretion analysis, renal clearance (CLR) was estimated using the midpoint method, accounting for fluctuations in plasma drug concentrations over time. CLR was calculated as CLR = Ae0 − 48 h / (C24h × T), where Ae0 − 48 h represents the cumulative amount of SET2 excreted in urine over 48 h, C24h is the plasma concentration at the midpoint of the collection period (24 h), and T corresponds to the total urine collection duration (48 h). Additionally, the fraction of the administered dose excreted unchanged in urine (FE0 − 48 h) was determined as FE0 − 48 h = (Ae0 − 48 h / Dose) × 100%.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) to evaluate the effects of SET2 on ECG parameters, blood pressure (BP), and biomarker levels. ECG and BP data were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Biomarker data were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. If assumptions for parametric analysis were not met, nonparametric alternatives were applied: the Kruskal-Wallis test and the Mann-Whitney U test. All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Following i.p. administration of 25 mg/kg, SET2 exhibited immediate Tmax, supporting the assumption that i.p. administration approximates intravenous (i.v.) delivery in this model. The mean plasma concentration-time curve is shown in Fig. 3a. From the pharmacokinetic data calculated by NCA (Table 3), the mean residence time (MRT) was 70.5 min, indicating rapid systemic elimination. The apparent steady-state volume of distribution (Vss/F) was 10.17 ml/kg, suggesting that SET2 is largely confined to plasma and extracellular fluids, with low tissue penetration. The apparent clearance (CL/F) was 0.144 ml/min/kg, a value moderate relative to hepatic blood flow, indicating that hepatic metabolism is the primary elimination route rather than flow-limited clearance. Consistent with this, renal excretion was minimal, with only 4.24% of the administered dose excreted unchanged in urine (FE₀–₄₈h; Fig. 3b), confirming that hepatic metabolism, rather than renal clearance, governs systemic elimination. Furthermore, urinary creatinine levels, measured in SET2-treated animals, remained stable throughout the 48-hour collection period (Fig. 5d), suggesting preserved renal function over the course of the experiment.

The pharmacokinetic profile of SET2 thus reflects limited tissue distribution, efficient hepatic metabolism, and a rapid elimination pattern, supporting the assumption of i.p. ≈ i.v. systemic availability, while further studies, such as metabolite identification in plasma, urine, and bile, are warranted to confirm the exact metabolic and elimination fate of the compound.

Electrocardiography and blood pressure

Following a single intraperitoneal injection of 25 mg/kg body weight, SET2 was well tolerated by Wistar rats, with no visually observable adverse effects. Heart rate, ECG and systolic blood pressure were monitored in order to screen for acute cardiovascular effects after SET2 administration (Fig. 4). At 2 min post-dose, when SET2 reached its estimated Cmax, no significant effects were observed on heart rate, ECG parameters (RR interval, PR interval, QRS complex duration, QT interval length, QTc interval, and ST height), or systolic blood pressure, all of which remained stable and comparable to those in control animals. Similar observations were made 2 h post-dose, with all monitored parameters remaining unchanged compared to the controls. In this experiment, SET2 did not interfere with the physiological electrical activity of the heart at its peak plasma concentrations.

Plasma and urine markers

The plasma markers screening included troponin I, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), urea, and uric acid (Fig. 5). With the exception of one marker, all remained unchanged following SET2 treatment (25 mg/kg, i.p.) compared to control animals. Troponin I was significantly elevated in SET2-treated rats (Fig. 5a). As a highly selective and sensitive marker for acute myocardial injury or coronary syndrome, its elevation in SET2-treated rats may indicate potential adverse cardiac effects. Although this is the most likely explanation, other extracardiac processes cannot be entirely ruled out37. Despite the severity of this finding, it is important to note that proper dose adjustment or reduction might mitigate this effect; however, further investigation into the exact cause of the troponin I elevation is crucial.

In this study, we monitored urine creatinine concentration over 48 h, which remained stable with no significant changes (Fig. 5d). All measured concentrations were consistent with previously reported reference values, which estimate urine creatinine levels to be approximately 84.38 mg/dL (or 7.46 mmol/l) in 8- to 12-week-old Wistar rats38,39. These findings suggest that acute kidney injury is unlikely in SET2-treated rats.

The only observed adverse effect of SET2 in this study was an increase in troponin I levels. However, dose used for the purpose of this pharmacokinetics study was considerably higher compared to estimated IC50 of SET2 (0.46 µM)40suggesting that this effect may be dose-dependent. Moreover, our preliminary in vitro experiments with H9c2 cardiomyoblasts that were exposed to 1, 10, and 40 µM concentrations of SET2 for 24 hours showed no reduction in cell viability in any of tested concentrations (see Supplementary Information). It is therefore possible that dose titration could mitigate or eliminate this response. Despite this finding, SET2 had no detectable impact on ECG parameters or heart rate during the observation period (2 h post-administration).

Conclusion

In the present study, we described the plasma pharmacokinetic profile, urinary excretion and acute cardiac effects of the TRPV2-selective inhibitor SET2. As a long-awaited selective drug probe, SET2 has the potential to enable the investigation of TRPV2-mediated mechanisms across various disease models, including neurological, cardiovascular, and oncological conditions. Given its specificity, SET2 may serve as a valuable molecular probe for further elucidating TRPV2 function in experimental settings. Following an intraperitoneal injection of 25 mg/kg, SET2 rapidly reached peak plasma concentration (Cmax = 1428 ng/ml or ≈ 3.55 µM) at 2 min post-dose. The plasma concentration also declined rapidly, with a MRT of 70.5 min, indicating short systemic exposure. Renal excretion played a minor role in elimination, with only 4.24% of the administered dose excreted unchanged in urine (FE₀–₄₈h), suggesting that metabolism is the primary clearance pathway. Plasma markers of hepatic and renal function remained unchanged following SET2 administration. However, troponin I, marker of myocardial injury, was significantly elevated. Despite this, SET2 had little to no effect on heart rate, ECG parameters, or systolic blood pressure. These findings suggest that SET2 holds promise as a pharmacological tool for future studies, but careful dose optimization will be essential to mitigate potential cardiac effects.
