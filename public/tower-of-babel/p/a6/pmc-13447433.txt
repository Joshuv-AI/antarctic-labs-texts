Psychometric Validation of a User-Centered Usability and Acceptance Instrument for Home-Based Cognitive Rehabilitation in Older Adults With Cognitive Impairment
Authors: Sol Jung, Doo Young Kim, Areum Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to develop and validate a user-centered questionnaire to assess usability and acceptance of a tablet-based computerized cognitive rehabilitation program for older adults. A total of 35 preliminary items were generated based on the principles of Universal Design (UD) and user acceptance theories, including the Technology Acceptance Model, Information Systems Continuance Model, Net Promoter Score, and System Usability Scale. After expert review and item refinement, 25 items were finalized and administered to 101 older adults with cognitive impairment. Exploratory factor analysis revealed a three-factor structure—Physical & Cognitive Accessibility, Operational & Emotional Comfort, and Therapeutic Expectation & Behavioral Intention—explaining 87.3% of the total variance. Internal consistency reliability for each factor was high (Cronbach’s α > 0.90). Correlation analysis showed that factor scores were significantly associated with age, gender, education level, and program usage variables (total time, average time, frequency), indicating that both demographic and behavioral factors influenced user responses. The strong inter-factor correlations also supported the structural coherence of the tool. These findings suggest that the developed instrument is a psychometrically sound and practically useful tool for assessing usability and user acceptance of digital rehabilitation technologies among cognitively impaired older adults. The tool may also inform user-centered design, clinical implementation strategies, and future research on digital therapeutics for aging populations. The validated item set demonstrates a distinctive strength in integrating accessibility-related usability grounded in UD principles with acceptance-related behavioral intentions, thereby differentiating this instrument from existing usability assessment tools for older adults.Keywords: Cognitive Impairment, Home-Based Cognitive Rehabilitation, Usability Instrument, User-Centered Design, Digital Rehabilitation

INTRODUCTION

With the rapid growth of the aging population, the number of individuals diagnosed with cognitive impairment continues to rise.

In South Korea, a recent nationwide epidemiological survey estimated a prevalence of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) among older adults aged 65 and above to be approximately 28.4% [1]. MCI is characterized by measurable cognitive decline beyond normal aging without significant impairment in daily functioning and is considered a transitional stage toward dementia.

In contrast, post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) refers to cognitive deficits occurring after a cerebrovascular event [2]. In stroke survivors, cognitive impairment affects approximately 30%–70% within the first year post-stroke [3]. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that around 39%–47% exhibit cognitive deficits specifically between 3 and 6 months post-event [4]. In a Korean stroke rehabilitation cohort, 31.1% of patients showed cognitive impairment at 6 months [5]. Unlike MCI, PSCI is often accompanied by functional disability and presents a more heterogeneous cognitive profile depending on lesion location and stroke subtype [6].

Given the increasing aging population and dual burden of neurodegenerative and vascular conditions, both MCI and PSCI represent critical targets for early intervention and home-based cognitive rehabilitation [6,7].

In response, increasing emphasis is being placed on the need for non-pharmacological interventions aimed at delaying cognitive decline and preserving functional independence in daily life [8,9,10]. Among such interventions, computerized cognitive rehabilitation training—utilizing digital technologies—has gained attention as an alternative to traditional one-on-one or paper-based interventions, overcoming limitations of face-to-face delivery and enabling remote, home-based cognitive rehabilitation [11,12,13,14].

This shift highlights not only the provision of technology, but also the importance of evaluating the extent to which users can meaningfully engage with and accept these technologies—referred to as usability and user acceptance, respectively [15,16]. In particular, older adults with cognitive impairment often face reduced information processing speed and physical limitations, which may hinder their access to and effective use of digital rehabilitation programs. These factors may, in turn, influence the overall efficacy of the intervention and the likelihood of continued use [17,18]. However, existing usability assessment tools are either lacking or not tailored to the specific needs of older adults with cognitive impairment, especially in the context of home-based interventions.

Although widely used standardized instruments—such as the System Usability Scale (SUS) and technology acceptance—based questionnaires—have demonstrated reliability in general adult populations, they were primarily developed for cognitively intact users and may not adequately account for age-related sensory decline, cognitive impairment, or reduced health literacy. Previous studies have reported that older adults with cognitive impairment often experience difficulties with abstract wording, double negatives, and technology-oriented terminology commonly embedded in existing tools, which may lead to response bias or reduced measurement validity. Moreover, many existing instruments focus on general system efficiency or satisfaction, rather than accessibility-related usability and sustained user acceptance in home-based settings for cognitively vulnerable populations [19,20,21,22].

Accordingly, the present study aimed to develop and validate a survey instrument designed to assess the usability and user acceptance of a tablet-based computerized cognitive rehabilitation program when used by older adults in a home environment. The survey was developed based on two theoretical foundations.

First, the principles of Universal Design (UD) provide guidelines for developing products and systems that are accessible and usable by individuals with diverse physical and cognitive abilities. These principles offer a suitable framework for evaluating digital devices designed for older adults, including those with cognitive impairments [23,24]. In this study, items were developed to measure user experience across the seven UD principles: equitable use, flexibility in use, simple and intuitive use, perceptible information, tolerance for error, low physical effort, and appropriate size and space for use.

Second, user acceptance refers to the extent to which individuals are willing to adopt and engage with a technology. Based on established models such as the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [25], the Information Systems Continuance Model [26], the Net Promoter Score (NPS) [27], and the SUS [28], we constructed items addressing three key subdomains of acceptance: expected effectiveness, emotional acceptability, and behavioral intention.

The specific objectives of this study were to:

1. Develop a usability assessment tool tailored for older adults, grounded in the principles of UD and user acceptance theory.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This study aimed to develop and validate a survey instrument designed to evaluate the user experience of older adults when using a tablet-based cognitive rehabilitation system. The study employed a tool development and validation approach, wherein the instrument was constructed based on the principles of UD and theories of user acceptance. Following expert consultation and item refinement procedures, the finalized questionnaire was administered to actual users, and its structural validity was examined through EFA.

Tablet-based cognitive rehabilitation system

Zenicog® (Mindhub Inc., Anyang, Korea) is a tablet-based cognitive rehabilitation system developed for patients with cognitive impairment due to neurological conditions such as stroke or dementia. The system provides structured stimulation across various cognitive domains—including attention, memory, and executive function—to promote cognitive recovery and enhancement. It is primarily used by individuals with dementia, MCI, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and acquired brain injury, including post-stroke patients.

The platform incorporates automated scoring technology, allowing patients to perform rehabilitation independently without third-party assistance (e.g., therapists or caregivers). Additionally, an AI-based difficulty adjustment system dynamically presents tasks tailored to the user’s performance level, thereby optimizing cognitive engagement and potential improvement. While Zenicog® is not classified as a medical device, it has been validated for home-based digital healthcare use. It meets U-Healthcare-grade standards for cybersecurity and software validation, having passed rigorous assessments of data integrity, confidentiality, and functional reliability.

To minimize potential bias arising from hardware variability and to ensure the reliability of the usability assessment (particularly for items regarding physical comfort and device size), all participants were provided with a standardized Android-based tablet. The device featured an 11-inch screen and weighed approximately 500 g, providing a consistent tactile and visual interface for the computerized cognitive rehabilitation program.

Instrument development process

The survey instrument developed in this study was designed to comprehensively assess the user experience of older adults with cognitive impairment when using a tablet-based cognitive rehabilitation system (Zenicog®). It aimed to capture key aspects such as accessibility, ease of use, emotional response, and intention for continued use. The development process followed a systematic sequence: theory-driven item generation, expert validity review, item refinement, and structural analysis.

Item development based on UD principles

The initial draft of the survey was constructed based on the seven principles of UD proposed by North Carolina State University [23,24]. These principles include: (1) equitable use, (2) flexibility in use, (3) simple and intuitive use, (4) perceptible information, (5) tolerance for error, (6) low physical effort, and (7) appropriate size and space for use. These were translated into concrete items that reflect the potential physical and cognitive barriers encountered by older adults and individuals with cognitive decline when engaging with digital technologies.

Each UD principle was elaborated into practical usability aspects such as screen layout intuitiveness, visual clarity, ease of operation, error tolerance, physical fatigue, and touch accessibility. Based on these, a total of 24 draft items were initially developed, of which 17 were retained in the final survey.

Item development based on user acceptance theory

To assess user-centered technology acceptance, an additional set of 11 items was developed. These items were designed with reference to established theoretical models, including the TAM [25], the Information Systems Continuance Model [26], the NPS [29], and the SUS [28]. The items targeted 3 key subdomains of user acceptance: expected effectiveness, emotional acceptability, and behavioral intention.

These questions aimed to capture users’ perceived trust in the program, satisfaction, willingness for repeated use, and intention to recommend it to others. Rather than evaluating functionality alone, these items were intended to assess the actual likelihood of user adoption and retention. Eight of the eleven items were included in the final version of the survey.

Data collection

The validation phase of this study—comprising EFA and internal consistency testing—was conducted using post-intervention data collected from older adult participants with cognitive impairment who completed a tablet-based cognitive rehabilitation system as part of an ongoing RCT. Specifically, participants completed the usability questionnaire after finishing the intervention period, allowing them to evaluate their actual user experience with the system.

For the validation analysis, only participants who had completed both the intervention and the usability survey by March 2025 were included. Once the minimum recommended sample size for factor analysis (N > 100) had been reached, psychometric evaluation of the instrument was initiated. Importantly, this methodological sub-study was analytically and conceptually independent from the primary clinical outcomes of the parent RCT and focused solely on evaluating the structural validity and reliability of the newly developed usability instrument.

The parent RCT comprises two arms: (1) a single-center trial for older adults with MCI, conducted at International St. Mary’s Hospital of Catholic Kwandong University between November 2023 and June 2025 (CRIS identifier: KCT0008968, Institutional Review Board [IRB] No. IS23OISE0063); and (2) a multi-center trial for post-stroke patients with cognitive deficits, conducted at both International St. Mary’s Hospital and Seosong Hospital between November 2023 and February 2025 (CRIS identifier: KCT0008969, IRB No. IS23OIME0062 and P01-202312-01-025 [Public IRB]).

All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. The full study protocol, including this psychometric validation as a secondary observational analysis, was approved by the listed above. Participants were explicitly informed that their data could be used for secondary analyses such as tool validation, and all procedures conformed to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The usability assessment was conducted specifically after the completion of the entire intervention period to ensure that participants provided feedback based on sustained practical experience. During the study, clinical status was monitored; any participants who experienced clinical worsening (e.g., stroke recurrence or significant cognitive decline requiring new medication) would have been withdrawn from the trial as part of the parent RCT’s protocol. A total of 108 individuals completed the survey. After excluding 7 cases with incomplete responses, a final sample of 101 respondents was included in the analysis. Consequently, the final 101 participants in this analysis represent a cohort that remained clinically stable and did not require pharmacological intervention throughout the study period. Accordingly, the final analytic sample consisted of 101 participants.

Sample size was determined based on general recommendations for EFA, which suggest a minimum of 5 participants per item or a total sample size of 100 or more. The final sample of 101 respondents met this criterion [30,31].

Data analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were performed using STATA BE version 18.5 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). To evaluate the structural validity of the developed survey instrument, an EFA was conducted. Additionally, internal consistency reliability analysis, descriptive statistics, and correlation analysis were performed to support the reliability and external validity of the results.

Assessment of suitability for factor analysis

Before conducting the factor analysis, the suitability of the data for EFA was evaluated using the following indicators:

• Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy: A KMO value of 0.60 or higher was considered acceptable for factor analysis [32].

• Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy: A KMO value of 0.60 or higher was considered acceptable for factor analysis [32].

• Bartlett’s test of sphericity: A statistically significant result (p < 0.05) indicated that the correlation matrix was not an identity matrix, thereby justifying the use of factor analysis [33].

EFA

EFA was conducted to identify the underlying factor structure of the survey instrument. The principal factor method was used for factor extraction, and promax rotation (an oblique rotation technique) was applied [34]. The number of factors was determined based on the following criteria:

• Eigenvalue ≥ 1.00 (Kaiser’s criterion)

• Eigenvalue ≥ 1.00 (Kaiser’s criterion)

• Visual inspection of the scree plot [35]

• Item inclusion criterion: Items with a factor loading ≥ 0.40 were retained within each factor [31]

The final number of factors was determined by considering the interpretability and theoretical coherence of the resulting structure. Factor scores were computed based on the extracted structure and were used in subsequent analyses.

Reliability analysis

Internal consistency for each factor was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. A coefficient of α ≥ 0.70 was considered indicative of acceptable reliability [36], while in exploratory research, α ≥ 0.60 was also considered acceptable [37].

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics were computed to examine response characteristics for individual items and factor scores. For each item and factor, the mean, standard deviation (SD), range (minimum and maximum), skewness, and kurtosis were analyzed to evaluate the normality and response tendency of the distributions.

• Program usage variables: Total number of uses, total usage time, and average usage time

The item-level mean scores ranged from 3.35 (q13) to 4.17 (q18, q25), while SDs ranged from 0.81 (q18) to 1.35 (q16), suggesting that the survey provided a sufficient level of discrimination among respondents (Supplementary Table 1). Skewness values ranged from −1.10 (q21) to −0.06 (q19), reflecting a tendency for responses to be skewed slightly toward higher ratings. However, according to the guideline thresholds of skewness < 2 and kurtosis < 7 [39], no substantial deviation from normality was detected.

These results suggest that the item responses were statistically well distributed without significant violations of normality, forming a stable foundation for subsequent factor analysis and reliability testing. Accordingly, the dataset was deemed appropriate for conducting EFA.

First, age showed a significant positive correlation with Factor 3 (Therapeutic Expectation & Behavioral Intention) (r = 0.245, p < 0.05), indicating that older participants tended to report higher expectations of rehabilitation effectiveness and greater receptiveness toward the technology.

Similarly, gender was positively correlated with Factor 3 (r = 0.206, p < 0.05), with female participants exhibiting a more favorable overall attitude toward the program.

In contrast, education level demonstrated a significant negative correlation with both Factor 2 (r = −0.200, p < 0.05) and Factor 3 (r = −0.222, p < 0.05), suggesting that participants with lower educational attainment perceived the system more positively.

Regarding program usage characteristics, total usage time (total_time) showed significant positive correlations with all three factors (Factor 1: r = 0.269; Factor 2: r = 0.251; Factor 3: r = 0.283), implying that longer engagement with the program was associated with better perceptions of accessibility, usability, and acceptance. In addition, total number of uses (total_num) was significantly correlated with Factor 3 (r = 0.215, p < 0.05), suggesting that greater usage frequency was associated with increased user acceptance and intention for continued use.

This indicates that while each factor captures a distinct dimension of user experience, they collectively represent a coherent and integrated framework for evaluating the usability and acceptability of the program.

DISCUSSION

This study developed and validated a survey instrument to assess the user experience of older adults with cognitive impairment when using the tablet-based cognitive rehabilitation system. EFA revealed a three-factor structure consisting of Physical & Cognitive Accessibility, Operational & Emotional Comfort, and Therapeutic Expectation & Behavioral Intention, with a high cumulative variance explained (87.3%). All three factors demonstrated strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α coefficients exceeding 0.90, indicating that the instrument reliably measures stable psychological constructs. While these high Cronbach’s α values indicate excellent internal consistency, values exceeding 0.90 may also suggest potential item redundancy. This likely reflects the intentional inclusion of conceptually related items to comprehensively capture usability and acceptance dimensions in older adults with cognitive impairment. Future research may therefore explore the development of a shorter version of the instrument that retains psychometric robustness while reducing respondent burden. Moreover, inter-factor correlations exceeded r = 0.56, suggesting that the three dimensions represent conceptually distinct yet interrelated components of a unified user experience framework. These results collectively support both the theoretical coherence and the psychometric reliability of the instrument.

Analysis of correlations between factor scores and participant characteristics yielded several meaningful insights. Older age was positively associated with Factor 3 (Therapeutic Expectation & User Acceptance) (r = 0.245, p < 0.05), suggesting that older adults may exhibit higher expectations and greater acceptance of digital rehabilitation technologies. Similarly, female participants demonstrated significantly higher scores on user acceptance compared to males (r = 0.206, p < 0.05). In contrast, higher educational level was negatively correlated with both Factor 2 and Factor 3 (r = −0.200 to −0.222), possibly indicating that more educated users—potentially more accustomed to sophisticated technologies—were less satisfied with simplified user interfaces. In terms of program usage characteristics, total usage time and average session time were significantly and positively correlated with all three factors, indicating that greater engagement led to higher perceived accessibility and acceptance. These findings underscore that user experience extends beyond ease of use and also reflects emotional receptivity and behavioral intention to continue use. Additionally, total number of sessions was positively correlated with Factor 3, suggesting that repeated use fosters increased acceptance and perceived effectiveness of the intervention. These results offer empirical support for considering objective usage data (duration, frequency) alongside subjective user feedback when evaluating digital rehabilitation tools.

The developed instrument provides a practical tool for systematically assessing the real-world user experience of tablet-based cognitive rehabilitation system among older adults with cognitive impairment. By integrating UD principles and user acceptance theory, the survey captures not only functional usability but also cognitive accessibility, emotional responsiveness, and therapeutic motivation.

Accordingly, this tool has broad applicability in both clinical and operational settings—for example, informing user-centered design, enhancing user retention, and guiding iterative development of digital health solutions. Beyond cognitive rehabilitation, it may also be extended to assess the user acceptance of other age-friendly digital therapeutics (DTx) or smart healthcare platforms. Furthermore, the validated instrument can be utilized in regulatory and certification processes. In Korea, the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety mandates usability documentation as part of the regulatory review for digital medical software. These requirements are particularly relevant for DTx, a subclass of software-based medical devices that emphasize patient-centered design and therapeutic engagement [41]. The survey tool may serve as a standardized measure to evaluate user-centered aspects of cognitive rehabilitation technologies intended for older populations.

This study was based on a cross-sectional sample of 101 participants, which limits the generalizability of the findings due to sample size and geographic constraints. Moreover, only EFA was conducted; thus, future studies should employ confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and structural equation modeling to validate the instrument’s structure.

Future studies should also perform CFA using independent samples to verify the stability of the factor structure. Moreover, multi-group CFA across different subgroups (e.g., MCI vs. PSCI) could validate measurement invariance. Establishing concurrent validity with existing usability or acceptance scales and conducting test-retest reliability assessments are also warranted to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the instrument.

Additionally, while the survey was self-administered, individual differences in cognitive functioning and digital literacy were not controlled for, which may have affected response reliability. Future research should explore stratified analyses based on cognitive levels, comparisons with caregiver assessments, and cultural adaptation to ensure broader validity and applicability.
