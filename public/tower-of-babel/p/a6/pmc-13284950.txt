Associations of Multiomics Biological Aging With Diabetic Retinopathy and Life Expectancy
Authors: Jun Yu, Yue Lan Gao, Yuzhou Zhang, Jian Ming Xu, Haoyu Chen, Mary Ho, Ka Wai Kam, Alvin L. Young, Chi Pui Pang, Clement C. Tham, Jason C. Yam, Li Jia Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the associations of omics-based biological aging with diabetic retinopathy (DR) and life expectancy among individuals with DR.MethodsWe included 68,672 UK Biobank participants with prediabetes or diabetes in the metabolomic cohort and 8716 in the proteomic cohort, from which biological age was estimated using a metabolomic aging score and proteomic age (ProtAgeGap), respectively. Associations with prevalent and incident DR were assessed using logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models, respectively. Life expectancy was estimated from survival curves.ResultsCross-sectionally, both the higher metabolomic aging score and ProtAgeGap were associated with a higher risk of prevalent DR. Prospectively, during a median follow-up of 13.40 years, 2491 participants (3.7%) developed DR among those free of DR at baseline in the metabolomic cohort and 358 (4.14%) in the proteomic cohort. Higher biological aging was associated with increased DR risk, with hazard ratios of 1.73 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.59–1.88) for the metabolomic aging score and 1.12 (95% CI, 1.07–1.17) for ProtAgeGap. These associations were independent of and additive to glycated hemoglobin levels and diabetes duration. At age 45 years, individuals with DR had reduced life expectancy compared with those without DR. Among those with DR, the highest quartile of metabolomic aging was associated with a reduced life expectancy compared with the lowest quartile.ConclusionsAdvanced metabolomic and proteomic aging were associated with a higher risk of DR, particularly in individuals with poor glycemic control or longer diabetes duration, while advanced metabolomic aging was associated with reduced life expectancy among those with DR.

As population aging accelerates, diabetes has become one of the fastest-growing chronic diseases worldwide and is projected to affect about 1.3 billion people by 2050.1 Among its disabling complications, diabetic retinopathy (DR) is the most prevalent and remains a leading cause of vision loss and blindness among working-age adults. In addition to its visual consequences, DR has been associated with increased risk of mortality.2–5 More than 90% of vision loss from DR can be prevented through timely detection and appropriate intervention,6 underscoring the importance of effective screening strategies and early intervention. However, despite growing interest in biomarker discovery, current knowledge of DR risk factors remains incomplete,7 with genetic studies offering limited insights.8 Expanding the scope of risk assessment is therefore necessary to improve the identification of individuals at high risk of DR and to reduce the overall disease burden.

Aging, an irreversible consequence of cumulative cellular stress and damage, is the dominant risk factor for most common chronic diseases.9 Aging-related pathologies (e.g., inflammaging, cellular senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired nutrient sensing) have been implicated in DR development.10 Chronological age is a strong but imperfect proxy for biological aging (BA). Recent omics-derived approaches that quantify BA provide more precise estimates by capturing systemic signals across multiple tissues.9 Previous metabolomic aging models, including mortality-trained MetaboHealth11 and chronological age-trained metabolomic age,12 have shown potential clinical utility for disease prediction. However, the association between BA processes and DR remains largely unclear. Previous studies on BA in DR have been constrained by reliance on a single aging biomarker, modest sample sizes, cross-sectional designs, invasive intraocular sampling procedures, and/or overlooking individuals with prediabetes.13–16

In this study, we hypothesized that more advanced BA, as captured by omics-derived measures, would be associated with a higher risk of DR. We conducted both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses to investigate the associations of two novel BA measures—a metabolomic aging score17 and a proteomic age18—with DR. We further assessed the association of advanced BA with life expectancy among individuals with DR.

Methods

Study Population

The UK Biobank (UKB) is a large-scale prospective cohort of over 500,000 participants recruited across 22 UK centers between 2006 and 2010.19 Baseline assessment included biological sampling, physical measurements, and touchscreen questionnaires; detailed protocols have been published previously.19 UKB received approval from the North West Centre for Research Ethics Committee (21/NW/0157). All participants provided informed written consent.

In this study, starting with 91,428 participants with diabetes or prediabetes, we analyzed 81,598 individuals with metabolomic data and 10,333 with proteomic data separately (Supplementary Fig. S1). After excluding participants with missing covariates, the metabolomic cohort comprised 68,672 individuals for cross-sectional analyses and 68,155 for longitudinal analyses (excluding baseline DR). Similarly, the proteomic cohort included 8716 and 8633 participants, respectively.

Metabolomic Profiling and Calculation of Metabolomic Aging Score

We utilized the latest release of the UKB nuclear magnetic resonance metabolomics data (August 2025), comprising 251 biomarkers from EDTA plasma of approximately 491,000 participants.20 Detailed protocols are described elsewhere (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/ukb/docs/nmrm_phase3_companion.pdf).

We calculated the metabolomic aging score using the method established by Zhang et al.17 Given that aging is characterized by a progressive decline of physiological function culminating in death,21 the original derivation study employed all-cause mortality as a global aging-related endpoint to construct a metabolomic aging score that captures adverse, aging-associated metabolic profiles.17 Briefly, a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression model identified 54 aging-related biomarkers associated with all-cause mortality. These biomarkers were weighted by their regression coefficients to form the final score (Supplementary Methods and Table S1), which represents the log-relative hazard of mortality for an individual. The score has previously been shown to outperform chronological age and other aging metrics in predicting short-term mortality risk and to improve risk prediction for age-related diseases.17 It is not interpretable in units of years; rather, it is a dimensionless linear predictor, with higher values indicating a less favorable metabolomic profile of BA. The metabolomic aging score correlated moderately with chronological age (Pearson's r = 0.285; Supplementary Fig. S2).

Proteomic Profiling and Calculation of Proteomic Age Clock

Plasma proteomic data were acquired using the Olink Explore platform, covering cardiometabolic, inflammation, neurology, and oncology panels, and expressed as Normalized Protein eXpression values on a log₂ scale.22 Detailed methodology regarding participant selection and quality control has been reported previously.22

We adopted the analytical framework described by Argentieri et al.18 to construct a proteomic age. Specifically, we constructed a proteomic aging measure using a gradient boosting framework (LightGBM) trained on 204 proteins that showed robust associations with chronological age (Supplementary Table S2). The resulting proteomic age demonstrated a strong correlation with chronological age (Pearson's r = 0.95; Supplementary Fig. S2). The proteomic aging clock was validated in independent cohorts from China and Finland and was shown to predict all-cause mortality and multiple common age-related diseases, supporting its robustness and generalizability across diverse populations.18 The ProtAgeGap was defined as the difference between predicted proteomic and chronological age, with values >0 indicating proteomic BA acceleration.

Assessment of Prediabetes, Diabetes, and Diabetic Retinopathy

Because metabolic disturbances related to diabetes may affect retinal health even before clinical onset, participants with diabetes and those with prediabetes were both considered at risk of developing DR23–25 and were therefore included in the analysis. Prediabetes was defined by the presence of impaired fasting glucose or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels between 5.7% and 6.4% (39–47 mmol/mol), consistent with the 2021 American Diabetes Association criteria.26 Diabetes was defined based on a self-reported physician diagnosis from the touchscreen questionnaire, current use of insulin or other glucose-lowering medications, or an HbA1c of 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or more.27 The ascertainment of DR was based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10, code H36.0).27 For the prospective analyses, participants free of DR at baseline were followed until the date of hospital record of incident DR, loss to follow-up, death, or censoring (December 19, 2022), whichever occurred first.

Covariates

The covariate adjustment set was defined according to known factors associated with DR,27,28 including baseline age (years), sex (female or male), ethnicity (white or nonwhite), smoking status (never, former, or current), body mass index (BMI, kg/m²), Townsend deprivation index (TDI; range, −6.26 to 11), systolic blood pressure (SBP, mm Hg), diabetes duration (years), medical history (cardiovascular disease [CVD] and chronic kidney disease [CKD]; yes or no), and baseline biochemical measurements (high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], mmol/L; total cholesterol [TC], mmol/L; and HbA1c, mmol/mol). For participants with prediabetes, the duration of diabetes was considered zero. Detailed codes for covariates are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Assessment of Different Biological Aging Measures

To compare the predictive performance of current omics-derived BA measures with established aging indicators, we additionally derived the MetaboHealth score based on 14 metabolites11; the frailty phenotype score, defined by five functional criteria29; PhenoAge, calculated from chronological age and nine clinical biomarkers; the residuals from regressing PhenoAge on chronological age, which were used to derive PhenoAge acceleration (PhenoAgeAccel)30; and leukocyte telomere length (LTL), measured by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (Supplementary Methods).

Statistical Analyses

Data analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]) and categorical variables as number (percentage).

First, we assessed cross-sectional associations between omics-derived BA measures and prevalent DR using logistic regression and evaluated their prospective associations with incident DR among DR-free participants using Cox proportional hazards models. We fitted three models: an unadjusted model, a model adjusted for age and sex, and a fully adjusted model that included age, sex, ethnicity, HbA1c, diabetes duration, smoking status, HDL-C, TC, BMI, SBP, TDI, history of CVD, and history of CKD. We analyzed the two BA measures as continuous variables and in quartiles. Dose–response curves were assessed using restricted cubic spline models, with the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles of each BA measure used as knots.

To mitigate potential confounding, we performed several sensitivity analyses: (1) restricting the sample to participants of European ancestry, (2) excluding those with poor glycemic control (HbA1c ≥9.0% [75 mmol/mol]), (3) adjusting for insulin or oral glucose-lowering medication use, (4) excluding participants with baseline CVD or CKD, and (5) using residuals from the metabolomic aging score (regressed on chronological age) to assess associations independent of age. Next, for prospective assessments, we further excluded DR events occurring within the first 2 years, censored follow-up at 10 years, and applied multistate models (R package mstate) to evaluate transitions from baseline to incident DR and to death, thereby accounting for death as a competing risk for DR.

As both HbA1c levels and diabetes duration are associated with the onset and severity of DR, we evaluated their joint effects with the two BA measures on incident DR. Diabetes duration was categorized into three stages (<5, 5–10, and ≥10 years), while HbA1c was dichotomized at the diabetes diagnostic threshold of 48 mmol/mol because the prediabetes range (39–47 mmol/mol) resulted in fewer than 10 DR cases when combined with BA quartiles. The additive interaction was assessed by calculating the relative excess risk caused by interaction, the attributable proportion, and the synergy index (Supplementary Methods). The multiplicative interaction was tested by adding product terms in the models. Subgroup analyses were performed, and we additionally conducted subgroup analyses by lifestyle status. Lifestyle scores were based on smoking, physical activity, diet, and alcohol consumption and categorized as unhealthy (0–1), intermediate (2), or healthy (3–4).31

Second, we adopted Harrell's C-index to evaluate the incremental discrimination of each BA measure beyond the clinical model. The reference clinical model comprised conventional risk factors derived from three established DR prediction models (Aspelund, Hippisley–Cox and Coupland, and Rhee),32–34 including age, sex, HbA1c, duration of diabetes, smoking status, SBP, BMI, HDL-C, TDI, and TC.

Third, we used flexible parametric proportional hazards models (Royston–Parmar, stpm2 in Stata) with age as the time scale to assess the association of two BA measures with life expectancy in people with and without DR. Residual life expectancy was estimated as the area under the predicted survival curve integrated up to age 100 years. Years of life gained (YLG) were defined as the difference in life expectancy between participants with accelerated versus nonaccelerated BA (or across quartiles). Given that chronic diseases become more prevalent and mortality data more robust after midlife, we reported YLG at age 45 years as the primary summary measure. We further validated the results by controlling for diabetic nephropathy (DN) to assess independence from DN and by excluding participants with baseline CVD, CKD, or cancer, all of which substantially affect mortality (diagnosis codes in Supplementary Tables S3–S4).

We generated two-tailed P values through regression analysis and deemed values less than 0.05 significant, except where specified otherwise.

Multiomics Biological Aging and DR at Baseline

The associations were consistent across unadjusted, age- and sex-adjusted, and fully adjusted models (Table 2). Full model results for all covariates are provided in Supplementary Table S6. After full adjustment, higher metabolomic aging score and ProtAgeGap were each associated with prevalent DR at baseline (odds ratio [OR] = 1.53; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.33–1.76; P < 0.001 and OR = 1.28; 95% CI, 1.15–1.43; P < 0.001, respectively; Table 2). In dose–response analysis, the metabolomic aging score showed a monotonic association with DR across quartiles, whereas ProtAgeGap exhibited a nonlinear association (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. S3). For instance, compared with Q1 of the metabolomic aging score, the ORs of DR were 1.60 (95% CI, 0.99–2.64), 2.38 (95% CI, 1.54–3.82), and 3.13 (95% CI, 2.03–5.00), respectively.

Multiomics Biological Aging and DR Over Follow-Up

During a median follow-up of 13.40 years (interquartile range, 12.59–14.25 years), 2491 participants (3.7%) developed DR among those free of DR at baseline in the metabolomic cohort. Each 1-SD increase in metabolomic aging score was associated with a 73% higher risk of incident DR in the fully adjusted model (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.73; 95% CI, 1.59–1.88; P < 0.001; Table 3). In the proteomic cohort, among 8633 participants free of DR at baseline, 358 (4.14%) incident DR cases were documented during follow-up. After full adjustment, each 1-year increment in ProtAgeGap was associated with a 12% higher risk of DR (HR = 1.12; 95% CI, 1.07–1.17; P < 0.001). Dose–response curves indicated a monotonically nonlinear rise in DR risk with the metabolomic aging score, while the association with ProtAgeGap was linear (Table 3; Supplementary Fig. S4).

Sensitivity Analyses and Subgroup Analyses

These associations remained robust across all sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Tables S7–S9). In sensitivity analyses using multistate models, both BA measures were associated with an increased risk of transition from baseline to DR and from baseline to death (Supplementary Fig. S5, Supplementary Table S10). In contrast, only a higher metabolomic aging score was related to an increased risk of transition from DR to death, whereas ProtAgeGap showed no such association. Subgroup analyses demonstrated effect patterns that were similar to the main results (Supplementary Tables S11–S12). Furthermore, associations between metabolomic aging score and incident DR were similar across lifestyle groups, while ProtAgeGap showed elevated risk only in the unfavorable lifestyle group, with no evidence of multiplicative interaction (Supplementary Table S13).

Joint Effect Analyses Over Follow-Up

Participants with both a highest-quartile metabolomic aging score and HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol had increased incident DR risk compared with the lowest-quartile and HbA1c <48 mmol/mol group (HR = 12.82; 95% CI, 10.25–16.04; P < 0.001; Table 4). For ProtAgeGap, participants with HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol and accelerated aging had the highest risk compared with those with low HbA1c and no accelerated aging (HR = 6.59; 95% CI, 4.72–9.19; P < 0.001).

Joint Effects Between Omics-Based Biological Age and HbA1c Levels and Diabetes Duration on the Risk of Incident DR

Analyses were conducted in participants free of DR at baseline. Models were adjusted for age at recruitment, sex, ethnic background, body mass index, systolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, HDL-C, HbA1c, diabetes duration, Townsend deprivation index, smoking status, baseline cardiovascular disease, and baseline chronic kidney disease. Note that when combined variables were used, their component variables were not additionally included as covariates to avoid overadjustment.

The risk of developing DR was significantly increased in individuals with diabetes duration ≥10 years and both advanced BA measures versus individuals with diabetes duration <5 years and no advanced aging by both metabolomic aging score (HR = 19.55; 95% CI, 15.60–24.49; P < 0.001) and ProtAgeGap (HR = 7.66; 95% CI, 5.40–10.85; P < 0.001; Table 4). Notably, both BA measures showed significant additive and multiplicative interactions with HbA1c and diabetes duration, except that the multiplicative interaction between ProtAgeGap and diabetes duration was not evident (P > 0.05; Supplementary Table S14).

Discriminative Performance

In discrimination analyses, adding the metabolomic aging score to the conventional clinical model modestly improved performance (C statistic 0.838; ΔC = 0.005; 95% CI, 0.001–0.009; P < 0.001), whereas ProtAgeGap provided no incremental value (P = 0.79; Supplementary Fig. S6, Supplementary Table S15). Among individual biological aging metrics, the metabolomic score showed the highest discrimination for incident DR (C statistic = 0.719; 95% CI, 0.707–0.730; Supplementary Fig. S7, Supplementary Table S16). When incorporated into the clinical model, performance increased to 0.856 (95% CI, 0.848–0.863), comparable to models including PhenoAgeAccel or the frailty index, while other metrics showed limited discrimination.

Life Expectancy Analysis

Figure A shows that individuals without DR had longer life expectancy than those with DR, corresponding to a gain of 6.82 years at age 45 years (95% CI, 5.75–7.88; Supplementary Table S17). Among participants with DR, those in the lowest quartile of the metabolomic aging score experienced a gain of 7.23 years of life expectancy (95% CI, 2.42–12.04) compared with those in the highest quartile (Fig. B; Supplementary Table S18). In individuals without DR, the lowest quartile of the metabolomic aging score was also associated with longer life expectancy, with a gain of 7.84 years at age 45 years (95% CI, 7.18–8.50) compared to the highest quartile (Fig. C, Supplementary Table S18). The pattern of results was similar among individuals aged 55 and 65 years. In contrast, accelerated proteomic aging was not associated with a significant reduction in life expectancy among individuals with DR (1.23 years; 95% CI, −4.18 to 6.65) but was associated with reduced life expectancy among those without DR (loss of 3.85 years; 95% CI, 2.99–4.71; Supplementary Table S19). Results remained consistent after further adjustment for DN and after excluding baseline CVD, CKD, or cancer (Supplementary Tables S20–S23). Estimates of years of life gained for PhenoAgeAccel and frailty status in individuals with DR are presented in Supplementary Tables S24 to S25, given their comparable discriminative performance to the metabolomic aging score.

Discussion

In this study, we have, for the first time, demonstrated that more advanced metabolomic and proteomic BA were associated with a higher prevalence of DR at baseline and an increased risk of incident DR. The risk associated with these two measures was independent of and additive to HbA1c levels and diabetes duration. At age 45 years, individuals with DR but without advanced metabolomic aging exhibited a longer life expectancy than those with DR and advanced metabolomic aging. These findings suggest future directions for DR risk assessment, although the causal roles of BA-related processes in the development and progression of DR remain to be established.

There is accumulating evidence linking DR with BA, including markers such as LTL, DNA-methylation clocks, and clinical biomarker–based age measures.13–16,35,36 However, previous studies on LTL have shown inconsistent associations with DR across studies.13,14 A cross-sectional study reported reduced mean telomere length in patients with DR compared with those without,13 whereas a larger Polish cohort study found no independent association between LTL and DR.14 Because DR involves systemic metabolic and microvascular alterations, a single biomarker (e.g., LTL) provides limited insight into organ-level or whole-body aging. Furthermore, a prospective analysis conducted by Roshandel et al.35 demonstrated that the four DNA methylation clocks were not associated with the risk of DR. Because tissues age at different rates, there are inherent limits to how well aging signatures in blood cells can capture and explain aging processes occurring in other organs. In contrast, the intrinsic sensitivity of metabolomics to physiological and disease-related perturbations enables it to capture system-wide changes in energy metabolism, amino acid turnover, lipid transport, and inflammatory pathways, whereas proteins act as direct mediators of biological processes in human disease.9,18 Our study, based on two omics-derived BA measures, consistently demonstrated their positive associations with DR risk in both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses after extensive adjustment. However, the incremental improvement in discrimination after incorporating the metabolomic aging score into the clinical model was modest (ΔC = 0.005) and may not translate into meaningful clinical benefit. While these BA measures have been shown to improve risk prediction for multiple chronic conditions, their added clinical value in DR prediction appears limited and requires further validation.

Several biological pathways may plausibly link accelerated BA to DR.37,38 Higher metabolomic and proteomic BA measures likely reflect systemic pro-aging states, including mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, metabolic dysregulation, endothelial dysfunction, cellular senescence, and impaired proteostasis, all of which have been implicated in DR pathophysiology.39,40 In the retina, these processes may not only promote endothelial injury, blood–retinal barrier breakdown, and progressive vascular leakage41 but also increase susceptibility to retinal neurodegeneration.42 Hyperglycemia-induced premature senescence of retinal endothelial cells may contribute to progressive retinal microvascular injury.43 Therefore, omics-derived BA measures may index systemic aging-related states that are biologically aligned with susceptibility to both retinal vascular injury and neurodegeneration in DR. In addition, prediabetes or diabetes itself may further accelerate BA through chronic hyperglycemia and disordered lipid metabolism, both of which are associated with cellular senescence and may further promote microvascular dysfunction. Hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress and metabolic perturbations have been implicated in accelerating systemic aging-related processes. While our prospective analysis and the exclusion of incident cases within the first 2 years of follow-up still support a positive association between advanced BA and DR risk, the potential for reverse causation cannot be entirely ruled out. Further studies are needed to elucidate these mechanisms and to determine the directionality and potential causal relationships between omics-derived BA and DR.

Moreover, the modifiability of BA has been proposed in several studies. For example, a recent study reported that a 12-week structured exercise intervention was associated with a reduction in proteomic age acceleration.44 Additionally, lifestyle factors such as smoking, sleep duration, diet, and physical activity have been associated with the metabolomic aging profile, MetaboHealth,45 and other blood-based biological age measures have been shown to decrease following dietary interventions such as fasting-mimicking diets.46 Of note, our findings suggest additive interactions between these two BA measures and glycemic burden (HbA1c levels and diabetes duration), whereby these factors may reinforce one another. Additive interaction represents the additional absolute risk that arises when two exposures occur together and is considered more meaningful for public health evaluation.47 Therefore, we speculate that modification of omics-based BA status may be associated with a lower risk of DR in populations with poor glycemic control or long diabetes duration; however, this hypothesis warrants further investigation.

We found that life expectancy is lower in patients with DR than in those without the disease. Previous studies support our findings, showing that DR is significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality2–4 and that this association is independent of DN.5 Notably, people with DR and the highest quartile of metabolomic aging had an increased mortality risk (HR = 2.19; 95% CI, 1.37–3.48) and lost 7.23 years of life expectancy at the age of 45 years, compared with those with DR in the lowest quartile of metabolomic aging. In this study, although the metabolomic aging score, PhenoAgeAccel, and frailty index showed comparable discriminative value when combined with clinical indicator models, the highest quartile of the metabolomic aging score was associated with a greater estimated loss of life expectancy (7.23 years) in individuals with DR compared with PhenoAgeAccel (4.44 years) and the frailty index (4.86 years). We also noted that people with DR and advanced metabolomic aging had increased mortality risk and lower life expectancy at age 45 years than people without DR without advanced metabolomic aging. Thus, monitoring metabolomic aging status and considering aging-targeted interventions may help identify and manage high-risk individuals with DR more effectively.

To explain the inconsistent results between metabolomic and proteomic aging measures—specifically the lack of incremental predictive value and nonsignificant reduction in life expectancy among individuals with DR for the proteomic measure—we propose two possibilities. First, the metabolomic aging score was trained on all-cause mortality and may therefore more directly capture systemic metabolic dysregulation, lipid metabolism, and energy-related pathways that are closely linked to diabetes and retinal microvascular injury. By contrast, the proteomic aging measure was optimized primarily for chronological age prediction and may be less specific to diabetes-related retinal pathology. Second, the substantial difference in sample sizes should be taken into account. The proteomic cohort (N = 8633) was smaller than the metabolomic cohort (N = 68,155), which likely reduced statistical precision and power. Despite these differences, both measures across different omics layers showed consistent and significant associations with incident DR risk. Hence, the effect of proteomic aging cannot be ruled out and may provide complementary information, warranting further investigation in larger and more balanced cohorts.

Several limitations should be considered. First, as a volunteer cohort, the UKB is not fully representative, as participants are generally healthier and wealthier.48 Consequently, participants may have a younger biological age than would be observed in the general population. Such selection characteristics are likely to bias associations toward the null, suggesting that our findings may be conservative. Second, this was an observational study. Although we identified a prospective association between advanced biological age and DR, causal inference cannot be made, even though the association remained robust after excluding incident DR cases occurring within the first 2 years of follow-up. Third, reliance on medical records and a single ICD-10 code for DR ascertainment may underestimate true prevalence, particularly in early asymptomatic stages. More importantly, the lack of information on DR staging (e.g., Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study grading) represents a drawback that limits the clinical implications of our findings. Since metabolomic and proteomic profiles, along with patterns of systemic comorbidities and diabetes-related mortality risk, are likely to vary with DR severity, our inability to distinguish between DR stages may have masked stage-specific biological associations. This averaging effect may underestimate stage-specific omics signatures and the mortality risk associated with advanced DR. Moreover, the elderly population with a long duration of diabetes faces a significantly high risk of both DR and mortality, requiring rigorous handling of death as a competing risk. Although we addressed this in our longitudinal analyses by applying multistate models to account for competing transitions from baseline to death, our cross-sectional findings remain potentially susceptible to survivor bias. Since those with the most advanced biological age and severe DR may have died prior to recruitment, our prevalent cohort likely consists of “survivors” with relatively better health profiles. This type of selection bias may attenuate the observed associations, suggesting that our findings are likely to underestimate the true effects. Fourth, metabolomic and proteomic profiling were based on a single time point, which may not capture fluctuations over time. Longitudinal studies with repeated measurements of BA and DR status would help address this limitation by evaluating whether greater increases in biological age over time are associated with a higher subsequent risk of DR progression. Fifth, the predominantly white composition of the cohort limits the generalizability to other populations. Lastly, as with observational studies in general, residual confounding may still be present despite adjustments for known confounders.

Conclusions

In summary, advanced metabolomic and proteomic biological ages were associated with a higher risk of prevalent and incident DR. Because interindividual differences in the pace of BA emerge early in adulthood, omics–based aging measures may help identify individuals at elevated risk before clinical microvascular complications develop.
