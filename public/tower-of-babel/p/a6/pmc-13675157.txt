Mechanism of palytoxin-induced ferroptosis in HaCaT cells via targeting TrxR1
Authors: Cheng Zhong, Zihan Zhao, Dong Chen, Longze Lü, Wanxin Ren, Xinhao Li, Bowen Deng, Chengming Yang, Yao Yang, Lianghua Wang, Mingjuan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPLTX (Palytoxin) is a fatty-chain polyether marine biotoxin originally produced by benthic dinoflagellates, particularly Ostreopsis ovata and Ostreopsis siamensis. Via the marine food web, PLTX bioaccumulates in numerous organisms such as corals and shellfish, and ranks among the most lethal non-proteinaceous natural compounds described to date. Thus far, its toxicological mechanism remains unclear, and no specific clinical antidote is available. In this study, we employed a limited proteolysis‑based chemical proteomics approach (Lip‑SMap) to analyze the protection of protease cleavage sites conferred by PLTX binding. Combined with DIA (data‑independent acquisition) mass spectrometry in HaCaT (human immortalized keratinocyte) cells, we identified TrxR1 (thioredoxin reductase 1) as an intracellular direct target of PLTX. Molecular docking simulations revealed that PLTX forms stable hydrogen bonds with the active‑site residues Q230 and N234 of TrxR1. BLI (Biolayer interferometry) further confirmed a moderate‑to‑high affinity interaction between them (KD = 5.415 × 10–1 μM). Confocal fluorescence imaging demonstrated significant spatial colocalization of PLTX with TrxR1 in the cytoplasm, providing visual evidence for their direct intracellular interaction. Functional assays showed that PLTX specifically inhibits TrxR1 activity in a concentration‑dependent manner, thereby compromising the cystine/GSH/GPx4 antioxidant axis. The ensuing cascade—glutathione (GSH) depletion, loss of GPx4 (glutathione peroxidase 4) activity, and build-up of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and malondialdehyde (MDA)—ultimately drives the cell toward ferroptosis. Importantly, overexpression of TrxR1 significantly reversed PLTX‑induced GSH depletion, the decrease in GPx4 activity, and MDA accumulation, confirming the central role of TrxR1 in PLTX toxicity. Collectively, our findings uncover a previously unrecognized mechanism by which PLTX induces ferroptosis through direct inhibition of TrxR1 in HaCaT cells. These findings not only offer a molecular basis for understanding the toxicity of PLTX, but also identify TrxR1 as a potential therapeutic target. As such, they provide a theoretical foundation for the prevention and treatment of marine toxin poisoning, as well as for the development of related antidotes.Graphical Abstract(1) Lip‑SMap chemoproteomics identified TrxR1 as a direct intracellular target of palytoxin (PLTX) in HaCaT cells.(2) PLTX specifically binds to TrxR1.(3) PLTX targets and inhibits TrxR1, mediating ferroptosis via the cystine/GSH/GPx4 axis.(4) Overexpression of TrxR1 reverses PLTX‑induced cytotoxicity and ferroptotic damage. Introduction

Marine biotoxins are a class of highly potent natural toxins produced by marine organisms. Due to their strong biological activities and complex toxicological mechanisms, they have long been a major focus of toxicological research. Chemically, these toxins primarily include polyethers, alkaloids, and macrolides, with most being structurally complex polyether compounds (Patocka et al. 2018). Palytoxin (PLTX) is an exceptionally potent polyether marine toxin primarily produced by benthic dinoflagellates, particularly Ostreopsis ovata and related species. By moving up the marine food web, PLTX becomes concentrated in diverse taxa—among them sea anemones, corals, and bivalve mollusks—and thereby gains entry to the human diet. Its potency is exceptional: among natural compounds with a fully resolved chemical structure, few non-peptide substances rival its toxicity (Croskey et al. 2024; Pelin et al. 2016a; Sosa et al. 2022). Consumption of contaminated seafood, such as shellfish and mussels, therefore represents an important route of human exposure. Additionally, during Ostreopsis blooms, PLTX and its analogues (ovatoxins) can be released into marine aerosols through wave action, causing respiratory symptoms (e.g., fever, cough, bronchoconstriction, conjunctivitis) in coastal populations via inhalation exposure (Dell’Aversano and Tartaglione 2018; Ruge 2014). The toxic effects of PLTX involve multiple molecular mechanisms, including interference with ion channels, disruption of cell membrane integrity, and aberrant activation of intracellular signaling pathways. These features render PLTX highly valuable for research on toxicological mechanisms and risk assessment (Kanai et al. 2025; Sala et al. 2009).

PLTX poisoning primarily affects the skin, eyes, respiratory tract, cardiovascular system, gastrointestinal tract, and nervous system, with epithelial, endothelial, and neural tissues being particularly susceptible. In severe cases, it can lead to clinical emergencies such as rhabdomyolysis, cardiac arrest due to myocardial injury, and anaphylactic shock (Thaikruea 2023). Additionally, PLTX acts as a cytolysin capable of forming pores in erythrocyte membranes, leading to osmotic hemolysis (Cavion et al. 2022; Hammond and Roy 2024; Pelin et al. 2016b). Human exposure to PLTX occurs predominantly through dermal contact, inhalation of toxin-containing aerosols, and, less frequently, ocular exposure. Cutaneous exposure typically causes acute burning pain and edema, which may progress to erythema and tissue necrosis. Ocular intoxication can produce a broad spectrum of corneal injuries, ranging from superficial punctate epithelial keratopathy to severe corneal melting and perforation (Farooq et al. 2017). Inhalation exposure is often accompanied by fever and respiratory symptoms such as bronchoconstriction, dyspnea, and wheezing, with some cases complicated by conjunctivitis (Ternon et al. 2022). Given the broad tissue tropism of PLTX and its strong pathological association with epithelial damage, we therefore adopted HaCaT keratinocytes as an epithelial model to dissect the cellular and molecular events that drive PLTX toxicity.

Despite decades of investigation, the molecular basis of PLTX toxicity remains incompletely understood, and its toxicological and pharmacological properties require further systematic characterization. Historically, research on marine biotoxins has primarily focused on their poisoning characteristics, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention strategies. At the molecular level, the conventional understanding has been that these toxins bind to specific receptors on the cell membrane, alter the function of relevant ion channels, and interfere with electrical signal transduction, thereby disrupting physiological functions and producing toxic effects (Pelin et al. 2020; Sosa et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024). Of the targets characterized so far, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is regarded as the chief receptor through which PLTX exerts its toxicity. A growing body of work shows that, upon engaging the sodium–potassium pump, PLTX drives a marked conformational change that turns the transporter into a non-selective cation channel—an event that destabilizes intracellular ion balance and eventually proves lethal to the cell. Specifically, after binding to the sodium‑potassium pump, the target protein dynamically transitions between an open conformation and its normal state, with approximately a 90% probability of remaining in the open state. The protein can revert to the closed conformation upon toxin dissociation. In the open state, millions of ions flow through this aberrant channel per second—a flux far exceeding the normal transport rate of approximately one hundred ions per second for unmodified transporter proteins. This severe disruption of ionic gradients can not only cause osmotic hemolysis in erythrocytes but also induce hypercontraction in cardiomyocytes and other muscle cells (Hu et al. 2019; Kanai et al. 2025; Qiu et al. 2023).

Furthermore, further hypotheses regarding the mechanism of action of this target have been proposed. For instance, PLTX may bind to the B3 molecule (Band 3 protein, AE1) on the cell membrane to form a complex, thereby further exacerbating intracellular ion imbalance. Beyond studies focusing on Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, other research has suggested that the damaging mechanism of PLTX may involve the induction of mitochondrial dysfunction, promotion of lactate dehydrogenase release, triggering of inflammatory responses, and modulation of related gene expression (Wang et al. 2024). To date, however, none of these studies has pinned down the precise molecular basis of PLTX-induced toxicity.

Further evidence suggests that Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase may not fully account for the diverse biological effects of PLTX. Ouabain (OUA), a well-established Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase inhibitor, competitively interacts with PLTX and is widely used to attenuate PLTX-induced cellular responses in vitro. However, studies in intact HaCaT keratinocytes have demonstrated that OUA only partially suppresses PLTX-induced cytotoxicity, despite its antagonistic effects on Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase-mediated signaling (Pelin et al. 2013a). Pharmacological analyses further revealed complex interactions between OUA and PLTX, with OUA exhibiting concentration-dependent competitive, non-competitive, or allosteric antagonistic properties under different experimental conditions. Importantly, the inability of OUA to completely abolish PLTX-induced toxicity suggests that additional OUA-insensitive binding sites or alternative molecular targets may contribute to the cellular effects of PLTX. These observations challenge the view that Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is the sole mediator of PLTX toxicity and support the existence of previously unrecognized molecular mechanisms. Consequently, the systematic identification of novel intracellular targets is essential for a more comprehensive understanding of PLTX-induced cellular injury.

Recent evidence has further expanded our understanding of the cellular responses elicited by PLTX. Cheng et al. (2022) reported that PLTX exposure markedly increased intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and shifted the levels of pivotal ferroptosis regulators—notably ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) and voltage-dependent anion channel 3 (VDAC3)—pointing to a possible role for ferroptosis in PLTX-induced cellular injury. In addition, fluorescence colocalization analyses revealed that PLTX is distributed not only at the plasma membrane but also throughout the cytoplasm of HaCaT cells, indicating that the toxin can access the intracellular compartment and may interact with previously unidentified intracellular targets (Cheng et al. 2022).

Collectively, these findings raise the possibility that PLTX-induced toxicity extends beyond membrane-associated signaling events and involves intracellular molecular mechanisms that remain largely unexplored. In particular, the identity of OUA-insensitive intracellular targets and their contribution to ferroptotic cell death have yet to be determined. Addressing these questions is essential for achieving a more comprehensive understanding of PLTX toxicity. To this end, we employed a multi-layered strategy integrating limited proteolysis–small-molecule mapping (Lip-SMap), molecular interaction analyses, and functional validation assays to systematically identify intracellular targets of PLTX and characterize the molecular mechanisms underlying PLTX-induced ferroptosis. Our findings reveal thioredoxin reductase 1 (TrxR1) as a direct intracellular target of PLTX and establish a mechanistic link between TrxR1 inhibition and ferroptotic cell death, providing new insights into the molecular basis of PLTX toxicity.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and chemicals

The human immortalized keratinocyte line HaCaT was sourced from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC, Wuhan, China). Routine propagation used high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, USA) to which 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA) had been added, with cultures kept under a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. Lyophilized PLTX (0.1 mg per vial; Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan) was reconstituted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a 1 mg mL−1 stock and archived at −20 °C. Immediately before each experiment, this stock was diluted in culture medium to yield freshly made working solutions.

CCK‑8 assay

HaCaT cells were plated in 96-well dishes (5 × 103 cells/well) and left to attach overnight before being challenged with the specified PLTX concentrations for 4 h. Viability was then quantified with the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Solarbio, Beijing, China) per the supplier’s protocol, and optical density was read at 450 nm on a SpectraMax M2 plate reader (Molecular Devices, USA). For co-treatment, PLTX (specified concentrations) was applied together with 10 μM auranofin for 4 h; a separate arm received 10 μM auranofin alone for 4 h.

Lip-SMap chemoproteomic analysis of toxin-target interactions

HaCaT cells were collected and disrupted in ice-cold PBS containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, China), after which total protein was quantified by a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) kit (Epizyme, China). Per condition, 100 μg of lysate received either PLTX (10 pM or 1000 pM) or a matched volume of vehicle (PBS); because PLTX was carried in PBS, residual dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) never exceeded 0.01%. Binding proceeded for 30 min at 25 °C under gentle agitation. Limited proteolysis was then initiated by adding proteinase K (PK; Thermo Scientific, USA) at a 1:100 (w/w) enzyme-to-substrate ratio and letting digestion run for 5 min at 25 °C, after which 2 × Laemmli buffer plus a 95 °C, 5-min heat step quenched the reaction. For downstream mass spectrometry, PK-treated material was reduced (20 mM DTT, 30 °C, 2 h) and alkylated (25 mM iodoacetamide, room temperature, 30 min, protected from light). Samples were diluted fivefold into 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC), digested overnight (16 h, 37 °C) with sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega, USA) at a 1:50 (w/w) ratio, then desalted over C18 spin columns (Thermo Scientific), dried, and taken up in 0.1% (v/v) aqueous formic acid (FA).

Liquid chromatography‑tandem mass spectrometry (LC‑MS/MS) was carried out by interfacing a nano-flow Easy nLC-1200 system with a Q-Exactive HF-X instrument (both Thermo Scientific). Peptides were resolved at 300 nL/min on a 25-cm column (75 μm i.d., packed with 1.9 μm C18 resin) using buffer A (0.1% FA in water) and buffer B (0.1% FA in 84% acetonitrile); the elution profile ramped B from 8 to 30% across 40 min, climbed to 100% over the next 10 min, and held at 100% for a further 70 min, for a 120-min cycle. Acquisition followed a data-independent (DIA) scheme. Survey scans spanned 350–1800 m/z (resolution 60,000 at m/z 200; AGC 3 × 10⁶; maximum fill 20 ms), and fragment spectra were collected across thirty variable isolation windows (resolution 30,000 at m/z 200; AGC 1 × 10⁶; maximum fill 60 ms; NCE 30 eV). Spiked iRT standards (Biognosys) anchored the retention-time scale. Spectronaut™ 15 (Biognosys) processed the raw files under default parameters, and peptides were deemed significantly changed when FC exceeded 1.5 or fell below 0.63 with a Student’s t-test p < 0.05.

Molecular docking simulation

The three‑dimensional structure of TrxR1 was retrieved from the UniProt and AlphaFold 2.0 databases. The structural file (SDF format) of PLTX was downloaded from a compound database. The PLTX structure was energy‑minimized using Discovery Studio software and converted into PDB format. Molecular dynamics simulation of PLTX was performed using GROMACS software to obtain its most stable conformation for subsequent docking. In the docking analysis, PLTX was set as the ligand and the TrxR1 protein as the receptor. Semi-flexible docking simulations were carried out by HEX version 8.0. The detailed protocol of molecular docking is described in Method S1.

BLI affinity assay

TrxR1 was labeled using biotin NHS-PEG12-Biotin (Thermo Scientific, USA), and free biotin was removed by purification with a PD MiniTrap™ G-25 desalting column (Cytiva, USA) (Bates et al. 2025). The labeled TrxR1 protein solution (prepared in PBS) was diluted to 1 μg/mL and immobilized onto the surface of a streptavidin (SA) biosensor (Sartorius, Germany). Subsequently, serial concentrations of PLTX solution (4, 2, 1, 0.5, 0.25, and 0.125 μM) were prepared in PBS. Kinetic binding analysis was performed at 25 °C using a Sartorius Octet K2 biomolecular interaction analyzer. The TrxR1-immobilized sensor probes were immersed into PLTX solutions of varying concentrations, with an association time of 120 s and a dissociation time of 240 s.

It should be noted that the PLTX concentrations used in the Lip-SMap assay (10 pM and 1000 pM) were selected for cell-free lysate binding conditions, where the interaction signal depends on solution-phase binding affinity rather than cellular uptake or downstream amplification. These concentrations differ from those used in live-cell functional assays (see Section "Cytotoxicity of PLTX on HaCaT Cells"), which were chosen to span one order of magnitude above and below the cellular IC₅₀ determined in intact HaCaT cells.

TrxR1 enzyme activity assay

HaCaT cells were seeded in 6‑well plates and subjected to the indicated treatments. For the specificity experiment, cells were divided into three groups: control (untreated), PLTX‑treated (3.73 × 102 pM), and okadaic acid (OA)‑treated (1.24 × 10–2 μM). For the concentration‑dependent experiment, cells were treated with PLTX at 50, 100, or 200 pM, or with the TrxR1 inhibitor auranofin (20 μM) as a positive control. PLTX was dissolved in PBS, whereas auranofin was dissolved in DMSO. The final concentration of DMSO in all treatment groups was maintained below 0.1% (v/v).

Two hours after treatment, monolayers were washed twice in chilled PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) fortified with a protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were clarified by spinning at 12,000 × g (15 min, 4 °C), and the cleared supernatant was assayed for protein with a BCA kit (Epizyme, China). Enzyme activity was then determined with a TrxR1 Activity Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) per the supplier’s directions: aliquots normalized to 20 μg protein were combined with a reaction cocktail of NADPH and 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), and the appearance of 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoic acid (TNB) was followed at 412 nm on a SpectraMax M2 reader (Molecular Devices, USA). Activity was derived from the TNB molar extinction coefficient (13.6 mM⁻1·cm⁻1) and scaled to untreated controls.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy analysis

Immunofluorescence staining was employed to label PLTX, TrxR1, and the cell membrane, followed by qualitative and subcellular localization analysis using a laser scanning confocal microscope. HaCaT cells were plated onto glass coverslips in 6-well plates and incubated for 24 h, then exposed to 2 × 104 pM PLTX for 5 min. Fixation used 4% paraformaldehyde (20 min), followed by permeabilization in 0.3% Triton X-100 (30 min) and a 10-min block in 5% QuickBlock™ Blocking Buffer for Immunol Staining (Beyotime, China). Except where noted, three TBST (Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20) rinses separated successive incubation steps. TrxR1 was probed with a primary anti-TrxR1 antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA; 1:250; 1 h, 37 °C) and then a CoraLite® Plus 647 secondary antibody (Proteintech, USA; 1:250; 1 h, room temperature, shielded from light). To label PLTX, a 5′-TAMRA-tagged PLTX-13 aptamer was applied, with a scrambled oligonucleotide serving as the negative control. PKH67 (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) delineated the plasma membrane, while Hoechst 33,342 (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) marked nuclei. Following staining, coverslips were mounted using an antifade mounting medium and imaged with a laser scanning confocal microscope. The PLTX-13 aptamer was first described by Gao et al. and has been demonstrated to possess strong affinity and selectivity for PLTX (Gao et al. 2017). Details regarding the aptamer sequence and its characteristics are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Table S1).

Aptamer-specific rescue assay

The PLTX‑specific aptamer (13‑S3) and a non‑specific control aptamer (unlabeled or 5′‑TAMRA‑labeled) were dissolved in binding buffer (20 mM Tris‑HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 5 mM KCl, pH 7.5). PLTX was incubated with each aptamer at a 1:1 molar ratio for 15 min at 25 °C with gentle agitation to allow complex formation. The resulting mixtures were added to HaCaT cells and incubated for 4 h. Cell viability was subsequently determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay as described above.

Intracellular ROS detection

The fluorogenic probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) reported intracellular ROS. After a 4-h exposure to PLTX (3.73, 37.3, or 373 pM), cells were rinsed three times in DMEM, loaded with 10 μM DCFH-DA (37 °C, 20 min, dark), and rinsed three further times in DMEM to clear unincorporated probe. A parallel positive control received the ROS inducer Rosup (100 μg mL⁻1) under otherwise identical handling. Imaging was performed at ×400 on an Olympus IX73 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Determination of reduced GSH and MDA levels

HaCaT cells were treated with PLTX at 3.73 × 102 pM and 3.73 pM for 4 h. For combination experiments, cells were treated with PLTX (3.73 pM or 3.73 × 102 pM) with or without auranofin (10 μM) for 4 h. The positive control group received the ferroptosis inducer Erastin (30 μM) for 4 h, while all other conditions remained identical to those of the experimental groups. Subsequently, the intracellular reduced GSH (Glutathione) and malondialdehyde (MDA) were measured strictly according to the manufacturer’s protocols using the GSH Content Assay Kit (Solarbio, China) and the MDA Content Assay Kit (Solarbio, China), respectively.

GPx4 activity assay

The procedures for cell culture and drug treatment were identical to those described in Section "Intracellular ROS Detection". The enzymatic activity of intracellular GPx4 (Glutathione Peroxidase 4) was measured using a Glutathione Peroxidase Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) strictly according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Cell transfection

The construction of lentiviral vectors for the overexpression and knockdown of TrxR1 was performed by Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd. A HaCaT cell model with stable TrxR1 overexpression was subsequently established via lentiviral infection. For the detailed protocol of lentiviral transduction and the TrxR1 overexpression sequence, refer to Method S2.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT–PCR)

TRIzol served to recover total RNA following the vendor’s protocol. From each sample, 1 μg of RNA was first cleared of contaminating genomic DNA with gDNA Eraser (Takara, China) and then converted to first-strand cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, China). Amplification employed SYBR Green chemistry on a QuantStudio™ 5 platform (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as the housekeeping reference. Primer details appear in Supplementary Table S1, and transcript abundance was expressed via the 2^ − ΔΔCt approach (Livak and Schmittgen 2001).

Western blot analysis

Whole-cell protein was extracted with M-PER™ Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher, USA), harvested with a cell scraper, and clarified by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C). After quantification with an Omni-Easy™ Instant BCA Protein Assay Kit (Epizyme, China), equal protein loads were resolved by SDS–PAGE (gels cast with a PAGE Gel Rapid Preparation Kit, Epizyme, China) and electro-transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes, which were then saturated in 5% non-fat milk/TBST (1 h, room temperature). Primary detection was carried out overnight at 4 °C with a mouse monoclonal anti-TrxR1 antibody (B-2, sc-28321; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA; 1:500) or a mouse monoclonal anti-GAPDH antibody (Beyotime, China; 1:1,000) in primary antibody dilution buffer (Beyotime, China), GAPDH serving as the loading reference. Membranes were rinsed three times in TBST and exposed for 2 h at room temperature to an HRP-linked goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Beyotime, China; 1:5,000 in Secondary Antibody Dilution Buffer, Beyotime, China). Signals were developed with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and captured on a ChemiDoc™ system (Bio-Rad, USA); TrxR1 and GAPDH resolved at the anticipated ~55 kDa and ~37 kDa. Densitometry was performed in ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA), with TrxR1 signals referenced to GAPDH and expressed relative to the matched control. Three independent biological replicates were analyzed by Student’s t-test, taking P < 0.05 as significant.

Statistical analysis

Every experiment comprised no fewer than three independent biological replicates, and values are reported as mean ± SEM. All analyses used GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA): paired conditions were contrasted by two-tailed Student’s t-test and multi-group data by one-way ANOVA, with significance set at P < 0.05.

Public proteomics data deposition and quality control

The mass spectrometry proteomics data generated in this study have been deposited in the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository (Perez-Riverol et al. 2022) under dataset identifier PXD060388 (available at https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pride/archive/projects/PXD060388). Raw data‑independent acquisition (DIA) data were acquired as a contracted service by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. (APT, Shanghai, China) using a Q‑Exactive HF‑X mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled to an Easy nLC‑1200 nanoflow liquid chromatography system. LC‑MS/MS acquisition parameters were as follows: a 120‑min gradient (8–30% buffer B over 40 min, 30–100% over 10 min, held at 100% for 70 min) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min; MS1 scan range 350–1650 m/z (resolution 60,000 at m/z 200; automatic gain control (AGC) target 3 × 10⁶; maximum injection time 20 ms); 30 variable DIA windows (resolution 30,000 at m/z 200; AGC target 1 × 10⁶; normalized collision energy 30 eV). iRT peptides (Biognosys) were spiked into each sample for retention time calibration.

Raw DIA files were processed using Spectronaut™ software (version 14.4.200727.47784; Biognosys). Peptide and protein identifications were filtered at a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1% at both levels (Q‑value cutoff = 0.01). The spectral library was generated from data‑dependent acquisition (DDA) data searched against the UniProtKB Homo sapiens reference proteome (uniprot‑human__204995‑2022.07.30.fasta, containing 204,995 entries as of 30 July 2022). Trypsin was specified as the protease, allowing a maximum of one missed cleavage; carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, and oxidation of methionine and acetylation of protein N‑termini as variable modifications. In total, 21,413 peptides and 2,814 protein groups were quantified across all nine samples (three biological replicates per condition: control, Drug1, Drug2). The median iRT retention time deviation was within ±1.5 min across all runs, and the average number of data points per chromatographic peak exceeded five, confirming the robustness of the DIA acquisition (see Section 4 of the APT project report, P20220905415, for full quality control metrics).

Results

Cytotoxicity of PLTX on HaCaT cells

To evaluate the cytotoxic effects of PLTX on HaCaT cells, cells were exposed to increasing concentrations of PLTX (3.73 × 103, 3.73 × 102, 3.73 × 101, 3.73, 3.73 × 10–1, and 3.73 × 10–2 pM) for 4 h, and cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. PLTX treatment resulted in a marked concentration-dependent reduction in cell viability (Fig. 1). Nonlinear regression analysis yielded an half‑maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value of approximately 50 pM, indicating the high sensitivity of HaCaT cells to PLTX exposure. Based on the IC₅₀ value, two concentrations were selected for subsequent mechanistic studies: 3.73 pM, representing a sub-cytotoxic concentration, and 373 pM, representing a strongly cytotoxic concentration. These concentrations enabled the investigation of both early cellular responses and pronounced toxic effects associated with PLTX exposure.

Identification of TrxR1 as an intracellular target via lip-SMap chemoproteomics integrated with DIA mass spectrometry

To identify direct intracellular targets of PLTX in HaCaT cells, we employed limited proteolysis–small molecule mapping (Lip-SMap), a chemical proteomics approach that enables the detection of protein–ligand interactions through ligand-induced protection of protease-sensitive regions. Coupled with data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry, this strategy allows proteome-wide identification and mapping of potential small-molecule binding sites without the need for chemical modification of the ligand (Feng et al. 2014; Guo et al. 2017; Leuenberger et al. 2017; Schopper et al. 2017; Tagore et al. 2008). Using this workflow, we systematically profiled PLTX-induced proteolytic protection patterns in HaCaT cell lysates and identified a subset of proteins exhibiting significant peptide abundance changes following PLTX treatment, suggesting direct or proximal interactions with the toxin.

To identify proteomic changes associated with PLTX binding, peptide abundances were quantitatively compared between untreated and PLTX-treated samples. Differential peptides were identified based on fold change (FC) and statistical significance (P value), and the results were visualized using volcano plots (Fig. 2B). PLTX treatment induced significant alterations in the abundance of a subset of peptides, consistent with ligand-dependent protection of protease-sensitive regions and the generation of distinct proteolytic signatures. Peptides whose abundance fell significantly (FC < 0.63, P < 0.05) were assigned to the down-regulated set, while those rising significantly (FC > 1.5, P < 0.05) formed the up-regulated set. Representative volcano plots from the low-concentration PLTX group (1 × 10–11 pM) revealed a distinct distribution of differentially abundant peptides relative to the untreated control, with the corresponding proteins showing the most prominent changes annotated in the plot (Fig. 2B).

To further characterize proteins associated with PLTX-induced proteolytic changes, we performed DIA-based quantitative proteomic analysis followed by Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and enrichment analyses. Functional classification revealed that the differentially represented proteins were distributed across diverse biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components (Fig. 2C).

GO enrichment analysis further demonstrated that PLTX-responsive proteins were significantly enriched in specific functional categories, indicating that PLTX binding is associated with coordinated alterations in multiple cellular pathways (Fig. 2D, E). Notably, the enriched terms were predominantly related to cellular redox regulation, metabolic processes, and intracellular protein functions, suggesting that PLTX may exert its toxic effects through modulation of intracellular signaling and stress-response networks.

As a positive control to validate the reliability of our Lip-SMap approach, we examined whether the dataset captured the established PLTX target Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Notably, peptides derived from the α1 subunit (ATP1A1) exhibited significant PLTX-induced abundance changes (see Supplementary Table S3 for peptide sequences and quantitative data), which is consistent with the classical mechanism (Hu et al. 2019; Kanai et al. 2025; Qiu et al. 2023). This result confirms the robustness of our chemoproteomic screening platform.

Interestingly, caveolin-1 (CAV1) was also identified among the PLTX-interacting proteins in our Lip-SMap analysis (Supplementary Table S3). To our knowledge, an association between PLTX and caveolin-1 has not been documented before. As the defining scaffold protein of caveolae—cholesterol- and sphingolipid-enriched membrane pits that mediate the endocytic uptake of varied ligands, toxins, and pathogens—CAV1 occupies a strategic position at the cell surface (Vilariño and Botana n.d.). Given CAV1’s central role in caveolae-mediated endocytosis, its marked upregulation in peptide abundance upon PLTX binding raises the possibility that PLTX may be internalized, at least in part, through caveolae-dependent mechanisms. This hypothesis, if validated, could provide a plausible explanation for how PLTX crosses the plasma membrane to access intracellular targets such as TrxR1. While further experimental validation is needed to confirm a functional role of CAV1 in PLTX internalization, this Lip-SMap finding offers an intriguing clue that may help elucidate the cellular entry pathway of this large, amphipathic toxin.

We identified 30 and 62 proteins as potential direct targets in the low-concentration (Drug1, 1 × 10–11 M) and high-concentration (Drug2, 1 × 10–9 M) PLTX treatment groups, respectively (Fig. 2E; full lists are provided in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). Among these candidates, we focused on TrxR1 for further validation because: (i) GO enrichment analysis of the high-concentration dataset revealed significant enrichment of redox-related functions, including “oxidoreductase activity” and “cellular responses to oxidative stress” (Fig. 2D); (ii) TrxR1 is a core element of the thioredoxin antioxidant system and a recognized modulator of ferroptosis—a regulated cell-death program already linked to PLTX cytotoxicity (Cheng et al. 2022); and (iii) TrxR1 was consistently identified in both treatment groups, suggesting its binding even at sub-cytotoxic concentrations. The functional significance of the PLTX–TrxR1 interaction was subsequently validated by molecular docking, biolayer interferometry (BLI), activity assays, and rescue experiments (see below).

We also investigated whether PLTX affects other reductases, such as glutathione reductase (GR). The results showed that GR was not identified as a PLTX-interacting protein in our Lip-SMap analysis (Supplementary Tables S4–S5), suggesting that PLTX does not directly target this reductase.

PLTX directly inhibits TrxR1 activity

Molecular docking simulation validates the interaction between PLTX and TrxR1

Building upon previous experimental observations that PLTX induces increased intracellular levels of ROS (Cheng et al. 2022), we selected the functionally relevant protein TrxR1 from the differentially expressed proteins as a potential target of PLTX. To investigate the molecular interaction mechanism between them in depth, we performed semi-flexible molecular docking analysis using HEX 8.0 software. The stable conformation of PLTX, optimized by molecular dynamics simulation, was used as the ligand, and the TrxR1 protein served as the receptor. The results revealed the formation of two hydrogen-bonding interactions between TrxR1 and PLTX (Fig. 3A), with a docking score of −436.21. Specifically, the carbonyl group of the amide moiety in Gln230 of TrxR1 formed one hydrogen bond with a hydroxyl group in PLTX. Simultaneously, the carbonyl group of the amide moiety in Asn234 formed another hydrogen bond with a different hydroxyl group in PLTX. These intermolecular interactions indicate favorable binding affinity between PLTX and TrxR1, suggesting the potential formation of a stable complex.

To further assess the functional relevance of Q230 and N234 in PLTX recognition, we introduced alanine substitutions at these two positions and repeated the molecular docking simulation. The Q230A/N234A double mutation abolished the key hydrogen-bonding interactions originally formed between PLTX and the wild-type residues. Consequently, the docking score became markedly less favorable, shifting from −436.21 to −385.16, and PLTX adopted a binding pose located farther from the TrxR1 binding pocket (Fig. 3B). These results further corroborate that Q230 and N234 are critical residues involved in stable PLTX–TrxR1 complex formation. Although these residues are located away from the internal hydrophobic active region of TrxR1 (which contains the Cys59/Cys64 and Cys497/Sec498 catalytic motifs), their distal interactions with PLTX may provide an important structural basis for future investigations into how ligand binding at remote sites perturbs the dynamical communication network of the TrxR1 active site and thereby modulates its enzymatic activity.

BLI assay validates the binding affinity between PLTX and TrxR1

The binding characteristics of PLTX and TrxR1 were quantitatively analyzed using BLI. PLTX exhibited concentration-dependent binding to immobilized TrxR1 over the tested concentration range (0.25–4 μM), as reflected by progressive increases in the equilibrium response signal (Fig. 3C, D). Global fitting analysis determined the average equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) to be 5.415 × 10–1 μM, indicating a moderate-to-strong binding affinity between PLTX and TrxR1.

PLTX inhibits TrxR1 activity in a concentration-dependent manner

As shown in Fig. 3E, TrxR1 enzymatic activity gradually decreased with increasing concentrations of PLTX, demonstrating a clear concentration-dependent inhibitory effect. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 3F, treatment with PLTX significantly inhibited TrxR1 activity in HaCaT cells, whereas treatment with OA (another polyether compound) did not induce a significant change, indicating that the inhibitory effect of PLTX on TrxR1 is specific.

Confocal fluorescence imaging verifies intracellular colocalization of PLTX and TrxR1

Confocal fluorescence imaging revealed significant spatial colocalization of PLTX and TrxR1 within HaCaT cells (Fig. 3H). Specifically, nuclei counterstained with 4′,6‑diamidino‑2‑phenylindole (DAPI) exhibited blue fluorescence; the immunofluorescence signal for TrxR1 (red) was predominantly localized in the cytoplasmic region; and the PLTX signal (yellow), detected using a tetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA)‑labeled PLTX‑specific aptamer, showed a diffuse distribution throughout the cytoplasm (Fig. 3I, J). No distinct yellow fluorescence was observed in the negative control group using a non‑specific aptamer, confirming the specificity of the binding signal. The superimposed fluorescence signals from the red (TrxR1) and yellow (PLTX) channels resulted in an orange color. Quantitative analysis using ImageJ software further confirmed a significant colocalization relationship between the two within the cells. These results provide morphological evidence suggesting that PLTX specifically binds to TrxR1 in cells.

It should be noted that the confocal imaging experiments used a higher PLTX concentration (20 nM, 5 min incubation) than the functional assays (picomolar range, 4 h). This higher concentration was chosen to obtain a rapid and sufficiently strong fluorescent signal for unambiguous subcellular localization, a common technical requirement in immunofluorescence microscopy.

Confocal imaging further revealed that PLTX was distributed not only throughout the cytoplasm but also along the plasma membrane and within the nucleus (Fig. 3G–J). This surface signal accords with the well-documented PLTX–Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase interaction, the toxin’s best-characterized plasma-membrane target. Binding of PLTX to the extracellular α-subunit of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase has been reported to induce a conformational transition that converts the pump into a non-selective cation channel (Hu et al. 2019; Qiu et al. 2023). The detection of intracellular PLTX signals further supports the ability of the toxin to access intracellular compartments, consistent with the identification of intracellular targets such as TrxR1.

Functional validation of TAMRA-labeled PLTX-specific aptamer

To exclude the possibility that TAMRA conjugation alters the binding specificity or bioactivity of the PLTX-specific aptamer, we performed a competitive cytotoxicity assay in HaCaT cells. PLTX was pre-incubated with equimolar amounts of unlabeled PLTX-specific aptamer (13-S3), TAMRA-labeled 13-S3, unlabeled control aptamer (non-specific sequence), or TAMRA-labeled control aptamer, following the annealing protocol described previously. As shown in Fig. 3G, both unlabeled and TAMRA-labeled PLTX-specific aptamers significantly rescued PLTX-induced cell death (p < 0.001), whereas control aptamers (with or without TAMRA) exhibited no protective effect. These results demonstrate that TAMRA labeling does not compromise the specific PLTX-binding capacity or the neutralizing activity of the aptamer, supporting its use in fluorescence imaging applications.

PLTX induces oxidative stress and ferroptosis by disrupting the cystine/GSH/GPx4 axis

Because the cystine/GSH/GPx4 axis is pivotal in holding ferroptosis in check, we next asked whether PLTX perturbs it. As the indispensable cofactor that lets GPx4 neutralize lipid peroxides, reduced GSH shields cells against oxidative injury and ferroptotic death (Kang et al. 2021); collapse of this defense is a defining feature of ferroptosis and characteristically presents as GSH loss, GPx4 silencing, and intensified lipid peroxidation (Gao et al. 2017; Kang et al. 2021; Yan et al. 2021).

Consistent with the inhibition of TrxR1, PLTX exposure compromised the cellular antioxidant defenses, reflected in markedly lower intracellular GSH and reduced GPx4 activity (Fig. 4A, B). In turn, these changes fostered intracellular ROS build-up and raised lipid peroxidation (Fig. 4C, D), generating terminal lipid peroxidation products such as MDA (Fig. 4E). Ultimately, this disrupts cellular redox homeostasis, induces oxidative stress, and drives the onset of ferroptosis.

Pharmacological inhibition of TrxR1 sensitizes HaCaT cells to PLTX-induced ferroptosis

To additionally validate the functional involvement of TrxR1 in PLTX‑mediated ferroptosis, we used auranofin, a specific TrxR1 inhibitor, in combination with subcytotoxic concentrations of PLTX. HaCaT cells were treated with PLTX alone (3.73 pM or 3.73 × 102 pM), auranofin alone (10 μM), or their combination for 4 h. Auranofin alone at 10 μM decreased cell viability to roughly 70% of baseline values (Fig. 4F). Co‑treatment with auranofin and PLTX significantly enhanced cytotoxicity compared to either agent alone; the combination of auranofin with 3.73 × 102 pM PLTX decreased cell viability to approximately 35% (p < 0.001 vs. PLTX alone). Consistent with these observations, the combination treatment further lowered intracellular GSH levels (Fig. 4G) and increased MDA accumulation (Fig. 4H) relative to PLTX or auranofin alone. These results demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of TrxR1 sensitizes HaCaT cells to PLTX‑induced ferroptosis, providing additional evidence that TrxR1 plays a central role in mediating PLTX toxicity.

TrxR1 overexpression rescues PLTX-induced toxicity

Construction and validation of a lentivirus-based TrxR1 overexpression cell model

We successfully established a HaCaT cell model stably overexpressing TrxR1 using lentiviral transduction technology. HaCaT cells were transduced separately with lentiviruses carrying the TrxR1 gene overexpression sequence (experimental group) or with empty vector control viruses (control group), and selected in growth medium with 2 μg/mL puromycin. After 48 h, distinct green fluorescent signals were observed in the experimental group under a fluorescence microscope (Fig. 5A), indicating successful initial model construction. Quantitative real-time PCR analysis at the mRNA level further confirmed that TrxR1 expression in the experimental group was approximately 3.2-fold higher than that in the control group, representing a highly significant difference (p < 0.0001; Fig. 5B). Western blot results also demonstrated significantly higher TrxR1 protein expression in the experimental group, approximately 1.49-fold that of the control group (Fig. 5C). A clear target band was observed at the expected molecular weight of approximately 55 kDa (p < 0.05; Fig. 5D), consistent with the predicted size of TrxR1. Collectively, these results confirm the successful establishment of a HaCaT cell line with stable TrxR1 overexpression at both the transcriptional and protein levels.

Cytotoxicity of PLTX on TrxR1-overexpressing and control cells

To determine whether elevated TrxR1 expression modulates cellular sensitivity to PLTX, TrxR1-overexpressing and vector control HaCaT cells were exposed to increasing concentrations of PLTX (3.73 × 103, 3.73 × 102, 3.73 × 101, 3.73, 3.73 × 10⁻1 and 3.73 × 10–2 pM) for 4 h. TrxR1 overexpression significantly increased cell viability across the entire concentration range tested (Fig. 5E), indicating enhanced resistance to PLTX-induced cytotoxicity. Consistent with this observation, the IC50 value for PLTX increased from 2.58 × 101 pM in control cells to 3.08 × 102 pM in TrxR1-overexpressing cells, representing an approximately 12-fold shift in sensitivity. These findings demonstrate that increased TrxR1 expression markedly attenuates PLTX-induced cytotoxicity and further support a functional role for TrxR1 in mediating the cellular response to PLTX.

Overexpression of TrxR1 reverses PLTX-induced alterations in oxidative damage and ferroptosis markers

Consistent with the protective effect of TrxR1 overexpression on cell viability, TrxR1-overexpressing cells exhibited markedly higher intracellular GSH levels and GPx4 activity than vector control cells following PLTX exposure (Fig. 5F, G). In parallel, the accumulation of MDA, a marker of lipid peroxidation, was markedly reduced (Fig. 5H). These results indicate that TrxR1 overexpression effectively counteracts PLTX-induced oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, thereby reversing the ferroptosis-related damage triggered by PLTX.

Discussion

The prevailing account of PLTX toxicity centers on its engagement of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α subunit at the cell surface, an interaction that reconfigures the pump into a non-selective cation channel, perturbs intracellular ionic homeostasis, and culminates in cell death (Artigas and Gadsby 2003b, 2003a; Carneiro and El-Deiry 2020). It should be noted, however, that the membrane‑level effects and the intracellular targeting of TrxR1 are not mutually exclusive. On the contrary, they may be part of a broader, interconnected cascade of cellular events triggered by PLTX. As previously reported, the Na⁺ overload induced by PLTX can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent oxidative stress (Pelin et al. 2013b). In this context, TrxR1 is of particular interest because of its central role in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis through the thioredoxin system. The present study identifies TrxR1 as a specific intracellular target of PLTX, thereby expanding the perspective on its toxic mechanism from membrane receptors to the intracellular antioxidant enzyme system. This finding does not contradict the established membrane‑targeting model; rather, it may provide a missing link between the initial membrane event and the downstream execution of ferroptosis. The research further elucidates that PLTX induces ferroptosis by targeting and inhibiting TrxR1, thereby disrupting the function of the cystine/GSH/GPx4 antioxidant axis. This conclusion aligns with the view proposed by Cheng et al. (2022) that PLTX can induce ferroptosis, and our study further identifies its upstream molecular target (Cheng et al. 2022).

TrxR1 is a central component of the thioredoxin system and plays an essential role in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis (Liu et al. 2022). Dysregulation of TrxR1 has been implicated in a wide range of pathological processes, highlighting its context-dependent functions in both disease progression and cellular protection. Within tumors, Nrf2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) signaling often drives TrxR1 upregulation, bolstering the antioxidant reserve of malignant cells and feeding into their survival, proliferation, and resistance to therapy (Wu et al. 2022). This dependence has made TrxR1 blockade an attractive anticancer avenue, with agents such as auranofin active across numerous preclinical settings (Günaydın et al. 2025). At the other extreme, diminished TrxR1 function correlates with heightened oxidative stress and tissue injury in neurodegenerative conditions such as Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s disease (Yang et al. 2024); the enzyme has likewise been tied to the control of inflammatory cascades, NF-κB (nuclear factor κB) signaling among them (Yeo et al. 2021).

A legitimate concern is whether PLTX indiscriminately inhibits other disulfide reductases, such as GR. Our Lip‑SMap chemoproteomic analysis did not detect GR‑derived peptides with PLTX‑dependent protection or depletion (Supplementary Tables S4, S5), indicating an absence of direct PLTX–GR interaction under the tested conditions.

Another important question that warrants consideration is whether the observed TrxR1 inhibition represents a direct targeting event or a secondary consequence of the membrane‑initiated ionic imbalance. Given that PLTX triggers a massive Na⁺ influx that can profoundly alter cellular redox homeostasis, it is plausible that the functional changes in TrxR1 might be influenced by the downstream effects of membrane perturbation. Nevertheless, several lines of evidence support a direct interaction between PLTX and TrxR1. First, the Lip‑SMap chemoproteomics platform identified PLTX‑dependent protection of TrxR1‑derived peptides in cell lysates, where the ionic imbalance that occurs in intact cells is absent. Second, the BLI binding experiments demonstrated a moderate‑to‑high affinity interaction between purified PLTX and recombinant TrxR1 in a cell‑free system (KD = 5.415 × 10–1 μM). Third, molecular docking simulations predicted specific hydrogen‑bonding interactions between PLTX and the active site residues Q230 and N234 of TrxR1. Collectively, these observations suggest that PLTX is capable of directly binding to and inhibiting TrxR1, while it remains possible that the membrane‑initiated oxidative stress may further exacerbate this effect in the cellular context. Future studies are warranted to clarify the relative contributions of direct targeting versus downstream modulation.

Notably, overexpression of TrxR1 significantly reversed PLTX‑induced cytotoxicity, which not only confirms the central role of TrxR1 in its toxic pathway but also suggests that TrxR1 agonists or related cytoprotective strategies may have therapeutic potential against PLTX poisoning. Beyond inducing oxidative stress, inhibition of TrxR1 can trigger a series of downstream critical events. For example, reduced TrxR1 activity leads to a decrease in reduced Trx (thioredoxin) levels, thereby relieving its brake on pro-apoptotic routes such as the apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1)–c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)/p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK) axis (Jin et al. 2015). Concurrently, alterations in its activity may also affect the function of transcription factors including NF‑κB (Raninga et al. 2016; Sakurai et al. 2004), which plays significant roles in inflammation, immunity, cell proliferation and apoptosis (Zhang et al. 2021), and is involved in cancer cell metabolic reprogramming and tumor microenvironment immune regulation (Yu et al. 2020). Therefore, the signaling network and cell death modalities triggered by PLTX through TrxR1 inhibition remain to be systematically elucidated. Furthermore, confocal fluorescence imaging revealed that PLTX, in addition to being distributed in the cytoplasm and binding to TrxR1, is also observed within the nucleus, suggesting potential nuclear targets and possible effects on genomic regulation. This observation also indicates that PLTX can cross membranes and enter both the cytoplasm and the nucleus.

Preliminary Lip‑SMap results show that, aside from Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, other molecules on the cell membrane can bind to PLTX, which may be involved in its internalization process. In this context, it is worth noting that CAV1 (caveolin‑1) was identified as a PLTX‑interacting protein in the same chemoproteomic analysis (Supplementary Table S3). CAV1 is the principal structural component of caveolae — cholesterol‑rich, flask‑shaped plasma membrane invaginations that act as specialized endocytic portals for the uptake of various ligands, bacterial toxins and viruses (Vilariño and Botana n.d.). The marked protection of CAV1 peptides upon PLTX exposure suggests a direct physical interaction, which, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported. This observation raises the intriguing possibility that PLTX may engage caveolae‑mediated endocytosis as a route of cellular entry. Such a mechanism could help explain how this large, amphipathic molecule—whose ability to cross biological membranes has remained a longstanding question—reaches the cytoplasm to exert its effects on intracellular targets such as TrxR1, as identified in this study. Given the size and amphipathic nature of PLTX, passive diffusion across the lipid bilayer is unlikely to be the primary mode of entry (Vilariño and Botana n.d.), making an active uptake mechanism highly plausible. It should be noted, however, that the Lip‑SMap identification of CAV1 as a PLTX‑interacting protein does not by itself establish a functional role in toxin internalization. Nevertheless, this finding provides a testable hypothesis that merits further investigation using complementary approaches such as siRNA‑mediated knockdown of CAV1, pharmacological inhibition of caveolae‑dependent endocytosis (e.g., with methyl‑β‑cyclodextrin or filipin), or live‑cell imaging of fluorescently labeled PLTX in the presence of endocytic pathway inhibitors. Elucidating the exact endocytic route(s) involved in PLTX uptake—whether caveolae‑mediated, clathrin‑dependent, macropinocytosis, or a combination thereof—remains an important direction for future research.

Conclusion

By integrating limited-proteolysis chemoproteomics (Lip-SMap) with complementary functional and proteomic analyses, this study identifies and experimentally validates, for the first time, TrxR1 as an intracellular target of PLTX. PLTX induces ferroptosis by targeting and inhibiting TrxR1, thereby disrupting the cystine/GSH/GPx4 axis. This disruption results in the accumulation of intracellular ROS, disturbance of cellular redox homeostasis, and subsequent promotion of lipid peroxidation and production of metabolites including MDA (Fig. 5I).
