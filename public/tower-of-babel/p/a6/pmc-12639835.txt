Leaf functional trait dataset of 93 dominant woody species from the central Western Ghats, India
Authors: Mahim Bhat, Sruthi Raghavendra, Gayathri Rajendiran, Vishnu Shreekara Bhat, Abhishek Pujari, Vaishnavi Pai, Nidhi Vastrad, H.N. Kumara, Vivek Pandi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present a comprehensive dataset of qualitative and quantitative leaf functional traits for 93 dominant woody species representing two distinct leafing phenologies and three growth forms from the central Western Ghats of India. Quantitative assessments were conducted for nine key traits: leaf area (LA), mean thickness (LTH), specific leaf area (SLA), leaf dry matter content (LDMC), leaf tissue density (LTD), leaf nitrogen concentration (Leaf N), carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C/N), and phytolith yield, following standard protocols. For each species, 30 leaves were sampled from a minimum of five individuals, totalling 2790 leaf samples. Qualitative traits, including leaf shape, margin, surface, texture, apex, base, type, and latex presence, were recorded in the field and validated using field manuals. The majority of species sampled were evergreen (74 %), with deciduous species comprising the remainder. Given the growing importance of plant functional traits in ecological research, this dataset offers valuable species-level leaf trait information at the regional scale. The phytolith yield data, in particular, represent one of the few globally available datasets, providing essential baselines for palaeoecological research and enabling quantitative reconstruction of vegetation composition and environmental change over millennial timescales.

Value of the Data

•This dataset provides high-resolution leaf-level data (n = 2790) on 17 functional traits for 93 dominant woody species of the central Western Ghats, supporting trait-based ecological research.•It enables assessment of both interspecific and intraspecific variation in leaf functional traits, which is critical for understanding species strategies and community assembly. Further, the dataset includes species with varying leafing phenologies and growth forms, providing meaningful comparisons.•All traits were measured using standardized protocols, ensuring methodological consistency and allowing immediate use in ecological and evolutionary studies.•The dataset encompasses phytolith yield data for woody dicotyledonous species, a parameter that is seldom reported in the literature compared to that of grasses. This information offers scope for meaningful comparisons of phytolith production across functional groups (e.g., dicots vs. monocots; woody vs. non-woody plants), and their representation in paleoecological records. Furthermore, given the relatively longer leaf turnover rates in woody dicots, these data may provide valuable insights for biogeochemical cycling studies, particularly in improving models of carbon cycling and vegetation dynamics.•The dataset provides a regional representation of leaf functional traits from the Western Ghats biodiversity hotspot, complementing global repositories such as the TRY database [3], and is suitable for integration into global syntheses (e.g., the leaf economics spectrum) to inform models of functional diversity, trait–environment relationships, and forest ecosystem functioning.

Background

Plant communities are dynamic systems shaped by species-specific adaptations and interactions with their biotic and abiotic environment [4]. Plant functional traits (PFTs) provide a powerful lens to understand these dynamics, as they reflect how species respond to environmental variability and determine their ecological roles [1,2,5]. However, such trait-based insights remain scarce in tropical wet forests with prolonged rainfall, where regional datasets are only beginning to emerge [6,7]. This study addresses this gap by presenting a comprehensive dataset of leaf functional traits of dominant woody plants in India’s Central Western Ghats, highlighting their adaptations to high rainfall, canopy heterogeneity, and intense competition.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Study site

The study was conducted in the Gerusoppa Reserve Forest, located within the Central Western Ghats of India (14°12′ N to 14°24′ N and 74°36′ E to 74°48′ E). Elevation ranges from 120 m – 580 m predominantly representing wet forest formations favored by higher rainfall (∼6000 mm/yr).

Species selection, leaf collection, processing and analysis

Species selection

Based on a qualitative plant inventory, we shortlisted 93 dominant woody plant species, mostly trees. Based on existing literature and relevant field identification manuals, each species was classified as either evergreen or deciduous according to its foliar phenology. Using the Plants of the World Online [8] database, the taxonomic status of these species was validated. To ensure representativeness, we focused on widespread and frequently encountered species that dominate forest structure and composition, thereby capturing the traits most relevant to ecosystem functioning in the study region. Rare species were excluded due to the low abundance of individuals needed for sampling and their limited representation of dominant ecological strategies in the study region. While this approach ensures robust sampling of dominant taxa, it may slightly underrepresent the overall spectrum of trait diversity at the community level, particularly those traits associated with rare or specialized species.

Sample collection and processing

For each species, we collected 30 mature, fully sun-exposed, and healthy leaves using a 10-meter pole cutter, from at least five individuals per species, amounting to 2790 leaves. Each leaf was assigned a unique sequential code to capture leaf-level information of traits for all species. The following leaf functional traits were measured: fresh weight (g), dry weight (g), mean thickness (LTH, mm), leaf area (LA, cm²), specific leaf area (SLA, cm² g⁻¹), leaf dry matter content (LDMC, mg g⁻¹), leaf mass per area (LMA, g cm⁻²), leaf tissue density (LTD, g cm⁻³), mass-based nitrogen concentration (Leaf N, mg g⁻¹), and carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratio.

Following the collection, all leaf trait measurements (summarised in Table 1) were conducted within two hours to minimise water loss due to evaporation. Prior to measurement, petioles were carefully removed from each leaf. Leaves were subsequently oven-dried at 60–70 °C for 48 h to determine dry weight (g). Leaf functional traits were quantified following standardised protocols with minimal modifications as outlined in [1,2]. LTH was measured using a digital micrometre at three points (two lateral margins and the apex), avoiding the midrib and secondary veins. LA (one-sided area) was determined using ImageJ software. SLA was calculated as the ratio of LA (scanned measurement of fresh leaf) to dry mass, whereas LMA was computed as the ratio of dry mass to LA. LTD was derived by dividing LMA by LTH (converted to cm). For elemental analysis, dried leaf samples were ground into a fine powder and analysed using a CNHS analyser (varioEL cube V3.1.8 (e638c54), CHNS Mode, Serial No 19,151,007; Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH, Germany) to quantify nitrogen (Leaf N), carbon (Leaf C), concentrations on a mass basis (mg g⁻¹), as well as the C/N ratio.

Phytolith extraction was carried out using the wet digestion method. Dried leaf samples were first cleaned in Alconox solution and rinsed thoroughly with distilled water, followed by treatment with 10 % HCl (v/v, prepared from 37 % stock) to remove carbonates. After oven-drying at 60–70 °C, ∼1 g of plant material was digested with concentrated H₂SO₄ (98 %) in a 60 °C water bath until organic matter dissolved. The cooled mixture was treated with 30 % H₂O₂ (30 % w/v stock solution) at 80 °C, with additional H₂O₂ added until the solution turned colourless, indicating complete removal of organics. The remaining acid-insoluble fraction (AIF) was repeatedly washed and centrifuged (2000 rpm for 15 min at room temperature) until neutral pH, oven-dried, and weighed. Phytolith yield was expressed as a percentage of the initial plant dry weight ( % DW) [9], enabling direct comparison of silica content across taxa with differing leaf morphologies and biomass, and thereby standardizing cross-species evaluations. A simplified schematic representation of phytolith extraction and quantification is given as supplementary material 1.

Limitations

In certain instances, it was not feasible to sample eight individuals per species due to the inaccessibility of canopy leaves. However, a total of 30 leaf samples were consistently collected from a minimum of five individuals per species. While this reduced sampling in a few cases may lead to a slight underestimation of within-species trait variance (˂ 5 %), it is unlikely to substantially bias interspecific comparisons, as the number of leaves sampled was consistent across species. Transporting the samples to the laboratory within 2–3 h posed logistical challenges, but was effectively managed using insulated boxes containing ice packs to preserve sample integrity.
