Buffalo chest after minimally invasive repair of pectus excavatum (MIRPE)
Authors: Francesco Donati, Marcelo Martinez-Ferro, Manuel Lopez, Michele Torre
Published: 2026

We thank Tedde and colleagues for their thoughtful letter and for expanding the discussion on iatrogenic “buffalo chest (BC)” following MIRPE (1). We appreciate their constructive perspective and their effort to propose practical measures that could potentially mitigate the clinical consequences of pleural communication.

Our survey-based study was designed to assess awareness, perceptions, and real-world practice variability among experienced Chest Wall International Group (CWIG) surgeons, and to generate hypotheses regarding potentially underestimated risks and preventive strategies. As such, our concluding remarks were intentionally cautious and framed as measures that may deserve consideration and further study rather than prescriptive (2).

With regard to the measure suggested by the authors, namely the post-operative routine placement of bilateral pleural pigtail drains, we do not believe that it is likely to effectively mitigate the risk of BC. It should also be emphasized that BC is not an immediate postoperative complication related to residual pneumothorax, but rather a condition associated with the occurrence of spontaneous pneumothorax that becomes bilateral in patients who have previously undergone MIRPE. This distinction is clinically relevant, as the event may occur months or even years after surgery, when any postoperative pleural air has long been completely reabsorbed, further limiting the potential preventive role of routine early postoperative pleural drainage.

We fully agree that this topic warrants prospective multicenter evaluation. A pragmatic, collaborative approach—such as a CWIG-led registry or multicenter study—could compare standardized postoperative pleural management strategies (including unilateral vs. bilateral drainage, degree of suction/aspiration, and selective vs. routine use) and capture clinically meaningful endpoints (residual pneumothorax, pleural effusion, need for interventions, readmissions, and occurrence of unilateral/bilateral spontaneous pneumothorax) alongside patient-centered outcomes such as pain and recovery.

We again thank the authors for their contribution to the ongoing discussion and for supporting further research aimed at improving safety in pectus surgery.
