Introduction

Pulmonary administration can provide distinct advantages, such as a rapid onset of action, direct delivery to lung tissues, and minimized systemic exposure [1]. These advantages are particularly valuable when the lungs are the primary target for treatment. However, the development of inhaled medications comes with unique challenges. One of the most significant obstacles is understanding the intrapulmonary pharmacokinetics (PK) of drugs [2]. The lung has a highly complex and heterogeneous structure, with distinct regions such as the conducting airways and alveolar spaces. Drug PK behaviour within this intricate environment depends on multiple factors, including particle size, formulation properties, and the interaction with lung physiology [3].

Current sampling techniques for lung PK studies include tissue biopsies and methods for obtaining epithelial lining fluid (ELF) concentrations, such as bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and bronchosorption [4]. Tissue biopsies require homogenization of lung tissue and mucus, often leading to overestimating drug concentrations at the site of interest [5]. BAL, though widely used, relies on urea correction to reduce dilution bias, but this method assumes equal urea levels in plasma and ELF, which is not always correct [6]. Moreover, the BAL method involves instillation of saline into an entire lung segment or lobe, resulting in a pooled sample lacking regional specificity [4]. Bronchosorption, on the other hand, is a novel technique that allows undiluted measurement of ELF drug concentrations and enables region-specific sampling along the bronchial tree [4]. However, these methods are invasive, requiring bronchoscopy, restricted to sparse sampling, and thereby yielding limited data quantities. Consequently, most clinical studies evaluating antimicrobials use lung-to-plasma concentration ratios or approximated area under the curve (AUC) ratios from sparse sampling as proxies for lung penetration ratio [7]. These metrics are often unavailable, and when reported they are typically derived from limited time points [7].

Population pharmacokinetic (popPK) modelling, when combined with limited sampling techniques, is a powerful approach for addressing sources of variability in drug disposition among patients [8]. By leveraging model-based simulations, popPK models can optimize sampling strategies by determining the most informative and efficient sampling times, thereby minimizing patient burden [9]. Lung PK is typically described using one or two compartments [10–13]. While these empirical models effectively characterize PK data, their parameters often lack physiological meaning, and extrapolation beyond the observed conditions can be unreliable, limiting their utility for predicting lung drug behaviour in untested scenarios.

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modelling, which incorporates anatomically accurate lung structures, offers a promising solution to these challenges [14]. PBPK models provide physiologically meaningful parameters that enhance understanding of drug distribution and affinities within the lung. By combining the population approach with the physiologically based approach, we can obtain parameters with physiological relevance while accounting for population-level variabilities.

This study aimed to develop a pulmonary population PBPK model for salbutamol, addressing the limitations of compartment popPK modelling methods that often lack physiological specificity. The model was used to (1) evaluate and compare different sampling techniques for their ability to estimate key lung PK parameters, and (2) identify preferred sampling strategies for hypothetical drugs with varying physicochemical properties. The findings offer valuable insights for optimizing lung PK sampling methodologies applicable to a broader range of inhaled compounds.

Methods

Study Design and Sampling Techniques

The population PBPK analyses were based on data from a previously published clinical PK study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03524066) [4]. In summary, this study investigated the lung PK of orally inhaled salbutamol in healthy, non-smoking volunteers aged 18–50 years with normal lung function. Thirteen participants in the inhalation cohort received a single 200-μg dose of commercially available salbutamol via a spacer device. Each participant underwent two lung sampling sessions using bronchoscopy at one of the following time intervals after drug administration: 1 and 6 h, 2 and 9 h, 3 and 12 h, or 4 and 18 h. In each session, five lung samples were collected sequentially in the following order: bronchosorption at the right middle lobe, bronchosorption at the left lingula, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) at the right lobe during the first session and at the lingula during the second session, followed by two mucosal biopsies from the same side of the lung. The first biopsy was taken from the right (or left) bronchus, and the second biopsy was taken from a different segment of the same bronchus by retracting the biopsy probe. Blood samples were collected at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h post-dose. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines, receiving approval from the independent ethics committee at Hannover Medical School. All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. Study data were anonymized prior to transfer to Uppsala University; no direct identifiers or linkable codes were shared.

Sampling techniques and bioanalyses have been described in detail previously [4] and are briefly summarized below. Bronchosorption was performed by inserting a catheter through the bronchoscope's working channel and placing its tip onto the bronchial mucosa for 30 s to absorb ELF. BAL involved instilling 100 mL of pre-warmed saline into the middle lobe or lingula and immediately aspirating it to retrieve ELF. Endobronchial forceps biopsies were conducted to collect tissue samples from the bronchial mucosa. Biopsy drug concentrations were expressed as µg/L, assuming a tissue density of 1 g/mL. Salbutamol concentrations were quantified in all sample extracts using reverse-phase ultra-HPLC coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Plasma and BAL fluid samples were prepared by protein precipitation, while biopsy samples were homogenized first and then processed similarly. Drug concentrations in BAL fluid were corrected for dilution using the urea method to estimate ELF concentrations: [Salbutamol]ELF = [Salbutamol]BAL *[urea]PLASMA/[urea]BAL.

Data Description and Data Handling

A total of 101 plasma and 100 lung PK samples were collected from 13 patients. The lung PK samples consisted of 32 observations from bronchosorption, 32 from tissue biopsy, and 36 from BAL. Among the plasma samples, 4 (3.96%) observations were below the lower limit of quantitation (BLQ). For lung PK samples, 10 (31.3%) from tissue biopsy and 6 (16.7%) from BAL were below the BLQ.

In plasma PK modelling, BLQ values were omitted from popPK analysis. For lung PK data, the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) for salbutamol in ELF, as measured using the BAL method, was calculated based on a population urea ratio [urea]PLASMA/[urea]BAL of 24.6 [15]. This resulted in an LLOQ of 0.492 nM for the BAL method. The M6 method was applied by assigning the first BLQ observation in each subject to LLOQ/2 [16], with all subsequent BLQ values excluded. Since the LLOQ value for tissue biopsy was unavailable, BLQ values of biopsy data were also omitted. Before analysis, all plasma and lung concentrations were transformed to logarithmic scale, and an additive error model was applied to the log-transformed data, as equivalent to a proportional error model on the untransformed scale.

Population-Based Minimal PBPK Modelling

A population modelling approach was applied to develop minimal physiologically based pharmacokinetic (mPBPK) models of orally inhaled salbutamol. First, systemic PK parameters were estimated with plasma observations only. Subsequently, systemic PK parameters were fixed, and post hoc individual PK parameters were derived as input, enabling the estimation of lung PK parameters using both plasma and lung PK data. In the lung PK estimation step, the model was fit simultaneously to all available pulmonary and plasma concentration data, including observations from bronchosorption, biopsy, and BAL, with separate residual error models applied to each data type. Population PK Modelling of Plasma PK

A previously reported salbutamol PK model structure as well as parameters from a model-based meta-analysis study was used as a starting point [17]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the model incorporates separate lung and gut compartments to account for two distinct absorption pathways, with a one-compartment model describing plasma PK. The fraction of the dose deposited in the lungs is set as F1, with the remaining portion (1-F1) deposited in the oral cavity, subsequently swallowed and absorbed through the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. Drug loss in the spacer was not accounted for, in line with the reported salbutamol PK model [17]. Complete availability was assumed for the lung-deposited fraction, whereas the GI-absorbed fraction was assigned a bioavailability of F2. Both pathways followed first-order absorption kinetics. Inter-individual variability (IIV) was tested sequentially on all parameters.

Software

Pirana and R 4.3.3 were used for data management, model diagnostics, graphical analysis, and evaluations [31].

Discussion

This work has developed a pulmonary population PBPK model for inhaled salbutamol in healthy subjects, using an informative dataset collected with three different pulmonary sampling techniques. We also introduced SSE for a PBPK model to inform sampling designs, focusing on parameters that are physiologically meaningful and directly related to drug properties. Although our approach relies on clinical PK data, prospective applicability is not fundamentally limited. Plasma PK data are typically available prior to pulmonary PK study design, enabling the systemic model to be informed by existing data, while lung PK parameters can be initially approximated from in vitro assays and BCS classification. Furthermore, unlike traditional bottom-up PBPK approaches where variability parameters are often fixed to arbitrary values, our approach estimates physiologically relevant parameters with associated uncertainty, which is an advantage for model-informed study design [33]. This approach enhances the model’s predictive power for unstudied conditions and provides insights for future lung PK assessments of drugs with different permeabilities.

Quantifying lung drug concentrations is clinically relevant since, for inhaled drugs, the lung is the primary target organ, and systemic plasma concentrations may poorly reflect drug exposure at the site of action. Understanding intrapulmonary PK is therefore essential for establishing pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic relationships, optimizing dose regimens, and supporting the development of inhaled therapies with improved efficacy and safety profiles [34]. The present study, by characterizing key pulmonary PK parameters with associated uncertainty, provides a foundation for linking measurable lung concentrations to pharmacodynamic endpoints in future studies.

The estimated human \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{u},\mathrm{l}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{g}}$$\end{document}Kp,u,lung is consistent with our prior model prediction (8.83) based on rat data [18], and higher than the reported experimental value in rats (6.6) [35]. The parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{f}}$$\end{document}Peff was, on the other hand, 1000 times higher than estimated from the rat data (4.2 × 10−4 m/h) [18]. The difference can be attributed to the different compartmentalization approach in the two models: the current model divides the lung into three distinct airway regions, whereas the previous model segmented it into 24-airway generations. Moreover, the current estimation is supported by multiple sources of data, including biopsy, that offer insights into tissue concentration, while the previous parameters were estimated from BAL data alone.

The model estimated different residual errors for the three sampling techniques. BAL had the highest residual error of 146%, followed by bronchosorption with 109% and biopsy with 84.8%. High residual errors with lung sampling techniques after inhalation are expected, especially for the BAL technique, which is subject to several limitations, including variable dilution, incomplete fluid recovery, and potential bias from urea diffusion. Previous modelling-based studies reported similarly high residual errors for the BAL method after nebulization administration, ranging from 68.8 to 105% [12, 36]. Although this level of uncertainty limits confidence in exact individual lung concentration, it does not preclude utility in drug development, where these data can inform population-level characterization of pulmonary exposure and support mechanistic (PB)PK modelling when interpreted jointly with plasma data. Lung sampling data are therefore valuable for comparing compounds with respect to site-of-action exposure, but should be viewed as supportive rather than stand-alone inputs for dose individualization.

Comparing different sampling techniques for parameter estimation (Fig. 3), we found that biopsy, despite its low analytical error, performed the worst due to higher bias when estimating \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{f}}$$\end{document}Peff. This could be due to homogenization of ELF and lung tissues during sample processing, which limits the spatial resolution of biopsy measurements. However, estimation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{f}}$$\end{document}Peff relies on data informing the drug's transport rate from ELF to tissues and ultimately to plasma. When using a single technique, BAL had worse performance than bronchosorption, which can be explained by the higher analytical error with BAL. Interestingly, among the combined techniques, bronchosorption plus biopsy performed similarly to the reference method (three-technique combination), followed by bronchosorption plus BAL and biopsy plus BAL. Since both bronchosorption and biopsy measure drug concentrations in the bronchial region, their combination offers lower analytical error and higher spatial resolution, improving accuracy and precision in parameter estimation. Therefore, if a combination of only two techniques are to be selected, bronchosorption plus biopsy would be the preferred choice.

The BAL-derived ELF-to-plasma ratio has long been used as a surrogate to assess drug candidates' lung retention and penetration. Our simulations showed high variability in ratios both between individuals and over time. Median ELF-to-plasma ratios stabilized more quickly for high-permeability drugs compared with low-permeability drugs. The simulations align with an observational study on inhaled polymyxin B (very low permeability), where the ELF-to-plasma ratio steadily declined between 2 and 6 h [37]. The variability in ELF-to-plasma ratios is most pronounced for drugs with very low permeability (Fig. 5d), which can be explained by slower equilibration between ELF and tissue compartments (Fig. 4d).

Figure 6 illustrates the limitations of both uniform and staggered sampling strategies with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{u},\mathrm{l}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{g}}$$\end{document}Kp,u,lung and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{f}}$$\end{document}Peff estimation when bronchosorption alone was employed within a single bronchoscopy. While staggered sampling did not consistently outperform uniform sampling, it helped mitigate the risk of substantial bias when drug properties were unknown. The performance of different sampling strategies can be attributed to the impact of drug permeability on pulmonary PK (Fig. 4). For high-permeability drugs, the rapid equilibration between ELF and tissue compartments resulted in substantial bias when the 0.5-h sampling point was omitted (Fig. 6a). Likewise, the 6-h uniform sampling approach failed to capture the intrapulmonary PK of salbutamol (Fig. 6b). Conversely, for drugs with very low permeability, the extended equilibration between ELF and tissue compartments (Fig. 4d) led to considerable bias when employing a 0.5-h uniform sampling scheme. These findings highlight the necessity of understanding drug permeability before suggesting an intrapulmonary PK study design. For prospective application, compound permeability can be estimated from in vitro assays and classified according to the BCS/iBCS prior to decisions on clinical study design, providing the necessary input to guide simulation-based sampling optimization.

The study was limited by its sample size and well-controlled population, likely contributing to the fact that IIV was only significant in clearance and the lung deposition fraction F1. The PPE (Fig. S2, see ESM) indicated that we may have missed identifying an up to 10% IIV in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{u},\mathrm{l}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{g}}$$\end{document}Kp,u,lung and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{f}}$$\end{document}Peff, but we expect the impact from variability of such low magnitude to be negligible. Secondly, extrapolation from the current model results to a diseased population was not addressed. A full understanding of target disease interactions with all physiological parameters is, however, lacking and should be further explored with dedicated studies.

Conclusion

We developed a pulmonary population PBPK model for inhaled salbutamol in healthy subjects using detailed intrapulmonary data collected through three different sampling techniques. Based on the SSE, bronchosorption plus biopsy was recommended as the best two-technique combination, while bronchosorption alone was the preferred single technique. Staggered sampling strategies would reduce the risk of high bias. However, optimal sampling time points depend on the permeability of the investigated drug.
