MEX3B aggravates osteoarthritis progression by post-transcriptionally activating TLR4-NF-κB signaling axis
Authors: Zeze Fu, Yanfeng Huang, Siqi Zhang, Jian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic degenerative joint disease characterized by cartilage degradation, synovial inflammation, and subchondral bone remodeling. Toll-like receptors (TLRs), particularly TLR4, are pivotal in driving the inflammatory responses central to OA pathogenesis. The RNA-binding protein MEX3B is implicated in post-transcriptional regulation, but its role in OA remains unexplored.MethodsBioinformatic analysis of the GSE114007 dataset identified differentially expressed RNA-binding proteins in OA cartilage. MEX3B expression was validated in human OA tissues. In vitro, an inflammatory model was established in primary murine chondrocytes using lipopolysaccharide (LPS). The functional role of MEX3B was assessed through knockdown experiments, evaluating its impact on TLR4 expression, NF-κB pathway activation (measured by p-p65 and p-IκBα levels), and the expression of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1α, TNF-α) and cartilage-degrading enzymes (ADAMTS5, MMP13). Mechanistically, RNA immunoprecipitation and domain-deletion assays were employed to confirm the direct binding of MEX3B to TLR4 mRNA via its KH domain. In vivo, the protective effect of MEX3B knockout was evaluated in a murine ACLT model, with or without intra-articular administration of the TLR4 agonist monophosphoryl lipid A (MPLA).ResultsMEX3B was significantly upregulated in OA cartilage. Its knockdown in chondrocytes suppressed TLR4 expression at both mRNA and protein levels, impaired TLR4 membrane localization, and inhibited the NF-κB pathway. This led to a marked reduction in inflammatory mediators and catabolic factors. Mechanistically, MEX3B directly binds to TLR4 mRNA through its KH domain, leading to increased TLR4 protein levels and pathway activation. In vivo, MEX3B deficiency mitigated OA progression, which was effectively reversed by the TLR4 agonist MPLA.ConclusionOur study unveils a novel mechanism whereby MEX3B, by post-transcriptionally regulating TLR4 expression, activates the NF-κB signaling cascade, thereby promoting inflammation and cartilage breakdown in OA. These findings position MEX3B as a promising therapeutic target for intervening in the inflammatory process of OA.Graphical abstractSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06798-0.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, MEX3B, TLR4, Inflammation, NF-κB signaling pathway, Chondrocytes

Introduction

Globally, osteoarthritis (OA) stands out as a highly prevalent chronic disorder affecting the joints, with an incidence that increases significantly with age. Among the older and middle-aged adults, OA is a predominant cause of persistent joint pain, impaired movement, and, in advanced stages, significant disability [4, 6]. The pathological features of OA are diverse and primarily manifest as progressive degeneration of articular cartilage (including loss of chondrocytes and extracellular matrix), synovial inflammation (synovial hyperplasia and inflammatory cell infiltration), and abnormal remodeling of subchondral bone. These pathological processes promote each other, ultimately leading to joint structure destruction and functional loss [1, 5, 16, 18, 31]. The pathogenesis of OA involves the interaction of multiple factors, including environmental risks, abnormal joint biomechanics, dysregulated pain signaling, and upregulated inflammatory pathways, although the specific molecular mechanisms remain unclear [10, 20, 21]. Current therapeutic strategies primarily include symptomatic treatments such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, physical therapy, and surgical interventions. However, these approaches only relieve clinical symptoms (e.g., pain and swelling) without halting cartilage degeneration [2, 3, 25, 37, 38]. Moreover, drug therapy often presents systemic side effects and lacks target specificity, while joint replacement surgery is only suitable for patients at the end stage of the disease [9, 34]. Therefore, the absence of etiological treatments that can effectively delay or reverse disease progression underscores the scientific and clinical importance of exploring OA molecular mechanisms and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

Excessive activation of innate immunity critically sustains OA-related inflammation. Among various mediators, the Toll-like receptor (TLR) family—essential pattern recognition receptors (PRRs)—detects damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and initiates downstream pro-inflammatory events, plays a central role in OA pathology [24, 29, 39]. TLR4, a critical member of the TLR family, can be activated by ligands such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and hyaluronan fragments. Through downstream signaling pathways like NF-κB, TLR4 induces massive secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and upregulates cartilage-degrading enzymes such as major degradative enzymes, namely matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTS), directly contributing to cartilage destruction and exacerbation of joint inflammation [11, 12, 23, 24, 32]. Previous studies have confirmed that TLR4 expression is significantly elevated in the cartilage and synovial tissues of OA patients, and its expression level is closely associated with disease severity, suggesting that TLR4 may be a potential therapeutic target for OA [8, 11, 29]. However, the upstream regulatory mechanisms underlying TLR4 overexpression in OA—particularly post-transcriptional regulation—remain insufficiently studied, thereby limiting the full understanding and therapeutic targeting of TLR4.

Gene expression modulation occurs post-transcriptionally when RBPs recognize and bind to particular sequences within mRNA molecules, influencing their stability, translational efficiency, and subcellular localization. RBPs are involved in the pathogenesis of various diseases [19, 22, 28, 30, 33]. MEX3B, a member of the MEX3 family, contains two conserved K-homology (KH) domains and is known to participate in post-transcriptional regulation by binding mRNA. Its roles in diseases such as cancer and inflammation have gradually emerged. For example, in hepatocellular carcinoma, MEX3B demonstrates upregulated expression that strongly correlates with advancing tumor grade and higher clinical stage. MEX3B promotes tumor progression by regulating cell cycle and post-translational modifications (e.g., ubiquitination), and it is associated with the tumor microenvironment (e.g., hypoxia and immune responses). In immune responses, MEX3B has been shown to upregulate the expression of interferon (IFN1), ISG15, MX2, and inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL6, IL8, TNFα) in response to poly(I:C) stimulation [13, 27, 35, 36, 40]. However, to date, the expression pattern, specific functions, and molecular mechanisms of MEX3B in OA have not been reported. Notably, bioinformatic analysis has indicated a potential association between MEX3B and TLR signaling pathways, providing a new direction for investigating whether MEX3B participates in OA-associated inflammation and cartilage degeneration through regulation of TLR family members, such as TLR4.

Based on the above context, this study focuses on the interaction between MEX3B and TLR4, aiming to determine the expression changes of MEX3B in OA and to explore its regulatory effects on TLR4 expression and inflammatory responses in chondrocytes. Furthermore, the study seeks to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which MEX3B participates in OA pathogenesis via TLR4, especially at the post-transcriptional level, and to validate the therapeutic potential of targeting MEX3B in animal models. The findings are expected to reveal novel regulatory mechanisms of TLR4 overexpression in OA and provide new molecular targets and theoretical foundations for the precision treatment of OA.

Materials and methods

Differential expression analysis of candidate RBPs

RBP data encompassing 1542 individual RBPs were obtained from the literature) [7]. Based on RNA-Seq data available from the GEO database (GSE114007), a transcript with altered expression analysis was performed on a cohort of 18 normal and 20 osteoarthritic cartilage samples. With a cutoff set at |logFC|> 1 and a p < 0.05, transcripts with altered expression were pinpointed. These were subsequently cross-referenced with established RBPs to determine DE-RBPs.

GO and GSEA analysis

For Gene Ontology (GO) annotation, we employed the publicly available tools provided by the GO Consortium (http://www.geneontology.org), while Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the GSEA software package (http://www.broadinstitute.org/gsea).

Primary chondrocyte isolation, culture, and treatment

Isolation of primary chondrocytes was performed using cartilage tissue derived from knee joints of 8–10-week-old C57BL/6 mice via digestion with 0.2% type II collagenase. For subsequent experiments, maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2, and high humidity, cells were propagated in DMEM/F12 medium containing 10% FBS, with subculturing limited to passages 2–3. We allocated the cells into the following experimental cohorts: Control; Mock inflammation model (stimulated with LPS at 100 ng/mL); Si-NC + inflammation group; Si-MEX3B + inflammation group; and TLR4 inhibitor group (treated with TAK-242).

Animal model and grouping

Eight- to ten-week-old male C57BL/6 wild-type and MEX3B knockout (MEX3B-KO) mice were utilized. Following i.p. injection of sodium pentobarbital (5% w/v, 30 mg/kg), ACLT surgery was performed to establish the OA model. The ACL was surgically severed post joint capsule exposure, and tissues were sutured layer-by-layer. Randomization was used to allocate mice into four groups (n = 6/group). These consisted of a Sham group, subjected solely to opening of the joint capsule, OA model group (ACLT-induced), MEX3B-KO + OA group, and MEX3B-KO + OA + TLR4 agonist group (treated with monophosphoryl lipid A, MPLA). Approval for all in vivo experiments was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital (SHDSYY-2024-1218-7).

ELISAs

To evaluate the inflammatory response, ELISA was conducted to detect IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-1α in the collected synovial fluid and supernatants from cell cultures.

Immunofluorescence staining

Following two washes with PBS, tissue sections were blocked and permeabilized for 1 h at room temperature using a solution of PBS containing 5% goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100. Subsequently, primary antibodies were diluted in this same blocking buffer. Tissue sections were then treated with this antibody solution and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After being washed thrice with PBS, a two-hour incubation followed using fluorescent secondary antibodies under room temperature conditions. Following further PBS washes, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (10 min) before anti-fade mounting. For chondrocyte processing, fixation was carried out using 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), followed by PBS washes. Permeabilization was achieved with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min, prior to a 30-min blocking step. The procedures for antibody staining, DAPI labeling, mounting, and imaging were the same as those used for tissue sections.

Histological evaluation

Knee joints were decalcified in EDTA at room temperature for approximately one month, followed by fixation in paraformaldehyde for 48 h, with the decalcifying solution changed every two days until bone tissues could be easily sectioned with a scalpel. Upon complete decalcification, joints were soaked in 50% ethanol for 12 h at RT, followed by graded ethanol dehydration. The tissues were then cleared in xylene and embedded in paraffin, with the posterior side of the joints uniformly positioned in embedding cassettes. Coronal sections were cut at a thickness of 5 μm. Excess paraffin blocks were trimmed to include the weight-bearing regions of the cartilage, and coronal sections were carefully collected for subsequent staining. After dewaxing and rehydration treatment, the sections were stained following the Safranin O-Fast Green staining protocol and hematoxylin-eosin staining method.

Western blotting

Protein expression profiles were compared between groups using western blotting.First, proteins were extracted from tissues or cells under cold conditions. Protein content was assessed by BCA assay, and 30 μg aliquots were resolved electrophoretically using 12% SDS-PAGE.The duration of membrane transfer was adjusted based on the molecular weight of the target proteins. Primary antibody incubation (4 °C, overnight) was preceded by membrane blocking (5% non-fat dry milk, 2 h, RT) and followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody treatment (2 h, RT). Signals were captured by chemiluminescent imaging after antibody incubation.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells for subsequent experiments both cartilage tissues and cultured cells following the protocol provided with the RNeasy Plus RNA isolation kit. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using the BioRT Master HisSensi cDNA Synthesis Kit. SYBR Green I dye was used as the fluorescent indicator in the PCR reactions. All primer sequences employed in the amplification process are comprehensively detailed in Table 1.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (v. 9.0). Unless otherwise indicated, data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and all experiments were performed with biologically independent replicates. For studies involving two cohorts, comparisons were made using Student’s t-test, while one-way ANOVA was employed for analyses across three or more groups. Statistical significance was annotated as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

Results

Profiling of dysregulated RBPs in OA

Based on transcriptomic data from cartilage tissues of OA patients and normal controls in the GSE114007 dataset, a Venn diagram analysis was performed to screen for OA-related candidate RBP genes. Volcano plot analysis further identified 75 DE-RBPs (Fig. 1A–B). The heatmap clearly showed expression patterns of these DE-RBPs across the two sample groups. Among them, MEX3B exhibited significantly higher expression in OA cartilage tissues compared to normal controls, making it one of the most markedly upregulated RBPs (Fig. 1C). These results suggest that aberrant overexpression of MEX3B may be closely associated with OA pathogenesis and progression, thereby identifying it as a core candidate for subsequent functional studies.

Identification of DE- RBP in OA. A Venn diagram showing intersection of gene sets. B Volcano plot of enriched differentially expressed proteins. C Heatmap of enriched differentially expressed proteins

Functional association of MEX3B with the TLR signaling pathway and inflammatory responses

Functional enrichment analysis of the 75 differentially expressed DE-RBPs was carried out with the Metascape platform, showing predominant involvement of these genes in key biological processes including the regulation of mRNA metabolism and translational control. Notably, they were significantly enriched in inflammation-related pathways, including the regulation of tumor necrosis factor production and cytokine synthesis/secretion, as well as the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 2A–C). Focused analysis of MEX3B revealed a strong correlation between its expression and NF-κB pathway activity. GO enrichment analysis further confirmed its involvement in TLR-mediated immune responses and inflammatory signal transduction. Correlation analyses also demonstrated significant positive associations between MEX3B expression and pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1α, IL-1β, and TNF-α (Fig. 2D–I). In summary, MEX3B may influence inflammatory responses in OA by interacting with the TLR signaling cascade.

Regulation of TLR4 expression and localization by MEX3B in chondrocytes

The in vitro research model consisted of primary chondrocytes of murine origin. The OA inflammatory model group exhibited marked upregulation of MEX3B and TLR4 expression at mRNA and protein levels relative to control specimens. Upon MEX3B knockdown, TLR4 mRNA and protein levels were markedly reduced compared to the Si-NC group, showing a trend similar to that observed in the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242-treated group (Fig. 3A–E). Further immunofluorescence staining revealed that LPS stimulation significantly enhanced TLR4 expression, while MEX3B knockdown substantially attenuated this effect, indicating that MEX3B regulates TLR4 expression (Fig. 3F–G).

MEX3B promotes inflammatory responses and cartilage degradation in chondrocytes via TLR4

In vitro assessments showed that the activation levels (phosphorylation status) of TLR4 downstream signaling molecules, p-NF-κB p65 and p-IκBα, were profoundly upregulated in the OA-induced group relative to the control, demonstrating canonical activation of the NF-κB pathway (Fig. 4A–C). Concurrently, mRNA expression levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1α, IL-1β, TNF-α) were markedly upregulated, and their secretion levels in the supernatant, as measured by ELISA, were also elevated (Fig. 4D–E). mRNA levels of cartilage degradation-related genes (MMP13, ADAMTS5) were markedly elevated compared to the control group (Fig. 4F–G). MEX3B knockdown led to notable reductions in these indicators—pathway activation, cytokine expression/secretion, and degradation gene expression—compared to the Si-NC group, showing inhibition effects comparable to TAK-242 treatment. These findings confirm that MEX3B activates the TLR4–NF-κB signaling axis to promote MEX3B promotes inflammatory activation and extracellular matrix catabolism, thereby establishing a critical mechanistic bridge to OA pathogenesis.

Molecular mechanism by which MEX3B regulates TLR4 via its KH domain

To determine whether MEX3B exerts its function through binding to lfTLR4 mRNA, the catRAPID protein-RNA interaction prediction tool was used to identify potential binding sites. The analysis suggested that MEX3B may interact with the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) of lfTLR4 mRNA via its KH domain (Fig. 5A). To validate this prediction, immunoprecipitation (IP) assays were performed on lysates from HDM-treated cells, followed by qPCR to assess binding between lfTLR4 mRNA and the MEX3B complex. It was observed that MEX3B interacted specifically with lfTLR4 mRNA, but not with sfTLR4 mRNA, and this interaction was enhanced under LPS stimulation (Fig. 5B). Additionally, MEX3B knockdown inhibited lfTLR4 translation (Fig. 5C). To investigate whether MEX3B promotes lfTLR4 translation in a KH domain–dependent manner, cells were transfected with plasmids encoding either wild-type MEX3B or a KH domain–deleted mutant (ΔKH). Wild-type MEX3B transfection resulted in significantly higher TLR4 protein abundance relative to control groups, whereas no such increase was observed in the ΔKH mutant group (Fig. 5E–F). Moreover, the KH mutant exhibited reduced binding affinity to lfTLR4 mRNA compared to wild-type MEX3B (Fig. 5G). These results demonstrate that MEX3B post-transcriptionally upregulates TLR4 expression by binding to TLR4 mRNA via its KH domain.

In vivo validation of MEX3B-mediated regulation of OA pathogenesis through TLR4

An OA model was established in C57BL/6 mice using ACLT surgery. Compared to the Sham group, histological evaluation of cartilage in the OA group revealed surface erosion and disorganized chondrocyte arrangement in H&E staining, Safranin O–Fast Green staining revealed a reduction in cartilage matrix staining intensity, suggestive of matrix loss. The scores from the OA Research Society International (OARSI) system were significantly higher (Fig. 6A–C). At the molecular level, the articular cartilage demonstrated a substantial upregulation in the expression of MEX3B and TLR4 proteins, along with heightened activation of TLR4 downstream molecules p-NF-κB p65 and p-IκBα. mRNA levels of MMP13 and ADAMTS5 were upregulated (Fig. 6D–H), and concentrations of IL-6, IL-1α, IL-1β, and TNF-α in synovial fluid were significantly increased (Fig. 6K). MEX3B knockout significantly reversed these pathological changes: cartilage damage was alleviated, OARSI and synovitis scores were reduced, and molecular indicators—including TLR4 expression, markers of downstream pathway activation and inflammatory cytokines were all substantially reduced relative to the OA group. However, intra-articular injection of MPLA in MEX3B-deficient mice led to notable increases in all these indicators relative to the MEX3B-KO group, effectively reversing the protective effects (Fig. 6I–J). These in vivo results confirm that MEX3B participates in OA-associated inflammation and cartilage damage by regulating TLR4 expression and function, our findings establish MEX3B as a novel and potential target for OA therapy.

Endogenous DAMP hyaluronan fragments activate the MEX3B–TLR4 axis in chondrocytes

To validate the relevance of the MEX3B–TLR4 axis under physiologically relevant OA conditions, we stimulated primary murine chondrocytes with low‑molecular‑weight hyaluronan fragments (HA‑fs), a well‑characterized endogenous DAMP in OA. HA‑fs treatment significantly upregulated TLR4 expression (Fig. S1A–E), enhanced the phosphorylation of NF‑κB p65 and IκBα (Fig. S1F–G), and increased the secretion of IL‑6 and TNF‑α (Fig. S1H–I). Importantly, knockdown of MEX3B markedly attenuated these HA‑fs‑induced responses, confirming that the regulatory role of MEX3B on TLR4 signaling is preserved in a DAMP‑driven inflammatory context.

Discussion

The pathological hallmark of OA lies in the vicious cycle between cartilage degeneration and joint inflammation, in which innate immune signaling pathways mediated by the TLR family serve as key molecular drivers [8, 11, 17, 29]. Through bioinformatics analysis, MEX3B was found to be upregulated in OA and significantly associated with the TLR signaling pathway, providing a directional basis for investigating the interaction between MEX3B and TLR family members, particularly TLR4.

In vitro experiments definitively confirmed the regulatory role of MEX3B on TLR4. In the LPS-induced OA model of chondrocytes, changes in MEX3B expression significantly affected TLR4 mRNA levels, protein expression, and membrane localization. MEX3B knockdown substantially reduced TLR4 expression and localization, thereby suppressing downstream NF-κB activation, there was a marked decline in the production of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1α, IL-1β, and TNF-α, and downregulating cartilage-degrading genes (MMP13, ADAMTS5). These effects closely mirrored those of the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242. Our study establishes that MEX3B regulates TLR4 at the cellular level, and demonstrates that this regulation directly impacts key pathological features of OA—namely, inflammation amplification and cartilage destruction—indicating that MEX3B may serve as an upstream regulatory factor of the TLR4 pathway. This finding adds to the growing body of evidence implicating RNA-binding proteins in OA pathogenesis [14, 15, 26].

Mechanistic investigations further elucidated the molecular basis of MEX3B’s regulation of TLR4. As an RBP, MEX3B maintains stable expression of TLR4 at the post-transcriptional level via direct interaction with TLR4 mRNA through its conserved KH domain. Deletion of the KH domain completely abolished this regulatory function, confirming its essential role. This finding broadens the current understanding of TLR4 regulation. While previous studies have primarily focused on transcriptional control (e.g., transcription factor binding), this study provides the first evidence that post-transcriptional regulation via RBPs—specifically mRNA stability—constitutes an important mechanism for TLR4 overexpression in OA. This offers new insights into the regulatory network governing TLR family member expression.

In vivo experiments using the ACLT-induced OA model validated the function of the MEX3B–TLR4 axis at the organismal level. The study showed that MEX3B knockout alleviated cartilage damage and synovial inflammation by downregulating TLR4, while MPLA administration reversed these protective effects, directly confirming that the pro-OA role of MEX3B is dependent on TLR4 activity. This comprehensive evidence—from molecular mechanisms to cellular functions and animal models—strongly supports the central role of the MEX3B–TLR4 axis in OA pathogenesis.

It should be noted that while our RIP-qPCR and KH-domain deletion experiments strongly implicate MEX3B in post‑transcriptional regulation of TLR4, they do not formally prove direct RNA binding. Future studies employing direct interaction assays (e.g., RNA pull‑down, EMSA) and more detailed characterization of domain‑deletion mutants will help to further elucidate the precise molecular mechanism.

From a clinical translational standpoint, our data support the targeting of MEX3B as a viable strategy for treating OA. Compared to direct inhibition of TLR4, targeting MEX3B may block the aberrant activation of TLR4 at a more upstream level, theoretically providing a more comprehensive suppression of TLR4-mediated inflammatory and catabolic effects. Future investigations are warranted to validate the correlation between MEX3B and TLR4 in human OA specimens and to explore the potential value of developing MEX3B-specific inhibitors. It is important to note that while our data establish MEX3B as a key post‑transcriptional regulator of TLR4 protein abundance, the precise molecular step—whether primarily through mRNA stabilization, enhanced translation, or both—awaits further dissection using direct translational assays such as polysome profiling.

First, this study did not investigate whether MEX3B has additional regulatory functions targeting other components of the TLR family. Second, the expression pattern of MEX3B in human OA tissues and its association with disease stage remain to be further validated. Third, the impact of long-term MEX3B inhibition on normal tissues, such as those involved in immune responses, requires careful evaluation. Subsequent studies should integrate clinical specimens and multiple OA models to elucidate the dynamic regulatory mechanisms of the MEX3B–TLR4 axis, thereby laying the foundation for its clinical translation. Fourth, it is important to note that our in vivo model, employing global MEX3B knockout and intra‑articular MPLA, reflects the integrated response of the whole joint. While consistent with our chondrocyte‑focused in vitro data, these experiments cannot exclude potential contributions from other TLR4‑expressing joint cells, such as synoviocytes or infiltrating immune cells. Future studies using cell‑type‑specific genetic models will help to delineate the precise cellular mechanisms by which the MEX3B–TLR4 axis drives OA pathology. Notably, although TLR4 was the focus of the present study, our transcriptomic analysis also revealed upregulation of TLR7 and TLR8 in OA cartilage, suggesting that MEX3B may have broader regulatory roles within the TLR family. Future investigations should explore whether MEX3B similarly modulates other TLR members, particularly TLR7 and TLR8, to fully elucidate its role in OA-associated inflammation and cartilage degeneration. Furthermore, while our data demonstrate that MEX3B knockdown/knockout suppresses inflammation and cartilage degradation coincident with TLR4 downregulation, we cannot fully exclude the possibility that MEX3B regulates other inflammatory transcripts contributing to the phenotype. Future studies employing TLR4 reconstitution in MEX3B-deficient settings will be crucial to definitively establish TLR4 as the necessary and sufficient downstream effector mediating the pro‑osteoarthritic function of MEX3B.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that MEX3B is upregulated in OA and regulates the expression and localization of TLR4 at the post-transcriptional level by directly binding to TLR4 mRNA via its KH domain. Activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway is induced by this process, leading to enhanced secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1α, IL-1β, TNF-α) and elevated expression of cartilage-degrading genes (MMP13, ADAMTS5), ultimately exacerbating joint inflammation and cartilage damage. Data from both in vitro and in vivo experiments established the TLR4 dependence of MEX3B’s pro-osteoarthritic actions. Specifically, knockdown or knockout of MEX3B alleviated OA pathological progression by inhibiting TLR4, while the administration of a TLR4 agonist reversed this protective effect. Collectively, this work establishes the MEX3B–TLR4 axis as a key driver of OA pathogenesis and highlights MEX3B’s potential as a novel drug target. Collectively, this work contributes a foundational framework for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying OA and for guiding the pursuit of novel therapies.
