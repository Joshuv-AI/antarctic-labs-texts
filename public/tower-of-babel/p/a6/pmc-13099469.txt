Comparative Effectiveness and Safety of Phosphorus-Lowering Drugs for CKD 3-5 Stages
Authors: Wenping Hou, Peitao Xie, Ya Fu, Cen Wang, Kun Wang, Yang Sun, Wei Wei, Lei Xu, Changhai Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of 12 phosphorus-lowering drugs for hyperphosphatemia in chronic kidney disease 3-5 stages.Study Design & MethodsSystematic review and network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs). We searched 3 databases from inception through September 2023 for RCTs evaluating 12 phosphorus-lowering drugs. We performed frequentist random-effects network meta-analyses and present mean differences and 95% CIs. Subgroup analyses were performed between the dialysis and nondialysis patients to assess robustness, source of heterogeneity, and risk of bias using the Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool.ResultsWe included 121 trials (18,376 participants) and compared 13 drugs or placebo. In terms of efficacy, except for sodium ferrous citrate, all drugs lowered the level of serum phosphorus compared with placebo. Sucroferric oxyhydroxide (PA21), nicotinic acid, and tenapanor were most likely to be ranked the best, second best, or third best. Calcium/magnesium carbonate, nicotinic acid, and colestilan posed lower risks for hypercalcemia than calcium-based phosphorus binders. All phosphorus-lowering drugs significantly affect serum intact parathyroid hormone levels compared with placebo. Colestilan, tenapanor, and PA21 posed a higher risk for gastrointestinal discomfort. In addition, iron-containing drugs showed positive effects on iron parameters.LimitationsFew high-quality RCTs; unclear allocation concealment and blinding; low evidence quality reduced reliability.ConclusionsPA21 has the best phosphorus-lowering effect in hyperphosphatemic adults with chronic kidney disease; considering efficacy and safety, calcium carbonate shows evidence of being the most appropriate drug with or without dialysis.RegistrationRegistered at PROSPERO (CRD42024500243).

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a persistent structural and functional disorder of the kidneys characterized by a history of kidney damage lasting for >3 months and has various etiologies.1 The global prevalence of CKD ranges from 11%-13%.2 With the increasing incidence of risk factors for CKD, such as population aging, type 2 diabetes, and high blood pressure, CKD is now one of the 3 leading causes of death worldwide.3 Hyperphosphatemia is a prevalent complication in patients with CKD, characterized by pruritus as its primary clinical manifestation.4 The severity of pruritus positively correlates with blood phosphorus levels, and up to 74% of dialysis patients experience this symptom, which significantly impairs their quality of life.5 Furthermore, elevated levels of blood phosphorus exhibit a robust correlation with cardiovascular disease,6 secondary hyperparathyroidism,7 soft tissue calcification,8 diminished bone density,9 and accelerated deterioration in kidney function.10 Available studies indicate a statistically significant 18% increase in the risk of mortality per 1 mg/dL elevation in blood phosphorus levels.11 Several studies have indicated that the public dissemination of phosphorus levels in food and drug often hinders our ability to effectively manage and attain target blood phosphorus levels solely through renal replacement therapy and dietary control.12, 13, 14, 15 Therefore, international guidelines recommend the concomitant use of oral phosphorus-lowering drugs or clinical treatment for hyperphosphatemia management to facilitate blood phosphorus control in patients.16

Toward the end of the previous century, aluminum-containing phosphate binders emerged as the initial generation of phosphorus-lowering drugs employed in clinical practice.17 Nevertheless, owing to the frequent occurrence of adverse effects such as anemia, neurotoxicity, and bone mineralization impairment,18 calcium-based phosphate binders superseded their aluminum-based counterparts. Although calcium-based phosphate binders exhibit consistent therapeutic efficacy and cost-effectiveness, their prolonged use may lead to an elevated calcium burden,19 thereby potentially resulting in hypercalcemia20 and vascular calcification, among other complications.21 In recent years, advancements in new drug research and development have led to the development of various types of phosphorus-lowering drugs. These include calcium-based options such as calcium carbonate (CC) and calcium acetate (CA), noncalcium and nonmetal-based alternatives such as sevelamer (SE), colestilan (CO), and tenapanor (TE), as well as metal-based choices such as lanthanum carbonate (LC), ferric citrate (FC), and sucroferric oxyhydroxide (PA21), abbreviations shown in Table 1.22, 23, 24Table 1List of AbbreviationsAbbreviationMeaningBIBixalomerCAcalcium acetateCaMgcalcium/magnesium carbonateCCcalcium carbonateCINeMAConfidence In Network Meta-AnalysisCKDchronic kidney diseaseCOcolestilanFCferric citrateiPTHintact parathyroid hormoneKDOQIKidney Disease Outcomes Quality InitiativeLClanthanum carbonateLDLlow-density lipoprotein cholesterolRRrelative riskSEsevelamerSFCsodium ferrous citrateSUCRAsurface under the cumulative ranking curveTEtenapanor

Some phosphorus-lowering drugs, such as SE and FC, exhibit additional therapeutic effects beyond hyperphosphatemia treatment. SE not only reduces lipid markers but also decreases all-cause mortality in dialysis patients,25 whereas FC improves iron stores in patients.22 In addition to ensuring the safety and effectiveness of drugs, the optimization of clinical benefits should be evaluated according to the characteristics of each drug, and the personalized use of drugs should be realized. Network meta-analysis provides a consistent framework to evaluate all available phosphate-lowering drugs and rank their efficacy and safety, even without head-to-head comparisons. For this purpose, we employed a network meta-analysis approach to compare 12 commercially available phosphorus-lowering drugs.

Methods

This study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42024500243). We performed the network meta-analysis according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.26

Information Sources, Search Strategy, and Selection Process

Two investigators (W.H. and P.X.) independently conducted a comprehensive review of the Cochrane Library, PubMed, and Embase databases on September 8, 2023, without restrictions regarding language, country, race, or region. Medical Subject Headings as well as relevant keywords were employed in the search for articles. Detailed information regarding the search terms and strategies can be found in Item S1.

Study Selection and Eligibility Criteria

Two independent reviewers (W.H. and C.W.) meticulously screened each title and abstract retrieved from the literature search. If either reviewer deemed a citation potentially relevant, the full text of the corresponding article was procured. Subsequently, both reviewers independently assessed the eligibility of all studies that underwent full-text evaluation. In instances in which multiple publications pertained to a single study, supplementary reports containing eligible outcome measures not presented in the primary report were incorporated as complementary information.

The inclusion criteria for eligible trials mandated the fulfillment of each of the following requirements: (1) enrollment of adult patients (aged ≥18 years) diagnosed with CKD, characterized by an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, as per the staging recommendations set forth by the American Kidney Foundation Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) group, encompassing both dialysis and nondialysis CKD patients classified within 3-5 stages; (2) randomization of participants to receive either a phosphate-lowering drug or a control treatment. The phosphate-lowering drugs considered included CC, CA, SE, LC, FC, bixalomer (BI), TE, PA21, nicotinic acid (NA), sodium ferrous citrate (SFC), calcium/magnesium carbonate (CaMg), CO, and placebo (PL); (3) reporting at least one of the following outcomes: serum phosphorus levels, serum calcium levels, or serum intact parathyroid hormone (iPTH); and (4) a minimum follow-up duration of 24 hours. Studies involving pediatric patients were excluded unless outcomes for adults were reported separately.

Data Collection and Risk of Bias Assessment

Study data were recorded in a customized data collection form accompanied by a comprehensive instruction manual. The following information was extracted from each study: author, year of publication, baseline characteristics of participants, number of participants in each arm at both the onset and completion of the study, trial duration, serum phosphorus levels, serum calcium levels, and serum iPTH levels. In instances where multiple measurements were provided during the follow-up period, the last measurement obtained was recorded. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s bias appraisal tool. Specifically, we evaluated the following factors: (1) adequate sequence generation, (2) allocation concealment, (3) blinding, and (4) intention-to-treat analysis; each question was rated as “high risk,” “low risk,” or “unclear,” with 2 reviewers (W.H. and K.W.) independently assessing each trial. Confidence in network meta-analysis effect estimates was evaluated for all outcomes and treatment comparisons using the CINeMA (Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis) methodological framework and application. In cases of disagreement, consensus was reached through open discussion.27

Data Items

The primary outcomes were changes in blood phosphorus levels. Secondary outcomes comprised serum calcium, calcium-phosphorus products, parathyroid hormone, serum creatinine, uric acid, hemoglobin concentrations, transferrin saturation percentages, serum ferritin, gastrointestinal-related adverse events, discontinuation because of adverse events, serious adverse events, and all-cause mortality.

Synthesis Methods

For the conventional meta-analysis, mean differences (MDs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using random-effects models. In the network meta-analysis, results from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were synthesized by employing a frequentist approach. Pooled MDs are reported for direct, indirect, and network effect estimates. The contribution plot illustrates the extent to which each direct comparison contributes to the indirect and overall network estimates.

The extent of network heterogeneity was assessed by comparing the common heterogeneity variance (tau [τ]) within each network to an empirical distribution of heterogeneity variances. A loop-specific approach was subsequently employed to evaluate the differences between direct and indirect treatment effect estimates (inconsistency factors) within triangular or quadrilateral loops in the network. Additionally, a design-by-treatment interaction model was used to derive a singular inference regarding the plausibility of assuming consistency within the network. Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine differences between subsets of the population of interest and to understand heterogeneity. Differences were assessed based on the patient and trial characteristics of dialysis and nondialysis.

All analyses were conducted using RevMan 5.3 and StataMP 17 (StataCorp LLP) with the network command and previously established routines. To rank treatments based on their probability of being the most effective for a specific outcome, the surface under the cumulative ranking curve was estimated using the network rank command. The relative effects of each intervention compared to PL were assumed to follow a multivariate normal distribution. Finally, the percentages associated with each possible rank were computed for all interventions. Statistical testing was two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Study Selection and Characteristics

Fig 1 illustrates the screening process for the studies. We identified 1,615 relevant articles from the Cochrane Library, Embase, and PubMed databases. After removing 798 duplicates, we evaluated 790 articles further. Based on a review of their titles and abstracts, 326 articles were excluded because of irrelevance. A comprehensive assessment of the remaining texts led to the exclusion of an additional 134 articles that did not meet our criteria; among these, 69 lacked pertinent outcomes. Consequently, a total of 121 eligible studies were included in the meta-analysis. Risk of Bias

Using the Cochrane Collaboration tool, we assessed the risk of bias across studies, which was frequently found to be high (Figs S1 and S2). Among the evaluated studies, 33 (27.5%) reported employing low-risk methods for random sequence generation, whereas 26 (21.67%) adequately concealed allocation. Additionally, 39 studies (32.5%) implemented blinding for both participants and investigators. Adverse events were systematically documented in 37 studies (30.83%), as detailed in Table S2.

To further explore which drug was most effective, surface under the cumulative ranking curves (SUCRAs) were abstracted to additionally rank the primary outcome improvements of drugs in aggregate. The SUCRA ranks primary outcomes in order of importance. PA21 was determined to be the best treatment for lowering serum phosphorus levels (SUCRA, 93.4%) followed by SFC (74.2%), LC (73.5%), SE (72.9%), and FC (64.1%). Serum Phosphorus in Nondialysis Patients

As shown in Fig 2B, we found 34 RCTs with 4,577 individuals that reported changes in serum phosphorus levels from baseline. All phosphorus-lowering drugs showed a lowering effect on serum phosphorus levels in nondialysis patients with CKD 3-5 stage compared with PL (Fig 3B), but the effects of PA21, SFC, CA, and CC were not statistically significant. The results were SE (MD, −0.31; 95% CI, −0.56 to −0.07), LC (MD, −0.36; 95% CI, −0.61 to −0.12), FC (MD, −0.49; 95% CI, −0.88 to −0.10), CO (MD, −0.81; 95% CI, −1.41 to −0.20), CA (MD, −0.19; 95% CI, −0.44 to 0.22), CC (MD, −0.11; 95% CI, −0.44 to 0.22), PA21 (MD, −0.31; 95% CI, −1.02 to 0.40), and SFC (MD, −0.27; 95% CI, −1.07 to 0.53) (Table S3B).

PA21, NA, and TE were most likely to be ranked the best, second best, and third best (SUCRA values of 89.8%, 68.6%, and 66.1%, respectively; Fig S3B). They were followed by CaMg, SE, LC, FC, CO, CA, BI, CC, and SFC (SUCRA values of 63.7%, 63.6%, 63.5%, 53.2%, 40.3%, 38.3%, 36.7%, 34.8%, and 29.3%, respectively). PL was definitively ranked as the least effective treatment.

Other Major Serum Biochemical Parameters

Creatinine and Uric Acid

Seven drugs were compared in the network diagram of serum creatinine values, and all drugs reduced serum creatinine compared with FC, but only SE was statistically significant. SUCRA values indicated that SE was best and FC was worst (Fig 5). Uric acid data are shown in Fig S7, and all interventions were superior to NA, but there was no statistical significance. The specific ranking order was CO, SE, PL, CC, CA, and NA (Fig 5).

Iron Parameters

For changes in hemoglobin levels, the network meta-analysis included 22 trials, cumulatively involving 3,634 participants (Fig S8A). In adults with CKD 3-5 stages, both FC and PA21 showed meaningful increases compared with PL, with FC (MD, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.40-0.83) showing the most significant hemoglobin upregulation, followed by PA21 (MD, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.02-0.72); detailed analysis results can be found in Table S7A. The SUCRA values are shown in Fig 5.

Fourteen RCTs including 7 drugs reported the results of transferrin saturation percentage (Fig S8B), and FC (MD, 12.44; 95% CI, 6.82-18.06) and PA21 (MD, 6.95; 95% CI, 3.39-10.51) showed a significant increase in transferrin levels compared with SE. Based on SUCRA values, the 3 iron-containing phosphorus-reducing drugs occupy the top 3 best treatment regimens (Fig 4). The results of the analysis of other interventions are shown in Table S7B.

FC was the most significant phosphorus-lowering drug for increasing ferritin (MD, 108.90; 95% CI, 11.69-206.11), followed by PA21 (MD, 107.36; 95% CI, 44.72-170.00). Moreover, FC, PA21, and SFC were ranked as the first, second, and third best treatment options for serum ferritin, respectively. The results of other drugs for ferritin are detailed in Fig S8C and Table S7C.

Lipid Indicators

A total of 25 studies with 1,977 patients reported high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels (Fig S9A), and the results showed no statistically significant differences among the 7 drugs, including PL (Table S8A). In terms of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), which was reported in 38 RCTs with 4,491 patients (Fig S9B), CO (MD, −32.29; 95% CI, −41.92 to −22.67) and SE (MD, −21.52; 95% CI, −28.44 to −14.59) significantly reduced the level of LDL (Table S8B), and CO and SE ranked first and second, respectively (Fig 4). A total of 22 studies including 2,388 patients reported triglyceride levels (Fig S8C); CO (MD, 69.61; 95% CI, 31.46-107.76), NA (MD, 67.43; 95% CI, 8.29-126.56), and SE (MD, 29.24; 95% CI, 1.24-57.24) significantly and meaningfully reduced the level of triglycerides compared with LC. The specific network meta-analysis results on high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, LDL, and triglycerides are detailed in Fig 5 and Table S8.

Severe Adverse Events

Serious drug-related adverse events were reported for 7 drugs including PL, and none was significant. TE, PA21, and SE were associated with the lowest incidence of serious adverse reactions compared with PL (Fig 5). The SUCRA rank of BI (11.9%) indicated it had the worst safety profile (see Fig S11 for details).

Discontinuation Because of Adverse Reactions

Network meta-analyses were conducted on 33 studies with 7,355 patients in total to evaluate discontinuation because of adverse reactions (Fig S12). As shown in Fig 6, the results showed that patients taking CC (relative risk [RR], 0.12; 95% CI, 0.02-0.63), SE (RR, 0.18; 95% CI, 0.04-0.75), and LC (RR, 0.1; 95% CI, 0.04-0.85) had a significantly lower probability of discontinuation compared with those taking TE. The SUCRA rankings were CC (88.9%), SE (75.2%), LC (72.7%), TE (16.1%), NA (18.6%), and CO (22.4%) (Fig S13).

All-Cause Mortality

Twenty studies reported all-cause mortality during the trial (Fig S14), and a meta-analysis of the network of all-cause deaths from phosphorus-lowering drugs showed some interesting results. For example, interventions with SE showed significantly lower all-cause mortality compared with PL (RR, 0.18; 95% CI, 0.05-0.73), without PA21 (RR, 0.23; 95% CI, 0.05-1.13) and CC (RR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.15-2.30). The difference in all-cause mortality between SE and these interventions was statistically significant. Fig 4 also suggests that SE may be the best treatment for reducing all-cause mortality, and PA21 showed the same effect compared with LC and CC. Specific meta-analysis results can be found in Table S9.

Subgroup Analyses

We separately compared patients who underwent dialysis and those who did not, as extracted from the included trials (Figs 3 and S3). For serum phosphorus, the subgroup analysis results of dialysis and nondialysis patients were basically consistent, proving that dialysis is not a factor affecting the heterogeneity of serum phosphorus. Data were insufficient in the individual subgroups to perform network meta-analyses investigating Ca×P production, iPTH, transferrin saturation percentage, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and ferritin. Moreover, subgroup analyses of creatinine, uric acid, and LDL were not performed because of significant inconsistency detected in the network. Therefore, we conducted an independent network meta-analysis for the available dialysis group data. The specific results can be found in Table S10 and Fig S15.

Discussion

This network meta-analysis study examined differences in the safety and efficacy outcomes of 12 phosphorus-lowering drugs (CC, CA, SE, LC, FC, BI, NA, PA21, CO, TE, CaMg, and SFC) in patients with CKD 3-5 stages with hyperphosphatemia. Compared with PL, all drugs except SFC significantly reduced serum phosphorus levels from baseline to endpoint. Previous reviews and meta-analyses have evaluated the effects of phosphorus-lowering drugs in patients with CKD and hyperphosphatemia.28,29 However, these studies did not comprehensively assess all available evidence, and many clinically relevant outcomes were not evaluated in previous analyses. Regarding safety assessments, existing studies lacked detailed analyses of key endpoints, including all-cause mortality, treatment discontinuation because of adverse events, and the incidence of serious adverse reactions during the trials. In contrast, our study incorporated a broader range of evaluation metrics not examined in prior research. To our knowledge, this network meta-analysis represents the most extensive investigation to date into the effects of phosphate-lowering treatments in patients with CKD 3-5 stages, including both dialysis (hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis) and nondialysis patients.

Continuous high serum calcium is a key factor leading to cardiovascular calcification.27 Although calcium-based phosphorus binders (CC and CA) significantly increase serum calcium levels, other drugs do not. A prospective cohort study of 439 patients with CKD without clinically diagnosed cardiovascular disease demonstrated that each 1 mg/dL (0.323 mmol/L) increase in serum phosphorus levels was associated with significantly increased calcification prevalence in the coronary artery (21%, P = 0.002), thoracic aorta (33%, P = 0.001), aortic valve (25%, P = 0.160), and mitral valve (62%, P = 0.007).30, 31, 32 It is recommended to limit the use of calcium-containing drugs in adults with CKD 3-5 stages receiving phosphate-lowering therapy.16 However, calcium-based phosphate binders (including CC and CA) have remained in clinical use since the 1980s because of their demonstrated efficacy and cost-effectiveness.33 The clinical application of calcium-based phosphate binders necessitates careful consideration of cost-effectiveness, therapeutic efficacy, and long-term clinical benefits.

SE is a calcium-free, metal-free, nonabsorbable polymer that binds phosphate through a crosslinked polymeric structure.34 SE contains multiple amine groups covalently bound to the polymer backbone via carbon atoms, which become protonated in the gastrointestinal tract and subsequently bind phosphate molecules through both ionic interactions and hydrogen bonding.35,36 In addition to its effect on serum phosphoric acid levels, SE binds bile acids, and binding bile acids with ion-exchange resins is a proven method of lowering blood cholesterol,37 which explains why we also observed the regulation of lipid levels with CO, which is also resin-based.38 The bile acid-binding properties of SE may impair fat absorption, potentially reducing uptake of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, and K).39 Therefore, clinicians should monitor and supplement with fat-soluble vitamins when using SE and CO.

Among all 12 investigated drugs, SE was the only agent associated with a significant reduction in all-cause mortality compared with PL. This survival benefit was also evident when comparing SE directly with CC, consistent with findings from both the PECERA40 and INDEPENDENT41 trials. Patients with CKD have a higher risk of cardiovascular disease,42 which is the leading cause of mortality in this population. The meta-analysis demonstrated that resin-based phosphate binders (SE and CO) significantly reduced LDL and triglyceride levels while maintaining high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels. Hyperlipidemia is a risk factor for cardiovascular events, which should account for at least part of the difference in all-cause mortality from use of SE.

Anemia is a frequent complication in advanced CKD,43 primarily caused by erythropoietin deficiency, iron metabolism disorders, blood loss, inflammation, malnutrition, and oxidative stress.44,45 Although treatment involves erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, iron supplementation, and nutritional support,46 this study found that iron-based phosphate binders (PA21, FC) not only controlled calcium-phosphorus levels but also improved iron stores. However, their use may be limited by stool discoloration, a common side effect,47 which could affect compliance and complicate detection of gastrointestinal bleeding, necessitating additional patient education. Effective erythropoietin requires adequate iron reserves,48 and iron-based phosphate binders (PA21, FC) significantly improved iron parameters in patients. This study also noted that compared with LC and CC, treatment with PA21 also seems to have a lower risk of mortality, which needs more clinical evidence to confirm.

TE and NA are not traditional phosphate binders. Conventional binders (eg, calcium/iron-based) form insoluble precipitates with dietary phosphate in the gut, limiting absorption, hence their requirement with meals. However, TE inhibits sodium-hydrogen exchanger 3, downregulating sodium-phosphate cotransport (via sodium-dependent phosphate transporter 2b) to reduce phosphate absorption.49 NA acts via GPR109A receptors to inhibit sodium-dependent phosphate transporter 2b,50 and it reduces active phosphate absorption through GPR109A receptor-mediated pathways.51 This unique mechanism explains both its efficacy ranking and distinct safety profile, showing lower hypercalcemia risk than calcium-based phosphorus-lowering drugs while avoiding metal-ion interactions. Because NA also modulates lipid metabolism,50 future studies should evaluate interactions between its phosphate-lowering and lipid-modulating effects.

As a targeted therapy, TE offers a low drug load, which is a key factor in medication compliance, especially in end-stage CKD in which phosphate binders account for 50% of total pill burden.15,52 Drug load (dose × frequency) is largely dose-dependent when dosing frequency is fixed. Thus, agents with lower doses or higher phosphate-lowering efficiency (eg, TE, PA21) may improve compliance. Observational and RCT data suggest suboptimal compliance with LC, likely because of its metallic taste.53 Metal-based binders generally require additional patient education to address tolerability and administration challenges.
