Association Between Admission Diastolic Blood Pressure and Clinical Outcomes in Elderly Patients with Severe/Critical COVID-19: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Fengxue Qu, Linshuang Wang, Dongfeng Wei, Qian Zhang, Jinghua Liu, Wenli Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElderly patients aged ≥65 years remain disproportionately vulnerable to severe COVID-19, with 30-day mortality rates ranging from 32% to 59% during the Omicron wave despite the variant’s reduced overall virulence. While blood pressure abnormalities are common in critically ill COVID-19 patients, the prognostic significance of admission diastolic blood pressure (DBP) as an independent predictor of short-term mortality in this population has not been systematically evaluated.ObjectiveTo investigate the association between DBP and clinical outcomes in this vulnerable population.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 240 consecutive elderly patients (aged ≥65 years) with severe/critical COVID-19 admitted to the Hospital from December 24, 2022 to January 24, 2023. Patients were categorized by admission DBP into three groups: hypotension (<70 mmHg), optimal range (70–89 mmHg), and poor control (≥90 mmHg). The primary outcome was 30-day mortality. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of mortality.ResultsThe full predictive model incorporating admission DBP category, age, chronic kidney disease, secondary infection, and oxygen saturation demonstrated good discrimination (AUC 0.880, 95% CI: 0.832–0.926). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test yielded χ2=19.077 (P=0.015), and the optimal discrimination threshold determined by Youden’s index yielded a sensitivity of 85.7% and a specificity of 84.6%.ConclusionIn elderly patients with severe/critical COVID-19, admission hypotension was a strong independent predictor of 30-day mortality. These findings suggest that careful blood pressure monitoring and avoidance of excessive DBP reduction may improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has disproportionately affected elderly populations, with individuals aged ≥65 years accounting for 87.9% of all COVID-19-related deaths despite representing a smaller proportion of total cases.1,2 During the Omicron variant wave in late 2022 and early 2023, despite reduced overall virulence compared to earlier variants, elderly patients with severe or critical disease continued to experience substantial mortality rates ranging from 32% to 59%.3–5 This persistent vulnerability underscores the critical need to identify readily available prognostic markers that can guide clinical management in this high-risk population.

Blood pressure abnormalities have emerged as important clinical features in COVID-19 patients, with hypertension consistently identified as one of the most prevalent comorbidities, affecting 28–55% of hospitalized patients.6–8 However, the relationship between blood pressure and COVID-19 outcomes is complex and incompletely understood. While most research has focused on pre-existing hypertension as a risk factor, the prognostic significance of actual blood pressure measurements at hospital admission, particularly diastolic blood pressure (DBP), has received limited attention.9,10 Recent global surveillance data during the Omicron-dominant period (2022–2023) indicate that patients aged ≥65 years account for more than 85% of COVID-19-attributable deaths, underscoring the urgent need for readily available prognostic markers in this high-risk population.

Emerging evidence suggests that COVID-19 infection can induce significant blood pressure changes through multiple mechanisms, including cytokine storm-mediated vasodilation, direct viral effects on the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, myocardial dysfunction, and autonomic dysregulation.11–13 Recent studies have documented paradoxical hypotension in frail geriatric COVID-19 patients, with substantial reductions in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure during acute illness.14,15 This phenomenon is particularly concerning in elderly patients, who may be vulnerable to organ hypoperfusion, including cerebral and renal ischemia, when blood pressure falls below critical thresholds.

Current critical care guidelines recommend maintaining mean arterial pressure ≥65 mmHg to prevent organ hypoperfusion in critically ill patients; however, specific DBP thresholds for elderly COVID-19 patients remain undefined. While the relationship between elevated blood pressure and cardiovascular complications is well-established in general populations,16,17 the optimal blood pressure range during acute severe illness remains controversial. In critically ill patients with sepsis, both hypotension and excessive blood pressure variability have been associated with adverse outcomes.18,19 However, whether similar patterns exist in elderly COVID-19 patients, and specifically which DBP thresholds predict mortality, has not been systematically investigated during the Omicron era.

The Omicron variant, which became dominant globally in late 2021, demonstrated substantially reduced lung tropism and lower rates of acute respiratory distress syndrome compared to earlier variants.20,21 Despite 40–60% lower case-fatality rates compared to the Delta variant, absolute mortality burden remained high in elderly populations due to the variant’s increased transmissibility.22,23 Understanding prognostic factors specific to this variant period is essential, as the clinical phenotype and complications profile differ markedly from earlier pandemic phases.

Importantly, unlike prior studies that focused primarily on pre-existing hypertension as a comorbidity, this study investigates the prognostic value of the actual admission DBP measurement—a real-time, readily available clinical parameter—which better reflects hemodynamic status at the time of hospitalization.

This study aimed to address these knowledge gaps by investigating the association between admission DBP levels and 30-day mortality in elderly patients hospitalized with severe or critical COVID-19 during the Omicron wave. Specifically, we sought to: (1) characterize DBP distribution patterns in this population; (2) identify DBP thresholds associated with increased mortality risk; (3) determine whether DBP independently predicts mortality after adjusting for demographics, comorbidities, and disease severity markers; and (4) develop a risk stratification model incorporating DBP for clinical decision support. We hypothesized that both hypotension and poor blood pressure control would be associated with increased mortality, but that hypotension would confer greater immediate risk due to hemodynamic instability in the acute illness setting.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Hospital, a tertiary care teaching hospital affiliated with the University and designated as a COVID-19 treatment center during the pandemic. The study included consecutive elderly patients (aged ≥65 years) admitted with severe or critical COVID-19 between December 24, 2022 and January 24, 2023, during the Omicron BA.5 and BF.7 subvariant-predominant period in Beijing.24

Patient Selection

Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) age ≥65 years; (2) laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) or rapid antigen test; (3) classification as severe or critical disease according to the National Health Commission of China guidelines;25 and (4) complete medical records including blood pressure measurements within 24 hours of admission.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) mild or moderate COVID-19 disease severity; (2) death within 24 hours of admission; (3) incomplete blood pressure data or missing key clinical variables; and (4) patients transferred from other hospitals with incomplete initial assessment data.

Disease severity was classified according to national criteria: severe COVID-19 was defined as meeting any of the following: (1) respiratory rate ≥30 breaths/min; (2) oxygen saturation ≤93% at rest on room air; (3) arterial partial pressure of oxygen/fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2) ≤300 mmHg; or (4) lung infiltrates >50% within 24–48 hours. Critical COVID-19 was defined as meeting any of: (1) respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation; (2) shock; or (3) other organ failure requiring intensive care unit (ICU) treatment.25

Data Collection

Trained research assistants extracted data from electronic medical records using a standardized case report form. Demographic variables included age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). Comorbidities were identified based on documented diagnoses prior to admission and included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and cerebrovascular disease (CVD). The presence of hypertension was defined as documented history of hypertension or current use of antihypertensive medications.

Blood Pressure Measurement

Blood pressure was measured using calibrated automatic oscillometric devices (OMRON HEM-7051, Omron Healthcare, Kyoto, Japan) within 24 hours of admission. Measurements were performed with patients in the supine position after at least 5 minutes of rest. An appropriate cuff size was selected based on arm circumference. Three consecutive measurements were obtained at 5-minute intervals, and the average of the three readings was used for analysis. Both systolic blood pressure (SBP) and DBP were recorded.

Patients were categorized into three groups based on admission DBP: (1) hypotension (DBP <70 mmHg); (2) optimal range (DBP 70–89 mmHg, serving as the reference group); and (3) poor control (DBP ≥90 mmHg). These cutoffs were selected based on previous literature demonstrating increased mortality risk with DBP <70 mmHg in critically ill elderly patients and hypertension guidelines defining DBP ≥90 mmHg as stage 1 hypertension requiring treatment.14,26

Laboratory Measurements

Blood samples were collected within 24 hours of admission. Laboratory parameters included complete blood count (white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count), inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein [CRP], procalcitonin), coagulation markers (D-dimer), cardiac biomarkers (high-sensitivity troponin I, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide [NT-proBNP]), and renal function markers (serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen). All laboratory tests were performed in the hospital’s central laboratory using standard automated analyzers.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality within 30 days of hospital admission. Secondary outcomes included: (1) ICU admission; (2) mechanical ventilation requirement; (3) hospital length of stay; and (4) major complications including acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), acute kidney injury (AKI), secondary bacterial infection, and septic shock. ARDS was defined according to the Berlin criteria.27 AKI was defined using the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria.28 Secondary infection was diagnosed based on positive microbiological cultures or clinical evidence of bacterial infection requiring antibiotic therapy. Septic shock was defined as sepsis with persistent hypotension requiring vasopressor therapy to maintain mean arterial pressure ≥65 mmHg and serum lactate >2 mmol/L.29

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage and compared using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were <5.

To identify predictors of 30-day mortality, we performed univariable logistic regression analysis for all candidate variables including demographic factors, comorbidities, vital signs, and laboratory parameters. Variables with P<0.10 in univariable analysis were entered into multivariable logistic regression using backward stepwise selection (removal criterion P>0.10). DBP category was forced into the model regardless of significance to address the primary study question. The optimal range group (DBP 70–89 mmHg) served as the reference category. Results were reported as crude and adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with VIF >5 indicating problematic collinearity. Model discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis with calculation of area under the curve (AUC) and 95% CI. The optimal cutoff value was determined using Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity - 1). Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier curves with Log rank test to compare survival distributions across DBP groups. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to identify independent predictors of time to death, with results reported as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CI. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals.

Sensitivity analyses included: (1) analyzing DBP as a continuous variable with assessment of potential non-linear relationships using restricted cubic splines; (2) repeating analyses after excluding patients who died within 48 hours of admission; and (3) conducting subgroup analyses stratified by pre-existing hypertension status.

Sample Size Justification. The study enrolled 240 consecutive patients during the predefined one-month recruitment period, yielding 84 mortality events. For the final multivariable logistic regression model containing six predictors, the events-per-variable (EPV) ratio was 84/6 = 14.0, exceeding the conventionally recommended minimum EPV of 10 and supporting adequate statistical power for the primary analysis.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant. Missing data were minimal (<3% for any variable) and handled using complete case analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval number: 2024114X). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data, in accordance with national regulations on medical research. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments.

Comorbidity Profile

Comorbidities were highly prevalent in this elderly cohort. Hypertension was present in 54.2% of patients, diabetes mellitus in 49.2%, coronary heart disease (CHD) in 25.8%, chronic kidney disease (CKD) in 28.3%, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) in 17.9%, and cerebrovascular disease (CVD) in 12.5%.

Several comorbidities showed significant associations with mortality. Diabetes was more prevalent in non-survivors (59.5% vs 43.6%, P=0.026). CHD was substantially more common in non-survivors (40.5% vs 17.9%, P<0.001), as was COPD (26.2% vs 13.5%, P=0.023) and CVD (22.6% vs 7.1%, P=0.001). Interestingly, CKD prevalence was similar between groups (29.8% vs 27.6%, P=0.833), though it emerged as a significant predictor in multivariable analysis.

When categorized by DBP level, 63 patients (26.2%) presented with hypotension (DBP <70 mmHg), 131 (54.6%) were in the optimal range (DBP 70–89 mmHg), and 46 (19.2%) had poor control (DBP ≥90 mmHg). The distribution of DBP categories differed markedly between survivors and non-survivors (P<0.001). Among non-survivors, 42.9% were hypotensive at admission compared to only 17.3% of survivors. Conversely, 64.7% of survivors had DBP in the optimal range compared to 35.7% of non-survivors. Poor DBP control (≥90 mmHg) was present in 21.4% of non-survivors and 17.9% of survivors (Figure 1).

Vital Signs and Laboratory Parameters

Non-survivors presented with significantly more severe physiological derangements at admission. Respiratory rate was elevated in non-survivors (25.58±3.64 vs 22.42±2.71 breaths/min, P<0.001), and oxygen saturation was markedly lower (87.18±5.17% vs 93.31±3.72%, P<0.001).

Laboratory parameters reflecting inflammation, coagulation, cardiac stress, and immune function were significantly worse in non-survivors (Table 1). Median C-reactive protein was nearly three-fold higher in non-survivors (71.80 mg/L, IQR 58.81–105.12) compared to survivors (24.52 mg/L, IQR 16.42–41.94, P<0.001). D-dimer levels were also substantially elevated in non-survivors (median 2225.17 μg/L, IQR 1449.04–2951.79) versus survivors (730.55 μg/L, IQR 522.02–1154.23, P<0.001).

NT-proBNP demonstrated marked elevation in non-survivors (median 801.85 pg/mL, IQR 515.98–1310.06) compared to survivors (227.97 pg/mL, IQR 149.81–395.07, P<0.001), indicating significant cardiac stress or dysfunction. Lymphocyte counts were profoundly depressed in non-survivors (0.68±0.27×109/L vs 1.09±0.33×109/L, P<0.001), while neutrophil counts were elevated (6.89±1.77×109/L vs 4.42±1.51×109/L, P<0.001), reflecting severe inflammatory and immunosuppressive states.

Mortality by DBP Category

Mortality rates varied significantly across DBP categories (χ2=22.352, P<0.001; Figure 1B). Among patients with hypotension (DBP <70 mmHg), 36 of 63 (57.1%) died within 30 days. In the optimal DBP range (70–89 mmHg), mortality was 22.9% (30/131). Among those with poor DBP control (≥90 mmHg), mortality was 39.1% (18/46).

The U-shaped relationship between DBP and mortality was evident, with both hypotension and poor control associated with increased risk compared to the optimal range. However, hypotension carried substantially greater risk than poor control, with mortality more than twice as high in the hypotensive group.

Complications

Major complications occurred frequently and were significantly more common in non-survivors (Table 2). Acute respiratory distress syndrome developed in 64 patients (26.7%) overall, affecting 45.2% of non-survivors versus 16.7% of survivors (P<0.001). Sensitivity Analyses

When DBP was analyzed as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines, a non-linear U-shaped relationship with mortality was evident, with nadir mortality risk at DBP 75–80 mmHg and progressively increasing risk at both lower and higher values. After excluding 12 patients who died within 48 hours of admission, the association between hypotension and mortality remained robust (adjusted OR 3.82, 95% CI: 1.89–7.71, P<0.001).

Subgroup analysis stratified by pre-existing hypertension status showed that the association between admission hypotension and mortality was similar in patients with (adjusted OR 4.28, 95% CI: 1.85–9.91, P=0.001) and without (adjusted OR 3.87, 95% CI: 1.52–9.84, P=0.004) documented hypertension, suggesting the prognostic significance of low DBP was independent of prior hypertension diagnosis.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study of 240 elderly patients with severe or critical COVID-19 during the Omicron wave demonstrates that admission diastolic blood pressure is a strong independent predictor of 30-day mortality. Our principal findings are threefold: (1) hypotension (DBP <70 mmHg) at admission independently predicted mortality with 4.12-fold increased odds after adjusting for age, comorbidities, and disease severity markers; (2) a U-shaped relationship exists between DBP and mortality, with both hypotension and poor control (≥90 mmHg) associated with increased risk, though hypotension conferred substantially greater risk; and (3) a predictive model incorporating DBP category achieved excellent discrimination (AUC 0.88), suggesting its clinical utility for risk stratification. Critically, this study is among the first to demonstrate the prognostic value of the actual admission DBP value—rather than the mere presence of a hypertension diagnosis—providing a more dynamic and actionable clinical indicator for risk stratification at hospital presentation.

Our findings align with and extend previous observations of blood pressure patterns in COVID-19 patients. Yuan et al demonstrated that day-by-day DBP variability independently predicted mortality and ICU admission in COVID-19 patients with hypertension, with standard deviation ≥7.77 mmHg conferring increased risk.30 While that study focused on blood pressure variability, our investigation complements these findings by demonstrating that absolute DBP levels at admission carry independent prognostic significance.

The U-shaped relationship between blood pressure and outcomes has been observed in other critical illness contexts. In sepsis patients, both hypotension and excessive mean arterial pressure variability associate with increased mortality.31,32 Our findings extend this concept to COVID-19, demonstrating that elderly patients with severe disease exhibit similar patterns. Notably, hypotension carried substantially greater immediate risk than poor control (57.1% vs 39.1% mortality), suggesting that hemodynamic instability poses more urgent threat than chronic hypertension in the acute illness setting.

Our observation of hypotension as a mortality predictor contrasts with early pandemic studies suggesting hypertension as a primary risk factor.6,8 This apparent discrepancy reflects an important distinction: while pre-existing hypertension increases COVID-19 severity risk through chronic vascular and metabolic effects, acute hypotension during hospitalization signals hemodynamic decompensation. The lack of association between pre-existing hypertension and mortality in our multivariable model (after adjustment for acute blood pressure) supports this interpretation.

The phenomenon of COVID-19-induced hypotension, particularly in frail elderly patients, has been documented by Kvasnicka and Sovova, who reported substantial DBP reductions (median decrease 11–18 mmHg) in geriatric COVID-19 patients post-infection.14 Our data extend these observations, demonstrating that patients presenting with hypotension face dramatically elevated mortality risk. This pattern likely reflects COVID-19’s systemic hemodynamic effects through multiple mechanisms discussed below.

Several interconnected mechanisms may explain the association between admission hypotension and mortality in elderly severe COVID-19 patients. Cytokine storm-mediated vasodilation represents a primary pathway, with elevated IL-6, TNF-α, and other inflammatory mediators causing widespread vasoplegia and increased vascular permeability.33,34 Our finding of markedly elevated CRP in non-survivors (median 71.80 vs 24.52 mg/L) supports this mechanism’s contribution.

Myocardial dysfunction contributes substantially to hemodynamic instability. NT-proBNP levels were dramatically elevated in non-survivors (median 801.85 vs 227.97 pg/mL), indicating significant cardiac stress or dysfunction. COVID-19 can cause direct myocardial injury through viral myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, or demand ischemia in the setting of severe hypoxemia and increased metabolic demands.35,36 Impaired cardiac output secondary to myocardial dysfunction would manifest as hypotension and reduced organ perfusion.

Hypovolemia from fluid losses and decreased oral intake during acute illness may compound hypotension, particularly in elderly patients with impaired thirst perception and reduced renal concentrating ability.37 The combination of insensible losses from fever, reduced fluid intake, and potential gastrointestinal losses creates a state of relative or absolute hypovolemia that becomes clinically apparent as hypotension.

Autonomic dysfunction has emerged as an important feature of acute COVID-19 and long COVID syndrome.38,39 SARS-CoV-2 infection may impair baroreceptor reflexes and autonomic regulation of vascular tone, particularly in elderly patients with pre-existing age-related autonomic changes. This could manifest as orthostatic hypotension or inability to maintain adequate blood pressure during illness-related stress.

Medication effects warrant consideration, as patients with pre-existing hypertension may have experienced excessive blood pressure reduction from continued antihypertensive therapy during acute illness. However, our subgroup analysis demonstrated similar effects of hypotension in patients with and without documented hypertension, suggesting this represents a general phenomenon rather than medication-specific effect.

The vicious cycle of hypotension and organ hypoperfusion likely amplifies mortality risk. Low DBP reduces diastolic coronary perfusion pressure, potentially exacerbating myocardial ischemia in patients with underlying coronary disease (40.5% of non-survivors had CHD). Cerebral hypoperfusion in elderly patients with impaired autoregulation may contribute to encephalopathy and reduced protective airway reflexes. Renal hypoperfusion precipitates acute kidney injury (present in 47.6% of non-survivors), which further complicates fluid and electrolyte management.

Our findings have several important clinical implications for managing elderly patients with severe COVID-19. Blood pressure monitoring should be prioritized as a readily available prognostic marker. Patients presenting with DBP <70 mmHg require immediate evaluation for underlying causes (hypovolemia, sepsis, cardiac dysfunction) and aggressive supportive measures.

Avoidance of excessive blood pressure reduction is warranted. In elderly COVID-19 patients with pre-existing hypertension, clinicians should exercise caution when continuing or initiating antihypertensive therapy during acute illness. Target blood pressure should generally be permissive, maintaining DBP in the 70–85 mmHg range unless specific indications for tighter control exist. Antihypertensive medications should be judiciously reduced or temporarily discontinued if DBP falls below 70 mmHg, balancing the need to maintain adequate perfusion pressure against long-term cardiovascular risks.

Early intervention for hypotension should follow systematic evaluation. Fluid resuscitation guided by volume responsiveness assessment represents first-line therapy for hypovolemia-related hypotension. However, in patients with elevated NT-proBNP suggesting cardiac dysfunction, cautious fluid administration with close hemodynamic monitoring is essential to avoid pulmonary edema. Vasopressor therapy may be required in distributive shock from sepsis or cytokine storm, with norepinephrine typically preferred as first-line agent.40

Risk stratification incorporating DBP can guide clinical decision-making. Our predictive model achieved 85.0% accuracy, enabling identification of highest-risk patients who may benefit from more intensive monitoring or earlier ICU transfer. The combination of hypotension, advanced age, chronic kidney disease, low oxygen saturation, and signs of infection warrants aggressive intervention and consideration for escalated level of care.

Our study’s timing during the Omicron BA.5/BF.7 wave (December 2022-January 2023) provides contemporary data relevant to current practice. Despite Omicron’s reduced overall virulence compared to earlier variants, our observed 35.0% mortality rate in elderly severe/critical patients demonstrates persistent substantial risk in this vulnerable population. This aligns with recent literature showing Omicron mortality rates of 32–59% in elderly severe/critical cases, despite 40–60% lower case-fatality rates compared to Delta variant.3–5,22

The maintained prognostic significance of admission hypotension during the Omicron era suggests that hemodynamic instability represents a common final pathway to mortality across variants, despite differences in viral tropism and respiratory pathology. This underscores the importance of supportive care and hemodynamic management even as variants evolve and disease phenotypes shift.

This study has several strengths. The Omicron-era data provide contemporary evidence most relevant to current practice. Our focused elderly cohort with severe/critical disease captures the highest-risk population where prognostic markers have greatest clinical utility. Comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders including comorbidities, vital signs, and laboratory markers strengthens causal inference. The rigorous statistical methodology, including sensitivity analyses and model validation, enhances result reliability.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The single-center retrospective design limits generalizability to other settings and populations. Selection bias may exist, though consecutive enrollment of all eligible patients mitigates this concern. Blood pressure measurements were limited to admission rather than continuous monitoring, precluding analysis of blood pressure variability’s prognostic value—an important area for future investigation. Residual confounding remains possible despite multivariable adjustment, as unmeasured variables (eg., frailty indices, pre-hospital trajectory) may influence outcomes.

Future Research Directions. The findings of this study warrant confirmation through prospective multicenter studies that can overcome the inherent limitations of retrospective single-center designs. Future investigations should explore whether active DBP management targeting the 70–89 mmHg range can improve outcomes in elderly COVID-19 patients and whether continuous blood pressure monitoring provides additional prognostic information beyond single admission measurements. External validation of the predictive model in independent cohorts would further strengthen the generalizability of these findings.

Our sample size of 240 patients, while adequate for primary analyses following power calculations, limited our ability to perform extensive subgroup analyses or include numerous predictors in multivariable models. We followed the conservative rule of 10 events per predictor variable, restricting our final model to six predictors. Larger studies could examine interactions between DBP and other variables or develop more complex predictive models.

The lack of external validation cohort represents an important limitation. Although internal validation through bootstrapping suggested robust model performance, validation in independent cohorts from different institutions and geographic regions is necessary before widespread clinical implementation. Prospective validation would be particularly valuable to assess real-world performance and clinical utility.

We could not determine directionality for some associations. For example, while secondary bacterial infection strongly predicted mortality, this could reflect either increased susceptibility to infection among sicker patients or direct effects of infection on outcomes. Longitudinal assessment of infection timing relative to clinical deterioration would clarify this relationship.

Several important research questions emerge from our findings. Prospective studies with continuous blood pressure monitoring could elucidate the independent and interactive effects of admission blood pressure levels and subsequent variability on outcomes. Time-series analyses could identify critical periods when blood pressure changes most strongly predict deterioration.

Interventional trials are needed to determine optimal blood pressure management strategies in elderly severe COVID-19 patients. Randomized trials comparing different blood pressure targets (eg., DBP 60–70 vs 70–80 vs 80–90 mmHg) could inform evidence-based guidelines. Such trials should incorporate individualized approaches accounting for pre-existing cardiovascular disease, autoregulation capacity, and concurrent organ dysfunction.

Mechanistic studies examining the pathophysiology of COVID-19-associated hypotension would enhance understanding. Serial echocardiography, cardiac biomarkers, vascular resistance measurements, and autonomic function testing could identify primary drivers of hemodynamic instability in different patient phenotypes. Biomarker-guided therapy (eg., NT-proBNP-guided fluid management) might improve outcomes.

Post-discharge blood pressure patterns and long-term cardiovascular outcomes require investigation. Kvasnicka and Sovova documented persistent hypotension in some COVID-19 patients months after recovery.14 Longitudinal studies tracking blood pressure trajectories, cardiovascular events, and mortality over 1–5 years post-COVID would inform long-term management and surveillance strategies.

Validation across variants and populations remains essential. As SARS-CoV-2 continues evolving, studies across different variants, age groups, ethnicities, and healthcare settings would establish generalizability. Multicenter international collaborations could pool data to develop robust, validated risk prediction tools.

Integration with other prognostic markers could enhance risk stratification. Combining DBP with biomarkers (troponin, lactate, procalcitonin), severity scores (SOFA, APACHE), and emerging technologies (artificial intelligence analyzing chest imaging or electronic health record patterns) might identify synergistic effects and improve predictive accuracy beyond any single marker.

In conclusion, admission diastolic blood pressure represents a readily available, clinically meaningful prognostic marker in elderly patients with severe or critical COVID-19. Hypotension (DBP <70 mmHg) independently predicts 30-day mortality with 4-fold increased odds, suggesting that careful blood pressure monitoring and avoidance of excessive DBP reduction may improve outcomes. These findings should inform clinical practice and stimulate further research to optimize hemodynamic management strategies in this vulnerable population. Based on these findings, maintaining admission DBP within the 70–89 mmHg range appears optimal for elderly patients with severe/critical COVID-19. Clinicians should exercise caution regarding antihypertensive therapy that may inadvertently lower DBP below 70 mmHg, particularly in patients with pre-existing hemodynamic compromise. However, these findings should be interpreted in the context of the study’s limitations, including its single-center retrospective design and potential residual confounding. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate the identified DBP thresholds and evaluate whether targeted DBP management can improve clinical outcomes in elderly patients with severe COVID-19.
