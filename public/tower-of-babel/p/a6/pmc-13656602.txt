Cysteine derivatives coordinate photosynthetic performance and osmotic adjustment to enhance drought tolerance in vegetable soybean
Authors: Bongiwe M. Khoza, Ned B. Bowden, Makoena J. Moloi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrought stress severely constrains photosynthetic efficiency in vegetable soybean. However, the role of cysteine-based biostimulants in coordinating photosynthetic regulation and osmotic adjustment remains poorly understood. This study evaluated how N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and N-acetylcysteine trisulfide (NAC-TS) modulate photosynthetic function, photoprotection, and osmotic adjustment to enhance growth and reproductive performance under drought in two vegetable soybean (Glycine max L. Merr.) cultivars with different yielding capabilities and drought sensitivities (UVE17 and AGS429).ResultsBoth cysteine derivatives slightly increased stomatal conductance under drought. N-acetylcysteine enhanced photosystem II (PSII) functionality in UVE17, as evidenced by increased performance index (PIabs), increased energy absorption and trapping per reaction centre, reduced energy dissipation, and improved pigment stability. In contrast, NAC-TS elicited stronger photochemical and energy flux responses in AGS429, while promoting higher chlorophyll and carotenoid accumulation in UVE17. These responses were closely associated with cultivar-dependent osmotic adjustment, where NAC increased relative water content and soluble sugars in UVE17, whereas NAC-TS enhanced proline and soluble sugars in AGS429. The coordinated improvement in water status, pigment stability, and osmotic balance helped sustain photosynthetic activity and protect the photosynthetic apparatus under drought stress. Consequently, these physiological adjustments translated into improved growth and reproductive performance, which were also strongly cultivar and treatment-dependent. NAC enhanced shoot biomass, leaf area, and shoot length (notably in UVE17), while NAC-TS increased shoot length and leaf area in both cultivars. At the reproductive level, NAC-TS showed improved reproductive development in AGS429 by increasing pod number.ConclusionsNAC and NAC-TS enhance drought resilience in vegetable soybean through coordinated regulation of photosynthesis, photoprotection, osmotic adjustment, and water relations, ultimately improving growth and reproductive performance. Their effects are strongly cultivar- and trait-dependent, highlighting the importance of genotype-specific optimisation of cysteine derivative applications for effective drought mitigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09484-9.

Introduction

Anthropogenic climate change has had a profound impact on agriculture in recent years, affecting crop productivity and water availability on a global scale [55, 84]. Human-induced climate change has intensified drought conditions, resulting in higher crop production losses, food shortages, and increased food insecurity [23, 49, 83]. Drought stress affects one of the most important biological processes in plants, photosynthesis, a fundamental driver of biomass accumulation and food production [67, 92]. Water deficit in plants disrupts both the biochemical components of photosynthesis by restricting CO2 availability and impairing electron transport, and destabilising chloroplast redox homeostasis [35, 91, 93]. Because crop yield ultimately reflects cumulative carbon assimilation, maintaining photosynthetic efficiency under drought conditions is central to sustaining food security in an increasingly warming climate. In addition, identifying nutritionally important crops and developing strategies to preserve their photosynthetic performance under water-limited conditions are critical research priorities.

Vegetable soybean, commonly known as edamame [Glycine max L. (Merr.)], is a promising nutrient-rich source of plant-based protein, positioning it as a viable meat alternative and a valuable crop for combating nutritional deficiencies. The crop is rich in vitamins, minerals, dietary fibre, and isoflavones, which contribute to its health-promoting properties [17, 28, 36, 58, 63]. Vegetable soybean is harvested at the reproductive six (R6) growth stage, when the seeds are fully developed within fresh, green pods. Their short life cycle also enhances their appeal as a cash crop, especially for smallholder farmers in resource-limited settings [9, 36]. Given these qualities, vegetable soybeans have great potential to improve food and nutrition security in Africa. However, their high sensitivity to drought stress poses a significant challenge to their cultivation, limiting their effectiveness in drought-prone regions [50, 82].

During drought, plants respond by closing their stomata to minimise water loss through transpiration [67]. However, this physiological response negatively impacts carbon assimilation, as stomatal closure reduces the uptake of carbon dioxide (CO2), which is essential for the photosynthetic reactions. Drought stress also negatively impacts photosynthetic pigments, reducing photosynthetic rate and energy production [35, 91, 93]. Drought stress damages chloroplast ultrastructure, reducing chlorophyll content and, consequently, impairing photosynthetic activity [73]. Hlahla et al. [29] observed a significant degradation of chlorophyll-a (Chl-a) in vegetable soybean under drought conditions, which negatively impacts the overall photosynthesis rate because Chl-a is the primary pigment involved in the process [75].

Photosynthesis is critically affected by the stomatal closure, leading to reduced internal CO2 concentration and constraining Calvin cycle activity [65, 95]. During drought stress, plants absorb light energy, leading to accumulation of excess excitation energy in chloroplasts. This imbalance promotes the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS). These ROS cause significant damage to photosystem II (PSII) and photosystem I (PSI), which are particularly susceptible to oxidative stress under drought. Damage to PSII inhibits water-molecule splitting, resulting in decreased molecular oxygen evolution and constraining linear electron flow from water to the plastoquinone pool. The subsequent reduction in electron transport efficiency compromises the overall photochemical performance, increasing the plant’s susceptibility to photoinhibition and irreversible photodamage, adversely affecting crop yield and quality [67, 86, 91, 99].

To counter these harmful effects, carotenoid pigments, beyond their established role in light harvesting within photosynthetic tissues [60], assume a critical role in protecting the photosynthetic apparatus under drought conditions. Specifically, they contribute to photoprotection by dissipating excess excitation energy and neutralising ROS [19, 64, 77]. In addition to photoprotective pigment responses, plants activate metabolic adjustments to maintain photosynthetic function during drought stress. Drought conditions stimulate the accumulation of osmoprotectants, including total soluble sugars and proline [85]. These compounds contribute to osmotic adjustment by maintaining cellular water potential and preserving cell turgor, which is essential for sustaining stomatal function and CO2 diffusion into the leaf for photosynthesis [86]. Nguyen et al. [56] observed that drought induces the accumulation of proline and soluble sugars in soybean, aiding in osmotic balance and protecting cellular structures, including chloroplasts, from drought-induced damage. Similarly, [50] reported that drought-tolerant vegetable soybean cultivars exhibited higher levels of these osmolytes, which were associated with improved photosynthetic performance and resilience under water-deficit conditions. Relative water content further reflects plant water status and the ability of leaves to maintain physiological processes such as photosynthesis, under stress. These physiological and biochemical adjustments help maintain photosynthetic efficiency and contribute to enhanced drought tolerance in vegetable soybean, which is particularly important given the crop’s sensitivity to drought [82]. Nevertheless, drought stress continues to significantly constrain vegetable soybean productivity, highlighting the need for strategies that specifically protect and enhance photosynthetic performance under water-limited conditions.

Recent studies have shown that cysteine derivatives, such as N-acetylcysteine (NAC), can mitigate the adverse effects of environmental stress in plants. NAC, a cysteine-derived compound naturally occurring in Allium species such as Allium sativum (garlic) and Allium cepa (onion), is considered a promising biostimulant due to its antioxidant properties [5, 57, 70]. These antioxidant properties protect the photosynthetic machinery from oxidative damage and improve plant tolerance to environmental stress. Evidence from various crop studies in dicates that NAC contributes to stress alleviation by improving photosynthetic performance,for example, in wheat subjected to heavy metal stress, NAC application improved root development and increased biomass accumulation [12]. Under drought conditions, NAC has been shown to enhance CO2 assimilation and facilitate the recovery of photosystem II in sugarcane [74]. Additionally, its application in wheat resulted in improved relative water content, increased grain number and total yield, and enhanced chlorophyll and osmolyte biosynthesis [20]. Early work by Zhang et al. [96] demonstrated that endogenous hydrogen sulphide (H2S) functions as a signalling molecule in plants. Subsequent studies have shown that exogenous H2S enhances growth, yield, and photosynthetic capacity in several crops during drought conditions [16, 80, 98]. More recently, N-acetylcysteine trisulfide (NAC-TS) has emerged as a novel compound that endogenously releases H2S. Owing to its enhanced stability and sustained release of H₂S, as demonstrated experimentally by Brown and Bowden [6], NAC-TS is considered a promising compound for improving plant resilience to abiotic stress and holds considerable potential for agricultural applications. Both NAC and NAC-TS are environmentally friendly and exhibit greater bioavailability and stability than traditional amino acid biostimulants [5, 6, 57].

Despite evidence that cysteine-derived compounds can mitigate stress, how N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and N-acetylcysteine trisulfide (NAC-TS) coordinate photosynthetic performance, photoprotection, osmotic adjustment, and yield under drought remains unclear. Notably, this study provides a comparative assessment of N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and the hydrogen sulfide (H₂S)-releasing derivative NAC-TS in modulating photosynthetic performance and yield-related traits under drought stress. It further evaluates how vegetable soybean cultivars with contrasting drought sensitivity and yield potential differentially respond to these compounds across key developmental stages. Therefore, this study evaluates the effects of NAC and NAC-TS on photosynthetic function, including chlorophyll content and chlorophyll fluorescence, as well as carotenoid content, proline, total soluble sugars, relative water content, and growth and yield traits, which serve as indicators of coordinated physiological and biochemical responses. The study also examines whether cumulative application across the developmental stages influences these effects. Together, these investigations aim to provide mechanistic insight into the role of cysteine derivatives in linking photoprotection, photosynthetic regulation, and plant water status to enhance drought tolerance and yield.

Material and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

The two vegetable soybean cultivars (AGS429 and UVE17, originally supplied by the Edamame Development Program, KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa) were germinated in seedling trays filled with Hygromix (Hygrotech (Pty) Ltd., Pretoria, South Africa), a primary seedling growth medium composed of peat moss (> 40%), vermiculite (> 20%), and polystyrene. Soybean leaf enlargement and net photosynthesis have been shown to be reduced when water availability falls below 50–60% of water-holding capacity, which can reduce yield by 39–45%, especially during reproductive stages such as R3-R6 [7]. AGS429 is a high-yielding cultivar with moderate yield loss under drought stress, while UVE17 is highly drought-sensitive with low yield [29, 50, 82]. AGS429 was selected to test whether the cysteine derivatives (NAC and NAC-TS) could enhance its drought tolerance, while UVE17 was included to assess whether these compounds could improve its drought resilience. The experiment was conducted in a greenhouse under natural day/night light conditions at the University of the Free State, Bloemfontein (29°6′31.94′′ S,26°11′18.95′′ E), with temperature maintained at 25/18 ± 2 °C (day/night). Relative humidity was not actively controlled and therefore varied under ambient greenhouse conditions.

Trial design and management

After germination, seedlings were transplanted at the vegetative one (V1) stage, when the unifoliate leaves were fully expanded above the cotyledons, and the first trifoliate leaves were emerging, into 10 kg of soil maintained at 100% water-holding capacity. At the third trifoliate (V3) stage, drought stress was imposed by withholding irrigation until soil water-holding capacity reached 30%, and this level was maintained throughout the reproductive stages until the experiment was terminated at maturity. Soil moisture was monitored daily using a Hydrosense II system (Campbell Scientific, Stellenbosch, South Africa) with a CS659 portable soil-moisture probe (12 cm), which measured volumetric water content (VWC) (100% WHC = 20.9% VWC). Additionally, to confirm soil moisture levels, pots were also weighed to ensure that the soil retained either 100% or 30% of its water-holding capacity.

To provide the crops with sufficient nutrients, the Wonder Plant Starter Fertiliser (Nutrico SA (Pty) Ltd., Johannesburg, South Africa) and Hygromix (Hygrotech Sustainable Solutions, Pretoria North, South Africa) were used at a 2:1 ratio. A total of 40 g of Wonder Plant Starter Fertiliser was dissolved overnight in 1 L of tap water, while 20 g of Hygromix was similarly dissolved in 1 L of tap water on the day of application. The resulting solutions were combined in a 20 L container containing 18 L of tap water. This nutrient solution was then applied to the pots at a volume of 130 mL per pot every two weeks. The experiment was conducted over two consecutive growing seasons: January–May 2024 (Year 1; Table S3) and February–June 2025 (Year 2; Table S4). Both seasons were analysed independently using the same factorial ANOVA structure to assess consistency in treatment responses across years. Well-watered data for both cultivars were recorded to establish baseline physiological characteristics (Table S5 & S6) but were not included in the factorial ANOVA because cysteine derivative treatments were applied only under drought conditions; therefore, the statistical model was restricted to drought-treated plants. Although the magnitude of statistical significance differed slightly between seasons for some parameters, particularly for cysteine derivative type, overall response patterns were largely consistent. For clarity and conciseness, Year 1 results are presented in the main text, while ANOVA outputs for both years are provided in the Supplementary Material.

N-acetylcysteine and N-acetylcysteine trisulfide application and data collection

A preliminary trial was conducted to identify the optimal treatment concentration. Based on preliminary results (Table S1 & S2), a concentration of 1.5 mg/mL was selected for the current study. To investigate the effect of application timing for the cysteine derivative (CD) solutions: N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and N-acetylcysteine trisulfide (NAC-TS), foliar applications were administered at three distinct growth stages of vegetable soybean: vegetative (V3 stage, V), flowering (R1 stage, F), and pod-filling (R3 stage, P). During the vegetative stage, CDs were applied once drought stress had commenced at five days after the full emergence of V3 leaves. The impact of application frequency was also evaluated, with treatments comprising a single spray at the vegetative stage (VFPx1), a second spray at flowering (VFPx2), and a third spray at pod-filling (VFPx3) (Table 1). Drought-stressed plants receiving only water served as the control group. To prevent contamination, each pot in the treatment group was sprayed separately until droplets formed on the leaves. Data collection was performed 10 days after each application of the cysteine derivatives. Notably, the CDs were applied 10 days after the initiation of drought stress when the pots had reached 30% WHC, and with all subsequent data collected 10 days post-application to maintain consistency. The experiment was arranged in a randomised complete block design with three replications, each replication consisting of two pots per plot.

Stomatal conductance

Leaf stomata are pivotal regulators of gas exchange, mediating the movement of CO₂ and water vapour between the plant and its environment. They dynamically modulate their aperture in response to external conditions and internal signals, thereby controlling CO₂ influx for photosynthesis and limiting water loss via transpiration [38]. Consequently, the degree of stomatal opening directly influences the rate of photosynthesis by regulating the availability of CO₂ within the leaf. Stomatal conductance (SC) was measured using an SC-1 leaf porometer (Li-Cor ADC BioScientific Ltd., Hoddesdon, UK) between 10:00 AM and 12:00 PM on the second fully expanded leaf. This time frame was selected to minimise diurnal variability, as environmental factors such as temperature, relative humidity, and radiant energy are relatively stable during this period. In operation, the porometer clamps onto the leaf surface, creating a sealed chamber that captures the humidity gradient between the leaf and the surrounding air. The device calculates gs based on this gradient, expressing the result in mmol.m⁻2.s⁻1. Stomatal conductance was measured on one fully expanded leaf from each of six plants per treatment (n = 6).

Chlorophyll fluorescence

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were evaluated using a pocket photosynthesis efficiency analyser (PEA) chlorophyll fluorometer (Hansatech Instrument Ltd., North America). Prior to measurement, the fully expanded second leaves were dark-adapted for 30 min using lightweight leaf clips with a closed shutter. The chlorophyll fluorometer was then attached to the leaf clip, followed by shutter opening and light radiation at 3500 μmol m−2.s−1. Chlorophyll fluorescence was measured on one fully expanded leaf from each of six plants per treatment (n = 6). The recorded parameters were downloaded using PEA Plus version 1.10 (Hansatech). The fluorescence parameters are used because they are among the most sensitive indicators of drought stress. The fluorescence parameters recorded included Fv/Fm (maximum quantum yield of PSII photochemistry), PIabs (performance index for energy conservation from photons absorbed by PSII until the reduction of intersystem electron acceptors), PItotal (performance index for energy conservation from photons absorbed by PSII until the reduction of PSI end acceptors), ETo/RC (electron transport flux per RC), DIo/RC (performance index for energy conservation from photons absorbed by PSII until the reduction of PSI end acceptors), ABS/RC (absorption flux (of PSII antenna Chl) per reaction centre, and TRo/RC (trapping flux per PSII RC) [15, 27, 47, 59].

Photochemical reflective index

The photochemical reflective Index (PRI) was evaluated using CI-710 s SpectraVue leaf spectrometer (CID Bio-Science, WA, USA) on the fully expanded second leaves, and it was measured on one fully expanded leaf from each of six plants per treatment (n = 6). Before the measurement session, the spectrometer was calibrated to ensure accuracy and repeatability. The leaf spectrometer non-destructively measures physiological responses to different environmental conditions. The readings were taken between 10:00 AM and 12:00 PM, a period characterised by stable light and temperature conditions. Over a broad wavelength range, including visible and near-infrared (NIR) light (360–1100 nm), the leaf spectrometer simultaneously analyses the transmission, absorption, and reflection of light by biological substances (CID Bio‑Science, WA, USA), [11, 51].

Chlorophyll and carotenoid contents

Drought stress downregulates the synthesis of essential pigments in plants. To quantify total chlorophyll (TChl), chlorophyll-a (Chl-a), chlorophyll-b (Chl-b), and carotenoids (CAR), a modified method based on Lichtenthaler [40] was used. Fully expanded second leaves were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve pigment integrity during transport to the laboratory. Frozen leaf powder (0.04 g) was homogenised in 2 mL of 80% (v/v) ice-cold acetone on ice. The homogenate was centrifuged for 10 min at 15 000 × g. The supernatant was used to measure the contents of CAR (480 nm), Chl-b (645 nm), and Chl-a (663 nm) against a blank containing 80% acetone (Cary 100 Bio, Varian, Australia). Individual pigment concentrations were calculated following the method described by Falcioni et al. [22], and the chlorophyll and CAR contents were expressed in milligrams per gram of fresh weight (mg.g⁻1 FW).

Relative water content

Leaf relative water content (RWC) is a critical metric for evaluating the effects of drought stress on foliage, as it reflects the balance between tissue water uptake and transpiration-driven water loss. This balance directly influences key physiological processes at the cellular level, including photosynthesis. In this study, RWC was determined following the method described by Gonzales and Gonzales-Vilar [25]. Specifically, samples were taken from the third fully expanded leaves between 10:00 AM and 12:00 PM, a period characterised by stable light and temperature conditions to minimise environmental influence on leaf water status [53]. Samples were immediately stored in sealed containers on ice to minimise moisture loss. From each leaf sample, ten discs (1.6 cm in diameter) were excised using a cork borer and weighed to obtain the fresh weight (FW). The discs were then submerged in 10 mL of distilled water in a test tube and allowed to hydrate for 24 h in a cold (4 °C), dark environment to facilitate full hydration while minimising water loss through stomatal closure and inhibiting photosynthetic activity. After hydration, the discs were gently blotted dry to remove surface water and weighed again to record the turgid weight (TW). Lastly, the discs were dried in an oven at 76 °C for 72 h to determine the dry weight (DW). The relative water content was calculated using the equation: %RWC = 100 × [(FW − DW)/(TW − DW)].

Proline content

Osmoprotectants such as proline (Pro) accumulate within intracellular components to protect cellular structures and help restore osmotic balance in plant cells, thereby preserving the integrity and functionality of the photosynthetic apparatus [26, 62]. The assay was conducted using a modified [8] method. Newly fully expanded leaves were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve metabolic integrity during transport to the laboratory. Leaf powder (0.15 g) was homogenised to a fine paste on ice using 2 mL of 70% ethanol (v/v). The homogenate was centrifuged at 3 000 × g for 10 min. The reaction mixture containing the supernatant, 20% (v/v) ethanol, and 1% (m/v) acidic ninhydrin (prepared in 60% (v/v) glacial acetic acid) was vigorously vortexed before incubation at 95 °C for 20 min. Samples were centrifuged at 10 000 × g for 10 min after cooling. A spectrophotometer (Cary 100 Bio, Varian, Australia) was used to measure the absorbance at 520 nm. Proline was calculated from the L-proline standard curve and represented as mg proline g−1 fresh weight.

Total soluble sugar content

Total soluble sugars (TSS) are important vital osmoprotectants that safeguard cellular structures and support the photosynthetic apparatus under drought [26]. A modified method of Irigoyen et al. [33] was used to determine the TSS. Young, fully expanded leaves were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve metabolic integrity during transport to the laboratory. Leaf powder (0.05 g) was homogenised in 1.25 mL of 96% (v/v) ethanol. Following a 10-min incubation period at 80 °C, the homogenate was centrifuged at 4 000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Anthrone reagent (1.5 mg/mL) was prepared in 72% (v/v) sulphuric acid. The mixture containing the supernatant and the anthrone reagent was vigorously vortexed and incubated at 80 °C for 15 min. The mixture was cooled to room temperature before absorbance was measured at 625 nm (Cary 100 Bio, Varian, Australia). A glucose standard was used to estimate TSS, which was expressed as mg glucose g−1 fresh weight.

Growth and yield parameters

The growth and yield parameters, such as plant height, number of branches, leaf area, shoot biomass, number of seeds, and seed mass were measured using various techniques. Plant height was recorded using a measuring tape, measured from the base to the tip of the tallest stem or leaf. Leaf area was measured using a portable leaf area meter (Bionics Scientific, BSLM101), which provides direct surface area readings. Shoot biomass was obtained by harvesting the above-ground parts of the plant, drying them to constant weight, and recording the mass using a digital scale. Seeds were manually counted at the R8 stage when they had matured into grain soybeans for future use in a seed bank. Lastly, the seed mass was determined using a digital scale.

These parameters are closely related to photosynthesis as they reflect the overall health and productivity of the plant. Plant height and the number of branches influence the overall canopy structure, which in turn affects light interception and thus photosynthetic efficiency. Leaf area directly impacts the surface area available for photosynthesis, with larger leaf areas typically associated with enhanced photosynthetic rates. Additionally, the plant’s biomass accumulation is closely tied to its photosynthetic capacity and overall growth. Moreover, reproductive traits such as seed number and seed mass provide insight into resource allocation under stress conditions. These indicators are particularly useful in evaluating the impact of cysteine derivatives on seed development and quality in vegetable soybean. As such, monitoring these morphological and physiological parameters offered a comprehensive understanding of the plant’s response to treatments and environmental stressors, ultimately contributing to the assessment of photosynthetic performance and overall growth [82].

Statistical analysis

The collected data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test in JASP version 0.19.1. Variables with a Shapiro–Wilk test p-value < 0.05 were considered non-normally distributed and were therefore subjected to data transformation to satisfy the assumptions of analysis of variance (ANOVA). A log₁₀ transformation was applied to the non-normal variables following the recommendations of Osborne [61] and [46]. At the vegetative stage, transformation was applied to RWC, DIo/RC, Chl-a, and TSS. During the flowering stage, SC, ETo/RC, Chl-b, TChl, and CAR were transformed. At the pod-filling stage, transformation was applied to SC, Chl-b, CAR, Pro, and TSS. These transformed datasets were subsequently used for ANOVA and post hoc mean separation analyses, whereas variables that satisfied the normality assumption (p ≥ 0.05) were analysed using the raw data. To assess the effects of cultivars, cysteine derivatives, and application timing on photosynthetic efficiency, the transformed data (where necessary) and the raw data were subjected to a factorial ANOVA in TIBCO Statistica version 13.5.0.17 to identify significant main effects and interactions among the factors. Following ANOVA, post hoc mean separation was performed using Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) test at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05 to determine statistically significant differences among treatment groups and ensure a precise interpretation of the results.

Discussion

Drought stress remains one of the most severe constraints on photosynthetic efficiency and crop productivity, and maintaining photosynthetic function under water limitation is critical for plant survival and agricultural sustainability [67, 95]. It negatively affects both the physiological and morphological traits of the vegetable soybean cultivars (Tables S5 and S6). In this study, foliar application of cysteine derivatives (CDs) differentially enhanced drought tolerance in vegetable soybean cultivars (UVE17 and AGS429). This improvement was primarily associated with the regulation of PSII photochemistry and maintenance of chlorophyll content, as well as improved osmotic adjustment and protection of the photosynthetic machinery. These responses varied with CD application, cultivar and developmental stage. Such stage-dependent effects are particularly important because soybeans are most sensitive to drought during flowering and pod filling, which are key yield-determining phases [13, 14, 88].

During drought stress, a key plant response is stomatal closure, which reduces transpirational water loss but simultaneously limits CO₂ uptake, thereby constraining photosynthetic efficiency and growth [66]. In the present study, cysteine derivatives, particularly NAC-TS, induced a non-significant upward trend in stomatal conductance, most notably in UVE17 during the vegetative and flowering stages. Although not statistically significant, this trend may indicate a potential improvement in CO₂ diffusion and photosynthetic performance under drought stress [81]. In vegetable soybean, maintaining gas exchange is critical due to its short growth cycle and high reproductive demand during pod development, where even brief limitations in carbon assimilation can impact yield formation. Consistent with this, drought-induced reductions in stomatal conductance have been widely reported in soybean and related crops [2, 4, 29, 30] reflecting a conserved stress response. At the physiological level, stomatal regulation by sulfur-containing compounds is highly context-dependent. Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) donors such as sodium hydrosulfide (NaHS) and GYY4137 have been shown to promote stomatal opening in Arabidopsis thaliana and Capsicum annuum by modulating ABA-induced nitric oxide signalling in guard cells, thereby influencing stomatal aperture regulation [41, 42]. However, contrasting effects have also been reported, where H₂S-related compounds induce stomatal closure under different experimental conditions, indicating strong dependence on species, developmental stage, and environmental context [24, 34, 69, 97].

Drought stress could further disrupt the balance between light absorption and carbon fixation, resulting in excess excitation energy and increased susceptibility of photosystem II (PSII) to photoinhibition. The maximum quantum efficiency of PSII, measured as Fv/Fm, is widely used to assess photochemical performance and the functional integrity of PSII [3, 39, 45, 76, 90]. In the vegetable soybean, Fv/Fm was found to be relatively insensitive to drought stress [29]. In the present study, NAC treatment significantly increased Fv/Fm in UVE17, while the remaining treatments showed only numerical improvements across both cultivars. Comparable results were reported by Zhao et al. [98], who observed increased Fv/Fm following NaHS application in foxtail millet, supporting the potential role of sulfur-containing compounds in improving PSII efficiency under stress conditions. The overall positive trends following cysteine derivative application may indicate partial stabilisation of PSII photochemistry and maintenance of photosynthetic activity under drought stress conditions [44]. Building on PSII efficiency, NAC treatment also enhanced several complementary indicators of photochemical performance under drought conditions. In UVE17, significant increases were observed in PIabs, ABS/RC, Chl-b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids, while AGS429 showed increases in TRo/RC, DIo/RC, Chl-a, Chl-b, and carotenoids. NAC-TS further enhanced Chl-a, Chl-b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids in UVE17, while in AGS429, it increased TRo/RC, DIo/RC, Chl-a, and Chl-b. Enhanced chlorophyll content in both cultivars supports improved light harvesting and maintenance of antenna efficiency, while carotenoids contribute to photoprotection by dissipating excess excitation energy and limiting oxidative stress [1, 52, 60, 67]. Together, these adjustments reflect improved coordination between energy capture and photoprotection, contributing to sustained PSII functionality under stress conditions. Consistently, PIabs increased in drought-stressed UVE17 when NAC was applied at the flowering and pod-filling stages, indicating improved energy conversion efficiency from absorbed photons and upregulation of PSII quantum efficiency during reproductive development under drought stress. Further insight is provided by energy flux parameters (ABS/RC, TRo/RC, DIo/RC and ETo/RC), which reflect the balance between energy absorption, trapping, energy dissipation and electron transport chain. Under drought stress, excess excitation energy increases non-photochemical dissipation and reduces photochemical efficiency due to PSII impairment [3, 67, 90]. In drought-stressed UVE17, NAC was the only cysteine derivative that increased ABS/RC, consistent with enhanced light absorption associated with higher Chl-b content. The slight reduction in DI₀/RC observed in UVE17 suggests lower energy dissipation per active reaction centre, indicating that a greater proportion of absorbed excitation energy was channelled towards photochemical processes. Together with the increases in Fv/Fm and PIabs, this response suggests improved PSII efficiency and maintenance of functional reaction centres under drought [31]. The maintenance of ETo/RC suggests sustained electron transport under drought stress in both cultivars and reflects numerical trends in DIo/RC, Fv/Fm, and PIabs. The numerical increase in PItotal further indicated improved coordination between PSII and PSI photochemistry and electron transport. In contrast, AGS429 exhibited increases in both TR₀/RC and DI₀/RC following NAC application. Although increased DI₀/RC indicates greater dissipation of excess excitation energy rather than enhanced photochemical efficiency per se, its concurrent increase with TR₀/RC and photosynthetic pigments suggests activation of photoprotective mechanisms to safely dissipate excess energy while maintaining PSII function under drought stress [8]. Therefore, the increased DI₀/RC in AGS429 is interpreted as an adaptive protective response rather than direct evidence of improved photosynthetic performance. Similarly, NAC-TS increased ABS/RC, TRo/RC, and DIo/RC, suggesting intensified energy absorption and redistribution to maintain PSII stability under drought stress. These coordinated responses indicate that cysteine derivatives improve excitation energy distribution and photochemical efficiency under drought conditions. Mechanistically, NAC enhances antioxidant capacity through glutathione biosynthesis, supporting protection of chloroplast structures, while NAC-TS may further strengthen redox homeostasis through H₂S- and sulfane sulfur–mediated signalling, contributing to improved PSII stability under stress [18, 21, 31, 32, 48, 68]. Photochemical adjustments were further supported by increases in the photochemical reflectance index (PRI), particularly in NAC-treated UVE17 during flowering and pod-filling stages. These changes indicate improved regulation of photochemical efficiency and non-photochemical quenching dynamics, reflecting enhanced flexibility in managing excess excitation energy under drought stress [54, 89, 100]. Together, these responses suggest a more efficient regulation of energy dissipation and improved photoprotective capacity under NAC or NAC-TS in either cultivar. Distinct cultivar responses suggest different acclimation strategies. UVE17 primarily maintains photosynthetic performance through improved pigment stability, enhanced photochemical efficiency, and stronger photoprotective regulation, while AGS429 exhibits stronger modulation of energy flux redistribution, particularly between trapping and dissipation processes, reflecting a more dynamic adjustment of PSII energy balance under drought stress.

The improvements in photosynthetic performance were supported by physiological adjustments that maintained cellular water status and metabolic balance. Plants treated with CDs showed a significant increase in leaf RWC. Notably, a triple NAC application substantially increased RWC in UVE17 during pod filling, indicating effective water retention in leaf tissues even under prolonged drought. Maintenance of leaf water status through increased RWC likely contributed to the preservation of chlorophyll content and PSII functionality observed in UVE17, thereby sustaining light capture and photochemical activity under drought stress. In addition, cysteine derivatives significantly increased proline and total soluble sugars, particularly during flowering and pod filling. These osmolytes are important for osmotic adjustment, maintenance of cellular turgor, membrane and protein stabilisation, and protection of chloroplast structures under water-deficit conditions [10, 26, 94]. Proline accumulation was more pronounced in AGS429 following NAC and NAC-TS treatment, accompanied by substantial increases in total soluble sugars. In contrast, UVE17 primarily showed increases in total soluble sugars following cysteine derivative application. These responses suggest that AGS429 relied more strongly on osmolyte-mediated drought adaptation, particularly through enhanced osmotic adjustment and maintenance of cellular hydration under stress conditions. In both cultivars, osmolyte accumulation likely complemented photoprotective mechanisms by stabilising chloroplast structures [87]and supporting continued photosynthetic carbon assimilation during drought stress.

Physiological improvements were reflected in growth and yield-related traits. Under drought, NAC increased branch number, leaf area, and shoot biomass in both cultivars, with stage-dependent responses. NAC-TS increased pod number in AGS429, while higher shoot biomass responses generally required multiple applications, particularly in this cultivar. Both cysteine derivatives enhanced leaf area, whereas shoot length responses were cultivar-specific: NAC increased shoot length only in UVE17, while NAC-TS increased it in both cultivars, with UVE17 requiring three applications. Although not significant, NAC induced numerical increases in seed number per plant in both cultivars, with differences in application frequency. In AGS429, NAC-TS produced a numerical increase in pod number. Although the response was not statistically significant, the observed trend may be biologically relevant, particularly given the cumulative contribution of small improvements in reproductive traits to overall crop performance under drought stress. These responses suggest improved assimilate production and partitioning. In AGS429, NAC-TS likely enhanced osmotic adjustment and photochemical efficiency, supporting biomass accumulation and reproductive sink development. In UVE17, maintained chlorophyll content, leaf hydration, and PSII function sustained photosynthetic activity and assimilate supply during reproduction, contributing to improved growth responses despite greater drought sensitivity [16, 43, 71, 79]. Increased vegetative growth further reflects improved chloroplast function, cell expansion, and resource uptake [16, 37, 43, 72, 78].

Conclusion

This study shows that cysteine derivatives (NAC and NAC-TS) enhance drought tolerance in vegetable soybean by coordinating the regulation of photosynthetic performance, pigment stability, energy flux, and osmotic adjustment, thereby improving growth and yield-related traits. In UVE17, improved performance was associated with increased chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids, together with higher Fv/Fm, PIabs, and ABS/RC, and elevated relative water content. These responses, supported by total soluble sugar accumulation (without proline increase), indicate enhanced photochemical efficiency, chloroplast protection, and osmotic balance sustaining vegetative growth under drought. In AGS429, treatments enhanced chlorophyll a and b, carotenoids, energy flux components (ABS/RC, TRo/RC, DIo/RC), relative water content, proline, and soluble sugars, reflecting stronger osmotic adjustment and energy reorganisation. NAC-TS further improved reproductive performance by increasing pod number under drought. Therefore, UVE17 relied mainly on improved photochemical efficiency and sugar-mediated osmotic adjustment, whereas AGS429 exhibited broader physiological plasticity, including osmolyte accumulation, energy flux regulation, and reproductive enhancement. These findings highlight the potential of cysteine derivatives as drought-mitigating agents with genotype-dependent effects. However, because the well-watered treatment served as a reference baseline rather than being included in the primary statistical analysis, the observed benefits should be interpreted as improved performance relative to untreated drought-stressed plants and not as evidence of complete restoration to well-watered conditions. Future studies should validate these responses under field conditions and incorporate formal statistical comparisons with well-watered controls to further establish the extent of drought tolerance conferred by these treatments.
