Total Hip Arthroplasty for Crowe III/IV Hip Dysplasia With Oblique Subtrochanteric Shortening Osteotomy and S‐ROM‐A Modular Stem: 10‐Year Outcomes
Authors: Tetsuo Hayama, Motoi Takahashi, Takuya Otani, Hideki Fujii, Yasuhiko Kawaguchi, Toshiomi Abe, Mitsuru Saito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHigh dislocation due to developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), classified as Crowe type III or IV, presents significant challenges in total hip arthroplasty (THA), particularly in preventing nerve complications while restoring leg length.AimThis study aimed to evaluate the clinical and radiographic outcomes of primary THA using an S‐ROM‐A modular stem with oblique subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy in patients with high hip dislocation; to identify the advantages, limitations, and possible countermeasures of this surgical approach.Patients and MethodsSubjects were 45 hips (37 patients) with high hip dislocation (Crowe III/IV) treated by primary THA using an S‐ROM‐A stem, with femoral shortening osteotomy performed at our institution. Outcomes 3 years after surgery were examined in 100% of the subjects. Postoperative complications and functional and radiographic outcomes were investigated.ResultsThe mean age at surgery was 65 years and the mean duration of postoperative follow‐up was 10.7 ± 3.4 years. There were no cases of postoperative infection, symptomatic pulmonary embolism, or neurological complications in the legs. Early postoperative dislocation occurred in two hips (4%, resolved conservatively) and osteotomy site nonunion in one hip (2%). Repeat surgery was required in only this case of nonunion (2%). The hip function score was significantly improved postoperatively. Leg lengthening after surgery was 0–56 mm (mean, 28 mm). Examination of radiographs revealed that it took 12 weeks for bone union at the osteotomy site in all hips except for the one hip with nonunion. No stem subsidence was found postoperatively, and “fixation by bone ingrowth” was achieved at the final follow‐up examination in all hips (100%), including the one that required repeat surgery.ConclusionTHA using an S‐ROM‐A modular stem with oblique subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy provided favorable mid‐term outcomes in patients with Crowe III/IV DDH. No neurological complications occurred, and functional and radiographic improvements were substantial. Our method, which emphasizes intraoperative sciatic nerve palpation for individualized leg lengthening, may serve as a practical and safe alternative to more complex monitoring systems. Further research incorporating objective intraoperative monitoring may help standardize this approach.Keywords: Crowe classification, high hip dislocation, modular stem, oblique subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy, sciatic nerve palsy, S‐ROM‐A stem, total hip arthroplasty

Introduction

Although total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a reliable treatment of developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) in adults, reconstruction is highly challenging in cases of high dislocation (i.e., Crowe III and IV [1]). To establish morphological and functional reconstruction, it is desirable to place the cup at the anatomical position or at the true acetabulum and to place the stem so that the proximal femur is in a functional position relative to the hip center. To achieve this, subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy is required to avoid excessive tension in soft tissues around the joint [2]. The questions of how to perform both osteotomy and THA successfully and how to prevent sciatic nerve palsy caused by excessive leg lengthening are keys to successful reconstruction.

Various femoral stems have been reported for cementless THA (e.g., monoblock stem, modular stem, and interlocking stem [3, 4, 5, 6]); and cemented THA [7] for this surgery. We have been using S‐ROM‐A modular stems because their characteristics are advantageous for the complicated surgical procedures that we perform.

Several subtrochanteric osteotomy techniques have been reported, including transverse, step‐cut, double‐chevron, and oblique methods (Figure 1) [8, 9, 10, 11], each with distinct mechanical characteristics and technical complexities. Among these, the oblique osteotomy offers advantages such as a broad bone contact area, ease of rotational adjustment, and facilitation of stable fixation [9].

Types of subtrochanteric osteotomy: (A) transverse, (B) oblique, (C) step‐cut, and (d) double‐chevron. Among these, we initially adopted the step‐cut technique but later transitioned to the oblique type due to its technical simplicity and superior rotational stability in Crowe III/IV hips. This figure serves to illustrate the rationale for our final choice of the oblique technique in this study.

Previous studies have attempted to define safe limits of leg lengthening—such as 20–40 mm or up to 10% of femoral length—but variability in patient anatomy and preoperative shortening complicates the application of fixed thresholds [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Moreover, intraoperative neuromonitoring using somatosensory evoked potentials, although effective, is not widely available due to its technical complexity and cost [15].

To address these challenges, our institution has adopted a strategy using intraoperative palpation of the sciatic nerve to adjust leg lengthening based on individual tension feedback. However, the efficacy and safety of this approach have not been fully validated in large cohorts.

Therefore, the objectives of this study were to investigate postoperative outcomes of primary THA for high hip dislocation involving reconstruction with an S‐ROM‐A modular stem, cup placement at the true acetabulum, subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy, and sciatic nerve palpation, and to determine the advantages of the technique and its limitations and possible countermeasures.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was a retrospective observational study evaluating clinical and radiographic outcomes of primary THA in patients with Crowe III or IV DDH. Ethical approval was obtained before study implementation (name: Study of intraoperative and postoperative outcomes of THA, approval number: 29‐043(8659)). We offered all eligible patients the opportunity to opt out of the study at any time.

Participants

Between March 2007 and December 2021, we performed THA on 2375 hips in our department. Among these cases, the subjects were 45 hips in 37 patients with highly dislocated DDH (Crowe III or IV) treated by primary THA with subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy, and more than 3 years had passed since the surgery.

Surgical Procedure

All surgeries were performed or supervised by the senior author. First, subtrochanteric femoral osteotomy was performed using a posterolateral approach with patients in the lateral decubitus position. The step‐cut technique was used in the first 7 hips, while the oblique technique (approximately 40° from lateral to medial direction) was used in the remaining 38 hips. The cup was placed at the true acetabulum: a cemented cup was used in 18 hips requiring a cup diameter of 36–40 mm (mean, 37 mm), while a cementless cup was used in 27 hips requiring a cup diameter of 42–48 mm (mean, 43 mm). The S‐ROM‐A stem (Depuy, Johnson & Johnson, Warsaw, IN) [16, 17], a modified S‐ROM modular stem with stem length shortened by 0–25 mm for Asian patients with a small physique, was used for reconstruction (Figure 2). A stem size was selected that offered a good fitting length of 20–40 mm during reaming of the distal diaphysis. The bone length to be removed, determined in preoperative planning, was taken into consideration when assessing the fitting length. After the proximal metaphysis was reamed to the size corresponding to the selected stem, a trial sleeve, trial stem, and head were inserted into the proximal bone segment only, and the trial head was reduced to the cup. Next, the distal bone segment was placed parallel to the reduced proximal bone segment, and the surgeon palpated the sciatic nerve while the leg was pulled caudally by the assistant (Figure 3). The leg was pulled until just before the point where the sciatic nerve tension became excessive (defined as “a rod‐like palpation, with no elasticity or mobility”), and the length of the overlapping portion of the distal bone segment with the proximal bone segment was measured in that condition. This intraoperatively measured bone shortening length (A) was compared with the bone shortening length (B) that was determined in preoperative planning based on the findings of leg length discrepancy (e.g., those observed on whole‐leg radiographs). When B > A, B was used to prioritize the correction of the leg length discrepancy. When A > B, A was used to prioritize prevention of nerve palsy, accepting an insufficient correction of the leg length discrepancy (Figure 4). After shortening osteotomy was performed, the proximal and distal bone segments were repositioned, the trial stem was inserted, and the hip was reduced again. If the tension of the surrounding tissue was too tight to reduce the hip, or if the sciatic nerve tension was excessive by palpation after reduction, additional removal of 5–10 mm of the distal bone segment, hip reduction, and sciatic nerve palpation were repeated. To determine the maximal leg lengthening, an acceptable maximal status for the findings of sciatic nerve palpation was defined as where the sciatic nerve tension became excessive when the knee was fully extended in a neutral hip position, but the tension decreased and elasticity and mobility were restored when the knee was flexed at 30°. Release of the soft tissue surrounding the hip was usually not required in cases with the dislocated femoral head within the gluteal muscle. However, if the femoral head articulated with the outer pelvic wall and a secondary acetabulum was formed, the joint capsule sometimes needed to be released, and less frequently, so did a part of the iliopsoas and gluteus minimus. In principle, prophylactic wiring was performed for either the proximal or distal bone segment, or both. In the final stem insertion, the proximal sleeve was first inserted into the proximal bone segment. The stem component was then inserted into the distal bone segment through the sleeve, and when the distal stem started to engage with the endosteal cortex of the distal bone segment at a correct anteversion angle, it became impossible to rotate the stem against the leg (distal segment). On the other hand, the proximal bone segment was freely rotatable until the sleeve was locked with the stem, the best bone contact was searched for by rotating the proximal segment, and fixation was completed at the point of best bone contact. If partial gaps between the segments formed after stem placement, they were filled with cancellous bone harvested from the femoral head. Furthermore, the diaphysis segment removed for bone shortening was halved, the cancellous bone was filled into the medullary cavity, and the resulting two pieces of bone grafts were transplanted and fastened, bridging the site of osteotomy. For postoperative treatment, if the sciatic nerve tension was high with the maximum leg lengthening (as described above), the knee was kept in the lightly flexed position for several days and gradually extended while carefully watching for the onset of leg numbness. Partial weight bearing was initiated 4 weeks after surgery, and patients were discharged from the hospital using crutches 6 weeks after surgery.

Outcomes and Definitions

The following perioperative complications were investigated: infections, dislocations, and symptomatic pulmonary embolism. The sciatic nerve was assessed based on the presence or absence of numbness and pain in the innervated area, sensory palsy, and motor palsy. The femoral nerve was assessed in a similar manner. The hip function and range of motion were examined before surgery and at the final follow‐up. Hip function was assessed using the Japanese Orthopedic Association scoring system (JOA score) [18]. Moreover, the length of the femoral shortening and the amount of leg lengthening were determined by comparing preoperative and postoperative radiographs. We also investigated time to bone union at the osteotomy site, subsidence of the stem (≥ 2 mm was considered significant), radiographical fixation of the stem according to Engh's criteria [19], and the presence of spot welds, defined as concentration of bone trabeculae or increases in the mineral density between the sleeve and the cortical bone.

Statistical Analysis

A two‐tailed paired t‐test was used for statistical comparisons, and a p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise noted. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP version 15.1.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Patient Characteristics and Follow‐Up

Among the subjects comprising 45 hips in 37 patients, 33 patients were women (40 hips) and 4 were men (5 hips). Mean age at surgery was 65 years (range, 37–78 years), and the mean postoperative follow‐up period was 10.7 ± 3.4 years (range, 3–17 years). Based on preoperative plain radiographs of both hips (anterior–posterior view), 8 hips were classified as Crowe III and 37 as Crowe IV. Direct examination and plain radiography were performed in all patients ≥ 1 year after surgery; for patients whose follow‐up was interrupted, we contacted them by phone for a re‐examination, and the final follow‐up rate was 100.

Perioperative Complications

Perioperative complications are shown in Table 1. There were no cases of infection or symptomatic pulmonary embolism. Also, there were no cases of neurological complications in the legs, such as numbness or sensory/muscle weakness in the areas innervated by the sciatic nerve and femoral nerve. Postoperative dislocation occurred in two hips (4%), both in the early postoperative period (approximately 1 week after surgery). Conservative treatment was successful, and there was no recurrence in either case.

Hip Function and Range of Motion

Hip functions were examined and statistically compared between before and after surgery in all patients, including the one who underwent repeat surgery. Mean values of hip range of motion (preoperative/final follow‐up) were 66°/92° for flexion, 15°/29° for abduction, 27°/37° for external rotation, and 20°/25° for internal rotation, indicating significant improvements in flexion and abduction. The mean total JOA score (pain/range of motion/ambulation/activities of daily living scores) was 44 (17/10/8/9) before surgery, which was significantly improved to 85 (37/17/15/16) at the final follow‐up (Table 2).

Radiographic Outcomes

Examination of radiographs revealed that it took 6–18 weeks (mean, 12 weeks) for bone union at the osteotomy site in all hips except for the one hip with nonunion. No stem subsidence was found postoperatively, and “fixation by bone ingrowth” was achieved at the final follow‐up examination in all hips (100%), including the one that required repeat surgery. “Unstable implant” and “stable fibrous fixation” were not observed in any hips. Spot welds were observed below the lower edge of the sleeve in all cases.

Femoral Shortening and Leg Lengthening

The length of femoral shortening was 12–65 mm (mean, 37 mm), and the amount of leg lengthening after surgery was 0–56 mm (mean, 28 mm). The amount of leg lengthening was 0–35 mm (mean, 20 mm) in 13 hips with preoperative formation of a secondary acetabulum but 13–56 mm (mean 32 mm) in 32 hips with the dislocated femoral head within the gluteal muscle, and this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.0012; Table 3).

Discussion

This retrospective study achieved a 100% follow‐up rate through direct clinical and radiographic evaluation at 1 year after surgery. The observed rates of early postoperative dislocation (4%) and osteotomy site nonunion (2%) were comparable or better than previously reported medium‐term outcomes [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. The mean amount of leg lengthening was 28 mm (range, 0–56 mm), yet no neurological complications were observed. These results underscore the importance of intraoperative leg length management and stable osteosynthesis, which together contributed to favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes.

Advantages of Oblique Osteotomy With S‐ROM‐A Modular Stem

One key to success in this reconstructive surgery is to achieve both stability between the proximal and distal bone segments and stability between the stem and the femur, leading to the establishment of both union of the osteotomy site and biological fixation of the implant. Another very important key is to perform appropriate leg lengthening while avoiding sciatic nerve palsy.

The step‐cut technique was performed in the first seven hips. Despite this technique's theoretical advantages, removal of the bone segment for shortening made the proximal and distal bone contact areas different in size and shape, so it was difficult to achieve good bone contact and mechanical stability. Furthermore, as described above, a small portion of the distal bone segment needed to be repeatedly removed to achieve appropriate soft tissue tension. Thus, step‐cut osteotomy that produces complex bone shapes was not thought to be a realistic option. On the other hand, oblique osteotomy [8] is simple and enables easy adjustment of the bone shortening length. Other advantages, such as a large contact area for osteosynthesis and rotational stability between bone segments, were considered beneficial for achieving bone union.

Various studies have used a proximal or distal fixation stem [3, 4, 5, 6]. The S‐ROM stem, used in this study, achieves both proximal and distal mechanical stability immediately after surgery, which is advantageous for establishing both bone union and stem fixation. The S‐ROM modular stem provides independent rotational adjustment of the proximal segment, which is especially advantageous in patients with femoral deformity [16, 17]. The combination of oblique osteotomy and use of this stem offers further advantages. The bone contact area created is large in oblique osteotomy, and the good contact largely depends on the rotational alignment between bone segments. This modular stem enables fixation of bone segments at the corresponding optimal rotation angle. At the same time, caution must be exercised for this combination of oblique osteotomy and this stem. The stem is inserted with axial impaction, and this causes shear stress in the oblique osteotomy plane, which can pose a risk of fracture. The S‐ROM stem system uses tight press fit fixation for both the proximal sleeve and the distal stem, and itself thus poses a risk of fracture at the proximal and distal regions upon insertion of the stem. The combination of oblique osteotomy could add another risk factor for fracture (i.e., shear stress). Thus, it is important to perform the following countermeasures: proactive prophylactic wiring at either the proximal or distal bone segment, or both; examination of the bone for fractures after insertion of the stem; and careful bone grafting at the osteosynthesis site.

Neurological Safety and the Role of Sciatic Nerve Palpation

There are numerous reports on leg lengthening and nerve palsy and on its prevention. Edwards et al. reported that the mean amount of leg lengthening was 2.7 cm (range, 1.9–3.7 cm) in patients who developed peroneal nerve palsy and 4.4 cm (range, 4.0–5.1 cm) in those who developed sciatic nerve palsy [12]. Sener et al. reported that leg lengthening by ≥ 4 cm increased the risk of nerve palsy [11], and Nercessian et al. reported that the safe limit of leg lengthening was 10% of the femoral length [14]. Also, intraoperative nerve monitoring using somatosensory evoked potentials showed that the mean amount of leg lengthening was 18 mm (range, 6–43 mm) in patients who did not develop postoperative sciatic nerve palsy [15]. Thus, the amount of leg lengthening that causes palsy varies among studies and individuals, so there is no absolute safe range of leg lengthening that prevents nerve palsy. Nerve monitoring using somatosensory evoked potentials requires devices and is complicated. This study used a simple and easy monitoring means, that is, palpation of the sciatic nerve. We regarded avoiding the excessive tension of the sciatic nerve as the absolute requirement during surgery, irrespective of the patient's height, femoral length, and the absolute amount of leg lengthening. To our knowledge, no previous studies used palpation of the sciatic nerve as a specific means to prevent nerve palsy or to determine the amount of leg lengthening. Assessment by palpation is not objective, and this is discussed as a limitation below. We did not find nerve palsy in all 45 hips with a mean leg lengthening of 28 mm (range, 0–56 mm). Leg length discrepancies were not completely resolved in some cases, but patients' satisfaction level was high because they subjectively perceived a profound effect of leg lengthening, with substantially better support provided by the hip joint and substantial leg lengthening. In our cohort, the amount of leg lengthening after surgery ranged from 0 to 56 mm (mean, 28 mm). While previous studies have reported an increased risk of sciatic nerve palsy with leg lengthening greater than 40 mm, no neurological complications were observed in our series—even in the five patients who underwent more than 45 mm of lengthening. This may reflect the benefit of our intraoperative tension‐based adjustment strategy using sciatic nerve palpation, rather than applying a fixed numerical threshold for all cases. Moreover, although complete leg length equalization was not achieved in all cases, the postoperative discrepancy was markedly reduced compared to the preoperative state (data not shown). In cases where a mild discrepancy remained, patients reported improved gait balance, reduced limping, and overall satisfaction due to the restoration of functional support from the hip joint. We also emphasize that leg lengthening was not pursued uniformly but rather tailored to each patient's intraoperative nerve tension findings. In some cases, the amount of bone shortening planned preoperatively (B) was adjusted intraoperatively (to A) to prioritize nerve safety over perfect correction of leg length discrepancy. This individualized approach helped to optimize both neurologic safety and functional outcomes.

Study Limitations and Future Directions

There are several limitations in this study. First, it was a single‐center study with a limited number of cases. Second, prevention of sciatic nerve palsy was done by subjective monitoring (i.e., palpation), not by objective means. Conclusion

This study investigated medium‐term outcomes (mean, 10 years and 8 months) of primary THA for 45 DDH using an S‐ROM‐A stem combined with oblique osteotomy (step‐cut osteotomy for the first 7 hips). Dislocation occurred in two cases, and osteotomy site nonunion occurred in one case. There were no neurological complications, and functional outcomes at the final follow‐up were favorable in all cases. Examination of radiographs revealed that it took 12 weeks for bone union at the osteotomy site in all hips except for the 1 hip with nonunion. No stem subsidence was found postoperatively, and “fixation by bone ingrowth” was achieved at the final follow‐up examination in all hips (100%), including the one that required repeat surgery.

The following were considered important: performing surgery with a good understanding of the characteristics of the stem and the osteotomy technique to be used, proactively applying prophylactic wiring to avoid fractures, and carefully transplanting bone grafts at the osteotomy site. Reconstruction with S‐ROM modular stem combined with subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy in primary THA for high hip dislocation is a useful surgical approach. Our method, which emphasizes intraoperative sciatic nerve palpation for individualized leg lengthening, may serve as a practical and safe alternative to more complex monitoring systems. Further research incorporating objective intraoperative monitoring may help standardize this approach.
