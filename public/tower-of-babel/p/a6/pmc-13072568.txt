Knowledge, attitudes, and patterns of nonmedical beta-blockers use among medical students: a comparative study between Lebanon and Armenia
Authors: Rawad Affan, Georges Jarrouge, Daniel Panossian, Shafika Assaad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe use of beta-blockers for off-label indications, such as the treatment of performance and examination anxiety-related issues, has increasingly surfaced in the literature as a phenomenon in medical students. Although the use of these medications may help relieve physical symptoms of anxiety associated with it, public health concerns have arisen considering the unsupervised medication use. This study aims to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and patterns of beta-blockers use among medical students in Lebanon and Armenia.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was carried out, using a standardized questionnaire, administered on a self-reporting basis, among medical students in both Lebanon and Armenia during the academic year 2025–2026. A set of sociodemographic variables, the use of beta-blockers in their lifetime and currently, use and pattern of use, sources of prescriptions, use of doses, efficacy, side effects, and knowledge and attitudes about beta-blockers were explored in the survey using descriptive and inferential statistical methods.ResultsA total of 600 participants were recruited (Lebanon: n = 292, and Armenia: n = 308). Beta-blockers use was reported by 23 Lebanese students (7.9%) and 26 Armenian students (8.4%). Use was significantly more common among males in both Lebanon (82.6% of users, p = 0.007) and Armenia (69.2% of users, p = 0.002). In Armenia, use was additionally associated with advanced academic year (p < 0.001). Self-prescription predominated in both countries (82.6% in Lebanon; 65.4% in Armenia). Most users reported perceived effectiveness in reducing anxiety (87.0% and 84.6%, respectively). Self-reported adverse effects were noted by 13 Lebanese users (56.5%) and 14 Armenian users (53.8%), most commonly hypotension. No statistically significant differences were observed between countries in patterns of use, dosing behavior, perceived effectiveness, or reported adverse effects.ConclusionsThe prevalence of nonmedical use of beta-blockers among Lebanese and Armenian medical students is relatively uncommon but includes self-prescription, unsupervised dose adjustments, and notable adverse effects. Similar misuse patterns across different regulatory systems suggest that academic stress and performance pressure play key roles, highlighting the need for targeted educational and supportive interventions to promote responsible medication use and healthier coping strategies.Keywords: Beta-blockers, Propranolol, Medical students, Self-medication, Anxiety, Public health, Lebanon, Armenia

Introduction

Beta-blockers have gained a significant attention for its off-label application in anxiety management, especially for performance or situational anxiety, including public speaking, stage performance, and examinations [1]. This medication alleviates the somatic manifestations of anxiety, including tremors, tachycardia, and perspiration, without directly influencing emotional or cognitive aspects [2].

The nonmedical use of substances for cognitive enhancement among students has also been recorded in Europe. A comprehensive study conducted across Dutch universities revealed that 1.7% of students reported utilizing beta-blockers like Propranolol for cognitive enhancement without a prescription. These findings underscore a broader trend of pharmacological self-regulation among students facing academic pressure [3].

A study exhibits the fact that students under academic pressures tend to use beta-blockers like Propranolol to reduce the symptoms of anxiety while presenting orally [4]. While another study carried out among university and medical students from the Middle East display the rising use of self-medication and non-medical use of beta-blockers to reduce stress and improve performance [5]. A study conducted among students from King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences showed that 14.5% medical students tend to use beta-blockers, mostly before medical exams or oral presentation [6]. Similarly, according to studies among the University of Jordan medical and dental students, 9.9% tended to use beta-blockers,58.6% obtained the drug without seeing a medical practitioner [5]. Furthermore, 12% of the Palestinian medical students who participated in a recent study used beta-blockers to reduce stress and anxiety-mostly before clinical exams. At the same time, most of the students self-prescribed it, though aware of the drug's potential side effects, they still chose to self-medicate [7].

In a study conducted among Saudi Arabian medical students, 6.5% of them used over-the-counter beta-blockers to alleviate their anxiety and improve their performance. Female students were found to have a higher tendency to use this drug than male students. These regional findings collectively suggest that beta-blockers use among medical students is influenced by academic stressors, accessibility, and perceptions of safety and efficacy [8].

In the case of Lebanon, the Ministry of Public Health decreed that the dispensing of medications by pharmacists without the prescription of a physician is legal only if the medications belong to the National Over the Counter Medicines List, which does not include serious ones such as cardiovascular medications like beta Blockers [9]. Yet according to the World Bank report, the fact that many prescription medications are sold over the counter in the country indicates that the prescription requirement is not being rigorously upheld [10].

The prevalence of self-medication in the overall Lebanese population was also seen to be highly prevalent at 79.1%, where there has been an emerging trend in the use of psychotropic and anxiolytic medications among the young demographic [11].

In contrast, the republic of Armenia maintains a more strictly enforced regulatory framework. The Law on Medicines classifies drugs as either prescription or over‑the‑counter, and the dispensing of prescription medicines without a valid prescription is prohibited [12]. Propranolol, a beta‑blocker, falls under the category of prescription-only medicines and must therefore be dispensed exclusively with a physician’s prescription in pharmacies [13].

Despite this regulatory structure, self-medication is becoming a bigger public health issue in Armenia. Inappropriate self-medication was found in a cross-sectional study in 383 adults, especially among those with postgraduate degrees (68%). It highlights the need for better patient education and pharmacist supervision by warning that such practices may result in misdiagnosis, drug interactions, and other health risks [14].

Mental health challenges among Armenian medical students have also been increasingly recognized. A recent report in Armenia has found that significant mental health issues exist for fourth-year medical students. The 2025 report found that close to one-third of medical students in Armenia meet the criteria for depressive illness, and this is higher in students in their earliest years and those who face stressors [15].

Despite this context, data specifically addressing beta-blockers use, knowledge, attitudes, and self-medication practices among Lebanese medical students remain limited. Likewise, evidence from the republic of Armenia focusing on beta-blockers use within medical student populations is limited too. Evaluating these aspects by a comparative public health approach examining these behaviors among Lebanon and Armenia’s medical university students, with a particular emphasis on its use for treating exam-related anxiety and depression, this study seeks to close this knowledge gap and aid in the creation of focused educational interventions, legislative responses, and mental health support services in higher education.

The study aims to determine the prevalence of beta-blockers use, evaluate students’ knowledge regarding its indications, contraindications, and potential adverse effects, and explore attitudes toward its safety, effectiveness, and acceptability for managing anxiety and performance-related situations. A comparative analysis will be conducted to identify differences between Lebanese and Armenian medical students, as well as to evaluate variations according to gender within and across both countries.

Materials and methods

Participants and study design

This study employed a cross-sectional design using an online, self-administered questionnaire aiming to determine the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of medical students in Lebanon and Armenia in using beta-blockers.

The target population included undergraduate and graduate medical students, and medical residents enrolled in medical universities in Lebanon and Armenia during the academic year 2025–2026. Medical residents were included as part of the medical training continuum, as they remain engaged in structured postgraduate clinical education and are exposed to performance-based academic and clinical evaluations. An English language questionnaire was distributed using a convenience sampling approach, including all eligible respondents during the data collection period.

A total of 600 participants were included in the study that comprised 292 students from Lebanon and 308 students from Armenia. The sampling technique used was recruitment via university groups, academic groups in whatsApp, and internet platforms in both Lebanon and Armenia so that the pool of the targeted population was broadened. All participants were informed about the study’s objectives, voluntary participation, anonymity, and confidentiality. Electronic informed consent were obtained prior to accessing the questionnaire.

Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted within the context of the study design and may not be fully generalizable to the entire population of medical students in Lebanon and Armenia.

Ethical considerations

This study adheres to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, including respect for participants, beneficence, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any time without consequence [16]. Participation is voluntary and anonymous, and no identifying information is collected. Ethical approval was obtained from the corresponding institutional review board prior to data collection. In Lebanon, this research received an approval from the Research Ethics Committee (REC) and the Human Research Protection Program (HRPP) at the Holy Spirit University of Kaslik (Approval Number: HRPP/202610/FT/0105). In Armenia, ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Yerevan State Medical University after Mkhitar Heratsi (Approval Number: N 3–10/25).

Survey structure

The questionnaire used in this study is designed with two major sections to comprehensively assess sociodemographic characteristics, history of beta-blockers use, usage patterns, and participants' knowledge and attitudes with respect to beta-blockers. The first section collects necessary baseline information: age, gender, academic year, country of study, enabling proper characterization of the sample and thus comparative analyses between countries and across genders.

The second section deals with the participants' history and usage patterns of the beta-blockers. It comprises questions designed to establish the usage of the beta-blockers, the occasion underlying the initiation of beta-blockers usage, and the source of recommendation, peer, health, and self-use. It also looks into the motivations behind the usage of the beta-blockers like academic stress, performance anxiety, peer, and psychological disorders. It also looks into the usage patterns of the beta-blockers through the investigation of the occasions in which the students usually consume the beta-blockers prior to the examination, presentations, interviews, and stressful occasions in relation to the consideration of the specific usage of beta-blockers among the students.

Besides usage patterns, this section has incorporated information about typical doses used by respondents and their familiarity with safe doses to be consumed. Even their perceived efficacy has been measured by asking respondents whether beta-blockers increase their performance, decrease physiological symptoms of anxiety, or help improve their concentration.

These questions on the side effects or complications are set to help identify if the respondents underwent any negative conditions like dizziness, hypotension, fatigue, or palpitations resulting from the intake of beta-blockers. Finally, the section measures the participants’ knowledge and attitudes on beta-blockers using questions related to their understanding of the pharmacological effect, if they are aware of its complications, and chance of prescribing the drug for others.

The questionnaire used in this study was adopted in its original form from a previously published and validated instrument developed by Al-Mohrej et al. (2018). It underwent content and face validity assessment by subject matter experts to ensure clarity, relevance, and appropriateness for assessing beta-blockers use, knowledge, and attitudes among medical and dental students (Al-Mohrej et al., 2018).

Data collection and storage

The survey was web-based, and data were collected via a secure digital platform. All participants provided electronic informed consent prior to their enrollment in the study, and participation was completely voluntary. No identifiable information was collected, and all responses remain confidential, accessible only to the research team. All data will be stored in password-protected files. The datasets generated and analyzed during the study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Data analysis

Data analysis are done by using SPSS, Version 27. Sociodemographic variables and the pattern of beta-blockers use are summarized using descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations). Associations between patterns of beta-blockers use, reasons for such use, knowledge levels, and demographic characteristics are tested through the use of inferential analyses, such as chi-square tests and odds ratio. Comparative analyses were carried out to delineate differences between Lebanese and Armenian medical students and those between male and female participants. The level of statistical significance are set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

Our results showed that the prevalence of beta-blockers use was proportionately low in both regions. In Lebanon, which is a mediterranean country, 7.9% of its medical students use beta-blockers. Whereas in Armenia, which is a caucasian country, 8.4% of its medical students use beta-blockers, with similarities in consumption pattern for both groups. The similarity in misuse patterns despite differing regulatory frameworks may reflect informal access to prescription medications, cultural normalization of self-medication within medical training environments. These findings are lower than the use of beta-blockers among Palestinian medical students in Al Najah National University reaching 12% [7], and about the same as the Jordanian medical and dental students of the University of Jordan 9.9% [5]. In contrast, Saudi Arabia reported higher prevalence of 30% of self-prescribing propranolol among medical and dental students in Riyadh (Al Mohrej et al., 2016). The beta-blockers of medical students of the King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences showed that the propranolol use has reached 14.4% [6]. Similar to our results, beta-blockers among health sciences students of the Umm al-Qura University of Makkah were 6.5% rate of the usage of propranolol [8].

However, when set in an international perspective, the prevalence found in Lebanese and Armenian medical students (7.9% and 8.4%, respectively) seems remarkably high when compared with a number of non-regional research literature addressing beta-blockers used for non-medical or performance-related reasons. In Switzerland, beta-blockers use was found in only 1.2% of university students in a study concerning neuroenhancement practices [17], and in the Netherlands, beta-blockers used for enhanced academic performance in university students represented a prevalence of approximately 0.9% of the whole university population [3]. Also, an Iranian research, with a prevalence of 4.7% of propranolol use among university students, still remains below the prevalence found among Lebanese and Armenian medical students [18].

Furthermore, users consuming beta-blockers in both Lebanese and Armenian samples were predominately male, and usage was concentrated in more advanced academic years, specifically in the clinical stage and residency. Male predominance of beta-blockers use parallels findings in Riyadh, in which 78% of users are male (Al Mohrej et al., 2016), while in Jordan, females predominates the beta-blockers use which was found to be 65.5% [5].

Among medical students in Lebanon, the majority of propranolol users reported limited experimentation, with around 60.9% having used beta-blockers five times or less, while 30.4% reported using them more than ten times. Similar pattern was observed among Armenian users, where most participants had used propranolol five times or less (61.5%), yet 30.8% reported use exceeding ten times. Regarding the contexts of use in stressful situations, Lebanese and Armenian students most commonly reported propranolol intake during final examinations (39.1% and 45.8%, respectively). The observed patterns may be explained by the competitive academic environment of medical training, where performance pressure, fear of failure, and high-stakes evaluations encourage pharmacological coping strategies. Similarly, the Lebanese and Armenian results more closely align with the Jordanian study, where examinations and the management of associated anxiety were the most frequent contexts for beta-blockers use [5]. However, this pattern differs from findings in the Palestinian sample, where propranolol use was predominantly associated with oral exams/OSCEs (89%) and presentations (39%) [7], and from the Riyadh sample, where roughly 90% of users reported propranolol intake in both oral exams/OSCEs and presentations (Al Mohrej et al., 2016).

Self-prescription was found to be a common practice among propranolol users in both Lebanese and Armenian samples. In Lebanon, the vast majority of users (82.6%) reported prescribing the drug to themselves. Similarly, in Armenia, 65.4% indicated self-prescription. This pattern aligns with previous regional findings, including the Saudi Arabian study where 86% of users took propranolol without prior medical consultation (Al Mohrej et al., 2016), and the Jordanian study where 58.6% of users self-prescribed the drug [5]. Comparable results were also reported in the Palestinian study, in which 60% of propranolol users obtained the medication through self-prescription [7].

Regarding dosing patterns, most Lebanese and Armenian propranolol users reported taking relatively low doses. In Lebanon, the most commonly reported dose was below 20 mg (39.1%), followed by 20 mg (30.4%), while a notable proportion indicated that the dose depended on the activity (21.7%). A similar pattern was observed in Armenia, where < 20 mg (42.3%) and 20 mg (30.8%) were the most frequent doses. These findings are comparable to the Saudi Arabian study, where 20 mg (31%) and activity-dependent dosing (29%) were also the most commonly reported patterns (Al Mohrej et al., 2016), and to the Palestinian sample, where 10–20 mg represented 56% of the self-prescribed beta-blockers among students [7]. In contrast, Jordanian students (72.4%) most frequently reported using 10 mg [5]. Episodes of dose escalation were also reported in both Lebanese (34.8%) and Armenian (30.8%) sample where users indicated that they had increased their dose at least once. In Lebanon, this increase in dosage was found to be caused most often during oral exams/OSCEs and presentations, whereas Armenian users most commonly increased doses before OSCEs or during final exams. This pattern reflects broader regional concerns regarding unsupervised dose adjustments, consistent with the 50% dose increase rate observed in Riyadh [19], while the Palestinian study reports less frequent dose escalation, which was around 16% of Palestinian medical students’ users [7].

Strengths

The strength of the study is in its uniqueness in being the first to conduct a comparison of Inappropriate beta-blockers use in both Lebanon and Armenia among medical students using a standardized questionnaire, enabling a cross-section analysis of prevalence and use patterns to be made between the two countries. The overall sample (N = 600) covers medical students of various years and levels of study in both countries, which supports the credibility and broader applicability of the findings.

Limitations

This study was a convenient cross‑sectional survey of Lebanese and Armenian medical students, so causal relationships cannot be inferred and findings may still be influenced by selection bias despite the national coverage. Being based on anonymous self-reported information may result in issues with recall bias and social desirability bias with matters of sensitive behavior and may so result in underreporting and/or overreporting of beta-blockers utilization, with online administration of the questionnaire may have introduced response bias and excluded students with limited digital engagement.

Future studies may need to incorporate the utilization of standardized elements of psychological stress or anxiety to directly correlate beta-blockers use with objectively measured levels of distress, and so may need to follow a longitudinal type of research effort to identify the precise nature of the link between academic-related stress and beta-blockers nonmedical use through mental health aspects.

Conclusion

This comparative cross-sectional study illustrates that the nonmedical use of beta-blockers among medical students in both Lebanon and Armenia, though relatively low in prevalence, represents a consistent and relevant public health concern. About one in twelve students in both countries reported use of beta-blockers, taken mostly for exam-related and performance anxiety management, not according to indications approved by medical authorities. Despite differences in national pharmaceutical regulations, the pattern of use, dosing behavior, perceived effectiveness, and adverse effects were remarkably similar across Lebanese and Armenian students.

For public health implications, it appears that there is a need for comprehensive intervention among medical schools. There is an integral need to design an educational program for rational pharmacotherapy, promote ethical prescribing practice, and enhance non-pharmacological services for stress management. Alongside, enforcement measures need to be ensured concerning prescriptions. There is also a need to monitor pharmacists, particularly in countries where over-the-counter prescription drugs are available.

In conclusion, addressing this issue requires coordinated efforts at the institutional, regulatory, and policy levels to promote safer coping strategies, protect student well-being, and ensure responsible medication use within future healthcare professionals.
