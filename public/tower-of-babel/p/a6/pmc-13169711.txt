The mediating role of academic engagement and the moderating role of academic self-efficacy in the relationship between critical thinking and academic competence: evidence from Chinese college students
Authors: Weilin Su, Chen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBased on social cognitive theory, this study explores the key factors influencing academic competence among Chinese college students from the perspective of critical thinking. A moderated mediation model is hypothesized and tested to examine the roles of academic engagement (as a mediator) and academic self-efficacy (as a moderator) in such relationships. Data were collected through a three-wave longitudinal design involving 363 Chinese college students. The empirical test results first show that Chinese college students’ critical thinking has a significant positive impact on their academic engagement and competence. Secondly, their academic engagement not only has a positive impact on their academic competence, but also plays a mediating role in the process in which critical thinking affects academic competence. Thirdly, academic self-efficacy can positively moderate the relationship between critical thinking and academic engagement, thereby affecting their academic competence. More specifically, Chinese college students with higher academic self-efficacy have a stronger correlation between critical thinking and academic engagement, and ultimately achieve better academic results than students with lower academic self-efficacy. Therefore, these findings not only contribute to our theoretical understanding of cognitive processes, academic engagement, and competence development in higher education, but also provide practical suggestions for improving academic competence from the perspectives of educational administrations, faculty, and college students themselves.Keywords: Critical thinking, Academic self-efficacy, Academic engagement, Academic competence, Moderated mediation model

Introduction

In today’s world, a new round of technological revolution and industrial transformation is taking place, and talent competition has become the core of the country’s sustainable development capabilities and comprehensive national strength competition [1]. How to cultivate top talents who can lead the development of core competitiveness in the future has received widespread attention from countries around the world and has become a major strategic task for China to build an educational power [2]. Under this development background, how to improve the quality of higher education talent training is an important issue that needs to be solved urgently [3]. Academic competence, defined as a set of positive qualities possessed by college students (e.g., cognitive adaptability, problem-solving skills), is a critical predictor of academic achievement and can be cultivated through targeted educational interventions [4, 5]. Therefore, studying the academic competence of college students will not only help universities improve the quality of talent training, contribute to society, and enhance China’s sustainable development capabilities and international competitiveness.

At present, most of the previous research on the affecting factors of academic competence of college students focuses on two aspects: internal academic factors such as learning burnout and learning strategies, and external environmental factors such as parenting styles, teacher teaching styles, and interpersonal relationships [6, 7]. Among them, critical thinking, as a kind of reasonable and introspective thinking, emphasizes a cautious attitude towards opinions, hypotheses, arguments, etc. [8]. Many studies in higher education consistently demonstrate its significant positive correlations with learning engagement, academic self-efficacy, and performance [8, 9]. Specifically, college students with high level of critical thinking are more likely to acquire high critical cognitive skills and are more likely to practice critical spirit in their daily learning process, and their academic performance and academic competence will also become better. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that college students’ critical thinking will have a positive impact on their academic competence.

At the same time, from the perspective of social cognitive theory [10, 11], academic competence is shaped through the dynamic interplay of three interconnected factors: personal factors, behavioral factors, and environmental factors. This framework posits that critical thinking, as a core cognitive resource, not only directly enhances academic engagement by enabling students to analyze problems deeply and engage with learning content more meaningfully, but also interacts with environmental factors—such as academic self-efficacy—to further shape academic competence [12]. Specifically, prior research has consistently highlighted academic engagement as a key variable in understanding students’ learning processes and improving academic outcomes [13, 14]. It refers to the time and effort students invest in educational activities, which serves as a proximal mediator linking critical thinking to academic competence [15]. For instance, students with high critical thinking skills are more likely to dissect complex concepts, identify underlying connections, and engage in reflective learning practices, thereby fostering deeper academic involvement and skill development.

However, the translation of critical thinking into actual engagement and competence is not automatic; it is moderated by individual perceptions of capability, such as academic self-efficacy. Self-efficacy, defined as confidence in one’s ability to achieve specific behavioral goals [11, 16], influences whether students perceive challenges as opportunities for growth or threats to their competence. When students possess both high critical thinking and strong self-efficacy, they are more likely to interpret difficulties as surmountable, persist in academic tasks, and leverage their cognitive skills effectively—amplifying the positive impact of critical thinking on engagement. Conversely, low self-efficacy may undermine this process, even among students with strong critical thinking abilities, by fostering self-doubt and disengagement. Therefore, exploring the moderating role of academic self-efficacy in the relationship between critical thinking and academic engagement is crucial for understanding how these factors collectively shape academic competence.

Taken together, this study aims to explore how to promote the academic competence of college students from the perspective of critical thinking. Specifically, we adopt a longitudinal multi-wave sampling method, using Chinese college students as a sample, to empirically verify a moderated mediation framework and derive three important contributions. To begin with, this study advances the literature on critical thinking by illustrating its direct predictive relationship with academic engagement and academic competence. Second, by conceptualizing academic engagement as a mediating mechanism, this study reveals the mechanistic path by which critical thinking affects academic competence, thus enriching academic engagement research. Third, by examining academic self-efficacy as a boundary condition, this study extends the understanding of contextual factors that moderate the critical thinking – engagement - competence relationship. In summary, these findings provide a nuanced perspective on cognitive-behavioral interactions in higher education settings.

Theoretical background and research hypotheses

Academic competence

The concept of “competence” originated in psychology and human resources management before being applied to pedagogy [17]. In educational contexts, academic competence is widely defined as a multidimensional construct encompassing skills, attitudes, and behaviors that contribute to academic success. Key components include foundational abilities such as reading, writing, computation, problem-solving, attention, questioning, and metacognitive learning strategies [18]. Building on this framework, a prevalent model for college students identifies three core dimensions: academic enablers (e.g., motivation, engagement), domain-specific skills (e.g., critical thinking, discipline-specific knowledge), and study strategies (e.g., time management, note-taking) [19]. Some scholars further emphasize study skills as a critical subset of academic competence, highlighting their role in facilitating efficient learning [20]. Chinese research on academic competence emerged in the 1990s, with studies adopting diverse analytical approaches. For instance, one study analyzed academic achievement through four lenses: knowledge application, self-concept, personality traits, and motivational drivers [21]. Another study constructed a seven-dimensional model for Chinese college students, incorporating learning adaptation, learning motivation, learning engagement, academic skills, academic resilience, academic emotion and academic efficiency [22].

Therefore, academic competence is not only a judgment of value and ability, but also an evaluation perspective that promotes individual’s self-awareness, self-development, and self-improvement. It not only focuses on learning itself, but also the consideration for individual’s learning development. Based on this, the academic evaluation of college students based on competence in this study is a learner-centered development evaluation, which is a practical multi-dimensional evaluation, examining college students’ professional literacy, core literacy and general ability.

Critical thinking and academic competence

Critical thinking is the ability to make reasonable decisions about what to do and what to believe [23]. It means that individuals can make rational judgments and self-regulatory judgments after evaluating and thinking based on appropriate standards [24]. The concept of critical thinking originates from reflective thinking theory, which emphasizes active, persistent, and systematic cognitive processing as a cornerstone of higher-order thinking [25]. In essence, critical thinking is “reasonable and reflective thinking about what to do and what to believe”, and it is a kind of thinking that can be evaluated by standards, which can be divided into two parts: thinking skills and thinking tendencies [26]. Individuals with high critical thinking generally have truth-seeking, fair, reflective, and open habits and attitudes. In their daily lives, they are accustomed to exploring and seeking evidence, trusting reason, facing personal biases frankly, making judgments prudently, being willing to reconsider and deal with complex things in an orderly manner, and persistently pursuing the most accurate conclusions possible [27].

As for the specific impact of critical thinking on academic competence, this relationship can be further elucidated through the lens of social cognitive theory, which posits that personal cognitive resources (e.g., critical thinking) may directly affect behavioral enactment (e.g., academic engagement) by enabling individuals to analyze tasks strategically and persist despite challenges [10, 16]. Empirical studies support this, showing that students with higher critical thinking skills report greater time spent on deep learning activities (e.g., synthesizing information, evaluating arguments) compared to peers relying on rote memorization [28, 29]. Consequently, college students with high levels of critical thinking dare to question, think independently, and engage in systematic analysis. They demonstrate tolerance and openness toward academic knowledge [30], actively seeking and acquiring more academic knowledge through these strategic behaviors, ultimately improving their academic competence. On the contrary, students with lower critical thinking levels are more likely to adopt negative learning attitudes, such as passive acceptance of information, which hinders the development of academic competence.

In addition, true critical thinkers not only possess cognitive skills but also embody critical thinking attitudes and habits. They typically exhibit strong curiosity and a desire for knowledge, with learning behaviors driven by intrinsic motivation and autonomy [31]. This self-directed learning approach aligns with social cognitive theory’s emphasis on self-regulated learning, wherein individuals proactively adjust their strategies based on task demands and feedback. Such students are more likely to develop higher-order academic abilities, as they persistently refine their thinking processes and adapt to complex challenges [32]. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that critical thinking will positively influence college students’ academic abilities, both through direct cognitive engagement and indirect behavioral pathways. Taken together, the current study posits that:

Hypothesis 1: Critical thinking of college students is positively associated with their academic competence.

Mediating role of academic engagement

With the rapid development of the economy and the improvement of people’s living standards, research on positive psychology and positive organizational behavior has gradually emerged. Among them, “engagement” as a positive state and psychological ability has begun to enter the field of vision of scholars and aroused the attention and interest of researchers [33]. For this study, the concept of academic engagement is derived from the research on engagement, emphasizing “the psychological and behavioral characteristics of students in the learning process”, including many aspects such as setting learning goals, completing learning tasks, and participating in the learning process [34]. Essentially, academic engagement embodies a concept of students’ learning readiness, intensity of involvement in the process, and emotional quality [35]. It is an important observation indicator to test the quality of students’ learning and an important predictor of academic achievement [36]. That is to say, the concept of academic engagement is of great significance for assessing student learning effects and guiding educational practice.

Meanwhile, the significance of academic engagement is further underscored by its role as a bridge between cognitive processes and behavioral outcomes [15, 34]. Social cognitive theory also frames engagement as a proximal outcome of self-regulated learning, wherein students who actively participate in educational practices (e.g., seeking feedback, practicing skills) develop stronger competencies over time [37]. Longitudinal studies confirm that engagement accounts for 30–40% of variance in academic performance, even after controlling for prior ability [38]. This finding is consistent with the widely adopted definition of multidimensional engagement, which defines engagement as the sustained investment of time and effort in a learning task across three dimensions: cognitive (e.g., strategic thinking), affective (e.g., interest), and behavioral (e.g., persistence) [39]. And, such multidimensional engagement not only reflects students’ immediate learning states but also predicts long-term academic success [40]. Hence, for college students, strengthening academic engagement and optimizing learning strategies can help them complete their studies better and improve their learning results and academic competence.

Furthermore, previous research has systematically examined antecedents of academic engagement, revealing its susceptibility to multiple influencing factors [20, 41]. Notably, empirical evidence demonstrates a strong positive correlation between critical thinking and academic engagement among college students, with critical thinking exhibiting superior predictive power for engagement levels compared to academic self-efficacy [42]. The underlying logic is that students with high levels of critical thinking have a strong need for knowledge, which drives their inquiry and verification behaviors [9, 31]. Their thirst for knowledge largely stems from intrinsic motivation, which can continuously enhance their cognitive, emotional, and behavioral engagement [14]. At the same time, this will make it easier for them to immerse themselves in the learning process, thus showing higher academic engagement [24]. To this end, this study infers that critical thinking can promote the improvement of academic competence by strengthening college students’ engagement in academic activities. Based on the above arguments, the current posits that:

Moderating role of academic self-efficacy

Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their capacity to achieve specific goals [10], directly shaping the effort invested and persistence in overcoming obstacles. When individuals apply learned skills to task completion, their confidence in success reflects their self-efficacy [43]. Social cognitive theory conceptualizes self-efficacy as a situation-specific belief in one’s ability to execute tasks effectively [16, 44]. For this study, academic self-efficacy is defined as a domain-specific extension of general self-efficacy, referring to students’ subjective judgment of their ability to successfully complete academic tasks [45]. Unlike learning ability, it emphasizes students’ self-assessed capacity to resolve problems when encountering academic difficulties [46], playing an irreplaceable role in fostering learning participation, sustaining task engagement, and achieving academic success [47]. Hence, students with high self-efficacy are more likely to perceive challenges as manageable, sustain effort in demanding tasks, and actively seek resources for improvement-processes that amplify the positive impact of critical thinking on academic engagement [48]. Conversely, low self-efficacy may induce a “threshold effect”: even with strong critical thinking skills, students’ engagement remains stagnant if they doubt their capacity to succeed [49].

Meanwhile, many scholars have pointed out that academic self-efficacy of students has always been a key factor affecting their academic engagement and improving their academic performance [50]. This indicates that students with high academic self-efficacy are generally more confident in achieving positive academic results, and will be more motivated to participate in various academic activities, ultimately demonstrating higher academic competence [51]. Therefore, this study has reason to predict that college students’ academic self-efficacy may play an important moderating role in the relationship between critical thinking and academic engagement, and further affect their academic competence. In specific, college students with high critical thinking will be more actively involved in academic activities because they are able to seek alternative solutions to problems and dare to challenge difficulties in the learning process, thus showing high academic engagement. However, college students with low critical thinking, due to the limitations of their cognitive framework and the influence of rigidity and other factors, will show a lack of confidence in communicating emotions and information with their peers when faced with the same situation, and will then show low academic engagement [52]. Hence, under the premise of balanced critical thinking, college students with high self-efficacy are likely to show strong academic engagement. And, in situations with low critical thinking, college students with low academic self-efficacy may show weaker academic engagement, due to their inability to seriously consider the context of the problem and make careful judgments [53]. Taken together, the current study posits that:

Hypothesis 5: Academic self-efficacy of college students moderates the association between their critical thinking and academic engagement. In other words, college students with higher academic self-efficacy demonstrate a stronger positive connection between their critical thinking and academic engagement compared to those with lower self-efficacy levels.

Based on this, we further infer that academic self-efficacy may also moderate the mediating role of academic engagement of college students in the relationship between their critical thinking and academic competence. Specifically, when college students have high academic self-efficacy, their critical thinking has a greater impact on academic engagement, and the mediating effect of their academic engagement on the relationship between critical thinking and academic competence is also stronger. On the contrary, when college students’ academic self-efficacy is low, the impact of their critical thinking on academic engagement will be weakened. In this case, the mediating effect of academic engagement in the associations between their critical thinking and academic competence will also be weakened. Taken together, the current study posits that:

Hypothesis 6: Academic self-efficacy of college students serves as a moderator for the indirect pathway linking critical thinking to academic competence through academic engagement. Methods

Procedure and participants

To rigorously test the research hypotheses and the overarching theoretical model, this study recruited a sample of 500 college students from five Chinese universities through online questionnaires administered between January and April 2025. The sample was strategically selected to reflect the diversity of China’s higher education landscape, encompassing two comprehensive universities, one normal university, and two specialized science and technology institutions. This institutional variety ensures broader representativeness of the findings. Participants were drawn from multiple disciplines, including humanities (n = 165), social sciences (n = 130), natural sciences (n = 110), and engineering (n = 95), thereby enhancing the applicability of the results across academic fields. Additionally, the selected universities are geographically distributed across northern (n = 2), eastern (n = 1), and southern (n = 2) regions of China, accounting for regional variations in educational environments.

In addition, to further improve the reliability of this study and reduce common method bias, we implemented a three-wave longitudinal design with a two-week interval between each assessment phase [44, 54, 55]. Wave 1 collected baseline data on critical thinking and demographics (gender, age, grade level). Wave 2 retained 420 participants (84% retention) to measure academic self-efficacy and engagement, while Wave 3 assessed academic competence through follow-up evaluations, yielding 363 complete responses (72.6% effective retention). Comparative analyses of baseline characteristics (gender, grade level, initial critical thinking scores) between completers and dropouts revealed no significant differences (p > 0.05), confirming negligible attrition bias.

After excluding invalid questionnaires (incomplete responses, patterned answers, or mismatched data), the final dataset comprised 363 valid cases (72.6% efficiency). Gender distribution included 163 males (44.9%) and 200 females (55.1%). Grade levels were evenly distributed: 98 freshmen (27.0%), 95 sophomores (26.2%), 91 juniors (25.1%), and 79 seniors (21.8%). This methodological rigor, combined with systematic attrition checks and demographic balance, strengthens the validity and generalizability of the longitudinal findings.

Instruments

All key constructs in this study, including critical thinking, academic self-efficacy, academic engagement, and academic competence, were assessed using validated measurement scales. Since the original scales of the main variables involved in this study are all in English, they need to be translated into Chinese. During the scale translation process, this study strictly followed the Brislin back-translation method [56], assigning two bilingual researchers to independently translate and back-translate the scales. Subsequently, an expert group was organized to discuss the translation results, and to conduct detailed analysis and correction of any ambiguities to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the translation. Furthermore, this study conducted a small-scale pre-test to check the Chinese-English translation and examine the semantic equivalence of all items. All participating students used a five-point Likert scale to answer questions, with options ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

Critical thinking In this study, we assessed the critical thinking abilities of participating college students using a well-validated five-item scale originally developed by Facione [57], which has demonstrated strong reliability and validity in prior research conducted within the Chinese cultural context [58, 59]. An example item from the scale is, “When studying, I often try to find alternative solutions to problems.” The Chinese version of the scale showed excellent fit indices (χ²/df = 2.51, RMSEA = 0.06, CFI = 0.92, GFI = 0.93, SRMR = 0.04) and high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.736). Furthermore, tests of measurement invariance conducted across gender and grade levels confirmed the presence of configural, metric, and scalar invariance, ensuring consistent measurement across different subgroups and supporting the validity of cross-group comparisons in interpreting our research findings.

Academic self-efficacy

This study used the nine-item scale developed by Cassaretto, Espinosa and Chau to test the academic self-efficacy of Chinese college students [46]. One sample item is “I feel confident in handling situations that test my academic abilities”. In the present study, the Cronbach’ for scores from academic self-efficacy was 0.826.

Academic engagement

This study used the nine-item short version scale developed by Schaufeli, Bakker and Salanova to examine the academic engagement of Chinese college students at Time 2 [36]. One sample item is “When I am studying, I get carried away.” In the present study, the Cronbach’ for scores from academic engagement was 0.825.

Academic competence

This study used the nine-item scale developed by Iqbal and colleagues to evaluate the academic competence of Chinese college students [60]. One sample item is “My academic performance inspires me to study hard”. In the present study, the Cronbach’ for scores from academic competence was 0.836.

Control Variables A large number of previous studies have shown that college students’ gender and grade will affect their academic efficacy and academic competence [4, 15, 22, 33, 45, 51, 61]. Consistent with these studies, this study selected gender and grade of college students as control variables to test the hypotheses and the entire theoretical model. Specifically, gender is a binary variable, coded as 1 = male and 2 = female. The grade of the participating Chinese college students was divided into four levels (1 = Freshman, 2 = Sophomore, 3 = Junior, 4 = Senior).

Analysis strategy

All statistical procedures for the current study were implemented using SPSS 24.0 and Amos 24.0 software. The analytical process began with confirmatory factor analyses (CFAs) conducted in Amos 24.0 to assess the discriminant validity of our theoretical framework. Model fit was assessed using multiple indices: Chi-Square/degree of freedom (χ2/df), standardized root mean square residua (SRMR), comparative fit index (CFI), the goodness of fit index (GFI) and)root means square error of approximation (RMSEA) [62]. Subsequent analyses in SPSS 24.0 included descriptive statistics and correlation matrices to examine preliminary associations among critical thinking, academic self-efficacy, academic engagement, and academic competence of Chinese college students. Hierarchical regression analyses were also further employed to explore directional relationships among the four core constructs. To validate the complete moderated mediation model, we utilized Hayes’ [63] PROCESS macro (Model 7) with bootstrap resampling (5000 iterations). This approach enabled rigorous testing of both direct and indirect effects within our theoretical framework.

Common method bias test

Although this study collected sample data at three different time points to mitigate common method bias, all core constructs were still self-reported by college students, thus posing a certain degree of risk of common method variance. Therefore, this study employed Harman’s single-factor test to assess whether all measurement items exhibited common method bias. The results indicated that the first factor explained 35.38% of the variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%. Furthermore, the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) results, incorporating all indicators, also demonstrated poor data fit (χ2/df = 7.741, RMSEA = 0.137, CFI = 0.856, GFI = 0.668, SRMR = 0.136), with fit indices significantly below the evaluation indices. In summary, common method bias in this study is not a significant issue.

Mediating effect testing

For testing the mediating effect of academic engagement in the association between critical thinking and academic competence, this study adopted Hayes and Preacher’ [64] framework for establishing mediating pathways. Specifically, this analysis method requires two consecutive verifications: First, there must be a statistically significant association between critical thinking and academic engagement; Second, after controlling for the direct association between critical thinking and academic competence, the residual association between academic engagement and academic competence and the indirect effect of critical thinking on academic competence must be significant as well. For this study, the results of Model 2 in Table 3 satisfy the first criterion. And, after adding academic engagement to the regression model, the results of Model 7 in Table 3 reveal that academic engagement positively predict academic competence (β = 0.250, p < 0.001), and critical thinking still has a significant effect on academic competence (β = 0.147, p < 0.05). These results therefore indicate that academic engagement partially mediates the pathway from critical thinking to academic competence among Chinese college students, thereby providing empirical support for Hypothesis 4.

Moderating effect testing

For testing the mediating effects of academic self-efficacy in the association between critical thinking and academic engagement, the results of Model 3 show that the interaction term of critical thinking and academic self-efficacy is significant in predicting academic engagement (β = 0.158, p < 0.001), which indicates that academic self-efficacy of Chinese college students play a significant positive moderating role in the influence of critical thinking on their academic engagement. Then, to intuitively examine the moderating effect of academic self-efficacy, this study created two subgroups, high and low academic self-efficacy, by adjusting the mean score ± 1 standard deviation (see Fig. 2 ). The analysis showed that critical thinking had a stronger predictive effect on academic engagement for students with high academic self-efficacy than for students with low academic self-efficacy. And, this finding confirms that academic self-efficacy of Chinese college students positively moderates the relationship between critical thinking and academic engagement, providing empirical verification for Hypothesis 5.

Moderating effect of academic self-efficacy in the relationship between critical thinking and academic engagement

In addition, following Cohen’s conventions for effect size classification [65], the key pathways in this study demonstrate distinct levels of practical significance: The pathway from critical thinking to academic engagement (β = 0.558, p < 0.001) exhibits a large effect, suggesting that a one-unit increase in critical thinking predicts a 0.558-unit rise in academic engagement, an impact exceeding 95% of similar cognitive intervention studies [66]. Besides, the relationship between academic engagement and academic competence (β = 0.334, p < 0.001) reflects a medium effect, aligning closely with the average effect size (r = 0.34) of active learning strategies in higher education reported by Freeman and college [67]. Finally, the moderating role of academic self-efficacy (β = 0.158, p < 0.001) qualifies as a small effect, indicating that while statistically significant, its practical influence is more modest, yet still underscores the value of incorporating self-efficacy-boosting strategies into interventions to support student success. These gradings collectively provide a nuanced understanding of how each pathway contributes to educational outcomes, bridging statistical significance with actionable insights.

Moderated mediation effect testing

Finally, to evaluate the moderated mediation effect and the comprehensive theoretical framework, this study utilized Bootstrap resampling (5000 iterations) to examine whether academic self-efficacy moderates the indirect pathway from critical thinking to academic competence through academic engagement [68]. The analysis outcomes, detailed in Table 4, revealed two key findings: First, the conditional indirect effect of academic self-efficacy achieved statistical significance (β = 0.042, SE = 0.014, 95% CI [0.019, 0.076]). Second, the mediating strength of academic engagement varied across self-efficacy levels: the indirect effect was substantially stronger under high academic self-efficacy conditions (β = 0.149, 95% CI [0.078, 0.226]) compared to low self-efficacy scenarios (β = 0.076, 95% CI [0.032, 0.140]). In other words, it can be inferred that as academic self-efficacy increases, the mediating role of academic engagement in the association between Chinese college students’ critical thinking and academic competence becomes more significant. Therefore, Hypothesis 6 and the overall theoretical model of the current study are fully supported.

Discussion

The primary objective of the current study was to discuss pathways for enhancing academic competence among Chinese college students through the lens of critical thinking. To this end, from the perspective of social cognitive theory, we developed and empirically examined a moderated mediation model to explore the mediating role of academic engagement and the moderating role of academic self-efficacy in the association between critical thinking and academic competence. Through statistical analysis of the three-stage samples collected, this study found that Chinese college students’ critical thinking has a positive impact on their academic engagement and academic competence. At the same time, the academic engagement of Chinese college students not only has a direct positive impact on their academic competence, but also plays a mediating role in the impact of critical thinking on their academic competence. In addition, Chinese college students’ academic self-efficacy positively moderates the impact of critical thinking on their academic engagement, which in turn affects their academic competence. This indicates that among Chinese college students with higher self-efficacy, critical thinking has a more significant driving effect on their academic engagement, which ultimately translates into more significant growth in academic competence; while among college students with lower self-efficacy, this indirect effect is significantly weakened.

Theoretical implications

The present study’s findings offer theoretical perspectives by further emphasizing the critical role of academic self-efficacy and academic engagement in the pathways through which critical thinking shapes academic competence of college students. As theoretical contributions, this research introduces a moderated mediation framework to identify pivotal factors that enhance students’ academic competence in higher education, approached from the dimensions of thinking, engagement and self-efficacy. Specifically, we firstly confirmed that critical thinking of college students could enhance their academic competence, which provides a new perspective for studying academic competence of college students. Most of the previous studies have explored the impact on academic competence from the perspectives of college students’ personality and psychological characteristics [4, 6, 21, 69]. This research finding also has important implications for effectively promoting the development of critical thinking theory in the field of education, and further enriching the academic competence theory [13, 22, 70].

Secondly, unlike previous studies [12, 35, 41], this study introduces academic engagement into the process of the impact of critical thinking on academic competence for the first time, verifying that the critical thinking of college students affects their academic competence via academic engagement which provides a new theoretical perspective for the theoretical community to understand the mechanism of critical thinking [43]. At the same time, the mediating role of academic engagement opens the black box of how critical thinking affects academic competence to a certain extent. Hence, the finding not only constructs a novel explanatory framework for understanding the nexus between critical thinking and academic competence, but also expands and deepens the theoretical discourse on participation in educational contexts, thereby advancing the refinement of existing scholarly paradigms [33, 51, 71].

Thirdly, the findings of this study also revealed that academic self-efficacy, defined as individuals’ confidence in their capacity to accomplish academic objectives [45, 47, 50], exerts a significant positive moderating effect on the pathways linking critical thinking, academic engagement, and academic competence. This conclusion further proves that college students’ academic engagement and competence do not occur in a vacuum, and their academic self-efficacy provides an individual context for critical thinking to occur, which not only further verifies the contextualization and effectiveness of critical thinking, but also enriches Self-efficacy theory has made important contributions. Besides, together with previous studies [16, 50, 72], these findings broaden the research on the boundary condition of critical thinking on academic engagement and academic competence, and provides education scholars with a new basis for understanding the conditions under which critical thinking is more likely to lead to greater academic engagement and academic competence [46, 73].

Lastly, considering the Chinese cultural context, it is essential to acknowledge the complex influences of Confucian values, which emphasize respect for authority and collectivism [1, 54, 74], on students’ critical thinking. On one hand, these values might suppress students’ willingness to directly question authority or present opposing viewpoints, potentially limiting the expression of critical thinking in traditional educational settings [75]. On the other hand, with the advancement of globalization and educational reforms, the modern Chinese education system increasingly encourages independent thinking and innovation [2, 3]. Consequently, the manifestation of critical thinking among Chinese college students may exhibit unique cultural characteristics, blending traditional values with modern educational ideals [76]. While our study found a positive impact of critical thinking on academic engagement and competence, the strength and form of this relationship may indeed vary across different cultural contexts. Therefore, future research should further explore the cultural specificity of these relationships to enhance the cross-cultural applicability and theoretical transferability of our findings.

Managerial implications

The research conclusions hold significant practical implications for higher education management, particularly in four key dimensions.

Firstly, given the well-documented positive impact of critical thinking on academic engagement and academic competence, universities should fully recognize the importance of cultivating students’ critical thinking abilities and implement targeted measures for implementation, follow-up, and improvement. For instance, in constructing a teaching environment, universities should prioritize creating an inclusive and psychologically safe classroom atmosphere. This necessitates the design of teaching strategies that encourage students to challenge existing viewpoints, express alternative perspectives, and substantiate their arguments with empirical evidence, thereby institutionalizing the epistemological principle of “evidence-based validation over dogmatism” [77]. Concurrently, teachers should inspire students to ask questions boldly, dare to question, and maintain an open attitude towards any viewpoint. Particularly in the classroom, group discussions and role-playing can be employed to stimulate students’ enthusiasm for participation, enabling them to spark intellectual insights through interaction [14, 24].

Secondly, the mediating effect of academic engagement provides higher education institutions with a new reflective perspective, enabling them to recognize that critical thinking serves as a catalyst for undergraduate academic engagement. This engagement, in turn, encourages students to participate more actively in academic activities, ultimately leading to an improvement in their academic competence. Therefore, this study advocates prioritizing the cultivation and continuous enhancement of college students’ academic engagement in educational practice. For example, during the process of problem-solving or task completion, instructors should offer timely encouragement to bolster students’ academic confidence; when students encounter obstacles, teachers should provide targeted guidance to help them overcome challenges through persistent efforts. The sense of accomplishment derived from striving to cope with and resolve difficulties will further enhance students’ learning engagement [78] and ultimately improve their academic competence [38, 46].

Thirdly, the moderating effect of academic self-efficacy underscores the strategic importance of enhancing college students’ academic self-efficacy through diversified interventions. Educational administrators should implement systematic monitoring of students’ self-efficacy development through multifaceted channels such as individual counseling, progress documentation, and structured surveys [51, 79]. This requires the establishment of a dynamic feedback mechanism that integrates qualitative insights from faculty-student interactions with quantitative data from standardized assessments, thereby enabling targeted interventions to strengthen students’ academic confidence. Simultaneously, it is essential to appropriately adjust the focus and investment in critical thinking based on the degree of students’ academic self-efficacy, so as to better control the level of students’ academic engagement [12, 35]. This also necessitates that, in the daily student management process, administrators must be adept at detecting changes in students’ self-efficacy, judging the required level of academic involvement, and accordingly selecting critical thinking strategies that meet students’ developmental needs [80].

Finally, concerning sociocultural factors, educational practitioners should strategically design targeted interventions to mitigate the constraining effects of cultural norms such as “saving face,” which may inhibit open academic discourse [74, 81]. On the one hand, universities should strive to create an inclusive environment by organizing multicultural activities and lectures to enhance students’ understanding and respect for different cultural backgrounds. Concurrently, open discussions should be encouraged in the classroom, providing students with a safe space to express themselves and share their viewpoints [14, 26]. On the other hand, an anonymous communication zone could be established: utilizing online platforms or school internal systems to create an anonymous feedback mechanism, enabling students to freely express their opinions, ask questions, or seek help without worrying about being judged or ridiculed by others [42]. This anonymity helps to alleviate concerns about saving face and promotes genuine communication [30, 72].

Limitations and future research

Notwithstanding the aforementioned theoretical and practical contributions, the current study acknowledges several limitations. Firstly, the scope of this empirical study is limited to a sample of college students from five universities in China. A large number of previous cross-cultural studies have shown that the cultural specificity of hierarchical authority perceptions and critical discourse norms [61, 82] may have different moderating effects on the operationalization of critical thinking skills among Chinese college students. This cultural context may affect cognitive engagement patterns and manifestations of self-efficacy, thus affecting the generalizability of our findings to educational systems with different sociocultural frameworks. Therefore, future scholars can try to retest the proposed theoretical model using samples from different cultural backgrounds and explore its cross-cultural applicability in detail. Secondly, although the current study theoretically constructed and verified an integrated model of the impact of critical thinking on academic engagement and competence, and adopted a multi-stage data collection method to improve the reliability of the research conclusions, all data came from college students, and it is impossible to truly infer the causal association among core variables. Therefore, the current study suggests that future research adopts longitudinal or experimental designs to explore the causal relationships among the core research variables in more detail. Thirdly, due to data limitations, this study only controlled for gender and grade variables, failing to encompass important factors such as previous academic performance, major, school level, socioeconomic status, and learning time, which may influence the relationship between critical thinking, academic self-efficacy, academic engagement, and academic ability. Future research collects more comprehensive academic background data and, by incorporating these covariates, more accurately isolate the impact of each factor on academic ability, thereby enhancing the validity and generalizability of this study. Finally, the current study just explores the influencing mechanism of academic competence from the perspective of critical thinking. However, the factors that affect the college students’ academic competence may be extensive and diverse, such as environmental factors (such as academic pressure [83]), individual factors (such as self-esteem, personality characteristics [6, 22, 72]), etc. Therefore, future research can further discuss the affecting factors and mechanisms of academic competence from the perspective of the interaction between individuals and the external environment in a more in-depth and comprehensive manner.

Conclusions

In this study, we conducted an in-depth exploration of the relationships among critical thinking, academic engagement, academic self-efficacy, and academic competence among Chinese college students, yielding a series of findings with both theoretical and practical significance.

From a theoretical perspective, this study underscores the vital roles of academic self-efficacy and engagement in how critical thinking affects college students’ academic competence. By introducing academic engagement as a mediator, it elucidates the intrinsic mechanism linking critical thinking to academic competence, constructing a new explanatory framework. Meanwhile, the study confirms that academic self-efficacy has a significant positive moderating effect on the relationship between critical thinking, academic engagement, and academic competence. This provides important boundary conditions for understanding the contexts in which critical thinking is more likely to translate into higher academic engagement and academic competence, further expanding the boundary conditions of critical thinking research and making a significant contribution to the development of academic theory.

In terms of practical implications, this study offers highly targeted actionable insights for higher education management and teaching practices. Universities should recognize the value of fostering critical thinking, creating inclusive, psychologically safe classrooms where students dare to question and express diverse views, using group discussions to boost engagement. The mediating role of academic engagement necessitates prioritizing its cultivation, with teachers providing timely encouragement and targeted guidance to enhance academic engagement. The moderating effect of academic self-efficacy highlights the need for systematic monitoring and personalized interventions to bolster it. Additionally, considering the influence of traditional Chinese values on the critical thinking of Chinese students, future research should explore cultural specificities to enhance the cross-cultural applicability of the findings.

In conclusion, by conducting an in-depth analysis of the relationships among critical thinking, academic engagement, academic self-efficacy, and academic competence, this study not only injects new vitality into the development of academic theories but also provides practical guidance for higher education management and teaching practices. We also hope that these findings will inspire more scholars to conduct in-depth explorations in related fields and jointly promote the improvement of higher education quality.
