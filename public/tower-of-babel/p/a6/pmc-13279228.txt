Breakthrough Stroke in Women With Atrial Fibrillation: A Call for Sex‐Specific Care and Better Outcomes
Authors: Bernadette Corica, Gregory Y. H. Lip
Published: 2026

Atrial fibrillation (AF) confers a 5‐fold higher risk of stroke compared with sinus rhythm; however, this risk is dependent on clinical characteristics, risk factors, and comorbidities. Importantly, the risk of stroke in AF is not homogeneous across sexes: older data show that being female carries a higher risk of ischemic stroke compared with their male counterparts, especially in the presence of ≥1 nonsex stroke risk factors. 1 Hence, female sex has been recognized as a risk modifier for stroke and is included in the CHA2DS2‐VASc score, which is widely used thromboembolic risk stratification in patients with AF.

Of note, women have been historically undertreated with oral anticoagulants, and more likely prescribed with off‐label low‐dose direct oral anticoagulants. 1 Also, should AF‐related strokes occur, they tended to be more severe and disabling in female patients compared with men. Nevertheless, recent data from Europe suggest that the gap between sexes has narrowed in the past decade, 2 , 3 likely as a result of increasing awareness of AF, the recognition of female sex as a stroke risk modifier, and the introduction of direct oral anticoagulants. More recently, the latest European guidelines for the management of stroke recommended the use of a nonsex CHA2DS2‐VA score as “the inclusion of gender complicates clinical practice both for health care professionals and patients (and) omits individuals who identify as non‐binary, transgender, or are undergoing sex hormone therapy.” 4 However, the findings in Finland and Denmark are not similar globally, where in some populations, female patients remain at persistently higher stroke risk compared with men, resulting in improved stroke risk reclassification with CHA2DS2‐VASc compared with the nonsex CHA2DS2‐VA score. 5

Nevertheless, despite the use of oral anticoagulants significantly reduced the risk of stroke, it does fully not abolish it. Data from the pivotal randomized controlled trials of direct oral anticoagulants in patients with AF showed that the risk of residual stroke is ~2% per year, which may reach 4% in observational studies, and even higher rates have been observed for patients with AF and prior history of stroke. 6

The mechanisms for the residual stroke risk are heterogeneous. These include the inappropriate therapy with oral anticoagulants 7 as well as competing causes as small vessels occlusion or other atherothrombotic causes, which often coexist with AF. 8 In addition, intrinsic patient‐related factors, including multimorbidity and risk factors such as carotid disease, increase the risk of thromboembolism in patients with AF, regardless of antithrombotic treatment. 9 In this context, female sex has been linked to a higher rate of breakthrough ischemic stroke during oral anticoagulants treatment, and worse outcomes compared with men, even though with some conflicting evidence on the actual independence from other risk factors. 10 , 11

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Foschi et al. 12 investigated sex differences on outcomes following breakthrough ischemic stroke using data from ASPERA‐R (Advancing Knowledge in Ischemic Stroke Patients on Oral Anticoagulants Registry), a large, multicenter, retrospective study of 1,649 patients with AF suffering a breakthrough ischemic stroke while on oral anticoagulants. After inverse probability weighting, women were less likely to experience recovery after the ischemic event compared with men. Also, women had higher ischemic recurrences after a breakthrough stroke in the first 90 days compared with men, mostly due to no oral anticoagulants resumption or oral anticoagulants interruption. These women with breakthrough stroke were older than men and presented with more severe strokes. Together, these findings are in line with multiple prior studies—not necessarily focused on AF‐related stroke—in which women experienced poorer outcomes at discharge or at 90 days after a stroke compared with their male counterparts. 13 , 14

The results of this study are important and have clinical implications. Notwithstanding the need for simplifying the thromboembolic risk assessment, these findings underline how trajectories of stroke in patients with AF (from incidence to functional sequelae and clinical outcomes) are influenced by sex, suggesting that female sex should still be accounted as a risk modifier, both for intrinsic stroke risk and in relation to their poorer functional recovery. In this context, Foschi et al. 12 highlight the disparity of treatment received after the breakthrough stroke, whereby women have lower probability of receiving thrombolysis. Although these factors could be influenced by stroke severity itself and the risk for potential complications (eg, intracerebral hemorrhage), they also underline that structural inequities in treatment may still play a role in influencing outcomes across sexes.

Of note, another important implication is the poststroke strategy to reduce the risk of recurrent events. Data from 2 large randomized controlled trials (TIMING [Timing of Oral Anticoagulant Therapy in Acute Ischemic Stroke With Atrial Fibrillation] 15 and ELAN [Early Versus Late Initiation of Direct Oral Anticoagulants in Post‐ischaemic Stroke Patients With Atrial Fibrillation] 16 ) suggested that the early restart of anticoagulation after a stroke may provide a greater benefit in women. Nevertheless, evidence for additional strategies to reduce residual stroke risk remains limited in both sexes. 17

Beyond treatment disparities, other sex‐based differences may have contributed to the increased vulnerability to breakthrough stroke observed in women in this study. Additional studies are needed to clarify which mechanisms and factors may be responsible for the observed sex‐based differences. These may include a differential response to oral anticoagulant treatment, along with differences in coagulation and thrombosis between sexes, availability of health care resources, presence of caregivers, and imbalances in the support and rehabilitation received after stroke. Racial differences in ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke are also evident, 18 , 19 and whether these contribute to residual risks in different ethnicities remains unexplored.

Taken together, the study from Foschi et al. 12 provides important and novel insights on the different natural history of women and men with AF after a breakthrough stroke despite oral anticoagulants. These data reinforce the importance of considering sex differences throughout the trajectories of patients with AF—even when a stroke occurs—and contribute to challenging the misperception that the stroke risk “gap” between women and men is entirely resolved. Conversely, sex differences in AF‐related stroke are still present and should not to be neglected; hence, women require adequate oral anticoagulant treatment for their intrinsic increased risk of stroke, especially at older ages, and because—compared with men—strokes in women are more severe and can lead to a greater loss of functional status. In this context, a key message from Foschi et al. 12 is that outcomes after stroke in women are not only a matter of intrinsic characteristics but can also reflect inequity between sexes. A sex‐neutral approach is therefore no longer sufficient when evidence shows sex‐specific patterns in risk, treatment, and outcomes. Physicians should systematically consider sex differences in all the phases of AF management: from stroke prevention, follow‐up, and acute management to postacute care. Clinical trials and observational studies must report sex‐stratified data on treatment efficacy and safety. These data are needed to design tailored sex‐specific interventions, which may reduce the persistent gap between sexes, and promote equity in stroke care.
