Spatiotemporal dependency data imputation for long-term health monitoring of concrete arch bridges
Authors: Zhu Longji, Yang Zhi, Li Jiaqing, Liang Wenhua, Min Jingchun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The structural health monitoring of bridge infrastructures is imperative for ensuring their uninterrupted functionality and mitigating potential hazards. Nevertheless, challenges arise Due to missing data and intricate latent dynamics embedded in field sensing measurements, complicating the forecasting efforts. Addressing these challenges, this paper introduces a pioneering CCF-BiGRU model designed for the imputation of missing data in bridge strain monitoring. This model harnesses the spatial correlations among sensor data, analyzed through cross-correlation algorithms, combined with the predictive capabilities of the BiGRU neural network. By conducting experiments with 5%-20% missing completely at random data in authentic bridge monitoring contexts, the study substantiates the efficacy of this approach. The CCF-BiGRU model surpasses its counterparts— BiGRU, BiLSTM, GRU, and LSTM models—in several critical performance metrics (root mean square error, correlation coefficient, and relative accuracy). Notably, its performance metrics remain consistent even as the proportion of missing data escalates. Specifically, in scenarios with 5% to 10% data omission, the CCF-BiGRU model consistently exhibits a uniform error distribution in MASE evaluations, highlighting its robustness. Although CCF-BiLSTM model shows similar interpolation performance, its computational cost is much higher. These compelling results validate the efficiency and reliability of the CCF-BiGRU method, a data-driven solution that not only shows promise but also excels in computational efficiency. It adeptly predicts and fills gaps in bridge strain monitoring data, thereby ensuring precise evaluations of bridge health.

Introduction

The reliability of structural health monitoring data is paramount for ensuring the operational efficiency of monitoring systems1,2. However, instances of data missing frequently arise due to interferences such as failures in monitoring equipment and faults in data transmission, which significantly impair the accuracy of structural health monitoring3,4. Therefore, the development of algorithms to impute missing data carries profound scientific significance and substantial practical value for the accurate assessment of the health of bridge structures5,6. Recent advancements in data-driven techniques for the recovery of monitoring data have achieved notable progress7–9, predominantly categorized into three methods: recovery approaches based on compressive sensing, statistical interpolation, and deep learning technologies.

The recovery approaches based on compressive sensing reconstructs signals from undersampled data by employing an observation matrix to project these signals into a low-dimensional space, ultimately restoring the original signal through the resolution of an optimization problem10,11. Hsu et al.12 developed a real-time compressive sensing (RTCS) network that efficiently and robustly reconstructs data affected by noise transmission and stripe effects with minimal training samples. This network utilizes linear projections compatible with 8-bit integers and a dual-stream architecture decoder, facilitating rapid deployment on edge devices and significantly reducing computational demands at ground stations. Shruti et al.13 explored the feasibility of utilizing reconstructed signals from missing sensor data to estimate the damage index by employing ultrasonic guided wave testing on honeycomb composite sandwich structures. The orthogonal matching pursuit algorithm was utilized to simulate and retrieve the lost signals. Meanwhile, Cao et al.14 applied compressive sensing methods to impute data in wireless sensor networks. However, this approach requires extensive prior knowledge, is time-consuming, does not support real-time recovery, and heavily depends on the sparsity of data in specific feature spaces, rendering it unsuitable for environments with high rates of data loss15.

Additionally, some scholars have conducted research on monitoring data recovery using statistical interpolation, capitalizing on the correlation characteristics of the monitoring data16,17. Anna et al.18 undertook riverbed mapping of selected river sections during the canalization of rivers, employing the global navigation satellite system combined with echo sounders and two navigation schemes. They assessed the accuracy of two interpolation methods—the triangulated irregular network and the natural neighbors method—for constructing water depth measurements along a 200-kilometer section of the Oder River in southern Poland. Meanwhile, Chen et al.19 developed a probabilistic distribution regression approach that recovers missing data using complete data from other sensors; Employing their understanding of data frameworks, Yang et al.20 effectively reconstructed the time histories of structural vibration responses that had disappeared randomly. However, these methods are primarily effective for recovering slowly varying signals and are not suitable for high-frequency signal recovery.

With the progression of artificial intelligence technology, the use of deep learning techniques for data recovery research has become more prevalent21–23. Owing to their capacity to extract more complex nonlinear correlations from data, these methods achieve higher recovery accuracy24. Fang et al.25 introduced a data-driven approach utilizing deep learning techniques to extract low-frequency signals from high-frequency ones. Local data-driven mappings were constructed using basic blocks of seismic data, which, alongside energy balance, were used to train a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). This CNN was capable of predicting low-frequency data from high-frequency data. After being trained using the Marmousi model and evaluated against the overthrust model and real-world field data, the CNN showcased its exceptional ability by precisely matching the predicted low-frequency data with the actual outputs in both the temporal and spectral domains. Utilizing a deep learning framework, Elimam et al.26 developed a cybersecurity mechanism focused on identifying manipulations in PMU data and restoring compromised measurement data. The framework encompasses a pair of deep learning models: the first focuses on attack detection, capable of recognizing attacks and pinpointing affected PMUs, and the second handles signal reconstruction, relaying restored signals back to the wide area monitoring and control system. This cybersecurity approach has proven the model’s effectiveness in accurately detecting network intrusions and restoring tampered signals with negligible errors in reconstruction, demonstrating outstanding efficacy. Oh et al.27 constructed a CNN data recovery model using complete strain monitoring data to recover missing strain responses. Although deep learning-based monitoring data recovery methods have made significant progress, existing methods mostly utilize the time correlation of data itself without tapping into the spatial correlation between sensors, failing to integrate the two effectively.

In recent years, hybrid deep learning models have advanced SHM data imputation by integrating spatiotemporal features. For example, CNN-BiGRU hybrids capture local spatial patterns and bidirectional temporal dependencies; a hybrid CNN-LSTM model was originally applied to air pollution prediction and later shown adaptable to SHM vibration data reconstruction28. GANs have been widely adopted to generate realistic spatiotemporal distributions for missing data, such as WGAIN-GP for bridge monitoring29, slim GANs (SGAIN) for large-scale sensor networks7, and modified GANs with spatiotemporal encoders for high-missing-rate reconstruction in bridges30. Variational autoencoder (VAE)–like frameworks also provide probabilistic imputation via latent representations encoding spatial–temporal variabilities, as demonstrated in autoencoder-based multi-channel signal recovery for SHM sensor networks31 and in unsupervised meta-learning for long-term bridge health monitoring with large data gaps9.

Transformer-based models leverage self-attention for long-range dependencies, including Transformer-GAN for bridge SHM data augmentation32‚33, convolutional Transformers for multivariate time-series imputation34, and general Transformer architectures for time-series imputation35. Graph neural networks (GNNs) have also been introduced for virtual sensing in complex systems to address heterogeneous temporal dynamics36. Other attention-based imputation models such as SAITS further extend self-attention to generic time-series imputation37. Comprehensive surveys on GNNs for time-series analysis—including forecasting, classification, imputation, and anomaly detection—have summarized these advances and challenges38.

Despite these progresses, many methods rely on complex architectures (e.g., GANs, Transformers) that raise computational costs or predefined spatial structures (e.g., in VAEs, CNN hybrids), limiting applicability to dynamic bridge strain data with variable missing patterns. In contrast, the CCF-BiGRU model proposed in this paper efficiently integrates data-driven spatial correlations with bidirectional temporal modeling, differing from and improving upon GNN-based hybrids. Unlike GNN-LSTM/GRU methods requiring predefined adjacency matrices—potentially biased if topologies miss dynamic correlations—CCF-BiGRU uses cross-correlation to autonomously quantify spatial dependencies and time lags from sensor data, without prior assumptions, better capturing bridge strain propagation under loads or environments. For instance, recent GNN hybrids like HTGNN for virtual sensing in complex systems36 or SAITS with self-attention for time-series imputation37 still depend on graph convolutions that may overlook non-local interactions in noisy bridge data.

Previous studies predominantly focused on employing data from single observation points, employing neural network architectures to predict and impute missing data within designated monitoring stations. However, the strain of bridge structures is not isolated; rather, there is a certain dependency between adjacent strain entities. Predicting each measurement point separately and then statistically modeling the results often leads to significant discrepancies between the predictions and the actual engineering environment. Overcoming the limitations of prior studies, this research capitalizes on the temporal and spatial relationships inherent in monitoring data. It employs the Cross-Correlation Function (CCF) algorithm to account for sensor spatial correlations and leverages the time series prediction capabilities of the BiGRU algorithm to propose a CCF-BiGRU bridge strain monitoring data recovery method. Through an example analysis of bridge strain monitoring data recovery, the effectiveness of this method is validated. The CCF-BiGRU model proposed in this research significantly advances bridge monitoring data imputation in three key aspects: (1) It pioneers the incorporation of spatial sensor positioning data into the imputation model, departing from the conventional emphasis on temporal dependencies in the data analysis. (2) The model employs a CCF algorithm to compute correlations among neighboring sensors, thereby alleviating the computational strain associated with extensive input data. (3) Through the design of extensive data missing scenarios, the study contributes fresh perspectives to the understanding and management of data gaps in bridge monitoring systems.

Problem statement

The evolutionary trajectory of bridge structures, from their inception to potential failure, spans a protracted period. Monitoring and predicting structural strain can significantly improve the reliability of bridge safety assessments. However, during the operational phase of bridge structures, natural erosion can induce physical environmental instability, frequently resulting in gaps in monitoring data. This study focuses on predicting and filling gaps in strain data during the bridge operation phase using historical time series. Nonetheless, the predictive task often encounters significant complexities when historical data exhibit profound nonlinearity and intricate dynamical patterns.

This paper focuses on accurately predicting and completing bridge structural strain monitoring data using historical strain records. Specifically, the sensor monitoring data should align with the cross-correlation function within Euclidean geometric space alongside adjacent sensor monitoring data, underscoring the importance of incorporating sensor location information in the prediction and completion of monitoring data. Additionally, bridge structural strain often exhibits trends over several months, reflecting long-term dependencies, and experiences substantial fluctuations, and monitoring data related to bridge structural strain are governed by specific underlying physical principles. Given the spatial correlations and temporal interconnections present in the data, we propose a CCF-BiGRU approach to predict and complete bridge structural strain monitoring data. This method effectively captures the spatial and temporal dimensions of the monitoring data, thereby providing a robust data prediction and completion model.

B. BiGRU structure

Distinct from the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), the Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) employs one less gating mechanism, requires fewer parameters, and enhances training efficiency without compromising the accuracy of predictions. The Bi-GRU (Bidirectional GRU) architecture is designed to capture local features effectively from both the forward and backward directions, thereby improving the global performance of the network. C. CCF-BiGRU missing data imputation model

In bridge structural health monitoring, sensor locations are fixed, but spatial correlations are dynamically governed by structural responses (e.g., strain propagation under loads or environmental changes). The Cross-Correlation Function (CCF) is particularly suited to this scenario. Unlike Euclidean distance-based weighting, which relies excessively on physical proximity and may overlook actual dynamic correlations (e.g., weaker ties between adjacent arch rib sensors than non-adjacent ones on the same load path), CCF directly captures data-driven correlations. It further improves upon Pearson correlation by identifying time-delayed dependencies (e.g., strain propagation lags under moving loads) that zero-time-lag metrics fail to quantify. Compared with graph-based methods (e.g., GNNs) that require predefined topologies—often introducing biases—and involve costly convolutions in large-scale networks (e.g., the 222-sensor system examined here), CCF autonomously determines optimal time lags without prior structural assumptions, thereby reducing computational complexity. For long-term monitoring (e.g., 32 months), this lower complexity ensures efficient processing of large datasets, making CCF particularly effective for modeling dynamic bridge responses.

Motivated by these considerations, this study develops an enhanced CCF-BiGRU neural network for spatiotemporal imputation and forecasting in SHM data. The model systematically addresses both spatial and temporal dependencies. Spatial dependencies are quantified through CCF, which measures correlations between the target sensor and others, ensuring that only the most relevant information is incorporated. Temporal dependencies are modeled by the Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit (BiGRU), which processes sequences in both forward and backward directions to leverage past and future observations. In practice, the integration proceeds in three steps: (1) CCF computes cross-correlation coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho_{XY}(\tau)$$\end{document}over a 180-day window, selecting neighbors with\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mid{{\rho _{{\text{XY}}}}(\tau )}\mid>\text{0.85}$$\end{document} and their optimal delays \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau_0$$\end{document}; (2) neighbor data are time-aligned using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau_0$$\end{document} and weighted by\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho_{\text{XY}}(\tau_0)$$\end{document} to construct spatial feature vectors; and (3) these vectors are concatenated with the target sensor’s historical series to form a multi-dimensional tensor, which is subsequently input to BiGRU for joint spatiotemporal learning.

By integrating CCF for spatial and BiGRU for temporal dependencies, the CCF-BiGRU model effectively captures complex interactions in structural health monitoring data, improving data imputation accuracy and robustness. Comprised of input, hidden, and output layers, the architecture is designed for efficient learning. The input layer preprocesses data, while the hidden layer, featuring multiple Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Units (BiGRUs), iteratively adjusts weights to minimize errors and achieve convergence. The output layer converts processed data back to its target data format, as show in Fig. 3. The input layer processes pre-processed time series data from sensors, enabling supervised learning and incorporating cross-correlation features (CCF) between related measurement points. Each time step within a chosen interval, T, serves as input, with corresponding data points serving as targets. This prepared data is then fed into the hidden layer for in-depth analysis.

Figure 3 depicts the input layer’s purpose, which is to convert preprocessed monitoring data into a format facilitative to learning, while fusion feature information from specific monitoring points connected via CCF to the target locations. To accomplish this, the layer employs T-step intervals, treating data from these periods as inputs at each measurement instance, with corresponding sample values serving as outputs. This sequential data is thereafter passed to the hidden layer, where each time step is individually indexed for processing.

The ability to dynamically modify the number of hidden layers in deep neural network structures holds critical importance, as it enables the model to adapt to the inherent characteristics of the data and informed by empirical evidence. However, an excessive number of layers may exacerbate overfitting, particularly when dealing with data exhibiting discernible patterns. Our study adopts a solitary hidden layer structure strategy to mitigate this issue. The selection of an effective activation function, σ(.), is crucial for the model’s architecture. We opt for the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) due to its proven advantages in preventing gradient vanishing and accelerating computation. The loss function is MAE, which quantifies the difference between predicted and actual outputs. MAE was chosen as the loss function for its robustness to transient outliers (e.g., environmental disturbances) in bridge strain data, preventing overfitting to anomalies and ensuring stable training—critical for capturing consistent structural trends. While RMSE evaluates overall error magnitude, MAE aligns with practical needs by prioritizing average error minimization, balancing robustness and relevance for long-term monitoring. The Adam optimization algorithm is employed to iteratively refine learning parameters until the loss stabilizes. Post-training, the output layer reverses the predicted values to their original time series format through reverse normalization.

Experimental example

A. Basic information

The Dongjiang Bridge, located in Huizhou City, China, is composed of two parallel sections and spans a total length of 805.73 m with a width of 11 m, which includes a 9-meter-wide roadway. The bridge deck is surfaced with cement concrete. Structurally, the bridge features 18 spans arranged in the following sequence: 20 × 5 + 8 + 75 × 7 + 8 + 20 × 4 m. The superstructure incorporates a variety of arch designs, including double-curved arches, box arches, and slab arches. Specifically, spans 1–5 and 15–18 are constructed with double-curved arches, each containing five arch ribs and nine transverse beams; spans 6 and 14 are designed as slab arches; and spans 7–13 utilize box arches. The bridge’s substructure consists of gravity piers.

B. Monitoring scheme

The vibrating wire strain sensors, renowned for their exceptional accuracy, stability, and swift response, are widely employed in diverse engineering applications, particularly within bridge, tunnel, and large-scale building construction projects. These sensors enable precise strain measurements of structures, facilitating the early detection of potential safety hazards and the initiation of remedial actions. Furthermore, they play a pivotal role in monitoring structural deformations, thus providing essential data for structural health monitoring. The BGK-4000 type vibrating wire strain sensor is particularly designed for long-term strain monitoring of concrete infrastructures such as bridges, foundations, piles, and dams. Its operational principle involves a steel wire, fixed in length and tensioned between two end blocks embedded firmly in concrete, as show in Fig. 4. Any deformation in the concrete shifts these blocks relative to each other, altering the wire’s tension and consequently its resonant frequency, which is then measured to assess concrete deformations.

In this project, BGK-4000 type vibrating wire strain sensors were deployed at the crown of the bridge arches. Specifically, 9 sensors were installed on the 20-meter span arch, 3 sensors on the 8-meter span arch, and 18 sensors on the 75-meter span arch, totaling 222 sensors, show in Fig. 4. Continuous monitoring of the bridge strain was conducted from February 2019 to October 2021, spanning 32 months.

The real-world dataset originates from the Dongjiang Bridge, a concrete arch bridge with 18 spans (including double-curved, slab, and box arches) in Huizhou, China. Monitoring spanned 32 months (February 2019–October 2021) using 222 BGK-4000 vibrating wire strain sensors installed at arch crowns. The sampling interval captured long-term strain trends, with data preprocessed via Kalman filtering to reduce noise. Environmental factors (temperature, humidity) and traffic loads influenced strain dynamics, but missing data primarily arose from random sensor/transmission faults (MCAR), unrelated to these factors.

C. Data missing experiment

(1) Data missing mechanism38.

Missing Completely at Random (MCAR): In the monitoring of bridge strain, data may occasionally be missing in a manner that is unrelated to any specific attributes of the monitoring data, such as the bridge’s strain or displacement. For instance, incidental equipment failures, such as battery depletion, can occasionally lead to data loss that is not inherently linked to the bridge’s operational status. In such cases, the lack of data does not introduce a systematic bias into the analysis outcomes, as it is an extrinsic factor rather than a reflection of the bridge’s condition.Missing at Random (MAR): Bridge monitoring datasets can exhibit randomly missing data when the gaps are linked to observable factors, not the unrecorded data. D. Missing data imputation

Bridge monitoring data is inherently unstable, nonlinear, and voluminous. In response to the issue of missing data in bridge monitoring, this study presents a CCF-BiGRU data imputation model that capitalizes on the spatiotemporal correlations inherent in the monitoring data. The missing data imputation flow chart show in Fig. 6 and the specific data recovery process is outlined as follows:

(1) Apply Kalman filtering to the time series data and normalize the filtered data;

(2) Utilize the Cross-Correlation Function (CCF) to identify the sensor monitoring data that shows the highest correlation with the target sensor data needing recovery;

(3) Split the referenced sensor data into training and test sets; the former for model development and the latter to evaluate the model’s generalization capability;

(4) Employ a sliding window approach to divide the time series data into training samples;

(5) Process these samples with randomly missing data and label the missing instances to fully capitalize on the spatiotemporal correlations during model training and data recovery;

(6) Construct a BiGRU neural network model to learn the spatiotemporal correlations within the data;

(7) Input the test set into the established data recovery model to reconstruct the missing data and evaluate the model’s ability to generalize;

(8) Assess the performance of the recovery model.

The state transition matrix A describes the change in the system’s state at each time step. In bridge monitoring, state changes are often minimal, and the state transition matrix is typically approximated as the identity matrix. The control matrix B accounts for the impact of control inputs on the system’s state. For bridge monitoring, there are usually no explicit control inputs, so this term can be neglected. The process noise covariance matrix Q represents the uncertainty in the system model. When estimating Q, it can be based on the range of state variations observed in the data. In this article, a small noise covariance value of 0.0001 is chosen. The observation matrix H describes how state variables map to observed values. For bridge monitoring, strain sensors directly measure state variables, making the observation matrix a unit matrix in this context. The observation noise covariance matrix R reflects the noise in the measurement process. This article selects R as 0.001 based on the sensor’s measurement accuracy. The initial state covariance matrix P represents the uncertainty in the initial state estimate. It is commonly chosen with a large initial value to account for the uncertainty. In this case, P is set to 1. Using these parameters and formulas, the Kalman filter effectively processes bridge monitoring data, removing noise, and enhancing data accuracy and reliability. These parameter choices are based on the specific characteristics of bridge monitoring data, ensuring the model’s performance in practical applications.

2) Cross-Correlation Function.

The cross-correlation function (CCF) is employed to assess the time-lag correlation between different sensors, which is a crucial step in spatial-temporal data interpolation. When selecting specific parameters for the CCF algorithm, the following aspects are primarily considered. Time window length: The length of the time window influences both the precision of correlation computation and the computational load. A Duration of 180 days is chosen in this study for the time window length. This Duration is sufficiently long to capture the primary features of the signal without excessively increasing computational complexity. Based on experimental analyses of bridge strain monitoring data, an optimal time delay of 7 days is assumed (reflecting weekly periodic changes). Normalization: During the calculation of cross-correlation, normalization is performed to ensure that the correlation values fall within the range of [−1, 1], facilitating comparison among data from different sensors. These parameters effectively identify the sensor monitoring data with the highest correlation to the target sensor’s data, thereby ensuring the accuracy and reliability of data interpolation.

Conclusion

Structural health monitoring of bridges is a crucial practice to ensure their normal operation and mitigate potential risks. However, in practical engineering scenarios, missing monitoring data frequently pose a challenge. This paper introduces a CCF-BiGRU bridge strain monitoring data imputation model, which leverages the cross-correlation algorithm to account for spatial correlations among monitoring data, enhanced by the predictive capabilities of the BiGRU algorithm. Through a case study on bridge strain monitoring, the effectiveness of this method was evaluated by conducting experiments with 5%−20% completely random data missing:

(1) Relative to the BiGRU, BiLSTM, GRU, and LSTM models, the CCF-BiGRU and CCF- BiLSTM model exhibits significant improvements across several metrics, achieving an optimal mean root mean square error of 4.83 and 4.85, correlation coefficient of 0.987 and 0.986, and relative accuracy of 0.42 and 0.45. Furthermore, as the volume of missing data increases, the performance metrics of the CCF-BiGRU and CCF- BiLSTM model demonstrate greater stability. Although CCF-BiLSTM model shows similar interpolation performance, its computational cost is much higher.

(2) In terms of data imputation, the CCF-BiGRU model outperforms the others. While the BiGRU, BiLSTM and GRU models possess some data imputation capabilities, the unimproved LSTM model struggles with randomly missing data, particularly at higher volumes, resulting in highly unstable imputation performance.

(3) The CCF-BiGRU model introduced in this paper is adept at accurately imputing missing monitoring data, showcasing a uniform and stable dispersion of data completion errors throughout the time series analysis.

(4) Unlike traditional methods that primarily focus on temporal correlations, the CCF-BiGRU model effectively integrates both spatial and temporal correlations. This dual approach allows for more accurate and reliable data imputation, addressing a critical gap in current methodologies.

(5) By accurately imputing missing monitoring data, the CCF-BiGRU model contributes to more reliable structural health monitoring. This advancement can lead to early detection of potential issues, timely maintenance, and improved overall safety and operational efficiency of bridge structures.

The CCF-BiGRU model addresses gaps in existing methods, providing a comprehensive solution for handling missing data in structural health monitoring. By integrating spatial correlations through the CCF and leveraging the predictive capabilities of the BiGRU algorithm, the model makes significant contributions to data imputation accuracy and reliability. This model can be integrated into real-time monitoring systems for continuous health assessments, preventive maintenance planning, risk assessment, and decision-making frameworks, significantly improving bridge safety and operational efficiency.

(1) Real-Time Monitoring and Alerts: The CCF-BiGRU model can be integrated into real-time bridge monitoring systems to provide continuous health assessments. By imputing missing data, the model ensures that gaps in data do not hinder the monitoring process, allowing for timely detection of anomalies and the generation of alerts for maintenance teams.

(2) Preventive Maintenance Planning: By providing accurate and complete datasets, the model enables more reliable trend analysis and condition assessment. Maintenance teams can use this information to plan preventive maintenance activities more effectively, addressing potential issues before they escalate into critical problems.

(3) Risk Assessment and Management: The CCF-BiGRU model can be utilized to enhance risk assessment frameworks by ensuring the availability of complete and accurate monitoring data. This comprehensive data can be used to assess the current risk levels of bridge structures more accurately, informing decision-makers about necessary interventions and resource allocation.

(4) Integration with Decision-Making Frameworks: The model’s output can be fed into decision-support systems used by bridge engineers and managers. These systems can leverage the imputed data to make informed decisions about maintenance priorities, budgeting, and long-term asset management strategies.

The application potential of the CCF-BiGRU model is substantial, offering significant enhancements to the effectiveness of structural health monitoring systems and ultimately improving bridge safety and operational efficiency. Despite the notable advantages of the CCF-BiGRU model in data imputation, there are inherent limitations. Real-world bridge monitoring faces three missing data types: intermittent failures (MCAR) with no structural link, where the CCF-BiGRU shows strong robustness (validated for 5%−20% gaps); prolonged outages (MAR) tied to observable factors, where the model works for short gaps but may degrade for long (> 30 days) outages; and systematic errors (NMAR) linked to unrecorded data, which limit the model as it cannot account for unobserved physical mechanisms driving missingness. Regarding computational scalability, the O(N2×L) complexity of CCF preprocessing, while manageable for our 222-sensor case, may face challenges with large-scale sensor networks (N > 500) due to quadratic growth with sensor count. Practical mitigation strategies include clustering sensors by structural zones to reduce effective N, sliding window segmentation for long sequences L, and threshold-based screening of top-correlated sensors, which balance correlation capture and computational efficiency to enhance scalability for real-world applications. Firstly, the computational complexity is high when processing large-scale datasets. Future research could explore more efficient algorithms or utilize distributed computing techniques to reduce computational costs. Secondly, the current model primarily focuses on strain data, lacking consideration for variations in environmental factors such as temperature and humidity. Incorporating multi-modal data fusion techniques in future work could further enhance the accuracy and reliability of data imputation. Additionally, although the CCF-BiGRU model performs well on specific datasets, its generalization capability needs further validation. Future research should involve testing the model across diverse bridge structures and monitoring datasets to improve its applicability and generalization. Finally, the model is currently designed for offline data imputation. Future work could explore real-time data processing and online learning methods to enhance the real-time monitoring and responsiveness of structural health monitoring systems. By addressing these limitations and incorporating more advanced techniques and methodologies in future research, the CCF-BiGRU model could play a more significant role in bridge structural health monitoring. Continuous improvement and expansion of this work will further enhance bridge safety and operational efficiency, making substantial contributions to the field of structural health monitoring.
