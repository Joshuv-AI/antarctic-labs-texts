Early results of robotic versus uniportal video-assisted thoracic complex segmentectomy: a propensity score-matched study
Authors: Wen-Ruei Tang, Ying-An Chou, Mu-Chou Lin, Ying-Yuan Chen, Wei-Li Huang, Chen-Yu Wu, Chao-Chun Chang, Yi-Ting Yen, Yau-Lin Tseng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/PurposeRobotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS) has increasingly been adopted in thoracic surgery, yet direct comparisons with uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (uVATS) for complex segmentectomy remain limited. We compared early perioperative outcomes between RATS and uVATS for complex pulmonary segmentectomy.MethodsPatients undergoing complex segmentectomy between 2023 and 2025 were reviewed. Complex segmentectomy was defined as resection of any segment other than the bilateral superior segment of the lower lobes, lingular, or superior segment of the left upper lobe. After the RATS learning curve, a 1:1 propensity score matching was performed based on age, sex, ASA classification, BMI, and specific segmentectomy procedures, yielding 81 matched pairs in each group.ResultsRATS was associated with a significantly longer skin-to-skin operative time (133 vs. 92 min; P < 0.001) and higher self-paid costs (USD 5,899 vs. 2,557; P < 0.001). Chest drainage duration and length of hospital stay were comparable between the groups. Major morbidity occurred less frequently in the RATS group (0.0% vs. 7.4%; P = 0.031), although prolonged air leak itself did not significantly differ between groups (0.0% vs. 6.2%; P = 0.063). RATS was also associated with greater N2 nodal dissection and a higher number of lymph nodes retrieved (median 7 vs. 2; P < 0.001). No conversions or in-hospital mortality occurred.ConclusionRATS provides comparable short-term safety for complex segmentectomy. Although it is associated with longer operative time and increased financial burden, RATS may offer potential advantages in reducing major morbidity. These findings support the selective use of RATS for anatomically complex or high-risk cases. Further studies should assess long-term oncologic outcomes and cost-effectiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11701-026-03545-9.Keywords: Propensity score, postoperative complications; Robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS); Segmentectomy; Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS)

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in Taiwan and accounts for a significant percentage of deaths annually worldwide [1, 2]. Alongside advancements in targeted therapy and immunotherapy [3–5], surgical technology has also rapidly evolved from open surgery to minimally invasive surgery (MIS). Since the introduction of video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) in the 1990s [6, 7], various types of MIS have been developed, including uniportal VATS (uVATS), robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS), and even uniportal RATS (uRATS) [8–11]. A growing body of data has shown that their oncological outcomes are comparable to those of open procedures [12–15].

The robotic system has revolutionized surgery in multiple aspects. It enhances visual clarity with a steady camera arm and 10x magnification [16, 17]. Additionally, it prolongs the careers of surgeons by offering an ergonomic platform that filters hand tremors [18, 19]. Most importantly, its arms provide an impressive seven degrees of freedom. Due to these benefits, RATS has gained popularity despite its higher costs. Compared to VATS, several reports have demonstrated its non-inferiority and safety [20–23]. However, most studies focus on lobectomy, bilobectomy, and pneumonectomy, with a few addressing simple anatomical lung resections such as segmentectomy [24, 25]. To the best of our knowledge, no existing evidence directly compares uVATS and RATS in complex segmentectomy.

Methods

Patient enrollment

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary teaching hospital in Taiwan and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of National Cheng Kung University Hospital (IRB No. B-ER-113-200). Since the first VATS procedure in 2007 and the first uVATS procedure in 2015, uVATS has become the mainstream approach for resectable lung cancer in our department. We performed our first RATS procedure in 2015 and completed over 100 RATS procedures by 2023. Consequently, we restricted inclusion to patients who underwent uVATS or RATS segmentectomy on or after January 1, 2023, when both approaches had been fully implemented at our institution.

To minimize the impact of surgeon learning curves, we further restricted the analysis to procedures performed by a surgeon who had already surpassed the learning curve for both uVATS and RATS anatomical segmentectomy. Consistent with prior learning-curve analyses indicating that technical competency is typically achieved after approximately 40–50 cases, we operationally defined proficiency as completion of at least 50 robotic segmentectomies and at least 50 uVATS segmentectomies [26–28]. Most procedures were performed by three experienced thoracic surgeons with assistance from four junior surgeons, and both surgical approaches were conducted by the same operative team. Patients who underwent simple segmentectomy were excluded. Complex segmentectomy was defined as resection of any segment other than the bilateral superior segments of the lower lobes, lingular segment, or superior segment of the left upper lobe [29].

Operative procedures

In uVATS, we typically created a four-centimeter utility port at the 4th or 5th intercostal space (ICS) along the mid-axillary line, adjusted according to the patient’s stature and target lesion location. In RATS, we created four working ports and one assistant port (Fig. 1). The working ports were generally located at the 7th or 8th ICS, depending on the lobe of the lesion. In our practice, ports were placed in a staggered (“zigzag”) configuration — i.e., avoiding insertion through the same intercostal space — to optimize working space and reduce instrument crowding, especially considering the relatively small thoracic dimensions and narrow intercostal intervals commonly encountered in our Asian patient population. The assistant port was triangulated between the two ventral ports. In both approaches, the patient was positioned in the full lateral decubitus position.

Staggered da Vinci Xi ports configuration. A wider horizontal spread of the staggered port configuration is adopted to better accommodate the relatively narrow intercostal spacing typical of Asian thoracic anatomy. A, assistant port; C, camera port

The intersegmental plane was primarily identified using the inflation-deflation method. The use of indocyanine green (ICG) was at the operator’s discretion. Preoperative three-dimensional (3D) vascular reconstruction was not routinely performed and was reserved for selected anatomically challenging cases or suspected vascular variations based on surgeon preference. Generally, a minimum of three mediastinal lymphadenectomy stations was recommended. Specifically, the 2R and 4R stations, as well as the 5 and 6 stations, were usually dissected for upper lobe lesions, whereas the subcarinal region was typically dissected for middle and lower lobe lesions.

Postoperative care

Chest tubes were routinely removed on postoperative day 1 if the following criteria were met: absence of detectable air leak, clear pleural drainage with a volume < 200 mL/day, and full lung expansion on chest radiography. Air leaks were assessed using a conventional analog drainage system rather than a digital drainage device. Routine postoperative care included mucolytic therapy, oral analgesics, incentive spirometry using Triflow®, and a standardized scenario-based postoperative pulmonary rehabilitation program [30].

Outcomes

Conversion was defined as changing from RATS to VATS or thoracotomy in the RATS group and to multiportal VATS or thoracotomy in the uVATS group. Major morbidity included in-hospital mortality, 30-day mortality, prolonged air leak (defined as air leak lasting more than five days [31–33]), new post–chest tube removal pneumothorax (defined as the presence of a pneumothorax that was not visible before chest tube removal [34]), respiratory failure, myocardial infarction, heart failure, deep vein thrombosis, chylothorax, and empyema. Key time intervals included skin-to-skin operative time, chest tube duration and length of hospital stay. Skin-to-skin operative time was defined as the interval from skin incision to completion of wound closure and included robotic docking and setup time in the RATS group.

Total medical cost consisted of National Health Insurance (NHI) coverage and self-paid expenses and was derived from hospital billing data. NHI coverage generally included ward admission, standard medications, routine nursing care, and conventional disposable medical supplies, whereas self-paid expenses included additional surgical materials and perioperative supportive items. For uVATS, self-paid expenses mainly included surgical scalpels, wound protectors, hemostatic agents, and specimen retrieval bags. For RATS, in addition to hemostatic agents and specimen retrieval bags, self-paid expenses primarily consisted of robotic arm sterile drapes and robotic instruments. Reported costs therefore reflected medical charges rather than actual institutional costs. Acquisition and long-term maintenance costs of the robotic platform were not included in the present analysis. All costs were converted using an exchange rate of 1 USD = 30 NTD.

Statistical analysis

Propensity scores (PS) were estimated using stratified multivariable logistic regression models including sex, age, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade, body mass index (BMI), and segmentectomy procedure strata. Propensity score matching was performed using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement with a caliper width of 0.2. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used to assess covariate balance after matching, with an SMD < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance.

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to analyze the data distribution. Non-normally distributed numeric variables are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). In the unmatched cohort, continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. After propensity score matching, paired analyses were performed to account for the matched structure. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and categorical variables were analyzed using McNemar’s test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate.

Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 Windows (IBM SPSS Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and statsmodels 0.13.2 in Python 3.9 environment on Windows 10 with an Intel® Core™ i7-1065G7 CPU (Intel Corp.).

Results

As illustrated in Fig. 2, a total of 957 patients underwent scheduled segmentectomy between January 1, 2023 and June 30, 2025. Among them, 495 were excluded because their operations were performed by surgeons without RATS qualification. An additional 87 cases involving simple segmentectomy were excluded. Seven patients were excluded because the robotic procedures were performed using the da Vinci Si system. Three cases were excluded due to concomitant lobectomy or mediastinal tumor resection, and another three were excluded owing to salvage procedures. Ultimately, 271 patients undergoing uVATS and 91 undergoing RATS were included. Baseline characteristics of the enrolled cohort are provided in the Supplementary Materials. After propensity score matching, 81 patients remained in each group. Propensity score distributions, Love plots, and detailed propensity score model diagnostics are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Perioperative outcomes are presented in Table 2. There were no significant differences in blood loss, conversion rates, chest drainage duration, or length of hospital stay between the two groups. However, RATS complex segmentectomy was associated with a significantly longer operative time (133 vs. 92 min; P < 0.001) and higher total medical cost (P < 0.001). Major morbidity in the uVATS group consisted of five prolonged air leaks and one chylothorax, whereas no major morbidity events occurred in the RATS group. Consequently, the RATS group demonstrated significantly lower rates of major morbidity (0.0% vs. 7.4%; P = 0.031), along with greater N2 nodal dissection and a higher number of lymph nodes retrieved (median: 7 vs. 2; P < 0.001). Discussion

Minimally invasive anatomical lung resection has evolved from multiportal VATS to uVATS and RATS, with emerging interest in uRATS approaches. Previous meta-analyses suggest that uVATS yields perioperative outcomes comparable to multiportal VATS in lung cancer surgery, supporting its adoption as an established minimally invasive platform [35]. Uniportal RATS has also been reported as feasible for a broad spectrum of thoracic procedures, including lung resections [11]. Nevertheless, technical constraints remain, including reduced instrument triangulation when fewer robotic arms are used and a substantial reliance on a well-trained bedside assistant, both of which may limit reproducibility across institutions. From a patient perspective, incision number and access-related discomfort remain important considerations, further motivating comparisons between uVATS and multiportal RATS in anatomically complex resections.

In this propensity score-matched cohort of complex segmentectomy, RATS was associated with significantly longer operative time. Prior comparisons between RATS and uVATS have reported variable findings in operative duration, often influenced by case mix (e.g., lobectomy-dominant cohorts) and institutional experience [24, 36]. In our setting, several factors may have contributed to prolonged operative time in RATS: (i) additional port creation and closure compared with a single uVATS utility incision; (ii) docking and system setup; and (iii) differences in parenchymal dissection tools and team workflow. These considerations may be particularly relevant for complex segmentectomy, which requires precise parenchymal division along intersegmental or intrasegmental planes. In this context, efficient energy devices such as the HARMONIC™ 1100 shears used in our uVATS procedures may facilitate simultaneous tissue transection and hemostasis. By contrast, in RATS, optimized workflow and close coordination between the console surgeon and bedside assistant are essential, particularly when endoscopic staplers are deployed by the bedside assistant rather than through a robotic stapling system, as practiced at our institution.

Higher costs were an expected trade-off of the robotic platform in our cohort, largely driven by robot-related disposable instruments. Our findings align with prior cost analyses showing that RATS is consistently more expensive than VATS, with the cost differential primarily attributable to robotic-specific supplies and capital costs [22, 24, 37, 38]. Notably, our data also distinguish National Health Insurance coverage from self-paid expenses, which is important in Taiwan where reimbursement structures differ substantially from other healthcare systems. Accordingly, cost comparisons should be interpreted within the context of local payment policies and may not be directly generalized across countries. To mitigate expenses, endoscopic staplers were deployed by the bedside assistant rather than using robotic staplers. While this strategy may reduce consumable costs, it increases reliance on the experience of the bedside assistant and close coordination with the console surgeon.

Despite longer operative time and higher cost, RATS demonstrated lower major morbidity and greater lymph node yield without compromising resection margins or conversion rates. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the absolute number of major morbidity events was small, with most events in the uVATS group attributable to prolonged air leak. Notably, prolonged air leak itself did not reach statistical significance between the two groups. Recent evidence has highlighted that prolonged air leak after anatomical segmentectomy is multifactorial and may be associated with patient-related factors, fissure completeness, emphysematous changes, and technical complexity of intersegmental dissection [31, 33]. Gioutsos et al. recently reported several risk factors associated with prolonged air leak after uniportal anatomical segmentectomy, emphasizing the importance of patient selection and fissure characteristics in perioperative outcomes [39]. In addition, the significantly greater extent of N2 station dissection in the RATS group may also have contributed to the higher lymph node yield observed in our cohort, rather than reflecting the surgical platform alone.

Although we matched important perioperative variables including BMI and specific segmentectomy procedures, and postoperative pathological findings showed no significant distribution differences between the RATS and uVATS groups, residual bias may still remain. Important clinical variables such as pulmonary function parameters (DLCO% and FEV1%), smoking exposure quantified by pack-years, inhaler use, COPD/emphysematous changes, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension were not uniformly available in this retrospective cohort and therefore were not incorporated into the propensity score model. Accordingly, the observed difference in major morbidity should be interpreted cautiously.

Nevertheless, our findings remain consistent with prior evidence suggesting that, as robotic programs mature beyond their early adoption phase, perioperative outcomes can equal or surpass VATS outcomes in selected settings [21, 36–38]. Furthermore, improved lymph node assessment with robotic approaches has been reported in several cohorts and may reflect superior visualization and instrument articulation during mediastinal dissection [38, 40]. However, differences in lymph node yield may also have been influenced by surgeon preference, institutional lymphadenectomy practices, and pathological processing rather than the surgical platform alone. Importantly, most published RATS–uVATS comparisons focus on lobectomy or mixed resections; our results extend these observations to complex segmentectomy, a procedure that demands meticulous hilar and parenchymal dissection. Collectively, these findings suggest that the technical advantages of robotic platforms may offer potential perioperative benefits in high-complexity sublobar resections, although confirmation in larger multicenter cohorts with long-term oncologic follow-up is still required.

This study has inherent limitations. First, the retrospective, nonrandomized design introduces potential residual confounding despite propensity score matching. Second, the study reflects single-center practice and surgeon-specific expertise. Most procedures were performed by three experienced thoracic surgeons within the same operative team, which may have improved procedural consistency between approaches but may also limit generalizability to lower-volume settings or institutions during earlier phases of the learning curve. Third, the follow-up is limited to short-term perioperative outcomes; long-term recurrence and survival require further study, particularly as the oncologic adequacy of segmentectomy continues to be refined. Finally, cost analyses are highly sensitive to institutional accounting practices and healthcare insurance structures and should be interpreted cautiously when extrapolated to other healthcare systems.

In conclusion, for complex segmentectomy, RATS was associated with lower major morbidity but at the expense of higher costs and longer operative time compared with uVATS. These results support the selective use of RATS for anatomically complex cases while underscoring the need for workflow optimization and cost-containment strategies, and further multicenter validation.
