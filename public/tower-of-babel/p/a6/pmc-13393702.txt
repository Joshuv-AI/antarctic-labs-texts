Structure-guided computational design of DNA tweezers for predicted recognition of the primary glioblastoma biomarkers S100A4 and midkine
Authors: Hayley Foo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is among the most aggressive malignant brain tumors and is characterized by severe infiltration into surrounding brain tissue, rendering early detection exceedingly difficult with current diagnostic imaging methods. Chosen biomarkers S100A4, with a role in cell motility and tumor metastasis, and midkine (MDK), connected to tumor microenvironment remodeling and expansion, are both linked to GBM's infiltrative proliferation. This study investigates a computational framework for the structure-guided design of DNA tweezer nanostructures, evaluated for predicted interactions with S100A4 and MDK. AlphaFold 3 modeling predicted the three-dimensional structures of eight three-stranded, hinge-scaffolded DNA tweezer candidates. DNA structures and target proteins were paired together in molecular docking simulations conducted with HDOCK, from which docking analyses predicted favorable binding arrangements of the complexes identified. Among the constructs investigated, DT3_8 exhibited the most favorable predicted interaction profile across the integrated computational analyses. Comparative docking against additional S100 family proteins with similar electrostatic characteristics further suggested that the predicted interaction profile of DT3_8 may not be explained solely by nonspecific electrostatic complementarity. Collectively, these findings provide a computational proof of concept for the design of dual-target DNA tweezer nanostructures and establish a foundation for future development and experimental evaluation of DNA-based biosensing platforms for glioblastoma-associated biomarkers.Keywords: Biosensing, DNA tweezers, Nanotechnology, Glioblastoma multiforme, Molecular docking, S100A4, Midkine

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most aggressive and lethal primary malignant brain tumor in adults. Characterized by rapid proliferation and extensive infiltration into surrounding healthy brain tissue [1], GBM is classified as a grade IV astrocytoma with a very poor prognosis. The current standard of care, including surgical resection, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy, remains largely ineffective as the median survival ranges between 15 and 18 months, and the 5-year survival rate is approximately 5–10% [2].

Current imaging-based approaches, including MRI and PET, provide anatomical information but fail to detect early molecular tumorigenic changes [3,4]. GBM exhibits structural heterogeneity and diffuse infiltration in regions inherently resistant to contrast-enhancement, making complete surgical resection nearly impossible [3]. Moreover, imaging modalities, namely, MRI, often struggle to distinguish true tumor progression from pseudoprogression, defined as the phenomenon when treatment combinations such as temozolomide chemotherapy and radiation indirectly and temporarily initiate tumor worsening and radiation necrosis [4]. Thus, a critical diagnostic gap exists between current structural imaging techniques and molecular changes that drive early GBM progression.

Among molecular drivers of GBM invasion, the calcium-binding protein S100A4 (metastasin-1) has emerged as a key biomarker [5]. S100A4 regulates cytoskeletal remodeling, epithelial-mesenchymal transition-like signaling, and metastatic pathways [6]. Elevated S100A4 expression also correlates with enhanced motility, increased metastatic potential, and poor clinical prognosis [6]. Structurally, S100A4 is enriched in lysine and arginine residues, producing a strongly positive electrostatic surface [7]. This charge distribution makes S100A4 an attractive biochemical target for negatively charged nucleic-acid nanostructures.

Midkine (MDK) was also identified as a second biomarker protein associated with GBM's infiltrative nature and a driver of proliferation [8], as evidenced by its extracellular location and function as a secreted protein from an initial UniProt identification. One of its most indicative features is that a surplus of midkine is linked to the mutated EGFRvIII protein, a key genotype that precedes GBM invasion and drives signaling pathways connected to GBM tumor progression [9]. For one, MDK is also a mechanistic catalyst for driving macrophage proliferation and expanding the tumor microenvironment [10] and is inherently positively charged at established core binding residues.

DNA nanotechnology offers a programmable and versatile platform for molecular sensing and targeted therapeutic delivery. DNA tweezers are mechanically responsive nanostructures assembled from synthetic oligonucleotides that transition between open and closed states upon target binding [11]. The negatively charged phosphate backbone of DNA enables electrostatic attraction to positively charged proteins such as S100A4 and MDK. In addition, DNA tweezers, essentially assembled at the nanoscale, can be functionalized with fluorescent reporters or therapeutic payloads, enabling diagnostic and theranostic applications [12].

Among the numerous molecular alterations associated with glioblastoma, S100A4 and midkine (MDK) were selected because they represent complementary drivers of tumor progression and collectively capture key aspects of the invasive glioblastoma phenotype. S100A4 is a calcium-binding protein that promotes cellular migration and epithelial–mesenchymal transition. Its overexpression has been correlated with increased glioblastoma invasiveness, and metastatic behavior, and thus plays a role in poor patient prognosis. Separately, MDK is a heparin-binding growth factor involved in angiogenesis, cell proliferation, and remodeling of the tumor microenvironment, contributing to tumor growth and therapeutic resistance. By simultaneously targeting biomarkers associated with features both in tumor cell invasion and microenvironmental expansion, a dual-target evaluation strategy may provide broader molecular coverage than approaches relying on a single biomarker. These complementary biological roles therefore provide the rationale for selecting S100A4 and MDK as the initial targets for computational DNA nanostructure design.

Developing a molecular-scale sensor that can be predicted to interact with S100A4 and MDK directly addresses the diagnostic shortcomings left by structural imaging. Because S100A4 and midkine overexpression reflect invasive tumor behavior before gross anatomical changes appear and can be tracked radiologically, a nanosensor capable of detecting this protein could provide earlier and biologically meaningful diagnostic information. Accordingly, this study sought to establish computational groundwork defining dual-target DNA tweezer nanostructures, designed and comparatively evaluated for their capability of predicted recognition of S100A4 and MDK. No prior study has computationally designed DNA tweezer nanostructures with a capacity for predicted dual-target molecular interaction with the glioblastoma biomarker proteins S100A4 and midkine with a methodology integrating AlphaFold 3, HDOCK, PLIP, electrostatic analysis, and molecular dynamics simulations.

Methods

The project followed a structured, multi-phase computational outline designed specifically to move from raw, sequenced data to an in silico-predicted protein-DNA nanodevice complex. Each phase built upon the previous one, combining systematic processes such as sequence retrieval, structure prediction, molecular docking, interaction analysis, and molecular dynamics simulations.

Phase I: protein modeling

The S100A4 amino acid sequence, identified as P26447 from the UniProt database [7], was: [MACPLEKALDVMVSTFHKYSGKEGDKFKLNKSELKELLTRELPSFLGKRTDEAAFQKLMSNLDSNRDNEVDFQEYCVFLSCIAMMCNEFFEGFPDKQPRKK] and used as one primary input for all further exploration. The MDK amino acid sequence was also identified similarly:[MQHRGFLLLTLLALLALTSAVAKKKDKVKKGGPGSECAEWAWGPCTPSSKDCGVGFREGTCGAQTQRIRCRVPCNWKKEFGADCKYKFENWGACDGGTGTKVRQGTLKKARYNAQCQETIRVTKPCTPKTKAKAKAKKGKGKD]. AlphaFold 3 was employed to simulate the three-dimensional structures of S100A4 and MDK using the amino acid sequence inputs and would also offer integral information about internally relevant attention mechanisms quantified by pLDDT confidence scores to identify the reliability and solvent accessibility of specific protein regions [13]. The center regions of both S100A4 and MDK, shown in blue, indicate higher confidence in structural prediction (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

Regions with lower confidence were visually inspected and simulated for surface structures and shape in ChimeraX [14] to confirm specific binding pockets identified by P2Rank [15] at a later time to inform blind docking simulations, in which binding sites are not pre-specified, thereby ensuring that docking results are unbiased by externally imposed constraints [16]. The final S100A4 and MDK structures were then exported in CIF format, converted to PDB on ChimeraX, and designated as the receptor for all subsequent docking simulations and phases.

Phase II: DNA tweezer design

A baseline DNA tweezer design consisting of three strands (Fig. 3), DT0, was selected as the starting template for which open- and closed-state configurations could be manually designed.

Schematic representation of DNA tweezer folding, with proposed open and closed states of the theorized DNA tweezer structure, with predicted protein-induced closure, though not explicitly simulated in the present study.

To improve mechanical rigidity and folding stability, the arms of the tweezer were elongated by 1-2 triplets of nucleotides in symmetric terminal nucleotide extensions, generating a set of eight distinct candidate sequences. Each candidate sequence was then input into AlphaFold 3 to predict its three-dimensional conformation. It is well established that the AlphaFold 3 algorithm has not been explicitly optimized for the structure prediction of nucleic acids, but the incorporation of nucleotide-level modeling in the latest version of the software has been explored computationally for the purpose of generating approximated geometries of DNA and identifying gross folding patterns [13]. The present research has employed the tool exclusively for obtaining template structures for the proposed tweezer arms, with further analysis done using specialized algorithms for protein-DNA docking and molecular dynamics simulations (HDOCK, GROMACS). The resulting models were examined for proper folding, symmetry between arms, and any extraneous presence of severe distortions or unintended secondary structures. Essentially, AlphaFold 3 was employed to obtain atomistic structural models that could be directly integrated for comparative computational analysis through the subsequent protein-DNA docking methodology. Candidates that displayed well-formed, stable open conformations with clearly defined arms were retained for docking. These DNA structures were also exported in PDB format for use as ligands in the docking phase.

Phase III: molecular docking & interaction profiling

Protein-DNA molecular docking was performed using HDOCK, a hybrid docking platform that combines template-based modeling with FFT-based global docking [17]. Docking calculations were performed in blind docking mode without predefined binding sites, allowing the algorithm to explore the entire protein surface for potential DNA-binding interfaces. Blind docking was performed using HDOCK's default global search algorithm without defining a docking grid or binding-site constraints. For each protein-DNA pair, HDOCK generated a set of ten predicted docking models representing different possible binding orientations and interfaces. To rank every model, HDOCK reported a docking score that reflected the software's confidence in binding predictions, as well as a ligand RMSD and an overall confidence score. These metrics were collected and compared across all eight DNA tweezer complexes. Throughout this study, HDOCK docking scores were interpreted as relative scoring-function outputs for ranking predicted protein-DNA complexes, as they do not exactly represent quantitative binding free energies.

Complexes with lower (more negative) docking score values were interpreted as having stronger, more favorable interactions [18]. Consistently low ligand RMSD values across the top models for a given tweezer indicated a higher ranking amongst the DNA tweezer candidates. Considering all these aspects, the highest-ranked candidate among both proteins was selected as the lead compound for PLIP analysis and molecular dynamics simulations, based on the most confident docking poses selected for each complex.

To understand the biochemical basis of the docking results, the top-ranked complexes from HDOCK were analyzed using PLIP [19]. For each selected protein-DNA complex, PLIP scanned the interface and identified specific non-covalent, crucial biochemical interactions, including hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, and salt bridges. Hydrogen bonds were evaluated based on donor-acceptor distances and angles, hydrophobic contacts were identified between non-polar residues and nucleobases or sugar-phosphate regions, and salt bridges were detected between positively charged amino acid side chains (such as lysine and arginine) and negatively charged phosphate groups on the DNA backbone. The number, type, and spatial distribution of these interactions were compared across candidates. Complexes with a higher quantity of viable hydrogen bonds and salt bridges, particularly involving S100A4's and MDK's basic residues, respectively, were interpreted as having stronger and more specific electrostatic and structural complementarity based on the extensive hydrophobic interactions between the two subjects within the protein-DT complex candidates.

Phase IV: molecular dynamics simulations

To evaluate the predicted structural stability of the highest-ranked complexes under physiologically representative ionic conditions that approximate the human body, molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS [20]. The selected protein-DNA complex would be placed in a 3D simulated environment with water molecules to mimic an aqueous physiological environment unique to the human body [21]. Appropriate ion concentrations, namely, sodium and chloride, were added to neutralize the system and approximate physiological and naturally occurring ionic strength [22]. Protein-DNA complexes were simulated using the AMBEL99SB-ILDN force field in a dodecahedral solvent box under physiological ionic conditions (0.15 M NaCL) at a temperature of 300K. Details regarding the underlying equilibration protocol, water model, and barostat are managed internally by the computational platform and were not user-configurable. Throughout the simulation, root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) was calculated to track overall structural drift of the complex over time, while root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) was used to measure the flexibility of individual residues and nucleotide positions. Stable RMSD values and low RMSF at the binding interface would indicate that the DNA tweezer maintained its structure and binding interactions with S100A4 and with MDK over time in a water-based environment.

Results

This multi-phase computational methodology generated a comprehensive evaluation of eight select DNA tweezer candidates and their interactions with the S100A4 protein, then sequentially with the midkine protein. Each stage of structure prediction, docking, interaction profiling, and molecular dynamics contributed distinct evidence that converged on candidate DT3_8 as the most viable nanodevice.

Structural modeling of S100A4, MDK, and DNA tweezers

AlphaFold produced a high-confidence structural model of S100A4, with pLDDT values indicating strong reliability across the EF-hand calcium-binding domains and the positively charged C-terminal region. The same result was obtained with AlphaFold's simulation of MDK's structural components. These regions are known to mediate protein-ligand interactions and therefore represent plausible docking interfaces for DNA-based nanostructures. The electrostatic surface map created using ChimeraX revealed that there were positive charges associated with lysine and arginine in S100A4 and MDK (Fig. 4), thereby justifying the proposed binding model of protein to DNA.

For the DNA tweezers, AlphaFold was used as a platform to run approximately 300 trials over the span of this study to computationally determine viable tweezer structures. Eight were completed and aligned with open-state mechanisms of a DNA tweezer (Table 1). AlphaFold predicted stable conformations for all eight candidates, with visualizations of structural confidence in the tweezers’ inherent open states (Fig. 5). However, structural inspection revealed meaningful differences: DT3_8 displayed the most symmetrical arm geometry, minimal strand curvature, and the fewest unintended secondary structures. These features suggested greater mechanical rigidity and more predictable binding behavior, making them strong candidates for subsequent docking analysis, which would be computationally supported in the next phase.

Interaction profiling and biochemical complementarity

Comprehensive interaction profiling using PLIP provided insight into the predicted performance of the DT3_8 construct relative to other candidate DNA tweezers. Quantitative analysis of both complexes formed with DT3_8 revealed a spatially dense and coordinated network of stabilizing intermolecular binding contacts.

Findings from PLIP simulations yielded a higher number of viable convergent hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic salt bridges for DT3_8 (Fig. 8), which suggests that it forms a structurally and chemically complementary binding interface with S100A4 and MDK. Enhanced interface specificity and structural persistence, as determined, are completely consistent with the improved docking scores observed for DT3_8, thereby supporting its prioritization for subsequent molecular dynamics evaluation and future computational and experimental validation.

Functionally, DT3_8's establishment of multiple hydrogen bonds between nucleotide base functional groups and polar residues on the proteins' surface stabilizes the orientation of the tweezer arms around both S100A4 and MDK, thereby reducing rotational freedom at binding faces. The distribution of hydrogen bonds was concentrated in solvent-accessible regions that allowed complementarily favorable donor-acceptor structure accessibility. In addition to hydrogen bonding, the hydrophobic contacts predicted between exposed nucleobase rings and nonpolar surface patches of both biomarkers contributed greatly to increased surface complementarity and partial solvent exclusion in the DT3_8 complex. Although DNA is predominantly hydrophilic due to its phosphate backbone, the aromatic bases can participate in van der Waals interactions when appropriately positioned [24].

These interactions support the proposed role of charge complementarity in stabilizing the initial binding interface. Compared with other candidate constructs, DT3_8 exhibited the highest number of predicted salt bridges, clustered around the positively charged C-terminal region of S100A4 and MDK.

Molecular dynamics and stability in a physiological environment

To assess whether the predicted interactions would remain stable under physiologically relevant conditions, namely, an aqueous environment with physiologically representative sodium chloride ion concentrations, molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS (Fig. 9, Fig. 10). RMSF analysis revealed that the regions of the tweezer directly involved in binding exhibited very low fluctuation, suggesting that the binding interface remained rigid and well-maintained over time. The two complexes, DT3_8/MDK and DT3_8/S100A4, maintained relatively low RMSD values throughout the simulation, indicating that both complexes exhibit minimal structural drift and strong global stability.

Several intermolecular hydrogen bonds identified by PLIP were maintained throughout substantial portions of the simulation trajectory, consistent with the overall stability indicated by the RMSD and RMSF graphical depictions, indicating that the interactions remained stable in a dynamic, water-based environment. This consistency is essential for any diagnostic or theranostic application, as nanodevices must maintain structural integrity in vivo, a complex and dynamic biological environment subject to enzymatic degradation, ionic variation, and protein competition.

Evaluation of binding specificity using comparison proteins

To evaluate whether DT3_8 indicated some preferential predicted interactions with S100A4 and MDK rather than generic electrostatic affinity for positively charged proteins, a cross-reactivity panel was constructed using five structurally related, similarly-charge-enriched proteins: S100A1, S100A6, S100B, and calmodulin. Each protein structure was retrieved from UniProt and modeled using AlphaFold under identical parameters as S100A4.

Docking simulations revealed that DT3_8 displayed substantially more favorable docking score values for S100A4 and MDK than for any other protein in the panel. In contrast, docking to S100A1, S100A6, S100B, and calmodulin resulted in inconsistent orientations and significantly fewer stabilizing interactions, as indicated by comparably reduced docking scores (Table 4).

To further investigate whether the observed docking preferences could be explained solely by electrostatic attraction, electrostatic surface potentials were calculated for S100A4, MDK, S100A1, S100A6, S100B, and calmodulin using APBS and modeled in ChimeraX (Fig. 10). Although all proteins exhibited localized positively charged surface regions capable of interacting with negatively charged DNA, differences in the spatial distribution and continuity of electropositive patches were observed. These findings indicate that electrostatic attraction is likely an important contributor to binding but may not fully account for the preferential docking behavior predicted for DT3_8. Because all proteins in the comparison panel exhibited positively charged surface regions, the observed docking preferences cannot be attributed solely to the presence of positive charge. Instead, the results suggest that surface topology and interface geometry may contribute to the predicted interaction differences. Among the eight candidate DNA tweezers evaluated, DT3_8 demonstrated the most favorable predicted computational performance.

Discussion

The present study stressed that the current investigation is purely computational, and this is an issue of great significance to take into consideration while interpreting the outcomes of the work. The present computational procedure was designed to evaluate the predicted binding interactions between the DNA tweezer constructs and glioblastoma-associated protein biomarkers. The results, thus, aptly demonstrate predicted protein association with modeled structures with features of DNA nanostructures. Although the present study supports the computational feasibility of the proposed DNA tweezer design, it does not constitute a complete biosensor platform. Signal transduction mechanisms, analytical sensitivity, limit-of-detection measurements, and validation using biological samples remain important future steps before clinical or diagnostic applications can be considered. Indeed, the lack of wet lab validation indicates that the estimated binding energies and steric interactions are purely theoretical. Specifically, even though the docking scores reflect the energetic calculations conducted under certain force field assumptions, they are not substituting for the actual measurements of dissociation constants and other parameters.

Although AlphaFold 3 was originally developed for biomolecular structure prediction and was not specifically designed for DNA nanostructure modeling, it was used in the present study to generate initial three-dimensional structural templates compatible with docking analyses. Alternative platforms such as NUPACK, oxDNA, CanDo, and Tiamat specialize in predicting nucleic acid secondary structure, coarse-grained mechanics, and general design, but do not directly provide atomistic coordinates optimized for protein-DNA docking. Consequently, AlphaFold 3 was selected as a practical modeling tool for the present computational screening framework. Nevertheless, the predicted DNA conformations should be interpreted as computational models, awaiting experimental validation. Future studies incorporating specialized DNA nanotechnology software and experimental structural characterization will further strengthen model confidence.

The second thing to note is that the issue of binding kinetics must be considered as well. Here, equilibrium docking energies are used as a stand-in for the true values, but Kon and Koff must be considered when considering biosensor capabilities. It is entirely possible for an affinity-based reaction between biomolecules to have such low rates of association that it cannot function properly in clinical settings, while having a high rate of dissociation will make signal production difficult. In the future, free energy landscapes might be calculated using steered molecular dynamics or umbrella sampling techniques. The present study employed a single standardized computational docking session for each DNA tweezer candidate to enable a relative comparison of predicted interaction scores. Although this approach facilitated systematic ranking of the candidate structures, future studies incorporating replicate docking calculations, alternative docking algorithms, and statistical evaluation will further strengthen the robustness of the computational predictions.

Further, this study's results depend on parameters such as electrostatic and docking interactions as surrogates for specificity. Real biological conditions add a layer of complexity since nucleases might cleave naked DNA; serum components might interact non-specifically with the tweezer; and the host immune system might interfere with any non-native nucleic acid. These aspects, which were not explored using the simulation models in the study, need to be further studied using cellular and ultimately in vivo systems. Phosphorothioate modifications or 2′-O-methylation to the DNA sugar-phosphate backbone is a common way of protecting against nuclease digestion.

It is also critical to position the work within current biosensor and aptamer technology paradigms. SELEX-derived aptamers against diverse cancer markers have been synthesized and exhibit specificity and high affinity, although the SELEX method itself relies on extensive empirical screening and does not allow programmability for multiple targets [25]. Electrochemical or fluorescent sensors have reached femtomolar sensitivity levels for protein targets but rely on single-target architectures and antibody conjugation, increasing production costs and instability [25]. In contrast, DNA tweezers provide a promising platform that is programmable, modular, and inherently able to undergo conformational switching in response to analyte binding, thus making antibody conjugation unnecessary. While no experimental affinity data are currently available for the candidate tweezers presented here, the rational design strategy provides a strong computational basis to position DT3_8 as an ideal target for SELEX optimization or hybridization studies.

The other limitation is that there are only eight tweezer sequences analyzed, thus constituting an insufficient sample size from the immense space of possible sequences when considering three-stranded hinge scaffolds. Although the systematic elongation approach involving one and two nucleotide extensions from the DT0 scaffold was a sensible choice, this does not mean there could be no better tweezers with different combinations of sequences and lengths of arms. While DT3_8 demonstrated the most favorable predicted interaction profile among the candidates examined, the vast sequence and structural design space of DNA nanostructures precludes identifying a globally optimal construct from this limited set. A more extensive computer-based search, which may involve machine learning techniques based on binding affinity data, would help find the best tweezers beforehand. Future studies incorporating high-throughput computational screening, machine learning-assisted sequence optimization, genetic algorithms, or evolutionary design strategies may enable systematic exploration of substantially larger DNA nanostructure libraries and facilitate the identification of optimized biomarker-responsive architectures.

Recent advances in nanomedicine have increasingly shifted towards biomarker-responsive systems capable of exploiting disease-associated molecular signatures for selective recognition and targeted delivery. These platforms incorporate mechanisms that substantially improve targeting precision while reducing nonspecific interactions, with molecular recognition elements that respond to specific proteins, receptors, or extracellular biomarkers enriched within pathological microenvironments. Accordingly, nanoscale biosensing and targeting strategies have expanded beyond conventional nanoparticle systems to include programmable metal-based nanocomposites capable of integrating multiple biological recognition mechanisms. The UiO-66 metal-organic framework developed for receptor-mediated recognition fitted to an acidic tumor microenvironment, for instance Ref. [26], illustrated how the engineering of nanoscale complexes can exploit multiple biological characteristics simultaneously to improve targeting specificity. Nanoscale engineering also yielded a major discovery within hyaluronic acid-decorated liposomes, which exploited CD44 receptor overexpression to achieve drug delivery to colorectal cancer cells [27], demonstrating that work with nanomaterials can similarly establish biomarker-responsive nanoplatforms as a viable strategy for precision medicine. Within this context, DNA nanostructures represent a complementary class of programmable nanomaterials whose sequence-defined makeup enables precise structural engineering and computational prediction of protein-DNA interactions. The present study extends this design by establishing a computational workflow for the investigation and comparative evaluation of DNA tweezer nanostructures predicted to interact with two complementary glioblastoma biomarkers, providing a foundation for future experimental biosensor development. Although these findings remain computational predictions requiring experimental validation, they establish a proof of concept for applying structure-guided computational design to the development of biomarker-responsive DNA nanodevices.

Strengths of this study include the integration of multiple complementary computational approaches in a sequential arrangement that commenced with AlphaFold 3 structural prediction, then moved to HDOCK docking, PLIP interaction analysis, molecular dynamics simulations, and electrostatic surface mapping. This consistent computational system efficiently evaluated candidate DNA nanostructures. In addition, dual targeting of S100A4 and MDK addresses biomarker heterogeneity in glioblastoma and provides a rigorous framework for future DNA nanodevice development.

Novelty

While DNA nanodevices have been explored for sensing small molecules, ions, and nucleic acids [28], there are currently no published studies that design DNA tweezers to detect S100A4, MDK, or any other glioblastoma-associated protein. Existing GBM research focuses largely on imaging-based diagnostics, radiomics, or nanoparticle-based contrast agents [28], none of which operate at the molecular scale to specifically recognize a stimulus. Likewise, although S100A4 and midkine are well-established as markers of GBM invasiveness [5,6,8], no work so far has combined these biomarkers with DNA nanotechnology to create a molecular biosensor.

This study fills this gap by computationally designing and evaluating DNA tweezers that are predicted to interact favorably with S100A4 and midkine in independent docking event analyses, with a clear framework integrating structure prediction, molecular-level docking, and molecular dynamics simulations into a single study to rationally confirm a single candidate for glioblastoma recognition. This approach is novel because it brings together DNA nanotechnology, two clinically relevant GBM biomarkers, and a systematic, dual-test-based computational design strategy that has not been applied in this context before.

Selectivity and cross-reactivity analysis

While the current assessment continues to be computational in nature, the fact that decreased structural fluctuation and increased numbers of hydrogen bonding and salt bridges are observed uniquely in the DT3_8/S100A4 and DT3_8/MDK complexes supports the possibility that the binding process may also involve geometric and biochemical compatibility at the protein-DNA interface, beyond solely electrostatic attraction. Notably, the reduced interactions with other positively charged proteins suggest that the mere presence of charge may not account for the behavior predicted for DT3_8.

Experimental verification will be necessary, however, to conclusively prove that selectivity is taking place. As such, future studies must involve competition assays using fluorescence or surface plasmon resonance (SPR) or microscale thermophoresis (MST) approaches between DT3_8 and its target proteins from the same cross-reactivity panel. The results will provide a means to determine the dissociation constant, kinetic rates of association, and selectivity ratio between DT3_8 and each target protein.

Challenges

This study was entirely computational, and experimental validation of predicted binding interactions has not yet been performed. Further, the DNA tweezer constructs’ nucleotide sequences were determined and evaluated in silico. Their physiological stability, structural integrity, and binding affinities still require experimental confirmation. For instance, the molecular dynamics conducted in this study was only an approximate simulation of the human body environment. It is immensely difficult to account for all extraneous variables and their unpredictable effects on the structure and stability of the DT3_8-protein complex.

Another issue that needs to be addressed is related to statistics. Scores and confidence levels provided here are based on the results of the best pose calculated by HDOCK and are not subjected to statistical error estimation and calculation of confidence intervals and/or replicate docking. Since no systematic reproducibility experiment was conducted to prove how variable the results might be, it is impossible to estimate how much variance the scoring function contains. In future studies, each complex must be docked several times independently, and then the mean score with the standard deviation can be obtained and compared using statistical tests for statistical significance. The author acknowledges that the current experiment is lacking in aspects like statistical sampling, confidence intervals, and replication experiments to enhance quantitative evaluation of docking reproducibility.

The following is a suggested protocol for validating the effectiveness of DT3_8 through an experiment in three steps. Firstly, the construction of the tweezer through chemical synthesis would be done through conventional methods of solid-phase oligonucleotide synthesis. This will be followed by the analysis of the product through native PAGE to determine whether it has folded correctly and achieved the right open-state configuration. Secondly, binding with recombinant S100A4 and MDK proteins will be analyzed through the FRET method, whereby fluorophore and quencher will be attached to opposite ends of the tweezer arms such that binding with the target proteins will cause a signal change due to closure of the tweezer arms. Thirdly, predicted selectivity would be experimentally evaluated by repeating the same FRET method with the panel proteins (S100A1, S100A6, S100B, calmodulin). Equilibrium dissociation constant Kd values would then be experimentally quantified using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) or microscale thermophoresis (MST) methods. In the same way that docking analyses for cross-reactivities serve as initial indicators of selectivity, experimental specificity measurements should be obtained to make conclusions about biomarker identification.

Future directions

Several avenues of future research could build on the findings of this study and advance the DT3_8 construct toward real-world applications. First, wet-lab synthesis and fluorescence-based detection assays are essential for validating computational predictions [28]. Experimental testing would determine whether target binding induces a conformational transition capable of producing a measurable fluorescent signal. The second important issue, related to the presence of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), is the need to optimize the CNS delivery for the DNA-based nanodevice in question. The blood-brain barrier presents a significant impediment for large, hydrophilic substances to pass into the central nervous system, hindering the passive delivery of the DNA nanostructure DT3_8. Moreover, there are limitations in terms of DNA being prone to enzymatic degradation, fast systemic clearance, and low circulation stability [2]. There are several potential ways to overcome the issue, among which are receptor-mediated transcytosis using transferrin receptors or low-density lipoprotein receptors, intranasal delivery as a method that does not completely rely on crossing the BBB, focused ultrasound technology, capable of transient BBB opening, and delivery within the lipid nanoparticles or polymer nanoparticles specially targeted to cross the BBB [29,30]. Further optimization of DT3_8 might include PEGylation or conjugation with targeting ligands. It is also critical that such delivery systems should retain the conformation-dependent sensitivity and selective affinity of the DNA tweezer structure, while reducing immune reactions and nonspecific targeting. Accordingly, further experiments using BBB models in vitro and animals will be required to verify whether the DT3_8 system could deliver enough analytes to intracranial tumors for diagnostic purposes. In addition, due to the programmable design of DNA-based technology, DT3_8 could potentially be integrated into multi-functional or multi-parametric nanodevices for simultaneous biomarker detection and drug delivery applications [31].
