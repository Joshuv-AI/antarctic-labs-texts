Mattress impact on lifestyle and back pain: a 25-year orthopedic literature review guideline
Authors: Caggiari Gianfilippo, Petrucci Filippo, Pescia Stefano, Arbau Alessandro, Fidanza Andrea, Ciatti Corrado, Puddu Leonardo, Delrio Giulia, Caria Elisa, Pinna Ilaria, Doria Carlo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep quality is influenced by several modifiable environmental factors, including the sleep surface. Low back pain, morning stiffness, shoulder discomfort, and muscle tightness on awakening may indicate inadequate bedding support. This systematic review evaluated whether mattress characteristics influence spinal alignment, sleep quality, and low back pain.MethodsA systematic literature search and qualitative synthesis were conducted for studies published from 1999 to 2023. PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Scopus, EMBASE, PsycINFO, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar were searched using terms related to mattress ergonomics, pain, vertebral-column alignment, sleep, and quality of life. Eligible studies evaluated adult or young-adult populations and reported outcomes related to low back pain, spinal alignment, pressure distribution, sleep comfort, or sleep quality. Methodological quality and risk of bias were appraised using literature evaluation and grading of evidence (LEGEND) and Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) tools.ResultsA total of 47 studies were included. The evidence most consistently favored medium-firm mattresses over very firm or very soft surfaces. Medium-firm support was associated with better pressure distribution, improved sleep quality, reduced morning stiffness, and low back pain intensity. Zonal support, air-adjustable systems, customized designs, and thermal microclimate optimization showed potential benefits, but heterogeneity in materials, outcome measures, follow-up duration, and participant characteristics precluded meta-analysis.ConclusionsMedium-firm mattresses represent the most defensible general recommendation for adults with nonspecific low back pain or morning stiffness. Mattress selection should also consider body habitus, age, sex-related anthropometry, preferred sleep position, and thermal comfort. Smart and adaptive mattress systems are promising but require larger, methodologically rigorous clinical trials before routine recommendation.Level of evidenceLevel III according to the Oxford 2011 Levels of Evidence.Review registrationNot registered.Trial registrationNot applicable.

Introduction

Sleep occupies approximately one-third of the human daily routine [1] and is essential for overall health, recovery, and daytime functioning [2]. Adequate, uninterrupted sleep supports metabolic regulation, neurocognitive recovery, immune function, hormonal release, and emotional balance [3, 4]. Although 8 hours of sleep is commonly recommended for adults, many individuals do not consistently achieve this duration because of occupational demands, shift work, lifestyle habits, stress, and environmental factors [5, 6]. Chronic sleep deficiency is associated with reduced quality of life and increased risk of metabolic, cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, and neurological disorders [7]. Therefore, the identification of modifiable factors that can improve sleep quality has relevant clinical and public-health implications.

The sleep surface is one of the most accessible and potentially modifiable components of the sleep environment. Mattress selection is often guided by subjective impressions of firmness, comfort, commercial claims, or the generic label of an “orthopedic mattress.” However, this label is not standardized and does not by itself guarantee biomechanical effectiveness or clinical benefit. Mattress characteristics may influence sleep quality through pressure distribution at the body–mattress interface, maintenance of neutral spinal alignment, support of the shoulder and pelvic girdle, facilitation of nocturnal movements, and regulation of the bed microclimate [8–13]. Inadequate bedding support may contribute to discomfort, lumbar pain, shoulder stiffness, muscle contracture, and perceived non-restorative sleep, particularly in individuals with low back pain, altered spinal mobility, or increased pressure sensitivity [14, 15].

The relationship between bedding support and low back pain is complex because it depends not only on mattress firmness but also on anthropometric characteristics, preferred sleeping position, age, sex-related body-shape differences, material properties, pillow–mattress interaction, and thermal comfort. Very firm surfaces may increase pressure peaks at the shoulders, pelvis, and sacral region, whereas excessively soft surfaces may allow the pelvis or trunk to sink, potentially disturbing neutral spinal alignment. Between these extremes, medium-firm support has been proposed as the most reasonable compromise between pressure relief and structural stability.

This systematic review was designed to answer the following PICO-framed question: in adults or young adults exposed to different mattress characteristics, compared with alternative sleep surfaces or personal bedding, which mattress features are associated with improved low back pain, spinal alignment, pressure distribution, and sleep quality? The objective was to synthesize the available orthopedic, biomechanical, ergonomic, and sleep-science evidence from 1999 to 2023 and provide clinically useful guidance for healthcare professionals advising patients with pain on awakening or sleep-related spinal discomfort.

Methods

This systematic review with qualitative synthesis included studies published from 1999 to 2023. The search covered publications available through 31 December 2023. Searches were performed in PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Scopus, EMBASE, PsycINFO, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar. Mendeley was used only as a reference-management tool and not as a bibliographic database. The search strategy was adapted to the indexing rules and syntax of each database. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were applied where appropriate, particularly for PubMed-based searches, and equivalent free-text terms were used in databases that do not support MeSH indexing.

The search terms included combinations of “mattress and ergonomics,” “mattress and pain,” “mattress and vertebral column,” “mattress and sleep,” and “mattress and quality of life.” Additional articles were identified by screening similar-article functions and reference lists of relevant publications. The search was designed to capture clinical, biomechanical, ergonomic, and sleep-science studies addressing the relationship between mattress characteristics and spinal or sleep outcomes.

Initial screening was guided by predefined eligibility criteria. Studies were eligible if they were written in English, published between 1999 and 2023, included adult or predominantly young-adult populations, and addressed low back pain, spinal alignment, sleep comfort, sleep quality, pressure distribution, or mattress-related biomechanical outcomes. Studies were excluded when the full text was unavailable, the publication date fell outside the defined period, the population was primarily pediatric or affected by conditions unrelated to the review focus, or the paper addressed topics not relevant to spinal alignment or low back pain. Papers focused exclusively on anti-pressure sore mattresses, ulcer prevention, humidity, ambient temperature, or isolated sleep-cycle measurements without spinal or musculoskeletal relevance were also excluded.

Methodological quality was assessed using the literature evaluation and grading of evidence (LEGEND) framework, while risk of bias was evaluated using Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) tools when applicable. Overall, methodological quality ranged from low to moderate. The most frequent limitations were small sample size, short follow-up, lack of blinding, heterogeneous mattress definitions, variable outcome measures, and the use of healthy volunteers rather than symptomatic populations.

After title and abstract screening, 479 potentially relevant papers were identified. Of these, 268 were excluded because they were duplicates or were considered irrelevant to the review question; including studies on unrelated populations such as rugby athletes, children, or pregnant women; and studies focused on unrelated outcomes such as mortality, allergies, rheumatologic conditions, or pressure ulcers. After abstract review, 103 additional papers were excluded. Of the 108 articles selected for full-text assessment, 61 were excluded because they did not directly address low back pain, spinal alignment, or clinically relevant sleep-surface characteristics. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 47 articles were included in the qualitative synthesis.

Results

A total of 47 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review (Table 1). The analysis showed a consistent relationship between mattress characteristics, spinal alignment, perceived comfort, and low back pain. The evidence can be grouped into three domains: firmness level and mechanical support; pressure distribution and individualized or zonal systems; and mattress microclimate and technological innovation.

Across the 25-year period, the most consistent clinical finding was the superiority of medium-firm mattresses over very firm or very soft alternatives. Kovacs et al. [21], in a large randomized, double-blind, controlled multicenter trial, demonstrated that medium-firm mattresses were associated with greater improvement in chronic nonspecific low back pain and disability than firm mattresses. Similarly, Jacobson et al. [20] reported improved sleep quality and reduced back pain after the introduction of medium-firm bedding systems. These findings indicate that a mattress should not simply be hard to be clinically useful; rather, it should provide enough compliance to reduce pressure peaks while maintaining sufficient resistance to preserve spinal alignment.

Biomechanical and ergonomic studies provide a plausible explanation for this observation. Hard surfaces may increase focal pressure over the scapular, pelvic, and sacral regions, while excessively soft surfaces may permit excessive sinking of the pelvis or trunk. Medium-firm configurations appear to offer a better balance between pressure redistribution and preservation of lumbar lordosis or neutral lateral alignment [22, 23]. Several studies using body-pressure mapping, spinal-curvature assessment, electromyography, or finite-element approaches supported the importance of interface pressure and spinal geometry in determining comfort and musculoskeletal symptoms [15, 23, 25, 28, 44, 46, 56, 61].

Specialized overlay systems, air-filled mattresses, zonal support, and customized designs showed potential benefits in selected contexts. Air-adjustable or active-control systems were reported to reduce pain, improve sleep quality, or improve spinal alignment in some studies [18, 24, 58, 60]. Zonal systems, including mattresses with variable stiffness or different support areas for the shoulders, waist, and pelvis, may be clinically relevant because the human body does not load the mattress uniformly. Studies evaluating customized or adaptive support suggested that tailored systems may improve comfort and spinal alignment compared with uniform surfaces [26, 27, 52, 54, 59].

Material composition also influenced outcomes. Latex and selected foam mattresses were often associated with improved pressure distribution and perceived comfort, whereas spring systems provided robust mechanical support and may facilitate changes in position during sleep [30, 36, 41]. However, the available evidence does not support the superiority of one material in all patients. The clinical effect depends on the interaction between material density, surface design, body weight, sleeping posture, and user preference. Mattress material should therefore be interpreted together with firmness and support geometry rather than as an isolated determinant of efficacy.

The mattress microclimate emerged as a relevant but sometimes underappreciated factor. Studies on temperature regulation and thermal comfort indicated that skin temperature, heat capacity, and the ability of the bed system to facilitate thermoregulation may influence sleep depth and continuity [29, 37, 47, 63]. High-heat-capacity mattresses and temperature-control strategies have been associated with changes in slow-wave sleep and subjective sleep comfort, suggesting that restorative sleep depends not only on spinal support but also on thermal interaction between the body and the sleep surface.

Within the prespecified 1999–2023 search window, smart- and active-control mattress systems were identified as emerging technologies capable of adapting support to the user’s posture or pressure distribution [4, 11, 58, 60]. These systems may be relevant for patients whose pain varies according to position or whose anthropometric characteristics make standard mattress recommendations less reliable. However, large-scale clinical validation in patients with chronic low back pain remains limited. A 2025 review on home sleep-monitoring technologies was not included among the 47 studies because it fell outside the prespecified search window; it was retained only as contextual evidence on recent developments in smartphone applications, smartwatches, and smart mattresses [49].

Discussion

The findings of this 25-year systematic review converge on the principle that mattress selection is a clinically relevant determinant of sleep quality and spinal health. The traditional recommendation of an “orthopedic” or very firm mattress is not supported by the available evidence as a universal rule. Instead, literature most consistently supports medium-firm mattresses as the best general compromise between pressure relief and structural support [20, 21, 48]. This conclusion is important because many patients still interpret firmness as equivalent to therapeutic efficacy, whereas the data indicate that excessive firmness may be as problematic as excessive softness.

The primary mechanism through which mattress firmness influences low back pain is the maintenance of neutral spinal alignment during sleep. In the lateral decubitus position, the shoulders and pelvis must sink enough to avoid high-pressure peaks but not so much that the spine assumes a curved or rotated posture. In the supine position, the mattress must support the lumbar region while allowing distributed contact across the trunk and lower limbs. Studies using spinal-curvature measurements, pressure mapping, and computational models indicate that inappropriate support may increase intervertebral stress, muscle activity, and stiffness on awakening [23, 27, 52]. The clinical superiority of medium-firm mattresses observed in controlled trials is therefore biomechanically plausible.

A key point for clinical practice is that mattress efficacy is not independent of individual patient characteristics. Body mass index, body shape, sex-related pelvic and shoulder morphology, age, and preferred sleeping position modify the interaction between the body and the mattress. Individuals with higher body mass may experience excessive sinking even on surfaces perceived as medium firm, while underweight individuals may experience localized pressure pain on surfaces that are well tolerated by others. Female users may have different pressure-distribution requirements because of pelvic morphology, and older adults may need additional pressure relief because of skin fragility, joint stiffness, and reduced mobility. These factors explain why a single mattress type cannot be prescribed with absolute certainty for all patients.

The evidence on zonal, air-adjustable, and customized mattresses is promising because these systems attempt to adapt support to regional anatomical needs. Mattresses with differentiated support zones may better accommodate the shoulders, waist, and pelvis, while active-control systems may adapt to postural changes during the night. Such technologies are conceptually attractive for patients with recurrent low back pain, restless sleep, or marked anthropometric asymmetry. Nevertheless, the clinical evidence remains preliminary, and many studies have small samples, short follow-up, or healthy volunteer populations. Future trials should include symptomatic patients, standardized pain and disability outcomes, longer follow-up, and clear reporting of mattress properties.

Thermal microclimate should also be integrated into mattress recommendations. A sleep surface that provides appropriate spinal support but retains excessive heat may still impair sleep quality. Conversely, materials that facilitate heat exchange and maintain a comfortable skin temperature may improve sleep continuity and slow-wave sleep [29, 37, 47]. This aspect is especially relevant in older adults, individuals with insomnia symptoms, and patients whose pain perception is amplified by fragmented sleep. The best mattress should therefore be considered as a biomechanical and thermoregulatory system, not merely as a support surface.

This review has several limitations. First, studies differed substantially in design, sample size, mattress classification, intervention duration, and outcome measures. Second, many studies used subjective comfort scores, which are clinically meaningful but difficult to compare across protocols. Third, mattress firmness was often described using commercial or qualitative terminology rather than standardized mechanical measurements. Fourth, several studies evaluated healthy participants, limiting direct generalizability to patients with chronic low back pain. Fifth, blinding is difficult in mattress trials because participants may perceive surface differences, which may influence subjective outcomes. Finally, the review was not prospectively registered, and this should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Despite these limitations, the evidence is sufficiently consistent to support practical recommendations. For adults with nonspecific low back pain or morning stiffness, a medium-firm mattress should be considered the most reasonable initial choice. The mattress should maintain neutral spinal alignment, distribute pressure over the shoulders and pelvis, facilitate position changes, and provide a comfortable thermal microclimate. In patients with atypical body habitus, persistent symptoms, older age, or specific pressure intolerance, zoned or adjustable systems may be considered, although the recommendation should remain individualized and cautious. Smart mattresses and artificial-intelligence-driven surfaces represent a promising field but should not yet replace clinically validated principles of support, pressure redistribution, and patient-specific assessment.

Future research should prioritize high-quality randomized controlled trials with standardized definitions of mattress firmness, objective measurements of body pressure and spinal alignment, validated pain and disability scales, and longer follow-up periods. Studies should stratify outcomes by body mass index, age, sex, sleep posture, baseline pain severity, and mattress age. Such data would allow clinicians to move from general advice toward more precise, individualized mattress prescriptions.

Conclusions

Over the past 25 years, available literature has progressively shifted away from the simplistic concept that a very hard “orthopedic” mattress is the best option for spinal health. The most consistent evidence supports medium-firm mattresses as a practical and clinically reasonable choice for improving sleep quality and reducing low back pain or morning stiffness. Their benefit appears to depend on the ability to preserve neutral spinal alignment while distributing pressure evenly across the shoulders, trunk, pelvis, and lower limbs.

The effectiveness of medium-firm support can be enhanced by appropriate surface design, topper characteristics, zonal support, and attention to thermal comfort. Because body habitus, age, sex-related anthropometry, sleep posture, and personal comfort all influence the body–mattress interaction, mattress recommendations should be individualized whenever possible. Technological advances, including adjustable and smart mattresses, may further improve personalized sleep support, but stronger clinical validation is required before they can be considered standard care. In current practice, clinicians should recommend mattresses that combine medium-firm support, pressure redistribution, neutral spinal alignment, and a comfortable microclimate, while avoiding unsupported claims based solely on the term “orthopedic.”
