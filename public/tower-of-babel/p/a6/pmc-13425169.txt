Work productivity in Behçet syndrome
Authors: Nergis Serin, Yesim Ozguler, Sinem Nihal Esatoglu, Vedat Hamuryudan, Gulen Hatemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesBehçet syndrome (BS) is a multisystem vasculitis that is most active during young adulthood. We aimed to evaluate work productivity and stability in patients with BS and compare it with diseased and healthy controls (HCs) in a cross-sectional study.MethodsWe evaluated 125 consecutive patients with BS for work productivity and stability using the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment Questionnaire (WPAI), Work Productivity Survey (WPS), and Work Instability Scale (WIS). The association of work productivity with demographic and disease features, including age, gender, education status, job type, disease duration, disease activity, health-related quality of life, and type of organ involvement was explored. Thirty patients with ankylosing spondylitis and 30 HCs were included as controls. Patients with ankylosing spondylitis (AS) and BS were evaluated with disease-specific activity indices.ResultsAmong the 125 patients with BS who were evaluated for work productivity, 53 (42%) reported absenteeism (mean 1.8 d/mo) and 60 (48%) reported presenteeism (mean 4.3 d/mo). WPAI, WPS and WIS scores were similar in patients with BS and AS and significantly impaired compared with HCs. Ocular, vascular, and nervous system involvement, disease activity, and higher Behçet disease quality-of-life scores were associated with work productivity impairment.ConclusionsImpaired work productivity is frequent in patients with BS and is associated with major organ involvement, disease activity, and impaired health-related quality of life. Work needs to be considered as a candidate domain for assessment in clinical trials of BS.

INTRODUCTION

Work productivity impairment is a well-known consequence of rheumatologic disorders. Work is important, not only for economic needs but also for self-esteem and a valued social role for the individual and for the well-being of society [1].

An increased risk of work disability has been recognised for a long time in patients with different rheumatologic conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA), ankylosing spondylitis (AS), psoriatic arthritis, and vasculitis [[2], [3], [4]]. More recent studies have focused on the impact of these conditions on individuals who are still working but experiencing difficulty or inefficiency during work. The importance of time off work due to disability or days missed from work due to health reasons (absenteeism) and reduced productivity while at work (presenteeism) were acknowledged as important outcomes [5].

Outcome Measures in Rheumatology Clinical Trials (OMERACT) worker productivity group has been working for more than a decade on improving assessment of work, including defining the contextual factors that influence worker productivity, and assessing the instruments used for the assessment of work productivity, regarding their feasibility, discriminative features and truth, and describing thresholds for meaningful differences [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]].

Development of specific instruments to assess work disability, productivity, and stability has enabled the measurement of these as outcomes during clinical trials [[11], [12], [13]]. Reliable work assessment is important for comparison of patient groups and disease states regarding work outcomes and to assess the impact of different treatment modalities on work. These are necessary for developing strategies to improve work disability and productivity.

Behçet syndrome (BS) is a multisystem vasculitis that usually starts during the second and third decades of life and is active through young adulthood, during which work status may be severely affected. Both genders are affected by BS with similar frequency, but it runs a more severe course with more major organ involvement in men [14]. Functional disability is common during active disease and due to damage caused by organ involvement, such as uveitis, nervous system involvement, venous and arterial thrombosis, and gastrointestinal involvement. From a work perspective, BS may be different from some of the other rheumatic conditions due to the wide variety of organs that can be involved, and heterogeneity among patients regarding the severity of organ involvement and damage.

There have been few previous studies evaluating work in patients with BS that did not include diseased or healthy control (HC) groups [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. The objectives of the current study were to evaluate impairment in work productivity in a sizeable group of patients with BS with different types of involvement, together with suitable controls including patients with AS and HCs and to elaborate on the predictors of impaired work productivity in patients with BS.

METHODS

Study design

This was a cross-sectional analysis of work productivity in a group of patients with BS, together with patients with AS and HCs. Consecutive patients with BS followed in the outpatient clinic of a dedicated Behçet Disease Research Center were evaluated for work productivity using the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment Questionnaire (WPAI) and Work Productivity Survey (WPS). Work stability was assessed using the Work Instability Scale (WIS). Patients with BS were assessed for disease activity with the Behçet’s Disease Current Activity Form (BDCAF) and Behçet’s Syndrome Activity Scale (BSAS) and for health-related quality of life (HRQoL) with Behçet’s Disease Quality of Life Scale (BDQoL). Additionally, patients with AS and healthy subjects were included as controls. Patients with AS were assessed for activity using the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) and for function using the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI). Additionally, patients were questioned and hospital charts were reviewed for demographic and disease characteristics, job type, and education status.

Patients

We included 125 consecutive patients with BS attending our outpatient clinic, who were employed and fulfilled ISG (International Study Group) criteria, and were willing to answer the questionnaires between January and December 2018. Thirty consecutive patients with AS, who were followed in our outpatient clinic during the same time, fulfilled modified New York criteria and were employed, and 30 healthy volunteers who were hospital staff without chronic diseases were also included. All patients and HCs provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Istanbul University Cerrahpaşa Medical School (2017-166581).

Outcome measure instruments

WPAI

WPAI consists of 6 questions assessing the impact of the disease on work and other daily activities during the previous 7 days. WPAI assesses work productivity by measuring the following: (1) absenteeism, which refers to work leave of absence related to disease, (2) presenteeism, which represents work impairment caused by the disease, despite being present at work, and (3) activity impairment, which indicates health-related effect on regular activities. Each component is expressed as percentage of impairment due to the disease (0%–100%), where a higher a percentage indicates a greater reduction. The Turkish version of the form was developed by translating into Turkish and backwards by 2 people. There were only slight differences in the back-translated version from the original. These were resolved through discussion and modification of the Turkish version. WPAI modified for AS (WPAI:SpA), which is a previously validated, reliable, and responsive tool, was used for patients with AS [21].

WPS

The WPS was initially developed for RA, which is based on self-reporting, with a recall period of 1 month [22]. It was later found to be a valid, responsive, and reliable instrument for measuring work productivity in patients with AS [23]. It consists of 9 questions and is organised into 3 sections. Section I: employment status (yes/no), type of work; section II (paid work): (1) absenteeism-days of work missed, (2) presenteism-days with productivity reduced by at least half, and (3) degree of interference on work productivity on a scale of 0–10; and section III (household work and activities): (1) absenteeism-days of nonpaid work missed, (2) presenteeism-days with productivity reduced by at least half, (3) absenteeism-days missed family, social, or leisure activities, (4) presenteeism-days with outside help, and (5) degree of interference on nonpaid work productivity on a scale of 0–10. Similar to a previous version used for scleroderma where the word ‘arthritis’ is replaced by ‘scleroderma’ [24], we modified the survey by replacing the word ‘arthritis’ by ‘Behçet syndrome’ and ‘ankylosing spondylitis’ for our patients such as ‘How many days in the last month did the patient miss work because of Behçet syndrome?’ and by omitting the disease-specific attribution for HCs to allow capture of work absence due to general health reasons. The Turkish version of these forms was developed by forward and backwards translation, through a similar process to WPAI.

WIS

WIS is a self-administered instrument which can define the mismatch between patients’ functional ability and the demands of their job at the time of filling the questionnaire [25,26]. WIS score cutoff points represent low (<11), medium (11-18), and high (19-20) levels of work instability. AS-WIS was previously translated, cross-culturally adapted, and validated in Turkish [27]. It consists of 20 ‘yes/no’ questions. We used this version for patients with BS and AS and HCs.

BDCAF

BDCAF is a patient and physician-reported instrument for assessment of overall disease activity in patients with BS [28]. It has 3 components: the BDCAF, which is scored between 0 and 12, with higher scores indicating more active disease, the patients’ global assessment of disease activity, and the physicians’ global assessment of disease activity, both scored between 1 and 7. These scores are evaluated separately and not intended to be combined. BDCAF assesses patient’s symptoms over the previous month before the clinic visit.

Behçet’s syndrome activity scale

BSAS is a patient-reported outcome measure developed for overall disease activity assessment [29]. The score ranges between 0 and 100 for BSAS. Higher scores indicate higher disease activity. Recall period is 1 month, similar to the BDCAF.

BDQoL scale

HRQoL was assessed using a disease-specific index for patients with BS, the BDQoL [30]. BDQoL assesses the patient’s HRQoL at the time of filling the questionnaire. The score for this index ranges between 0 and 30. Higher scores of BDQoL refer to greater impairment in quality of life.

BASDAI

BASDAI is a patient-reported activity index specifically developed for AS [31]. Score ranges from 0 (no disease activity) to 10 (maximal disease activity). A cutoff of 4 is used to define active disease.

BASFI

BASFI is used for the assessment of function in patients with AS [32]. Score range is between 0 and 10, with 0 reflecting no functional impairment and 10 reflecting maximal impairment.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of the study groups were expressed as mean (SD) or median (IQR) for continuous variables, according to their distribution, and number (percentages) for categorical variables. One-way ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) or Kruskal-Wallis test was selected according to the distribution of continuous variables, and the Chi-square test was selected for categorical variables for comparisons between BS, AS, and HCs. Chi-squared test for categorical variables or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables was used for comparisons of WIS scores (moderate and high vs low), WPS scores (absenteeism + vs absenteeism−; presenteeism + vs presenteism−) and WPAI scores (absenteeism + vs absenteeism−; presenteeism + vs presenteism−). Correlations between continuous numeric variables (WPAI, WPS, WIS, BDQoL, BSAS, and BDCAF scores) were assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient (r). Multivariate logistic regression analyses with odds ratios (OR) and 95% CI were used to study predictors of each score. Independent variables were age, gender, education status, job type, disease duration, type of organ involvement, BDQoL, BSAS, and BDCAF. Variables significant at P < .25 were entered into a multivariable logistic model. All analyses were performed on SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), and a P value < .05 was indicative of statistical significance.

Seventy-four (59%) patients with BS had missed work hours in the past week (absenteeism), 93 (74%) patients reported having work impairment (presenteeism), and 81 (65%) patients had work productivity impairment (presenteeism + absenteeism). The mean ± SD percentages of absenteeism, presenteeism, and presenteeism + absenteeism were 10.0 ± 18.6, 37.0 ± 29.5, and 40.7 ± 30.1, respectively. WPS

Fifty-three (42%) patients with BS reported missing work days (absenteeism), and 60 (48%) patients with BS reported that their productivity was reduced at least by half (presenteeism) in the previous month. The mean missing days of work was 1.8 d/mo, and the mean number of days with decreased productivity was 4.3 d/mo among patients with BS. These were similar to patients with AS (1.3 and 4.2 d/mo, respectively) (P = .8) and significantly higher than HCs (0.1 and 1.2 d/mo, respectively) (P = .006).

Correlation of work productivity with disease activity and quality of life

There was a significant but weak correlation between WPAI-absenteeism and BDQoL (r = 0.33, P < .001) and no correlation with BSAS and BDCAF. WPAI-presenteeism was moderately correlated with BDQoL (r = 0.60, P < .001) and weakly correlated with BSAS (r = 0.35, P < .001) and BDCAF (r = 0.23, P = .01).

There was a significant but weak correlation between absenteeism and BDQoL (r = 0.30, P = .001) and BSAS (r =0.26, P = .004) and no correlation with BDCAF. WPS-presenteeism and BDQoL was moderately correlated (r =.50, P < .001) and weakly correlated with BSAS (r = 0.26, P = .003) and BDCAF (r = 0.20, P = .03).

There was a strong correlation between moderate to high WIS score and BDQoL (r =.74, P < .001) and a weak correlation with BSAS (r = 0.36, P < .001) and BDCAF (r = 0.35, P < .001).

DISCUSSION

This study showed that impaired work productivity is frequent in patients with BS. Among the 125 patients with BS in our work productivity cohort, 59% experienced absenteeism and 74% experienced presenteeism. These frequencies were similar to those of our patients with AS and were significantly higher than those of the HCs who were hospital staff without any known medical conditions. The same was true for the number of missing days of work and days with decreased work productivity. In addition to paid work, household work, family, and social activities were also impaired in patients with BS.

In a previous cost-analysis study that was conducted in our centre, 43% of patients with BS reported lost work days due to BS [18]. Workday loss was higher among patients with eye (49%), vascular (46%), and neurologic involvement (60%) as compared with patients with isolated mucocutaneous and joint involvement (22%). We also found that having major organ involvement was a significant risk factor for presenteeism. Damage caused by major organ involvement, as well as disease activity, may be responsible for this. These findings are consistent with emerging cost-of-illness data indicating that productivity loss and other indirect costs constitute a substantial component of the overall socioeconomic burden of BS [33]. In a recent European analysis, nonhealthcare costs and work-related productivity losses contributed markedly to total disease burden, further supporting the relevance of work productivity as a key outcome domain.

Similar observations have been reported in other studies from Türkiye, where approximately 30% of patients with BS had missed work, and ocular involvement was associated with greater work loss [16]. A study led by the same group assessed the predictors of workday loss and reported that workday loss was associated with both vascular and ocular involvement [17].

Qualitative patient interviews and Delphi exercises conducted within the OMERACT Behçet Syndrome Working Group highlight that impaired work participation and productivity are one of the key components of disease impact in BS [34,35]. In qualitative interviews, patients described difficulty maintaining employment because of relapsing disease activity, fatigue, pain, and major organ involvement such as eye or neurologic involvement, often reporting reduced productivity at work, job modification, or leaving work altogether [34]. These findings suggest that reduced productivity while working often precedes permanent work disability. Similarly, the Delphi exercise showed that both physicians and patients endorsed work productivity as a core outcome for all clinical trials [35].

A recent multinational post-hoc analysis of the BS Overall Damage Index (BODI), a validated measure of cumulative organ damage in BS, assessed work productivity using the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment: General Health questionnaire [19]. Similar to our findings, that study reported a high prevalence of work productivity impairment among employed patients, affecting approximately two-thirds of the cohort. Higher BODI scores were independently associated with unemployment and worse work productivity, particularly presenteeism. Ocular and musculoskeletal damage were identified as important contributors to absenteeism and overall work impairment. Similarly, in our study, ocular involvement, along with other major organ involvements, was associated with impaired work productivity. It should be noted, however, that musculoskeletal damage in the BODI also includes treatment-related damage and, therefore, does not correspond directly to a specific organ involvement, which may partly explain differences between the 2 studies. Notably, work productivity impairment in the BODI study was associated with disease activity as assessed by physician global assessment, rather than BDCAF. At the time the present study was conducted, a BS-specific validated damage index such as BODI had not yet been developed, which limited our ability to directly assess the impact of permanent organ damage on work outcomes.

Another recent multinational study evaluated presenteeism and activity impairment in patients with BS and recurrent aphthous stomatitis [20]. In that study, disease activity in BS was assessed using the BDCAF, and patients were compared according to active (BDCAF score ≥1) vs inactive (BDCAF score = 0) disease status. Using this dichotomous definition, disease activity was associated with work productivity impairment; however, these associations were largely driven by specific active clinical manifestations rather than the overall BDCAF score. Active genital ulcers and eye involvement were associated with increased presenteeism and overall work impairment, whereas active joint involvement was associated with higher absenteeism, presenteeism, and overall work impairment compared with inactive disease.

Taken together, these studies, including ours, indicate that clinically relevant disease manifestations are associated with impaired work productivity and daily functioning in BS. However, currently available disease activity instruments such as BDCAF may not fully capture the aspects of disease burden that are most relevant to work productivity. The lack of a consistent association between disease activity indices and work productivity in our study may reflect differences between inflammatory activity and the overall disease burden experienced by patients. Instruments such as BDCAF and BSAS primarily capture transient clinical manifestations, whereas work participation may also be influenced by cumulative organ damage, persistent visual or neurologic deficits, and other aspects of disease burden such as fatigue, pain, sleep disturbance, and psychosocial factors. These domains may persist even during periods of low inflammatory activity or remission and, therefore, may not be adequately reflected by activity scores alone. This is further supported by the strong and consistent relationship between impaired quality of life and work productivity impairment in our cohort, suggesting that global disease impact, rather than transient disease activity alone, may play a more important role in determining work productivity in BS, potentially reflecting unmeasured factors such as cumulative organ damage and persistent functional consequences.

Apart from ocular involvement, there was limited overlap in the specific organ manifestations associated with work productivity impairment across the 3 studies. This discrepancy is likely related to differences in study populations, including disease duration, types of organ involvement, and the proportion of patients with active vs inactive disease. In addition, the use of different outcome definitions and analytical approaches may have contributed to the observed variability. Together, these findings highlight the heterogeneity of BS and suggest that the impact of individual organ manifestations on work productivity may vary across patient populations. When organ involvement was reclassified as major organ involvement (ocular, vascular, or neurologic) vs isolated mucocutaneous involvement, a similar pattern of associations was observed. Major organ involvement remained related to higher presenteeism, whereas no meaningful differences were detected for absenteeism, WPS outcomes, or work instability. This consistency between organ-specific and grouped analyses suggests that the overall functional burden associated with major organ disease, rather than differences between individual organ types, may be more relevant for work-related outcomes in BS.

The use of multiple work-related instruments in this study was intended to capture complementary dimensions of work participation, as no single measure is specific to BS. Short-term productivity (WPS), broader activity impairment (WPAI), and risk of future work disability (WIS) may reflect distinct but related aspects of disease impact in this heterogeneous multisystem condition. Although WIS reflects vulnerability to future work loss rather than current productivity impairment, it may still provide clinically relevant information in chronic multisystem diseases such as BS, which are characterised by fluctuating disease activity and cumulative damage. Evaluating these domains together may, therefore, provide a more comprehensive description of work-related burden in BS.

The main limitation of our study was that none of the work assessment instruments had been validated for BS. The OMERACT Worker Productivity group has been working to optimise the assessment of work for over a decade and recommended the use of a number of instruments including WPAI and WPS [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Presenteeism global measures from both the WPAI questionnaire and the RA-specific WPS were endorsed by OMERACT [7]. The OMERACT work productivity group later recommended Workplace Activity Limitations Scale, Work Limitations Questionnaire with modified physical demands scale, Work Ability Index, WPS, and WPAI for assessing at-work productivity impairment. Moreover, they have further enhanced through assessing the test-retest reliability, construct validity, and feasibility and by defining thresholds for meaningful differences [9]. However, none of these instruments was specifically validated for use in patients with BS. Although we did not evaluate the psychometric properties of these instruments, their previous use in different rheumatologic conditions such as AS, scleroderma, and vasculitis have encouraged us to use these instruments in BS [3,24,26]. Formal psychometric validation was beyond the scope of the present study; however, the observed associations of these measures with clinically relevant disease features and quality-of-life impairment may provide preliminary construct-level support for their applicability in BS. Dedicated longitudinal validation studies remain necessary, particularly to support the use of these instruments as outcome measures in clinical trials evaluating treatment effects on work-related outcomes.

Another limitation was that we included consecutive patients who attended our outpatient clinic. Thus, we may have missed patients with severe disability such as those with severe nervous system or eye involvement. This may have resulted in underestimation of work productivity impairment in our cohort. In addition, certain organ involvements, such as active joint and gastrointestinal involvement, were relatively infrequent in our cohort, which may have limited our ability to detect their specific impact on work productivity outcomes. Because the primary objective of this study was to assess work productivity impairment, analyses were necessarily restricted to currently employed participants, as absenteeism and presenteeism cannot be meaningfully evaluated in unemployed individuals. Consequently, exclusion of patients with work disability may have led to underestimation of the overall work-related burden of BS. Future longitudinal studies incorporating broader employment status outcomes, including unemployment, long-term sick leave, and early retirement, are needed to better characterise the full work-related burden of BS.

Although all groups were broadly within the working-age period, typically corresponding to the third decade of life, HCs were not fully matched to the patient groups with respect to age, sex distribution, educational status, and job characteristics, which may have influenced comparisons of work-related outcomes and should be considered when interpreting between-group differences. Residual confounding by age and occupational characteristics cannot be excluded. Absenteeism and presenteeism were dichotomised for regression analyses to allow clinically interpretable models distinguishing the presence vs absence of meaningful work productivity impairment. However, dichotomisation may lead to loss of information and could have reduced the ability to detect certain associations. In addition, potential confounding by unmeasured factors such as current treatments (including glucocorticoids, conventional immunosuppressants, and biologics) and comorbid symptoms affecting work performance (eg, pain, fatigue, mood symptoms, and sleep disturbance) could not be accounted for in this cross-sectional design, which limits causal interpretation of the observed associations.

In conclusion, this study assessing work productivity in patients with BS compared with diseased and HCs suggests that work productivity impairment is frequent and clinically relevant in this population; however, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the cross-sectional design and the limitations of the measurement tools. It is important for physicians to realise and address this issue during the management of patients. Further studies are needed to optimise the assessment of work productivity and incorporate it into BS clinical trials.
