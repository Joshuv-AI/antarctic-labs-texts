Integrated E-nose, volatilomics, and transcriptomics analysis of the aroma formation mechanism during pitaya fruit ripening
Authors: Liangjie Ba, Xiaoling Long, Chenglin Luo, Sen Cao, Jianye Chen, Donglan Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pitaya fruit is of high nutritional and economic value, yet the molecular mechanisms governing its aroma formation during ripening remain poorly understood. This study employed an integrated approach combining E-nose, HS-SPME-GC-MS, and transcriptomics to investigate the formation and dynamic changes of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and their underlying molecular mechanisms in “Zihonglong” pitaya from Guizhou Province, China. A total of 856 VOCs were tentatively identified, among which terpenoids, esters, and ketones were the predominant chemical groups. Based on relative odor activity values (rOAV > 1), 79 compounds were identified as key contributors to aroma perception. Transcriptomic analysis indicated that α-linolenic acid metabolism, terpenoid biosynthesis, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis were closely associated with volatile formation during ripening. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) further revealed two major regulatory modules, designated “Turquoise” and “Blue”, which showed strong correlations with the accumulation patterns of characteristic aroma metabolites. Multivariate analysis of E-nose responses successfully distinguished unripe fruit from ripening stages, demonstrating its potential for rapid maturity screening. This study provides mechanistic insights into aroma evolution during pitaya ripening and supports the development of aroma-based quality evaluation strategies.

Introduction

Pitaya (Hylocereus polyrhizus), a Cactaceae species originating from the Americas, has established a firm foothold in global tropical and subtropical fruit markets. Valued for its vibrant pigmentation, refreshing texture, and rich profile of bioactive compounds—including betalains, dietary fiber, and vitamins—pitaya has secured robust consumer preference and significant economic standing in international trade1,2. Driven by evolving consumer demands, the evaluation criteria for fruit quality are shifting from mere appearance and physicochemical indices to a comprehensive sensory experience. Notably, flavor characteristics, particularly aroma composition, have emerged as critical determinants of consumer acceptance and market competitiveness3,4. Fruit ripening is a complex and dynamic physiological process involving chromatic transition, textural softening, sugar-acid metabolism, and the accumulation of characteristic volatiles. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying aroma compound biosynthesis during this process, a comparative transcriptome analysis was conducted in Prunus mume5. Since secondary metabolites constitute the chemical basis of flavor, decoding their dynamic evolution during maturation is essential for defining edible quality6. The ripening of red-fleshed pitaya is typically categorized into three ripening stages: the green fruit stage, the color turning stage, and the ripe stage. Prior literature has extensively covered pigment metabolism (e.g., betalain biosynthesis) and postharvest preservation7–10. A systematic characterization of the aroma formation patterns and their underlying molecular basis in pitaya is still lacking. Compared with well-characterized fruit species such as apple11, citrus12,13, and peach14, our limited understanding of the pitaya aroma metabolic network constrains the development of aroma-based quality evaluation standards and targeted flavor improvement strategies.

Fruit aroma is orchestrated by the synergistic action of various volatile organic compounds (VOCs), primarily comprising esters, aldehydes, alcohols, terpenes, and ketones15. The synthesis of these compounds exhibits distinct phasic characteristics and is strictly regulated by metabolism, including fatty acid, terpenoid, and phenylpropanoid metabolism16–18. The fatty acid pathway serves as the primary source of straight-chain aldehydes, alcohols, and esters. These compounds contribute fresh, green background notes to the fruit’s aroma. Unsaturated fatty acids are catalyzed by lipoxygenase (LOX) and hydroperoxide lyase (HPL) to generate C6/C9 aldehydes, which are subsequently reduced to alcohols by alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) and further esterified by alcohol acyltransferase (AAT). For instance, the expression of AaLOX and AaHPL in Actinidia arguta dictates the sensory shift from “grassy” to “fruity” notes, while the upregulation of LOX2S and ADH during blueberry ripening promotes the accumulation of alcohol-based flavor compounds19,20. Terpenoids primarily contribute floral and sweet notes to the fruit; their synthesis depends on the universal precursors isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP) and dimethylallyl pyrophosphate (DMAPP) generated via the cytosolic mevalonate (MVA) pathway and the plastidial methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathway21. In fruits such as grapes, the accumulation of monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes is closely correlated with the expression of key enzyme genes like DXS and HMGR22,23. Furthermore, the phenylpropanoid pathway, in addition to participating in pigment synthesis, generates aromatic volatiles such as benzaldehyde, significantly enhancing aroma complexity24. Nevertheless, the dynamic accumulation patterns of these key aroma-active compounds and the regulatory mechanisms of related genes during red-fleshed pitaya ripening remain unclear.

Given the trace abundance and chemical diversity of fruit volatiles, comprehensive characterization requires advanced analytical strategies. While traditional sensory evaluation is often limited by subjectivity, gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS)—despite its quantitative precision—may not intuitively reflect the holistic odor fingerprint. Consequently, coupling electronic nose (E-nose) technology with multi-omics has emerged as a robust paradigm in modern flavor science. The E-nose utilizes sensor arrays to rapidly discriminate aroma signatures across maturity stages25, while integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses (e.g., WGCNA) effectively map “gene-metabolite” networks to identify key regulatory nodes26.

The contribution of the LOX pathway to the “grassy” notes of white‑fleshed pitaya has been documented27, however, red‑fleshed cultivars exhibit more complex aroma characteristics, and whether their volatile composition and regulatory mechanisms differ remains unclear. This study addresses this gap by integrating multi‑omics analyses.

To address this knowledge gap, the present study investigated the dynamic transitions of volatile profiles during the ripening of pitaya “Zihonglong”. Fruits were evaluated across three key maturity stages (HG, HP, and HR) using a combination of E-nose analysis and HS-SPME-GC-MS. By integrating this volatile profiling with transcriptome sequencing and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), we aimed to uncover the underlying metabolic pathways and potential key genes regulating aroma biosynthesis. Furthermore, we sought to explore the feasibility of using coordinated E-nose and volatile data for maturity discrimination. The primary significance of this research lies in elucidating the molecular and metabolic mechanisms of pitaya aroma formation, thereby providing a reliable scientific basis for flavor evaluation and maturity assessment, as well as an integrative multi-omics framework applicable to other fruit crops.

Results

Phenotypic and aroma characterization during pitaya ripening

The most prominent phenotypic transformations observed during pitaya ripening involved a distinct chromatic shift in both the peel and pulp, as well as a significant expansion in fruit volume (Fig. 1A). At the green fruit stage (HG), the fruit appeared tender green and small, with compact and upright scales. The pulp was milky white to pale yellow, containing small, pale yellow, tender seeds. As maturity advanced, the fruit volume gradually expanded. By the color turning stage (HP), the fruit remained predominantly green, but the scales began to unfurl, and pale pink halos appeared in certain areas. The pulp exhibited a pale purplish-pink hue with small black seeds. At the ripe stage (HR), anthocyanin accumulation in the peel was complete, presenting a vivid rose-red color. Using an E-nose, the aroma profiles of pitaya were characterized across distinct maturity stages (Fig. 1B). W1W sensors, responsive to sulfides and terpenes (2.12–6.45 V), and W5S sensors, responsive to nitrogen oxides (1.01–4.83 V), exhibited markedly higher response intensities compared to the remaining sensors. This observation suggests that sulfides, terpenoids, and nitrogen oxides play crucial roles as contributors to the overall aroma profile of pitaya. Interestingly, the response values and patterns at the HP and HR stages were highly similar, whereas a distinct upward trend was observed from the HG stage to the later stages (HP and HR). These results suggest that the volatile organic compound (VOC) profiles undergo significant changes from the green stage to the color turning stage, but remain relatively stable thereafter.

Principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 1C) showed a combined contribution rate of 90.83% (PC1: 80.34%, PC2: 10.49%). HG samples formed a distinct cluster separated from the others along PC1; however, samples from the HP and HR stages exhibited considerable overlap. These findings demonstrate that while the E-nose can effectively differentiate green pitaya (HG) from those in later ripening phases, it is difficult to distinguish between the color-turning (HP) and ripe stage (HR) stages based solely on these E-nose sensors, likely due to their highly similar overall aroma profiles.

As depicted in the radar chart (Fig. 1D), the sensory profile of pitaya underwent significant dynamic changes during the ripening stages. Throughout the postharvest ripening process, the sweet and floral characteristics exhibited a sustained increase. In contrast, the green and herbal aromas showed a continuous decline. At the HG stage, the aroma profile was predominantly driven by distinct green (6.80) and herbal (5.97) notes, with other aroma attributes being essentially imperceptible. As the fruit progressed to the HP stage, both fruity and sweet notes began to gradually intensify, while the green aroma rapidly diminished. Interestingly, the herbal note exhibited a slight decrease during this transition, maintaining a relatively distinct presence. By the HR stage, the aroma profile transitioned to being primarily defined by fruity, sweet, and floral notes. It is noteworthy that, reflecting the non-climacteric nature of pitaya, the fruity and sweet characteristics did not exhibit an explosive burst; rather, they plateaued at a moderate intensity (scores of approx. 5.2). Furthermore, a weak but perceptible background of green and herbal notes (scores of 1.53 and 1.90, respectively) persisted even at the fully ripe stage, constituting the unique sensory signature of this cactus fruit.

HS-SPME-GC-MS analysis of VOCs during the pitaya ripening process

HS-SPME-GC-MS analysis revealed that pitaya ripening involves substantial dynamic changes in volatile organic compounds (VOCs). A total of 856 (total detected) VOCs28–30 were detected across the three maturity stages (Fig. 2A), including terpenoids (152, 17.76%), esters (142, 16.59%), ketones (116, 13.55%), heterocyclic compounds (96, 11.21%), alcohols (91, 10.63%), aldehydes (65, 7.59%), hydrocarbons (57, 6.66%), acids (38, 4.44%), phenols (23, 2.69%), aromatics (22, 2.57%), amines (20, 2.34%), ethers (14, 1.64%), nitrogen compounds (11, 1.29%), halogenated hydrocarbons (7, 0.82%), and sulfur compounds (2, 0.23%). The relative abundance of volatile metabolites fluctuated across the three maturity stages, reflecting continuous metabolic shifts during pitaya ripening (Fig. 2B). Most VOC classes—particularly ketones, esters, and alcohols—exhibited a transient decrease at the color turning stage (HP) before reaching their maximum levels at the ripe stage (HR). The accumulation of these compounds at HR aligns with the development of the characteristic sweet and fruity flavor in mature fruit, a process typically driven by upregulated fatty acid and esterification pathways. In contrast, terpenoids and aldehydes maintained relatively stable levels at both the HG and HR stages, with only a marginal dip at HP. Their high abundance at HG corresponds to the initial “green” sensory notes, whereas their persistence at HR serves to enrich the overall complexity of the final aroma profile. Furthermore, hydrocarbons remained at low basal levels during early development but increased sharply at HR. This late-stage accumulation points toward an involvement in ripening-related physiological processes, such as epicuticular wax formation or fruit senescence.

Principal component analysis, based on the metabolomic dataset, showed distinct separation of samples from the three maturity stages into individual clusters (PCA) (Fig. 2C). The two primary principal components accounted for a combined total of 92.14% of the explained variance, with PC1 contributing 66.47% and PC2 contributing 25.67%. These results indicate significant differences in VOC profiles at different ripening stages, as well as the reproducibility of experimental samples and data reliability.

Based on screening criteria (fold change ≥2 and ≤0.5), 551 differential metabolites were obtained. A Venn diagram illustrating the relationship between differential metabolites across comparisons (HP vs. HG, HR vs. HP, and HR vs. HG) revealed 479, 233, and 299 differential metabolites, respectively (Fig. 2D). Notably, 100 metabolites were common to all three comparisons, highlighting the substantial divergence in volatile profiles throughout the growth process.

Furthermore, to better visualize class-specific variations among the 551 differential metabolites, the heatmap (Fig. 2E) was grouped by chemical categories. This visualization reveals distinct, stage-dependent accumulation patterns. For instance, specific subsets of differential compounds, particularly ethers, phenols, and heterocyclic compounds, primarily exhibited high relative abundances at the early HG stage. Conversely, the transition to the HP stage was accompanied by a general decrease in the signals of most differential volatile categories. At the fully ripe HR stage, a targeted upregulation was visually evident, with dense clusters of hydrocarbons, ketones, esters, alcohols, and acids showing their peak accumulations. Notably, terpenoids maintained highly abundant signals at both the HG and HR stages, contributing significantly to the profiles of both unripe and fully ripe fruits. Overall, these categorized distribution patterns suggest a synchronized, class-driven metabolic reprogramming during maturation, which corresponds well with the distinct sample separation observed in the PCA findings.

Differences in volatile compounds (rOAV > 1) among three different maturity stages of pitaya fruits

At the green fruit stage (HG), 12 volatile compounds exhibited exceptionally high rOAVs (ranging from > 1.0 × 10³ to > 1.6 × 10⁶; Table 1), indicating their primary contribution to the early flavor profile. As ripening progressed, these odorants displayed distinct accumulation patterns. Several compounds—including butanoic acid,3-methyl-,2-phenylethyl ester (floral, fruity), 2(5H)-furanone,5-ethyl-3-hydroxy-4-methyl- (sweet, fruity, caramel), cyclohexanone,2,2,6-trimethyl- (pungent, thujone, labdanum), 3-octen-2-one (earthy, spicy, herbal), 3-buten-2-one,4-(2,6,6-trimethyl-1-cyclohexen-1-yl)- (floral, woody, sweet), trans-β-ionone (dry, powdery, floral), 2-methylisoborneol (earthy, musty), (Z,Z)-3,6-nonadienal (fatty, soapy, cucumber), and non-8-enal (smoky, plastic)—initially decreased at the color turning stage (HP) but increased again at the ripe stage (HR). This late-stage accumulation likely enriches the complex aroma of mature pitaya. In contrast, (E)-2-nonenal (fatty, green, cucumber), 2-nonenal (fatty, green, waxy), and dodecanenitrile (citrus, orange, peel) declined continuously throughout ripening, which aligns with the gradual fading of the characteristic “green” notes of the HG stage. Additionally, the rOAVs of damascone,β- (fruity, floral, berry) and 2,4-undecadienal (green, buttery, spicy) increased gradually during maturation. Notably, 1-nonen-3-one reached a distinct peak at the HP stage (rOAV > 4.6 × 10⁴), providing a pungent and mushroom-like note specifically during this developmental transition.

The Venn diagram (Fig. 3B) highlighted 707 DEGs shared across all comparisons, suggesting that these genes likely function as core components in the fruit ripening process. To further elucidate their roles, particularly in relation to flavor metabolism, KEGG functional annotation was examined for these shared DEGs. The annotation results revealed that a specific subset of these genes is mapped to pathways directly associated with pitaya aroma formation. Specifically, among these 707 genes, 20 are annotated to phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940), 6 to alpha-linolenic acid metabolism (ko00592), and 7 to terpenoid backbone (ko00900) and sesquiterpenoid/triterpenoid biosynthesis (ko00909). Notably, several genes within this core group are annotated as key structural enzymes driving these aroma-related metabolisms, such as COMT and PLA2G16. The continuous differential expression of these metabolic genes across all ripening stages highlights their continuous contribution to the dynamic flux of volatile organic compounds.

Furthermore, volcano plots (Fig. 3C–E) illustrated the distribution patterns of differentially expressed transcripts in the HP vs. HG, HR vs. HP, and HR vs. HG comparisons, based on the criteria of |log2FC|≥ 1 and p < 0.05. KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs revealed the dynamic involvement of specific metabolic routes across different ripening stages (Fig. 3F–H). Specifically, in the HP vs. HG comparison, alpha-Linolenic acid metabolism (P = 7.35 × 10⁻⁵, Rich Factor = 0.36), Phenylalanine metabolism (P = 0.00087, Rich Factor = 0.41), and Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (P = 0.0089, Rich Factor = 0.26) were significantly enriched. In the HR vs. HG comparison, Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (P = 0.00045, Rich Factor = 0.32) and alpha-Linolenic acid metabolism (P = 0.00062, Rich Factor = 0.39) also showed significant enrichment. Notably, for the HR vs. HP comparison, Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis demonstrated extremely significant enrichment (P = 8.41 × 10⁻¹¹, Rich Factor = 0.18). Conversely, although certain aroma-related pathways—such as Monoterpenoid biosynthesis, Sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis, and Terpenoid backbone biosynthesis—did not consistently reach strict statistical enrichment thresholds (P > 0.05), specific subsets of DEGs were continuously mapped to these routes. This coordinated metabolic activity likely lays the foundation for the subsequent accumulation of aromatic precursors. These findings underscore the close association between stage-specific DEGs and these six critical biosynthetic routes.

To identify modules closely linked to aroma biosynthesis, we cross-referenced these stage-specific modules with targeted metabolite data based on a predefined threshold (|r|> 0.7, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4B). The early-stage Turquoise module exhibited highly significant positive correlations with early-accumulating compounds (e.g., D-carvone, r = 0.92, p < 0.001). In contrast, the late-stage Blue module met the correlation threshold primarily through significant negative correlations with diminishing early volatiles (e.g., 2-nonenal, r = −0.71, p < 0.05). Recognizing their prominent associations with aroma trait variations, our subsequent pathway analyses focused on these two modules.

The Turquoise module appears to act as a “metabolic cornerstone” during early development. In terpenoid metabolism, it integrates HMGCR, which encodes the key rate-limiting enzyme of the MVA pathway, with core MEP pathway genes (DXS, DXR, GCPE, FDPS). This co-expression pattern suggests a transcriptional coordination that likely provides an ample precursor pool for later terpenoid biosynthesis. Simultaneously, this module establishes a metabolic framework for the phenylpropanoid pathway via the robust co-expression of PAL, CYP73A, 4CL, and CCR. Furthermore, the enrichment of genes encoding PLA2G, PLA2G16, and LOX2S implies that fatty acid-derived volatile precursors begin to accumulate substantially during early fruit development.

As the fruit matures, the Blue module emerges as a central co-expression hub for flavor modification. Exhibiting high specificity for the phenylpropanoid branch, this module is highly enriched with catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) genes, including novel variants (e.g., novel.4078, novel.4916). Acting as terminal modifiers, these gene products likely catalyze intermediate methylation, contributing to the formation of benzenoid compounds characteristic of mature pitaya. Furthermore, the continued detection of genes encoding LOX2S and PLA2G16 within this module suggests sustained lipid turnover.

Collectively, the dynamic shifts in pitaya flavor metabolism coordinate closely with the concerted actions of these two modules. The Turquoise module highlights early developmental activity in upstream pathways to establish substrate reserves, whereas the Blue module characterizes the late-stage upregulation of downstream modifying enzymes at maturity. This stage‑dependent metabolic reconfiguration temporally coincides with the biochemical stratification that ultimately defines the mature pitaya aroma profile.

Notably, L-menthone surged from near-zero at the HG stage to high levels at HP and HR, identifying it as a critical contributor to the minty, herbal aroma of mature pitaya. Although perillyl alcohol also peaked at HR, its lack of specific odor characteristics implies it may function as a metabolic intermediate rather than a key aroma contributor.

To explore potential correlations, a correlation analysis was performed between pathway differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and metabolites using a parameter threshold of |r | ≥ 0.8 and P < 0.05 (Fig. 5B). The network comprised 17 genes connected to 10 metabolites via 110 edges. Interaction richness indicated that L-menthone interacted with the most genes (14), followed by D-carvone, 1,8-cineole, valencene, and (S, E)-nerolidol (13 each). The network demonstrates that key terpenoids are highly correlated with specific DEGs. Nine genes (e.g., HU01G00006, HU10G00790) exhibited high network connectivity (degree = 8), interacting with the majority of the targeted metabolites. The most highly connected gene, HU03G02093, exhibited the highest average correlation (|r|= 0.955, P < 0.05). Such high correlations have been documented in sweet cherry, where gene–metabolite association networks employed |r|> 0.90 with many pairs exceeding 0.9535, and in Korla pear, where volatile correlation networks reported values as high as r = 0.96 between key metabolites36. These strong associations suggest that these key associated genes may potentially participate in multiple metabolic branches, accompanying the physiological and biochemical changes observed during ripening. These associations, while correlative, highlight candidates for future functional characterization.

Metabolic changes and co-expression analysis of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis during pitaya ripening

The phenylpropanoid pathway is closely associated with fruit ripening quality and flavor formation (Fig. 6A)37. Using phenylalanine as the core precursor, enzymes including phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL), aromatic-L-amino-acid carboxy-lyase (DDC), and primary-amine oxidase (AOC3) catalyze the synthesis of aromatics such as phenylacetaldehyde, benzaldehyde, and benzyl alcohol. The relative content of phenylacetaldehyde increased stepwise, peaking at the HR stage. This accumulation suggests it contributes rich floral and honey-like notes to mature pitaya38. Conversely, benzaldehyde peaked at the HG stage but declined significantly at HP and HR. This implies it is a primary contributor to almond- and cherry-like sweet aromas in early maturity, consistent with observations in peach and tomato39. Notably, the downstream reduction product, benzyl alcohol, exhibited an HG > HR > HP accumulation pattern. Integrating transcriptomic data, this trend may be attributed to the expression patterns of alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH). Specifically, ADH is hypothesized to facilitate the conversion of residual benzaldehyde to benzyl alcohol at the HR stage despite the diminished precursor pool, thereby coordinating aroma balance. Metabolic changes and co-expression analysis of α-linolenic acid metabolism during pitaya ripening

The fatty acid metabolic pathway, the principal source of C6/C9 aldehyde and alcohol volatiles, is closely linked to aroma formation via dynamic flux changes (Fig. 7A)41,42. Mechanistically, phosphatidylcholine (PC) releases free α-linolenic acid (C18:3) via PLA2G and PLA2G16 hydrolysis. Subsequently, lipoxygenase (LOX2S) oxidizes α-linolenic acid into 9(S)- or 13(S)-HPOTrE intermediates. Gene expression profiles revealed that LOX2S family genes were highly expressed at the green fruit stage (HG). This high expression parallels the accumulation of C6 aldehydes, such as (E)-2-hexenal, suggesting a potential association between LOX2S and the production of characteristic grassy notes in immature fruit. In apple, specific LOX isoforms have been demonstrated to catalyze the formation of C6 aldehydes and to regulate the provision of precursors for ester biosynthesis during ripening43. Further downstream, ADH reduces aldehydes to alcohols like (Z)-3-hexen-1-ol, providing precursors for ester biosynthesis. GC-MS analysis indicated a metabolic flux shift towards ester synthesis as fruit entered the ripe stage (HR). To examine the relationship between the dynamic shift in volatile profiles and E-nose sensor responses during pitaya ripening, a correlation analysis was conducted based on the relative contents of 12 key volatile organic compounds and the data from 10 sensors. The 12 key volatiles were defined as those mapping to terpenoid, phenylpropanoid, or fatty acid pathways and exhibiting rOAV > 1 in at least one ripening stage; only volatiles with significant correlations (p < 0.05) were retained, as presented in Fig. 8. Given the broad sensitivity and non-specific response characteristics of E-nose sensors, the observed correlations are interpreted here as indicative of coordinated temporal variation trends rather than direct sensor-compound specificity. The second group (W1S, W2S, W1W, W5S, W2W) demonstrated a contrasting macro-pattern, co-varying positively with L-menthone but negatively with several other aromatic compounds (e.g., benzaldehyde, (S, E)-nerolidol, and geraniol). During the late ripening stages, the significant increase in L-menthone coincided with distinct shifts in the signals of this sensor subgroup. This inverse statistical trend implies that the shifting pattern of these sensors could potentially serve as a composite indicator for distinguishing late-stage ripening characteristics.

The third group (W6S) displayed a temporal trend that negatively co-varied with (E, Z)-2,6-nonadienal and (E, E)-2,6-nonadienal. In conclusion, although E-nose sensors are characterized by broad sensitivity and limited selectivity, the correlation heatmap provides useful trend-level information. These findings demonstrate that the holistic shift of the E-nose fingerprint is highly synchronized with the dynamic accumulation of key volatiles. Rather than relying on specific compound recognition, this macroscopic synchronization provides a supportive chemical context for the feasibility of using the E-nose as a rapid screening tool to monitor pitaya ripening.

Discussion

Aroma quality is a primary determinant of consumer preference and marketability, relying heavily on the dynamic profile of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) evolved during maturation45,46. As depicted in the radar chart (Fig. 1D), pitaya’s sensory profile underwent significant dynamic changes: green and herbal notes dominated the early HG stage but continuously declined, while fruity and sweet notes plateaued at a moderate intensity at full maturity. This reflects the typical non-climacteric nature of pitaya, where aroma evolution is a gradually cumulative process. By integrating E-nose and GC-MS technologies, this study systematically characterized the aroma evolution from the green fruit stage (HG) to the ripe stage (HR). Overall, the total volatile content exhibited a distinct U-shaped trend. This deviates from the continuous linear accumulation pattern observed in many ripening fruits. This U-shaped trend may reflect a metabolic transition at the HP stage where early-stage defensive volatiles are degraded before the onset of ripening-specific synthesis, suggesting a temporary shift in carbon substrate allocation. Similarly, previous studies have reported analogous stage-dependent accumulation patterns in other tropical fruits, closely correlated with physiological maturity47.

E-nose sensor arrays demonstrated notable potential for the rapid, non-destructive discrimination of ripening stages. Sensors W1W and W5S exhibited the highest response intensities, indicating that sulfides and broad-spectrum terpenoids constitute the basic background skeleton of pitaya aroma, similar to findings in grape flavor research48. To explore the associative patterns between overall sensor responses and volatile profiles, we constructed a correlation heatmap (Fig. 8). Regarding the correlation analysis between sensors and key VOCs (Fig. 8), it is important to note that E-nose sensors possess inherent broad-spectrum sensitivity rather than precise compound-specific recognition. Therefore, these statistical correlations do not imply a direct, exclusive chemical binding, but rather suggest that specific sensors (e.g., W1C, W3C) can serve as effective “associative probes” for tracking the macroscopic dynamic shift of characteristic flavor phenotypes. This multi-sensor tracking strategy connects microscopic metabolomic variations with macroscopic odor fingerprints, confirming the complementary advantages of sensor arrays, thereby providing a data-driven scientific basis for maturity discrimination.

Aroma formation is closely associated with complex gene co-expression networks. In this study, WGCNA identified the Turquoise module as highly associated with the green fruit stage (HG), showing significant positive correlations with key aroma substances including (S,E)-Nerolidol and (E)-2-Nonenal. The transcripts enriched in this module may potentially participate in the early-stage biosynthesis of these volatiles. While biologically associated with plant defense mechanisms against early-stage pathogens or herbivores49, these compounds simultaneously establish the characteristic “green” and “fresh” aroma profile at the HG stage. As the fruit matures, the identified “metabolic shift” from the Turquoise module to the Blue module—which statistically correlates with the decline of early‑stage aldehydes such as benzaldehyde—likely contributes to the sensory transition from predominantly “grassy” to a more balanced fruity and sweet character. From an ecological perspective, this finely tuned metabolic transition selectively downregulates harsh defensive compounds while upregulating attractant volatiles to signal full ripeness.

Transcriptomics provided further insight into the metabolic reorganization underlying pitaya aroma formation. Terpenoid biosynthesis is synergistically supplied by the cytosolic MVA and plastidial MEP pathways50,51, and our profiling revealed that terpenoids not only represented the most abundant volatile class but also maintained high signal intensity at both the HG and HR stages. The WGCNA modules offered a framework for interpreting this sustained terpenoid prominence across ripening. At the HG stage, the Turquoise module was enriched with upstream backbone genes such as HMGCR and DXS, likely ensuring a robust precursor supply that drove the early accumulation of primary intermediates like Geraniol. As the network transitioned to the Blue module during ripening, this precursor provision was maintained—through ACTC and FDPS—while additional pathways, particularly α‑linolenic acid metabolism and phenylpropanoid modification, became increasingly prominent, collectively contributing to mature aroma complexity. This stage‑dependent shift suggests a division of metabolic labor, in which the MEP pathway may primarily drive early terpenoid accumulation at the HG stage, whereas the MVA pathway assumes a more prominent role in sustaining precursor pools for terpenoid modification during late ripening52,53. Consistent with this view, studies in grape have shown that the biosynthesis of monoterpenes such as linalool and geraniol is closely associated with the expression of upstream pathway genes including HDR and TPS, with marked variation among cultivars54.

Additionally, fatty acid and phenylpropanoid metabolisms are suggested to drive substantial flavor remodeling55. Our results identified esters, terpenoids, and aldehydes as the predominant volatile classes in pitaya fruit. While esters were highly diverse and abundant, their specific biosynthetic routes remain largely under-annotated in pitaya KEGG genomic databases, suggesting the involvement of unique alcohol acyltransferase (AAT) isoforms or alternative pathways that warrant further exploration. During ripening, the activation of ADH genes potentially promotes the enzymatic reduction of transient aldehydes to corresponding alcohols, providing the necessary substrates for esterification. Unlike some climacteric fruits56, pitaya maintains a high diversity of esters without a singular explosive burst, reflecting a sustained esterification activity regulated by non-climacteric ripening signals.

However, these findings are based on statistical correlations and thus cannot imply direct causality. Future experimental validation will be essential to confirm the functional roles of candidate genes such as ADH and TPS. In parallel, the potential competition for carbon flux between phenylpropanoid‑derived aroma precursors and betalain pigment biosynthesis warrants further investigation. This study provides the first integrated multi-omics characterization of aroma formation during pitaya ripening, establishing a foundation for aroma-based quality evaluation and offering a transferable framework for flavor research in other non-model fruit species.

In summary, this study systematically elucidated the regulatory mechanisms underlying aroma formation during pitaya fruit ripening by integrating E-nose, HS-SPME-GC-MS, and transcriptomic analyses. E-nose results revealed significant differences in volatile organic compound profiles among different ripening stages. GC-MS analysis indicated that terpenoids, esters, and ketones were the most abundant volatile classes detected in pitaya. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) indicated that the Turquoise module was primarily associated with the biosynthesis of fundamental aroma skeleton compounds and the accumulation of defense-related metabolites during early fruit development. In contrast, the Blue module was linked to the fine regulation and transformation of flavor compounds in the late ripening stage, mainly through key modifying enzymes such as COMT and 4CL. Transcriptomic results suggested that the dynamic transition of pitaya aroma from green/grassy to fruity and mildly floral characteristics was largely regulated by three major metabolic pathways, including α-linolenic acid metabolism, terpenoid biosynthesis, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. Correlation analysis between E-nose sensors and key volatile compounds further supported the feasibility of applying E-nose technology for rapid maturity discrimination of pitaya fruit. Collectively, these findings provide a theoretical foundation for aroma-based quality evaluation and deepen our understanding of the molecular regulation of flavor formation in pitaya fruit.

Methods

Materials

Sampling was conducted on August 13, 2025, at a pitaya cultivation base in Huishui County, Guizhou Province, China (106°23’–107°05’ E, 25°24’–26°17’ N). “Zihonglong” pitaya fruits (Hylocereus polyrhizus) were harvested at three distinct maturity stages: the green fruit stage (HG, approximately 15 days after flowering), the color turning stage (HP, 21 days after flowering), and the ripe stage (HR, 28 days after flowering), representing commercial maturity. Immediately after harvest, the fruits were transported to the laboratory and stored for subsequent analysis.

Analysis of the E-nose

A portable electronic nose (PEN 3, Airsense Analytics GmbH, Schwerin, Germany) equipped with a ten-sensor metal oxide semiconductor array was used to characterize pitaya aroma profiles. The sensors targeted specific volatiles: W1C (aromatics), W5S (nitrogen oxides), W3C (ammonia), W6S (hydrogen), W5C (alkenes/aromatics), W1S (methane), W1W (sulfides/terpenes), W2S (alcohols, aldehydes, ketones), W2W (organic sulfides), and W3S (long-chain alkanes). For each measurement, 10.0 g of homogenized pitaya sample was sealed in a 20 mL headspace vial and equilibrated at 30 °C for 30 min. The operating parameters were set as follows: flush time 200 s, reset 5 s, pre-injection 5 s, flow 300 mL/min, and measurement cycle 100 s. This analytical procedure was performed across all three maturity stages (HG, HP, HR), with three biological replicates assessed per stage. The measurement protocol was adopted19, and data were processed using the instrument’s integrated PCA module.

Detection of VOCs by HS-SPME-GC–MS

Pitaya fruit samples were ground in liquid nitrogen and vortexed to ensure homogeneity. An aliquot of 2.0 g homogenate was accurately weighed into a 20 mL headspace vial, followed by the addition of 2 mL saturated NaCl solution and 20 μL of internal standard solution (3-hexanone-2,2,4,4-d4, 10 μg/mL), yielding a final internal standard concentration of 0.10 μg/g in the sample. The vial was immediately sealed. All analyses were performed with three biological replicates.

For extraction, a 120 μm DVB/CWR/PDMS fiber was utilized for solid-phase microextraction. Before the analysis, the fibers were pre-regulated to remove any possible contaminants. The extraction conditions were set as follows: the sample heating temperature was 60 °C, the equilibration time was 5 minutes, the extraction time was 15 minutes, and the desorption time at 250 °C was 5 minutes.

Volatile profiling was conducted via Headspace Solid-Phase Microextraction coupled with Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC-MS) using a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) for separation. High-purity helium (≥ 99.999%) functioned as the carrier gas at a constant flow of 1.2 mL/min, with the system operating in splitless injection mode at 250 °C. The thermal gradient began at 40 °C (held for 3.5 min), increasing to 100 °C at 10 °C/min, then to 180 °C at 7 °C/min, and finally reaching 280 °C at 25 °C/min (held for 5 min). For detection, the mass spectrometer operated in electron impact (EI) mode (70 eV) with a scan range of 50–550 m/z. Operational temperatures were maintained at 230 °C (ion source), 150 °C (quadrupole), and 280 °C (transfer line).

Volatile compounds were identified by matching mass spectra against the NIST 2020 library using Agilent MassHunter Qualitative software (deconvolution peak width = 20, minimum match factor = 70). Calculation of relative odor activity value (rOAV)

The relative odor activity value (rOAV) is a semi‑quantitative metric used to identify candidate key aroma compounds in foods by relating compound concentrations to their sensory thresholds, thereby evaluating the relative contribution of individual volatiles to the overall aroma profile. In this study, rOAV was calculated using internal standard‑derived semi‑quantitative concentrations (Cᵢ) and their corresponding odor thresholds in water. Compounds with rOAV ≥ 1 were considered primary contributors to the sample’s aroma and were regarded as candidate key odorants for subsequent sensory validation. Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation was conducted by a trained panel of 10 assessors (5 males, 5 females, aged 20–30). Panelists were screened via basic odor recognition tests and trained for one week using chemical reference standards. Five aroma attributes (green, fruity, sweet, herbal, and floral) were evaluated. Nine samples (three biological replicates for each of the HG, HP, and HR stages) were diced and presented in randomly coded, capped opaque containers to strictly eliminate visual bias caused by flesh color changes. Samples were equilibrated at 25 °C for 15 min to enrich headspace volatiles prior to olfactory assessment. A mandatory 2-min resting interval was enforced between samples to prevent olfactory fatigue. Attribute intensities were rated on a 9-point scale (0 = imperceptible, 9 = extremely strong), and the final score for each replicate was calculated as the mean of the 10 panelists’ ratings. The method was adapted from a previous study20 with modifications.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Samples were homogenized in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. For transcriptome sequencing, three biological replicates per stage (HG, HP, HR) were utilized. Technical replicates were not performed. Sequencing was conducted by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

For RNA extraction, the CTAB-PBIOZOL protocol combined with ethanol precipitation was employed for total RNA extraction. Following extraction, pellets were resuspended in 50 µL of DEPC-treated water. Quality control involved assessing both integrity and concentration using a Qubit fluorometer alongside a Qsep400 high-throughput bio-fragment analyzer.

For library construction, libraries were constructed using strand-specific transcriptome sequencing technology. Initially, mRNA was purified and fragmented into short sequences using fragmentation buffer at an optimized temperature. Synthesis of first-strand cDNA utilized random hexamer primers, while strand specificity was achieved during second-strand synthesis by substituting dTTP with dUTP. Subsequent processing included end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation. Magnetic beads were employed to isolate library inserts ranging from 250–350 bp, which were then PCR-amplified and purified. Library quantification and size distribution profiling relied on a Qubit fluorometer and the Qsep400 system, respectively. For data pre-processing, fastp software filtered raw reads by removing adapters, poly-N sequences (>10%), and low-quality bases (Q ≤ 20 exceeding 50%). Downstream analysis proceeded exclusively with the resulting high-quality clean reads.

For read alignment and differential expression analysis, reference genome files (Genes-new_ID.fa.gz) and corresponding annotations (Genes-new_ID.gff3.gz) were obtained from the Pitaya Genome Database (http://www.pitayagenomic.com/). Genome indexing and read alignment were carried out using HISAT2, mapping high-quality clean reads to the reference genome. Transcript reconstruction was performed with StringTie, which applies a network flow algorithm combined with de novo assembly strategies, resulting in improved accuracy and computational efficiency relative to Cufflinks. Gene expression at the gene level was calculated using FeatureCounts and then normalized to FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, and genes with an adjusted P-value (FDR) < 0.05 and |log₂FoldChange | ≥ 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was performed using the relative content of differential metabolites as phenotypic data combined with the FPKM matrix of DEGs (filtered for low expression and low variability). The analysis was conducted on the Metware Cloud platform (https://cloud.metware.cn). A soft thresholding power of RsquaredCut = 0.85 was selected, and co-expression modules were constructed using dynamic tree cutting (minimum module size = 50, merge height = 0.25). Furthermore, the expression patterns of modules across different developmental stages (HG, HP, HR) were visualized using a module-sample expression heatmap. Pearson correlation analysis was performed between module eigengenes and both developmental stages and differential metabolites to identify significantly correlated modules (|r|> 0.7, P < 0.05). Core genes within modules (module membership kME > 0.8 and correlation with target metabolites |r|> 0.7) were extracted for network visualization and functional enrichment analysis.

Statistical analysis

All results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data processing and summarization were performed using Microsoft Excel 2020. Statistical significance was evaluated using IBM SPSS Statistics 27. Graphical visualizations, including PCA plots, heatmaps, and hierarchical clustering, were generated via the Metware Cloud online platform. Radar charts were plotted using Origin 2024 software.
