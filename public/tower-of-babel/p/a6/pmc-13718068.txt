Unveiling compulsive buying behavior through the lens of loneliness: exploring the mediating mechanism of psychological vulnerability and fear of missing out
Authors: Rabindra Kumar Pradhan, Peeyush Anand, Shilpa Sabu, Adnaan Ahmad, Kailash Jandu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCompulsive buying is a growing behavioral concern not only in Western societies but also in emerging economies. Despite its rising prevalence, limited research has examined the psychosocial antecedents of compulsive buying within non-Western cultural contexts. Addressing this gap, the current study draws on the Stimulus–Organism–Response (S–O–R) framework to explore how loneliness may contribute to compulsive buying through internal psychological mechanisms.ObjectiveTo examine whether loneliness predicts compulsive buying behavior among Indian consumers and whether psychological vulnerability and Fear of Missing Out mediate this relationship.MethodUsing a cross-sectional correlational design, data were collected from 469 Indian participants selected via purposive sampling. Validated self-report measures were administered, and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was employed to test the hypothesized mediation model. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.ResultsThe findings revealed that loneliness significantly predicted compulsive buying behavior. Furthermore, psychological vulnerability and Fear of Missing Out partially mediated this relationship. These results highlight that when an individual’s need for belongingness is unmet, it can lead to compulsive buying as a compensatory behavioral response.ConclusionThis study advances S–O–R theory by demonstrating that loneliness, a persistent emotional condition arising from societal factors, functions as a stimulus activating psychological mechanisms that drive compulsive buying. It represents one amongst the few empirical examination of loneliness as an S-O-R stimulus in compulsive buying research and the first investigation of dual mediating pathways (psychological vulnerability and Fear of Missing Out) in an Indian context, offering culturally-grounded insights for interventions targeting maladaptive consumer behavior in emerging economies.

Introduction

Compulsive buying behavior (CBB), defined as recurrent, irresistible purchasing that serves as an impulsive response to negative emotions, has emerged as a significant behavioral concern in contemporary consumer societies [1, 2]. Unlike utilitarian consumption motivated by product value or necessity, compulsive buying reflects attempts to regulate mood, relieve stress, and seek social validation [3, 4]. While such behavior offers short-term emotional relief, it often leads to severe psychological distress, interpersonal conflict, financial strain, and persistent guilt [5, 6].

Recent estimates indicate that approximately 5–8% of the global population exhibits symptoms of CBB [6]. In India, its prevalence is rising rapidly, particularly among youth [7]. This escalation is propelled by an expanding digital economy, higher disposable incomes, and the frictionless accessibility of e-commerce and social media platforms [8]. Yet, research on the psychosocial antecedents of CBB remains largely Western-centric, leaving cultural nuances within emerging economies underexplored [9]. Understanding the psychological mechanisms that drive CBB in collectivistic yet increasingly individualistic contexts such as India is therefore critical.

The contemporary digital landscape has blurred the boundary between social interaction and consumption. Social media and online shopping environments amplify emotional triggers such as envy, comparison, and the fear of missing out (FoMO), each capable of fueling compulsive buying tendencies [10]. Among young Indians, constant exposure to curated lifestyles on digital platforms intensifies material aspirations and heightens pressure for social validation. Such conditions render them particularly vulnerable to emotional triggers that promote compensatory consumption behaviours [11].

Loneliness, an aversive emotional state arising from perceived deficiencies in social relationships, has recently drawn attention as a psychological driver of maladaptive behaviors [12]. The rise of urban living, solo households, and digital connectivity without emotional intimacy has made loneliness a growing public health concern, often termed a “modern epidemic.” For many individuals, shopping or online purchasing provides temporary relief from emotional isolation, functioning as a self-soothing mechanism [13]. Prior research shows that loneliness can impair emotion regulation, increase susceptibility to external validation, and foster psychological vulnerability, patterns closely aligned with compulsive buying [3].

Several converging societal trends have established loneliness as a chronic environmental condition among Indian youth. First, urban migration and the rise of solo living arrangements have disrupted traditional joint family structures, reducing face-to-face social support networks [14]. Second, the expansion of nuclear families has diminished the intergenerational support systems that historically buffered against social isolation [15]. Third, and critically, the pervasive use of social media among Indian youth has paradoxically intensified feelings of loneliness despite increased digital connectivity [8]. Collectively, these trends illustrate how structural and technological transformations in India have reshaped interpersonal relations, rendering young adults particularly vulnerable to emotional deprivation and the compensatory behaviours it fosters.

Psychological vulnerability refers to a maladaptive cognitive pattern characterized by dependence on achievement or external affirmation for self-worth [16]. Lonely individuals often display this vulnerability, relying on social comparison and external approval to sustain self-esteem. The proliferation of social media further intensifies this dynamic by constantly exposing users to others’ achievements and possessions, precipitating FoMO, defined as the apprehension that others are enjoying rewarding experiences without one’s participation [17]. Both psychological vulnerability and FoMO represent organismic processes that translate loneliness into behavioral responses such as CBB.

Guided by the Stimulus–Organism–Response (S-O-R) framework [18], the present study conceptualises loneliness as an intrinsic psychosocial stimulus that activates internal emotional (FoMO) and cognitive (psychological vulnerability) states, which in turn precipitate compulsive buying behavior as a coping response. Although the S-O-R model traditionally positions external cues as stimuli, recent research acknowledges internal affective states, such as social loneliness, as valid initiating stimuli that elicit psychological and behavioral reactions [19]. By integrating loneliness into the S-O-R paradigm, this study extends its explanatory scope from environmental triggers to psychosocial antecedents.

Accordingly, the present research advances existing literature in three ways. First, it theoretically expands the S-O-R framework by framing loneliness as an intrinsic stimulus rather than a boundary condition. Second, it identifies dual mediating mechanisms, psychological vulnerability and FoMO, that translate emotional deficits into compulsive consumption. Finally, it provides context-specific insights into CBB among Indian youth, contributing to the cross-cultural understanding of consumer psychology in emerging digital economies.

Present study

While there has lately been a surge in studies focusing on understanding compulsive buying behavior and its antecedents, a majority of them have focused on personality and stress factors [20, 21]. We try to extend this line of research in the present study by incorporating the psycho-social variable of loneliness in the stress-coping framework by considering it as an intrinsic stimulus resulting from a lack of quality interpersonal interactions. Thus, loneliness itself assumes the role of a stressor, which entails a suitable coping reaction from the individual. Furthermore, it has been highlighted in previous research that there is a need to explore the role of psychological variables that could act as mediators in the relationship between intrinsic factors and CBB [21]. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to examine the mediating mechanism of psychological vulnerability, an unhelpful cognitive belief, in the context of shopping behavior. Based on the extant literature and theoretical framework, the current study seeks to empirically examine the relationships among loneliness, compulsive buying behavior, FoMO, and psychological vulnerability. Besides, the present study investigates the mediating role of psychological vulnerability and FoMO in the relationship between loneliness and compulsive buying, as shown in Fig. 1.

Literature review and hypotheses development

Theoretical background

The present research draws upon the Stimulus–Organism–Response (S–O–R) framework [18], which explains how environmental or situational stimuli elicit internal psychological states (organism) that, in turn, drive behavioral responses. Initially developed in environmental psychology, the framework has been widely applied to consumer behavior to elucidate how external cues, such as store atmospherics, social influence, or digital stimuli, shape affective and behavioral outcomes [22, 23].

Recent theoretical advances have broadened the S–O–R model’s scope by recognizing that chronic psychological and emotional conditions, when arising from enduring environmental or societal contexts, can also function as stimuli influencing behavioral responses [19, 24, 25]. For example, Chen et al. [19] conceptualized social loneliness as an emotional stimulus within the S–O–R framework to explain tourist happiness, while Tan [24] treated internal social needs as stimuli driving smartphone addiction. These theoretical expansions demonstrate that when emotional conditions represent persistent environmental realities rather than fleeting moods, they legitimately operate as stimuli activating cognitive and emotional mechanisms.

Building on this contemporary interpretation, the current study conceptualizes loneliness as an intrinsic psychosocial stimulus that triggers organismic processes, specifically, psychological vulnerability (cognitive mechanism) and fear of missing out (FoMO), an emotional mechanism, culminating in compulsive buying behavior (CBB) as the behavioral response. Within this framework, loneliness serves as a chronic affective condition embedded in modern sociocultural environments, particularly in urban India, where digital hyper-connectivity paradoxically coexists with emotional isolation. The two organismic mediators, psychological vulnerability and FoMO, represent internal mechanisms translating loneliness into maladaptive consumer behavior.

Compulsive buying behavior

Compulsive buying represents a chronic, repetitive purchasing pattern driven by affective impulses rather than rational evaluation of product utility [1, 3, 4]. Characterized by irresistible urges and transient emotional relief followed by guilt or regret, it reflects a maladaptive coping strategy for regulating negative emotions [5, 6]. The psychological consequences of which are financial distress, interpersonal conflict, and low self-esteem, underscore its similarity to behavioral addictions.

Emerging evidence shows that the digital retail environment amplifies these tendencies by providing frictionless access, continuous stimuli, and algorithmic reinforcement of impulsive purchases [26]. Within collectivistic yet fast-modernizing societies such as India, compulsive buying among youth is increasingly visible [27]. Thus, understanding its psychosocial underpinnings requires examining how enduring emotional deficits like loneliness are cognitively and emotionally processed to produce compulsive consumption.

Loneliness and compulsive buying

Loneliness, the subjective perception of inadequate social connection, comprises social, emotional, and existential dimensions [12]. It has been widely recognized as a distressing emotional state linked to various maladaptive behaviors, including compulsive consumption. Empirical studies indicate that lonely individuals often engage in shopping to temporarily mitigate feelings of emptiness or isolation [3, 28].

Recent research underscores loneliness as a predisposing psychological condition contributing to compulsive buying tendencies [29]. The COVID-19 pandemic further accentuated this dynamic, with prolonged isolation intensifying emotional distress and promoting compulsive online consumption [30].

Within the S–O–R framework, loneliness is thus conceptualized as a stimulus, a chronic psychosocial condition that initiates internal emotional and cognitive processes leading to compensatory consumption. Consequently, the first hypothesis is proposed:H1: Loneliness will positively predict compulsive buying behavior.

Psychological vulnerability as a mediator

Psychological vulnerability reflects a maladaptive cognitive schema characterized by dependence on external affirmation and achievement for self-worth [16]. Individuals with high psychological vulnerability exhibit fragile self-concepts and heightened reactivity to stress or social evaluation [31].

Loneliness exacerbates this vulnerability by amplifying dependence on external validation and diminishing self-regulatory capacity [12]. Persistent social isolation heightens cognitive susceptibility to maladaptive coping, manifesting in compensatory behaviors such as compulsive buying [3, 31]. Within the S–O–R framework, psychological vulnerability thus represents a cognitive organismic mechanism through which loneliness, as a chronic stimulus, shapes consumption responses.

Accordingly, the second hypothesis is formulated:H2: Psychological vulnerability will mediate the relationship between loneliness and compulsive buying behavior.

Fear of missing out (FoMO) as a mediator

The fear of missing out (FoMO) refers to an individual’s apprehension that others are having rewarding experiences without their participation [17]. Social media intensifies FoMO by offering constant visibility into others’ activities and possessions, fostering feelings of inadequacy and exclusion [32].

Empirical evidence shows that loneliness positively predicts FoMO [33, 34]. Lonely individuals, deprived of fulfilling relationships, experience heightened sensitivity to social comparison and fear of exclusion. This emotional tension often propels them toward dysregulated buying behavior as a symbolic means of regaining belongingness or social validation [35].

In the present framework, FoMO functions as an emotional organismic mechanism, translating loneliness into compulsive buying by generating anxiety and an urge to participate vicariously through consumption. Therefore, the third hypothesis is proposed:H3: FoMO will mediate the relationship between loneliness and compulsive buying behavior.

Integrating these perspectives, the current research extends the S–O–R model by positioning loneliness as an intrinsic psychosocial stimulus that activates two organismic pathways—psychological vulnerability (cognitive) and FoMO (emotional), culminating in compulsive buying behavior as the response. This framework not only elucidates the emotional–cognitive mechanisms underlying CBB but also contextualizes the phenomenon within India’s sociocultural milieu, where digital consumerism and emotional disconnection coexist.

Method

Research design

The present study employed a cross-sectional, correlational research design to investigate the relationship between loneliness and compulsive buying, and the mediating roles of psychological vulnerability and Fear of Missing Out (FoMO). The Stimulus–Organism–Response (S–O–R) framework guided the conceptual model.

Sampling frame and strategy

Participants for this study were selected using a purposive sampling technique, which involves selecting individuals based on the researcher’s judgment regarding their relevance to the study’s objectives. Purposive sampling was appropriate for this study as it enabled the inclusion of participants relevant to the context of compulsive buying behavior, particularly individuals who engage in regular purchasing and social media activities associated with such tendencies. This approach is also referred to as judgmental sampling [36] and allows the researcher to identify participants who best meet the inclusion criteria [37]. The inclusion criteria required participants to be proficient in English and not have any diagnosed psychological disorders.

Data were collected both online (via Google Forms) and offline to ensure broader geographic reach and diversity. In total, 495 individuals responded to the survey. After applying data cleaning procedures, such as removing incomplete, inconsistent, or duplicate responses, the final sample comprised 469 valid responses used for analysis.

The adequacy of the sample size was confirmed using G*Power 3.1, which indicated that a minimum of 170 participants would be sufficient to detect a medium effect size (f² = 0.15) with 99% power at a 0.01 significance level in a model with three predictors. The final sample size of 469 thus far exceeded this threshold. In addition, it met the structural equation modeling (SEM) recommendation of maintaining an indicator-to-sample ratio of at least 10:1 [38].

Sample characteristics

The final sample included 469 participants (46.8% males and 53.2% females) from diverse demographic profiles. With a mean age of 22 years, the participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 40 years. The educational qualification of the respondents was as follows: undergraduate (45.7%), graduate (12.6%), post-graduate (22.3%), and doctorate (19.4%). Out of 469 participants, 84% were unmarried, and 16% were married. A total of 20.8% of the participants were from rural settings, 30.1% of the participants were from semi-urban settings, and 49.1% of the participants were from urban settings. While 44.6% of participants were students, 13.2% were government employees, 22.4% were working in the private sector, and 19.8% were unemployed.

Procedure

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Indian Institute of Technology Kharagpur and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [39] and the Belmont Report [40]. Participants were informed about the study’s purpose and design, their rights to privacy, autonomy, and voluntary participation, and the assurance of confidentiality. No incentives were provided, and participants could withdraw at any point without consequence.

To minimize potential bias, all participant queries were addressed through standardized written instructions and neutral clarifications that avoided suggestive or leading responses. Data collection was conducted via self-administered surveys, either online (Google Forms) or offline (paper-based), and involved no intervention, manipulation, or collection of sensitive or biological data. The procedures thus posed minimal risk to participants, and all were debriefed after completing the questionnaire.

Measures

Loneliness

UCLA Loneliness Scale (Version 3), developed by Russell [41], was used to measure the extent of loneliness among the participants. The scale consists of 20 items rated on a four-point Likert Scale (O= often feel this way, S= sometimes feel this way, R= rarely feel this way, and N = never feel this way). A high score on the scale indicates high self-reported loneliness. Sample items include “How often do you feel that you lack companionship?”. The UCLA-LS demonstrates robust psychometric properties across diverse cultural contexts [41, 42].

Psychological vulnerability

The Psychological Vulnerability Scale (PVS) developed by Sinclair and Wallston [16] was used to measure psychological vulnerability among the participants. The scale consists of six items rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Does not describe me at all) to 5 (Describe me very well). A higher score on the scale indicates higher psychological vulnerability. Sample items include “I feel entitled to better treatment from others than I generally receive.” Prior research has confirmed the PVS’s psychometric validity and cross-cultural stability, indicating its suitability for use in diverse populations [43, 44].

Fear of missing out

The extent of FoMO was assessed through a 10-item scale rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not all true of me) to 5 (Extremely true of me) adopted from the study of Przybylski et al. [17]. A higher score on the scale indicates a high anxiety about missing out on opportunities or social events. Sample items include “I fear others have more rewarding experiences than me.” Empirical evidence indicates that the FoMO scale possesses sound psychometric qualities across varied cultural settings [45, 46].

Compulsive buying

Richmond’s compulsive buying scale, developed by Ridgway et al. [47], was used to assess compulsive buying behavior among the participants. The scale consists of six items rated on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). A higher score on the scale indicates high compulsive buying behavior. Sample items include “Much of my life centers around buying things.” The Compulsive Buying scale has shown strong reliability and validity across multiple cultural contexts [47, 48].

Data analysis procedure

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS (v26) and AMOS Graphics (v24). Preliminary analyses included descriptive statistics, normality checks, and intercorrelations among the study variables. To assess multicollinearity, Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) and tolerance values were examined for all predictors.

An Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted using principal component analysis with varimax rotation to examine the underlying factor structure and assess item-level performance. Sampling adequacy and sphericity assumptions were evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity, respectively [49].

Following EFA, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed using maximum likelihood estimation in AMOS to evaluate the measurement model. Convergent and discriminant validity were assessed through Average Variance Extracted (AVE), Composite Reliability (CR), Maximum Shared Variance (MSV), and Average Shared Variance (ASV). In addition, Heterotrait–Monotrait ratio (HTMT) values were calculated to confirm discriminant validity.

Once the measurement model demonstrated acceptable fit, the structural model was tested to evaluate the hypothesised mediation paths. Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) with maximum likelihood estimation was used to estimate direct and indirect effects. Bootstrapping procedures with 5,000 bias-corrected samples and 95% confidence intervals were employed in AMOS to test the significance of mediation. An indirect effect was considered significant if the confidence interval did not contain zero.

Common method variance

To address the potential for common method bias during data collection, measures were taken to ensure participant anonymity by refraining from requesting any personally identifiable information that could link responses to specific individuals. Moreover, participants were instructed that there is no such thing as a right or wrong answer, and they are expected to respond honestly. Common method variance (CMV) was checked using Harman’s one-factor test [50]. The result showed that a single factor emerging from exploratory factor analysis using the principal axis factoring method was accountable for only 27.2% of the variance, less than the established cut-off value of 50%, indicating that CMV was not an issue for our study. Additionally, the common latent factor method was used to address the issue of common method bias [50]. It was found that the confirmatory factor analysis had a poor model fit (χ2 /df = 8.45, comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.41, Tucker-lewis Index (TLI) = 0.46, normative fit index (NFI) = 0.45, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) = 0.99, standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) = 0.92); thus, the possibility of the common method variance was ruled out in the present study.

Multicollinearity diagnostic

To assess multicollinearity among the independent variables, Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) and tolerance values were examined using linear regression. The VIF values for all predictors ranged from 1.331 to 1.381, and all tolerance values were above 0.70, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity [51]. These results suggest that the predictors used in the structural equation model are statistically independent and suitable for further analysis.

Exploratory factor analysis

To validate the measurement structure prior to confirmatory factor analysis, an Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted using principal component analysis with varimax rotation. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.902, indicating excellent sampling adequacy. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ²(903) = 5293.97, p < .001), confirming the appropriateness of factor analysis [52].

Four components with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, consistent with the hypothesized number of latent constructs: Loneliness, Psychological Vulnerability, FoMO, and Compulsive Buying. Items with substantial loadings (> 0.40) on their respective factors and minimal cross-loadings were retained. Based on the EFA results, L1 and L3 from the Loneliness scale and FO1 and FO2 from the FoMO scale showed weak or diffuse loadings and were thus identified as problematic. However, these items were ultimately removed based on confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to achieve optimal model fit, consistent with standard practices in scale purification and SEM [53]. The complete component matrix from EFA, including all item loadings and cross-loadings, is provided in Appendix Table 5.

Items with low factor loading (below 0.40) and high modification indices were removed to attain an acceptable fit [51].

Reliability and validity analysis

Convergent and discriminant validity were assessed to evaluate the construct validity of the measurement model. Convergent validity was established using average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR), following the criteria proposed by Fornell and Larcker [54]. Discriminant validity was assessed using both the traditional metrics of maximum shared variance (MSV) and average shared variance (ASV), as well as the more robust Heterotrait-Monotrait ratio (HTMT) recommended by Henseler et al. [55].

The standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.40 to 0.80, and all constructs demonstrated AVE values > 0.50 and CR values > 0.70, indicating satisfactory convergent validity (see Table 3). For all constructs, MSV and ASV values were lower than AVE, thereby confirming discriminant validity. In addition, the maximum HTMT value for each construct remained well below the conservative threshold of 0.85, with the highest being 0.10 for Fear of Missing Out and Compulsive Buying, further supporting discriminant validity.

Measurement model

After assessing the model fit of the individual constructs, the CFA of the full measurement model, which comprised three latent factors, namely loneliness, psychological vulnerability, and compulsive buying, was performed. The measurement model fit the data well with χ2 = 1180.75, χ2/df = 1.80, CFI = 0.92, NFI = 0.91, TLI = 0.93, SRMR = 0.047, and RMSEA = 0.050 CI (LO90 = 0.045, HI90 = 0.055). Therefore, it was concluded that latent variables can be represented by the data.

Structural model

Path analysis was performed after getting an acceptable full measurement model to test the hypothesized mediation model. The first structural model comprised loneliness as an independent variable and compulsive buying as a dependent variable. As per the results, the model showed good fit-indices (χ2 = 709.62, χ2/df = 1.87, CFI = 0.91, NFI = 0.92, TLI = 0.90, SRMR = 0.051, and RMSEA = 0.057, CI (LO90 = 0.051, HI90 = 0.063).

In the second model, psychological vulnerability was introduced as a mediator in the relationship between loneliness and compulsive buying. The model exhibited a good fit as indicated by model fit indices values χ2 = 956.94, χ2/df = 1.67, CFI = 0.90, NFI = 0.91, TLI = 0.93, SRMR = 0.048, and RMSEA = 0.05 CI (LO90 = 0.04, HI90 = 0.06).

With respect to hypothesis testing, the SEM results indicate that loneliness positively predicts compulsive buying (β = 0.27, 95% CI [0.13, 0.41], p < .01), leading to the acceptance of H1. Loneliness positively predicted psychological vulnerability (β = 0.52, 95% CI [0.44, 0.60], p < .001) and FoMO (β = 0.45, 95% CI [0.37, 0.53], p < .001). Furthermore, psychological vulnerability (β = 0.22, 95% CI [0.09, 0.35], p < .01) and FoMO (β = 0.27, 95% CI [0.13, 0.41], p < .01) positively predicted compulsive buying. Discussion

The present study provides empirical support for the hypothesis (H1) that loneliness significantly predicts compulsive buying behavior (CBB). This finding aligns with previous research suggesting that socially isolated individuals often engage in excessive consumption as a compensatory mechanism to fulfil unmet psychological needs such as belongingness and self-worth [3, 56]. Within the Indian context, where collectivistic cultural values coexist with increasing urban alienation, this result acquires heightened significance [29]. The complex social and emotional functions of consumption make loneliness an especially salient factor influencing consumer behavior. Lonely individuals often develop coping mechanisms such as materialistic attachment or reliance on possessions as substitutes for human relationships, leading to compulsive buying [57]. Social inclusion motives further drive these behaviors, as material acquisition temporarily alleviates feelings of social isolation [58]. These findings are consistent with earlier work by Faber and O’Guinn [2], who observed that compulsive buyers often lack meaningful interpersonal connections and turn to consumption for emotional relief.

Loneliness was also found to significantly predict psychological vulnerability, a cognitive condition marked by an externally validated sense of self-worth. This supports Cacioppo et al.’s [59] loneliness model, which posits that perceived social disconnection heightens vigilance toward social threats and increases dependency on others for validation [56, 59]. Lonely individuals frequently report elevated social anxiety, hypersensitivity to evaluation, and reliance on external affirmation [60]. These tendencies manifest as cognitive distortions such as excessive self-evaluation and perceived rejection [31], which collectively represent psychological vulnerability. This hypervigilance toward social threat fosters biases in social cognition, including expectations of negative evaluation and increased perceptions of rejection [61]. The findings corroborate prior evidence linking loneliness with maladaptive cognition and dependency on external validation [62].

Consistent with H2, psychological vulnerability significantly predicted CBB, reinforcing the conceptualization of compulsive buying as rooted in cognitive fragility rather than mere impulsivity [63]. Psychologically vulnerable individuals, characterized by fragile and discrepant self-concepts, are more likely to engage in compensatory consumption to momentarily restore self-esteem [3, 63]. This mechanism aligns with the compensatory model of consumption, which explains how buying temporarily regulates damaged self-image.

FoMO also showed a significant positive relationship with CBB, supporting H3. Individuals experiencing FoMO, particularly in digital environments, are more prone to impulsive and excessive buying [17, 64]. In this study, FoMO emerged as an emotional response mechanism mediating the link between loneliness and CBB. This aligns with findings by Hussain et al. [65], who showed that social media exposure and digital comparison amplify consumer anxiety and materialistic behavior in South Asian contexts. FoMO-driven consumers may engage in compulsive purchases to mirror others’ consumption or prevent perceived exclusion [66]. Among Indian youth, who are heavily engaged on social media platforms such as Instagram and WhatsApp, FoMO intensifies the urge to purchase items that symbolize belonging and participation in aspirational lifestyles [67].

Collectively, these results underscore that the cognitive and emotional mechanisms activated by loneliness operate both independently and interactively to shape consumer decision-making. The association between loneliness and CBB was partially explained by psychological vulnerability, highlighting those unmet psychological needs for belongingness and self-worth can lead to maladaptive coping through consumption. FoMO similarly mediated the loneliness–CBB relationship, suggesting that emotional discomfort from social exclusion drives impulsive purchases to simulate social inclusion [17, 66]. Together, these findings emphasize that loneliness triggers distinct but interconnected pathways, cognitive (psychological vulnerability) and emotional (FoMO), that converge in compulsive buying.

Importantly, this study makes a novel theoretical contribution by positioning loneliness as a psychosocial stimulus within the S–O–R framework. Traditionally, the “stimulus” in S–O–R models refers to external environmental cues such as marketing messages or store atmospherics. The current work extends this framework by demonstrating that a chronic internal state, loneliness, can function as an enduring situational stimulus capable of activating organismic responses. These responses include cognitive susceptibility (psychological vulnerability) and emotional apprehension (FoMO), which jointly drive behavioral outcomes such as CBB. This reconceptualization advances S–O–R theory by integrating enduring emotional and cognitive antecedents of consumption that emerge from persistent social–emotional conditions rather than transient environmental triggers.

The dual mediation model therefore provides a nuanced theoretical understanding: loneliness (stimulus) activates organismic processes, psychological vulnerability (cognitive) and FoMO (emotional), which together precipitate compulsive buying (response) [18]. This work extends prior research by showing that these mechanisms operate simultaneously rather than independently, addressing an important gap in the literature [68]. Furthermore, by emphasizing psychosocial rather than purely dispositional antecedents, the study shifts the focus from personality-based predictors (e.g., impulsivity, neuroticism) to contextual and affective dynamics that drive compulsive consumption [69]. In India’s rapidly digitalizing and urbanizing society, where emotional disconnection and online consumerism coexist, these findings provide culturally grounded insight into the psychological foundations of CBB.

Implications

The current study contributes to the growing body of knowledge on what makes people prone to compulsive buying by proposing a conceptual model grounded in the stimulus-organism-response (S-O-R) framework, by examining the underlying mechanism of how loneliness leads to compulsive buying tendencies through psychological vulnerability and FoMO. In doing so, it highlights the psychological and emotional dimensions of compulsive consumption in the context of increasing digitalization and socio-emotional disconnection, particularly among young adults in emerging economies like India.

Theoretical and practical implications

Theoretical implications

Grounded in the S–O–R theoretical framework, this study advances existing models of consumer behavior by conceptualising loneliness as a chronic emotional stimulus and integrating psychological vulnerability (cognitive) and FoMO (emotional) as dual organismic mediators. While previous applications of the S–O–R model have focused on external environmental triggers, this research demonstrates that persistent psychosocial states can similarly activate internal cognitive–emotional mechanisms that drive behavioral responses. By doing so, it extends the explanatory reach of S–O–R theory beyond environmental psychology to encompass emotionally driven consumption in digital and social contexts [18, 70].

Furthermore, introducing psychological vulnerability, a cognitive schema marked by dependence on external validation, as a mediating construct adds a novel dimension to consumer behavior research. This expands traditional frameworks of CBB that emphasize self-esteem or impulsivity [3, 63] by incorporating a psychosocial variable that links emotional disconnection to compensatory consumption. The findings also contribute to cross-cultural generalizability, illustrating that S–O–R processes manifest differently in emerging economies. In India’s collectivistic yet increasingly individualistic environment, loneliness interacts with digital consumerism to shape behavioral addiction patterns, enriching global understanding of cultural variability in CBB [71].

Practical implications

The findings carry significant implications for marketing ethics, consumer protection, and mental health intervention, particularly within India’s youth-driven digital economy. Urban Indian consumers are increasingly exposed to algorithmic advertising, social media influence, and aspirational lifestyles that normalize impulsive and emotion-driven consumption. The study highlights that individuals high in loneliness, FoMO, and psychological vulnerability are especially susceptible to such triggers.

Consumer behavior and targeting

Retailers and marketers should recognize that emotion-driven consumers constitute a psychologically vulnerable segment. Campaigns emphasizing scarcity or social comparison (e.g., “trending now,” “limited offer”) may inadvertently exploit FoMO and reinforce maladaptive consumption. Instead, marketers should adopt value-based storytelling, inclusive branding, and community-oriented engagement that foster authenticity rather than social competition.

Marketing ethics and strategy

In India’s expanding e-commerce sector, brands must move toward ethical digital marketing that prioritizes consumer well-being. FoMO-based advertising strategies should be replaced with messaging that promotes self-reflection, mindful spending, and sustainable consumption. Such approaches align with evolving consumer awareness about mental health and enhance long-term brand trust [72].

Psychological interventions and public health

For mental health practitioners, the results underscore the need for integrated interventions that address both cognitive and emotional mechanisms. Therapeutic programs, particularly cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), emotion regulation training, and group therapy, should target loneliness, maladaptive self-evaluation, and FoMO-induced anxiety. Social support interventions can buffer the negative effects of loneliness and promote healthier coping strategies [18, 73].

Culturally contextualized support in India

Given the erosion of traditional support systems due to urbanization, public mental health initiatives should emphasize community-based interventions, peer mentorship, and offline social spaces to rebuild connectedness among Indian youth. Programs grounded in local cultural narratives and languages are likely to yield greater engagement and long-term impact [68].

Digital literacy and awareness

Educational campaigns should cultivate digital literacy, helping young users recognize and regulate their emotional reactions to online comparison and advertising. Schools, universities, and corporate wellness programs can integrate digital well-being curricula to mitigate FoMO and impulsive online buying behaviors [74] .

Policy-level interventions

At a policy level, regulators could strengthen guidelines under the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020 and the Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI) to ensure responsible digital marketing practices. Transparency in influencer marketing and clearer labelling of emotionally charged advertisements could help protect vulnerable consumers from manipulation.

Limitations and future directions

Despite its contributions, this study has certain limitations. The sample was predominantly composed of young adults (mean age = 22 years; range = 18–40), reflecting a youth-heavy demographic profile. While this group is particularly relevant for examining compulsive buying in digitally active populations, the age concentration limits the generalizability of the findings to older consumers. Future research could broaden the age range to examine whether similar psychosocial mechanisms operate among middle-aged and older adults, or whether generational differences exist in how loneliness, psychological vulnerability, and FoMO influence CBB. Employing stratified or multi-group analyses could provide a more nuanced understanding of these age-based variations.

FAdditionally, the use of self-report measures may introduce response biases. Future studies could adopt longitudinal or experimental designs to better establish causal relationships among variables. Examining moderating factors such as gender, digital engagement, and socioeconomic status could also help contextualize the relationships observed.

Conclusion

In summary, this research advances theoretical understanding by reframing loneliness as a psychosocial stimulus that elicits both cognitive and emotional organismic responses leading to compulsive buying. Situated within India’s rapidly evolving digital ecosystem, the study provides culturally grounded evidence that psychological vulnerability and FoMO are key mediating mechanisms translating social disconnection into maladaptive consumption. Beyond its academic contribution, the study offers actionable insights for marketers, policymakers, and mental health professionals to foster ethical, sustainable, and psychologically informed practices in the era of digital consumerism.
