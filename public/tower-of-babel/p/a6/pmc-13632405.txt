Effects of reality orientation therapy on activities of daily living and other clinical outcomes in dementia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Nanami Mikami, Shogyoku Bun, Hiroaki Kazui, Naoto Kamimura, Tomoyuki Ohara, Kenji Yoshiyama, Ryo Shikimoto, Yu Mimura, Hiroyoshi Takeuchi, Masaru Mimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveReality orientation therapy (RO) is a widely used non-pharmacological approach aimed at maintaining cognitive abilities and alleviate dementia-related symptoms. Although some evidence supports its beneficial effects on cognitive function, its impact on activities of daily living (ADL) and behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD, or neuropsychiatric symptoms) remains unclear. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the effects of RO on ADL, BPSD, and cognitive function.MethodsThis review included individuals with dementia or cognitive impairment of neurodegenerative or vascular origin. Studies examining classroom-based or 24-hour RO interventions were included, with control groups receiving treatment as usual. Both randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental studies were considered.ResultsIn total, 16 studies were included in the systematic review, of which 13 were included in the meta-analysis; individual studies contributed to multiple outcomes (6 on ADL, 13 on cognitive function, 4 on depressive symptoms, and 4 on other BPSD). RO was associated with improvements in cognitive function (Standardized Mean Difference [SMD] = 0.60, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.43 to 0.77, p < 0.001) and reductions in depressive symptoms (SMD = -0.69, 95% CI = -1.37 to -0.00, p = 0.05). However, no significant effects were observed for ADL (SMD = -0.08, 95% CI = -0.45 to 0.30, p = 0.69) or other BPSD (SMD = -0.55, 95% CI = -1.96 to 0.87, p = 0.45).ConclusionsRO was associated with improvements in global cognitive function and depressive symptoms in individuals with dementia. However, no significant effects were observed on ADL or other BPSD. These results should be interpreted with caution given the methodological limitations, including study heterogeneity and potential biases. Future well-designed RCTs with larger sample sizes, longer intervention periods, and extended follow-up are needed to rigorously assess the clinical effectiveness of RO. Given its low cost and feasibility, RO may serve as a component of comprehensive dementia care. However, its role—particularly regarding functional outcomes and BPSD—warrants further evidence before it can be firmly defined. Introduction

The global prevalence of dementia is rising, with an estimated 55 million individuals affected in 2019 and projections reaching 139 million by 2050 [1].

Although pharmacological treatments such as cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine are commonly used for the symptomatic management of dementia, their clinical efficacy remains limited [2]. More recently, anti-amyloid antibody therapies have shown only modest effects on slowing decline in activities of daily living (ADL), including bathing, dressing, toileting, and transferring, and are associated with substantial costs and potential adverse events [3, 4]. Consequently, non-pharmacological interventions continue to play an essential role in dementia care.

One such non-pharmacological intervention is reality orientation therapy (RO), which was developed in the late 1950s and formalized in the early 1960s [5]. RO aims to reduce confusion and disorientation by drawing on preserved cognitive capacities and reinforcing basic orientation information, including personal identity, time, and place, while also addressing social challenges, including wandering and isolation [5]. RO sessions typically involve repeated verbal, visual, and tactile reinforcement of orientation cues [6].

RO is typically practiced in two forms: classroom RO, which consists of structured group sessions often using an orientation board displaying key information (e.g., date, weather, the next meal), and 24-hour RO, in which staff engage individuals in reality-based communication during daily care interactions [6].

Classroom RO is the most widely implemented format and has been associated with improvements in cognitive function in multiple studies [7–21], whereas fewer studies have examined 24-hour RO, although positive cognitive effects have also been reported [12, 16, 22]. These cognitive benefits can be cautiously interpreted in light of evidence suggesting that some learning capacity and cognitive plasticity may be preserved in early dementia [23], and that learning through observation and guidance may also be preserved to some extent in Alzheimer’s disease [24]. Repeated exposure to orientation cues may therefore engage preserved learning mechanisms and support cognitive function. Such cognitive improvements may also translate into functional benefits. A recent meta-analysis by Raimo et al. [25] reported a moderate association between global cognition and basic ADL among older individuals with or without cognitive impairment (r = 0.38). Although this association does not necessarily imply that cognitive improvement leads to functional improvement, it provides a rationale for examining whether RO-related cognitive benefits may also be accompanied by changes in ADL. In addition, maintaining orientation to time, place, and person may be important for ADL, including toileting, maintaining sleep–wake routines, and recognizing caregivers. RO may also be relevant to behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD, or neuropsychiatric symptoms), such as apathy, depression, agitation, and delusions. The Progressively Lowered Stress Threshold model, a conceptual framework for dementia care, suggests that behavioral symptoms may occur when individuals with dementia are exposed to external or internal demands that exceed their reduced ability to cope [26]. Disorientation to time, place, or person may increase this burden because it makes daily situations less predictable and difficult to understand. From this perspective, RO may help reduce BPSD by providing repeated and consistent orientation cues, thereby making the environment easier to interpret and narrowing the gap between such demands and the person’s coping capacity.

In recent years, RO has increasingly been incorporated into multi-component non-pharmacological interventions, such as cognitive stimulation therapy, rather than being implemented as a standalone therapy. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses examining cognitive stimulation therapy or RO combined with other non-pharmacological interventions have suggested potential improvements in both cognitive function and BPSD [27–29].

However, the therapeutic effect of RO as a standalone intervention remains unclear. Evaluating RO in isolation not only clarifies its independent therapeutic value but also provides insight into its significance within combination therapies.

Among the relevant outcomes, ADL warrants particular attention, as ADL decline in individuals with dementia is associated with poorer quality of life [30, 31], increased caregiver burden [32, 33], and higher rates of institutionalization [34, 35]. However, ADL has received limited attention in prior syntheses of standalone RO. To date, only two reviews have examined standalone RO [29, 36], and neither evaluated ADL as an outcome. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Spector et al. [6] reported improvements in cognition and behavior, but did not examine ADL; moreover, the included studies featured small samples and methodological limitations, lacking detailed descriptions of randomization procedures or analytical methods. A more recent meta-analysis by Chiu et al. [29] conducted primary analyses for cognitive function, behavioral symptoms, and depressive symptoms. However, these analyses included not only standalone RO but also RO delivered in combination with other interventions, such as reminiscence therapy or cognitive training; therefore, the main effect estimates do not isolate the effect of RO alone. A subgroup analysis for standalone RO was reported only for cognitive function (standardized mean difference [SMD] = 0.50), whereas no analogous standalone-RO analyses were provided for behavioral symptoms or depressive symptoms, and ADL was not evaluated. Consequently, the specific effects of standalone RO on ADL and depressive symptoms remain unclear. Additionally, Chiu et al. restricted inclusion to randomized controlled trials (RCTs), which minimized potential biases but may have limited generalizability; incorporating quasi-experimental studies may better capture the real-world effectiveness of standalone RO, given that much of the foundational RO literature consists of non-randomized studies published in the 1980s and the 1990s.

Given this knowledge gap, the present study aims to update and extend prior findings on standalone RO by designating ADL as the primary outcome, with cognitive function and BPSD as secondary outcomes, and including both RCTs and quasi-experimental studies.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to PRISMA guidelines [37] and was registered in PROSPERO on August 9, 2024 (CRD42024572672). During first-stage screening, the comparator criterion was amended on March 10, 2025, from ”usual care, no treatment, or other non-pharmacological interventions” to ”usual care or no treatment”, to better isolate the specific effect of RO. This amendment was made prior to full-text screening and data extraction, without knowledge of the outcome data of any included study. This revision is documented in the PROSPERO registry.

Search strategy and selection criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study (PICOS) design framework [38]. We included original studies (RCTs or quasi-experimental designs) involving individuals with dementia or cognitive impairment of neurodegenerative or vascular origin, evaluating classroom or 24-hour RO compared with usual care or no treatment, and reporting outcomes related to ADL, cognitive function, or BPSD. Here, the term “vascular origin” was used broadly to encompass dementia or cognitive impairment attributed to cerebrovascular diseases, including multi-infarcts or other vascular mechanisms. Given that a substantial portion of the RO literature predates modern diagnostic criteria for neurodegenerative and vascular cognitive disorders, we adopted this inclusive operational definition to ensure inclusion of foundational studies in which participants were described using historical terminology (e.g., “senile dementia” or “organic brain syndrome”).

The comparator labelled as “usual care” or “no treatment” referred to one of two broad conditions, depending on the participants’ care setting: (i) routine institutional care in nursing homes, residential homes, or psychogeriatric wards, without any structured cognitive or psychosocial intervention; or (ii) outpatient or community-based care, comprising either standard outpatient management alone or family caregiving without a formal therapeutic program. We excluded studies involving cognitive impairment due to non-neurodegenerative causes (e.g., infection or traumatic brain injury), those evaluating RO in combination with other interventions, studies lacking a control group, non–peer-reviewed publications, and conference papers or theses.

We used the following electronic databases: MEDLINE, CENTRAL, CINAHL, Scopus, and Web of Science. To ensure a comprehensive search and avoid omitting relevant studies, search terms were limited to those related to the intervention and participants’ condition. In addition to the electronic database searches, we conducted manual searches for relevant articles and screened the reference lists of all included studies to identify additional eligible studies. We only included studies published in English or Japanese, and considered articles published up to March 10, 2025.

Details of the search terms are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Study selection and data extraction

Using the search strategy described above, we retrieved publications from each database from inception to March 10, 2025. Four authors (SB, NM, RS, and YM) independently screened titles and abstracts for relevance. Subsequently, full-text articles were reviewed for eligibility. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved by consensus, and if consensus could not be reached, a fifth reviewer (MM) served as an arbitrator. Corresponding authors were contacted to obtain missing data; studies for which the necessary summary statistics could not be obtained or derived were excluded from the meta-analysis.

We extracted data on all reported outcome measures within the predefined outcome domains. The primary outcome was improvement in ADL, and secondary outcomes comprised cognitive function and BPSD. When multiple scales were used within the same outcome domain in a single study, we followed a pre-specified hierarchy to select one measure for the meta-analysis: (1) a scale with established psychometric properties for the target construct (e.g., the Mini-Mental State Examination for cognitive function), (2) the scale most frequently used across the included studies to maximize comparability in pooled analyses, and (3) the primary outcome as designated by the original authors. For each study, change scores from baseline to post-intervention were extracted as the primary effect estimates. When outcomes were assessed at multiple post-intervention time points (e.g., end of intervention and longer-term follow-up), only the assessment closest to the end of the intervention was used, to ensure comparability across studies.

Two authors (SB and NM) independently extracted data using a pre-designed standardized form. The following information was collected from each included study: authors, publication year, study design, participant characteristics (age, diagnosis, and sample size), details of the intervention and comparison conditions, outcome measures, and main results (e.g., means, standard deviations [SD], and effect sizes). Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (MM).

Risk of bias (quality) assessment

Two authors (SB and NM) independently assessed the risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [39] for RCTs, and the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool [40] for quasi-experimental studies. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (MM) served as an arbitrator. Risk-of-bias plots were generated using the robvis web application [41] to visualize the judgments obtained from the RoB 2 and ROBINS-I assessments.

Strategy for statistical analysis

The meta-analysis was conducted in R (version 4.4.1) using the metafor package with a random-effects model. Effect sizes were calculated from means and standard deviations (SDs), with missing SDs derived from t-values, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), or standard errors. The treatment effect was estimated using Hedges’ g with 95% CIs. Given the limited number of available RCTs across outcomes (ranging from none to three), we pre-specified the pooled analysis of RCTs and quasi-experimental studies as the primary analysis. Design-stratified estimates are presented within the same forest plots to facilitate direct inspection of each design’s contribution. Additionally, we conducted exploratory subgroup analyses according to the type of RO intervention, classifying studies as classroom RO, 24-hour RO, or combined classroom plus 24-hour RO. These analyses were considered exploratory because only one study evaluated 24-hour RO alone, and the three studies combining classroom and 24-hour RO did not allow us to isolate the independent effect of 24-hour RO. BPSD outcomes were classified into depressive symptoms and other BPSD, as several studies used depression-specific scales such as the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) [42]. Statistical heterogeneity between studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic. Substantial heterogeneity was defined as a p < 0.1 in the Cochran’s Q test or an I² ≥ 50%. Consistent with the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) guidance [43], publication bias was assessed for outcomes with at least 10 included studies using Egger’s and Begg’s tests; a p < 0.05 in either test was defined as indicative of publication bias. The trim-and-fill method was used to estimate corrected effect sizes.

Results

Study selection

Figure 1 presents the flow diagram of the study selection for the present systematic review and meta-analysis. A total of 540 records were identified through the initial search. After removing duplicates (n = 207), 333 records remained for screening. Of these, 285 were excluded based on the titles and abstracts, leaving 48 records. Along with 3 additional studies identified through manual searching, a total of 51 studies underwent full-text review. Following a thorough assessment, 16 studies were included in this systematic review. Of these, 13 met the inclusion criteria for the meta-analysis. All 13 studies assessed overall cognitive function, 8 evaluated BPSD, and 6 assessed ADL. Studies that appeared to meet the inclusion criteria but were excluded after full-text review are presented in Supplementary Table 2, along with the primary reasons for exclusion.

Study characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the included studies. Participants were identified using study-specific diagnostic criteria or cognitive assessment scales, most commonly the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [44] and the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke–Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Disorders Association (NINCDS-ADRDA) criteria [45]. Of the 16 studies, 14 focused on dementia, while 2 focused on cognitive impairment [7, 21]. Among the studies on dementia, 9 specifically addressed Alzheimer’s disease [8–12, 15–17, 19]. Mean participant age ranged from 69.8 to 84.5 years across studies. Only one study explicitly specified a minimum age criterion (≥ 60 years) [21]. Additionally, 8 studies reported age ranges. Ten studies reported the sex distribution of participants, with females outnumbering males in 7 studies. Notably, 2 studies [8, 14] included exclusively female participants. Twelve studies employed classroom RO as the intervention, 1 employed 24-hour RO, and the remaining 3 employed a combination of both interventions, with treatment as usual serving as the control condition. The frequency of sessions ranged from once a week to daily, with each session lasting between 30 and 60 min. The intervention period ranged from 3 weeks to 12 months, resulting in a total number of sessions ranging from 10 to 240. The 13 studies included in the meta-analysis had sample sizes ranging from 10 to 156 participants, totaling 636 individuals with dementia or cognitive impairment. Three eligible studies [11, 13, 21] were excluded from the meta-analysis because the required summary statistics were unavailable, either due to data no longer being accessible or inability to locate author contact information despite reasonable efforts.

Methodological quality of the included studies

Tables 2 and 3 summarize the risk of bias across all included studies. Only one study [16] reported randomization and allocation concealment methods. Four studies implemented blinding of the outcome assessors [11, 12, 16, 17]. Nine studies reported attrition rates ranging from 0% to 25% [8–11, 13, 16, 17, 20, 22].

Activities of daily living

Systematic review

ADL was evaluated in 8 studies [8, 12, 13, 16–19, 21], including 2 RCTs and 6 quasi-experimental studies. The outcome measures used in these studies included the Katz Index [46], Stewart’s Activities of Daily Living [47], Barthel Index [48], Lawton Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale [49], and the Geriatric Rating Scale-Activities of Daily Living [50].

No study reported a statistically significant improvement in ADL between the intervention and control groups. Additionally, 3 of these studies [12, 16, 17] also investigated the effects on instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), but none demonstrated statistically significant differences between the intervention and control groups.

Meta-analysis

A total of 6 studies were included in the meta-analysis [8, 12, 16–19]. The single RCT was rated as having some concerns (RoB 2), and the 5 quasi-experimental studies were rated as having moderate to serious risk of bias (ROBINS-I). As shown in Fig. 2a, the pooled effect of RO intervention on ADL was not statistically significant (standardized mean difference [SMD] = -0.08, 95% CI = -0.45 to 0.30, p = 0.69). Substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed (Q = 12.0, p = 0.03; I² = 60.5%). Sensitivity analyses excluding each study one by one yielded consistent, non-significant results. Heterogeneity markedly decreased only after exclusion of the study by Zepelin et al. [18] (p = 0.44, I² = 12%). Publication bias was not assessed due to the limited number of included studies. Subgroup analyses by study design (RCTs vs. quasi-experimental studies) yielded non-significant effects, with no evidence of a statistically significant subgroup difference (χ² test for subgroup differences: p = 0.38).

Global cognitive function

Systematic review

Sixteen studies, including 5 RCTs and 11 quasi-experimental studies, assessed the effects of RO on global cognitive function. The outcome measures used in these studies included MMSE, Cognitive Assessment Scale (CAS) of Clifton Assessment Procedures for the Elderly (CAPE) [51], the Koskela Test [52], the Mental Status Questionnaire (MSQ) [18], the Test for Reality Orientation with Geriatric Patients (TROG) [14], Scales for Outcomes in Parkinson’s Disease–Cognition [53] and Kingston Dementia Rating Scale [54].

In 14 of the 16 studies, the RO group showed significant improvements in cognitive function compared with the control group. The effect sizes in each study ranged from 0.16 to 1.36. However, two studies [18, 21] did not observe significant improvements.

Meta-analysis

The meta-analysis conducted on 13 studies is shown in Fig. 3a. The three RCTs were rated as having some concerns to high risk of bias (RoB 2), and the 10 quasi-experimental studies were rated as having moderate to serious risk of bias (ROBINS-I). Global cognitive function was significantly improved in the RO group compared with the control group (SMD = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.43 to 0.77, p < 0.001).

Forest plot (a) and funnel plot (b) of the meta-analysis on cognitive function

The funnel plot for publication bias is presented in Fig. 3b. No heterogeneity was observed between the studies (Q = 10.6, p = 0.56; I² = 0%). Egger’s (p = 0.09) and Begg’s (p = 0.31) tests showed no evidence of funnel plot asymmetry. The trim-and-fill adjusted effect size was 0.52 (95% CI = 0.35 to 0.68 p < 0.001), consistent with the original significant effect. Subgroup analyses by study design (RCTs vs. quasi-experimental studies) showed statistically significant effects in both groups. A χ² test for subgroup differences (p = 0.29) confirmed consistent benefits across study designs, comparable to the main analysis.

Behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia

Systematic review

Ten studies, including 3 RCTs and 7 quasi-experimental studies, assessed the effects of RO on BPSD. Outcome measures used in these studies include the Clifton Assessment Procedures for the Elderly-Behavioral Rating Scale (CAPE-BRS) [55, 56], the GDS [42], 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) [57], the Geriatric Rating Scale [50], the Modified Beck Depression Inventory [58], and the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) [59]. One study [18] employed a custom rating scale developed by the research group.

Of the 10 studies, 4 [7, 8, 15, 22] reported statistically significant improvements in the RO group, while the remaining 6 [13, 16–19, 21] found no significant effects. Five studies [8, 15, 17, 19, 21] specifically examined depressive symptoms, with 2 [8, 15] showing significant improvements. Among 5 studies [7, 13, 16, 18, 22] that assessed other BPSD, such as apathy and disruptive behavior, two [7, 22] reported statistically significant effects.

Meta-analysis

A meta-analysis of depressive symptoms used data from four quasi-experimental studies [8, 15, 17, 19] (Fig. 4), all of which were rated as having moderate to serious risk of bias (ROBINS-I). The pooled results showed a statistically significant effect of RO on depressive symptoms (SMD = -0.69, 95% CI = -1.37 to -0.00, p = 0.05); however, moderate heterogeneity in effect sizes was suggested across the included studies, although the heterogeneity test did not reach conventional statistical significance (Q = 7.6, p = 0.06; I² = 63%). Given the limited number of studies, publication bias and sensitivity analyses were not performed.

A separate meta-analysis on other BPSD, based on four studies [7, 16, 18, 22] (Fig. 5a), found no significant effect of RO (SMD = -0.55, 95% CI = -1.96 to 0.87, p = 0.45). The two RCTs were rated as having some concerns to high risk of bias (RoB 2), and the two quasi-experimental studies were rated as having serious risk of bias (ROBINS-I). Heterogeneity among studies was significant (Q = 20.6, p < 0.01; I² = 91%). Publication bias was not assessed due to the limited number of included studies. Subgroup analyses by study design (RCTs vs. quasi-experimental studies) also demonstrated statistically non-significant effects in both subgroups. No significant heterogeneity in effect sizes was observed between the two subgroups (χ² test, p = 0.94).

Exploratory subgroup analyses by RO format

The effects of RO, stratified by RO format, are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1a–c. For ADL (Supplementary Fig. 1a), neither the classroom RO subgroup (three studies; SMD = 0.12, 95% CI = -0.28 to 0.53, p = 0.56) nor the combined classroom plus 24-hour RO subgroup (three studies; SMD = -0.29, 95% CI = -1.05 to 0.47, p = 0.45) showed a significant beneficial effect, and no study evaluated 24-hour RO alone. The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (p = 0.43). For cognitive function (Supplementary Fig. 1b), the classroom RO subgroup showed a significant beneficial effect (nine studies; SMD = 0.77, 95% CI = 0.52 to 1.01, p < 0.001), and the combined classroom plus 24-hour RO subgroup also showed a smaller but significant beneficial effect (three studies; SMD = 0.41, 95% CI = 0.17 to 0.66, p < 0.001). The single study evaluating 24-hour RO alone showed an effect estimate in the same favorable direction (SMD = 1.04, 95% CI = 0.05 to 2.02, p = 0.04). The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (p = 0.097). For other BPSD (Supplementary Fig. 1c), no clear beneficial effect was observed for the classroom RO subgroup (one study; SMD = -1.41, 95% CI = -3.06 to 0.25, p = 0.10) and the combined classroom plus 24-hour RO subgroup (two studies; SMD = 0.44, 95% CI = -0.18 to 1.05, p = 0.16). The single study evaluating 24-hour RO alone showed a favorable effect estimate (SMD = -2.30, 95% CI = -3.59 to -1.01, p < 0.001). Although the omnibus test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (p = 0.001), this finding was based on sparse data, with only one or two studies in each subgroup, and should be interpreted cautiously. No RO-format subgroup analysis was performed for depressive symptoms because all studies included in this outcome used classroom RO.

Discussion

RO is a long-standing non-pharmacological intervention that has been widely used in both clinical and everyday care settings. Its simplicity and adaptability have contributed to its broad application in dementia care. However, despite its widespread use, the effectiveness of RO on key clinical outcomes has not been systematically evaluated. One such outcome is ADL, given that cognitive impairment can compromise an individuals’ ability to perform ADL, thereby reducing their independence and quality of life. Therefore, evaluating the impact of RO on ADL is essential to understanding its clinical relevance and potential to support functional autonomy. However, previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses on RO have primarily focused on cognitive outcomes, with limited attention to ADL. The present review addresses this gap by examining the effects of RO on ADL, while also assessing its impact on cognitive function and BPSD. Our work extends prior syntheses, particularly the most recent meta-analysis by Chiu et al. [29] in four key respects. First, to our knowledge, this is the first review to designate ADL as the pre-specified primary outcome for standalone RO. Second, although Chiu et al. conducted pooled analyses for behavioral symptoms and depressive symptoms, the studies they included combined standalone RO with other interventions such as reminiscence therapy and cognitive training, and subgroup estimates for standalone RO were reported only for cognitive function. Therefore, the present study provides the first quantitative synthesis of the effect of standalone RO on depressive symptoms as a distinct outcome, and the first update on other BPSD since Spector et al. [6]. Third, whereas Chiu et al. restricted inclusion to RCTs, we additionally incorporated quasi-experimental studies, which capture a substantial portion of the foundational RO evidence base and improve external validity for real-world care. Fourth, our search extends the literature by approximately seven years (through March 2025) and restricts comparators to usual care or no treatment, while Chiu et al. permitted active comparators, including cognitive intervention or physical activities. This approach helps isolate the specific effect of RO from the effects of active comparator interventions. Our cognitive-function findings are consistent with the standalone-RO subgroup estimate of Chiu et al. and represent a replication and extension under a more rigorous, pre-specified standalone-only design, whereas our findings on ADL, depressive symptoms, and other BPSD provide new information that was not available from prior syntheses. The last review focusing on BPSD as a primary outcome of standalone RO was conducted over two decades ago by Spector et al. [6], further underscoring the need to re-evaluate these outcomes in light of more recent evidence.

Activities of daily living

Across the 8 studies included in this systematic review, no statistically significant effects of RO on ADL were observed, regardless of the study design (RCT vs. quasi-experimental study) or intervention type (classroom RO or classroom RO plus 24-hour RO). The meta-analysis of 6 studies also yielded a non-significant effect, and sensitivity analyses excluding each study confirmed the consistency of these results. Taken together, these findings suggest that short-term standalone RO (i.e., up to 12 months), whether delivered as classroom RO alone or as classroom RO combined with 24-hour RO, may have little or no measurable effect on ADL. This has important clinical implications, suggesting that short-term RO alone may be insufficient to produce clinically meaningful improvements in ADL.

Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to methodological variations across studies. For example, in the study by Zanetti et al. [17], the intervention was interrupted by 5- to 7-week rest periods between one-month active intervention phases. Across the included studies, the cumulative intervention time varied widely, ranging from 945 to 7,200 min. Such heterogeneity in dose and delivery may have compromised the accurate estimation of RO’s effects on ADL. Future trials should therefore consider more standardized, sustained, and intensive interventions.

Beyond methodological issues, several factors may explain the absence of beneficial effects on ADL. First, although our analysis showed that RO improved global cognitive function, this improvement may have been insufficient to produce measurable changes in basic ADL. Raimo et al. [25] reported a moderate association between global cognitive function and basic ADL in individuals with dementia, which supports the rationale for examining ADL as a potentially cognition-related outcome of RO. However, such an association does not necessarily imply that modest improvements in global cognition will translate into detectable functional gains, because functional performance may not change in parallel with global cognition alone. Consistent with this interpretation, the most recent Cochrane review of cognitive stimulation therapy [27] — which incorporates RO together with other non-pharmacological activities targeting multiple cognitive domains — reported only small effect-size estimates on basic ADL (SMD = 0.19, 95% CI -0.03, 0.41, I2 = 0%, moderate-quality evidence). These findings suggest that the cognitive benefits associated with RO may not be large enough, or may not be of a type that readily produces clinically detectable improvements in ADL. Second, ADL performance depends substantially on physical abilities such as mobility and balance, which are not directly targeted by RO. In this respect, an overview of systematic reviews and meta-analyses examining the effect of exercise on ADL in individuals with mild cognitive impairment or dementia found significant positive effects of exercise — particularly multicomponent programs including cognitive, physical, and multitasking exercises — on ADL [60]. From this perspective, RO may be more plausibly conceptualized as one component of a multidomain intervention — combined with physical exercise and cognitive training targeting other domains such as executive function and memory — rather than as a standalone strategy for improving ADL. In summary, future well-designed, adequately powered trials should consider longer intervention durations and follow-up periods, together with multidomain designs that combine RO with other non-pharmacological interventions including physical exercise and other cognitive training.

Cognitive function

Sixteen studies assessed the effects of classroom-based RO on global cognitive function, and 14 of them reported significant improvements. The remaining 2 studies did not demonstrate significant effects. Among these, Scanland’s study [21] reported six deaths during the four-month intervention period, which may have influenced the estimated intervention effect.

The meta-analysis of 13 studies also suggested a beneficial effect of RO, yielding a pooled effect size of 0.60 (95% CI = 0.43 to 0.77, p < 0.001) [6].This result aligns closely with that reported by Spector et al. (2000) [6] and with the standalone-RO subgroup estimate from Chiu et al. [29], which found similar effect sizes (SMD = 0.59, 95% CI = 0.22 to 0.95; and SMD = 0.50, 95% CI = 0.25 to 0.76, respectively). However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Most of the pooled studies were quasi-experimental in design, and several were judged to be at overall serious risk of bias. The convergence of effect estimates across reviews therefore provides only suggestive evidence of a beneficial effect of RO on cognitive function, and adequately powered, well-designed RCTs with low risk of bias are needed before firmer conclusions can be drawn.

Behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia

Depressive symptoms

Of the 5 included studies, 2 [8, 15] reported significant improvements in depressive symptoms, whereas the other three [17, 19, 21] did not. Although the meta-analysis of four studies [8, 15, 17, 19] yielded a statistically significant pooled estimate, this finding should be interpreted with considerable caution. All four studies employed a quasi-experimental design, raising concerns about selection bias and confounding, and the pooled analysis was further limited by moderate heterogeneity and the small number of included studies (n = 4). If a true effect exists, it may partly reflect the mechanisms proposed by the Progressively Lowered Stress Threshold model, in which repeated orientation cues reduce the burden associated with disorientation [26]. More speculatively, supportive and socially engaging therapeutic atmosphere associated with RO may also contribute to improvements in depressive symptoms. Further high-quality RCTs employing standardized outcome measures are needed before any conclusion can be drawn.

Other behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia

Five studies examined the effects of RO on other BPSD, two of which [7, 22] reported symptom improvement. A meta-analysis of four of these studies yielded a non-significant effect. Although the point estimate was in the direction of improvement (SMD = -0.55, 95% CI = -1.96 to 0.87), the confidence interval was wide and crossed the null, and the heterogeneity was substantial (Q = 20.6, p < 0.01; I² = 91%). Therefore, the present findings are insufficient to determine whether standalone RO improves other BPSD. These findings contrast with the pooled results for depressive symptoms, which suggested a possible beneficial effect. This apparent discrepancy may partly reflect the limitations of the present meta-analyses. Another plausible explanation is that other BPSD, such as apathy, agitation, psychotic symptoms, or disruptive behavior, may have more heterogeneous origins and may require more individualized or multidomain approaches. Our findings differ from those of Spector et al. [6], who reported a significant benefit (SMD = -0.64, 95% CI = -1.20 to -0.08). However, their meta-analysis included only 24 participants across five studies and used outcome measures that incorporated ADL components, thereby limiting their specificity for BPSD. In contrast, the present meta-analysis focused on outcome measures more closely aligned with the definition of BPSD and included a larger total sample. Given the small number of studies, high risk of bias, and substantial heterogeneity, this result should be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no effect.

Exploratory analyses by RO format

To explore potential differences across RO formats, we conducted exploratory subgroup analyses by intervention type. Classroom RO and combined classroom plus 24-hour RO showed significant beneficial effects on cognition, whereas the single study evaluating 24-hour RO alone showed an effect estimate in the same favorable direction. In contrast, no clear beneficial effects were observed for ADL or other BPSD, except in the single study evaluating 24-hour RO alone for other BPSD. These results should be interpreted cautiously because this study [22] was a quasi-experimental study with serious risk of bias. The evidence for other BPSD was particularly sparse, with only one classroom RO study, two combined classroom plus 24-hour RO studies, and one 24-hour RO study. Therefore, the other BPSD subgroup results, including the significant omnibus test for subgroup differences, should be regarded as unstable rather than evidence of reliable differences between RO formats. In addition, studies combining classroom and 24-hour RO provide only indirect information on the possible additional contribution of 24-hour RO, because they did not directly compare classroom RO alone with classroom plus 24-hour RO within the same trial. Further well-designed studies are needed to clarify the independent effects of 24-hour RO and its possible additive effects when combined with classroom RO.

Implications for future research and clinical practice

The findings of the present review have implications for both future research and clinical practice. Future trials should include outcomes beyond cognitive measures, incorporating ecologically valid assessments of functional ability and BPSD to clarify the clinical utility of RO. Future RCTs should also test whether longer intervention periods, higher session frequency, or 24-hour RO formats can produce functional gains that were not observed in the present review. Beyond standalone RO, combining RO with other non-pharmacological interventions — particularly physical exercise — may be especially promising, given the important role of physical ability in ADL performance. For clinical practice, the available evidence remains limited in both quantity and methodological quality, with most included studies being quasi-experimental and at moderate-to-serious risk of bias. Accordingly, clinicians should interpret the apparent cognitive benefits observed in the present review with caution when making treatment decisions. However, given its low cost and feasibility, RO may still be considered as one component of a broader multimodal care approach while awaiting higher-quality confirmatory evidence.

The strengths of this study include its novel focus on the effects of RO on ADL in individuals with dementia—an outcome that, to our knowledge, has not been addressed in previous systematic reviews or meta-analyses. In addition, the use of treatment as usual in the control groups may have reduced the risk of underestimating the effects of RO, as unintended interventions in the control condition have been noted in a previous study [6]. While the value of RCTs is well recognized, the inclusion of quasi-experimental studies that met predefined quality criteria may have enhanced statistical power and improved the generalizability of the findings to daily clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, the pooling of RCTs and quasi-experimental studies in the primary analysis, while increasing statistical power, may have introduced bias due to the inherent methodological differences between designs. Additionally, the considerable heterogeneity in intervention content, such as frequency or length of intervention, may have introduced bias, because observed differences may partly reflect variation in how RO was implemented rather than the efficacy of the intervention per se. Furthermore, the selection of a single measure when multiple scales were available for the same outcome domain may have introduced outcome-reporting bias.

Second, publication bias could not be formally assessed for ADL, BPSD, and depression because the number of included studies for each of these outcomes was below the threshold at which funnel plot asymmetry tests yield reliable results.

Third, some older studies used broad definitions of dementia or cognitive impairment. This may have contributed to clinical heterogeneity in the study populations and may limit the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, the content of “usual care” likely varied between studies even within our two broad categories (institutional routine care and outpatient standard care), potentially contributing to between-study variability in effect estimates. In addition, potential confounders such as sex differences were not adequately addressed.

Fourth, most available data were derived from classroom-based RO, highlighting the need for future studies evaluating 24-hour RO delivered in home-based settings. Furthermore, analyses were restricted to short-term effects, as all interventions lasted 12 months or less, limiting inference on long-term outcomes in this progressive condition.

Finally, the exclusion of three eligible studies from the meta-analysis due to the unavailability of summary statistics may have affected the pooled estimates. In addition, restricting the analysis to English and Japanese publications may have resulted in incomplete evidence.

Conclusion

The present systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that RO may be associated with improvements in global cognitive function and depressive symptoms in individuals with dementia. However, no significant effects were observed for ADL or other BPSD, indicating that the functional benefits of standalone RO remain unproven. In light of the limitations noted above, these results should be interpreted with caution, and the clinical relevance of RO remains to be fully established. Future well-designed studies with larger sample sizes, longer intervention periods, and extended follow-up are needed to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of standalone RO. Given its low cost and feasibility, RO may still serve as one component of comprehensive dementia care. However, its role—particularly with respect to functional outcomes—warrants further evidence before it can be defined.
