Effects of a clinical metagenomics intervention on clinical outcomes, healthcare costs, and health-related quality of life in patients with sepsis or septic shock: results of the randomized-controlled DigiSep trial
Authors: Thorsten Brenner, Annabell Skarabis, Stefan J Schaller, Thilo von Groote, Christian Putensen, Ulf Günther, Martin Sauer, Sebastian O Decker, Fabian Dusse, Manfred Weiss, Klaudiusz Suchodolski, Tim-Philipp Simon, Peter Rosenberger, Onnen Moerer, Matthias Unterberg, Jens-Christian Schewe, Hendrik Bracht, Selina Hutzl, Manuel Feißt, Ursula Marschall, Patrick Brandenburg, Philip Stevens, Juliana Schmidt, Mathias W Pletz, Marc M Berger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEarly pathogen detection is crucial in sepsis. We hypothesized that detection of microbial circulating cell-free DNA by metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) improves clinical outcomes and health-related quality of life without increasing healthcare costs.MethodsThis randomized, controlled, interventional, open-label, multicenter trial was conducted in 24 intensive care units across Germany. The intervention group (n = 200) received mNGS diagnostics in addition to standard-of-care microbiology, compared with standard-of-care microbiology alone (control group; n = 189). The primary endpoint was the Desirability of Outcome Ranking/Response Adjusted for Duration of Antibiotic Risk (DOOR/RADAR) score.ResultsThe DOOR/RADAR score was not significantly improved at 28 days after sepsis onset (intervention group: 3.21 ± 1.54; control group: 3.49 ± 1.51; 95% CI − 0.58 to 0.03). However, other secondary endpoints were improved, including a reduced duration of mechanical ventilation (intervention group: 6.6 ± 9.4 days; control group: 9.3 ± 10.6 days; 95% CI − 5.03 to − 0.34) and faster shock resolution (intervention group: 6.9 ± 7.4 days; control group: 8.8 ± 8.5 days; 95% CI − 3.75 to − 0.04). Health-related quality of life at 90 days (EQ-5D-5L) was improved in the intervention group (0.312 ± 0.386) compared with the control group (0.208 ± 0.373; p = 0.047). In the subgroup with available claims data (33.2% of participating patients), healthcare costs over 180 days did not differ.ConclusionThe DOOR/RADAR score as primary endpoint was not significantly improved by mNGS. Exploratory secondary analyses revealed improvements in secondary endpoints. Take home message

In patients with sepsis or septic shock the detection of microbial circulating cell-free DNA by metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) in addition to routine microbiological diagnostics failed to improve the DOOR/RADAR score as primary endpoint in the intervention group. Exploratory secondary analyses suggest that mNGS may optimize anti-infective therapy, thereby improving clinical as well as patient-centered outcomes. However, further studies are needed to confirm these exploratory and hypothesis-generating findings.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. With up to 50 million cases and ~ 11 million deaths annually, sepsis accounts for nearly 20% of worldwide deaths [2]. Early initiation of therapy is associated with improved outcomes [3] and current guidelines recommend the administration of anti-infectives in adults with suspected septic shock or a high likelihood of sepsis within 1 h [4]. In particular, critically ill septic patients who receive inadequate empirical anti-infective therapy face higher risks of adverse outcomes [5]. Therefore, early identification of sepsis-causing pathogens is critical. Although culture-based techniques represent the diagnostic gold standard, they are associated with significant weaknesses (e.g., low sensitivity/specificity) [6]. The detection of circulating cell-free DNA through metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has proven to be a promising alternative [7]. While previous studies have demonstrated that mNGS is applicable for identifying causal pathogens in septic patients, a prospective multicenter evaluation of the mNGS-based approach in a large patient cohort was lacking [7–15]. Therefore, the multicenter non-interventional Next GeneSiS trial was conducted to evaluate the performance characteristics of the mNGS-based approach and its potential impact on anti-infective treatment regimens in patients with sepsis [16]. This study revealed a positivity rate of 70.5% for mNGS-based diagnostics, compared with 19.4% for blood cultures (BCs), within the first 3 days after sepsis onset. Based on expert recommendations, additional knowledge of mNGS findings would have led to adjustments in anti-infective treatment in 32.6% of cases. Notably, these potentially inadequately treated patients exhibited significantly worse outcomes compared with those receiving appropriate treatment. However, because of its non-interventional design, the Next GeneSiS trial and several other clinical studies did not allow a definitive conclusion regarding the possible benefit of mNGS diagnostics in septic patients [8, 14–20]. Thus, the objectives of this randomized, controlled, interventional DigiSep trial were to evaluate the effects of an mNGS-based approach for pathogen identification in patients with sepsis or septic shock on clinical outcomes, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), and healthcare costs in real-world scenarios [21].

Methods

Trial design

The DigiSep trial was a randomized, controlled, interventional, open-label, multicenter study that enrolled patients with sepsis or septic shock in intensive care units (ICUs) across 24 hospitals of the German Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care (GSAIC) Trials Group [21]. See Supplemental Methods for additional details.

Patients

Adult patients aged ≥ 18 years with sepsis or septic shock and with a diagnosis within < 24 h were eligible for study inclusion [1]. Exclusion criteria were age < 18 years, refusal to give consent, anticipated discharge from the ICU within the first 72 h following inclusion, and primary palliative treatment intent. In addition, patients were not included if clinicians were not committed to aggressive treatment, and if death was deemed imminent or inevitable. Readmitted patients who had previously been included during the same hospitalization could not be included a second time. All patients were treated according to current sepsis guidelines [4, 22]. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal representatives prior to study inclusion.

Randomization and blinding

Patients were randomized (1:1) into either the control group (CG) or the intervention group (IG). Patients were unable to infer their group allocation due to their general state of health and the identical nature of study visits in both groups. Blinding of the attending physicians was not possible. See Supplemental Methods for additional details.

Interventions

Pathogen diagnostics in the CG were performed by standard-of-care (SOC) microbiological analyses, supplemented by optional infectious disease consultations. In the IG, a comprehensive digital precision diagnostics concept was implemented, comprising mNGS-based diagnostics in addition to SOC microbiological analyses and optional infectious disease consultations. For the IG, samples were transferred overnight by a logistics integrator, after which mNGS-based analyses were conducted using the CE-IVD-marked DISQVER® test (Noscendo GmbH, Duisburg, Germany), as further described in Supplemental Methods. In both groups, the choice of anti-infective therapy and the duration of treatment were based on the local standard and not dictated by the study protocol to better reflect everyday clinical practice.

Sample collection

Standard-of-care microbiological analyses of potential pathogens in the different specimens were performed according to current sepsis guidelines [22] and the usual practice in each participating institution (including rapid multiplex PCR if it was part of the local standard). Blood culture diagnostics included the collection of BC sets (2 × aerobic/2 × anaerobic) at baseline and 3 days later. At the same time and from the same site, blood samples were obtained for mNGS-based pathogen diagnostics. Additional sampling for mNGS-based diagnostics could be performed up to day 14 whenever the attending physician requested further BC collection. Routine microbiological findings from other biological samples were also considered for retrospective expert panel evaluation (see below) if obtained within 3 days before or after the extraction of blood samples for mNGS-based diagnostics.

Data collection and management

Baseline data were collected at the time of sepsis diagnosis. Clinical data were assessed at baseline and at 3, 7, 14, and 28 days thereafter. Outcome evaluations were conducted at 28, 90, and 180 days after sepsis onset. Cost data for each patient for an individual period of 180 days across different health service areas were extracted from claims data provided by involved statutory health insurance providers (BARMER, AOK Rhineland/Hamburg, and Techniker Krankenkasse Hamburg), thereby continuously capturing healthcare costs from a payer perspective. HRQoL was assessed using the EQ-5D-5L questionnaire at baseline and at 90 and 180 days. See Supplemental Methods for additional details.

Study outcomes

The DigiSep trial primarily evaluated whether additional mNGS-based diagnostics improve the Desirability of Outcome Ranking/Response Adjusted for Duration of Antibiotic Risk (DOOR/RADAR) score [23]. The DOOR component reflects the overall clinical outcome, consisting of ICU/intermediate care unit (IMC) length of stay (LOS), 28-day mortality, and acute kidney injury requiring kidney replacement therapy, whereas the RADAR component reflects the duration of anti-infective treatment. A detailed list with additional outcomes is provided in Supplemental Table S1. Safety outcomes were based on adverse-event reporting.

Retrospective expert panel evaluation

The potential clinical value of the mNGS-based approach was assessed by three independent specialists in infectious diseases or intensive care medicine, who retrospectively evaluated the plausibility and therapeutic relevance of the mNGS and BC results, as further described in Supplemental Methods. This retrospective expert panel evaluation of the potential diagnostic utility of mNGS in patients with sepsis or septic shock was exploratory and hypothesis-generating in nature.

Statistical methods

Sample size This calculation was based on a differentiated outcome analysis of study patients from four major German sepsis studies and has been described in detail in the study protocol [21]. With 180 patients per study arm, a power of 90% could be achieved under the assumption that with a relative effect p of 0.6 (common language effect size), a patient from the IG would have a lower DOOR/RADAR score than one from the CG. Allowing for a dropout rate of 12.5%, a total of 410 randomized patients (n = 205 per arm) were required.

Analyses of clinical outcomes Because no interim analysis had been prespecified, the primary hypothesis was first analyzed after study completion using the Mann–Whitney U test in the intention-to-treat population. Missing DOOR/RADAR scores were replaced using multiple imputation methods. The imputation model was based on age, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score at sepsis onset, and survival, and it assumed that missing values for the primary outcome were “missing at random”. In addition, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and descriptive p values for the effect estimates are reported. More detailed information on secondary outcome analyses is given in the Supplemental Methods. The study protocol had prospectively specified the use of an expert panel to retrospectively assess the maximum potential benefit of mNGS diagnostics. The independent responses were analyzed based on majority rule and are described with descriptive methods. All statistical tests were two-sided and performed using R version 4.2.0 in a validated environment. Detailed information on the statistical methods was prespecified in a statistical analysis plan finalized before database closure (Supplementary Appendix).

Analyses of healthcare costs and HRQoL Direct healthcare costs per patient were calculated by summing costs across different health service areas within each patient’s individual 180-day observation period. Outliers in total costs were excluded based on the 99th percentile. HRQoL was descriptively assessed using both the EQ-5D-5L index and visual analog scale. The data were not normally distributed, so the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann–Whitney U) was applied for group comparisons.

Results

Recruitment, participant flow, and patient characteristics

In total, 409 patients were recruited between March 16, 2022, and August 8, 2023. After the exclusion of 20 patients (CG: n = 11, IG: n = 9), 389 patients (CG: n = 189, IG: n = 200/group imbalance due to center-stratified block randomization) were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The characteristics of patients in the two study groups were comparable (Table 1, Supplemental Results).

Primary and secondary outcomes

The DOOR/RADAR score as primary endpoint did not differ between the study groups (Table 2, Supplemental Table S2). Moreover, survival was comparable at 28 days (CG: 26.1%; IG: 25.3%) as well as at 180 days (CG: 40.2%; IG: 32.5%; p = 0.174/Fig. 2). However, mNGS-based diagnostics were associated with reductions in both the absolute and relative duration of ventilation, and with a shorter time to shock resolution (Table 2). No adverse events were reported.

HRQoL

The EQ-5D-5L index suggested a short-term improvement in HRQoL at 90 days in the IG (IG: 0.312 ± 0.386; CG: 0.208 ± 0.373; p = 0.047), but no difference at 180 days (IG: 0.220 ± 0.370; CG: 0.236 ± 0.386; p = 0.926; Supplemental Figure S3).

Healthcare costs

Within the representative cohort of patients for which costs data were available (33.2%), mean healthcare costs per patient, calculated over 180 days, were also comparable between the study groups (IG: 96 719.22 ± 104 559.40 €; CG: 99 196.95 ± 111 391.80 €; p = 0.883; Supplemental Tables S3a, b).

NGS-based performance characteristics and results of the retrospective expert panel

Positivity rate

At baseline, the positivity rate of mNGS diagnostics was more than four times higher than that of BCs (Fig. 3a). By day 3, the positivity rates decreased for both diagnostic approaches; however, at this time point, the rate for mNGS remained nearly 10 times higher than that of BCs (45.9% vs. 4.7%; Fig. 3a). When summarizing all time points, the overall positivity rate of mNGS diagnostics was approximately sixfold higher than that of BCs (Fig. 3b–d, Supplemental Results).

Plausibility

In an exploratory and hypothesis-generating analysis of the potential diagnostic utility of mNGS (using a retrospective expert panel evaluation), there was a plausibility rate of 84.5% for positive mNGS findings across all time points (Fig. 3e). For negative mNGS findings, plausibility was 41.8% at baseline but increased to 92.6% by day 3 (Fig. 3f, Supplemental Results). Plausibility of BC findings was not systematically assessed within the retrospective expert panel evaluation.

Therapeutic relevance of positive and negative findings

In this retrospective expert panel evaluation, positive and plausible mNGS findings were judged to be therapeutically relevant in 95.9% of cases, whereas positive BC results were considered therapeutically relevant in 83.8% of cases (Fig. 4a). Importantly, therapeutically relevant and plausible positive findings from mNGS (n = 351) occurred nearly five times more frequently than therapeutically relevant and positive findings from BCs (n = 62) (Fig. 4a). By contrast, plausible negative mNGS (n = 199) and negative BC findings (n = 617) were rarely classified as therapeutically relevant, although this therapeutical relevance was six times higher for mNGS findings (25.6% vs. 4.1%) (Fig. 4b). When positive and negative results were considered together, mNGS demonstrated a six times higher therapeutic relevance (78.4%) than BCs (12.6%) (Fig. 4c). In absolute terms, therapeutically relevant findings from mNGS diagnostics occurred four times more often than from BCs (Fig. 4c).

Therapeutic consequences of mNGS diagnostics

Therapy changes based on mNGS diagnostics, in addition to BCs, were implemented by attending physicians in only 14.3% of IG patients, with escalation being the most frequent therapy change (55%), followed by de-escalation (30%) and a combination of both (15%). However, retrospective expert assessment suggested that anti-infective treatment should have been modified in 32.3% of CG patients with a completely diametrical pattern (de-escalation: 62.3%, escalation: 21.3%, no majority: 16.4%) (Supplemental Figure S5a, b). To assess the maximum potential benefit of mNGS diagnostics, the primary outcome was re-evaluated in the CG according to the retrospective expert panel’s recommendations (change [n = 61] vs. no change [n = 128]). This exploratory and hypothesis-generating re-analysis indicated that mNGS-based pathogen diagnostics could have significantly improved the DOOR/RADAR score (Supplemental Table S7) as well as survival (Supplemental Figure S6a, b). Notably, CG patients for whom escalation of therapy was recommended presented the poorest survival (Supplemental Figure S5c, d). Detailed statistical metrics for the consistency of the expert panel ratings are provided in Supplemental Table S8a, b.

Discussion

The DigiSep trial is the first randomized-controlled trial to investigate the potential benefits of an mNGS-based approach for pathogen detection in septic patients admitted to the ICU [21]. Although the primary outcome did not show an improvement, it demonstrated enhanced microbial detection, while several secondary outcome analyses generated hypotheses that warrant further investigation. Because the primary endpoint was neutral and no adjustment for multiplicity was applied to the secondary outcomes, all secondary findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

Rapid pathogen detection by mNGS is a promising tool for identifying unknown infections from body fluids [15, 17, 18, 24]. To date, a causal impact of the mNGS-based approach on clinical outcomes in patients with sepsis has not been demonstrated, and its relevance for patients’ HRQoL and healthcare costs has likewise remained unclear. In line with several other studies, the DigiSep trial demonstrated a six times higher rate of positive mNGS findings compared with standard BC diagnostics [14, 25, 26]. Beyond the higher positivity rate, the main pathogen findings from plasmatic mNGS and BC diagnostics were broadly comparable. However, mNGS detected the respective pathogens significantly more often and was less prone to contamination. This is particularly important given that false positives due to contamination or translocation are considered a potential limitation of mNGS [8, 27]. In general, it needs to be considered that pathogen detection does not necessarily equal infection and therefore needs to be carefully evaluated for every assay. For instance, while previous studies have demonstrated a rapid clearance of the microbial signal upon source control [28], in cases of disseminated infections, positivity based on microbial DNA is prolonged in comparison to blood culture growth [29].

In the DigiSep trial, positive mNGS findings were deemed plausible in nearly 85% of cases and were judged by the retrospective expert panel to have therapeutic relevance in more than 90%. Implausible positive mNGS results were in 69% due to the vote “Pathogen does not fit clinical symptoms” (Supplemental Figure S4b). In these cases, mNGS revealed a purely viral result in 77%. While these are not considered contaminations, they are suitable examples to illustrate the point of presence of DNA in contrast to infection.

These diagnostic performance metrics underscore the potential clinical utility of mNGS. Several secondary outcomes favored the intervention group, although the mechanisms underlying these findings remain uncertain. This finding is consistent with the previous investigations, all of which were non-interventional and therefore allowed for hypothesis generation rather than providing causal evidence [18, 30].

Despite the outcome benefits of secondary endpoints associated with mNGS in the DigiSep trial, we did not observe a significant improvement in the DOOR/RADAR score in the IG. This could be due to the low rate of therapy changes based on mNGS diagnostics, which were documented in only 14.3% of patients. By contrast, studies conducted by our research group have shown that mNGS-based therapy changes are typically recommended in 30–50% of cases [9, 16]. Similarly, a retrospective analysis of pediatric oncology patients with suspected bloodstream infections showed that mNGS findings influenced antimicrobial regimens in 54.3% of samples [20]. Thus, the rate of therapy changes based on the mNGS results was lower than had been expected based on previous studies. Notably, the study protocol prospectively specified the use of a retrospective expert panel to evaluate the maximum potential benefit of mNGS diagnostics [21]. This panel concluded that modification of anti-infective treatment would have been indicated in 32.4% of patients, which is in line with the previous studies [25]. The discrepancy between expert recommendations and observed clinical practice may reflect challenges in integrating mNGS findings into routine decision-making. Why a change in the anti-infective treatment regimen occurred in only a relatively small proportion of cases was not systematically assessed and, therefore, remains speculative. We cannot determine the extent to which mNGS findings actually reached the treating clinician at the patient’s bedside, even though they were provided via an online portal. In addition, a lack of confidence and limited experience with the new diagnostic method may also have contributed to the mNGS results not being consistently taken into account. However, the lack of adherence to mNGS findings should not be related to the complexity of the reporting, as it does not differ from the presentation of classic microbiological findings (Supplemental Figure S7a–c).

The expert panel’s assessment revealed a significantly higher DOOR/RADAR score as well as other improved secondary outcomes in patients for whom mNGS-based adjustments of anti-infective therapy were recommended. Interestingly, the subgroup for which an escalation of anti-infective treatment was recommended had the worst outcome. These findings, which—however—can only be regarded as hypothesis-generating, suggest that failure to consider therapy-relevant pathogens negatively impacts outcomes in patients with sepsis. They further emphasize the urgent need for targeted anti-infective regimens, best achieved through early integration of mNGS- and culture-based microbiological diagnostics, combined with intensive training measures and timely infectious disease/microbiology consultations to ensure optimal adherence to findings [31, 32].

However, even with the low rate of therapy changes, several exploratory secondary outcomes favored the intervention group. In the subgroup with available claims data, healthcare costs did not differ between study groups, suggesting no signal of increased costs despite clinical improvements, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Notably, while there were differences in the duration of ventilation, these did not translate into differences in overall or inpatient costs. This may suggest that the observed variations in resource utilization were not substantial enough to affect total healthcare expenditures, which are likely driven by multiple factors that were similar between the groups. Moreover, HRQoL was significantly improved in the IG at day 90, although this effect was not sustained at day 180. This may reflect a transient benefit of earlier targeted treatment that diminishes over time. In the HRQoL analysis, mortality was accounted for by assigning a utility of zero in the case of death, thereby reducing this source of bias. Furthermore, missing HRQoL data among surviving patients at follow-up cannot be fully excluded; however, as this issue could affect both the day 90 and day 180 assessments, it is unlikely to fully explain the observed pattern. Whether broader implementation of mNGS diagnostics and improved integration into antimicrobial decision-making would affect costs or HRQoL will require evaluation in future studies [31, 33, 34].

Several limitations of the DigiSep trial require consideration. As an open-label trial, attending physicians were aware of group assignments. This knowledge could have subconsciously influenced clinical decisions related to secondary outcomes, such as weaning from ventilation or vasopressors. Despite countermeasures intended to promote adherence to mNGS findings, the low rate of mNGS-based therapy adjustments in IG patients (with a completely diametrical response pattern of physicians in everyday clinical practice compared with the expert panel) and the limited use of optional infectious disease consultations (IG: n = 2 vs. CG: n = 0) suggest otherwise. Therefore, mandatory consultations whenever results were transmitted and clear standardization for empiric anti-infective therapy, mNGS-guided therapy, and treatment duration would have been advisable. Furthermore, the need to perform mNGS in external reference laboratories led to a substantial, primarily logistics-related delay until the results were available, which likewise hinders the concept of optimizing anti-infective therapy at the earliest possible time. Although the time-to-report delivery for mNGS diagnostics was documented, the time-to-result for BCs was not systematically captured. Consequently, no direct comparison can be made regarding the potential time advantage of mNGS diagnostics over BCs, as described in the literature [18, 30], representing a missed opportunity to evaluate such a new ‘rapid’ diagnostic tool. For an unrestricted comparison of the performance characteristics between BC and mNGS diagnostics, it would have been helpful to assess not only the plausibility of the mNGS findings but also the plausibility of the BC findings. This is based on the fact that BC is regarded as the diagnostic gold standard for sepsis/septic shock, but it is characterized by numerous diagnostic limitations. In addition, the relevance of the mNGS and BC findings was assessed by an independent expert panel, taking into account not only the respective findings themselves but also the complete set of additional microbiological results as well as other biomarkers and the patient’s clinical condition. However, a certain degree of subjectivity in the assessment of relevance of the mNGS and BC cannot be ruled out. Even though a significant improvement in HRQoL could be observed at day 90 in the IG, the reasons for its disappearance by day 180 are uncertain and would thus be pure speculation. Finally, because the study was funded by the Innovation Fund of the Joint Federal Committee (G-BA), healthcare cost analyses were limited to patients insured by BARMER, AOK Rhineland/Hamburg, and Techniker Krankenkasse Hamburg, representing 33.2% of the cohort. Because this subgroup was shown to be representative for the overall population, the healthcare cost analyses can be regarded as robust.

In conclusion, the detection of microbial circulating cell-free DNA by mNGS in addition to routine microbiological diagnostics in patients with sepsis or septic shock failed to improve the DOOR/RADAR score as primary endpoint. However, several secondary outcomes favored the intervention group, so that further studies with adequate adherence to mNGS findings and consistent integration of mNGS results into anti-infective treatment planning are needed to confirm these hypothesis-generating findings.
