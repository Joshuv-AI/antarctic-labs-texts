Intraoperative dexmedetomidine reduces postoperative sleep disturbance in older adults undergoing major abdominal surgery: a single-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial
Authors: Hui-ping Peng, Jing-hui Hu, Li-ping Fan, Xiu Yang, Hua-yue Liu, Xi-sheng Shan, Hong Liu, Nazneen Sudhan, Fu-hai Ji, Ke Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Postoperative sleep disturbance (PSD) after major abdominal surgery in older adults is common and impairs recovery. Dexmedetomidine is a selective α2-adrenergic agonist with sedative, analgesic, and anti-inflammatory properties that promotes natural sleep. We hypothesized that intraoperative dexmedetomidine would reduce PSD in this population.Methods:In this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 210 older adults undergoing major abdominal surgery were randomly assigned (1:1:1) to receive intraoperative normal saline, dexmedetomidine 0.3 μg/kg/h, or 0.6 μg/kg/h. The primary outcome was PSD on the first postoperative night (Athens Insomnia Scale score ≥ 6). Secondary outcomes included PSD on until nights 30; pain at 24 and 48 h; delirium and depressive symptoms within 7 days; and recovery quality on postoperative days 1‒3. Sleep patterns on nights 1 and 2 were assessed using wrist actigraphy. This trial was registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Identifier: ChiCTR2300073163; principal investigator: Ke Peng; dated July 23, 2023; https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=199410).Results:203 patients (median age 71 years; 67.5% male) completed this trial. Compared with normal saline (n = 67), both low-dose (n = 68) and high-dose dexmedetomidine (n = 68) significantly reduced first-night PSD (20.6% vs 65.7%; relative risk 0.31, 95% CI 0.19–0.50; NNT 2.2; P < 0.001; and 11.8% vs 65.7%; relative risk 0.18, 95% CI 0.09–0.34; NNT 1.8; P < 0.001, respectively). The two doses did not differ. Dexmedetomidine was associated with lower sleep disturbance on night 2 and higher recovery quality, without between-group differences in hypotension, bradycardia, delirium, or 30-day mortality. Exploratory actigraphy analysis (n = 20 per group) showed longer total sleep time and lower wake-time percentage associated with dexmedetomidine.Conclusions:Intraoperative dexmedetomidine infusion reduced the relative risk of first-night PSD by 70–80% among older adults undergoing major abdominal surgery. These findings require confirmation in multicenter trials.

Introduction

As global populations age, growing numbers of older adults undergo major abdominal surgery. Aging shortens total sleep time, reduces deep sleep and advances circadian phase, predisposing patients to postoperative sleep disturbance (PSD)1,2. PSD can intensify pain, trigger delirium and depression, delay recovery, prolong hospital stay, and increase healthcare costs3–6. Current intervention strategies such as environmental control, psychological support, melatonin, and benzodiazepines offer limited benefit or carry cognitive side effects, underscoring the need for a safe and effective prophylactic strategy.

Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective α2-adrenergic agonist with sedative, analgesic and anti-inflammatory properties, activates endogenous sleep-promoting pathways and induces a non-rapid-eye-movement stage N2-like state7,8. Perioperative administration of dexmedetomidine has been associated with improved sleep quality, but previous trials were conducted primarily in younger patients or mixed-age populations9–11. Older adults exhibit distinct sleep architecture changes, altered dexmedetomidine pharmacokinetics, and higher PSD risk after major surgery. Consequently, geriatric-specific evidence remains sparse and inconsistent, underscoring the need for a dedicated trial.

We conducted this single-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to test whether intraoperative dexmedetomidine reduces the incidence of PSD compared with normal saline in adults aged 65 years or older undergoing major abdominal surgery, and whether the effect differs between the high and low doses. We hypothesized that dexmedetomidine infusion would lower PSD risk and improve recovery quality, with a greater effect at the higher dose.

Our findings show that intraoperative dexmedetomidine at 0.3 and 0.6 μg/kg/h significantly reduces the incidence of first-night PSD compared with placebo, without increasing adverse events. The findings suggest that low-dose dexmedetomidine may be a useful adjunct in the perioperative care of older adults undergoing major abdominal surgery.

Methods

Ethics and study design

This randomized, double-blind, three-arm, placebo-controlled trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (approval No. 2023-160) and registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (identifier: ChiCTR2300073163; principal investigator: Ke Peng; dated July 23, 2023) before the first patient enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written consent was obtained from all participants. Reporting follows the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guideline (Supplementary Data 1). The protocol has been published12. The statistical analysis plan was developed as part of the study protocol, which was published before the first patient enrollment and is provided in Supplementary Data 2.

Patients

We enrolled patients aged ≥ 65 years, ASA physical status I − III, scheduled for elective laparoscopic or open major abdominal surgery with an expected duration ≥ 2 h and planned postoperative ward care. An expected surgical duration of ≥ 2 h was applied to define major abdominal surgery according to standard perioperative criteria13,14. Exclusion criteria were pre-existing sleep disturbance (Athens Insomnia Scale [AIS] score ≥ 6); heart rate <50 beats/min, sick sinus syndrome, second-degree or greater atrioventricular block without a pacemaker, or cardiac insufficiency; hepatic or renal failure; chronic use ( ≥ 90 days) of opioids, sedatives, antidepressants, or anxiolytics; and schizophrenia, epilepsy, myasthenia gravis, or Parkinson’s disease.

Randomization and blinding

A researcher not involved in recruitment, clinical care, data collection, or outcome assessment performed online block randomization (1:1:1, permuted blocks of 3 and 6) using the online tool (https://www.91trial.com). The generated allocations were printed and sealed in opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes. On the day of surgery, a research nurse not involved in subsequent care or assessments opened the envelope, prepared study drug in identical 50-mL syringes, and delivered it to the anesthesia team. All solutions were clear and colorless fluids indistinguishable by sight. Patients, clinicians, outcome assessors, and the statistician remained blinded to group assignment.

Anesthesia and perioperative care

Patients were admitted to the hospital the day before surgery. A trained investigator screened eligibility, collected baseline data, and completed the preoperative assessment of sleep quality using the AIS15, depressive symptoms using the 15-items Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15)16, frailty status using the FRAIL (fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illness, and loss of weight) scale17, and cognitive function using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE).

To minimize circadian disruption and the potential impact on study outcomes, all surgical procedures were scheduled between 08:00 and 13:00. All patients were cared for in standardized two-bed surgical wards (approximately 22–25 m2) with uniform sleep-hygiene measures implemented (dim lighting, noise reduction, ear plugs, eye masks, avoidance of non-urgent nighttime procedures).

After routine monitoring and radial artery cannulation, anesthesia was induced with propofol 1.5–2 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.3 µg/kg, and cis-atracurium 0.2 mg/kg. Maintenance consisted of sevoflurane 1–3%, titrated to bispectral index 40–60, and remifentanil 0.1–0.2 µg/kg/min, titrated to mean arterial pressure within 20% of baseline and heart rate 50–100 beats/min. Postoperative analgesia comprised i.v. flurbiprofen 50 mg at wound closure followed by patient-controlled intravenous fentanyl (background 1 mL/h, bolus 2 mL, lock out 10 min) for 48 h; no regional blocks were used.

Study interventions

Study drug infusion began immediately after induction and continued until skin closure. Patients received dexmedetomidine infusion at a rate of 0.3 µg/kg/h (the DEX-0.3 group) or 0.6 µg/kg/h (the DEX-0.6 group), or volume-matched normal saline placebo infusion (the normal saline group). These infusion rates reflect routine clinical dosing and are in line with a recent real-world cohort study18.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of PSD on the first postoperative night, defined as an AIS score ≥ 615. The AIS was chosen over more comprehensive instruments (e.g., Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index) because its brevity facilitates postoperative assessment, and it has been validated for insomnia severity in various surgical patients including Chinese cohorts11,15,19–24. Patients were asked to rate their sleep quality in the ward by a blinded assessor (HPP or LPF).

Secondary outcomes included PSD on nights 2, 3, 5, 7 and 30; pain at rest and on movement at 24 and 48 h postoperatively, assessed using the 0‒10 numerical rating scale; postoperative delirium within 7 days, assessed using the 3-min Confusion Assessment Method25; depressive symptoms within 7 days, assessed using GDS-15; and quality of recovery at 24, 48 and 72 h, assessed using the 15-items quality of recovery scale (QoR-15)26,27.

Prespecified exploratory sleep patterns were recorded on the first two postoperative nights in consecutively recruited patients using a consumer activity wristband (Mi Band 7)28. The sample size (n = 60) was determined by the exploratory nature of these data and logistical constraints (limited device availability and dedicated research personnel). Variables included total sleep period, total sleep time, light- and deep-sleep duration and percentage, rapid eye movement sleep, awakening times, wake after sleep onset, and wake time percentage.

Statistical analysis

According to previous reports, the incidence of PSD on the first postoperative night ranged from 19% to 44%18,20,29,30. We assumed a PSD rate of 25% with normal saline, 12% with DEX-0.3, and 6% with DEX-0.6. For the overall three-group comparison, the calculation indicated 189 patients were required using a two-sided χ2 test with α = 0.05 and 80% power (PASS 11.0). Allowing for a 10% attrition, we planned to enroll 210 patients (70 in each group).

Continuous variables are presented as mean (SD) or median (IQR). Categorical variables are summarized as numbers (%). Demographic and baseline data were analyzed with descriptively only. Perioperative and exploratory sleep data were compared across the three groups using one-way analysis of variance, the Kruskal-Wallis test, or the χ2 test, as appropriate; no pairwise comparisons were undertaken for these data.

The primary outcome (PSD incidence) was analyzed using the Pearson χ2 test for the overall three-group comparison, followed by pairwise χ2 tests with Bonferroni correction. Treatment effects are reported as relative risk (RR) and absolute risk reduction (ARR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) using the Koopman asymptotic score method. Number needed to treat (NNT) is also reported. Logistic regression with a treatment-by-subgroup interaction term was used for subgroup analyses. A prespecified exploratory comparison combined both dexmedetomidine groups vs normal saline. Secondary outcomes were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test or the χ2 test. When the overall test was significant, pairwise comparisons were performed with Dunn’s test or Bonferroni correction. According to the published protocol12, the primary and secondary outcomes were prespecified. For the primary outcome, prespecified subgroups were sex, preoperative AIS score (0–3 vs 4–5), and preoperative GDS-15 scores (0–5 vs 6–15); age (65–75 vs > 75 years), surgical duration ( < 180 vs ≥ 180 min), and surgical approach (laparoscopic vs open) were examined post-hoc. The actigraphy substudy was prespecified.

All analyses followed a modified intention-to-treat principle, including every randomized patient who underwent the scheduled operation. Missing data were handled by complete case analysis. No imputation was performed because the primary outcome had no missing data and secondary missing data were sparse31. Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For the prespecified pairwise comparisons of the primary outcome, the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold was P < 0.017 (0.05/3). Secondary, subgroup, and actigraphy analyses were not adjusted for multiple comparisons and are exploratory.

Results

From August 2023 to May 2024, we screened 268 older adults undergoing major abdominal surgery (Fig. 1). Of these, 210 were randomized. Seven participants were excluded after randomization due to surgery canceled, unplanned intensive care unit admission, or withdrawal of consent, leaving 203 patients in the primary analysis (67 with normal saline, 68 with DEX-0.3, and 68 with DEX-0.6). Primary outcome

On the first night after surgery, 66 of 203 (32.5%) patients experienced PSD: 44/67 (65.7%) in the normal saline group, 14/68 (20.6%) in the DEX-0.3 group, 8/68 (11.8%) in the DEX-0.6 group (P < 0.001) (Table 3). Discussion

In this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of adults aged ≥ 65 years undergoing major abdominal surgery, two-thirds of those receiving saline experienced PSD on the first postoperative night. Continuous intraoperative dexmedetomidine infusions at 0.3 and 0.6 μg/kg/h reduced the incidence to 20.6 % and 11.8 %, respectively, corresponding to a 70–80% relative risk reduction (NNT = 1.8–2.2). The benefits persisted to postoperative night 2 and were accompanied by less pain, fewer depressive symptoms and better quality of recovery. Exploratory actigraphy findings suggested longer total sleep time and lower wake time percentages associated with dexmedetomidine.

Our findings extend previous retrospective and small-scale studies, adding randomized controlled evidence that intraoperative dexmedetomidine adjunct can reduce PSD in older adults undergoing major abdominal surgery18,30,32. By contrast, Dong et al. found no reduction in PSD incidence in women undergoing breast cancer surgery11. The discrepancy is likely explained by lower baseline risk in that cohort (younger age, shorter surgery, less inflammatory insult). Our trial confirmed the efficacy of dexmedetomidine at two clinically feasible infusion rates in these high-risk surgical patients. While the sample size was calculated for the overall three-group comparison, not for individual pairwise comparisons vs the normal saline group, the observed effect sizes exceeded the design assumptions, and the narrow confidence intervals support the robustness of these comparisons.

Mechanistically, dexmedetomidine may act through several pathways. The observed reduction in PSD may be partially mediated by improved postoperative analgesia and reduced opioid requirement. In addition, dexmedetomidine acts on α2-adrenergic receptors in the locus coeruleus to suppress norepinephrine release and activates ventrolateral preoptic sleep-promoting neurons, generating a physiological N2-like sleep state33,34. It may also attenuate surgery-induced neuroinflammation and reduce nociceptive transmission, thereby facilitating sleep initiation35–37. These pathways are biologically plausible but were not directly measured in this trial; they remain hypothetical explanations requiring future biomarker studies.

Strengths of our trial include adherence to a published protocol, concealed allocation, blinded outcome assessors, and complete follow-up for the primary endpoint, minimizing selection and detection biases. We focused specifically on adults aged ≥ 65 years, a high-risk cohort frequently under-represented in previous trials, thereby providing directly relevant evidence for geriatric surgical care. While allocation concealment and outcome assessor blinding were maintained, the intraoperative team may have suspected allocation based on hemodynamic patterns. However, clinically relevant hemodynamic events and extubation times did not differ across groups, and no unblinding was reported. The observed PSD rate (65.7%) in the normal saline group exceeded our a priori assumption, reflecting the high-risk nature of older adults undergoing major abdominal surgery. This higher event rate increased statistical power beyond the planned 80%. The narrow 95%CIs for the treatment effect (0.19–0.50 for DEX-0.3 and 0.09–0.34 for DEX-0.6) indicate robust and precise estimates unlikely to be altered by sampling variability.

Several limitations should be noted. First, exclusion of patients with pre-existing sleep disturbance and chronic sedative or opioid use enhances internal validity but reduces external validity; our findings may not apply to older adults with baseline insomnia or those on long-term psychotropic medications, who represent a large proportion of real-world perioperative practice. Second, while the AIS is validated for postoperative use, its brevity limits mechanistic insight into specific sleep domains. Future studies should consider complementary instruments such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index or polysomnography to capture broader sleep dimensions. Third, although baseline AIS scores reflected home sleep prior to admission, we did not assess sleep during the preoperative hospital night, when disruption may already begin38. Fourth, standardized ward sleep-hygiene measures were implemented as background controls applied uniformly to all groups, but adherence was not systematically audited; variability in compliance could have introduced unmeasured confounding, and our findings may not generalize to less controlled ward environments. Fifth, actigraphy sleep data remain exploratory and underpowered for mechanistic inference; these data should be interpreted with caution given the suboptimal performance of consumer wearable devices compared with reference standards28. Sixth, subgroup analyses, particularly post-hoc subgroups with small sample sizes, produced wide confidence intervals and unstable estimates; these are hypothesis-generating and should not guide clinical decisions. Seventh, safety conclusions are limited by sample size. While common hemodynamic events were similar across groups, less frequent cardiovascular outcomes were not adequately captured. Next, the trial population was predominantly at ASA physical status II; while this distribution reflects real-world surgical scheduling in older adults with age-related comorbidities, our findings may not confidently apply to the healthiest or higher-risk patients. Last, the study was conducted at a single center, and multicenter replication is needed to enhance generalizability.

In conclusion, intraoperative dexmedetomidine 0.3 and 0.6 μg/kg/h significantly reduced the incidence of first-night PSD among older adults undergoing major abdominal surgery without increasing adverse events. These promising findings suggest considering low-dose dexmedetomidine as part of perioperative care in this high-risk surgical population, but replication in larger, multicenter trials and in populations with more prevalent baseline sleep disturbance is needed.
