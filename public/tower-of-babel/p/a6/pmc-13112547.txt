Diagnosis and Outcome of a Fulminant Case of Dengue Myocarditis
Authors: Brett Slajus, Muhammad Hamza Khan, Aline Arif, Phillip DeChristopher, Nina M Clark
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue is a viral zoonotic disease found in tropical and subtropical countries and transmitted by arthropod vectors, primarily Aedes mosquitoes. Dengue manifestations vary, and reinfection can trigger severe disease, including myocarditis. Nonspecific symptoms and serologic cross-reactivity among flaviviruses make etiological diagnosis difficult.Case SummaryA 21-year-old otherwise healthy woman presented with fever and cardiogenic shock requiring mechanical circulatory support after returning from Brazil. Dengue virus (DENV) serotype-2 antibodies were found, with evidence of prior DENV serotype-1 infection. The patient was treated supportively and eventually recovered full cardiac function.DiscussionDengue is one of the fastest growing mosquito-borne illnesses globally and is caused by 1 of 4 virus serotypes. There is no specific treatment for the infection, and flavivirus antibody assays are highly cross-reactive, requiring plaque reduction neutralization assays or other specialized tests to differentiate flavivirus infections.Take-Home MessageDengue shock syndrome with myocarditis may require mechanical circulatory support but carries a good prognosis for recovery.Key words: cardiogenic shock, dengue shock syndrome, flavivirus, myocarditis

History of Presentation

During the last 5 days of a 2-week mission trip to Brazil, a 21-year-old woman experienced cough, fatigue, myalgias, headache, and fever to 102 °F (38.9 °C). While flying back to the United States, she had 2 syncopal episodes, nausea, and diarrhea. Other trip members experienced similar but less severe symptoms, which self-resolved. After landing, she presented to a U.S. hospital and was tachycardic and hypotensive. Electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus tachycardia with low voltage but no ischemia. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed an ejection fraction of 10% with a normal-sized left ventricle. There was mild concentric ventricular hypertrophy, no regional wall motion abnormality, and a small pericardial effusion without tamponade physiology. The right ventricle was small and contractility normal and valves were normal. The patient was started on dobutamine and epinephrine for cardiogenic shock, and an intra-aortic balloon pump was placed. Thrombosis of the right femoral artery was found, and thrombectomy was performed. Chest computed tomography angiography demonstrated a small pericardial effusion without pulmonary embolism with no coronary calcifications, and abdominal imaging showed no focal hepatic lesions, mild periportal edema, periportal and retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy, and scant free pelvic fluid. Creatine kinase (730 U/L) and high-sensitivity troponin (7,995 ng/L) were markedly elevated; coronary angiogram was not performed. Empiric ceftriaxone, azithromycin, and metronidazole were started for sepsis. Initial laboratory work-up showed hyponatremia, mildly elevated aspartate aminotransferase (50 IU/L) and normal complete blood count, but liver function tests worsened within 24 hours of presentation (aspartate aminotransferase: 1,300 IU/L, alanine aminotransferase: 1,250 IU/L), and there was leukocytosis (white blood cell count: 23.2 K/μL with no eosinophilia) and thrombocytopenia (80 K/μL).

Past Medical History

During a trip to Brazil 1 year ago, the patient experienced a less severe flu-like illness, which self-resolved. There was no other notable past medical, social, or family history, and no tobacco, alcohol, or drug use.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the patient's fever, recent travel, young age, and negative blood cultures and TTE findings, her cardiogenic shock was thought most likely due to myocarditis from infection or rheumatologic condition, less likely toxic exposures. The differential diagnosis included viral infection (eg, human immunodeficiency virus, Coxsackie B, influenza, SARS-CoV-2, West Nile virus, hemorrhagic fevers), bacterial infection (eg, acute rheumatic fever, diphtheria, rickettsial disease), or protozoal infection (eg, toxoplasmosis). The noninfectious differential included autoimmune diseases including systemic lupus erythematosus, sarcoidosis, and giant cell myocarditis.

Investigations and Management

The patient was transferred to a quaternary care center for advanced therapies, where she was emergently cannulated for venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) owing to pulseless electrical activity arrest, and the intra-aortic balloon pump was removed. She then had a left-sided percutaneous Impella 5.5 device (Abiomed) placed. A pericardial window was created, with return of 200 mL of straw-colored fluid. On day 7 she was converted to venovenous ECMO.

A rheumatologic work-up included negative ANA, anti-dsDNA, ENA profile, myositis panel, and ANCA screens. Six endomyocardial biopsy samples showed no significant inflammatory changes on hematoxylin and eosin stain (Figures 1A and 1B) or on CD3 or CD68 stains for T-lymphocytes and macrophages/monocytes, respectively (Figures 1C and 1D). Electron microscopy of these samples showed unremarkable myocytes and no viral particles. Immunostains for CD3 and CD68 stained rare T cells and histiocytes, respectively. No bacteria were identified on 16s rDNA polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of the myocardial tissue. Infectious work-up included negative blood cultures, pericardial fluid culture, blood cell–free DNA sequencing (Karius), viral respiratory PCR panel, malaria smear, and negative antibody testing for HIV, Zika, chikungunya, Ehrlichia, Rickettsia, Lyme, Trypanosoma cruzi, Coxsackie B, Brucella, Toxoplasma, and Coccidioides. Coxsackie A24 IgG was slightly elevated at 1:100 (reference: <1:100). Blood PCR for adenovirus, herpes simplex virus, human herpesvirus-6, and parvovirus B19 were also negative.

West Nile virus IgM was positive and initially IgG negative, although IgG was positive 7 days later. IgM antibodies to dengue were also positive. Dengue IgG antibody testing was initially equivocal, but repeat was positive. Blood for plaque reduction neutralization (PRNT) assay was sent to the Centers for Disease Control & Prevention Reference Laboratory to distinguish between West Nile virus and dengue virus (DENV). The PRNT titer for West Nile virus and St Louis encephalitis virus were negative, while DENV-1 and DENV-2 titers were positive at 1:320 and 1:40, respectively.

The patient experienced ongoing severe ventricular failure with multisystem organ damage, including coagulopathy (platelet counts down to 38 K/μL on hospital day 4) and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding requiring blood transfusions and embolizations of gastric arteries. She developed seizures, with head computed tomography showing hypodense cerebellar lesions concerning for infarct versus infection. She also developed anuric renal failure requiring continuous renal replacement therapy and bilateral lower extremity compartment syndrome secondary to hematomas, which required multicompartment fasciotomies. She was evaluated and approved for heart transplantation, but her family declined transplant.

Outcome and Follow-Up

On day 3 the patient's left ventricular ejection fraction was 10% (Video 1), which improved to 40% on day 14 and 50% on day 21 (Video 2). ECMO was discontinued on day 17 and Impella removed on day 23. She required tracheostomy and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube placement and was eventually transferred to a hospital closer to her family on day 69, where these were eventually removed and dialysis was no longer required. She has since made a full recovery.

Discussion

The primary vector for dengue transmission is the Aedes aegypti mosquito.1 Dengue and its vector are endemic to Brazil, and dengue is considered one of the fastest spreading mosquito-borne viral illnesses, with 50 to 400 million global cases and 22,000 deaths annually.1, 2, 3, 4 Dengue transmission increases with increased precipitation and higher temperatures, with cases rising during Brazil's wet season (October to May).3 There are 4 serotypes of dengue (DENV-1 to DENV-4); infection confers immunity only to each specific serotype and not others.

Dengue infection may be asymptomatic or lead to only a febrile, flu-like syndrome which lasts 3 to 7 days. Dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) is a severe form of the disease that is characterized by higher levels of circulating virus, and progresses from the febrile phase to a critical phase where there is vascular leak, thrombocytopenia with bleeding and myocarditis. Renal failure or other prolonged organ injury may occur.1,2 Dengue shock syndrome (DSS) is defined by a rapid weak pulse and narrow pulse pressure or hypotension in the setting of DHF,1,2 criteria met by our patient.

Repeat infection with an alternate serotype is associated with greater severity of disease,5 including DHF and DSS, at least in part related to antibody-dependent enhancement. Preexisting, non-neutralizing antibodies form complexes with viruses and are taken in by monocytes and macrophages where the virus can replicate exponentially,1 leading to high-level viremia. There is also increased activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in severe dengue compared with uncomplicated dengue,5 which may contribute to increased inflammatory state and plasma leakage. Second infections can also lead to less robust antibody production to the infecting serotype compared with antibodies from prior infection, a phenomenon known as “original antigenic sin”5 and likely seen in our patient's case.

There are 3 gold standards for diagnosis of dengue: nucleic acid or antigen detection, serologic testing, and virus isolation in culture.2,6 In the U.S., serologic testing is most readily available. Serologic IgM and IgG testing after 7 days of infection is often positive, however flaviviruses are highly cross-reactive on antibody testing, especially if previously exposed or vaccinated to other flaviviruses.2,5, 6, 7 PRNT is used to differentiate between flaviviruses and dengue serotypes, although it is technically challenging and takes up to 7 days.6 For dengue, the serotype with higher PRNT titer signifies the original infecting serotype.6 In this patient, higher DENV-1 titers suggested it as the prior infection and DENV-2 as the more recent, secondary infection.

Data are limited regarding the prevalence of dengue myocarditis given lower prevalence of DSS and under-reporting, but myocarditis has been shown to occur in 21% to 78% of persons in various reports.4,7 And, while dengue antigens have also been found extensively in myocardial endothelium and cardiomyocytes in a fatal case, there was minimal associated inflammatory infiltrate,8 similar to findings in the biopsies of our patient. Still, multiple samples of cardiac tissue may be required for confirmation of myocarditis given its patchy nature.9 Myocarditis in severe dengue may occur not just from viral infection of cardiac tissue but also from immune system overactivity leading to myocardial damage and decreased cardiac function.1,5,7,9 Dengue myocarditis may manifest not only as reduced ejection fraction but as atrioventricular conduction disorders,8 and it is reasonable to obtain TTE imaging in persons with severe symptoms of DENV infection, particularly those with cardiac enzyme elevations or electrocardiographic changes.4

Dengue has an unpredictable clinical course. Management of DHF includes judicious fluid resuscitation owing to high capillary permeability as well as transfusion to manage coagulopathy and bleeding complications.1,2 Anticoagulants and antiplatelet therapy should generally be avoided during this period.1,2 There are no effective antiviral medications for dengue, and there is insufficient evidence regarding steroids or immunoglobulin in dengue myocarditis. If hepatitis is present, patients may possibly benefit from N-acetylcysteine.1,2 Fulminant myocarditis is a syndrome characterized by inflammation of the heart leading to sudden and severe cardiac dysfunction, with outcomes including arrhythmias, multiorgan failure, or death.9 It may require mechanical circulatory support and even transplantation, although the latter should be deferred during the acute critical phase. There are limited data on cardiac recovery rates in dengue.

A tetravalent, live-attenuated vaccine is commercially available and has approximately 80% efficacy.1 It is indicated for individuals aged 6 to 16 years living in endemic areas with laboratory-confirmed previous dengue, and thus is not routinely administered to travelers.1

Conclusions

Dengue is among the fastest-growing mosquito-borne viral illnesses. It can cause coagulopathy, increased capillary permeability, electrolyte abnormalities, and multisystem organ dysfunction including myocarditis, encephalitis, renal failure, and hepatitis. Management is supportive and may require transient mechanical circulatory support. Differentiation between dengue and other flaviviruses can be demonstrated through PRNT assays.
