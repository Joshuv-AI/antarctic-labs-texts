Outcomes of gastrectomy in octogenarians and nonagenarians: a comparative analysis in the era of minimally invasive surgery
Authors: Kenjiro Ishii, Yusuke Akimoto, Osahiko Hagiwara, Toshiyuki Enomoto, Takaharu Kiribayashi, Koji Asai, Takuya Nagata, Manabu Watanabe, Yoshihisa Saida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs the population of Japan continues to age rapidly, an increasing number of patients aged ≥ 80 years are undergoing surgery for gastric cancer. Although minimally invasive techniques have improved surgical safety, operative risks in the super-elderly population (≥ 85 years) remain a significant concern.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center study analyzed 72 patients aged ≥ 80 years who underwent gastrectomy for gastric cancer at our hospital between January 2014 and August 2024. Patients were stratified into two groups: the super-elderly group (≥ 85 years, n = 26) and the younger-elderly group (80–84 years, n = 46). Clinical characteristics, perioperative outcomes, and postoperative complications were compared between the two groups. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe mean age was 83.7 (range: 80–93) years. Comorbidities were common, particularly cardiovascular disease (19.4%) and diabetes mellitus (27.8%). Compared with the younger-elderly group, the super-elderly group had a longer preoperative hospital stay (5.8 vs. 4.5 days) but a shorter overall hospital stay (14.3 vs. 21.1 days). Minimally invasive surgery was less frequently performed in the super-elderly group (42.3% vs. 56.5%). Postoperative complications of Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 2 occurred in 40.3% of all patients, with a lower incidence in the super-elderly group (34.6% vs. 43.5%). Mortality from non-cancer-related causes did not differ significantly between the groups.ConclusionsWith appropriate preoperative optimization, gastrectomy can be performed safely in patients aged ≥ 85 years. Individualized treatment strategies that account for comorbidities are essential for achieving favorable outcomes in this expanding population.

Background

Gastric cancer, a globally prevalent malignancy, poses a significant health challenge, particularly in Japan where its incidence remains high. Gastric adenocarcinoma, which is the most common histologic type, is further classified into intestinal and diffuse types according to the Lauren classification. The diagnosis of gastric cancer involves a comprehensive approach that starts with upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and biopsy, followed by cross-sectional imaging such as computed tomography (CT) to assess the extent of disease and determine the clinical stage. In younger patients with locally advanced gastric cancer, neoadjuvant chemotherapy is administered following the recommendations of a multidisciplinary tumor board. However, the treatment approach is less standardized in older patients with locally advanced gastric cancer. Despite advanced age, each case is individually evaluated by the multidisciplinary tumor board at our institution to determine the best therapeutic strategy, including the potential use of neoadjuvant therapy, based on performance status and comorbidity profile. In this patient population, nutritional management is crucial, as studies have shown that preoperative malnutrition significantly increases the risk of postoperative complications and mortality. Therefore, our team includes nutritionists in the preoperative phase to optimize the nutritional status of the patient, a practice shown to improve outcomes in surgical oncology [1].

Japan is experiencing an unprecedented demographic shift, marked by rapid growth in the population aged ≥ 80 years. This trend is particularly relevant to gastric cancer, which remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in Japan [2, 3]. As life expectancy increases, a greater number of older adults are being considered for potentially curative surgical interventions, including gastrectomy.

However, super-elderly patients (≥ 85 years) often present with multiple comorbidities and reduced physiological reserves [4]. Octogenarians have lower 5-year overall survival rates (59.8–75.6%) than younger patients (66.7–87.0%) [5, 6]. This difference is largely attributed to higher rates of non-cancer-related mortality (e.g., cardiovascular or respiratory diseases), rather than poorer oncologic outcomes [7].

Recent advancements in minimally invasive surgery (MIS) have reduced surgical stress, complication rates, and length of hospital stay, thereby supporting its adoption in older patients [8]. Although several studies have demonstrated acceptable surgical outcomes in older individuals, data specifically addressing the super-elderly population remain limited [9].

Older patients vary in chronological age as well as in functional status and perioperative risk. In clinical practice, gastrectomy is approached with caution in patients aged ≥ 85 years due to their increased surgical vulnerability. Therefore, identifying clinical factors that influence surgical safety and outcomes is essential for tailoring appropriate treatment strategies [10, 11].

This study aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics and perioperative outcomes of gastrectomy in patients aged ≥ 80 years, with a focus on comparing those aged ≥ 85 years and those aged 80–84 years. This analysis aimed to facilitate safer and more individualized surgical decision-making for older patients with gastric cancer.

Methods

This retrospective study included patients aged ≥ 80 years who underwent gastrectomy for gastric cancer at our institution between January 2014 and August 2024. Data were extracted from medical records and included the following variables: age, sex, medical history, comorbidities, duration of hospital stay, type of surgical procedure, operative time, estimated blood loss, postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ II), and cause of death.

Surgical procedures were performed by experienced gastrointestinal surgeons. The specific type of gastrectomy was determined based on the tumor location and staging and included distal gastrectomy, total gastrectomy, and proximal gastrectomy. Reconstruction methods varied and included commonly used techniques such as Billroth I, Billroth II, and Roux-en-Y. The study cohort included five patients who underwent robotic-assisted gastrectomy, a minimally invasive technique. Robotic surgery has been associated with improved postoperative recovery and shorter hospital stays, which are particularly important benefits in the older population [12].

Patients were divided into two groups based on age: the super-elderly group (≥ 85 years) and younger-elderly group (80–84 years). All collected variables were compared between the two groups.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test, whereas continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test and are presented as mean ± standard deviation. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study included 72 patients with a mean age of 83.7 years (range: 80–93 years), of whom 26 (36.1%) were aged ≥ 85 years. Only nine patients (12.5%) had no notable comorbidities. A history of aortic or cardiac surgery was noted in 19.4% of patients, and diabetes mellitus was noted in 27.8%. The super-elderly group had higher proportions of patients with a history of aortic or cardiac surgery (23.1% vs. 17.6%), impaired respiratory function (11.5% vs. 2.2%), and atrial fibrillation (7.7% vs. 2.2%) than the younger-elderly group. Diabetes was also more prevalent in the younger-elderly group (23.1% vs. 30.4%) (Tab 1).

Hospital stay

The mean preoperative hospital stay was 3.7 days overall. The super-elderly group had a significantly longer preoperative stay than the younger-elderly group (5.8 vs. 4.5 days, p < 0.05). Conversely, the super-elderly group had a significantly shorter overall hospital stay (14.3 vs. 21.1 days, p < 0.05) (Tab 2).

Perioperative data and surgical details

Intraoperative factors

There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of operative time, estimated blood loss, or surgical procedure (distal, proximal, total, or pylorus-preserving gastrectomy and type of reconstruction). MIS, including laparoscopic and robot-assisted approaches, was performed in 51.4% of the patients. Although not statistically significant, MIS tended to be less frequent in the super-elderly group (42.3% vs. 56.5%, p = 0.10) (Tab 2).

Postoperative complications

Postoperative complications of Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 2 occurred in 40.3% of all patients. The incidence was slightly lower in the super-elderly group than in the younger-elderly group (34.6% vs. 43.5%), although the difference was not significant. The incidence of anastomotic leakage was significantly higher in the younger-elderly group than in the super-elderly group (Tab 3.).

Cause of death

Death from non-cancer-related causes was observed in 10 patients (13.9%), with no significant difference between the super-elderly and younger-elderly groups (11.5% vs. 15.2%, p = 0.66). Among the fatal non-cancer-related causes, pneumonia was the most common (Tab 3.).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that with optimized preoperative management, gastrectomy for gastric cancer can be performed safely in patients aged ≥ 85 years. Comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes were common among the study participants. Notably, the super-elderly group, despite higher rates of cardiac comorbidity, had no increased complication or mortality risk, consistent with previous findings [5, 8, 9, 13].

The longer preoperative hospital stay observed in the super-elderly group likely reflects efforts, including heparinization, nutritional support, physical rehabilitation, and cardiovascular monitoring, to stabilize the underlying conditions. This preoperative optimization may have contributed to the lower complication rates and shorter total hospital stay observed in this group [14, 15].

Moreover, MIS was less frequently performed in the super-elderly group (approximately 40%), who presented with high-risk profiles including cardiac surgery history. This finding might have resulted from careful patient selection, considering the presence of reduced cardiac function and ongoing anticoagulation. Recent studies have also suggested that MIS is feasible even in patients aged over 85 years if they are carefully selected [9, 10]. Increasing the rate of MIS in the super-elderly population could contribute to additional improvements in postoperative outcomes.

Regarding the cause of mortality, the rate of non-cancer-related mortality is reportedly high in patients aged ≥ 80 years [16]. In the present study, the rate was as high as 13.9%; however, the difference between the two age groups was not significant.

Moreover, our data support the view that advanced age alone should not preclude surgical intervention; however, physiological and functional status must be comprehensively assessed.

Nevertheless, this study has some limitations. The relatively small sample size, particularly in the super-elderly group (n = 26) was a primary limitation of the study, which reduced the statistical power and increased the risk of a Type II error, where a true difference between groups may not be detectable. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings is limited and may not extend to the broader population of super-elderly patients with gastric cancer. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate our observations. Because of the retrospective study design and inherent selection bias of surgical candidacy, our findings may not be generalizable to all octogenarians with gastric cancer, particularly those deemed unfit for surgery.

Although we observed favorable surgical outcomes in a substantial number of patients aged ≥ 85 years, we acknowledge the absence of the structured assessment for frailty as a limitation in our data collection. Frailty is a clinical syndrome characterized by a decline in physiologic reserve and function across multiple systems, rendering it a critical predictor of postoperative complications and recovery in older surgical patients. Although comorbidities were recorded, objective measures, such as geriatric assessment tools, nutritional status, and physical function scores, were not included. However, this limitation provides an important context for our findings. Patients aged ≥ 85 years who achieved favorable outcomes may have had unmeasured physiologic reserves and were not frail despite their chronologic age. This concept is supported by recent studies, including a report by Marano et al. [17], who demonstrated that handgrip strength, a simple and effective surrogate marker for sarcopenia and frailty, was a more accurate predictor of the length of hospital stay than age alone. Our results likely reflect the importance of such individual patient characteristics, emphasizing that chronologic age alone is not sufficient as a predictor of surgical outcomes. Therefore, our findings strengthen the argument for a personalized, rather than age-based, approach to surgical planning and preoperative risk assessment in older patients with gastric cancer. Furthermore, our findings should be contextualized within the broader biologic framework of aging and gastric cancer. Although we focused on clinical outcomes in the present study, future efforts should explore the integration of biologic markers into preoperative risk stratification approaches in older patients. Boccardi et al. [18] has proposed serum CD26 levels as a potential novel diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in gastric cancer, linking tumor biology to patient outcomes. Investigating whether biomarkers reflecting broader biological processes, such as immunosenescence and tumor burden, can enhance the preoperative assessment of older patients may be worthwhile. Incorporating such molecular insights, in addition to chronologic age, functional assessments, and frailty scores, can lead to a more comprehensive and accurate prediction of surgical risk, paving the way for truly personalized perioperative care.

Additionally, postoperative follow-up was conducted according to institutional guidelines. Patients were routinely followed up with clinical examinations, laboratory tests, and endoscopic and CT scans at regular intervals (e.g., every 3–6 months for the first 3 years and then annually). However, we did not assess the long-term oncologic outcomes and quality of life (QOL) in this study. In the future, multicenter studies with additional standardized QOL metrics and frailty indexes should be conducted to refine surgical indications and optimize care in super-elderly patients.

Conclusion

Gastrectomy in patients aged ≥ 85 years can be performed safely if they are provided with appropriate perioperative care similar to that for those in the early 80s. Preoperative optimization and individualized treatment planning are critical to minimize the surgical risk in this population. As the super-elderly demographic continues to increase worldwide, further studies evaluating the long-term outcomes and QOL of the super-elderly patients with gastric cancer after surgery is necessary.
