Modeling of colchicine biotransformation by COVID-19 associated Klebsiella pneumoniae: synergistic effects of cell adaptation and gamma irradiation
Authors: Walaa S Hendawy, Amany Badr El-Deen Abd El-Aziz, Heba Abd-Alla El-Bialy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicrobial biotransformation strategies for prescribed drugs have attracted great interest because the biotransformation process simulates what happens in the human metabolism and may be considered a resource for new pharmaceuticals.MethodsThe promising candidate for colchicine biotransformation was identified using Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization Time-of-Flight (MALDI-TOF/MS). Colchicine was quantified by a modified procedure based on the reduction of alkaline potassium permanganate and HPLC. Response surface methodology (RSM) was used for optimizing and modeling colchicine biotransformation. The colchicine-metabolizing products were identified using liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization–tandem mass spectrometry.ResultsFifteen bacteria infected the upper respiratory tract during the COVID-19 pandemic were collected and screened for colchicine resistance. One of them could metabolize 41.38% colchicine in enriched SF2 medium supplemented with 7gl− 1 colchicine and was identified as Klebsiella pneumoniae-4. The biotransformation of colchicine was optimized using RSM upon exposing K. pneumoniae-4 cells to gamma irradiation stress. The predictive model revealed the superiority of cell adaptation before the colchicine conversion process. The RSM optimizer showed the maximum colchicine biotransformation was achieved at variable levels: 0.41 kGy irradiation dose and 9.55 d incubation time. Chromatographic analysis showed a variation in the chemical composition of colchicine metabolized products.ConclusionsThe main colchicine metabolized products; 3-(4-hydroxyphenyl) propanoic acid, 4’-hydroxy-2’-methylacetophenone, dihydrorotenone, and glabridin, potentially could be applied in the medical field for more functions than those described for the parent compound.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-026-01174-4.Keywords: Colchicine, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Bio-transformation, Response surface methodology, Gamma radiation, LC-ESI-MS/MS

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic is one of the biggest threats to humans since World War II because of the generalized social, environmental, economic, and health concerns. The Coronavirus disease-2019 (COVID-19) was named SARS-CoV-2 virus due to its high homology (~ 80%) to the SARS-CoV virus that spread during 2002–2003 [1]. The new virus primarily targets the respiratory system, although it also affects other organ systems, causing fever, dyspnea, dry cough, headache, dizziness, generalized weakness, diarrhea, and vomiting as common symptoms [2]. Furthermore, it was difficult to access basic services like sanitation, garbage collection, and clean water, especially in developing nations during the pandemic, which also affected the medical sector [3]. Health restrictions imposed by government officials and policymakers have led to the deterioration of human psychology, changes to the educational system, employee terminations, and a reduction in the overall budget of human resources [4].

COVID-19 has a high prevalence and is easy to spread, and has no established treatments or vaccines at the outset of its global spread. With increasing mortality rates all over the world due to the endemic new virus, there is no time to adopt novel therapeutics and medications. Alternatively, repositioning pre-existing drugs may offer a fast and appealing strategy with known characteristics, safety, and dosage used. Colchicine is a pseudoalkaloid drug that is considered one of the repurposed drugs that were recommended to guideline protocols for patients suffering from mild or severe forms of COVID-19 (https://www.covid19treatmentguidelines.nih.gov/therapies/miscellaneous-drugs/).

Prior to its repositioning for COVID-19, colchicine was considered the first-line treatment for acute gouty arthritis, prophylaxis, Familial Mediterranean Fever, Behcet’s disease, and sweet syndrome. Additionally, it prevents the painful episodes of serositis and amyloidosis [5]. During COVID-19, colchicine has a direct anti-inflammatory effect by inhibiting the synthesis of tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-6, neutrophil chemotaxis (adhesion and mobilization), disruption of superoxide production, monocyte migration, and the secretion of matrix metalloproteinase-9, as well as suppressing the secretion of cytokines and chemokines. All these are potentially beneficial effects that might diminish the COVID-19 inflammatory storm [6, 7]. The recommended dose of colchicine is 1.5 mg twice daily as a loading dose, followed by 0.5 mg twice daily for 3 weeks [8]. Alternatively, the common adverse effects of colchicine are restricted to diarrhea and gastrointestinal disorders, liver and hematological changes, muscle toxicity, and neuropathy at higher doses than those recommended by doctors [9]. Furthermore, cytokine-driven hyper-inflammation in COVID-19 patient lungs is highly associated with the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NET), which form readily in response to the presence of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and fungi. A high prevalence of bacterial co-pathogens—specifically Chlamydia pneumoniae, Haemophilus parainfluenzae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Streptococcus pneumoniae—was observed in COVID-19 patients, involving both initial coinfections and subsequent secondary infections [10].

Colchicine derivatives, such as 3-demethylcolchicine, colchicoside, and thiocolchicoside, which are about 35 times less toxic than the parent molecule and exhibit improved therapeutic properties for anti-inflammatory and anti-tumor drugs, are in high demand for the treatment of certain types of leukemia and solid tumors [11]. The parent compound, colchicine, is extracted from the meadow saffron plant, Colchicum autumnale (Liliaceae), and other colchicum species, but the availability of colchicine derivatives in the plant kingdom is so limited [12]. As a result, numerous efforts have been developed to synthesize colchicine derivatives or recover them from alternative routes.

Colchicine is first transformed into the more stable thiocolchicine [III] through a multi-step chemical thiol methylation process. However, due to the chemical demethylation’s lack of regionselectivity and low conversion yields, different 1-, 2-, and 3-demethyl derivatives and dimethyl derivatives are obtained [13]. Many advantages could be gained from the microbial transformation, including a one-step simple reaction, environmental friendliness, and intriguing regio-, stereo-, and enantio-selectivity [14]. Moreover, improving microbial transformation has many strategies including selecting new candidates that have higher efficiencies or optimizing the conversion processes [15]. Plenty of reports were available on the microbial demethylation of colchicine by successive growing bacterial species mainly belonging to Bacillus spp on increasing concentrations of colchicine up to 10 gl− 1 with enriching or adaptation protocols [16]. In COVID-19 patients suffering prolonged pneumonia, the administration of colchicine creates an unintentional selective environment (selective stress). This sustained exposure may facilitate the metabolic adaptation of resident bacterial co-pathogens, potentially leading to the biotransformation of the drug.

This study aims to leverage the prolonged administration of colchicine in COVID-19 patients with bacterial secondary infections to model bacterial adaptation to xenobiotic substances as drugs. This adaptive process facilitates the biotransformation of the drug into metabolites that may exhibit altered toxicity and therapeutic efficacy against inflammation. Furthermore, this provides clinical guidance for dosage optimization, suggesting a potential need for dose escalation in cases of bacterial co-infection with Corona virus cases. Notably, bacterial drug metabolism often mimics human metabolic pathways (human-microbial metabolic bypass), offering significant pharmaceutical potential for the synthesis of medically relevant derivatives not naturally occurring in the plant kingdom. This study establishes the framework for the industrial-scale production of these derivatives using statistical modeling as response surface methodology, supported by characterization through validated chromatographic techniques.

Methods

Selection and screening of colchicine-resistant bacteria

Fifteen bacterial isolates that were previously isolated from the upper respiratory tract of COVID-19 patients (Doctors treated them with colchicine for more than three weeks) were kindly provided by a private medical laboratory (License number 5366/2011) according to Regulation of Clinical Medical Research (Egyptian Law No. 214/2020) and Egyptian Code of Medical Ethics (No. 238/2003). The selected bacteria were streaked on Mueller Hinton agar medium (HiMedia®India) to check microbial purity and maintained in tryptic soya broth: glycerol (1:1v/v) at -20 °C for upcoming experiments. The selected bacteria were cultivated in 50 ml of SF2 medium [17] at 10% supplemented with 1gl− 1 colchicine (PRATAP®India) and incubated for 48 h at 37 °C. The intensity of the bacterial growth was visually monitored, and the increase in the growth yield was expressed as positive signs; the most colchicine-resistant bacteria were chosen for further screening experiments.

The highest concentration of colchicine that incompletely inhibited the growth of colchicine-resistant bacteria was determined using the microtiter plate technique [13]. Increasing concentrations of colchicine up to 7gl− 1 were serially adjusted in a 96-well microtiter plate before receiving 100 µl of double-strength LB medium inoculated with 10 µl of each colchicine-resistant bacterium at the same inoculum density (1 × 106 CFU/mL) and 20 µl of resazurin (0.062% w/v), which acts as a redox indicator to visually determine the viability of bacteria under colchicine stress [18]. The inoculated plate was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, and the change in the color from blue to pink is an indication of bacterial growth and colchicine resistance.

The most colchicine-resistant bacteria were individually grown in SF2 media supplemented with 7gl− 1 of colchicine and incubated at 37 °C. At the end of the incubation period, the colchicine residue was determined in enrichment cultures, and the bacterial strain that achieved the highest biotransformation percentage was selected for the upcoming studies. This bacterial strain was further identified using Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Mass spectrum was acquired by Microflex MALDI-TOF MS (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) (Scientific Services). MALDI BioTyper 2.0 software was used for analysis and comparison with the MALDI BioTyper database.

Optimization of colchicine biotransformation by response surface methodology

For optimizing colchicine biotransformation by selected bacteria, six variables, including aeration, agitation, carbon sources, incubation time, and cell type (previously exposed to colchicine or not), as well as low-dose of gamma rays as a stimulator for the bacterial metabolic system, were screened in a preliminary experiment. Three variables, including incubation time, cell type, and gamma radiation were selected and studied using central composite design (CCD) to explain the interaction relationships among variables at five levels (α, -1, 0, + 1, +α.) and the response [19]. The percentage of biotransformation yield (as the response term) was statistically analyzed by employing analysis of variance (ANOVA). Additionally, the influence of cell type on biotransformation efficiency was assessed using an independent samples t-test.

The response was diagrammed in the form of contour and response surface plots, created by Minitab version 19.1 against variables under investigation, to navigate the experimental design space and to determine the optimal values [20]. Data validation was studied via calculating the Average Absolute Deviation (AAD), Bias Factor (Bf), and Accuracy Factor (Af) to quantify the biotransformation model.

Pre-cultures either supplemented with colchicine (5gl− 1) or not were incubated overnight at 28 ± 2 °C under shaking conditions (150 rpm) to induce adapted and non-adapted bacterial cells, respectively. Then, the bacterial cells were harvested and re-suspended in saline and aseptically irradiated with low doses of gamma irradiation (0, 0.15, 0.5, 0.85, and 1.0 kGy) using the Cobalt-60 model Indian gamma cell (Modified) located at the National Center for Radiation Research and Technology, Egyptian Atomic Energy Authority. The dose rate at the time of the experiment was 0.885 kGy/h. At the end of incubation times (1, 2, 4, 7,10 days), the cell biomass was separated from the SF2 medium, and the culture filtrates were adjusted to pH 10 before being subjected to extraction with chloroform (V/V) three times [21]. The organic layers containing colchicine and its derivatives were collected, filtered with a 0.2-µm Millipore filter, evaporated to dryness, and quantitatively analyzed.

Quantitative analysis of colchicine and characterization of colchicine derivatives

Colchicine was quantitatively determined by modifying a procedure previously described by Suad and his collaborators in 2019. It is based on the oxidation of colchicine using alkaline potassium permanganate (Piochem®Egypt) as the oxidizing reagent, the absorbance of the reaction was read at 403 nm [22]. The procedure was validated according to the standard international guidelines. The Linearity of colchicine was studied using four different concentrations (1.25, 2.5, 3.75, and 5.0 mg/mL) in triplicate. The calibration curve was plotted, and the limits of detection (LOD) and quantitation (LOQ) were determined. Additionally, the percentage of relative standard deviation (RSD) was calculated.

Colchicine dissolved in methanol was filtered through a 0.2-µm Millipore filter before analysis via an Agilent 1100/1200 series HPLC (Agilent®Germany) equipped with a ZORBAX Eclipse plus C18 (150 m × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) and a UV–Vis detector. The mobile phase was composed of methanol: water (6:4, v/v) at a flow rate of 2 ml/min [23]. Five microliters of the samples were injected, detected at 240 nm, and quantified using Agilent Chemstation software 3D, Rev. B.04.03.16. Linearity of the procedure was established by injecting standard solutions (50, 75, and 100 µg/mL) three times. The slope of the calibration curve and the standard deviation of the colchicine’s peak area were calculated to determine the limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantitation (LOQ) as well as the percentage of RSD (Relative Standard Deviation).

Purification of colchicine derivatives was performed using the thin-layer chromatography technique. TLC plate pre-coated with Silica Gel 60 F254 with the following dimensions: 10 × 20 cm, 0.25 mm thickness (Merck, Germany) was activated at 105 °C for 30 min. At each incubation time under investigation (0, 2, 4, 7, and 10 d), colchicine and colchicine derivatives that were recovered as previously described were applied as a band of 6 mm width 2 cm apart from the bottom edge of the TLC plate. The chromatographic chamber was pre-saturated with the mobile phase [A mixture of dichloromethane: methanol (Piochem®Egypt); 9:1 V/V] for 12 h before spotting [14]. The separation process was developed by ascending chromatography up to 3 cm from the plate’s edge at ambient temperature. The plate was dried in air, and the developed spots were visualized by UV fluorescence quenching at 365 nm. Distinct spots of colchicine and its derivatives were scratched, eluted in methanol, and characterized by a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (T60 UV/Vis spectrometer®China) in the range between 190 and 600 nm. Colchicine and its derivatives were analyzed using liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-ESI-MS/MS) with an Exion LC AC system for separation and a SCIEX Triple Quad 5500 MS/MS system equipped with electrospray ionization (ESI) for detection. The separation was performed with an Ascentis® Express 90 Å (Ascentis®USA) C18 Column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.7 μm). Two eluents were used in the mobile phase: 5mM ammonium formate, pH 3 (A); and HPLC-grade acetonitrile (B). The flow rate was 0.3 ml/min, and the injection volume was 5 µl. For MS/MS analysis, positive ionization mode (+ ESI) over the mass range m/z 100–1000 was applied.

Results

Selection of a bio-transformed candidate for colchicine

In the present study, seven bacterial isolates out of fifteen (One Escherichia coli, two Haemophilus influenzae, four Klebsiella pneumoniae, four Pseudomonas aeruginosa, two Staphylococcus aureus, and two Streptococcus pneumoniae) achieved visually higher growth density upon culturing on SF2 medium supplemented with colchicine (1gl− 1) as shown in Table (S1). They were three K. pneumoniae, two P. aeruginosa, one H. influenzae, and one Streptococcus pneumoniae. After that, the selected pathogenic strains were screened for colchicine resistance up to 7gl− 1; results showed the ability of K. pneumoniae-4 to resist colchicine up to 7gl− 1, followed by H. influenzae-2 which was resistant to 2.5gl− 1 colchicine and S. pneumoniae-2 up to 2gl− 1, whereas the rest of the isolates failed to grow at colchicine higher than 1gl− 1 (Plate 1).

The mathematical model is very reliable because of a non-significant lack of fit (2.91) with the R-square value (0.9838) that was close to 1, indicating a better fit of the model to experimental data. The predictive model demonstrated exceptional reliability and precision across the 52 experimental trials. The calculated Bias Factor (Bf) of 1.0004 and the Accuracy Factor (Af) of 1.020 indicate that the model with a high degree of predictive power. This is further supported by a low Average Absolute Deviation (AAD) of 3.84%. Moreover, comparative analysis revealed that cell type is a valuable variable where the adapted cell achieved a significantly higher mean biotransformation rate (70.11%) than non-adapted cells (58.82%), a difference confirmed as statistically significant by an independent t-test (t = 3.766, p < 0.001).

A positive sign in the linear coefficients in the regression equation points to a direct relationship, whereas the positive sign of the interaction coefficients (X1*X2) refers to the synergistic effect of these variables. Results also revealed that the regression intercept represented the model prediction when each component is located at zero of its normalized level, is higher in adapted cells than in non-adapted cells. Contour plots of three variables under investigation show a high colchicine biotransformation percentage was achieved at 6 days of incubation time or more for adapted cells whereas the incubation time was delayed up to 9 days for non-adapted cells (Fig. 3). The enhancing impact of gamma radiation on K. pneumoniae-4 cells for achieving the maximum colchicine biotransformation yield was more obvious in non-adapted cells since an irradiation dose less than 0.3 kGy or higher than 0.8 kGy, achieved low biotransformation yields whereas exposing adapted cells to a dose higher than 0.8 kGy only negatively effect on colchicine biotransformation. Figure 4 shows the surface plots of colchicine biotransformation percentage for variables under investigation, either for adapted K. pneumoniae-4 cells or non-adapted ones. At the beginning of the incubation period (less than 72 h), the colchicine biotransformation percentage was less than 40% in non-adapted cells; this percentage elevated to nearly 60% in adapted cells. The latter percentage decreased with increasing irradiation dose to less than 40%; the negative effect of higher irradiation doses sharply declined with increasing incubation time (≈ 10 d).

Finally, the RSM optimizer showed the maximum colchicine biotransformation process was achieved at variable levels: 0.41 kGy irradiation dose and 9.55 d incubation time. Any change in irradiation dose level, either by increasing or decreasing above the optimal value, negatively affects colchicine biotransformation as shown in Fig. (S2). Alternatively, a slight increase in incubation time, higher than 9.6 days, was accompanied by declining colchicine biotransformation percentage.

Validation: The optimum values of variables under investigation estimated using the quadratic regression model were evaluated to assess the success of the predicted model for colchicine biotransformation by K. pneumoniae-4. The mean value of the experimental biotransformation was 87.37566% (Standard error mean = 1.3483%, n = 3). The 95% confidence interval ranged between 81.93921, and 90.56439%. The low relative deviation (1.30%) confirms the high predictive accuracy of the response surface model for colchicine biotransformation and demonstrates the reliability and validity of the model under the investigation conditions.

After 10 days of colchicine transformation by K. pneumoniae-4, two spots with olive green and watermelon red colors had 0.77 and 0.52 Rf. values. Discussion

Colchicine inhibits the microtubule polymerization process, which leads to the failure of microtubule assembly, development, and elongation, thus preventing membrane-dependent functions necessary for SARS-CoV-2 intake, transport, and replication. Colchicine, a long-standing anti-inflammatory drug typically used for gout; as a potential candidate to combat the “cytokine storm” associated with COVID-19 [24]. Secondary bacterial infections during this pandemic increased, particularly in serious cases, owing to immune dysregulation. Treating severe cases suffering from secondary bacterial infection for a long time with colchicine favors selective pressure and the development of robust enzymatic drug-detoxification pathways in these microbes. The isolation of bacteria adapted to colchicine was prioritized from clinical cases involving prolonged drug exposure, rather than high-concentration exposure, due to the drug’s inherent systemic toxicity. Particularly, the therapeutic dosages recommended for long-term ailments such gouty arthritis, familial Mediterranean fever (FMF), or Behçet’s illness are usually higher than the colchicine dosage regimens found in COVID-19 guidelines. Furthermore, the isolation of these adapted strains in COVID-19 patients is facilitated by the accessibility of clinical samples, such as sputum, which frequently harbor these bacterial co-pathogens [25].

Previous studies showed that K. pneumoniae was among the most frequent pathogens isolated from COVID-19 patients in Intensive Care Units (ICUs), often manifesting as ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) and tracheobronchitis [26]. In the present study, the colchicine concentration in the screening experiments did not exceed 7gl− 1 since a previous review revealed the major challenges facing the microbial conversion of colchicine are its low solubility in the liquid medium as well as its toxicity at higher concentrations due to feedback inhibition [16]. Similarly, the rate of demethylation activity of colchicine using Bacillus megaterium ACBT03-M3 declined at high concentrations of pseudoalkaloid, colchicine, due to loss of the retention capacity of the cell membrane and leakage of enzymes and metabolites from cells [13]. Alternatively, a previous study found that the energy metabolism, cell division process, and pathogenicity of microbes were interrupted by colchicine metabolism [27].

As shown in Table 1, the maximum conversion of colchicine in the initial screening experiment is nearly 40%; this ratio is comparable to that recorded by Zhang and his collaborators in 2017, where the microbial transformation of colchicine to its 2 and 3 O-dimethyl derivatives by Streptomyces griseus ATCC 13,273 was achieved by yields up to 51 and 31%, respectively [14]. The exposure of the secondary infectious bacteria, accompanied by SARS-CoV-2, to fluctuating sub-lethal concentrations, provides the selective pressure necessary for the microbial biotransformation with enzymes like amidases and/or cytochrome P450-like monooxygenases. NADPH is a cofactor, and NADP-cytochrome P450 reductase is a coenzyme required for P450 enzyme activity. Therefore, electrons are transferred from NADPH to NADPH-cytochrome P450 reductase and subsequently to cytochrome P450 during the cytochrome P450 reaction. As a result, one oxygen atom is inserted into the substrate, and molecular oxygen is reductively activated [21]. During the colchicine metabolization, bacteria may add or remove chemical groups such as hydroxyl or acetyl, which changes the hydrogen bonding efficiency and disturbs the ligand between the virus and receptor site. The demethylation process was followed by glycosylation, due to the glycosyltransferase activity that affects the amylase activity of the microbial candidates [16]. This alters the chemical structure (3D configuration) of colchicine; the resulting derivative may lose its affinity for the tubulin binding site, making the drug pharmacologically “silent” or inactive (Pharmacodynamic effect). The bacteria act as a “secondary liver” within the lung, accelerating the clearance of the drug locally before exerting its systemic or local impact (Pharmacokinetic effect).

The bioconversion of colchicine is essentially extracellular due to its toxicity; thus, its derivative products are recovered from cell-free filtrate as mentioned in the methods section. The interaction of the solvent system with cell-free fermentation broth affects the recovery of colchicine derivatives, as previously mentioned for alkaloids derived from plant or microbial origin [28]. The composition of fermentation media is a limiting factor in the colchicine conversion process since both glucose and glycerol enhance the process of glycosylation. In addition, the richness of yeast extract with amino acids (glutamic and alanine) and metals such as iron, calcium, and magnesium ions might have been helpful in the biosynthesis of monooxygenase enzymes [13].

A statistical optimization design is a cost-effective combination of statistical and mathematical methods that are recommended to optimize all variables without necessitating a large number of experimental runs, thereby developing and formulating novel products [20]. The biotransformation of colchicine by Klebsiella pneumoniae-4 underwent a significant evolution from its initial screening to its optimized state. Initially, during the preliminary screening phase, the native K. pneumoniae-4 isolate demonstrated a baseline biotransformation efficiency of 41.38 ± 2.05% over a 5-day incubation period. The biotransformation process was drastically improved via optimizing variables under investigation by more than a twofold increase (87.12%) in efficiency compared to the initial screening results.

RSM modeling showed that the colchicine biotransformation process was accompanied by a slight decrease at incubation times higher than 9.55 days, indicating that the responsible enzymes, likely part of the secondary metabolism, reach maximum induction during the late stationary phase. After that, the decline, as shown in Fig. (S2) is attributed to nutrient depletion; thus, cells may re-metabolize derivatives or undergo enzymatic denaturation due to pH shifts in the fermentation medium. A previous study found that colchicine bioconversion was maximized between 36 and 48 h of bacterial growth (more than threefold) after adapting the recombinant cells of E.coli with 7gl− 1 colchicine [11]. Furthermore, the use of adapted cells alone provided a significant advantage over non-adapted ones, achieving a mean rate of 70.11% versus 58.82%, thereby confirming that both metabolic adaptation and precise environmental tuning were essential in maximizing the conversion of colchicine.

In the present study, low-doses of gamma rays were manipulated to stimulate cellular metabolism and enzyme synthesis, which is known as hormesis [29]. It was found a significant synergistic interaction between irradiation and time (P < 0.011) was found, suggesting that gamma radiation at 0.41 kGy serves as a catalytic stressor, potentially upregulating P450 gene expression that is responsible for alkaloids, flavonoids, and fatty acids biosynthesis. Thus, the biotransformation of colchicine is a reservoir for the precursors of these valuable compounds. However, the negative quadratic effects reflect a threshold beyond which ionizing radiation causes irreversible oxidative damage to the bacterial cell membrane, resulting in microbial cell death [30].

The developed HPLC method revealed an excellent linearity within the range of 50–100 µg/mL (ppm). The method exhibited acceptable RSD percentages for quantitative analysis, although the RSD value at 50 ppm is slightly high (3.48%). Regarding the UV-vis spectrophotometric procedure, the low RSD percentages recorded for different colchicine concentrations suggested that the procedure is robust and exhibits high repeatability, minimizing the risk of experimental error during routine analysis of colchicine. TLC results showed the appearance and disappearance of metabolizing products of colchicine with increasing incubation time, suggesting these are transient intermediates in a multi-step enzymatic pathway, ultimately yielding stable end-products [31]. LC-ESI-MS/MS studies showed several commercial colchicine metabolized products that have pharmaceutical properties, including 3-(4-hydroxyphenyl) propanoic acid, 4’-hydroxy-2’-methylacetophenone, dihydrorotenone, and glabridin. Various mechanisms of anticancer activity have been identified at the top of reviews published in 2020 including inhibitors for angiogenesis, carbonic anhydrase, topoisomerase, telomerase, and hormone, as well as alkylating, antimitotic, and miscellaneous agents [32]. A previous study found that naringenin and 3-(4-hydroxyphenyl) propanoic acid (HPPA) belong to human gut microbial metabolites, where the latter compound was transformed by the action of microbial β-glucosidases and efficiently suppresses influenza infection [33]. Alternatively, 3-(4-hydroxyphenyl) propanoic acid was used as a raw material for industrial production of (1E,4E)-1,7-Bis(4-hydroxyphenyl) hepta-1,4-dien-3-one that exhibits better anti-tumor activity, higher bioavailability, and superior stability than curcumin [34]. Thiosemicarazone derivatives; 4’-hydroxy-2’-methylacetophenone is clinically described as an anticancer chemotherapeutic agent for tuberculosis treatment [35]. Other pharmaceutical properties of 4’-hydroxy-2’-methylacetophenone including testosterone hydroxylase inducer, aryl hydrocarbon hydroxylase inhibitor, beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase inhibitor, acidifier, and acidulant, are previously mentioned [36].

On the same trend, dihydrorotenone (DHR) is a mitochondrial electron transfer chain complex inhibitor that effectively reduces the expression of cancer-associated fibroblasts by impairing their ability to engage in tumor-promoting activities such as facilitating cancer cell proliferation and colonization. As a result, DHR disrupts the tumor cell cycle and is considered a drug-interrupting cell fibroblast formation [37]. Glabridin has a wide range of pharmacological properties, including antibacterial, antitumor, anti-inflammatory, anti-osteoporosis, and blood glucose and lipid regulators, as well as neuroprotective effects [38]. The most promising action of glabridin is its scavenging ability for reactive oxygen species that are overproduced in cancer cells, causing distribution in the cellular metabolism, modulating gene expression, and inducing genomic instability. High ROS levels enhance activation of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) signaling in cancerous cells via direct activation of oncogenes, such as Akt, or by inhibition of phosphatases [39]. Glabridin treatment inhibited Akt signaling in cancer cells [40]. The metabolic processing of colchicine by the human microbiome—specifically by opportunistic pathogens during COVID-19 or similar respiratory outbreaks-suggests certain pharmaceutical derivatives may exhibit higher cytotoxicity than the parent colchicine molecule or even standard chemotherapeutics like doxorubicin and cisplatin [41]. Similarly, the molecular docking analysis revealed that goat milk peptides exhibit high binding affinity towards the IL-6 receptor complex (IL-6R/gp130), suggesting their potential as inhibitors to prevent the hyper-inflammatory response associated with SARS-CoV-2 [42]. Recent docking studies have identified natural antiviral ligands from the Phyllanthus genus that effectively interact with the Delta variant spike protein, highlighting the role of bioactive natural products in multi-target viral inhibition [43].

This study elucidates the significant clinical and biotechnological implications of microbial adaptation within the complex microenvironment of COVID-19 patients. Prolonged exposure to colchicine under the physiological stress of pneumonia induces a directed development of secondary bacterial infection, converting the respiratory system into a favorable environment for the biotransformation of xenobiotic medications like colchicine. The present study suggests that the metabolization of colchicine with the secondary bacterial infection candidates, such as Klebsiella pneumoniae, may result in derivatives or metabolites with medical importance but having no anti-inflammatory action; thus, the suppression of the cytokine storm is reduced. As the presence of secondary bacterial infections may accelerate the breakdown of colchicine into inactive or toxic metabolites, the standard dosing regimen becomes obsolete. This may lead to treatment failure despite describing the optimal colchicine dose for individual clinical cases.

Consequently, this means “one-dose-fits-all,” which has been applied in national medical protocols during pandemic outbreaks, should be reconsidered, as the success of the anti-inflammatory impact is controlled by the patient’s own bacterial fingerprint. On the other hand, microbial metabolism may lead to derivatives with anti-inflammatory properties, such as glabridin, that support the action of the parent drug “colchicine” in a synergistic relationship that accelerates the curing process. Bacterial metabolites thus act as bioactive modulators that dictate the success or failure of anti-inflammatory interventions, while mimicking human metabolic pathways to provide a unique biological model for studying drug derivatives. Understanding these metabolizing pathways is vital for precision medicine during pandemics. Furthermore, the isolation of these modified strains offers a foundation for the industrial-scale manufacture of new, medically relevant colchicine derivatives. The combination of sustainable fermentation techniques with statistical modeling would be an alternative route to green production of pharmaceutical compounds, rather than conventional methods, including chemical synthesis or botanical recovery.

Conclusion

Microbial biotransformation is a successful tool for inducing metabolic derivatives of medical drugs, an alternative to recombinant human drug metabolizing enzymes, liver microsomes, and hepatocytes. In the present study, a typical schematic approach to studying the microbial transformation of colchicine was achieved. The microbial candidate (Klebsiella pneumoniae-4) that could metabolize colchicine was selected from fifteen clinical strains that were predominant during the COVID-19 outbreak. The integration of MALDI-TOF/MS, validated UV-vis, and HPLC methodologies provided a robust analytical framework, confirming the reliability and sensitivity of the biotransformation measurements. For the first time, the biotransformation process of colchicine was modeled via the response surface methodology after improving the metabolic system of the microbial candidate with gamma radiation (0.41 kGy) where incubation time and cell adaptation are the primary determinants of biotransformation efficiency. The study demonstrated that pre-adapting K. pneumoniae-4 cells to colchicine significantly enhances metabolic throughput. Under the optimized conditions of a 9.55-day incubation period, the model achieved a maximum biotransformation yield of 87.38%, a result closely aligned with the predicted values. Qualitative and quantitative characterization via LC-ESI-MS/MS confirmed the conversion of colchicine into fifteen distinct derivatives. Notably, the process yielded several compounds of significant pharmaceutical interest, including glabridin, 3-(4-hydroxyphenyl) propanoic acid, and dihydrorotenone. The present study provides a scalable and reliable bioprocess for the production of bioactive colchicine derivatives, offering a promising alternative to traditional chemical synthesis for the development of novel therapeutic agents. A further study interested in enzyme and gene system responsible for colchicine bio-transformed products recovered from this study will be performed.
