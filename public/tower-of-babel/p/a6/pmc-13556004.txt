Metal-phenolic networks: a promising strategy for cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Menglei Wang, Xuefeng Li, Zheyuan Xu, Yang Wang, Yiru Zheng, Aoxiang Li, Lingnan Chen, Meiqi Zhao, Daowei Ding, Xuming Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancer immunotherapy has revolutionized oncology, yet its clinical efficacy remains constrained by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, poor immune cell infiltration, low tumor immunogenicity, and inefficient delivery of immunomodulators. Metal-phenolic networks (MPNs), coordination assemblies formed from polyphenolic ligands and metal ions, have attracted increasing interest for their dual role in integrating material-intrinsic immune modulation with drug delivery. Metal ions such as Mn²⁺ and Fe²⁺ mediate context‑dependent immune regulation. Mn²⁺ boosts cGAS‑STING activation by enhancing dsDNA sensing and cGAMP‑STING interactions, while Fe2⁺ induces immunogenic cell death through Fenton reactions, with activities governed by factors including metal dose and redox state. Meanwhile, polyphenolic ligands provide structural scaffolding, interfacial assembly capability, cargo-loading capacity, and complementary bioactivity. The dynamic coordination bonds of MPNs further confer responsiveness to pH, glutathione, reactive oxygen species, ATP, and external stimuli, thereby enabling controlled payload release within tumors. This review critically summarizes the composition, assembly mechanisms, functionalization strategies, and stimuli-responsive properties of MPNs, with a particular focus on their applications in cancer immunotherapy encompassing immunotherapeutic agent delivery, intrinsic immunomodulation, combination therapy, and diagnostic imaging. We also examine major current limitations, including batch-to-batch reproducibility, pharmacokinetic uncertainty, metal-associated toxicity, premature disassembly, and the predominance of preclinical evidence. Overall, MPNs represent a promising class of coordination-based biomaterials for mechanistically informed cancer immunotherapy, but progress toward clinical translation will require clearer structure-activity relationships, reproducible manufacturing, clinically relevant in vivo validation, and long-term safety assessment.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Cancer is the second leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for nearly 20 million new cases and 9.7 million deaths in 2022, with more than 35 million new cases projected by 2050 [1]. Immunotherapy, particularly such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), CAR-T cell therapy, and tumor vaccines, activates or restores the patient’s own immune system to achieve durable control over certain malignant tumors, representing a paradigm shift in oncology treatment [2–4]. However, the clinical application of immunotherapy still faces significant limitations. Immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) characterized by weak tumor antigenicity, inadequate antigen presentation, limited immune cell infiltration, and abundant immunosuppressive cells and signals, leads to “cold” tumors with poor immunotherapy response [5]. Off-target toxicity and low delivery efficiency of current therapies present another critical challenge. Current immunoadjuvants face different issues such as significant toxicity upon systemic administration, difficulty in effectively accumulating and penetrating target cells at tumor sites, as well as short circulation time [6]. Consequently, the development of innovative strategies capable of specifically targeting tumor tissues, overcoming the immunosuppressive barrier, and synergistically enhancing immune responses has emerged as a critical objective that demands focused research and advancement.

In this context, nanotechnology has attracted extensive attention [7]. While nanomedicines offer programmable physicochemical properties and delivery potential, their clinical performance is often constrained by heterogeneous enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects and biological barriers [8, 9]. Among various nanoplatforms, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have achieved clinical success, particularly in mRNA vaccine delivery, yet they primarily serve as passive carriers with limited intrinsic immunomodulatory activity [10]. Polymeric nanoparticles offer versatility in drug loading and surface functionalization, but generally lack inherent bioactivity and may face concerns regarding degradation behavior, long-term residue, or formulation complexity [11]. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), while offering high porosity and tunable composition as another significant metal-based platform, are limited by more demanding synthesis conditions, higher cost, and increased structural complexity [12, 13]. Metal-phenolic networks (MPNs), as an emerging and highly versatile class of supramolecular materials, have introduced renewed momentum into cancer immunotherapy. MPNs are amorphous coordination networks formed by the assembly of multivalent metal ions and polyphenolic ligands, typically under mild aqueous conditions [14]. Unlike many conventional nanocarriers, MPNs can integrate intrinsic bioactivity with delivery functionality. MPNs not only serve as effective drug delivery vehicles, but also exhibit biological activities originating from their constituent polyphenols and metal ions depending on composition, dose, redox state, and biological context [15]. For example, Mn²⁺ has been shown to enhance the sensitivity of cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) to double-stranded DNA and strengthen cGAMP-STING interactions, thereby promoting cGAS-STING pathway activation [16]. Fe³⁺-based MPNs release Fe²⁺ in the TME, catalyzing Fenton reactions and inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) [17]. At the same time, the dynamic metal-phenolic coordination chemistry of MPNs enables modular assembly, facile surface coating, cargo co-loading, and responsiveness to tumor-associated stimuli such as pH, glutathione, reactive oxygen species, and ATP [18]. These properties make MPNs especially attractive for applications requiring simultaneous delivery, microenvironment responsiveness, and active immunomodulation [19–21].

Importantly, the value of MPNs lies not simply in novelty, but in their potential to occupy a practical niche that is not fully addressed by existing platforms. Compared with LNPs and most polymeric nanoparticles, MPNs more readily combine carrier function with composition-derived immunological activity. Compared with MOFs, MPNs are generally fabricated under milder, faster, and more aqueous-compatible conditions, which is advantageous for coating cells, proteins, bacteria, and other biologically sensitive substrates. In addition, MPNs can be readily engineered into nanoparticles, surface coatings, or hybrid theranostic systems, facilitating multimodal integration of immunotherapy and imaging [22, 23]. Nevertheless, these advantages should be interpreted with caution. The biological effects of polyphenols and metal ions are context-dependent, and issues such as metal-associated toxicity, pharmacokinetics, batch reproducibility, and regulatory complexity remain significant barriers to clinical translation.

Existing reviews on MPNs have mainly focused on non-oncological applications or broad cancer theranostics, whereas their role in cancer immunotherapy has not been critically summarized (Table 1) [21, 24–28]. Given the growing evidence showing that MPNs can simultaneously function as immunomodulatory materials, stimuli-responsive carriers, and multifunctional therapeutic interfaces, a dedicated review is timely. In this review, we provide a comprehensive and critical overview of recent advances in MPNs for cancer immunotherapy. We first elucidate the building blocks of MPNs as well as their immunomodulatory properties, and then delve into their assembly mechanisms and stimuli-responsive behaviors to clarify the chemical basis of their smart functionalities. Finally, we highlight representative applications of MPNs in cancer immunotherapy and discuss the major translational barriers that need to be addressed for future clinical development.

Composition of MPNs

MPNs are supramolecular materials self-assembled under mild conditions via coordination between polyphenols and metal ions [29]. The assembly process is rapid, facile, and versatile, yielding either colloidal nanoparticles in solution or conformal coatings on diverse organic, inorganic, and biological interfaces [30]. The functional versatility of MPNs arises from the synergistic interplay of their two core building blocks: metal ions serve both as bioactive nodes and structural crosslinkers, while polyphenols function as multifunctional ligands and architectural scaffolds. This division of labor endows MPNs with an integrated platform that seamlessly couples intrinsic therapeutic activity with intelligent delivery capabilities, transcending conventional inert carriers [31].

Metal ions serve as the central determinants of the intrinsic therapeutic and immunomodulatory functions of MPNs [16, 32, 33]. Upon dissociation in the TME, these ions act as bioactive signals, engaging with cellular pathways to influence immune responses [34]. Iron ions, as essential trace elements for life, combine high connectivity with low biotoxicity [35]. These attributes, especially the capacity of Fe²⁺ to catalyze the Fenton reaction for generating highly cytotoxic •OH from endogenous H₂O₂, make them the most extensively utilized metal species in MPNs and a cornerstone of Fenton reaction-based tumor therapy [26, 36]. This process, known as chemodynamic therapy, not only directly kills tumor cells but also potently induces ICD, releasing damage-associated molecular patterns that recruit and activate antigen-presenting cells [37, 38]. Mn²⁺ has emerged as a prominent class of immunomodulatory metal ions [39, 40]. Through systematic screening of various metal ions, Sun et al. demonstrated that Mn²⁺ amplified STING agonist activity by 12‑ to 77‑fold, an effect consistently observed across different human STING haplotypes [16]. Through the co-delivery of Mn²⁺ and immune agonists, it is possible to achieve the dual synergistic activation of innate immunity. Copper ions (Cu²⁺/Cu⁺) share Fenton-like catalytic activity with iron and can induce cuproptosis, a novel form of regulated cell death triggered by the aggregation of lipoylated mitochondrial enzymes, which is increasingly recognized for its potential immunogenicity [41]. The selection of metal ions in MPNs is expanding from conventional ions such as Fe³⁺, Mn²⁺, and Cu²⁺ toward those with more diverse functionalities. Zinc ions (Zn²⁺) serve as essential micronutrients that lead to ROS-induced mitochondrial injury and the inhibition of glycolysis [42, 43], while aluminum ions (Al³⁺) function as classic FDA-approved vaccine adjuvants by promoting antigen depot effects and enhancing humoral immunity [44–46]. Beyond these common metals, emerging research has introduced less conventional ions such as hafnium (Hf⁴⁺), tungsten (W⁶⁺) and lanthanide ions, which act as radiosensitizers due to their high atomic numbers, enhancing radiation therapy efficacy and promoting antigen release [47–49]. Vanadyl ions (VO²⁺) have recently been shown to induce endoplasmic reticulum stress-mediated ICD, expanding the functional repertoire of MPN metal components [50]. Furthermore, dual-metal systems have been developed to achieve synergistic effects, such as Mn²⁺ combined with Al³⁺ to amplify immune responses or Fe³⁺ combined with Mn²⁺ to simultaneously induce ferroptosis and STING pathway activation, demonstrating the power of rational metal selection in MPN design [51, 52].

Polyphenols, characterized by aromatic rings and hydroxyl groups, engage biomolecules through hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds, and are distinguished by their biocompatibility and biodegradability [53]. As an FDA-approved, low-toxicity plant polyphenol, Tannic acid (TA) is widely used due to its higher stability and biocompatibility than other phenols [54]. TA can rapidly and stably crosslink with various metal ions, resulting in robust MPN coatings or nanoparticles that exhibit excellent colloidal stability and pH-responsive dissociation [55]. Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), the major catechin from green tea, serves as an effective chelating agent and possesses inherent biological activities that can complement immunotherapy, such as suppressing regulatory T cells (Tregs) or modulating metabolic pathways [56, 57]. Certain natural polyphenols exhibit dual functions as both structural components and therapeutic agents. Shikonin, a naphthoquinone compound, not only participates in MPN assembly but also induces ICD through ferroptosis and necroptosis, functioning as a powerful immunostimulant [17]. Chlorogenic acid (CHA) demonstrates immunomodulatory properties by inhibiting Treg activity, while gossypol contributes chemotherapeutic effects to induce ICD when incorporated into MPNs [58, 59]. Beyond natural polyphenols, synthetic modifications have expanded the functional repertoire of phenolic ligands. Wang et al. introduced synthetic polyphenols into the MPN platform by conjugating them with dendrimers via phenylboronic ester bonds, enabling them to function simultaneously as structural components of the MPN and as chemotherapeutic agents [60]. Polydopamine (PDA), as a polyphenol-like material, possesses both photothermal conversion capability and metal coordination capacity. Leveraging this, Liu et al. constructed a PDA-Mn MPN coating on the surface of gold nanoclusters through coordination assembly between PDA and Mn²⁺, achieving the multifunctional integration of photothermal therapy, chemodynamic therapy, and STING activation [61]. This rich diversity of phenolic ligands, ranging from naturally occurring compounds to synthetically engineered derivatives, provides tremendous flexibility in tailoring MPN properties for specific immunotherapeutic applications, enabling precise control over network stability, drug loading capacity, and stimuli-responsive behavior.

Assembly mechanisms and functionalization strategies

Assembly mechanisms and synthesis

The assembly of MPNs relies on the coordination-driven supramolecular interactions between phenolic ligands and metal ions, enabling rapid and versatile fabrication of functional nanomaterials under mild conditions [62]. The formation of MPNs is primarily governed by the coordination bonds between the galloyl or catechol groups of polyphenols and metal ions [63, 64]. Importantly, the coordination chemistry underlying MPN assembly is dynamic and depends on ligand deprotonation, metal identity, and local stoichiometry. In catechol- or gallol-containing systems, metal-phenolic interactions can exist in mono-, bis-, and tris-coordination states, which differ in coordination number and crosslinking capability. In general, lower-order coordination is more likely under acidic conditions, where phenolic groups are less deprotonated, whereas neutral to mildly alkaline conditions favor higher-order coordination and denser network formation [65, 66]. These coordination states are key determinants of MPN architecture, influencing crosslinking density, compactness, permeability, and disassembly kinetics. Accordingly, MPNs are better regarded as dynamic coordination assemblies rather than fixed static frameworks. Upon mixing, metal ions act as crosslinkers, bridging multiple phenolic ligands to form amorphous three-dimensional networks. This coordination process is highly dynamic and reversible, proceeding spontaneously within seconds to minutes at ambient temperature in aqueous solutions without the need for organic solvents or complex equipment [67].

Several synthetic strategies have been developed to engineer MPNs with controlled compositions, morphologies, and functionalities. The one-step assembly method involves simply mixing metal ions and phenolic ligands in solution, leading to the rapid formation of MPN nanoparticles, films, or coatings on various substrates (Fig. 1A) [68, 69]. This approach is straightforward, scalable, and compatible with a wide range of metal ions and natural or synthetic polyphenols. The particle size, film thickness, and network density can be tuned by adjusting precursor concentrations, pH, and metal-to-ligand ratios.

The multistep layer-by-layer assembly involves sequential deposition of phenolic ligands and metal ions onto substrates, allowing precise control over film thickness and composition (Fig. 1B) [70, 71]. This method yields MPNs with higher metal ion content and tailored multilayered structures, which are advantageous for applications requiring tunable permeability, mechanical properties, and drug release kinetics [72]. The thickness typically increases with the number of deposition cycles and solution pH [25].

Emulsion-based interfacial assembly exploits the immiscible liquid-liquid interface to form MPN films or capsules. Metal ions and phenolic ligands dissolved in different phases coordinate at the droplet interface, producing stable MPN-coated emulsions or hollow spheres after phase separation (Fig. 1C) [73]. This method is particularly suitable for encapsulating hydrophobic drugs and creating multifunctional theranostic platforms.

Template-assisted assembly utilizes sacrificial templates such as CaCO3 nanoparticles, polystyrene spheres, or mesoporous silica to create hollow MPN capsules or porous structures with defined sizes and shapes (Fig. 1D) [74]. After MPN coating, template removal yields hollow capsules with high surface area and loading capacity, ideal for drug delivery and imaging applications [75]. This strategy also enables the fabrication of ellipsoidal, tubular, or hierarchical MPN architectures by using templates with specific geometries.

Emerging techniques such as electrotriggered assembly, spray coating, and mechanochemical synthesis offer additional routes for MPN fabrication. Electrotriggered assembly uses anodic oxidation to generate metal ions locally, enabling spatially controlled MPN deposition on conductive substrates [76]. Spray coating provides rapid and uniform MPN films on large-area surfaces with tunable thickness [77].

Although current MPN assembly strategies are versatile, each involves trade-offs relevant to biomedical translation. One-step assembly is simple and scalable but offers less control over heterogeneity and reproducibility. Layer-by-layer assembly enables more precise control over thickness and composition, yet is more time-consuming and less scalable. Emulsion-based interfacial assembly is suitable for hollow structures and hydrophobic cargos, but multiphase processing may complicate uniformity and purification. Template-assisted assembly provides precise architectures and high loading capacity, though template preparation and removal increase complexity. Emerging methods such as electrotriggered assembly and spray coating offer unique fabrication advantages, but their relevance to systemic nanomedicine is less established. Therefore, assembly strategy should be selected according to the desired material format, cargo properties, and translational requirements, including reproducibility, scalability, and quality control.

In addition to the assembly route, key preparation parameters also shape MPN performance in a mechanistically important manner. For example, the metal-to-ligand ratio influences coordination saturation and crosslinking density, thereby affecting particle compactness, cargo retention, and release behavior. pH regulates phenolic deprotonation and coordination state, while ionic strength, solvent environment, and mixing sequence can alter nucleation, colloidal stability, and structural homogeneity. Metal identity and oxidation state further determine coordination strength, redox activity, and degradation behavior. Although these relationships are often system-dependent, they are highly relevant to biomedical applications because changes in MPN structure can ultimately affect circulation stability, cellular uptake, intracellular disassembly, and immune activation. From a translational perspective, understanding these parameter-property relationships will be important for reproducible manufacturing and reliable in vivo performance.

Characterization techniques

A range of analytical techniques are employed to comprehensively characterize the physicochemical properties of MPNs. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential analysis are used to determine hydrodynamic size, polydispersity index, and surface charge, offering a preliminary assessment of colloidal stability and cellular interactions. However, DLS results are sensitive to the solution environment and sample concentration and could not reflect the dry-state size. Therefore, batch reproducibility such as triplicate measurement and coefficient of variation, as well as colloidal stability in relevant buffers and serum over time should be evaluated. Morphological features, including size and core-shell structure, are visualized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Given that electron microscopy captures only local fields and cannot represent the whole population distribution, representative images from multiple batches and a comparison between DLS and TEM sizes to infer aggregation or swelling are required. UV-Vis spectroscopy confirms the characteristic absorption peaks of metal‑phenolic complexes, verifying successful formation of the main skeletal structure, yet it cannot perform quantitative structural analysis. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analyze surface elemental composition and chemical bonding, further corroborating coordination interactions. It is essential to confirm that metal ions are in the expected oxidation state and that no toxic synthesis residues remain. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) accurately quantifies the total metal ion loading in MPNs, whereas high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) enables precise measurement of drug loading and encapsulation efficiency. Both methods require strict standard curve calibration to ensure accuracy. Nevertheless, ICP‑MS does not reveal release kinetics or the free vs. bound metal pool, and HPLC does not assess drug stability within the MPNs. Hence, metal‑ion release kinetics in simulated physiological buffers, including cumulative release profiles, should be reported, along with drug release kinetics under relevant conditions and stability during storage. Collectively, beyond basic physicochemical parameters, translational quality controls including serum stability, protein corona analysis, endotoxin and sterility testing, hemocompatibility, immune cell toxicity, biodistribution and clearance studies, and metal‑ion release kinetics, are important to adequately assess the readiness of MPNs‑based cancer immunotherapies for in vivo and clinical translation.

Functionalization strategies

The remarkable versatility of MPNs for cancer immunotherapy stems not only from their intrinsic building blocks but also from the diverse functionalization strategies that enable precise control over their physicochemical properties, targeting capabilities, and therapeutic payloads. The abundant phenolic hydroxyl groups on MPN surfaces serve as versatile anchors for post-modification, allowing researchers to tailor these platforms for specific immunotherapeutic applications.

One of the most straightforward functionalization approaches involves surface coating with biocompatible polymers. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) modification, or PEGylation, is widely employed to enhance MPN colloidal stability, prolong blood circulation time, and reduce non-specific protein adsorption [78]. PEGylated polyphenols can be directly incorporated during MPN assembly or conjugated post-synthesis, yielding nanoparticles with improved pharmacokinetic profiles [49]. Hyaluronic acid (HA) coating represents another important strategy, leveraging HA’s intrinsic targeting affinity for CD44 receptors overexpressed on various tumor cells [79]. HA-modified MPNs exhibit active tumor targeting, enhanced cellular uptake, and potential immunomodulatory effects due to HA’s biological activity.

Targeting ligand conjugation significantly enhances MPN accumulation at tumor sites and specific immune cell populations. For instance, Xu et al. physically coated the MPN surface with fibronectin (FN), leveraging its specific recognition of αvβ3 integrin to achieve active targeted delivery to tumor cells [18]. Zhang et al. constructed an MPN platform co-loaded with TMZ and a DHODH inhibitor (TBFP-MT) via the self-assembly of PEG-polyphenol and Fe³⁺, and further modified it with T(10) and cMBP dual-targeting peptides to enable blood-brain barrier penetration and precise targeting of drug-resistant glioblastoma cells [80]. Furthermore, functional modification of the polyphenolic components within MPNs can impart new responsive properties. Zhang et al. designed a disulfide bond-based amphiphilic polyphenol (PP-SS-DA), which not only retains the metal coordination capability of polyphenols but also introduces a GSH-responsive cleavage function, enabling specific responsiveness to the high GSH environment in tumor cells [81].

The modular nature of these functionalization strategies allows for the rational design of MPN-based immunotherapeutics tailored to specific cancer types, immune microenvironments, and treatment regimens. By carefully selecting functional components and assembly conditions, researchers can create MPN platforms with optimized size, surface charge, targeting specificity, drug loading capacity, and release kinetics for enhanced cancer immunotherapy.

Stimuli-responsive mechanisms

The TME exhibits distinct physicochemical characteristics that distinguish it from normal tissues, including acidic pH, hypoxia, elevated glutathione (GSH) levels, high concentrations of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and immunosuppression [82–84]. These unique features not only support tumor progression and immune evasion but also provide exploitable triggers for smart drug delivery systems. MPNs have emerged as particularly promising platforms for TME-responsive cancer immunotherapy due to their dynamic and reversible coordination bonds, which enable spatiotemporally controlled drug release, targeted therapeutic intervention, and reduced systemic toxicity. While multi-stimuli responsiveness enhances precision, increasing system complexity may compromise reproducibility and translational feasibility.

pH responsiveness is the most widely exploited mechanism in MPN-based platforms (Fig. 2A). It arises from the pH-dependent stability of metal–phenolate coordination: under neutral to weakly alkaline conditions, deprotonated phenolic groups favor coordination and network formation, whereas acidic conditions weaken coordination and promote disassembly. In TA-based systems, pH can also shift the balance among mono-, bis-, and tris-coordinated species, thereby affecting crosslinking density and release behavior. The mildly acidic tumor microenvironment provides effective triggers for weakening metal-phenolic coordination and promoting payload release. For instance, Fe³⁺-TA-HADA MPN capsules showed accelerated doxorubicin release under acidic conditions [85], while Mn²⁺-TA-coated nanoparticles exhibited enhanced payload release in acidic tumor interstitial fluid [86]. Importantly, this process is metal-dependent rather than universal. Fe³⁺ generally forms more stable TA-based networks than Mn²⁺, while Cu²⁺ often shows intermediate but system-dependent behavior. Although binding strengths have been reported for different metal–polyphenol pairs, direct quantitative comparison across studies remains difficult because they depend strongly on ligand structure and experimental conditions.

Redox responsiveness, particularly to GSH, represents another crucial mechanism for TME-targeted drug delivery (Fig. 2B). Cancer cells typically exhibit elevated intracellular GSH levels compared to normal cells, creating a strongly reducing environment [87]. GSH-responsive properties can be engineered into MPNs through two primary strategies involving the incorporation of disulfide bonds into the polyphenolic backbone or the exploitation of redox-active metal ions. Disulfide-containing polyphenols undergo thiol-disulfide exchange reactions in the presence of high GSH concentrations, leading to network degradation and payload release [81]. Simultaneously, GSH can reduce Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺ or Cu²⁺ to Cu⁺, not only triggering MPN disassembly but also amplifying oxidative stress through Fenton or Fenton-like reactions that generate cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (•OH).

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) responsiveness has emerged as a sophisticated targeting strategy, capitalizing on the elevated ATP levels in tumor microenvironments (Fig. 2C) [88]. ATP-responsive MPNs are typically designed using competitive coordination mechanisms, where ATP molecules compete with phenolic ligands for metal ion binding, triggering network disassembly. The t-P@TFP nanoparticles exemplify this strategy, where ATP-triggered disassembly of TA-Fe³⁺ films following photothermal activation enabled sequential drug release and inhibition of ATP-dependent drug efflux pumps, overcoming multidrug resistance [89].

ROS responsiveness addresses the oxidative nature of inflammatory TMEs. Boronic ester-containing polyphenols can be incorporated into MPNs to confer ROS-sensitive degradation, as demonstrated in systems where tumor-associated ROS levels trigger payload release (Fig. 2D). For instance, Xiang et al. constructed a TA-Pt (II) / Fe (III) nanocomplex (PTI) with dual pH- and ROS-responsive drug release. Elevated levels of ROS in the TME promote the oxidation of the galloyl groups of polyphenols, thereby disrupting the metal-phenolic coordination bonds and triggering controlled release of platinum-based therapeutics to induce effective ICD [90].

Photothermal responsiveness exploits the intrinsic near-infrared (NIR) absorption of MPNs arising from ligand-to-metal charge transfer transitions (Fig. 2E). TA-Fe³⁺ complexes exhibit strong absorption in the NIR region and achieve a photothermal conversion efficiency of 32.09% under 808 nm NIR laser irradiation [51]. Furthermore, the photothermal effect can be tuned by selecting different metal ions, with VO2⁺-TA complexes exhibiting broad 400–900 nm NIR absorption and a photothermal conversion efficiency of 19.3% [91].

In addition to well-established triggers such as pH, GSH, ROS, and photothermal effects, MPNs can also respond to X‑rays to achieve therapeutic amplification (Fig. 2F). In the PPWQ platform developed by Wang et al., W⁶⁺ enhances ROS production upon X‑ray irradiation, resulting in DNA damage that synergizes with quercetin‑mediated metabolic modulation and PD‑L1 blockade to promote radiosensitization and immune activation [48]. Similarly, in Hb@Hf‑Ce6 NPs, X‑rays‑excited Hf triggers radioluminescence to activate Ce6 for ROS generation in radiodynamic therapy, thereby eliciting a comprehensive antitumor immune response [92].

Beyond active responsiveness to external stimuli, MPNs can achieve passively regulated drug release through rational structural design. This process is governed not by triggers such as pH or GSH but by the intrinsic architecture of the material itself, thereby enabling sustained and controlled delivery kinetics. For example, CFZ-pTA-alb nanocapsules were constructed via interfacial supramolecular assembly, in which a TA-Fe³⁺ MPN shell was deposited onto a carfilzomib core, followed by an outer albumin coating to form a dual diffusion barrier. The densely crosslinked TA-Fe³⁺ network effectively impedes CFZ release from the core, while the albumin layer further prolongs the diffusional path length and increases resistance [93]. The synergistic action of these two barriers results in the sustained and gradual release of CFZ.

Applications of MPNs in cancer immunotherapy

Despite the transformative impact of cancer immunotherapy, its efficacy is limited by distinct but interconnected barriers that vary across therapeutic modalities. For ICIs, durable responses usually require pre-existing tumor antigen presentation and sufficient effector T-cell infiltration; thus, immune-desert/immune-ignorant tumors lacking T-cell priming, as well as T-cell-excluded tumors in which lymphocytes remain trapped in the stromal periphery, often show poor responsiveness. Even in inflamed tumors, immunosuppressive myeloid cells, regulatory T cells, inhibitory cytokines, metabolic competition, and alternative checkpoint pathways can drive dysfunction of tumor-reactive lymphocytes. In the clinical setting, these mechanisms contribute to both primary resistance, in which patients fail to respond initially, and acquired resistance, in which tumors relapse after an initial benefit from ICIs [94]. Other immunotherapeutic modalities face additional delivery-related bottlenecks. Immunostimulatory agonists, cytokines, and adjuvants often suffer from short circulation, poor tumor selectivity, and dose-limiting systemic toxicity [95]. Therapeutic proteins and antibodies are further restricted by biological barriers such as inefficient cellular uptake and limited access to intracellular targets [96]. Cancer vaccines and ICD-inducing strategies may also be constrained by insufficient antigen release, suboptimal dendritic-cell activation, or a failure to convert immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones [97]. Therefore, effective platforms should not only improve drug delivery, but also enhance tumor immunogenicity, reverse immunosuppressive niches, and enable mechanism-guided combination therapy. As summarized in Fig. 3, these features make MPNs well suited to address multiple barriers in cancer immunotherapy. MPNs are particularly attractive because they offer mechanistic specificity beyond that of conventional inert nanocarriers. Their metal ions can participate in immune activation or tumor sensitization processes, such as cGAS-STING pathway activation, ROS amplification, and ICD induction, while polyphenolic ligands provide structural scaffolding together with complementary immunomodulatory or protective functions. Moreover, the dynamic coordination chemistry of MPNs enables pH-, GSH-, ROS-, ATP-, or externally triggered drug release, facilitating spatiotemporal control over payload exposure in tumors. Through this combination of intrinsic bioactivity, TME responsiveness, and modular co-delivery capability, MPNs are well positioned to address the heterogeneous barriers that limit different forms of cancer immunotherapy [98, 99]. Delivery enhancement of immunotherapeutic agents

Among different applications, delivery enhancement has relatively direct translational relevance for many clinically used or clinically emerging immunotherapeutic agents, including antibodies, cytokines, adjuvants, nucleic-acid agonists, and therapeutic proteins, are limited by poor stability, short circulation, off-target toxicity, or inefficient intracellular access. MPNs are useful to address these barriers through mild encapsulation, surface shielding, tumor-responsive disassembly, and endosomal escape. Therapeutic antibodies represent a central class of cancer immunotherapeutics, but their large molecular size, hydrophilicity, and limited membrane permeability largely restrict them to extracellular or membrane-associated targets [125, 126]. However, many immunologically relevant targets, including intracellular PD-L1 domains, phosphorylated STAT3, mutant RAS, and MYC, are located in the cytoplasm or nucleus [127]. Therefore, expanding antibody action from extracellular targets to intracellular compartments is an important frontier. A polymeric MPN nanocarrier, constructed through self-assembly of polyphenol-conjugated polymers and Fe³⁺, encapsulated antibodies within approximately 30 nm core-shell complexes with near-neutral surface charge (Fig. 4A) [32]. These complexes exhibited excellent pharmacokinetics and tumor accumulation following intravenous administration. Within tumor cells, the acidic endosomal environment triggered MPN disassembly, and the associated buffering effect arising from coordination-bond dissociation facilitated endosomal escape, releasing functional antibodies into the cytosol to bind intracellular antigens. This strategy demonstrates the potential of MPNs to extend the targetable space of antibody therapeutics.

MPNs are also useful for converting toxic or unstable immunomodulators into safer therapeutic formulations. Melittin, a naturally occurring cytolytic peptide, has potent antitumor activity but is severely limited by hemolytic toxicity. TA-Fe³⁺ MPN encapsulation shielded the positive charges of melittin, reducing hemolysis while preserving its ability to induce ICD. Co-loading with the immune adjuvant R848 generated a nanodrug that promoted both local and systemic antitumor immune responses, including abscopal effects in bilateral tumor models [101]. Similarly, the STING agonist cGAMP suffers from poor intracellular delivery and enzymatic degradation [39]. An MPN-based competitive coordination platform enabled co-delivery of cGAMP, zoledronic acid, and DOX, thereby integrating STING activation, pyroptosis induction, and antitumor immune stimulation against orthotopic breast tumors and metastases [110].

Biological vectors can also be engineered using MPN coatings. Live attenuated bacteria are attractive for cancer immunotherapy because of their tumor-hypoxia tropism and inherent immunoadjuvant properties, but their clinical use requires improved controllability and biosafety [128, 129]. Yang et al. developed an MPN coating system based on attenuated Salmonella VNP20009, in which Fe³⁺-TA coatings enabled integration with a CRISPR/dCas9 gene-editing system to enhance tumor pyroptosis and antitumor immunity (Fig. 4B) [113]. Although such live-vector and gene-editing systems remain more exploratory than antibody or small-molecule delivery platforms, they highlight the versatility of MPNs in regulating complex biological therapeutics.

Intrinsic immunomodulatory functions of MPNs: immune activation and immunogenic remodeling

Beyond serving as delivery vehicles, MPNs can directly participate in cancer immunotherapy through their intrinsic immunomodulatory properties, particularly by promoting immune activation and immunogenic remodeling of the TME. These functions arise from coordinated actions of metal ions and phenolic ligands, which can engage innate immune signaling, amplify oxidative stress, induce immunogenic tumor-cell death, and improve antigen presentation. A representative mechanism is activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, a central innate immune axis that induces type I interferon production and dendritic-cell activation. Mn²⁺ can potentiate cGAS-STING signaling by enhancing dsDNA sensing and stabilizing cGAMP-STING interactions [130–132]. Accordingly, Mn-based MPNs can function not only as carriers but also as immunostimulatory materials. For example, CpG-ODN-coated Mn-phenolic network nanoparticles released Mn²⁺ in acidic endosomes of antigen-presenting cells, thereby promoting STING activation and antitumor immunity [86].

Another clinically important strategy is to increase tumor immunogenicity through ICD or other inflammatory cell-death programs. Ferroptosis can induce ICD and thereby trigger antitumor immunity. TA-Fe³⁺ MPN-based platforms, including MFTA and FeShik nanomedicines, leverage this mechanism to trigger ICD through ferroptosis and necroptosis, promoting dendritic cells (DCs) maturation, antigen cross-presentation, and cytotoxic T lymphocyte infiltration [17, 100]. Recently, MPNs have also been designed to induce dual immunogenic death pathways. Cuproptosis and pyroptosis have gained increasing attention as newly identified ICD modalities in tumor immunotherapy [133–136]. Copper-coordinated chlorogenic acid nanoparticles released Cu²⁺ and CHA in the TME, resulting in ROS amplification, GSH depletion, pyroptosis, and cuproptosis (Fig. 4C) [118]. This dual-mode tumor-cell death enhanced ICD, stimulated dendritic-cell maturation and CD8⁺ T-cell activation, and helped reprogram the immunosuppressive TME.

Cancer vaccines elicit tumor-specific immune responses and represent a key immunotherapeutic strategy for cancer treatment [137]. MPNs are well suited for vaccine construction owing to their interfacial assembly, adhesive properties, stimulus-responsive disassembly, antigen-preserving ability, and adjuvant integration. For whole-cell vaccination, autologous tumor cells contain patient-specific neoantigens but are difficult to process without antigen loss [138]. He et al. developed a whole-cell cancer vaccine by forming an in situ MnTA nanocloak on tumor cell surfaces, preserving antigenic complexity while introducing Mn²⁺-mediated immunostimulatory activity [102]. For vesicle-based vaccines, bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) are natural adjuvants but are limited by immunotoxicity and insufficient tumor specificity [139–141]. Fe³⁺-TA coatings were used to construct OMV@MPN nanovaccines with dual functions of attenuation and in situ antigen generation (Fig. 4D, E) [108]. In addition, CHA-Fe³⁺ MPNs co-loaded Ovalbumin (OVA) and the immunologically active polyphenol CHA through one-step assembly, achieving lymph-node-targeted delivery and robust antitumor immune activation [58]. These vaccine-oriented studies remain largely preclinical, but they are highly relevant to personalized cancer vaccination because they address antigen preservation, adjuvanticity, and lymphoid delivery.

Combination therapy

Combination therapy is one of the most clinically relevant directions for MPN-based cancer immunotherapy because many tumors fail to respond to monotherapy due to redundant immune-evasion pathways, poor antigenicity, hypoxia, stromal exclusion, or adaptive resistance. In this subsection, priority is given to combinations involving clinically established treatment modalities, especially chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immune checkpoint blockade, whereas more exploratory strategies such as gas therapy, irreversible electroporation, and sonodynamic therapy are discussed more briefly.

Chemotherapy/chemodynamic therapy combined with immunotherapy

ICD bridges direct tumor killing and antitumor immunity by exposing calreticulin and releasing damage-associated molecular patterns, which recruit and activate DCs to promote cross-presentation of tumor antigens and prime tumor-specific T cell responses [142, 143]. MPNs are particularly suitable for ICD-mediated combination therapy because they can integrate cytotoxic agents, catalytic metal ions, immune adjuvants, and checkpoint inhibitors within a single responsive platform. For chemotherapy/chemodynamic therapy combined with immunotherapy, MPNs can co-deliver chemotherapeutic agents and Fenton-reactive metal ions to amplify oxidative stress and ICD. PFS-NP nanoparticles incorporated disulfide bond-based amphiphilic polyphenols, dopamine-modified cisplatin prodrugs, and Fe³⁺ through coordination reactions [81]. After cellular entry, responsive release depleted intracellular GSH, activated cisplatin, increased H₂O₂ levels, and promoted Fenton reactions, thereby inducing ICD and antitumor immunity. PTI nanocomplexes, constructed through coordination of TA with 1,2-diaminocyclohexane-Pt(II), achieved high platinum loading and dual-responsive release, induced ICD, promoted CTL infiltration, reduced immunosuppression, and synergized with immune checkpoint blockade [90]. Similarly, hypoxia-relieving MDP nanoparticles combined DOX chemotherapy with Fe³⁺-mediated chemodynamic therapy to amplify ICD and antigen release (Fig. 5A) [119]. When combined with PD-1 blockade, this platform elicited systemic antitumor responses against both primary and distant tumors.

Photothermal therapy combined with immunotherapy

Photothermal therapy provides another route to local tumor destruction and immune priming. However, photothermal ablation alone often fails to generate durable systemic immunity [144]. MPNs can overcome this limitation by combining photothermal conversion, ICD induction, and immune checkpoint blockade. The t-P@TFP nanoparticles enabled NIR-triggered multistage reactions, ATP-responsive paclitaxel release, hyperthermia-induced ICD, and potentiation of checkpoint blockade in multidrug-resistant tumors [89]. AuNCs@PDA-Mn integrated photothermal therapy, chemodynamic therapy, Mn²⁺-mediated STING activation, and ICD induction into a multimodal immunotherapeutic platform (Fig. 5B) [61]. STVN vanadyl nanocomplexes serve as both photothermal agents and ICD inducers through endoplasmic reticulum stress [91]. In bilateral melanoma models, NIR-triggered STVN suppresses primary and distant tumor growth, reduces lung metastasis by 8.3-fold, and prevents tumor recurrence without additional adjuvants.

Radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy

Radiotherapy-based combinations are especially relevant from a clinical perspective because radiotherapy is already widely used in oncology [145, 146]. High-Z element-containing MPNs can act as radiosensitizers while simultaneously modulating the TME. DSPM nanoparticles integrate lanthanide-doped radiosensitizers with Mn²⁺ through metal-phenolic coordination [49]. pH-responsive disassembly triggers radiosensitizer release to sensitize cancer cells to X-rays while Mn²⁺ promotes STING pathway activation, benefiting DC maturation and systemic immune therapeutic performance against metastatic tumors. PPWQ nanoparticles incorporated W⁶⁺, quercetin, and PD-L1-blocking peptides within PEG-polyphenol scaffolds, combining ROS-mediated DNA damage, CD36 inhibition, reduced immune evasion, dendritic-cell activation, memory CTL generation, and macrophage repolarization (Fig. 5C) [48]. Hb@Hf-Ce6 nanoparticles further integrated hemoglobin-mediated oxygen delivery, hafnium-based radiosensitization, and Ce6-mediated radiodynamic therapy, improving hypoxia relief and antitumor immunity in combination with PD-1 blockade [86].

Other therapies combined with immunotherapy

Metabolic, gas, and physical therapies represent emerging but still less clinically mature combination directions [147–149]. PC-IFNα@MPNs reprogrammed glucose metabolism by enhancing IFNα-mediated glucose uptake and glycolysis in CD8⁺ T cells while suppressing tumor-cell glycolysis, thereby improving T-cell infiltration and ICB efficacy in ICB-insensitive tumor models (Fig. 6A) [116]. ZnO@EGCG MPNs inhibited both glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism, inducing starvation-associated ICD and reversing immunosuppression [107]. In gas therapy, FeCO-IR820@Fe(III)TA integrated CO release, photothermal therapy, ferroptosis induction, and CTLA-4 blockade, suppressing breast tumor growth and lung metastasis (Fig. 6B) [104]. Another biomimetic system, CG/MC/U-M, combined CO therapy, cuproptosis, STING activation, and efferocytosis blockade for metastatic adenoid cystic carcinoma [105]. Other physical modalities, including (IRE) and sonodynamic therapy (SDT), have also been combined with MPN-mediated immune activation [150, 151]. For example, CMP nanoparticles combined Mn²⁺-TA MPN coordination with CpG-ODN coating to promote macrophage polarization and synergize with IRE-induced ICD [86], while PALF nanocomplexes embedded lactate oxidase and atovaquone within MPNs to remodel the immunosuppressive TME and enhance sonodynamic therapy [114]. These approaches broaden the application scope of MPNs, although their translational priority will depend on safety, device compatibility, and reproducible manufacturing.

Diagnostic applications

Diagnostic applications represent a clinically important but more enabling axis of MPN-based immunotherapy. Compared with therapeutic platforms alone, image-guided and diagnostic MPN systems can support patient stratification, treatment monitoring, biodistribution analysis, and early response assessment, all of which are essential for personalized cancer immunotherapy. The intrinsic properties of metal ions in MPNs provide opportunities for label-free or minimally modified imaging [152, 153]. Paramagnetic ions such as Mn²⁺, Fe³⁺, and Gd³⁺ can serve as MRI contrast agents [154, 155 ]. For example, Fe³⁺-containing platforms such as DOX-TAF@FN exhibited r₁ relaxivity values up to 6.1 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, enabling high-sensitivity T₁-weighted tumor MRI for treatment guidance (Fig. 7A) [18]. Mn²⁺-based MPNs such as DPGMA combine STING pathway activation with MRI capability, providing a theranostic strategy for monitoring immune activation [60]. Photoacoustic imaging can also be achieved using the strong near-infrared absorption of TA-Fe³⁺ complexes or polydopamine-based MPNs, enabling visualization of tumor sites and nanoparticle distribution [100]. Multimodal imaging platforms, such as BaGdF₅@MPN nanocomposites, further combine CT, MR, and luminescence imaging through europium-phenolic network coatings [23].

Beyond in vivo imaging, MPNs also demonstrate significant potential for ex vivo immunophenotyping and diagnostic applications. SERS-fluorescence dual-modal nanoprobes were engineered by coating plasmonic nanocapsules with MPNs, enabling one-step conjugation of Raman reporters and fluorescently labeled antibodies (Fig. 7B) [22]. These probes allowed multiplex detection of EGFR and CD44 in cultured cells and quantitatively distinguished mixed cell populations with results consistent with flow cytometry. Such diagnostic functions may facilitate patient stratification, treatment monitoring, and evaluation of immunotherapeutic responses.

Translational barriers

Despite the encouraging preclinical results of MPNs in cancer immunotherapy, clinical translation remains challenging. First, batch reproducibility and physicochemical stability hinder reliable large-scale manufacturing. Variations in metal-ligand coordination, particle size distribution, morphology, and polydispersity can compromise batch-to-batch consistency, which is a fundamental requirement for Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP). In addition, the dynamic coordination bonds of MPNs, while advantageous for stimuli-responsive release, may also undergo premature dissociation during blood circulation, resulting in unintended cargo leakage and reduced tumor-specific delivery efficiency.

Second, in vivo pharmacokinetics and biodistribution impose additional translational limitations. As with many nanomedicines, MPNs are prone to sequestration by the reticuloendothelial system, leading to substantial accumulation in the liver and spleen. The long-term retention and slow clearance of metal ions may raise important safety concerns, including manganese-associated neurotoxicity, copper-mediated oxidative damage and hepatotoxicity, iron overload, and vanadium-related organ toxicity. Moreover, the biological effects of metal ions are often highly dose-dependent and may even be biphasic, further complicating therapeutic optimization. For example, Mn²⁺ may function as a pro-inflammatory immune activator at moderate doses but become immunosuppressive or toxic at higher exposure levels. Likewise, excessive Cu²⁺ release may exacerbate oxidative injury in normal tissues. Importantly, no universal maximum tolerable dose (MTD) can currently be defined for Cu- or Mn-containing MPNs in animal models, because tolerability depends strongly on formulation-specific factors such as metal loading, coordination stability, ligand chemistry, particle size, route of administration, dosing frequency, and biodegradation kinetics. Although many published studies report acceptable short-term biosafety within their tested dose ranges, formal dose-escalation studies, rigorous MTD determination, and long-term evaluations of neurotoxicity and hepatotoxicity remain limited. Future translational studies should therefore incorporate standardized dose-escalation protocols, quantitative pharmacokinetic analysis, clearance profiling, and organ-specific toxicological assessment to define safer therapeutic windows.

Third, biological safety and immunotoxicity require careful consideration, especially for structurally or biologically complex MPN-based systems. For example, MPN-coated attenuated bacteria and gene-editing platforms such as CRISPR/dCas9 raise additional concerns related to bacterial shedding, insertional mutagenesis, and unintended immune activation. Similarly, nanoplatforms involving biomimetic membranes, gas therapeutics, or cuproptosis-inducing strategies may trigger off-target immunostimulation, prolonged inflammation, or systemic cytotoxicity if not precisely controlled. In this context, endotoxin contamination, sterility assurance, immunogenicity, and dose-dependent toxicity should be regarded as essential quality and safety parameters during preclinical development.

Fourth, regulatory classification and product comparability remain poorly defined. MPN-based therapeutics do not fit neatly within existing regulatory frameworks. For instance, a bare MPN nanoparticle may be classified as a nanomedicine, whereas an MPN system delivering antibodies or nucleic acids could be regulated as a combination product. Likewise, a live bacterium coated with an MPN may fall under the scope of biological or gene therapy regulations. Such ambiguity complicates the design of preclinical safety packages, the establishment of comparability criteria, and the selection of appropriate regulatory submission pathways. To date, MPN-based cancer immunotherapy has not entered clinical trials, and regulatory guidance specifically tailored to this emerging material class remains unavailable.

Finally, validation in large-animal models remains lacking. Most available preclinical evidence has been generated in murine tumor models, which differ substantially from human patients in immune system composition, tumor microenvironment, metabolism, and treatment response. Therefore, comprehensive evaluations of safety, pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and therapeutic efficacy in large-animal models are needed before first-in-human studies can be realistically considered. Moreover, the therapeutic performance of MPNs should be benchmarked against conventional small-molecule STING agonists, clinically approved immunotherapies, and established nanomedicines in relevant preclinical settings. Tumor heterogeneity represents another translational challenge for MPN-based immunotherapy. Endogenous triggers relevant to MPN activation, including acidity, GSH, ROS, ATP, and hypoxia, vary across tumors and intratumoral regions, and their quantitative sufficiency for reliable clinical activation remains poorly defined. Besides, the immunological effects of MPNs should not be viewed as strictly linear, because antitumor immunity in vivo involves dynamic interactions among tumor, stromal, and immune compartments. These issues warrant more quantitative trigger profiling, systems-level immune analysis, and validation in clinically relevant models. Addressing these translational barriers will facilitate the rational development of safer, more controllable, and regulatory-compliant MPN platforms, thereby accelerating their advancement toward clinical cancer immunotherapy.

Conclusion and perspectives

MPNs have evolved from simple coordination coatings into a versatile class of programmable immunomodulatory biomaterials for cancer therapy. Their significance lies not only in their ability to encapsulate and deliver therapeutic agents, but also in their capacity to function as active therapeutic interfaces that integrate metal-ion-mediated immune activation, polyphenol-derived biological regulation, stimuli-responsive disassembly, multimodal co-delivery, and imaging-enabled monitoring. These properties enable MPNs to address several major limitations of current cancer immunotherapy, including inefficient intracellular delivery of biologics, insufficient tumor immunogenicity, immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments, systemic toxicity of immunostimulatory agents, and the lack of real-time treatment monitoring. Therefore, MPNs are emerging as coordination-driven immunotherapeutic platforms capable of linking material chemistry with tumor immunology. A major trajectory will be the rational design of MPNs according to specific immunological barriers, such as STING insufficiency, defective antigen presentation, T-cell exclusion, hypoxia-driven immune resistance, or poor intracellular access to therapeutic proteins and antibodies. In this context, the most promising near-term translational opportunities may arise from relatively simple, biodegradable, and compositionally well-defined MPNs that enhance approved or clinically validated treatments, including immune checkpoint blockade, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, cancer vaccines, and protein-based therapeutics. In the longer term, MPN-based systems could be tailored to patient-specific tumor immune phenotypes, enabling biomarker-guided selection of metal ions, phenolic ligands, payload combinations, and release triggers.

Nevertheless, this vision will require substantial progress in several key areas. First, the structure-activity relationships governing MPN-immune interactions should be clarified, such as how metal species, ligand chemistry, coordination stability, particle size, surface charge, and degradation kinetics influence biodistribution, innate immune activation, antigen presentation, and adaptive immune responses. Second, translationally relevant safety evaluation should extend beyond short-term tumor inhibition studies to include long-term metal metabolism, immune-related adverse events, hemocompatibility, organ accumulation, reproductive and developmental toxicity, and large-animal validation where appropriate. Third, manufacturing strategies should move toward scalable, GMP-compatible processes with strict control of composition, batch-to-batch reproducibility, endotoxin levels, residual reactants, colloidal stability, and storage conditions. Finally, future studies should prioritize clinically meaningful models and endpoints, including orthotopic and metastatic tumors, immune checkpoint blockade-resistant models, humanized immune systems, recurrence prevention, survival benefit, and predictive biomarkers of response.

Overall, MPNs provide a chemically flexible and biologically active platform for reshaping cancer immunotherapy. Their future impact will depend on moving from increasingly complex multifunctional designs toward rationally simplified, mechanistically validated, and clinically alignable systems. If these challenges are addressed, MPNs have the potential to become an important enabling technology for cancer immunotherapy, particularly as adjuvant platforms that improve the precision, potency, and monitorability of existing therapeutic regimens.
