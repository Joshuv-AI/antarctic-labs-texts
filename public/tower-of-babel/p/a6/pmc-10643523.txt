Effect of tissue viscoelasticity and adjacent phase-changed microbubbles on vaporization process and direct growth threshold of nanodroplet in an ultrasonic field
Authors: Kangyi Feng, Xinyue Li, Anqi Huang, Mingxi Wan, Yujin Zong
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the behavior of nanodroplets converted into microbubbles with applied ultrasound is an important problem in tumor therapeutical and diagnostic applications. In this study, a comprehensive model is proposed to investigate the vaporization process and the direct growth threshold of the nanodroplet by following the vapor bubble growth, especially attention devoted to the effect of tissue viscoelasticity and adjacent phase-changed microbubbles (PCMBs). It is shown that the ultrasonic energy must be sufficiently strong to counterbalance the natural condensation of the vapor bubble and the tissue stiffness-inhibitory effect. The softer tissue with a lower shear modulus favors the vaporization process, and the nanodroplet has a lower direct growth threshold in the softer tissue. Moreover, the adjacent PCMBs show a suppression effect on the vaporization process due to the negative value of the secondary Bjerknes force, implying an attractive force, preventing the nanodroplet from escaping from the constraint of the adjacent PCMBs. However, according to the linear scattering theory, the attractive force signifies that the constraint is weak, causing the direct growth threshold to increase in the range of 0.09–0.24 MPa. The weak increase in threshold demonstrates that the direct growth threshold is relatively unaffected by the adjacent PCMBs. The prediction results of our model are in good agreement with the experiment results obtained by the echo enhancement method, in which the threshold is relatively independent of the intermediate concentration. The findings presented here provide physical insight that will be further helpful in understanding the complex behavior of the nanodroplet responses to ultrasound in practical medical applications.Keywords: Nanodroplet, Phase-changed microbubble, Vaporization process, Threshold, Viscoelastic tissue

Introduction

Perfluorocarbon (PFC) nanodroplets are receiving a growing interest in tumor diagnostic and therapeutic ultrasound applications [1], [2], [3], [4]. Compared to the classic gas-filled microbubbles restricted in the vasculature, they are submicron-sized and have longer circulation time, which allows them to extravasate from the leaky tumor vasculature and accumulate in tumor tissue by the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect [5], [6]. The inherent volatility of the liquid-state nanodroplets provides a favorable thermodynamic driving force for vaporization [7], [8]. Adequate acoustic energy can effectively reduce the local pressure below the saturation vapor pressure of the nanodroplets, and thus, the liquid-state nanodroplets can vaporize into gaseous microbubbles. However, it remains a pressing need to understand the response of nanodroplets to ultrasound in viscoelastic tissue, especially the vaporization process and the corresponding required ultrasound energy.

The boiling point of perfluoropentane (PFP, C5F12) is 29 °C under ambient pressure (1 atm), so the PFP nanodroplets with a polydisperse size distribution are in a superheated state at physiological temperature (37℃) [9]. Notably, larger nanodroplets exhibit a smaller Laplace pressure, rendering them more prone to spontaneously vaporize into microbubbles in a superheated state [10] or after a round of exposure to ultrasound [11], which may result in the coexistence of nanodroplets and phase-changed microbubbles (PCMBs). It is well known that the oscillating microbubbles generate scattering pressure in the ultrasound field, which can affect the pressure on the surface of the adjacent microbubble, and further influence its motion behavior [12], [13], for example, attraction, and repulsion, either suppressing or enlarging the maximum expansion ratios [14], [15], [16]. Importantly, the pressure at the nanodroplet’s surface also plays a crucial role in governing the motion equations of nanodroplets under a superheated state. Therefore, when the nanodroplets and PCMBs coexist in tissue, it needs to be further confirmed whether the scattering pressure generated from the adjacent PCMBs affects the nanodroplet’s behavior in ultrasound.

The acoustic vaporization process of micro-sized droplets dispersed in water has been observed by ultra-high-speed microscopy [17], [18], [19], [20]. These direct observations suggested that once fulfilled the nucleation conditions, the negative pressure of ultrasound instigated the emergence of one or potentially multiple vapor bubble nuclei at the center of the droplets [21]. Subsequently, these nascent vapor bubbles underwent one of two distinct fates: either collapsing until their volume dissipates or successfully vaporizing into microbubbles, and the ultimate fate of the vapor bubble largely depended on various factors, including acoustic parameters [22], the geometric properties of droplets [23], and the characteristics of the surrounding medium [24]. It can be seen that these experimental observations allow us to obtain a good understanding of the transient process of vaporization, and provide a reliable basis for theoretical modeling and simulation.

Some theoretical studies have extensively investigated the vaporization process of micron-sized droplets by modeling a concentric three-phase physical model (vapor bubble-PFC-water) [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]. They assumed that a vapor bubble had already nucleated at the center of an individual droplet and the investigation of the vaporization process as the growth of the vapor bubble, considered the heat transfer at the interface between the vapor bubble and the droplet. Doinikov et al. assumed a non-constant evaporation rate and finite droplet size, allowing for an accurate description of the vaporization [27]. Based upon the model of Doinikov et al., Lacour et al. further categorized the ultimate fates of the vapor bubble into three distinct regimes: irreversible collapse, complete vaporization, and intermediate behaviors [30]. They employed a pressure-frequency phase diagram to predict the required energy for vaporization under different frequencies and showed that the required energy does not vary monotonically with the frequency. Cho and Son et al. proposed similar research to investigate the regimes and further explain the complexity of this non-monotonical variation due to the vapor bubble radius with multiple rebounds [31]. Recently, Park et al. expanded the theoretical studies to the submicron-sized droplets and found that the ultimate fates of nanodroplets were similar to the three regimes studied in micron-sized droplets, suggesting the concentric three-phase model is available to nanodroplets as well [32].

The limited optical resolution of microscopy and the inherent opacity of tissue significantly obstacles to directly observing the vaporization process of nanodroplets in tissues. Therefore, a theoretical physical model would be useful to guide the investigation of this difficult-to-observe vaporization process. By now most theoretical research focuses on the vaporization process of an individual micro-sized in water with ultrasound excitation for imaging or therapeutic application within blood vessels. For the extravascular applications of nanodroplets, the influence of surrounding tissues and possible adjacent PCMBs should be considered additionally in modeling the vaporization process of the nanodroplet.

In this context, we develop a physical model to understand the vaporization process of a PFP nanodroplet in viscoelastic tissues, taking the effect of the adjacent PCMBs into account. The viscoelasticity tissue interpreted by the Kelvin-Voigt model and the scattering pressures generated from the adjacent PCMBs is considered to elucidate the pressure at the nanodroplet’s outer surface. The vaporization process mainly focuses on the growth of the centered vapor bubble (i.e., the time-dependent changes in the vapor bubble radius). Numerical simulations illustrate various ultimate fates of the vapor bubble response to different acoustical excitation parameters, the shear modulus of tissue, and with or without adjacent PCMBs. Corresponding to the different physical characteristics of the ultimate fates of the vapor bubble, the required minimum energy in the direct growth is defined as the direct growth threshold, and the dependence of this threshold on tissue viscoelasticity and adjacent PCMBs is also investigated.

For simplicity, some common assumptions are used: (i) the radial oscillation of the vapor bubble and the nanodroplet remained spherical symmetry; (ii) the pressure inside the vapor bubble is spatially uniform; (iii) the heat flux due to diffusion in the vapor bubble is neglected by assuming a uniform temperature; the reasonability of this assumption can be confirmed through the ratio (order of magnitude) 10−2 of thermal conductivity and the diffusion coefficient of the liquid and gas phases [20]. Additionally, during the vaporization process, the nanodroplet and the viscoelastic tissue are considered incompressible mediums, and thus, the density of the nanodroplet ρ2 and the density of the tissue ρ3 are constant. This is a reasonable assumption because the liquid velocity caused by the radial oscillation of the nanodroplet and tissue is smaller than their sound speed.

Model validation

To determine the appropriate interpolation number and time step and ensure the convergence of Eq. (20), we consider the time-dependent changes in vapor bubble radius (i.e., R1-t curves) under different time and space resolutions, as shown in Fig. 2. The results with interpolation numbers NT of 15, 18, and 20, and time steps △tmax = 0.5 ns 0.05 ns, and respectively, correspond to different space and time resolutions. The gray dotted line represents the change in the driving pressure with time. The calculations are continued until the mass of the vapor bubble reaches 0.0001, indicating the numerical precision of the calculation. It can be observed that the R1-t curves show without significant difference for different time and space resolutions meaning the convergence of Eq. (20). Based on these results, we select the interpolation number NT = 20 and the time step △tmax = 0.05 ns in our calculation, as they provide sufficient accuracy and convergence. It is worth noting that an anomalous phenomenon is observed at t < 0.0005 μs, the R1-t curves do not increase with increasing negative pressure, as indicated by the lower left red box in Fig. 2, which has also been reported in a previous study [30]. This can be attributed to the temperature discontinuity at the interface between the vapor bubble and the nanodroplet. At the initial time t = 0, the temperature inside the vapor bubble T1 calculated by the Clausius–Clapeyron equation (Eq. (12)) is 303 K, which is lower than the ambient temperature T1 of 310 K (37℃). Therefore, the driving pressure first drives the temperature inside the vapor bubble to increase and ensure its continuity, which leads to the condensation-heat transfer at the bubble-nanodroplet interface and the vapor bubble radius decreases over a short period driven by negative pressure.

The subplots in the top right show the adjacent PCMBs radius with different driving pressures during the vaporization process.

When the driving pressure amplitude increases at a given frequency, three ultimate fates of the vapor bubble can be obtained, as shown in Fig. 3. In Fig. 3(a), where Pa = 1 MPa, the insufficient acoustic energy cannot counterbalance the natural condensation of the vapor bubble in ND or ND-PCMBs, leading to irreversible condensation until its volume dissipates, this fate is defined as regime 1, which has been observed in the experiment research [53]. The irreversible condensation of the vapor bubble occurred within t < 0.015 μs, the driving pressure is minimal within such a short period, and the adjacent PCMBs oscillation has a smaller amplitude, as seen in the subplot of Fig. 3(a). The scattering pressure generated from the adjacent PCMBs is positively correlated with their oscillation amplitude, and hence, the smaller scattering pressures have a relatively small effect on the nanodroplet. Consequently, the R1-t curves exhibited the same trend in ND and ND-PCMBs.

In addition, as shown in Fig. 3 (b) and (c), where Pa = 2.321 or 3 MPa, the increased acoustic energy allows the vapor bubble to complete growth in ND (red lines), while in ND-PCMBs (black dotted line), the vapor bubble radius firstly increases during the negative pressure phase of the acoustic wave and subsequently shrank in the following positive pressure phase. This phenomenon suggests that the adjacent PCMBs show a slight suppression effect on the vapor bubble growth. It can be seen that the increased acoustic energy enables the adjacent PCMBs to undergo large amplitude oscillation, as seen in the subplot in Fig. 3 (b) and (c), in which the amplitude increases approximately two times, which generates a relatively large scattering pressure acting on the surface of the nanodroplets, shows a suppression on the vapor bubble growth. Moreover, this shrinkage in ND-PCMBs leads to a significant increase in the pressure inside the vapor bubble [32]. Insufficient acoustic energy (where Pa = 2.321 MPa) cannot balance the increasing trend of the pressure inside the vapor bubble during the positive pressure phase and that results in condensation. In contrast, the higher energy (where Pa = 3 MPa) can resist this increasing trend and ensure the vapor bubble grows into a microbubble. These behaviors of the vapor bubble similar to Fig. 3 (b) and (c) in ND-PCMBs, characterized by a rebound to condensation/growth, are defined as regime 2. It is difficult to detect experimentally but may have been observed at least once in the previous research [17], where the radius of a single droplet increased by a maximum, then shrinks to the lowest observable size before finally growing.

In Fig. 3 (d), Pa = 3.45 MPa, the vapor bubble experiences direct growth in ND and ND-PCMBs within a very short time due to the sufficiently high driving pressure; this behavior of direct growth is defined as regime 3. Previous experimental research has observed this similar behavior [54]. It is worth noting that the sufficiently high driving pressure makes the adjacent PCMBs occur large amplitude oscillation in such a short time in the subplot of Fig. 3 (d), which produces a significant scattering pressure on the nanodroplet interface. However, the R1-t curves in Fig. 3 (d) do not undergo the similar behaviors in Fig. (b) and (c) inhibited the vapor bubble direct growth, just slightly requiring a little longer time for the vapor bubble growth in ND-PCMBs. It is evident that while the adjacent PCMBs show a slight suppression effect on the vapor bubble growth, the driving pressure amplitude more significantly affects the vapor bubble growth. Therefore, according to the different ultimate fates of the vapor bubble response to ultrasound, achieving the vapor bubble growth requires sufficiently high acoustic energy to resist the natural condensation of the vapor bubble and the compression of positive acoustic pressure.

According to the theoretical modeling in Section 2, the motion equation governing the vaporization process of the nanodroplet contains four physical variables at thermodynamic equilibrium: normalized pressure P1/p0 and temperature T1 inside the vapor bubble, normalized evaporation rate at the bubble-nanodroplet interface ξ, and normalized vapor bubble mass flux ζ. The change of these variables over time is presented in Fig. 4 (a)–(d), which can further understand the physical nature of the vaporization process. It can be observed that at t = 0 μs, the inherent pressure inside the vapor bubble P10 is higher than the ambient sound pressure p0 (P10/p0 = 1.4), indicating a tendency towards natural condensation of the vapor bubble.

Specifically, as shown in the red line where Pa = 1 MPa in Fig. 4, corresponding to regime 1 in ND-PCMBs, the pressure inside the vapor bubble (P1) remains higher than the ambient sound pressure (p0) throughout the first negative pressure period (at 0 μs < t < 0.084 μs). The driving pressure could not promote the reduction of P1 and counteract the natural condensation. Consequently, the temperature inside the vapor bubble T1 increases above the ambient temperature of 310 K, producing a negative normalized evaporation rate ξ/ωRbρ2<0, and condensing heat transfer occurring at the vapor bubble-nanodroplet interface, i.e., the heat transfer from the vapor bubble to the nanodroplet. This leads to a decrease in the normalized vapor bubble mass flux ζ to 0, the vapor bubble happens a rapid and irreversible collapse without any growth.

The blue and black lines where Pa = 2.321 MPa and 3 MPa present in Fig. 4, correspond to regime 2 in ND-PCMBs. In both cases, P1 and T1 decrease during the first negative pressure phase, which results in the evaporation heat transfer (ξ>0) from the nanodroplet to the vaper bubble and an increase in ζ, therefore, the vapor bubble begins to grow. When Pa = 2.321 MPa, the subsequent positive pressure phase leads to a substantial and continuous increase in P1 and T1, causing the condensing heat transfer, and the vapor bubble radius decreases. Inefficient pressure that cannot resist the compression of positive acoustic pressure, the vapor bubble mass decreased to 0 and the vapor bubble rebounded to condensation. In contrast, when Pa increased to 3 MPa, sufficient pressure ensured that the vapor bubble mass did not decrease to 0, and the vapor bubble radius continued to grow until the vapor bubble completed growth in the subsequent negative pressure phase. Moreover, it is worth noting that T1 exceeds the critical temperature calculated by Eq. (12) Tc = 430 K and P1 occurs at a maximum value, and inducing the vapor bubble is in a subcritical state.

When Pa = 3.45 MPa, corresponding to regime 3 in ND-PCMBs, the pressure directly decreases P1, causing the evaporation heat transfer, and the vapor bubble experiences direct growth. It is important to emphasize that although the possibility presents for the vapor bubble to complete growth in regime 2, it is accompanied by a rapid rebound that leads to extreme dynamics such as high temperature and pressure. One can know that the direct growth may be of interest for medical applications due to the rebound may induce tissue damage in regime 2. Therefore, the required minimum acoustic pressure in the direct growth of the vapor bubble in regime 3 is defined as the direct growth threshold Pth.

Effects of the tissue viscoelasticity

As depicted in Fig. 5, the solid line R1(t) represents the vapor bubble radius that is significantly influenced by the shear modulus (G) of the viscoelastic tissue, and the gray dotted line represents the sinusoidal driving pressure (Pa = 2.8 MPa). It can be seen that the shear modulus notably inhibits the vapor bubble growth. Specifically, for the limit case where the shear modulus is removed for the viscoelastic tissue, i.e., corresponding to water or blood (G = 0 MPa), sufficient pressure prompts the vapor bubble to direct growth in the first negative pressure period. In contrast, under the same ultrasound field, when the shear modulus increases G = 0.3 MPa and G = 0.5 MPa, corresponding to the greater stiffness of the tissue, the vapor bubble experiences rebound and then growth and irreversible condensation, respectively. Consequently, it can be seen that, after considering the effect of the viscoelastic tissue, competition emerged between negative pressure-driven growth promotion and tissue stiffness-induced inhibitory effects. When the growth effect of the negative pressure-driven is greater than the inhibitory effect of the tissue stiffness, the vapor bubble can complete its growth, and the opposite condition occurs condensation. In addition, the shear modulus primarily affects the vapor bubble growth during the first negative pressure period, while the final evaporation rate remains nearly unaffected indicated by the similar slopes of the two curves during the final vaporization phase.

The time-dependent changes in the vapor bubble radius in different viscoelasticity tissues in ND-PCMBs at Pa = 2.8 MPa.

The direct growth threshold Pth of the vapor bubble at each driving frequency i.e., f - Pth curves, with the different shear modulus (G = 0, 0.3, and 0.5 MPa), is presented in Fig. 6. Firstly, it can be seen that when the shear modulus from 0 MPa increases to 0.5 MPa, and the threshold significantly increases in the calculated frequency range. This can be attributed to the stiffness of the tissue inhibits the direct growth of the vapor bubble and thus requires more energy to grow. These findings are consistent with the experimental results of Williams et al. on the threshold of DDFP in polyacrylamide gel phantoms [24].

The direct growth threshold as a function of frequency with different shear modulus in ND-PCMBs.

In Fig. 6, all the f - Pth curves exhibit a turning point, indicating the threshold variation with frequency is non-monotonic. Defining the frequency corresponding to the minimum direct growth threshold in the curve, i.e., the frequency at the turning point, as the optimal frequency f∗, and its corresponding optimum direct growth threshold Pth∗ can be obtained. The direct growth threshold is inversely proportional to frequency below the optimal frequency (the low-frequency regions f<f∗). When the frequency is higher than the optimal (the high-frequency regions f>f∗), the threshold increases with frequency. This non-monotonicity dependence of the threshold to frequency has been observed in the previous study by Lacour et al. for the growth threshold of a single droplet in water [25]. Furthermore, when the shear modulus increases, the optimal frequency shifts toward higher frequencies, which might be attributed to the inhibitory effect of viscoelastic tissue increasing the resonance frequency of the physical model.

It is obvious to see that in the low-frequency region, the shear modulus causes the threshold susceptible to the driving frequency and the surrounding medium to increase to be more pronounced. Lower frequency ultrasound driving provides a longer window for the vapor bubble growth via rectified heat transfer, making a more pronounced competition appears between the negative pressure promotion and the tissue stiffness-inhibitory for the vapor bubble growth, resulting in a more pronounced increase in the threshold. Moreover, it can be seen that in the f - Pth curve representing G = 0 MPa, the threshold is not equal to zero, indicating the threshold is influenced by the finite size of the nanodroplet. Overall, compared to previous studies focusing on the vaporization process of the droplet in water, these results suggest that tissue viscoelasticity play a significant role in influencing the vaporization process of nanodroplet, which is necessary for understanding their potential applications in medical treatments.

Effects of the distance between nanodroplet and adjacent PCMBs

The droplet concentration plays an important role in the required acoustic energy for vaporization, which has been studied in previous research [19], [55]. In our simulation, the concentration is approximated as the distance between the nanodroplet and the adjacent PCMBs, d = 29 μm, 60 μm, and 130 μm correspond to concentrations of 107, 106, and 105 nanodroplets/ml, respectively. These distances are chosen to be more than 18 times the combined initial radii of the nanodroplet and the adjacent PCMBs, ensuring the distance remains greater than the combined radii at any instant, which could prevent the non-spherical motion of the nanodroplet and the adjacent PCMBs in ND-PCMBs. Fig. 7(a) illustrates the vapor bubble radius with different rebound behaviors at three distances in the negative pressure period. When the distance decreases, the oscillation amplitude of adjacent PCMBs within the first negative pressure phase of the acoustic wave decreases, as shown in Fig. 7 (b), thus, the first local maximum radius of the vapor bubble is suppressed and slightly requires more time to growth in Fig. 7(a), while the vapor bubble shows the same behavior (rebound to growth) in these three distances due to the approximate oscillation amplitude of adjacent PCMBs.

(a) The time-dependent changes in the vapor bubble radius, (b) the time-dependent changes in the adjacent PCMBs radius during the vaporization process, and (c) the secondary Bjerknes force, under different distances d at Pa = 2.8 MPa in ND-PCMBs.

Due to the adjacent PCMBs’ second acoustic radiation, the nanodroplet is subjected to a secondary Bjerknes force that affects its relative motion. According to the linear scatter theory, the nanodroplet is repelled by the adjacent PCMBs and their behavior out-of-phase when Fb→n > 0 and is attracted to adjacent PCMBs and their behavior in phase when Fb→n < 0. Comparing the absolute value of the secondary Bjerknes force varies with the distance as presented in Fig. 7(c). When the distance is set as 29 μm, Fb→n attaines a maximum. If the distance increased to 60 μm and 130 μm, Fb→n decreases, until Fb→n attains a minimum zero when the distance tend towards ∞. Furthermore, the value of the secondary Bjerknes force is consistently negative in our calculation (Fb→n < 0), meaning that not only the behavior of the vapor bubble and the PCMBs are in phase, but also the vapor bubble in the center of the PCMBs is less likely to separate from the adjacent PCMBs, resulting in more difficult to break free from the constraint of the PCMBs. Therefore, the constraint effect of the adjacent PCMBs on the first local maximum radius can be described by the secondary Bjerknes force, and this constraint effect is strengthened by decreasing distance.

The numerical results of Fig. 8 present the effect of the three cases d = 29 μm, 60 μm, and 130 μm on the direct growth threshold in ND-PCMBs. These f - Pth curves still exhibit the same low-frequency and high-frequency variations as previous curves in Fig. 6: a decrease below the optimal frequency f∗, which has been observed in many experimental studies for microdroplets and nanodroplets [56], [57], [58], and follows by an increasing one consisted with reported results [59], [60]. In the low-frequency, it is evident that the threshold is relatively independent of the distance, which can be described as the vapor bubble growth mainly initiated by rectified heat transfer [40]. In other words, if the nanodroplet is under the same medium, the threshold depends mostly on the value of the initial vapor bubble radius R10, the larger surface of the vapor bubble allows for more heat transfer to occur and results in a lower threshold. The vapor bubble growth in the same medium mainly reflects a competition between the time needed a tendency towards natural condensation on one side, and on the other side the time of acoustical expansion. Consequently, the distance affects the threshold only at very high frequencies beyond the optimum point.

The effect of the distance between the nanodroplet and the adjacent PCMBs on the direct growth threshold in ND-PCMBs.

The feature appearing here in the high frequency, the threshold slightly increases as the distance increases due to the scattering pressure generated from the adjacent PCMBs. Some experimental studies observed that increasing droplet concentration (corresponds to decreased distance) can be favorable for vaporization [[10], [61], which can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, an increase in droplet concentration results in an increase in the total volume of liquid droplets, which leads to a higher probability of nucleation occurring within the total droplet volume [53]. Additionally, due to the polydisperse size distribution of the droplets, a higher concentration may also increase the probability of the presence of larger droplets due to droplet fusion in the region of excitation [20], [53]]. These factors might result a lower associated threshold for vaporization. It is important to note that the model used in the current work does not consider the possibility of droplet fusion or nucleation. This is an aspect that can be addressed in future work to further enhance the understanding of the vaporization process. By incorporating such factors, it would be possible to investigate the effects of droplet fusion and nucleation on the vaporization threshold and overall dynamics of the system.

To further understand the direct growth threshold in the high-frequency (when f>f∗) under these three distances d = 29 μm, 60 μm, and 130 μm, the driving frequency is set as 6 MHz and its corresponding threshold is presented in Fig. 9. The effect of the radius of the adjacent PCMBs also be investigated, we set different radii of the adjacent PCMBs: Rb0 = 0.75, 1, 1.5 μm, corresponding approximately to initial nanodroplet radii of 150, 200, and 300 nm, i.e., the PFP nanodroplet to expand approximately 5 to 6 times after vaporization. Observing the results under the fixed frequency, noticed that the direct growth threshold experiences a slight increase as Rb0 and distance d are increased. For driving frequencies higher than the optimal frequency, the scattering pressure associated with the distance and radius of the adjacent PCMBs inhibits the vapor bubble growth, and this inhibitory effect becomes prominent when Rb0 increases and d decreases. However, it is worth noting that the threshold only slightly increases in the range of 0.09–0.24 MPa at various Rb0 values for different distances. This weak increase of the threshold can be considered that the threshold is approximately independent of the distance and the radius of the PCMBs. This result is consistent with previous experimental observation studies about the intermediate droplet concentrations at a given frequency (107, 106, and 105 droplets/ml corresponding to our distance d = 29 μm, 60 μm, and 130 μm, respectively) [62]. A similar conclusion was also obtained about the phase-changed threshold of a DDFP nanoemulsion in tissue-mimicking phantoms remained relatively independent of the concentration within the range of 0.15 %-0.4 % [55]. Indeed, various studies have consistently shows that the weak nonlinear coupling always results in an attractive secondary Bjerknes force (Fb→n < 0) between the nanodroplet and the adjacent PCMBs [12], [48], [63]. This is in agreement with the linear Bjerknes theory as both nanodroplet and the adjacent PCMBs are weakly driven below their resonance frequencies. Consequently, the threshold remains independent of the distance and the radius of the adjacent PCMBs, and the effect of the PCMBs on the threshold is relatively weak.

Conclusion

A comprehensive physical model is developed to describe the vaporization process and the direct growth threshold of the nanodroplet by following the growth of a vapor bubble, already nucleated within a nanodroplet. A particular focus is on the effect of tissue viscoelasticity and adjacent PCMBs to dig deeper into the potential of nanodroplets in tumor medical applications. The growth of the vapor bubble is theoretically and numerically investigated and classified into three regimes: regime 1 (irreversible condensation), regime 2 (rebound to condensation/growth), and regime 3 (direct growth). And the required acoustic energy in regime 3 is defined as the direct growth threshold. Our numerical analysis finds that the vapor bubble growth and the direct growth threshold are strongly dependent on the tissue viscoelasticity, with a high value of the shear modulus, being the most non-favorable for growth and needing more energy. In the vapor bubble evolution, competition emerged among the negative pressure-promotion, the tissue stiffness-inhibitory, and the natural condensation of the vapor bubble, the negative pressure must be sufficiently strong to counterbalance the stiffness-inhibitory effect and natural condensation.

By analyzing the influence of the adjacent PCMBs, which shows slight suppression on the vapor bubble growth and the direct growth threshold. When the distance increases and the radius of the adjacent PCMBs decreases, the suppression effect becomes weaker. The influence intensity can be measured by the magnitude of the secondary Bjerknes force, which is closely related to their separation distance and the radius of the adjacent PCMBs. The value of the secondary Bjerknes force remains negative calculated in our numerical results indicating the constraint effect of the adjacent PCMBs. Notably, according to the linear scatter theory, the weak nonlinear coupling always results in a negative secondary Bjerknes force. Therefore, the distance and the radius of the adjacent PCMBs are relatively independent of the vapor bubble growth and the direct growth threshold, and correspond to experimental results in tissue-mimicking phantoms, highlighting the validity of our method.

In this paper, only the effects of tissue viscoelasticity and adjacent PCMBs on the vaporization process of nanodroplets are considered. It is observed that the nucleation conditions and location had significant effects, and its characteristics are very complicated. Therefore, more detailed investigations are necessary in the future to elucidate the detailed mechanism of the vaporization process.
