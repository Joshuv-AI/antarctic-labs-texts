Reasons for encounter in video contacts at a Danish out-of-hours primary care service: a questionnaire study
Authors: Mette Amalie Nebsbjerg, Katrine Bjørnshave Bomholt, Claus Høstrup Vestergaard, Morten Bondo Christensen, Linda Huibers
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate reasons for encounter in telephone triage contacts to an out-of-hours primary care service for which general practitioners (GPs) use video consultations (video contact), overall and stratified for patient age and time of day.DesignA cross-sectional questionnaire study among GPs doing telephone triage in an out-of-hours primary care service. The questionnaire was integrated into the electronic patient registration system, popping up after every third video contact. This setup automatically linked patient register data, including age and sex, with the questionnaire data.SettingThe study was conducted from 5 September 2022 to 21 December 2022 at the out-of-hours primary care service in the Central Denmark Region.Participants649 volunteer GPs who answered 2452 questionnaires.Main outcome measuresReasons for encounter in video contacts registered with codes from International Classification of Primary Care (ICPC) coding system, V.2 (ICPC-2).ResultsGPs doing telephone triage in out-of-hours primary care used video contacts for a broad range of ICPC-2 codes within few ICPC-2 chapters. 83% of all reasons for encounter were due to symptoms in chapters S ‘Skin’ (34.5%), R ‘Respiratory’ (21.8%), A ‘General and unspecified’ (14.7%) and L ‘Musculoskeletal’ (12.0%) in the ICPC-2 classification system. Video contacts concerning skin and musculoskeletal symptoms were more frequent among older children and adults compared with young children. Respiratory symptoms and general and unspecified symptoms dominated by fever were more frequent among video contacts for young children compared with older children and adults.ConclusionOur study suggests a focused use of video contacts in an out-of-hours primary care setting; the majority of registered ICPC-2 codes were within few ICPC-2 chapters.

Background

Video use was widely enabled in general practice, both in and outside office hours, but remains sparsely used in many countries.17 One of the reasons is general practitioners’ (GPs) uncertainty about the intended target population.6812 Two surveys from daytime general practice in Norway reported that GPs found video consultations most suitable for contacts concerning mental health issues, administrative purposes and follow-up consultations.8 10 In line with this, an interview study among primary care doctors from the USA found video consultations suitable for chronic disease management, mental health issues and medication issues (eg, prescriptions and renewals).9 However, these findings cannot be generalised to an out-of-hours primary care (OOH-PC) context, as the reasons for encounter differs between OOH-PC contacts and contacts to daytime general practice,1316 and as video has tended to be used as a triage tool in OOH-PC, rather than as a consultation tool.7 12

Studies have investigated reasons for encounter in OOH-PC contacts.1314 1720 A Danish study from 2010 found that the most frequent reasons for encounter in telephone triage contacts to OOH-PC considered fever, upper respiratory tract infections and skin conditions.13 Similarly, a study from 2011 investigating reasons for encounter in eight European OOH-PC services showed that the most frequent reasons for encounter were general and unspecified symptoms, and respiratory symptoms.14 In OOH-PC contacts, reasons for encounter differ across age groups, with a high prevalence of fever and symptoms related to respiratory tract infections for contacts concerning young children and skin, musculoskeletal and abdominal symptoms for contacts concerning adults.14 17 18 Moreover, time of contact to OOH-PC is also expected to vary across patient age groups, with proportionally more adult patients calling during the night.

Thus, reasons for encounter in OOH-PC are well-known, but knowledge about reasons for encounter in OOH-PC telephone triage contacts with use of video (video contact) is lacking. In Denmark, video contacts were hastily implemented at the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, without clear guidelines for its use. Therefore, GPs decide themselves whether the telephone triage contact is suitable for video use, based on their clinical experience. More insight into the diagnostic scope of video contacts could contribute to development of guidelines, as well as provide foundation for further research and development of interventions and policies in this field. Thus, the aim of this study was to investigate reasons for encounter in video contacts at OOH-PC, overall as well as stratified for patient age and time of day.

Methods

Design

We conducted a cross-sectional questionnaire study among GPs doing telephone triage shifts in the OOH-PC service in Central Denmark Region from 5 September 2022 to 21 December 2022.

Setting

In Denmark, most healthcare is public and free of charge for all citizens. The healthcare system is centrally regulated, but most services are provided by the local governments of the five Danish regions. OOH-PC services are open on weekdays from 16:00 to 08:00 hours, and 24 hours during weekends and holidays. GPs and GP trainees (hereafter referred to as GPs) are obligated to cover shifts in their regional OOH-PC service. GPs perform telephone triage and decide on the triage outcome (self-care advice, referral to face-to-face consultation—such as a clinic consultation or home visit—or referral to hospital). When the GP answers a telephone triage contact, they gather information about the reason for encounter. Based on this information and their clinical experience, the GP assesses whether the telephone triage contact is suitable for video use. If so, a video link is sent via text message and, when activated by the patient, a one-way video connection is established allowing the GP to see the patient, but not vice versa. We refer to telephone triage contacts as being either a video contact or a telephone contact, depending on whether or not video was used during triage. GPs are paid fee-for-service, using remuneration codes for telephone contacts (€10) or video contacts (€31).

Data collection

To answer the question about reasons for encounter, the International Classification of Primary Care coding system, V.2 (ICPC-2)21 was built into a pop-up questionnaire integrated into the electronic patient registration system of the regional OOH-PC service. The ICPC-2 coding system consists of 18 chapters; 1 concerning procedure and 17 concerning symptoms and diagnostics. Whereas daytime GPs coded reasons for encounter using ICPC-2 codes, GPs do not use codes at the OOH-PC services. Consequently, these data are not routinely registered and has to be collected prospectively. The pop-up questionnaire data could automatically be linked to patients’ registration data from the OOH-PC contact and their personal identification number. This personal identification number provides data on patients’ age and sex. The questionnaire was set to emerge after every third video contact. The questionnaire also consisted of six more questions (online supplemental appendix 1). The results of these questions are to be presented in a separate article.

The questionnaire was integrated into the electronic patient registration system, requiring GPs to complete it between patient telephone triage calls. To minimise disruption to the GPs’ workflow and reduce patient waiting times, we decided to have the questionnaire appear only after every third video consultation. To avoid the sample being dominated by a few very active GPs, we allowed each individual GP to participate a maximum of five telephone triage shifts.

Data handling and analyses

Patients’ age and sex were calculated using the patients’ personal identification number. We excluded 28 video contacts (1.1%) with a missing personal identification number from analysis. Questionnaires lacking data on reason for encounter were also excluded from analysis (n=83; 3.3%). For stratification, patients’ age was categorised in three groups: 0–4 years, 5–17 years and ≥18 years. This categorisation was chosen because of the low number of video contacts concerning elderly patients (table 1). Time of day was categorised in two groups: 08:00–23:00 hours and 23:00–08:00 hours.

Results

Study population

During the study period, 649 unique GPs had at least one telephone triage shift. These GPs were invited 2597 times when logging into the electronic patient registration system at the beginning of a telephone triage shift. Of these, 1330 invitations were accepted (participation rate: 51.2%), but 430 invitations (32.2%) did not produce a pop-up questionnaire as the GP conducted fewer than three video contacts during their shift. The remaining 900 accepted invitations resulted in 2452 completed questionnaires with an ICPC-2 code (online supplemental appendix 3, flowchart). Patients receiving a video contact that ended with a pop-up questionnaire were comparable for sex and age with those that ended without a pop-up questionnaire (table 1).

Top 20 most used ICPC-2 codes for video contacts

The 20 most frequently used ICPC-2 codes accounted for 56.3% of all registered ICPC-2 codes; more for contacts with young children (0–4 years, 66.0%) than for older patients (about 50%) (table 2). ICPC-2 codes S18 ‘laceration/cut’, A03 ‘fever’, R05 ‘cough’, S06 ‘localised rash’, R74 ‘acute upper respiratory infection’, R04 ‘breathing problem, other’, S76 ‘skin infection, other’ and L12 ‘hand/finger symptom/complaint’ were used most often (ranging from 3.0% to 5.8%). The distribution of this top 20 varied for the three age groups. For young children, R05 ‘cough’, A03 ‘fever’ and R74 ‘acute upper respiratory infection’ were used most frequently. For older children, S18 ‘laceration/cut’, A03 ‘fever’ and S06 ‘localised rash’ were most frequently used. The codes used most often for adults were S18 ‘laceration/cut’, S76 ‘skin infection, other’ and L12 ‘hand/finger symptom/complaint’.

We found no difference in the most frequently used ICPC-2 codes during daytime (08:00–23:00 hours) compared with nighttime (23:00–08:00 hours) (not in table).

Top five most used ICPC-2 chapters for video contacts

The most frequently used ICPC-2 chapters in video contacts were S ‘Skin’, R ‘Respiratory’, A ‘General and unspecified’, L ‘Musculoskeletal’ and E ‘Eye’ (table 3). Whereas chapters R and A were more frequently used for contacts concerning children aged 0–4 years, chapters S and L were more often used for contacts with older children and adults.

Discussion

Summary of results

GPs doing telephone triage in OOH-PC use video for contacts with a broad range of ICPC-2 codes within few ICPC-2 chapters. The most frequent reasons for encounter in video contacts were fever, skin, musculoskeletal and respiratory symptoms. Whereas skin and musculoskeletal symptoms were more frequent among video contacts for older children and adults, fever and respiratory symptoms were more frequent among video contacts concerning young children. The following ICPC-2 codes were used most often: S18 ‘laceration/cut’, A03 ‘fever’, R05 ‘cough’, S06 ‘localised rash’, R74 ‘acute upper respiratory infection’, R04 ‘breathing problem, other’, S76 ‘skin infection, other’ and L12 ‘hand/finger symptom/complaint’ to code reasons for encounter in video contacts.

Strengths and weaknesses of the study

This study is based on a large dataset that combined data from questionnaires with register data. The risk of recall bias was reduced as GPs answered the questionnaire immediately after a video contact. Furthermore, due to our restriction of maximum participation (five shifts per GP), we were able to include many unique GPs, which increased the generalisability of the results. Our study also had some limitations. First, the study lacked a control group due to the absent opportunity to use ICPC coding in the OOH-PC registration system. Without a control group, we could not conclusively determine whether the dominant reasons for encounter in video contacts resulted from GPs finding contacts with these reasons for encounter most suitable for video contacts or if these reasons for encounter had high prevalence in OOH-PC in general. Second, selection bias may be present because GPs who declined participation may differ from participating GPs in their way of using video. Third, when generalising the results, one should take into consideration that telephone triage by GPs is uncommon in most countries with comparable OOH-PC services.22 GPs’ decision-making about video use may differ from that of other triage professionals, such as nurses, and patients may contact OOH-PC with different and/or more varied symptoms, knowing that they are guaranteed contact with a GP.

Comparison with other studies

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate reasons for encounter in video contacts in OOH-PC. We found that the most frequent reasons for encounter for video contacts were fever, skin, musculoskeletal and respiratory symptoms. Huibers et al14 investigated reasons for encounter in all OOH-PC contacts (both telephone triage contacts and face-to-face consultations) across eight European countries, including Denmark. In line with our study, they found that most contacts concerned skin, respiratory and musculoskeletal symptoms, as well as general and unspecified symptoms. A scoping review from Foster et al17 found that respiratory, skin, abdominal, musculoskeletal and unspecified symptoms were common reasons for encounter in OOH-PC contacts. Moth et al13 investigated reasons for encounter in Danish OOH-PC telephone contacts in 2010. Compared with our study, their ICPC codes covered a broader range of ICPC-2 chapters, and only 5 of our top 20 ICPC-2 codes (S18, A03, R05, S06, R02) were among their top 20. Consequently, it seems that GPs use video for a selected group of patient conditions.

In line with existing literature, we found that the most frequently used reasons for encounter in video contacts differed according to patient age groups.14 17 Fever and respiratory symptoms were more frequent reasons for encounter among young children, whereas skin and musculoskeletal symptoms were more frequent among older children and adults. Huibers et al14 found that respiratory and general and unspecified symptoms were frequently used for children (aged 0–17 years), and identified ear symptoms (chapter H) as a frequent reason for encounter in this age group. They also found that adults (aged 18–44 years) often presented with musculoskeletal, respiratory and skin symptoms, whereas cardiovascular symptoms were frequent for older adults (aged >65 years). Foster et al17 found that fever and gastrointestinal symptoms were the most common in contacts concerning young children (aged 0–4 years), while cardiovascular diseases and gastrointestinal symptoms were most common in older patients (add age range). However, these studies included all OOH-PC contacts (both telephone triage contacts and face-to-face consultations), which could explain the difference between their findings and ours.

Our study indicated a focused use of video contacts, with the majority of reasons for encounter being from few ICPC-2 chapters and the top 20 reasons for encounter covering a relatively large part. This focused use of video could be explained by the fact that video is used as a triage tool in OOH-PC, offering additional information that helps the GP in directing patients to suitable care within an appropriate timeframe.7 Thus, one-way video provides additional visual information for a triage decision, rather than aiming to support relational care. Payne and Clarke found that GPs experienced notable advantages from using video, especially in paediatric cases like fever.7 For older children and adults, our findings indicated that video is used for reasons for encounter that can be assessed by video (eg, musculoskeletal symptoms like traumas and skin problems like rash or lacerations), thereby providing additional information for GPs in their decision-making process.

Implications for clinicians and policymakers

Our study suggests that video contacts are used for ICPC-2 codes within few ICPC-2 chapters and that the most frequently used ICPC-2 chapters vary for patient age groups. GPs seem to use video for telephone triage contacts with specific reasons for encounter, as a triage tool where visual input can aid their decision-making. This information could be relevant for the development of guidelines for video use in OOH-PC telephone triage contacts. However, more studies are needed to explore whether the findings are consistent across different OOH-PC organisations and types of triage professionals, as well as to assess the quality and safety of video triage.

Conclusion

GPs performing telephone triage in OOH-PC use video consultations for a range of reasons for encounter (ie, range of ICPC-2 codes within a few ICPC-2 chapters). The most frequently used reasons for encounter depend on the patient’s age. Video contacts concerning skin and musculoskeletal symptoms were most frequent among older children and adults, whereas fever and respiratory symptoms were most frequent in video contacts concerning young children.
