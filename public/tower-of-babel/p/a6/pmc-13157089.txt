Renal Disease in ROSAH Syndrome Masquerading as Lupus Nephritis: A Case Report and Cohort Analysis
Authors: Anirban Ganguli, Manisha Uppal, Shigeaki Umeda, David E Kleiner, Christina Kozycki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractROSAH (retinal dystrophy, optic nerve edema, splenomegaly, anhidrosis, and headache) syndrome is a rare, autosomal dominant autoinflammatory disorder caused by gain-of-function mutations in ALPK1. These mutations activate NF-κB signaling and drive development of a multisystemic disease. Beyond the hallmark features captured in the ROSAH acronym, affected individuals may experience recurrent low-grade fevers, deforming arthritis, and gastrointestinal inflammation. Prior to the identification of a unifying genetic cause, patients can be misdiagnosed with a range of immune-mediated conditions, such as systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis, multiple sclerosis, and systemic lupus erythematosus. Here, we report a 44-year-old man who was given a diagnosis of lupus nephritis before being found to have ROSAH. He presented with mild chronic kidney disease and subnephrotic proteinuria. Renal biopsy revealed immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis with minimal mesangial proliferation, resembling class II lupus nephritis, despite the absence of clinical or serological markers of systemic lupus erythematosus. In parallel, we leveraged the world’s largest reported single-center ROSAH cohort to assess the prevalence of chronic kidney disease in this rare genetic syndrome.Index Words: Case report, chronic kidney disease, immune-complex glomerulonephritis, ROSAH syndrome

Introduction

ROSAH (retinal dystrophy, optic nerve edema, splenomegaly, anhidrosis, and headache) is a rare autosomal dominant autoinflammatory disorder caused by gain-of-function mutations in ALPK1.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Four pathogenic variants—p.Thr237Met, p.Thr237Ala, p.Tyr254Cys, and p.Ser277Phe—have been identified.2, 3, 4 Clinical features include progressive blindness, optic nerve edema, anhidrosis, enamel defects, short dental roots, hyposalivation, recurrent low-grade fevers, arthritis, malaise, and headaches and therapies that have been attempted include corticosteroids and cytokine inhibitors targeting IL-1, IL-6, and TNF.1,5,6 Given its recent description, the full phenotypic spectrum of ROSAH is incompletely defined. Renal involvement was initially noted in 4 of 11 individuals (36%), with limited descriptive data.4 We report a case of biopsy-proven immune complex glomerulonephritis in a genetically confirmed patient with ROSAH and leverage data from the world’s largest single-center ROSAH cohort to explore the prevalence of renal involvement.

Case Report

A 46-year-old man with proteinuric chronic kidney disease (CKD) was referred to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) for comprehensive evaluation of systemic illness. Born prematurely at 29.5 weeks gestation, he experienced frequent fevers, myalgias, headaches, and cervical lymphadenopathy during childhood, which resolved by late adolescence. At age 19, he developed acute bilateral vision loss, diagnosed as optic neuritis, and was treated with high-dose corticosteroids, with only transient improvement followed by continued visual decline leading legal blindness by age 44. He was diagnosed with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis in his mid-20s. In his early 30s, he developed mild thrombocytopenia and moderate splenomegaly. Renal abnormalities emerged around age 33, with serum creatinine 1.2-1.3 mg/dL and subnephrotic proteinuria (urinary protein-creatinine ratio ranging from 400 to 800 mg/g), accompanied by systemic symptoms of fatigue and polyarthralgia. Infectious serologies (human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis A and B viruses) were negative. Autoimmune workup (antinuclear antibody, anti-double-stranded DNA, anti-Smith, anti-ribonucleoprotein, anti-Sjögren syndrome-related antigens A/B, and anti-centromere antibodies) was unremarkable, and serum complement levels were normal. A comprehensive diagnostic work-up was initiated.

A renal biopsy was performed at age 41 to elucidate the etiology of renal dysfunction. Histopathology revealed 14 glomeruli (1 globally sclerotic), focal segmental mesangial hypercellularity without endocapillary proliferation, necrosis, or crescents (Fig 1). Immunofluorescence showed 1+ mesangial IgM, IgG, and IgA, 2+ C3, and no C1q, light chains, or albumin. Electron microscopy revealed electron-dense deposits only in the mesangium (Fig 2). With no clear cause for immune complex deposition, a presumptive diagnosis of class II lupus nephritis (LN) was made. The patient was started on hydroxychloroquine and mycophenolate mofetil. Hydroxychloroquine was discontinued after 1.5 years due to retinal toxicity risk and was replaced with belimumab. Genetic testing at age 44 due to concerns of inherited retinal degeneration revealed heterozygosity for the ALPK1 p.Thr237Met variant, leading to the diagnosis of ROSAH syndrome. He was referred to the NIH for further evaluation.

Pedigree analysis demonstrated maternal inheritance; both the patient’s mother and daughter carried the same ALPK1 variant. His mother developed CKD in her 70s in the setting of IgMκ monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance. Paternal history was notable only for type 1 diabetes, and the parents were nonconsanguineous. Imaging revealed moderate splenomegaly. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed increased mineralization for age in the basal ganglia, red nucleus, and substantia nigra, whereas wrist magnetic resonance imaging revealed erosive arthropathy, small effusions, and marrow edema. Bone marrow biopsy demonstrated normocellular trilineage hematopoiesis with cytopenias attributed to splenomegaly. Renal pathology was re-evaluated at NIH with the original pathologist and, given the atypical histologic features and negative systemic lupus erythematosus serologies, idiopathic immune complex glomerulonephritis was favored over LN. Subsequently, mycophenolate mofetil was discontinued and tocilizumab, an IL-6 receptor antagonist, was initiated, resulting in improvement of fatigue. Losartan (25 mg/d) was initiated for nephroprotection. Over the next 2 years, renal function has remained stable, with estimated glomerular filtration rate >60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and the most recent urinary protein-creatinine ratio 286 mg/g (Table 1).

Cohort Analysis

To assess renal disease prevalence in ROSAH, we retrospectively analyzed 50 patients who consented for an autoinflammatory disease natural history study (NCT00001373). Forty-eight individuals from 19 unrelated families carried the ALPK1 p.Thr237Met variant; one had ROSAH features and a genetically confirmed parent but declined testing; another carried the p.Tyr254Cys variant. Median estimated glomerular filtration rate at enrollment was 97 mL/min/1.73 m2 (interquartile range, 73-110), calculated per Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2024 guidelines using race-free Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) 2021 (adults) and 2009 Bedside Schwartz (pediatrics) equations, with entry values shown on a CKD heatmap (Fig 3).7,8 Four patients (8%) met CKD criteria at baseline, with age at diagnosis ranging from 14 months to 77 years. Etiology was known in the index case and in another patient (alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency cirrhosis with hepatorenal syndrome requiring liver-kidney transplant at 16 months) but unknown in others. Diabetes mellitus and hypertension prevalence were 2% and 4%, respectively. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use was common among 3 adults with CKD.

Discussion

ROSAH was first described in 2019 in 5 unrelated families harboring heterozygous, missense variants in ALPK1.4 Although phenotypic expression is variable, characteristic features include optic disc elevation, intraocular inflammation, and retinal dystrophy that can progress to blindness by mid-adulthood if untreated. Systemic manifestations often include splenomegaly (ranging from mild to massive), episodic fever, malaise, transient cytopenias, deforming arthritis, and hypohidrosis.1,4,5,6Additional findings include recurrent headaches with basal ganglia mineralization on magnetic resonance imaging, enamel defects, hyposalivation that can mimic Sjögren’s syndrome, and transient C-reactive protein elevations.1,6 Despite the overlap with autoimmune syndromes, most individuals lack high-titer autoantibodies such as antinuclear antibodies or anti-Sjögren syndrome-related antigen A/B antibodies.1,4

The optimal treatment remains uncertain; reported interventions include immunomodulatory therapies such as glucocorticoids and anti-cytokine agents such as tocilizumab, adalimumab, and JAK inhibitors.1,2,5,6 Selective pharmacologic inhibition of ALPK1 may also represent a future therapeutic option, as a direct ALPK1 inhibitor has recently advanced into early-phase clinical development.9

Although ROSAH-associated ALPK1 variants are understood to be activating or gain-of-function mutations, the underlying mechanisms of disease remain incompletely defined. Wild-type ALPK1 is activated by bacterial nucleotide sugars, including ADP-heptose, triggering innate immune signaling.10 However, recent biochemical studies have suggested that some ROSAH-associated variants may also exhibit aberrant activation by endogenous nucleotide sugars, including UDP-mannose, potentially resulting in low-level, infection-independent signaling and raising the possibility that ROSAH represents a novel disease mechanism involving altered ligand specificity of a protein kinase.11,12

Although ALPK1-associated systemic inflammation is increasingly recognized, renal involvement is poorly characterized. In the initial case series, mild renal impairment was noted in several individuals without data on estimated glomerular filtration rate, proteinuria, or histology, raising the possibility of renal involvement in ROSAH.4 To explore this, we assessed renal function at enrollment in 50 individuals with ROSAH, identifying CKD in 8%, lower than the US general population estimate of 14% (Fig 3).8 Although chronic systemic inflammation could predispose to amyloid A amyloidosis, only 1 case has been reported—a patient with microalbuminuria and fat pad biopsy-confirmed amyloid deposits.1 Traditional CKD risk factors such as diabetes and hypertension were less prevalent in our cohort than in the general population, likely reflecting its younger age distribution (only 8% were older than 65 years compared with 33.2% in the US Renal Data System 2024 data).8 However, heavy nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use for headaches and arthritis may confer elevated CKD risk in ROSAH, although this was not observed, underscoring the need for longer follow-up, given the time and dose-dependent nature of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug nephrotoxicity.13 Although ALPK1 overexpression has been implicated in renal injury in diabetic nephropathy mouse models, and genome-wide association studies link ALPK1 variants with diabetes and cardiovascular disease, its direct role in renal disease remains speculative and unproven.14, 15, 16

Our index case illustrates how renal histology in a patient with undiagnosed systemic inflammatory disease could be misattributed to LN, despite absent serological and pathologic support. In retrospect, his cytopenias and splenomegaly were better explained by ROSAH. Mesangial immune complex deposition, though common in early LN, was deemed atypical here due to the lack of typical systemic lupus erythematosus features. The only other possibility was immune complex glomerulonephritis, and no secondary cause (infections, paraproteinemias, malignancy) was found. Although the biopsy classification as International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society class II LN appeared appropriate, it lacked some of the classic features of LN such as ‘full-house’ immunofluorescence, intense C1q staining, extraglomerular deposits, subendothelial/subepithelial deposits, and endothelial tubuloreticular inclusions.17,18 This injury pattern has been recently described as lupus-like nephritis, where the lupus-like renal histopathology is seen in the absence of extrarenal systemic lupus erythematosus features.19 In the largest published case series on lupus-like nephritis (n = 27), patients were more likely to be men, older, have Caucasian ancestry, and have greater tubulointerstitial scarring but lesser propensity to progress to renal failure than their LN counterparts.19 Secondary causes for immune complex deposition could be identified in only 10 cases.19 No prior association between lupus-like nephritis and ROSAH has been reported so far.

This case highlights the need to reconsider diagnoses as new data emerge. In ROSAH, features such as cytopenias and splenomegaly mimic conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus as well as rare genetic syndromes and may introduce cognitive anchoring bias that obscures alternative explanations. Renal disease in such settings should not be automatically attributed to a genetic disorder without thorough evaluation of other potential causes.

In conclusion, early observations and our case of immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis raised the possibility of renal involvement in ROSAH. However, detailed cohort analysis did not demonstrate an increased prevalence of CKD. This finding highlights the need for systematic evaluation of unexplained proteinuria or reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate to rule out coexisting renal diseases, in the context of rare genetic syndromes including ROSAH.
