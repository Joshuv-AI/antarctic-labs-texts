Association between dietary consumption and menopausal symptoms severity among women in selected local government areas, Lagos state, Nigeria
Authors: Saratu Omagbemi Ajike, Mary Adebimpe Abe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiet is increasingly recognised as a modifiable factor influencing the severity of menopausal symptoms. While global studies have examined this relationship, there is limited evidence on how culturally specific dietary patterns relate to menopausal health among African populations.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 376 postmenopausal women. Data were collected using structured questionnaires. χ2 tests and Cramer’s V were used to evaluate associations between dietary intake and menopausal symptom severity, with post hoc standardised residuals to identify contributing categories.ResultsMost participants were aged 51–55 (34.8%), with vaginal dryness reported as the most severe symptom (38.6%). Menopause severity was moderate, with most (83%) respondents experiencing mild-to-moderate menopausal symptoms. Indigenous diet was moderately consumed by 51.6%, the Western diet was highly consumed by 44.7% and processed foods were moderately consumed by 91%. There were statistically significant associations between all three dietary patterns and menopausal symptom severity. For Indigenous food, moderate consumption was marginally associated with being asymptomatic (Standard Residual (SR)=+1.9), while high consumption was marginally associated with fewer asymptomatic cases (SR=–1.9) (χ²(2)=8.31, p=0.016, φc=0.149). Western food intake showed a strong inverse association, with asymptomatic women over-represented among low consumers (SR=+5.9) and under-represented among high consumers (SR=–3.1) (χ²(4)=50.09, p<0.001, φc=0.495). Similarly, low processed food consumption was strongly associated with being asymptomatic (SR=+7.6), while moderate consumption was associated with fewer asymptomatic cases (SR=–2.5) (χ²(4)=78.21, p<0.001, φc=0.795).ConclusionThe associations between dietary patterns and menopausal symptoms severity reflect potential influences of varied dietary patterns on the menopause symptom experiences. These associations suggest that culturally relevant dietary strategies may contribute to managing menopausal symptoms among Nigerian women, warranting further longitudinal investigation.Keywords: Dietary patterns, Nutrition assessment

Background

Menopause is an inevitable life event that affects every woman, marking the end of menstruation and fertility.1 It signifies a pivotal transition in a woman’s life, with a decline in oestrogen levels. This hormonal shift often leads to various symptoms, including hot flashes, mood swings and sleep disturbances, which can significantly impact quality of life.2 Emerging research indicates that lifestyle factors, particularly dietary patterns and physical activity, play crucial roles in influencing the severity and prevalence of these menopausal symptoms.3

Untreated menopausal symptoms can worsen age-related health issues such as obesity, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease and osteoporosis, impacting overall well-being.2 4 Addressing women’s health during menopause is essential, with lifestyle changes like a healthy diet, exercise, stress management and sufficient sleep helping to reduce symptoms and enhance quality of life.5–7 Although environmental, genetic and medical factors influence symptom severity, diet-related lifestyle factors are particularly significant in determining health outcomes during menopause.8

Research across various populations shows that diet influences menopausal symptoms. In Iran, three dietary patterns—vegetables and fruits (VF), mayonnaise, liquid oils, sweets and desserts (MLSD) and solid fats and snacks (SFS)—were identified, with VF linked to fewer symptoms and MLSD and SFS associated with more symptoms.5 In Australia, diets high in fruits, vegetables and whole grains were linked to fewer menopausal symptoms.9 Japanese studies indicate that soy and isoflavone-rich diets reduce hot flashes and other symptoms.10 Overall, diets rich in fruits, vegetables and whole grains tend to lessen menopausal symptoms, while poor dietary habits, often influenced by Western diets, are associated with increased risk of chronic diseases and menopausal symptoms.11 12

Hormonal changes and imbalanced eating habits affect the metabolism of protein, fat and carbohydrates in menopausal women.13 14 High consumption of processed meals, rice/flour products, organ meats and sauces was associated with higher levels of cardiovascular risk factors, such as blood pressure and triglycerides, according to a Taiwanese study on postmenopausal women.15 Olowokere et al16 discovered correlations among menopausal problems, body mass index and food trends in Nigeria. Furthermore, low food consumption was associated with an increase in body mass index, which in turn was associated with menopausal problems.17 This emphasises how nutrition may affect menopausal health outcomes.

While specific studies directly linking dietary patterns and menopausal symptoms in Nigeria are limited, broader research indicates a rising prevalence of obesity among Nigerian adults, significantly influenced by lifestyle and eating habits.16 This trend suggests that dietary patterns prevalent in Nigeria may affect health outcomes during menopause, emphasising the need for targeted research in this area. Traditional Nigerian diets typically include whole grains, legumes, vegetables and locally sourced plant-based foods, which provide dietary fibre, antioxidants and phytoestrogenic compounds that may help regulate metabolic and inflammatory processes during menopause. Still, there is a growing trend towards the consumption of Western and processed foods.18 Geographical, social, technological, cultural factors and seasonal food availability18–21 influence regional variations in the consumption patterns.

There is a growing need to focus on this group, with the menopausal population projected to reach 1.2 billion by 2030 and to account for up to one-fifth of women by 2050.22 This study examined menopausal symptom severity and its association with dietary patterns among women in Lagos state, Nigeria, and identified the most strongly associated dietary patterns. Given Lagos’ dietary diversity, the findings provide context-specific evidence to inform targeted interventions and health promotion strategies for menopausal women in Nigeria.

Theoretical framework

Blum’s model of determinants of health provided a framework for this study. This model was first introduced in the early 1980s, underscoring a multifaceted understanding of health that integrates individual behaviour, environmental factors, genetic factors and medical care factors.23 The model suggests that health outcomes are not isolated events but are influenced by the dynamic interplay of these domains. This perspective emphasises the role of individual behaviours, such as dietary habits and physical activity, within the broader biopsychosocial context. This study adapted an aspect of the ‘Lifestyle’ component of Blum’s Model of Health Determinants, particularly focusing on dietary consumption as a lifestyle factor and examining its association with menopausal symptoms severity.

Methods

Research design and setting

A cross-sectional research design was adopted for this study to explore the relationship between dietary consumption and menopausal symptoms severity among menopausal women in Lagos state, Nigeria. The study was conducted in Lagos state between February and April 2025. Lagos state comprises 20 local government areas (LGAs). To ensure a diverse and representative population sample, the investigation focused on five selected LGAs: Alimosho, Ikeja, Oshodi-Isolo, Lagos Mainland and Ojo. These LGAs were selected to ensure variation in population density, socioeconomic status and cultural diversity, comprehensively representing the state’s demographic and health dynamics. The Babcock University Health Research Ethics Committee provided clearance for the study: BUHREC 127/25. Permission was also sought from all accessed primary healthcare centres (PHCs).

Population and sampling

The study population comprised postmenopausal women aged 45–75 years attending PHCs in Lagos. Eligible participants were Nigerian nationals, irrespective of educational level, who had not undergone hysterectomy or bilateral oophorectomy and who provided informed consent. Women were excluded if they had cancer, psychiatric disorders, substance use disorders, cognitive impairment or prior hysterectomy/oophorectomy. Of 451 women approached, 376 met the inclusion criteria. 55 declined participation, 11 had undergone hysterectomy and 9 were awaiting diagnosis for non-menopausal conditions (online supplemental B figure 1).

The formula used for the sample size calculation was Cochran’s formula: n=Z2P (1−P)/e2, where n=sample size, Z=1.96 at a 95% confidence level, p=prevalence of menopausal symptoms among the post-menopausal women in a similar study24 and e=0.05, to obtain a sample size of 451 considering a 10% non-response. A multistage sampling procedure was employed to select the study participants. First, the five LGAs were selected using simple random sampling, followed by the selection of one comprehensive PHC using simple random sampling, and in the third stage, convenience sampling was used to select respondents who were present during regular clinical hours in the PHCs. Written informed consent was obtained from each eligible respondent before data collection commenced. Respondents were given the option to choose whether they would participate in the general lounge or elsewhere within the health facility. Data were collected based on preferred location. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality and anonymity were assured. The study complied with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection and measures

The instrument used for data collection was a composite adopted and adapted questionnaire comprising the Menopause Rating Scale (MRS) questionnaire,25 A 62-item quantitative Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) with items adapted from similar studies based on dietary patterns among adults in Nigeria,26 and a socio-demographic section. Reviews from three experts in public health promotion and two in nutrition confirmed content validation by assessing the relevance, clarity and cultural appropriateness of the food items. Following minor modifications were made to ensure the questionnaire adequately captured commonly consumed foods within the study population. To ensure reliability, a pilot test was conducted with 10% of the sample size among menopausal women attending Oshodi PHC, Oshodi-Isolo LGA, Lagos state. The internal consistency of the instrument was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, and the results indicated high reliability. The questionnaire was structured into three sections: socio-demographic characteristics, which were measured as categorical and continuous variables; the MRS, which assessed menopausal symptoms severity (dependent variable) with 11 menopause-related symptoms across three domains: physical (4 symptoms), mental (4 symptoms) and genitourinary (3 symptoms). This section was scored on a 4-point Likert scale with response options: 1=mild, 2=moderate, 3=severe and 4=very severe. Thus, the level of severity of symptoms was measured on a 44-point rating scale as 0–11 (asymptomatic), 12–35 (mild to moderate), 36 and above (severe to very severe) based on standard measures; and the 62-item modified, semi-quantitative FFQ. This assessed participants’ food intake and categorised it into three food groups (independent variables): Indigenous diet (local foods with origins from Nigeria commonly consumed in many households consisting of complex carbohydrates, meats, vegetables and drinks), Western diet (typical foods with European or Western origin such as pasta, fried foods, packaged cereals and similar items) and processed foods (ultra-processed flour-based snacks). Dietary pattern scores were derived based on the frequency of consumption of food groups captured by the FFQ. The scoring approach focused on habitual intake patterns rather than estimation of absolute nutrient quantities, which is consistent with approaches used in epidemiological studies in sub-Saharan and Nigerian populations examining dietary behaviour.27 Dietary pattern scores were derived based on the frequency of consumption of food items reported in the FFQ. Each frequency category was assigned a numerical score reflecting increasing levels of intake (never or rarely=1 (seldom consumed), one to three times per month=2 (occasionally consumed), one to four times per week=3 (regularly consumed) and five times per week to more than once daily=4 (frequently consumed) and scores were summed across relevant food groups to generate a composite dietary pattern score as used in similar studies28 to characterise habitual dietary patterns. The resulting scores were categorised into low, moderate and high consumption groups based on the distribution of the total scores among study participants, measured on 108-point, 88-point and 48-point rating scales. This was done to facilitate interpretation and comparison across participants.

Data analysis

The data collected were coded, entered, cleaned and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.27. Descriptive statistics (means, SD and frequencies) were used to describe the data, and the χ2 test for independence was used to examine associations between the dietary patterns and menopausal symptoms severity. Additionally, Cramer’s V was used to determine the strength of association between significant dietary consumption groups and menopausal symptoms severity. All hypotheses were tested at a significance level of p≤0.05. No missing data were found before the analysis.

Table 1 shows that a total of 367 women participated in the study, with the most common age group being 51–55 years (34.8%), while the least common was 40–45 years (14.6%). In terms of education, the largest group held tertiary qualifications (41%). Occupationally, the highest proportion was retired (40.7%), and the lowest was in formal sector employment (15.7%). For pregnancies, the highest group had 5–6 pregnancies (27.1%), while the lowest had none (13.8%). In terms of the number of children, most had 3–4 children (26.9%) and the fewest had 7 or more (12.8%). The most frequent age of menopause was 47–49 years (44.1%), and the least frequent was 40–43 years (4.5%). Lastly, the most frequent post-menopause duration was 1–5 years (37.5%), while the least frequent was over 15 years (0.3%).

Post hoc analysis indicated that moderate Indigenous food consumption was marginally associated with being asymptomatic (SR=+1.9), while high consumption was marginally associated with being symptomatic (SR=–1.9). Asymptomatic women were over-represented among those with low Western (SR=+5.9) and low processed food intake (SR=+7.6), and under-represented at high Western (SR=–3.1) and moderate processed food intake (SR=–2.5), indicating strong inverse associations.

Discussion

Menopausal women in this study were mostly between early and mid-50s and had attained menopause shortly before their 50s. This aligns with other women globally, with most women globally attaining menopause between 40 and 55 years old.29 Studies in Nigeria have reported mean menopausal ages of 46.1–48.45 years.30 Women in this study also align with others as reported by other regions. For instance, in America, the average age of menopause is 51.4 years; in South Africa, 49 years and in other parts of West Africa, rates such as 49 years in Niger, 48 in Ghana and Mali, 49 years are reported.31

The symptoms reported in this study were similar to those reported elsewhere. However, symptoms varied across settings. In Port Harcourt, Nigeria, 82.5% of women had experienced menopausal symptoms, with higher rates of depressive mood (73.1%) than hot flushes (68.3%), vaginal dryness (55.0%), sexual dysfunction (48.1%) and insomnia (42.5%).32 Others reported forgetfulness as the most experienced symptom. Conversely, studies confirmed lower symptom severity (mean scores 0.4–1.4), potentially owing to higher levels of educational attainment, with emphasis on socio-demographic effects.33 As has already been noted, symptoms vary in menopausal women.5

The level of symptom severity experienced in this study closely aligns with other studies34 35 which also reported that most respondents experienced mild-to-moderate symptom severity. The mean score of MSS in the present study was higher than that reported by Yoshany et al36 at 10.21 (SD=6.50). Other studies also revealed varying MSS scores lower than those of this study. Heavey et al37 reported a mean score of 10.76±7.77, with a score of 17 indicating severe MSS. The differences reported may result from the impact of socio-cultural factors, social underlying features of race, genetics, people’s perception of menopause, variances in sample size, study design and instruments.

The study found substantial relationships between food patterns and menopausal symptom severity in women, indicating that food habits may play an essential role in the onset and severity of menopausal symptoms. The Indigenous dietary pattern was moderately consumed by most individuals and had a minor correlation with menopausal symptoms intensity. Women who consumed moderately were somewhat more likely to be asymptomatic than those who consumed excessively. Traditional diets in many African settings are typically characterised by higher intake of whole grains, legumes, vegetables and minimally processed foods, which are rich in fibre, micronutrients and phytochemicals that may help regulate metabolic and inflammatory processes during menopause.38 Others have also reported similar findings.5 Although the weak association suggested by the post hoc residuals draws caution in interpreting this relationship in this study.

Conversely, Western and processed dietary patterns were inversely associated with MSS. These diets are characterised by high levels of refined carbohydrates, saturated fats, sugars and additives, and have been associated with increased systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation.9 For example, a cross-sectional study of postmenopausal women found that diets high in fats, sweets and processed foods were positively associated with more severe physical and psychological menopausal symptoms, whereas diets rich in VF were inversely associated with symptom severity.5 Dietary patterns and menopausal symptoms can also be influenced by socioeconomic factors, health literacy and unmeasured variables. Therefore, while the present study demonstrates significant associations, these relationships may be partially explained by residual confounding and the findings should be interpreted within this broader context.

The results of the present study, however, support evidence that increasing westernisation of diets may negatively affect women’s health during the menopausal transition and thus provide further evidence on the influence of habitual dietary patterns on symptom severity during menopause. These results are particularly relevant in settings like Nigeria and many parts of sub-Saharan Africa, undergoing rapid nutrition transition, where traditional diets are increasingly replaced by westernised ones. Promoting healthier dietary practices that emphasise whole foods and reduce ultra-processed food consumption may therefore be an important strategy for improving menopausal women’s quality of life. Furthermore, promoting healthier dietary patterns that preserve beneficial aspects of traditional diets while limiting ultra-processed foods may also present an important strategy for improving women’s menopause health.

Strengths and limitations

This study contributes to the limited evidence on diet and menopausal symptoms in sub-Saharan Africa. Its strengths include the use of culturally relevant dietary patterns, a large sample size and inclusion of diverse ethnic groups, enhancing applicability and generalisability. However, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Dietary intake and symptom severity were self-reported, introducing recall and social desirability bias. Dietary assessment relied on frequency rather than quantity, reducing precision. Convenience sampling at PHCs may limit representativeness. Although the FFQ underwent expert validation, it was not validated against a reference method in this population, and residual confounding (eg, physical activity, body mass index, psychosocial stress) cannot be excluded. Further longitudinal and multiregional studies are needed to confirm these findings and clarify underlying mechanisms.
