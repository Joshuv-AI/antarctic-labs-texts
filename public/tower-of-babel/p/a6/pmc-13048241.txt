Biaxially ductile supramolecular polymer networks
Authors: Tong Wu, Zixiang Zhou, Junting Huang, Zifan Wang, Du Wang, Jingzhou Wang, Zhengkai Han, Bohang Wu, Xinling Wang, Chengyu Zeng, Hui Li, Dan Xing, Jianhao Lin, Shaobo Ji, Yang Jiao, Ying Jiang, Yizheng Tan, You Zhou, Rui Shi, Zehuan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supramolecular polymer networks are transiently cross-linked soft materials that have exceptional material properties such as high toughness, contractility, and recyclability. Previous studies have focused on optimizing their uniaxial mechanical features but did not manage to impart them with biaxial ductility. Here, we develop a set of biaxially ductile supramolecular polymer networks constructed by slow-dissociative, noncovalent cross-linkers. Retarding cross-link dissociation kinetics enables access of glass-like networks, exhibiting both enhanced compressibility and biaxial stretchability. Dynamic dissociation/reassociation of transient cross-linking junctions benefits to address biaxial deformation locking, thus achieving a compressive strength of more than 160 megapascals and a large areal strain up to 10,000%. A universal method of quantifying biaxial mechanical properties for supramolecular networks is also reported. This work enlightens the design and construction of biaxially ductile polymeric materials, holding substantial promise in applications including bioelectronic interfaces, soft robots, and tissue implants.

INTRODUCTION

Supramolecular polymer networks (SPNs) are a class of soft materials consisting of linear covalent polymers transiently cross-linked by noncovalent interactions (1, 2). Dynamic and reversible dissociation/reassociation equilibrium within noncovalent cross-links can dissipate applied energy, thus endowing SPNs with unique material properties including high stretchability (3) and toughness (4), contractility (5), self-healing (6), recyclability (7), etc. Pioneering reports have revealed that it is cross-link dynamics, rather than equilibrium thermodynamics, that are paramount in determining the material properties of SPNs (8–10). Most previous systems focus on the fabrication of stretchable SPNs, exploiting the fast-dissociative noncovalent cross-links; while the short life-time (τ = kd−1, kd denoted as dissociation rate constant) of these cross-links limits the viscoelastic properties of SPNs in both the viscous flow and rubber-like states. In contrast, retarding dissociation kinetics within noncovalent cross-links enables the access to another type of glass-like materials exhibiting extreme compressibility (11), which expands the overall picture of dynamic mechanics for SPNs. Although promising advances have been made, the material requirements for some demanding applications have not yet been met. A major limitation of SPNs is achieving high biaxial ductility with enhanced mechanical strengths.

Uniaxial compression can be theoretically regarded as equivalent deformation as biaxial tension (Fig. 1A) (12). According to the eight-chain rubber elasticity model (Arruda-Boyce model) (13), eight orientations of polymer chains in a cubic space are considered as a function of three principal global stretches λx, λy, and λz. This cube is allowed expansion along each principal direction expressed as λx λy λz = 1. Uniaxial compression provides two directions of equal principal stretch, λx = λy = λuc−1/2, λz = λuc, and biaxial tension also offers two venues of principal tensile stretch, λx = λy = λbt, λz = λbt−2; this lays down the foundation of their mechanical similarity. However, both of them suffer from deformation locking: An initially isotropic network will reach limiting chain extension, as deforming in both x and y directions leads to a planar state of chain orientation that allots stretches through drawing the materials from the transverse directions. Most reported systems of covalently cross-linked or slide-ring networks have been restricted by such deformation locking, and their maximum biaxial tension is less than 2500% (14–16).

Lately, a ground-breaking report pioneered the incorporation of pearl-necklace chains (PNCs) into polymer networks, enabling the access to a hyperelastic hydrogel with a reversible biaxial areal strain of more than 10,000% (17). This system mitigated biaxial deformation locking through efficient unfolding and refolding of subnanometric pearls, formed from heterogeneous hydration of weakly hydrophobic polyelectrolytes in water-scare conditions (less than 35 wt % water). While such PNC design requires precise control over hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity of ionic monomers, for now only applicable to a few example of polyelectrolytes, i.e., poly[2-(acryloyloxy)ethyl]trimethylammonium chloride. Recently, a library of slow-dissociative, noncovalent cross-linkers (kd < 1 s−1) have been developed to construct a set of glass-like SPNs exhibiting compressive strengths up to 100 MPa with no fracture, even when compressed at 93% strain over 12 cycles of compression and relaxation (Fig. 1B) (11). On account of mechanical similarity interpreted as above, we envisage that accessing glass-like networks with high compressibility could allow us to endow the SPNs with enhanced biaxial ductility, providing a solution to address the critical problem of biaxial deformation locking.

Here, we described a set of biaxially ductile SPNs based on noncovalent cross-linkers with slow dissociation kinetics. As proof of concept, a doubly charged (BPyVI) guest monomer and a singly charged (NVI) guest monomer, containing ionic motifs for ion-dipole interactions and aromatic rings for π-π stacking, were prepared and used as shown in Fig. 1B. Through host-enhanced π-π interactions mediated by cucurbit[8]uril(CB[8]) (18), the resultant ternary complex, BPyVI-NVI-CB[8], acted as slow-dissociative, noncovalent cross-links (kd < 0.01 s−1) to expand the dynamic mechanics of SPNs toward a glass-like range. The resultant SPNs, containing more than 75 wt % water, not only exhibited excellent compressibility with a record-high strength of 164 MPa at 94.9% strain but also had exceptional biaxial stretchability with a true strength up to 4.43 MPa and a biaxial areal strain of more than 10,000%. To the best of our knowledge, above mechanical performance achieved in our system exceed those of single-network (SN), double-network (DN), and entangled hydrogels as well as elastomers reported to date (Fig. 1C), holding great promise in applications including tissue engineering, flexible electronics, and soft robotics.

RESULTS

Construction and viscoelasticity

First of all, we used a first guest monomer BPyVI that tends to bind with CB[8] stepwise: The first-step binding constant K1 is (6.1 ± 1.1) × 108 M−1, and the second one K2 is (1.2 ± 0.1) × 105 M−1, indicative of their negative binding cooperativity (K1 > K2; table S1). In other words, the 1:1 complexation of BPyVI-CB[8] is thermodynamically favored, allowing the access of different secondary guest motifs. On this basis, another monomer NVI containing a naphthyl group was designed and used as the second guest. Critically, NVI has relatively large hydrophobic volume, which offers a strong enthalpic driving force to slow the secondary kd through releasing more of the conformationally constrained water from the CB[8] cavity. To investigate binding thermodynamics and dissociation kinetics of BPyVI-NVI-CB[8], isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was used. Fitting titration curves with the sequential binding site model and the kinetic isothermal titration calorimetry (kinITC) method (figs. S7 and S8) (19) provided us with their second-step binding constant of Ka = (8.6 ± 0.3) × 105 M−1 and dissociation rate constant (kd = 0.0088 ± 0.0006 s−1), respectively. The mechanism behind the formation of the BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] complexes is attributed to thermodynamic social self-sorting, evidenced by a substantial preference with a difference in Gibbs free energy of 17.5 kJ mol−1 (table S1 and figs. S7 and S10). Compared to reported CB[8]-mediated ternary complexes (11, 20, 21), the BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] complex has high binding affinity and slow dissociation kinetics, qualified as slow-dissociative cross-linkers for the fabrication of glass-like SPNs.

On account of the low kd value, the ternary BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] complex was used as the cross-linking motif to prepare a set of SPNs through in situ photopolymerization (6 hours at 365 nm) of arylamide monomers (CM = 2.0 M, 95 mol %) and the BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] cross-linkers (2.5 mol %) in aqueous media. To gain in-depth insights into viscoelasticity of the obtained SPNs, time-temperature superposition (TTS) experiments were performed. Alongside a decrease in temperature from 70° to 0°C, this SPN exhibited a continuous transition from a rubber-like to glass-like state (Fig. 2A). In comparison, the control networks without cross-linkers or cross-linked by BPyVI-NVI are limited in the rubber-like range at room temperature (fig. S12). In addition, apparent activation energy (Ea) for the SPN cross-linked by BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] was calculated to be 19.1 kcal mol−1, which indicates considerable energy-dissipating capability within the obtained materials. Creep recovery experiments further showed that this SPN is endowed with creep resistance under static deformation conditions (fig. S18). Together, the slow-dissociative BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] cross-linkers have enabled us to access a set of glass-like SPNs, feasible for investigation on their compressive and biaxial tensile properties as well as mechanical similarity.

Compressibility and stretchability

Having access to glass-like SPNs upon slow-dissociative cross-linkers led us to investigate their enhanced compressibility. Typical uniaxial compression tests were carried out on a preformed, cylinder-shaped SPN (14.3 mm D, 4 mm H, where D is the diameter and H is the height) by compressing the specimen until the load force or strain was reached, followed by room temperature self-recovery (fig. S19A). Initially, SPNs formed from 2.5 mol % BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] cross-linkers at CM = 2.0 M were measured at different deformation rates until maximum strain (94.9%) of the universal test machine and the corresponding stress-strain curves. Increasing deformation rates from 1 to 50 mm min−1 resulted in a pronounced upshift in their compressive response with a peak strength at 164 MPa (fig. S19B). This indicated a deformation rate–dependent compressive behavior of the obtained glass-like SPNs, likely attributing to the dynamic and reversible rearrangement of CB[8]-mediated cross-linking within the network structure. Even under the highest compressive loads, no fracture or permanent deformation was observed within the SPN, which self-recovered rapidly and completely to its initial state (fig. S20A and movie S1). To further explore the ultimate strength of these glass-like materials, six SPNs cross-linked by 2.5 mol % BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] were fabricated through varying total monomer concentration CM from 1.0 to 6.0 M (solid content = 11 to 53 wt %; Fig. 2B and table S2).

A maximum compressive strength was found up to 1.03 GPa for the BPyVI-NVI-CB[8]–cross-linked SPN at CM = 6.0 M without fracture observed at 92% strain and followed by near-complete self-recovery to its original state with negligible residual deformation (fig. S20B and movie S2). Varying CM within the SPNs further achieved well control over their compressive strengths from megapascal to gigapascal levels. To contextualize our findings in functional soft material systems, we compared them to state-of-the-art analogous systems with reported high compressive strengths. In fig. S21 and table S2, the compressive strengths for a series of single- or double-network (S/DN) hydrogels (green) are shown as well as those reported for bovine articular cartilage (blue). Across the water content range from 50 to 80 wt %, the values found for the BPyVI-NVI-CB[8]–cross-linked SPNs developed in this work (red) surpass both S/DN gels and the bovine articular cartilage by at least one order of magnitude. Despite high water contents up to 75 wt %, the compressive strength of our glass-like SPNs prepared at CM = 2.0 M reached a high level of more than 160 MPa, exceeding the record of 100 MPa reported in our previous work (11) and those values for bulk polymeric materials such as elastomers (Fig. 1C and table S3). These findings highlight the extreme compressibility of glass-like SPNs constructed by BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] cross-linkers, representing an important advancement in high-performance soft materials.

Room temperature self-recovery was then investigated through cyclic compression tests on SPNs formed from 2.5 mol % BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] cross-linkers at CM = 2.0 M and deformation rate r = 10 mm min−1. Compression-retraction cycles at varied loading forces from 1 to 26 kN are shown in Fig. 2C, exhibiting quasi-plastic deformation, for which the dynamic CB[8] host-guest interactions play a critical role. Similar to other reported supramolecular networks (22, 23), this phenomenon indicated the effective energy dissipation arising from force-induced rupture of BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] cross-linkers as sacrificial bonds. More impressively, fast and near-complete self-recovery across a range of recovery time from 1 to 6 min was observed in the continuous cyclic compression at the maximum strain and evidenced by their overlapping of loading-unloading curves (fig. S22). To further study the cyclic compressibility of such glass-like SPNs, a series of multicycle compression tests at the highest (26 kN) force loads was conducted (Fig. 2D).

Cyclic stress-strain curves obtained at 26 kN displayed almost perfect overlap across 10 cycles, demonstrating remarkably reversible compressibility of the SPN at ultrahigh strength up to 160 MPa. To quantify such reversible superiority of our glass-like SPNs, the reversibility index (U2/U1) is defined as the ratio of the area covered by the second cycle loading curve (U2) to that covered by the first cycle (U1) on the compressive stress-strain curves. At a compressive strain of 94.9%, our glass-like material achieved a U2/U1 value of 97.6% and maintained high reversibility around 98% in subsequent cycles, surpassing most reported S/DN gels and elastomers as well as bovine articular cartilage (Fig. 2E and table S4). This is attributed to the slow-dissociative cross-links within the SPN that can function as quasi-chemical cross-links to form an elastic network, resulting in near-complete recovery through elastic retraction and reassociation of the ternary BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] complexes.

To investigate whether high polymer content was responsible for self-recovery, we implemented additional experiments through compressing original SPN specimens at high polymer content (up to 40 to 50 wt %) and their recovered ones after 10-min waiting time (fig. S24). After compression to 92% strain, pure PAAm networks cannot self-recover to its original shape and size, while SPNs cross-linked by BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] demonstrated near-complete self-recovery to its initial state. These data indicate that the high polymer content does not make dominant contribution to self-recovery of our SPNs. In addition, these SPNs also exhibited advantageous crack resistance over PAAm networks under continuous compression for 100 cycles (fig. S26).

Uniaxial tensile stress-strain behavior of our glass-like SPNs was also studied through stretching a dumbbell-shaped specimen at a deformation rate of 50 mm min−1 until its fracture (Fig. 2F). The resultant materials exhibited excellent uniaxial stretchability with a true tensile strength of 26.1 MPa, a maximum elongation at break up to 2600%, and work of fracture of more than 8.0 MJ m−3. In addition, the precut SPN specimens can be merged into an integral to restore 61.7% of initial maximum strain after self-healing for 24 hours, indicating their capability of network rearrangement (fig. S15). These results established the basis for the exploration of biaxial tensile properties of our glass-like SPNs as follows.

To further emphasize the extreme compressibility of our glass-like SPN, a large specimen (35 mm L, 35 mm W, 4 mm T; where L is the length, W is the width, and T is the thickness) was prepared for a car-compression test. This specimen was sandwiched between two polyethylene films and positioned between a pair of stainless steel plates to avoid any slippage or contamination. A four-wheel car with a gross weight of 2000 kg was driven and held on top of the specimen for 1 min. Subsequently, the car repeatedly compressed the specimen 30 times, and no fracture or irreversible deformation was observed on account of near-complete self-recovery (Fig. 2G and movie S3).

Biaxial ductility

Having elucidated enhanced compressibility of the glass-like SPNs led us to explore their biaxial ductility. One of the main difficulties to evaluate biaxial ductility of SPNs in a quantitative manner is the lack of standard test method with feasible design of specimen size and shape to distribute applied biaxial tensile stress. Only five relevant standards can be found in the database of International Organization for Standardization (ISO), either designed for textile (ISO13118 and ISO24281) or metallic materials (ISO16842, ISO16808, and ISO/AWI25586); none of them are suitable for SPN characterization. To address this issue, we designed and used a SPN specimen with a cross-shaped geometry as shown in Fig. 3A and fig. S27 for biaxial tension experiments, which can mitigate stress concentration during stretching. According to an eight-chain rubber elasticity model (13), mechanical similarity of uniaxial compression and biaxial tension was then studied by synchronizing mechanical testing and finite element simulations (Fig. 3, B and C).

Mechanical similarity.

(A) Schematic diagram, (B) experimental photographs, and (C) finite element simulations of uniaxial compression and biaxial tension, indicative of their mechanical similarity. SPNs were prepared with 2.5 mol % BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] at CM = 2.0 M.

To investigate mechanical similarity, the global stretches in the z direction for uniaxial compression λz-uc and biaxial tension λz-bt should be equal, λz-uc = λz-bt. Thus, we assumed that if λz-uc = 0.5, then λx-uc = λy-uc = λx-bt = λy-bt = (λz-uc)−1/2 = 1.414; in other words, the theoretical strain in x and y directions for both uniaxial compression and biaxial tension should be equal as 41.4%. On this basis, we performed two sets of mechanical testing and finite element simulations using a hyperelastic model to describe strain distribution of a cylinder-shaped and a cross-shaped specimen when applied to a compressive strain of 50% and a biaxial tensile train of 41.4%, respectively. Figure 3C revealed that the surface linear strain for the cylinder-shaped specimen was around 37% while that for the cross-shaped specimen mainly ranged from 33 to 43%. Such similar surface strain in these two different models provided a solid support to the mechanical similarity of uniaxial compressibility and biaxial stretchability for our glass-like SPNs.

Typical biaxial tension experiments were carried out on a preformed, cross-shaped SPN through biaxially stretching the specimen until the load strain or its fracture (Fig. 4A and movie S4). SPNs cross-linked by 2.5 mol % BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] at CM = 2.0 M were tested until maximum strain (550%) of the universal test machine exhibiting an ultralarge areal strain of more than 3000% without fracture while withstanding a 600-g loading weight (Fig. 4B). Increasing deformation rates from 10 to 90 mm min−1 resulted in a pronounced upshift in their biaxial tensile response (equivalent strains along the X and Y axis) with a peak strength at 0.93 MPa (Fig. 4C). This clearly indicated a deformation rate–dependent biaxial tensile behavior of the obtained glass-like SPNs, likely arising from the dynamic dissociation and reassociation of CB[8]-mediated cross-links within the network structure. To further explore the ultimate biaxial tensile strength of these materials, six SPNs cross-linked by 2.5 mol % BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] were fabricated through varying total monomer concentration CM from 1.0 to 6.0 M (solid content = 11 to 53 wt %; Fig. 4D and table S2).

A maximum biaxial tensile strength was found up to 4.43 MPa for the BPyVI-NVI-CB[8]–cross-linked SPN at CM = 6.0 M with elongation at break more than 250% strain. Varying CM within the SPNs further achieved elegant control over their biaxial tensile strengths from kilopascal to megapascal levels. The increased network density within the SPN at CM = 6.0 M and high solid content (53 wt %) resulted in a substantial decrease in biaxial elongation, which could be attributed to the restricted mobility of polymer chain segments and the kinetically constrained rearrangement of noncovalent cross-links. Notably, most previous reports of supramolecular materials did not manage to quantify their biaxial tensile stress-strain behaviors and had to make a compromise to use uniaxial stress-strain curves to estimate their biaxial tensile properties (17, 24, 25). To contextualize our findings, we compared our glass-like system to other analogous materials known to display high biaxial stretchability (Fig. 1C and table S5). Despite a composition of more than 75 wt % water, the above values found for the SPN material reported here expand the region of biaxial tensile properties, distinct from SN, DN, and entangled hydrogels as well as elastomers reported to date.

Cyclic biaxial tensile tests were carried out to evaluate self-recovery of the glass-like SPNs constructed with 2.5 mol % BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] cross-linkers at CM = 2.0 M. Biaxial stretching-retraction cycles at different load strains from 134 to 542% are shown in Fig. 4E, demonstrating quasi-plastic deformation. This resulted from the energy dissipation through force-induced dissociation and subsequent reassociation of noncovalent cross-links within the SPN structure. Furthermore, room temperature self-recovery across a range of recovery time from 2 to 10 min was observed in the continuous cyclic biaxial tension at the maximum strain (550%) and evidenced by their overlapping of loading-unloading curves (Fig. 4F). The calculated reversibility index increased with extended recovery time and then achieved the maximum of U2/U1 more than 90% with a stress-recovery efficiency more than 86% (fig. S28A).

Multicycle biaxial tension tests at the maximum load strain (550%) were further performed through consecutive stretching and retraction for three times (Fig. 4G and fig. S28B), demonstrating the highly reversible cyclic biaxial stretchability of the glass-like SPNs. This is likely on account of the slow-dissociative transient cross-links within the SPN that can form semipermanent cross-linking junctions serving as elastic springs, leading to near-complete self-recovery through elastic retraction and reformation of the ternary BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] complexes. To further emphasize the extreme biaxial ductility of our glass-like SPNs, a cuboid specimen (20 mm L, 20 mm W, 5 mm T) constructed by 2.5 mol % BPyVI-NVI-CB[8] and CM = 1.5 M was prepared for a hand stretching experiment. This SPN specimen was biaxially stretched by 10 hands with substantial efforts until an areal strain up to 10,000% and subsequently self-recovered to its initial area within 30 min (Fig. 4H and movie S5). No fracture or structural damage was observed throughout deformation and recovery. Impressively, our SPNs developed in this work and the lately reported PNC systems both display exceptional biaxial areal strains, which complete the overall picture of biaxial ductility in water-abundant and water-scarce polymer networks, representing a major milestone in high-performance soft materials.

Biaxial strain sensing

Surface of human skin and soft tissues often experiences dynamic multidimensional deformation during natural movements, and biaxial strain sensing therefore offers an effective method for comprehensive analysis of biomechanical behaviors on tissue surface. To demonstrate the utility of our glass-like SPNs for flexible bioelectronic applications, we firstly evaluated their biocompatibility upon ISO 10993-12 guidelines (26). After 24-hour incubation with mouse chondrogenic cells, hardly any dead cell was observed by fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 5A) indicative of a cell viability more than 98%, satisfying biosafety requirements for bioelectronics in vivo (5, 27, 28).

On this basis, we fabricated a resistive biaxial strain sensor containing a cross-shaped SPN specimen (Fig. 5B) and tested its resistive response through applying different biaxial strains controlled by the universal test machine. Plots of relative resistance changes in the X and Y axis for the SPN were obtained over a wide range of incremental strains (Fig. 5C and fig. S35) from 17 to 100% or at orthogonal strains (Fig. 5D) in which γ(X) = 17% and γ(Y) = 0% and then γ(X) = 0% and γ(Y) = 17%. This SPN sensor demonstrated biaxial mechanical stability and electrical resistance stability, feasible for anisotropic sensing of biaxial extension on the tissue surface in the X and Y axis.

SPN-based biaxial strain sensors were further applied as a patch to monitor complicated physiological situations (i.e., lung respiration) that involve different biaxial strains in the X and Y axis (Fig. 5E). A three-dimensional (3D) printed lung model was periodically inflated and deflated by a respiratory pump at the same rate [breath per min (BPM)] with controlled air flow for simulating its expansion and contraction at different physiological states. Specifically, we fixed the lung breathe rate at 24 BPM and controlled air flow from 3, 6, to 9 liters min−1, representing different depths of respiration. Our SPN-based biaxial strain sensors can detect distinct and stable patterns of relative resistive changes under simulated conditions (Fig. 5F and movie S6).

To further demonstrate its utility in anisotropic sensing, the SPN-based patch was attached to different regions on the lung surface (Fig. 5G), enabling localized respiratory monitoring at position 1 (side-upper), position 2 (side-lower), and position 3 (front-upper). Figure 5H showed that our SPN sensor provided stable and distinct patterns across position 1, position 2, to position 3, indicating the difference in anisotropic expansion of the front and side parts as well as the upper and lower regions on the lung model. These results highlight the remarkable biaxial ductility of our glass-like SPNs and display their potential applicability in wearable bioelectronic devices for use in real-time monitoring of surface biaxial motions on human skin and soft tissues.

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, we have successfully developed a set of biaxially ductile SPNs enabled by noncovalent cross-linkers with slow dissociation kinetics. On the basis of mechanical similarity of uniaxial compression and biaxial tension, the resultant networks with more than 75 wt % water not only exhibited enhanced compressibility with a strength of 164 MPa and no fracture at 94.9% but also displayed exceptional biaxial stretchability with a true strength of 4.43 MPa and an areal strain of more than 10,000%, which completes the overall picture of biaxial ductility in polymeric hydrogels. This feature benefits from slow-dissociative supramolecular interactions that can act as both dynamic sacrificial bonds and semipermanent cross-links, overcoming the critical problem of deformation locking. A universal method of measuring biaxial stress-strain behaviors is established, addressing the bottleneck in quantification of biaxial ductility for supramolecular polymeric systems. We envision that this work will provide a general platform to exploit glass-like networks in the design and construction of biaxially ductile soft materials, holding great promise for myriad applications including bioelectronic interfaces, soft robotics, and tissue engineering.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Unless otherwise stated, all the chemicals in this work were used as received without further purification: acrylamide [for molecular biology, 99%, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), Aladdin], 1-vinylimidazole (98%, Adamas), benzyl chloride (99%, Adamas), 2-(chloromethyl)naphthalene (≥97%, Aladdin), 1,2-bis(chloromethyl)benzene [99%, gas chromatography (GC), Tokyo Chemical Industry (TCI)], pyridine (99.5%, Adamas), acetonitrile (99.9%, HPLC, Tianjin Concord Technology), N,N-dimethylformamide (99.9%, HPLC, Tianjin Concord Technology), 2-hydroxy-4′-(2-hydroxyethoxy)-2-methylpropiophenone (I-2959, photoinitiator, 98%, Sigma-Aldrich), deuterium oxide (D2O, D 99.8 atom %, J&K Scientific), chloroform-d (CDCl3, D 99.8 atom %, J&K Scientific), and other conventional solvents [99.7%, Analytical Reagent (AR)] were purchased from Beijing Tong Guang Fine Chemicals Company. Cucurbit[8]uril (CB[8]) was synthesized and isolated from the mixture of cucurbit[n]uril (CB[n]) derivatives by using a previously reported strategy (29). Milli-Q water was obtained from a Laboratory Ultrapure Water System (18.2 Mohm·cm, 25°C). Unless otherwise noted, all the sample solutions for characterization were prepared in D2O or Milli-Q water under heating and ultrasonication.

SPN fabrication

Before material fabrication, the requisite guest molecules BPyVI and NVI were synthesized through salt formation reaction, with their synthetic pathways illustrated in fig. S1. A predetermined stoichiometric mixture of acrylamide, noncovalent cross-linker, and photoinitiator (I2959) was prepared in a glass vial and sonicated in Milli-Q water until complete dissolution. The resulting precursor solution was sealed and purged with nitrogen for at least 30 min to remove dissolved oxygen from the liquid phase. Following this degassing step, the oxygen-free precursor solution was injected into prefabricated glass molds using a syringe, ensuring complete mold filling. The filled molds were subsequently subjected to in situ photopolymerization under 365-nm ultraviolet (UV) irradiation. After one-pot polymerization, the resultant SPNs were removed from the glass molds and precisely sectioned into predefined sizes and shapes using a cutting die. The as-prepared SPNs were directly used for subsequent characterization or demonstration without additional postprocessing. Owing to the sufficiently prolonged photopolymerization duration, the residual acrylamide content remained below 1%, exhibiting negligible impact on further application.

Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy

1H nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectra were acquired in D2O at 298.15 K on a Bruker AVANCE NEO (600 MHz) apparatus. 1H-1H correlation spectroscopy NMR spectra were recorded using a Bruker AVANCE III HD 400 (400 MHz) apparatus in D2O at 298.15 K. Chemical shifts are recorded with internal reference HDO set to 4.79 parts per million (ppm).

Isothermal titration calorimetry

ITC experiments were conducted on a Malvern MicroCal PEAQ-ITC apparatus at 298 K in Milli-Q H2O. In a typical titration for homoternary complexation, the host molecule (CB[8]) was loaded in the sample cell at a concentration of 0.05 mM, and the guest molecule was loaded in the syringe at a 20-fold higher concentration of 1.0 mM. In a typical titration for second binding, the 1:1 host-guest complex (BPyVI-CB[8]) was loaded in the sample cell at a concentration of 0.5 mM, and the second guest molecule was loaded in the syringe at a 10-fold higher concentration of 5.0 mM. In general, one titration experiment consisted of 1 injection of 0.6 μl and 32 consecutive injections of 1.2 μl with 90-s intervals between injections. The first one or two data points were removed before data analysis as they may contain contamination, and the resultant ITC curves were fitted by appropriate models (i.e., sequential binding sites for homoternary complexation, one set of sites for second-step binding) in MicroCal Analysis Centre software to gain thermodynamic information. The above ITC data were further analyzed by the kinITC method (19), using Affinimeter-ITC-Advanced software, to investigate association/dissociation kinetics.

Rheology

Rheological characterization was conducted by a Discovery Hybrid Rheometer HR-10, TA Instruments, with a Peltier Plate for temperature control. All measurements were performed upon a 20-mm parallel stainless steel plate geometry, with gap settings optimized to ensure complete sample coverage across the plate surface, and necessary calibration for geometry was carried out before testing. Frequency sweep measurements were conducted at 1.0% strain in the frequency range from 0.1 to 100 rad s−1. TTS experiments were performed upon a series of frequency sweep measurements at 0.1 to 100 rad s−1 and 1% strain with temperatures varying from 0° to 70°C with 10°C intervals. To calculate all horizontal shift factors αT for different temperatures, 20°C was used as the reference, αT (20°C) = 1. All the obtained αT values were analyzed through Arrhenius equation to calculate the apparent activation energy Ea of local chain motion for the resultant SPNs. Creep-recovery experiments were performed through applying 1000 Pa for 1200 s followed by recovery for 1200 s.

Compression

Following the SPN fabrication, compressive tests were conducted at room temperature, using an MTS Criterion Electromechanical Test System equipped with a 50-kN load cell. In a typical compressive experiment, a cylinder specimen with a fixed shape (14.3 mm D, 4 mm H; where D is the diameter and H is the height) was positioned between two stainless steel plates and compressed at variable deformation rates (1, 5, 10, 30, and 50 mm min−1) to obtain stress-strain curves. Compression-retraction cycles were performed by compressing the specimens until reaching predefined loading forces ranging from 1 to 26 kN. The self-recovery capability of the glass-like SPNs was further investigated through cyclic compressive tests with variable waiting periods ranging from 1 to 6 min. Multicycle compressive tests were performed by compressing a cylinder specimen until set load force (26 kN) at a deformation rate of 10 mm min−1 and relaxing the specimen at a deformation rate of 1 mm min−1. This whole cycle comprising the above two steps was repeated 10 times with 6-min intervals between successive cycles. For multicycle compressive experiments conducted at loading force of 8 kN, the cycle number was further increased to 25 times with an interval duration of 2 min between successive cycles. Crack resistance was evaluated through cyclic compression on precut cylinder specimens of the SPNs and pure PAAm networks (D = 10 mm), in which the length of the cut is 3 mm. Long-term durability was studied through continuous compression without waiting time.

Tension

Tensile tests were implemented, just after the network fabrication, on a Sunstest UTM2502 Tester equipped with a 100-N load cell at room temperature. In a typical tensile test, a dumbbell-shaped specimen, following the ISO4661-1 standard with certain dimensions (10 mm L, 2 mm W, 2 mm T), was clamped by roller grips and stretched at a specific rate (millimeter per minute) until break of the specimen. Precut SPN specimens were merged into an integral for 24-hour self-healing at room temperature and then measured through tensile tests.

Biaxial tension

Equal biaxial tensile tests were conducted, just after the network fabrication, on a CARE Measurement and Control IPBF-300 In-situ Biaxial Mechanical Test System. In a typical equal biaxial tensile test, a preformed, cross-shaped specimen with a fixed shape shown in section S6 was subjected to two orthogonally aligned loading forces and stretched at variable deformation rates (10, 30, 60, and 90 mm min−1) to obtain biaxial stress-strain curves. Cyclic equal biaxial tensile tests with variable waiting time from 2 to 10 min were carried out to study self-recovery of the resultant SPNs. Stretching-retraction cycles were performed by biaxially stretching the specimens until reaching predefined elongations ranging from 134 to 542%. Multicycle biaxial tensile tests were conducted by biaxially stretching a specimen until the set elongation (550%) at a deformation rate of 60 mm min−1, relaxing the specimen at the same rate, and repeating the above two steps as one whole cycle for six times with 10-min waiting time between successive cycles. Long-term durability was studied through continuous biaxial tension without waiting time.

Electrical tests for sensing compressive strain

Electrical tests were performed using a digital multimeter (KEYSIGHT 34461A). Capacitance-time curves were recorded with a NPLC (number of power line cycles) of 1.0. Relative capacitance changes used for a compressive strain sensor were calculated by∆C/C0(%)=(C−C0)/C0×100%(1)C0 and C were the capacitance of the sensor before and after deforming, respectively. The capacitive pressure sensor was fabricated through sandwiching a dielectric polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) film (20 mm D, 0.1 mm T) with two conductive SPN layers (14.3 mm D, 4.0 mm H), as shown in section S7. Characterization of capacitance changes of SPN capacitive sensor under varied pressure and cyclic compression were measured by synchronizing a digital multimeter with a universal mechanical testing machine (MTS Criterion Electromechanical Test Systems, operating at a rate of 10 mm min−1). Through linear fit, the average sensitivity was calculated in their corresponding pressure range (S1, S2). A standard commercial knee joint skeletal model was used to simulate cartilage compression and joint movement. A sandwich-structured, SPN-based capacitive sensor was used as an artificial cartilage and placed at the joint interface. Copper wires were used to connect the SPN capacitive sensor with the multimeter. During simulated knee joint movement, real-time capacitance changes were recorded to monitor compressive strain under low-/high-pressure states.

Electrical tests for sensing biaxial tensile strain

Resistance-time curves were recorded with a NPLC of 1.0. Relative resistance changes used for biaxial tensile strain sensor were calculated by∆R/R0(%)=(R−R0)/R0×100%(2)R0 and R were the resistance of the sensor before and after deforming, respectively. The SPN-based biaxial strain sensor was fabricated through cutting by a cross-shaped mold, as shown in section S8. Resistance changes versus strain along the X axis and the Y axis from 0 to 100% were tested independently by two digital multimeters synchronized with a biaxial mechanical testing machine (IPBF-300, operating at a deformation rate of 60 mm min−1). A cross-shaped specimen was mounted to the grips with copper tapes and then wired to the digital multimeter. On the basis of the biaxial stretching rate and the time sequence data of resistance changes recorded by the multimeter, the strain of each axis was calculated correspondingly. This biaxial tensile strain sensor was further used to monitor human respiratory movement on a 3D-printed acrylic resin lung model, where its simulated expansion amplitude and breath rate were controlled by a commercial respiratory pump. The SPN strain sensor was attached to the lung model through a biocompatible polyurethane (PU) tape for intimate surface adhesion and dehydration prevention. The air flow was tuned from 3, 6, to 9 liters min−1 with a controlled pumping rate at 24 BPM to simulate different respiratory states. Last, this SPN sensor was attached to three certain positions (position 1, side-upper; position 2, side-lower; position 3, front-upper), revealing difference in deformation during simulated respiration.

Finite element simulations

ABAQUS with a static model was used in finite element simulations. We use a hyperelastic model in material section and input experimental stress-strain curves, including uniaxial tension (Fig. 2F), uniaxial compression (Fig. 2B), and biaxial tension (Fig. 4D), into test data to calculate strain energy. The optimal method for calculation of the strain energy was selected, which is Ogden. The Poisson’s ratio sets at 0.45, calculated through uniaxial compression data. We primarily use Hex for meshing; however, in cases of irregular stress concentration where manual meshing fails to resolve the issue, Tet combined with adaptive meshing is used. In the adaptive remeshing rules, error indicator variables are element energy and Mises equivalent stress, with the refinement and coarsening set to 10 ensuring the maximum accuracy. During the simulations, both uniaxial compression and biaxial tension cases exhibit geometric symmetry. Therefore, we reduced the computational domain to one-quarter of the original model to decrease computational cost while maximizing the achievable meshing. For uniaxial compression, the model is a cylindrical specimen with a diameter of 14.3 mm and a thickness of 4 mm, which is compressed between two analytical rigid planes, with the friction coefficient set to 0.01. For biaxial tension, the specimen geometry and dimensions are shown in fig. S27.

Small-angle x-ray scattering

Small-angle x-ray scattering (SAXS) profiles of the SPNs were collected on a SAXSLAB GANESHA system with a 50-kV test voltage and a 0.6-mA test current that produces x-ray photons (λ = 1.54 Å). The scattering intensity of SAXS was measured as a function of momentum transfer vector, q, defined asq=4π/λ×sinθ(3)2θ is the scattering angle.

Biocompatibility testing

Pretreatment of sample is conducted before biocompatibility testing. The SPNs were weighed and sterilized by UV light for 2 hours and then extracted by the culture medium according to the ISO 10993-12 guidance (0.2 g ml−1 of medium for 24 hours at 37.0°C). A filter with a pore size of 0.22 μm was used to remove possible solid residues of SPNs in the extract. Meanwhile, mouse chondrogenic cells (ATDC5) were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology, PM150210) at 37.0°C, in 95% humidity and 5% CO2. Cells were passaged every 2 to 3 days. For the live/dead assay, ATDC5 were incubated in the well plates at a concentration of 105 ml−1 for 24 hours. After that, the culture medium was replaced with the same volume of the extract (pure medium in the control group). After the cells were cocultured with the extract for 24 hours, cells were stained using a Calcein/PI Live/Dead Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, C2015S) for 20 min. After washing twice with phosphate-buffered saline, the cells were imaged under a fluorescence microscope. The excitation wavelength of N,N-dimethylaniline N-oxide (DMAO) is 503 nm, while the excitation wavelength of propidium iodide (PI) is 535 nm. For the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, the incubation of ATDC5 and coculture with the extract was consistent with the Live/Dead assay as above. The ATDC5 were seeded in a 96-well plate, and 10 μl of CCK-8 solution (Lablead, CK001-500T) was added to each well. After incubation for 2 hours, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer. The background absorbance produced by wells containing no ATDC5 was subtracted from all wells.
