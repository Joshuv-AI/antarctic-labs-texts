Mechanistic insights into CAR-mediated repression of the HNF4α-FABP1 axis and inhibition of HepG2 cell proliferation
Authors: Zhihui Li, Linhao Li, Ritika Kurian, Jean-Pierre Raufman, Hongbing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe constitutive androstane receptor (CAR) modulates the transcription of numerous genes involving drug metabolism, energy homeostasis, and cell proliferation. While rodent studies suggest an oncogenic role for murine CAR in liver cancer development, emerging evidence indicates that human CAR (hCAR) may exhibit a tumor-suppressive role in hepatocellular carcinoma; notably, overexpressing hCAR suppresses human hepatoma cell proliferation. Yet, the molecular mechanisms whereby hCAR suppresses hepatoma cell proliferation are poorly understood. Our recent RNA-seq analysis of human hepatoma cells revealed that fatty acid binding protein 1 (FABP1), a pleiotropic modulator of lipid metabolism and cancer progression, was a top gene downregulated by hCAR. Here, we report a molecular mechanism whereby hCAR downregulates FABP1 expression by modulating HNF4α signaling. Knocking down hCAR expression in a HepG2-hCAR stable cell line restores the suppressed expression of FABP1 and HNF4α, while knocking down HNF4α alone is sufficient to suppress FABP1 expression. Luciferase reporter assays revealed concentration-dependent hCAR suppression of HNF4α-mediated FABP1 transactivation. This inhibitory crosstalk between hCAR and HNF4α was further confirmed by chromatin immunoprecipitation assays, where hCAR decreased HNF4α occupancy of the FABP1 promoter. Mechanistically, hCAR inhibits HNF4α expression by prompting deacetylation of histone H3 in the HNF4α P1 enhancer region, resulting in a repressive chromatin configuration and reduced HNF4α transcription. Furthermore, overexpressing FABP1 partially rescued hCAR-suppressed cell growth. Collectively, these results uncover the hCAR-HNF4α-FABP1 axis as a novel mechanism underlying hCAR-mediated gene repression.Keywords: CAR, FABP1, HNF4α, Gene regulation, Hepatoma cell proliferation

Introduction

The constitutive androstane receptor (CAR, NR1I3), a member of the nuclear receptor superfamily, plays a crucial role in modulating the expression of genes encoding hepatic drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters and influences the metabolism and clearance of both xenobiotic and endobiotic chemicals [1–3]. Outside this canonical signaling pathway, accumulating evidence indicates that CAR affects key physiological and pathophysiological processes by modulating energy metabolism, inflammation, and cell growth [4–6]. Significant species-specific differences have been documented between rodent and human CAR (hCAR) [7], particularly regarding their non-canonical biological functions [8]. Using phenobarbital and 1,4-bis [2- (3, 5-dichloropyridyloxy)] benzene (TCPOBOP) as model CAR activators, studies in mouse models firmly established that murine CAR functions as a tumor promoter, stimulating hepatic cell proliferation and liver tumor formation [9,10]. Such a mode of action for CAR in rodent hepatic tumor formation, however, is not plausible for humans, where treatment with phenobarbital does not alter hepatocyte proliferation, and long-term clinical use of phenobarbital was not associated with increased human liver cancer incidence [11,12]. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that hCAR may exhibit a tumor-suppressive role in the development of human hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [13–15]. In this scenario, hCAR is associated with the perturbation of numerous genes related to cell proliferation, apoptosis, and energy homeostasis.

Fatty acid binding protein 1 (FABP1), also known as liver fatty acid binding protein (L-FABP), is pivotal for the regulation of lipid metabolism and other cellular signaling pathways [16]. Enriched in the liver, FABP1 binds and transports a spectrum of key biomolecules, such as fatty acids, peroxisome proliferators, bile acids, bilirubin, and heme, into multiple subcellular compartments, including mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, and nucleus [16,17]. By controlling the intracellular trafficking of these molecules, FABP1 maintains heme homeostasis and coordinates the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [18]. Thus, FABP1 has been regarded as an important endogenous cytoprotective protein.

In HCC, important changes occur in cellular lipid metabolic homeostasis, favoring consistent cell proliferation. Studies using single cell RNA-sequencing and FABP1 immunohistochemistry revealed FABP1 expression in 47–100% of HCC samples, preferentially in more advanced cancer stages [19,20]. Genetic and pharmacological repression of FABP1 reduces liver tumor growth [19], while overexpressing FABP1 induces angiogenesis and migration of hepatocellular cancer cells, thereby augmenting cancer metastases [21]. Our recent RNA-seq analysis revealed that FABP1 is a top gene downregulated by hCAR in hepatoma cells, accompanied by suppressed cell proliferation [14]. The underlying molecular mechanisms driving hCAR-induced downregulation of FABP1 are not fully illustrated.

Hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α (HNF4α, NR2A1) is a master hepatic transcription factor that controls the expression of approximately 40% of the genes expressed in hepatocytes [22], maintaining hepatocyte differentiation and liver-specific functions [23]. A cognate HNF4α binding site was identified in the proximal region of the FABP1 promoter, and conditional knockout of the HNF4α gene disrupts hepatic FABP1 expression in mice [24], establishing HNF4α as a key modulator for FABP1 gene transcription. Notably, as a pleiotropic gene modulator, HNF4α functionally interacts with numerous other transcription factors [e.g., HNF1α, pregnane X receptor (PXR), and CAR] through either cooperative or suppressive mechanisms [25]. Indeed, HNF4α synergistically enhances CAR- and PXR-mediated transcription of SULT2A1 [26], while it fosters negative crosstalk with CAR in the regulation of CYP7A1 and miR–122 [27,28].

In the current study, we provide experimental evidence to illustrate molecular mechanisms by which hCAR suppresses FABP1 expression. Utilizing a combination of real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), immunoblotting, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), luciferase reporter, and histone acetylation assays, we identified a hCAR-HNF4α-FABP1 axis, whereby hCAR downregulates HNF4α by inducing histone H3 deacetylation at the HNF4α P1 enhancer region. This results in a repressive chromatin state that suppresses HNF4α expression. Alternatively, hCAR directly inhibits HNF4α interaction with the putative HNF4α-binding motif in the FABP1 gene promoter. Notably, fully functional ligand-binding domain (LBD) and DNA-binding domain (DBD) are required for hCAR to execute its roles in gene downregulation. Pharmacological deactivation of hCAR partially rescued the suppressed HepG2 cell proliferation. Our findings highlight important crosstalk among hepatic nuclear receptors that coordinate the endobiotic function of hCAR.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and biological reagents

Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium was purchased from Corning (Corning, NY); Penicillin-Streptomycin, Fetal Bovine Serum, Opti-MEM™ I Reduced Serum Medium, and Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent were all purchased from ThermoFisher (Rockford, IL). Doxycycline (Dox) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). X-tremeGENE 9 DNA Transfection Reagent was from Roche Diagnostics Corporation (Indianapolis, IN). Primers for RT-PCR and site-directed mutagenesis were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc. (Coralville, IA). The Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System was purchased from Promega (Madison, WI). All other tissue culture supplemental materials were purchased from Corning, ThermoFisher, or Sigma-Aldrich.

Plasmid constructs

Lentiviral expression plasmids of wild-type hCAR (hCAR1) and an alternative splicing variant of hCAR (hCAR3) were generated by cloning the full-length cDNA into the EcoRI site of a modified pWPXLd lentiviral vector as described previously [29]. The expression plasmid of FABP1 (EX-C0056-Lv105) and its control plasmid (EX-NEG-Lv105) were purchased from GeneCopoeia (Rockville, MD). The knockdown lentiviral plasmids of hCAR (TRCN0000021638) and HNF4α (shHNF4α−1 TRCN0000019189; shHNF4α−2 TRCN0000019191) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The pCR3-hCAR, pCR3-hCAR3 and CYP2B6–2.2 kb reporter plasmids were generated as described previously [30,31]. The FABP1 promoter region was PCR-amplified using FABP1-promoter forward and FABP1-promoter reverse primers as described previously [32] and listed in supplemental Table S1. The PCR product was subcloned into the MluI and NcoI sites of pGL3-basic vector, namely pGL3-FABP1-Luc. The plasmid was then confirmed by sequencing.

Site-directed mutagenesis

Site-directed mutagenesis of the DBD of hCAR was carried out within the zinc finger motifs of a modified pWPXLd-hCAR expression vector. The QuickChange Multi Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit from Stratagene (Santa Clara, CA) was used to introduce the mutations following the manufacturer’s instructions with the primers outlined in the supplemental Table S1 as hCAR-C28A-mut and hCAR-C53A-mut. The mutated nucleotides that convert the cysteine at positions 28 and 53 to alanine in the zinc finger motifs are indicated with underlined capital letters. The new plasmid constructs were sequenced to confirm the presence of the correct mutations.

Cell culture and lentiviral infection

HepG2 and HEK293T cells were obtained from the American Tissue Culture Collection (Manassas, VA). The Tet-on Dox-inducible hCAR stable HepG2 cell line (HepG2-hCAR) was generated and cultured as previously described [14]. The Yh18 cell line, a HepG2-derived cell line stably expressing V5-tagged hCAR, was obtained from Dr. Masahiko Negishi (National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences, National Institutes of Health, Research Triangle Park, NC) [33]. All cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin in humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. The lentiviral knockdown plasmids of hCAR (TRCN0000021638) and HNF4α (shHNF4α−1TRCN0000019189; shHNF4α−2 TRCN0000019191), and the lentiviral expression plasmid of hCAR1, hCAR3, hCAR-C28A-mut, hCAR-C53A-mut were used for lentivirus packaging as described previously [34]. HepG2 and Yh18 cells were seeded at the density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates 24 h before infection with the packaged lentivirus. Seventy-two h post infection, total RNA and proteins were prepared for RT-PCR and Western blot analyses.

Transient transfection assays

HepG2 and HEK293T cells were seeded at the densities of 1 × 105 and 3 × 105 cells per well, respectively, in 24-well plates and cultured overnight before transfection. The HepG2 cells were transfected with CYP2B6–2.2 kb reporter construct in the presence of pCR3-hCAR, or pCR3-hCAR3, or hCAR mutants (hCAR-C28A or hCAR-C53A) using X-tremeGENE 9 DNA Transfection Reagent following the manufacturer’s instructions. The HEK293T cells were transfected with the pGL3-FABP1-Luc reporter plasmid in the presence of pCR3-hCAR, or pCR3-hCAR3, or pcDNA3.1-HNF4α using the same transfection reagent. The pRL-Tk Renilla plasmid was used as an internal control reporter. Forty-eight h after transfection, cell lysates were assayed for luciferase activities normalized against the activities of co-transfected Renilla luciferase using Dual-Luciferase Kit. In these experiments, appropriate amount of the pcDNA3.1 empty vector was added to ensure that the total amount of plasmid DNA used in each transfection was the same. Data were represented as mean ± S.D. of three individual transfections. For FABP1 overexpression, HepG2-hCAR cells were transfected with the FABP1 (EX-C0056-Lv105) expression plasmid or its control plasmid (EX-NEG-Lv105) using X-tremeGENE 9 DNA Transfection Reagent as described above.

RT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo-Fisher, Rockford, IL) and reverse transcribed to cDNA using a High-Capacity cDNA Archive Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) following the manufacturers’ instructions. RT-PCR assay was performed on an ABI StepOnePlus real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster, CA) using SYBR Green PCR Mastermix (Qiagen, Germantown, MD). All primer sequences are listed in Tabel S1. Induction values were calculated according to the following equation: fold over control = 2ΔΔCt, where ΔCt represents the differences in cycle threshold numbers between the target gene and glyceraldehyde–3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and ΔΔCt represents the relative change in these differences between control and treatment groups.

Western blot analysis

Cell homogenate proteins were resolved on SDS-polyacrylamide gels (4–12% or 12%) and electrophoretically transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Subsequently, membranes were blocked with 5% milk and incubated with antibodies against CAR (diluted 1:1000; PP-N4111–00, Perseus Proteomics), FABP1 (diluted 1:1000; sc−271591, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), V5-Tag (diluted 1:1000; #13202 s, Cell Signaling Technology), HNF4α (diluted 1:1000; sc−374229, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), HNF4α P1 (diluted 1:1000; PP-K9218–00, Perseus Proteomics), HNF4α P2 (diluted 1:1000; PP-H6939–00, Perseus Proteomics), Acetyl-Histone H3 diluted (Lys9/Lys14) (diluted 1:1000; #9677 s, Cell Signaling Technology) Histone H3 (diluted 1:1000, #4620, Cell Signaling Technology) or β-actin (diluted 1:5000; A3854, Sigma-Aldrich) at 4°C overnight. Blots were washed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase secondary antibodies and developed with West Pico chemiluminescent substrates (ThermoFisher).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays (ChIP)

The ChIP assay was performed using the Cell Signaling ChIP Kit (#9003, Cell Signaling Technology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 2.5 × 106 HepG2 or HepG2-hCAR cells were seeded into a 10-cm dish. Twenty-four h after seeding, the cells were treated with Dox (1 μg/ml) or vehicle control for 72 h. These cells were then cross-linked by directly adding formaldehyde (final concentration, 1%) into medium, and incubated for 10 min at room temperature, washed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (#7012, Cell Signaling Technology), lysed, and digested by Micrococcal Nuclease. Immunoprecipitation was performed overnight at 4°C using anti-HNF4α (AB181604, Abcam), anti-Acetyl-Histone H3 (Lys9/Lys14) (#9677 s, Cell Signaling Technology) antibodies or normal IgG (#2729, Cell Signaling Technology) followed by precipitation using ChIP-Grade Protein G Magnetic Beads (#9006, Cell Signaling Technology). The immunoprecipitated DNA was purified according to the Cell Signaling ChIP Kit’s instruction. Quantitative PCR was performed using specific sets of primers as outlined in Table S1.

Cell proliferation analysis

Cell proliferation was assayed using the CCK−8 kit (Enzo Life Sciences, Inc.) according to the manufacturer's instructions. HepG2-hCAR cells were seeded at the density of 5 × 103 per well in 96-well plates 24 h before transfected with FABP1 overexpression vector or its control plasmid. Transfected cells were treated with Dox (1 μg/ml) or CINPA1 (5 μM) every other day. On day 7, CCK−8 reagent (10 μl) was added to each well followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1.5 h. The absorbance was read at 450 nm using the GLOMAX 96 microplate reader (Madison, WI). Relative cell growth rate was normalized against control.

Matrigel scaffold-based 3D tumor spheroid culture

HepG2-hCAR cells were transfected with FABP1 expression vector or empty control vector as outlined above. Matrigel (Cat. 356231, Corning) was first diluted with culture medium to 5 mg/ml. By gently pipetting, we placed 12.5 μl drops of the diluted Matrigel on pre-warmed 24-well plates, avoiding formation of air bubbles. The plates were incubated 30 min at 37°C (until the matrix solidified). Twenty-four h after transfection, cells were resuspended to 4 × 104 cells/ml in the diluted Matrigel, and 12.5 μl of the Matrigel cell suspension was added onto the pre-coated Matrigel base in 24-well plates (1 drop/well). The plates were incubated in a 37 °C incubator under 5% CO2 for 30 min to solidify the domes. Subsequently, culture medium (1 ml/well) was added to the Matrigel doom-containing plates. Cells were treated with Dox (1 μg/ml) or CINPA1 (5 μM) every other day when the culture medium was replaced. HepG2 spheroid was defined as a compact rounded organoid with a distinct border of diameter ≥ 70 μm containing cells indistinguishable from one another. On days 3, 6, and 8, live images were taken under a bright field microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti). The number and diameter of tumor spheroids were quantified using NIS-Element Analysis software (version 5.30.04).

Statistical analysis

All results are expressed as mean ± S.D. of triplicate determinations unless otherwise specified. Statistical comparisons were made using one-way analysis of variance followed by a post hoc Dunnett's test or Student's t test where appropriate. The criterion of significance was set at p < 0.05, and statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 10 software (GraphPad Inc., La Jolla, CA).

Results

hCAR represses FABP1 expression in HepG2 Cells

Our previous RNA-seq analysis of HepG2-hCAR cells (GEO access number: GSE308994) revealed FABP1 as a top gene strikingly downregulated by hCAR. To investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms, we first validated the RNA-seq data in Tet-on-inducible HepG2-hCAR and Yh18 hCAR-stable cell lines [14,33]. As shown in Fig. 1A, in the inducible Tet-on-HepG2-hCAR cells, doxycycline (Dox) treatment concentration-dependently increased hCAR as expected, while decreasing FABP1 mRNA expression. Notably, the same Dox treatment in parental HepG2 cells did not significantly alter either hCAR or FABP1 expression (Fig. 1B). In these two cell models, similar hCAR and FABP1 protein expression results were observed when treated with vehicle control and Dox (1 μg/ml) for 72 h, respectively (Figs. 1C, 1D). Conversely, in Yh18 cells, knockdown of V5-tagged hCAR significantly increased FABP1 mRNA and protein expression (Figs. 1E, 1F). Over-expressing hCAR in Hep3B cells also repressed FABP1 expression (Supplemental Fig. S1), suggesting that in human hepatoma cells hCAR reversibly controls FABP1 expression.

hCAR downregulates FABP1 gene expression in HepG2 cells. HepG2-hCAR and parental HepG2 cells were treated with various concentrations of Dox as indicated or vehicle control for 72 h. (A) In HepG2-hCAR cells, Dox treatments increased mRNA expression of hCAR and decreased expression of FABP1 in a concentration-dependent manner. (B) In parental HepG2 cells, mRNA expression of hCAR and FABP1 was not affected by the same Dox treatments. (C, D) Western blotting analyses demonstrated that the protein levels of hCAR and FABP1 in these cell lines are consistent with the observed mRNA changes, 72 h after Dox (1 μg/ml) or vehicle control treatment (CT). (E) In a separate experiment, hCAR expression in Yh18 cells was efficiently abolished by lentivirus shRNA-hCAR knockdown. (F) Knockdown of hCAR in Yh18 cells upregulates the mRNA and protein levels of FABP1. Data were expressed as mean ± SD, n = 3. **p < 0.01.

hCAR-mediated reduction of HNF4α downregulates FABP1 expression

Previous studies showed that multiple transcription factors including HNF4α can regulate FABP1 transcription through putative binding motifs located in the proximal promoter of the FABP1 gene (Fig. 2A). In Tet-on inducible HepG2-hCAR cells, Dox treatment markedly reduced the mRNA expression of HNF4 with relatively modest reductions observed for FOXA1, PPARα, PXR, and C/EBPα (Fig. 2B). Importantly, the same Dox treatment had no detectable effect on the expression of these genes in parental HepG2 cells (Fig. 2C). We further showed that Dox treatment suppressed HNF4α mRNA and protein expression in Tet-on-HepG2-hCAR cells but not in parental HepG2 cells (Figs. 2D, 2E). We also found that shRNA knockdown of hCAR in Yh18 cells provokes HNF4α mRNA and protein expression (Fig. 2F). Notably, shRNA silencing of HNF4α alone in HepG2 cells is sufficient to reduce FABP1 mRNA and protein expression (Figs. 2G, 2H). These findings support a key role for HNF4α in hCAR-mediated downregulation of FABP1.

Functional impairment of the hCAR LBD or DBD abolishes its repressive effect on HNF4α and FABP1 transcription

The ligand-binding domain (LBD) is essential for nuclear receptor interactions with coregulators, facilitating gene expression [35,36]. A splice variant of hCAR, namely hCAR3 [37], containing a five-amino acid insertion in the LBD (Fig. 3A), disrupts the otherwise constitutively activated wild-type hCAR as demonstrated by a luciferase reporter assay in HepG2 cells (Fig. 3B). We then infected HepG2 cells with lentiviruses expressing hCAR or hCAR3 at levels to generate comparable quantities of these proteins (Fig. 3C). Intriguingly, overexpressing hCAR but not hCAR3 reduced the mRNA and protein expression of HNF4α and FABP1 (Figs. 3D, 3E). Similar results were observed in Hep3B-hCAR and Hep3B-hCAR3 stable cell lines (Supplemental Fig. S2), suggesting that a functional hCAR LBD is required for its repressive regulation of HNF4α and FABP1 expression.

Relative luciferase activity was measured using the Promega dual-luc reagent. Next, we tested whether the DBD of hCAR affects its downregulation of HNF4α and FABP1. The hCAR DBD contains two zinc finger motifs that are important for recognition of and binding to specific DNA sequences upstream of its target genes. To disrupt the interaction between hCAR and its target genes, we constructed two hCAR mutants (hCAR-C28A and hCAR-C53A) in the zinc finger motifs (Fig. 4A). In a luciferase reporter experiment, we co-transfected a CYP2B6–2.2k luciferase plasmid [31] and hCAR wild-type or its mutants in HepG2 cells. Compared to wild-type hCAR, both mutants exhibited significantly lower transcriptional activity (Fig. 4B). When overexpressed at comparable levels in HepG2 cells (Fig. 4C), neither hCAR mutant significantly altered the expression of HNF4α or FABP1, in contrast to the repressive effects observed with the wild-type hCAR (Figs. 4D, 4E). Collectively, these findings indicate that both functional hCAR ligand- and DNA-binding domains are both required to maintain the repressive effects of hCAR on HNF4α and FABP1 expression.

Pharmacological deactivation of hCAR rescues HNF4α and FABP1 expression

Realizing that a fully functional hCAR is required for its downregulation of HNF4α and FABP1, we next tested whether pharmacological inhibition of hCAR could rescue this downregulation. CINPA1 is a selective hCAR inverse-agonist, exhibiting potent hCAR deactivation without perturbing the activity of its sister receptor, PXR [38]. As expected, in a HepG2 cell luciferase reporter assay, CINPA1 treatment markedly suppressed hCAR constitutive activity (Fig. 5A). Unexpectedly, in Tet-on inducible HepG2-hCAR cells, cotreatment with Dox and CINPA1 further increased hCAR expression (Figs. 5B, 5F). Importantly, despite the moderate increase in hCAR protein abundance, CINPA1 markedly inhibited hCAR-mediated CYP3A4 expression (Fig. 5C). Subsequent experiments revealed that CINPA1 treatment significantly reversed hCAR-repressed expression of HNF4α and FABP1 at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figs. 5D, 5E, and 5F). CINPA1-mediated rescue of hCAR-suppressed FABP1 expression was also observed in a Tet-on Hep3B-hCAR cell line (Supplemental Fig. S3). Together, these results provide further evidence that a fully functional hCAR maintaining high-basal activity is required to suppress HNF4α and FABP1 expression.

hCAR but not hCAR3 suppresses HNF4α-mediated transactivation of the FABP1 promoter

To determine whether hCAR competitively inhibits HNF4α-mediated FABP1 transcription, luciferase reporter assays were conducted in HEK293T cells co-transfected with the pCR3-FABP1-luc reporter plasmid and HNF4α expression plasmid in the presence and absence of hCAR or hCAR3 expression vectors (Fig. 6A). As predicted, our results showed that HNF4α significantly enhanced FABP1 luciferase activity (Fig. 6B). Notably, HNF4α-enhanced luciferase activity was concentration-dependently reduced by wild-type hCAR (Fig. 6B), but not by the splicing variant hCAR3 (Fig. 6C). In a subsequent ChIP assay (Fig. 6D), we showed that HNF4α protein was efficiently recruited onto the FABP1 promoter in HepG2 cells (Fig. 6E), while Dox-induced hCAR expression markedly decreased HNF4α binding at the FABP1 promoter. Our findings suggest that a functional hCAR suppresses FABP1 expression by both downregulating HNF4α expression and blocking its interaction with the FABP1 regulatory region.

hCAR represses HNF4α transcription by promoting histone H3 deacetylation in the P1 promoter of HNF4α

Next, we investigated the mechanisms by which hCAR suppresses HNF4α expression. The HNF4α gene is transcribed using two distinct promoters, P1(proximal) and P2 (distal), leading to the expression of P1-HNF4α and P2-HNF4α isoforms [39]. We showed that in HepG2 cells, P1-HNF4α represents the predominant isoform, and the expression of neither P1- nor P2-HNF4α is affected by Dox treatment (Figs. 7A, 7B). In contrast, in the Tet-on-HepG2-hCAR cells, Dox-induced hCAR expression markedly repressed both mRNA and protein levels of P1-HNF4α, while it only affected P2-HNF4α mRNA but not protein expression, likely due to the low basal levels (Figs. 7C, 7D). Using an H3K9K14ac antibody, ChIP assays revealed that in Dox-treated HepG2-hCAR but not normal HepG2 cells, the level of histone H3K9K14 acetylation was significantly reduced in the HNF4α P1 enhancer region (Fig. 7E). Subsequent immunoblotting experiments showed that in either normal HepG2 or HepG2-hCAR stable cells, Dox treatment does not markedly alter global levels of H3K9K14 acetylation (Fig. 7F). These data suggest that epigenetic modification of chromatin acetylation plays a role in hCAR-mediated repression of HNF4α expression.

Overexpression of FABP1 partially rescues hCAR-mediated inhibition of HepG2 cell proliferation

Our recent work demonstrated that hCAR plays a tumor suppressive role in HCC development and hepatoma cell proliferation [14]. Given that FABP1 is a top hCAR-downregulated gene in HepG2 cells, we wondered whether overexpression of FABP1 could reverse hCAR-inhibited HepG2 cell proliferation. We showed that transfection of the FABP1 expression plasmid in the Tet-on HepG2-hCAR cells effectively reversed hCAR-repressed FABP1 mRNA and protein expression (Figs. 8A, 8B). Importantly, overexpression of FABP1 partially but significantly rescued hCAR-suppressed HepG2 cell proliferation (Figs. 8C, 8D). In 3D cultured HepG2 spheroid assays, we demonstrated that hCAR markedly suppressed spheroid formation (Fig. 8E). This suppression was significantly alleviated by FABP1 overexpression, as evidenced by increases in both spheroid number (Fig. 8F) and diameter (Fig. 8G) on day 8. A time-dependent reversal of hCAR-suppressed spheroid growth by FABP1 was also observed on days 3, 6, and 8 (Supplemental Fig. S4). These findings indicate that hCAR-mediated suppression of FABP1 expression contributes to the observed inhibition of HepG2 cell proliferation.

Pharmacological inhibition of hCAR partially reverses hCAR-suppressed HepG2 cell growth

To determine the functional integral of hCAR in suppressing HepG2 cell growth, we conducted cell proliferation experiments in both 2D and 3D cultures of Tet-on HepG2-hCAR cells. As shown in Figs. 9A and 9B, pharmacological deactivation of hCAR by CINPA1 significantly increased hCAR-suppressed cell growth rate. In separate experiments, CINPA1 treatment partially rescued the number and size of HepG2 spheroids that were repressed by hCAR on day 8 of treatment (Figs. 9C, 9D, and 9E), with a time-dependent recovery trend observed at earlier time points (Supplemental Fig. S5). From a translational point of view, these findings raise the possibility that pharmacological inhibition of hCAR activity may represent a novel avenue to suppress hCAR-dependent cancer cell proliferation.

Discussion

The nuclear receptor CAR controls the expression of myriad genes involved in drug metabolism and disposition, energy homeostasis, and cell growth. A growing body of evidence reveals that significant species specificity exists between hCAR and its rodent counterparts, particularly in the regulation of energy metabolism and cell proliferation [8,14,40]. As a xenobiotic sensor, CAR from different species shares a conserved common mechanism for upregulating drug-metabolizing and transporter genes by binding to and transactivating the consensus xenobiotic response elements located in their regulatory regions [41]. In contrast, CAR modulates energy metabolism and cell proliferation through more complex mechanisms involving both upregulation and downregulation of related genes [42]. Comparatively, knowledge regarding the mechanisms underlying CAR-driven gene downregulation is limited. In the present study, we elucidated how hCAR suppresses FABP1 expression through the HNF4α signaling pathway. We uncovered that overexpression of hCAR in HepG2 cells not only affects HNF4α interaction with the FABP1 promoter by functional competition but also inhibits HNF4α transcription by decreasing H3 acetylation at its P1-enhancer region. Together, these combined modes of action coordinate a marked suppression of FABP1 expression via the hCAR-HNF4α-FABP1 axis.

Although CAR typically acts as a positive gene regulator facilitating drug metabolism and clearance, it also downregulates genes often involved in lipid metabolism, inflammation, and cell proliferation. In the latter scenario, direct binding of CAR to target gene promoters is usually not required. For instance, CAR downregulates PEPCK and CYP7A1 via protein-protein interaction and/or protein degradation that diminish the accessibility of other transcription factors [27,43]. Indeed, we found that hCAR significantly suppresses the expression of FABP1 in HepG2 cells, despite the absence of a consensus CAR response element upstream of the FABP1 gene, suggesting this downregulation may occur through an indirect mechanism. Expression of FABP1 is governed predominantly at the transcriptional level. To date, multiple transcription factor response elements have been mapped into the promoter region of FABP1, including binding motifs for PPARα, FOXA1, AP1, C/EBPα, HNF1α, and HNF4α [25,32,44,45]. Our RNA-seq analysis revealed that overexpression of hCAR markedly reduced HNF4α expression compared to other involved transcription factors (Fig. 2B). Moreover, HNF4α knockdown in HepG2 cells significantly suppressed FABP1 expression. These findings establish that HNF4α plays a pivotal role in hCAR-mediated suppression of FABP1, prompting us to further investigate the molecular mechanisms driving this hCAR-HNF4α-FABP1 signaling.

Unlike most ligand-dependent nuclear receptors, CAR can be activated by both direct ligand binding and indirect mechanisms involving phosphatase-mediated T38 dephosphorylation and subsequent nuclear translocation [46,47]. Interestingly, CAR is spontaneously accumulated in the nuclei of and constitutively activated in nearly all immortalized cell lines, including HepG2 cells [48]. We showed that ectopic hCAR expression in HepG2 cells alone is sufficient to downregulate HNF4α and FABP1 expression. To gain insight into whether a functionally integral hCAR is required in this gene downregulation process, we evaluated the expression of HNF4α and FABP1 in HepG2 cells expressing hCAR bearing defective LBD or DBD, respectively. A splicing variant of hCAR, namely hCAR3, contains an in-frame insertion of five amino acids in the LBD [37]. This characteristic lowers its transcriptional activity in hepatoma cells to a minimal level compared to that of wild-type hCAR [49]. We found that hCAR3 does not alter the expression of either HNF4α or FABP1 as observed in HepG2 cells expressing comparable levels of wild-type hCAR. Moreover, hCAR but not hCAR3 was able to suppress HNF4α-induced FABP1 transactivation through competition. Similarly, we observed that disruption of DBD function by site-directed mutation in the zinc finger motifs of hCAR fails to modify the expression of HNF4α and FABP1. Moreover, pharmacological deactivation of hCAR by CINPA1 in HepG2-hCAR cells rescues HNF4α and FABP1 expression and recovers hCAR-suppressed cell growth. Together, these findings indicate that the integrity of a functional hCAR is essential for downregulating HNF4α and subsequently FABP1. It is noteworthy that, unlike what occurs in immortalized cell lines, CAR is sequestered in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes in vivo and in the absence of agonistic stimulation remains inactive [48,50]. Our current findings raise the possibility that pharmacological modulation of hCAR activity in vivo is a potential therapeutic strategy for HCC and other metabolic disorders, warranting further investigation.

Functional interplay between CAR and HNF4α was reported previously. Miao et al. reported that CAR can repress HNF4α transcriptional activity by inhibiting its binding to the DR1 motif in the CYP7A1 promoter region [27]. Similar repressive crosstalk between CAR and HNF4α is implicated in the regulation of the gluconeogenic genes, such as PEPCK [51]. On the other hand, HNF4α promotes CAR-mediated induction of CYP2C9, CYP3A4, and SULT2A1 through synergistic interactions [26,52]. Notably, there are putative binding sites for both CAR and HNF4α at the regulatory regions of these positively regulated genes, whereas the suppressive crosstalk often occurs when target genes only comprise binding motifs for one receptor. Using luciferase reporter and ChIP assays, we found that hCAR overexpression decreases HNF4α binding to the FABP1 promoter and thereby reduces its transcriptional activity.

In addition to functional competition between hCAR and HNF4α in FABP1 transcription, overexpression of hCAR is associated with decreased HNF4α expression in hepatoma cells. The HNF4α gene is driven by two promoters that generate P1- and P2-HNF4α isoforms, with the P1-HNF4α predominantly expressed in HepG2 cells. Compared to the extensive studies focusing on the functions of HNF4α as a master hepatic regulator, investigations regarding the transcription of HNF4α itself remain limited. It is well known that histone acetylation is often observed in active chromatin regions, indicative of increased transcription. We found that hCAR overexpression promotes H3 (K9 and K14) deacetylation at the HNF4α P1 enhancer, without significantly altering the overall H3K9K14 acetylation levels across whole chromosomes. These data are in line with a previous report demonstrating that activation of PXR, a sister receptor of CAR, reduces H3 acetylation to downregulate the transcription of HNF4α [39].

FABP1 is at the crossroads of energy metabolism and cancer development. Through sequential steps, FABP1 facilitates the metabolism, transport, and clearance of unesterified long-chain fatty acids, thereby protecting cells from oxidative stress [53]. Its role in HCC, however, is rather complicated and even controversial. Inoue et al. reported that FABP1 is downregulated in small HCCs associated with elevated β-catenin signaling and poor differentiation [54]. Others observed an opposite expression pattern of FABP1 in late-stage HCCs [19,55]. Moreover, when compared with wild-type mice, liver tumor growth in FABP1−/− mice is significantly attenuated [19]. Using plasmid-driven overexpression of FABP1 in a Tet-on HepG2-hCAR cell model, we demonstrated that FABP1 expression substantially though not completely reversed hCAR-mediated suppression of HepG2 cell proliferation. Notably, pharmacologically inhibition of hCAR by CINPA1 also partially rescued hCAR-dependent inhibition of cell growth. Our findings suggest FABP1 could be one mechanism contributing to the observed tumor suppressive role of hCAR.

It is worth noting that our study does come with a few limitations. First, all studies were carried out in engineered hepatoma cells, where endogenous hCAR expression is minimal and ectopically introduced hCAR is constitutively active. Although primary human hepatocytes and fully differentiated HepaRG cells maintain low basal hCAR activity, these cells are quiescent and generally unresponsive to proliferative cues. Together, these features limit the pharmacological and translational potential of our findings, and future studies using more physiologically relevant models, including human-transgenic mouse models, are warranted. Second, while we focused on deciphering the hCAR-HNF4α-FABP1axis as a key pathway through which hCAR downregulates FABP1, growing evidence reveals that FABP1 transcription is governed by multiple transcription factors. For instance, CAR is known to inhibit PPARα activation, and PPARα positively regulates FABP1 transcription [56,57]. A more complete illustration of how hCAR downregulates FABP1 will therefore require future studies that incorporate multiple transcription regulators in parallel.

In conclusion, we identify FABP1 as a novel gene downregulated by hCAR and illustrate that HNF4α serves as a transcriptional mediator bridging this gene downregulation. Our findings uncover two molecular bases underlying the hCAR-HNF4α-FABP1 regulatory signaling: 1) hCAR reduces the level of histone H3 acetylation at the HNF4α P1 enhancer region to diminish HNF4α expression, and 2) hCAR suppresses HNF4α-mediated transactivation of FAPB1 by functional competition. Importantly, the functional integrity of hCAR is essential for its gene-suppressive activity in hepatoma cells. While further investigation is needed to fully delineate the comprehensive mechanisms underlying hCAR-mediated suppression of HCC, our current findings suggest the hCAR-HNF4α-FABP1 axis represents a novel mechanism underlying hCAR-mediated gene repression.
