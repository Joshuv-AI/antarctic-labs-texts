Neuropsychiatric symptom phenotypes for early detection of risk in older adults
Authors: Fareshte Erani, Caitlin M Terao, Shanna Cooper, Alexandra J Weigand, Katherine J Bangen, Emily C Edmonds, David G Coughlin, Kelsey R Thomas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe examined whether early neuropsychiatric symptom (NPS) patterns were associated with cognitive and neuropathologic changes.METHODSLatent class analysis within 20,599 cognitively unimpaired older adults in the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center dataset identified NPS phenotypes. Progression to cognitive impairment, cognitive trajectories, and neuropathology across phenotypes were assessed.RESULTSFour NPS phenotypes were identified: (1) Low‐All, (2) High‐Depression, (3) High‐Agitation/Anxiety/Irritability (hAAI), and (4) High‐All. Risk of progression to mild cognitive impairment (MCI)/dementia differed across phenotypes (Low‐All < High‐Depression < hAAI < High‐All, p < 0.001). High‐All and hAAI showed the lowest baseline performance and lower practice effects on cognitive tests, while High‐Depression had persistently lower attention scores. The hAAI group had greater amyloid and neuritic plaque burden and higher arteriosclerosis severity compared to other groups (p < 0.01)DISCUSSIONHigher global NPS, and those with high agitation, anxiety, and irritability, showed greater odds of cognitive decline than depressive symptoms alone, underscoring the potential prognostic utility of early NPS patterns in predicting cognitive decline.Highlights Identified four, data‐driven neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) phenotypes in cognitively unimpaired older adultsNPS phenotypes were differentially linked to progression, cognition, and pathologyHigh‐all and high‐anxiety, agitation, and irritability had poorer baseline cognitionNPS phenotypes may serve as early indicators of neurodegenerative disease risk Keywords: AD risk, data‐driven, mild behavioral impairment, neuropathology, neuropsychiatric symptoms

INTRODUCTION

Research has demonstrated that cognitive heterogeneity exists among older adults prior to the development of dementia. Beyond the traditional amnestic/nonamnestic distinction, 1 , 2 data‐driven approaches applied to neuropsychological test data have identified multiple mild cognitive impairment (MCI) subtypes 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 that show unique associations with Alzheimer's disease (AD) biomarkers and trajectories of functional decline and dementia risk. 3 , 5 , 10 , 11 These methods, when applied to cognitively unimpaired (CU) individuals, have further delineated subtle cognitive decline phenotypes (i.e., low‐executive, low‐visuospatial, low‐memory/language) who progressed to MCI/dementia at faster rates than a subgroup of participants who performed in the average range across neuropsychological tests, 12 , 13 , 14 highlighting the potential of data‐driven methods for early detection and intervention in neurodegenerative diseases. However, the focus thus far on leveraging patterns of cognitive performance for early detection and prognostic risk fails to account for other behavioral and psychological indicators of a dementia prodrome that may be easily accessible and scalable and, importantly, impacts quality of life. 15

Neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) represent a promising prognostic marker that may be sensitive to future clinical progression. NPS encompasses a range of behavioral and psychological symptoms including depression, anxiety, apathy, agitation, aggression, delusions, hallucinations, irritability, sleep disturbance, and aberrant motor behavior (e.g., repetitive movements, inability to keep still, irregular pacing). 16 Recent evidence suggests that the presence of NPS in CU older adults is associated with faster time to dementia progression, even in the absence of cognitive symptoms, 17 underscoring their potential utility for early detection. This observation has been formalized in the construct of mild behavioral impairment (MBI), which characterizes later‐life emergent and sustained NPS as a risk state for cognitive decline and dementia. 18 While global NPS burden is informative, certain symptoms may be especially prognostic. For example, elevated levels of depression, irritability, and agitation have been associated with faster progression to AD across both CU and MCI groups. 19 Moreover, early manifestations of specific symptoms such as irritability, anxiety, and apathy have also been identified as independent predictors of cognitive decline. 20 , 21

While the number of NPS and presence of specific symptoms have been shown to be associated with rate of progression to MCI/AD, less is known about whether distinct psychiatric symptom patterns emerge early and whether they differentially predict cognitive decline. Although research on the interplay between NPS and cognition is limited, findings suggest that certain symptoms, such as agitation, may be a strong marker of rapid cognitive impairment 22 and that the presence of apathy, but not depression, may better predict patients with amnestic MCI who progress to AD. 23 As such, specific clustering of NPS (e.g., high‐agitation and high‐apathy) may indicate differential risk of cognitive decline. While frameworks such as MBI underscore the importance of later‐life behavioral changes, they typically group symptoms by domain and do not test whether empirically derived symptom profiles differ in their association with dementia risk. Additionally, while prior studies have examined associations between individual NPS and neuropathological changes, 24 , 25 no studies, to our knowledge, have examined these relationships among patterns of NPS (phenotypes) and longitudinally in CU populations. Identifying neuropathologic changes that differ across NPS phenotypes may offer insight into distinct pathways through which NPS confer increased risk for cognitive impairment. Therefore, the current study aimed to (1) derive NPS phenotypes using latent class analyses (LCA), 26 (2) characterize phenotypes on baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, (3) examine rates of progression to MCI/dementia and longitudinal cognitive decline, and (4) in exploratory analyses, examine neuropathologic outcomes by NPS phenotype. We hypothesized that phenotypes reflecting unique patterns of NPS would emerge that would be predictive of subsequent risk of progression to MCI/dementia, current and longitudinal cognition, and neuropathologic outcomes.

METHODS

NACC data

Archival data from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) Uniform Data Set (UDS) were used. 27 , 28 Baseline data were collected from 2005 to 2022 across 46 Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC) study sites. Participants at ADRCs take part in a standardized assessment battery (i.e., UDS) to measure the presence and severity of MCI or dementia. Each participant is accompanied by an informant who knows the participant well–typically a spouse, adult child, or friend. More detailed information regarding the UDS and NACC has been published (naccdata.org). Written informed consent to participate in the study was obtained from all participants or their caregivers at each individual ADRC, as approved by individual institutional review boards (IRBs) at each site; the current study used only de‐identified data. Inclusion criteria for enrollment in an ADRC include stable health status with no history of major stroke, neurologic disorders, severe psychiatric illness, substance abuse, or learning disability.

Diagnostic procedures

Participants completed clinical, neurologic, and neuropsychological evaluations as part of the ADRC/NACC research protocol. A diagnosis of normal cognition, impaired‐not‐MCI (i.e., used for participants whose presentation did not clearly fit into the normal cognition or MCI categories), MCI, or dementia was determined at baseline and at each subsequent annual visit by the consensus of an interdisciplinary team or physician at each site. 29 Dementia diagnoses were determined by NACC using National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke–Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Association criteria. 30 , 31 For participants diagnosed with a cognitive disorder, the presumptive primary etiologic diagnosis and any contributing conditions are specified in the NACC database. For the current study, participants who had a consensus diagnosis of “impaired, not MCI,” “MCI,” or “dementia” at their baseline study visit were excluded from the present study.

To determine diagnosis and etiology at subsequent visits, we assigned a single primary diagnosis based on the consensus conference “primary and probable cause of observed cognitive impairment” of each participants’ final available study visit data. Major neurocognitive diagnoses included: AD, Lewy body disease (LBD), Parkinson's disease (PD), atypical parkinsonian disorders (e.g., multiple system atrophy [MSA], progressive supranuclear palsy [PSP], corticobasal degeneration [CBD]), frontotemporal dementia (FTD; including FTD with motor neuron disease, behavioral variant FTD, and FTD not otherwise specified), primary progressive aphasias (PPA), and cerebrovascular (e.g., vascular brain injury and vascular dementia). If multiple major neurocognitive diagnoses were present, all were retained. For participants without a major neurocognitive diagnosis, MCI subtypes included: language, attention, executive functioning, visuospatial, and multiple domain (amnestic or non‐amnestic). If a participant met criteria for a multiple domain MCI, this diagnosis took precedence over other MCI subtypes, and no additional cognitive domain classifications were assigned. Participants were considered to have progressed to cognitively impaired status if they had a diagnosis of MCI or dementia at their final study visit; final diagnoses of CU or “impaired, not MCI” were considered not to have progressed. Of note, “impaired, not MCI” were considered non‐progressors since this status reflects subtle or borderline difficulties that do not meet criteria for MCI or dementia, and to avoid overestimating progression. To evaluate the robustness of this approach, we conducted sensitivity analyses in which “impaired, not MCI” were either reclassified as progressors or excluded from the analytic sample in our Cox regression and cognitive composite models. Clinical diagnoses with etiology were listed based on their final diagnosis at their last available study visit.

A composite psychiatric history variable was also derived to indicate whether participants had a self‐reported history of anxiety, depressive episode within the past 2 years, bipolar disorder, PTSD, OCD, schizophrenia, developmental psychiatric conditions (e.g., ADHD, autism spectrum, dyslexia), and/or other psychiatric disorders.

NPS measures

Neuropsychiatric symptoms were identified using the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI‐Q) and Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS). The NPI‐Q is a brief, informant‐rated version of the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) 32 developed for use in clinical settings and previously validated against the full NPI. 33 The NPI‐Q captures the presence and severity of 12 behavioral and psychological symptoms commonly observed in individuals with cognitive disorders. The 12 items, each assessing 12 NPS domains include: delusions, hallucinations, agitation/aggression, dysphoria, anxiety, euphoria, apathy, disinhibition, irritability/lability, and aberrant motor activity. An informant indicates whether each symptom is present (yes or no), frequency, severity, and duration, though the current study focused on presence only given the relatively low frequency of endorsed symptoms, which limited the feasibility of modeling frequency or severity in LCA. The GDS (short form) is a screening measure for depressive symptoms in older adults made up of 15 items. Participants answer “yes” or “no” for each item, 34 where we used the item‐level responses in our analyses. Including both NPI‐Q and GDS items allowed us to capture a broader and more nuanced range of neuropsychiatric symptoms and enabled us to integrate informant‐related and self‐reported perspectives. While the NPI‐Q reflects observable behavioral symptoms, the GDS provides insights into participants’ subjective mood experiences. This complementary approach enables the identification of NPS phenotypes that vary along multiple symptom axes, allowing us to capture a broad range of NPS and a more nuanced variation in mood‐related symptomatology that may not have been fully addressed by the NPI‐Q depressive item alone.

Clinical progression and cognitive measures

Cognitive composite scores were computed at each visit using neuropsychological test data from the first 6 years of follow‐up for participants included in the LCA. Raw scores from individual tests were standardized into z‐scores across all participants and visits included in this study. The sample‐based z‐scores reflect relative performance within the study population. Domain‐specific cognitive composites were then calculated by averaging the standardized z‐scores for tests within each cognitive domain. Composite scores were derived using the measures with the most available data. In cases where a primary test was unavailable, alternative tests in the same domain were available as substitutes (e.g., Logical Memory instead of Craft Story; see Table S1). 14 The neuropsychological tests included measures of learning and memory (Immediate and Delayed Recall from Craft Story or if not available, Logical Memory or, for participants assessed in 2020 as part of T‐Cog, the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test [RAVLT]), attention/processing speed (Forward Digit Span or Number Span and Trail Making Test Part A), executive functioning (Backward Digit Span or Number Span and Trail Making Test Part B), and language (Category Fluency [animals, vegetables] or UDS fluency [F, L] and Boston Naming Test [BNT] or Multilingual Naming Test [MiNT]).

Neuropathology measures

For neuropathologic outcomes (Table S2), we included measures of AD severity (i.e., Thal phase, Braak stage, CERAD, AD neuropathologic change [ADNC]) and primary pathology classifications (i.e., AD, limbic‐predominant age‐related TDP‐43 encephalopathy [LATE], FTD, LBD, atherosclerosis, arteriosclerosis, cerebral amyloid angiopathy [CAA], and hippocampal sclerosis) for autopsy‐confirmed participants. Autopsy was performed using a standardized protocol. 35

For AD staging, neuritic plaque density was estimated using methods recommended by CERAD, 36 and Braak stage was used for neurofibrillary tangle pathology severity. 37 Pathologic diagnosis of AD was made using National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) consensus criteria. 38 , 39 AD primary pathology also included intermediate and high ADNC classifications.

Participants were classified as having LATE if TDP‐43 inclusions were observed in the amygdala, hippocampus, or neocortex in the absence of frontotemporal lobar degeneration with TDP‐43 pathology (FTLD‐TDP).

To account for heterogeneity within FTD, participants were further classified into two broad subtypes: FTLD‐Tau and FTLD‐TDP. FTLD‐Tau included subtypes such as progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal degeneration, and Pick's disease as well as chronic traumatic encephalopathy and tangle‐dominant FTLD, while FTLD‐TDP included cases with TDP‐43 pathology.

Lewy body pathology was staged according to consensus LBD guidelines, with modification as suggested in Montine et al. (2012) to simplify the number of brain regions examined, and additional modification into “Limbic/Neocortical,” “Brainstem/olfactory/amygdala/unspecified,” and “Brainstem only” subtypes. 38 , 40 , 41

We also classified participants as having CAA, arteriosclerosis, and atherosclerosis if they were rated as moderate/severe. Finally, hippocampal sclerosis was assessed and classified when neuronal loss in the CA1 and subiculum, accompanied by gliosis, was out of proportion with the degree of AD pathology. 42 , 43

Statistical analysis

LCA was conducted using the poLCA package in R 44 to identify classes of participants based on NPS profiles. Variables included in the LCA were selected based on a combination of theoretical relevance, statistical redundancy, model fit criteria, and contribution to model interpretability. First, positive valanced GDS items (“I feel happy”) were reverse coded so that all higher scores reflect greater NPS symptom burden (“not feeling happy”). Spearman's correlation coefficients were examined across all NPI‐Q and GDS variables to identify redundant items, with moderately to highly correlated items reduced to a single representative variable (e.g., “not feeling happy” was retained over “not in high spirits” and “not feeling satisfied”). Items with low endorsement or overlap with retained variables (e.g., “feeling empty”, “feeling bored”, “feeling hopeless”) were also excluded. For the NPI‐Q, the depression item was excluded due to moderate correlations with GDS total score and irritability, and “disinhibition” and “aberrant motor behavior” were dropped due to very low endorsement and conceptual overlap and correlation with “agitation.” Model fit indices, including Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), were used to compare LCA models with different variable sets. Finally, candidate variables were included based on conceptual relevance to NPS. The final set of variables was selected to maximize interpretability while maintaining a well‐fitting and parsimonious model and included the following 8 variables from the NPI‐Q and 5 variables from the GDS: delusions, hallucinations, agitation, anxiety, apathy, irritability, nighttime behaviors, appetite, activity levels, fearfulness, social withdrawal, happiness, and energy levels.

LCA models were fit ranging from two to five latent classes, and the optimal number of classes was determined based on fit indices, entropy, interpretability, and clinical relevance. Specific fit indices included: BIC, AIC, log‐likelihood, and entropy. Model comparison supported the four‐class solution as optimal. While AIC and BIC decreased with additional classes, improvement beyond the four‐class model was small (i.e., “elbow” showing diminishing returns after four classes). Entropy was highest for the three‐class model (0.737), remained high in the four‐class model (0.73), and dropped in the five‐class model (0.69), indicated reduced classification certainty. Given favorable balance of fit, interpretability, and sample sizes of each class, we retained the four‐class model for subsequent analyses (see Table S3 for fit indices comparisons). 45 After selecting the optimal model (four classes), posterior class probabilities were examined to validate class membership and interpret class profiles. Participants were grouped to their most likely latent class based on their highest posterior probability (mean across classes = 82%).

We fit a Cox proportional hazards model to examine progression to a combined designation of MCI or dementia. The outcome variable was time to progression, defined as the number of years since baseline visit until their first observed transition to consensus diagnosis of MCI or dementia for those who did not revert to a cognitively unimpaired status. The event variable indicated whether a participant progressed to MCI or dementia. Survival time was calculated at the number of years from baseline to the first MCI/dementia diagnosis. Participants with no follow‐up data were treated as censored, and those who remained cognitively unimpaired for their duration of follow‐up were censored at their last visit. LCA‐derived phenotype was included as the primary predictor, with additional covariates for age, sex, years of education, and history of or current psychiatric diagnoses. The model was fitted using the survival package in R, 46 with hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals used to interpret the relative risk of progression across phenotypes. Given that NPS phenotype violated the proportional hazards assumption, we fit a second model incorporating a time interaction term which showed consistent directionality of effects. A third model was fit using stabilized inverse probability of censoring weights (IPCW) to account for differential follow‐up. IPCW weights were derived from a logistic regression model predicting censoring status from covariates. The IPCW model produced hazard ratio estimates consistent with the unweighted model, suggesting that censoring did not substantially bias the observed associations. However, the time‐interaction model and IPCW model showed a higher AIC values, therefore, we retained the unweighted model as the primary analysis.

For cognitive outcomes, we used four mixed effects models using the nlme package in R, 47 with LCA‐derived phenotype as the predictor and cognitive domain composite scores as the outcome variable. Each model included fixed effects for time (years since baseline visit), phenotype, and their interaction, as well as covariates including age at baseline, education, sex, and history of or current psychiatric diagnoses. We modeled a random intercept and random slope for time for each participant and all models used maximum likelihood estimation. To assess whether trajectories differed across classes, we performed Type‐III tests of the phenotype x time interaction term using Satterthwaite denominator degrees of freedom. To control multiplicity across outcomes, we applied Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) to the four omnibus p‐values and report adjusted values. All pairwise comparisons were conducted using estimated marginal means, with Tukey‐adjusted p‐values to account for multiple comparisons.

For neuropathology, we first examined whether NPS phenotype was associated with the presence of neuropathological diagnoses at autopsy using binary logistic regression to model the odds of each pathology as a function of NPS phenotype. For ordinal neuropathologic outcomes, we then ran ordinal logistic regression models with the phenotypes as the predictor and AD staging (Thal, Braak, CERAD, and ADNC score), atherosclerosis, and arteriosclerosis as the outcome variables. Covariates for all neuropathology models included age at first visit, time from first visit to death (years), and sex (male/female). All pairwise comparisons were conducted using estimated marginal means, with Tukey‐adjusted p‐values to account for multiple comparisons. Neuropathological analyses across models applied Benjamini–Hochberg FDR corrections to account for multiple comparisons. Given the number of comparisons, neuropathological results were interpreted as exploratory and intended to inform future hypothesis‐driven studies.

RESULTS

Participants

Participants were 20,599 individuals aged ≥ 50 (mean age = 70.8; standard deviation [SD] = 9.0; mean education = 16.24 [SD = 6.3]; 66% female; 77% White, 17% Black or African American, 3.1% Asian; 92% non‐Hispanic) who were CU at baseline. Results of the LCA identified four NPS phenotypes: (1) Low‐All (n = 14,650) with low endorsement across all aspects of the NPI‐Q and GDS; (2) High‐Depression (n = 3467) with high endorsement of GDS items; (3) High‐Agitation/Anxiety/Irritability (hAAI; n = 1932) with high endorsement of agitation, anxiety, and irritability items on the NPI‐Q; and (4) High‐All (n = 550) with consistently high endorsement across GDS and NPI‐Q (see Figure 1 and Table 1).

There were demographic differences across the four NPS phenotypes (see Table 1). The High‐All group was significantly younger than the three other phenotypes. The phenotypes also differed in years of education, as the Low‐All group had a higher level of education than the other phenotypes. The High‐All group had fewer women relative to the other phenotypes. The proportion of White participants was highest in the hAAI group. Progression to MCI/dementia

Of the 20,599 CU participants at baseline, 15,496 participants had at least a second visit with available data for cognitive status after an average of 6.1 years of follow‐up (SD = 4.4, range from 0.3 to 18.8 years). Of the 15,496 participants, 3662 (24%) progressed to a final consensus diagnosis of Impaired, not MCI (n = 578, 16%), MCI (n = 1882, 51%), or dementia (n = 1202, 33%) after a mean 7.52 years of follow‐up (SD = 4.5, range = 0.6–18.8 years).

The Cox proportional hazards model included the 20,599 cognitively unimpaired participants, of whom 3084 (15%) progressed to MCI or dementia over follow‐up. The model demonstrated good predictive accuracy (Concordance = 0.7), with significant effects for NPS phenotype. Relative to the Low‐All group, the High‐All group had the greatest risk (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.48, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.01, 3.07, p < 0.001), followed by the hAAI group (HR = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.59, 2.00, p < 0.001), and then the High‐Depression group (HR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.28, 1.55, p < 0.001). Relative to the High‐Depression group, the High‐All group (HR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.48, 2.07, p < 0.001) and hAAI group (HR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.08, 1.47, p < 0.01) had higher risk. Finally, relative to the hAAI group, the High‐All group showed higher risk of progression (HR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.15, 1.74, p = 0.02). Covariates demonstrated expected associations such that higher age at baseline was associated with increased risk of progression (HR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.08, 1.09, p < 0.001), while female sex was associated with a significantly reduced risk (HR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.83, 0.96, p < 0.01. History of or current psychiatric conditions was also associated with increased risk (HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.11, 1.34, p < 0.001).

To examine whether the effect of NPS phenotypes on risk of progression changed over time, the Cox model with time interactions was fit and showed that risk associated with each NPS phenotype increased over time. Compared to the Low‐All group, the High‐All group showed the greatest change in risk of progression to MCI or dementia over time (HR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.42, 1.86, p < 0.001), followed by hAAI (HR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.29, 1.47, p < 0.001), and then High‐Depression (HR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.14, 1.28, p < 0.001). When we re‐ran the Cox model under three alternative assumptions when (1) participants with “impaired, not MCI” were included as progressors; (2) when participants with “impaired, not MCI” were excluded from the sample; and (3) when participants with any active/recent psychiatric diagnoses were excluded from the sample, estimates remained significant and consistent (see Table S5 for full results and Figure S1 for Kaplan–Meier curves for Alternate Model 3).

Kaplan–Meier curves depicting rate of progression to MCI/dementia over time by NPS phenotype are shown in Figure 2. A log‐rank test revealed significant group differences in survival curves (χ 2 = 6380, df = 7, p < 0.001), indicating that progression rates differed across NPS phenotypes.

Clinical diagnoses and etiology

Of the 15,496 participants with at least a second follow‐up visit, 11,764 (76%) participants had a diagnosis of “normal cognition” at their last available visit. NPS phenotypes and cognitive composites

NPS phenotype x time interactions were significant for all cognitive domain composites after FDR correction, indicating differential trajectories across NPS phenotypes (Memory: F(3, 11,273) = 3.58, p = 0.01; attention: F(3, 10,051) = 7.41, p < 0.001; executive function: F(3, 9,779) = 7.62, p < 0.001; and language: F(3, 10,599) = 5.38, p = 0.001).

We found that baseline memory composite score significantly differed across NPS phenotypes. Compared to the Low‐All group, the hAAI (β = −0.12, 95% CI: −0.16, −0.07, p < 0.001) and High‐All (β = −0.23, 95% CI: −0.32, −0.15, p < 0.001) groups had lower baseline memory scores. The High‐All (β = −0.19, 95% CI: −0.28, −0.1, p < 0.001) and hAAI (β = −0.07, 95% CI: −0.13, −0.02, p = 0.04) groups also had lower baseline scores compared to the High‐Depression group. Longitudinally, memory scores showed greater practice effects over time (β = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.05, 0.06, p < 0.001), but the trajectory differed by phenotype. Compared to Low‐All, the hAAI (β = −0.01, 95% CI: −0.03, −0.001, p = 0.01) and High‐All (β = −0.03, 95% CI: −0.05, −0.001, p = 0.01) groups showed significantly slower increase in memory scores/practice effects over 6 years. The High‐All group had the lowest baseline memory performance and least change (i.e., increase in scores due to practice) over time (see Figure 3A).

NPS phenotypes and neuropathology

The pathology models are considered exploratory due to the smaller sample size and large number of neuropathology outcomes. While several within‐outcome significant findings survived adjustment for multiple comparisons of the different phenotypes, these results did not survive adjustment for the multiple neuropathology models that were explored. Thus, findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating. Across binary logistic regression models, NPS phenotypes were not significantly associated with odds of presence/absence of AD, arteriosclerosis, atherosclerosis, CAA, or hippocampal sclerosis, nor significant differences across classes in the odds of FTLD‐Tau, FTLD‐TDP, or LATE. While the association did not remain significant after adjusting for multiple comparisons, the High‐Depression phenotype had higher odds of Lewy body pathology in the brainstem only (OR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.09, 2.94, p = 0.02). No other phenotype was significantly associated with Lewy body pathology subtypes. However, the High‐All group showed a higher proportion of limbic/neocortical Lewy body pathology (19%) relative to other classes, though this was not statistically significant.

Across ordinal logistic regression models predicting AD neuropathological burden (Thal phase, Braak, CERAD, and ADNC), we found the hAAI phenotype was associated with higher Thal phase (OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.03, 2.34, p = 0.03) and CERAD score (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.07, 2.24, p = 0.02) relative to the Low‐All group. Pairwise comparisons showed significantly higher CERAD scores in hAAI compared to High‐Depression (p = 0.03). The proportional odds assumption was violated for the Thal model, particularly for hAAI and High‐All phenotypes. However, a sensitivity analysis using a generalized ordered logit model revealed that hAAI was most strongly associated with advanced Thal stages. Demographic covariates such as older age at initial visit and time from visit to death were consistently associated with greater Thal and Braak stages, while longer disease duration and female sex were associated with higher Braak, CERAD, and ADNC scores.

Across ordinal logistic regression models examining vascular pathology, the hAAI phenotype was associated with greater arteriosclerosis severity (OR = 1.90, 95% CI: 1.31, 2.69, p < 0.001) compared to the Low‐All group and High‐Depression group (OR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.22, 2.75, p = 0.02). There were no associations between NPS phenotype and atherosclerosis. Older age at baseline and longer time from initial visit to death were also associated with greater arteriosclerosis and atherosclerosis.

DISCUSSION

Using data‐driven analyses, we identified four distinct NPS profiles that were differentially associated with risk of progression to MCI/dementia, cognitive performance, and neuropathologic outcomes. The current study provides evidence that early‐emerging patterns of NPS may be indicative of increased risk for clinical progression. Across nearly all analyses, individuals who endorsed NPS across multiple neuropsychiatric domains exhibited the most rapid progression, with nearly twice the likelihood of developing MCI or dementia, as well as worse baseline and longitudinal cognitive performance. Generally, the hAAI phenotype showed the highest odds of more advanced neuropathological markers, including greater amyloid spread and neuritic plaque burden. While the High‐All findings reinforce the link between overall NPS burden and disease progression, the emergence of intermediate phenotypes — hAAI and High‐Depression — suggests that specific symptom patterns, not just total symptom burden, also convey prognostic clinical and neuropathological significance.

The differential risks of clinical progression across early‐emerging NPS phenotypes support the potential utility of NPS‐based screening to identify individuals at heightened risk for MCI or dementia. Our four phenotypes closely mirrors a prior NACC study using LCA on NPI‐Q items within a CU sample, where the “complex” group – similar to our High‐All phenotype‐ also showed the highest risk. 48 However, while that study found the second‐highest risk in the High‐Depression group, we observed greater risk in the hAAI group. Methodological differences may explain these discrepancies. For instance, their depression group included agitation, nighttime behaviors, and motor symptoms, whereas our High‐Depression group was derived from GDS items, capturing more specific depressive features, with agitation clustering into our hAAI group. Despite these differences, our findings align with broader research, 49 , 50 indicating that agitation, anxiety, and irritability symptoms may be stronger predictors of clinical progression than depressive symptoms alone.

Emerging frameworks like MBI highlight that later‐life onset of NPS, such as agitation and irritability, may serve as early behavioral indicators of underlying neurodegenerative processes. 18 Studies have shown that symptoms such as agitation and irritability are associated with an increased risk of developing MCI and dementia, independent of baseline cognitive status. 51 Growing research also suggests that there may be distinct neurobiological mechanisms underlying NPS‐driven cognitive decline that relates to agitation and irritability in particular. For example, neuroimaging studies associate agitation and anxiety with structural and metabolic alteration in AD‐related regions such as the medial temporal lobe, prefrontal cortex, and anterior cingulate cortex. 52 , 53 Overall, our findings support this literature, suggesting that NPS phenotypes characterized by agitation, anxiety, and irritability may reflect early behavioral markers of disease vulnerability and confer elevated progression risk.

We also found that NPS phenotypes were associated with cognitive performance across memory, attention, executive functioning, and language domains. As expected, the High‐All phenotype showed lower baseline scores across all domains and less improvement/greater decline in memory, executive functioning, and language over time. Interestingly, the hAAI phenotype showed a similar pattern, with lower baseline memory, attention, and executive function, and slower gains in memory and attention, despite endorsing fewer total symptoms than the High‐All group. It is possible that the salience of certain symptoms, such as the behavioral dysregulation with agitation and irritability, are early markers of cognitive vulnerability.

Consistent with the broader literature, 54 the High‐Depression group showed lower baseline attention and executive functioning scores and less improvement/greater decline in attention, executive functioning, and language over time compared to the Low‐All group. However, the milder cognitive effects in this group relative to hAAI and High‐All suggest that not all NPS confer the same degree of cognitive risk or follow the same trajectory. Together, these findings reinforce that specific early NPS patterns are not only linked to increased risk of clinical progression but also to distinct baseline and cognitive trajectories.

When examining NPS phenotypes and neuropathologic outcomes, we found the hAAI phenotype was associated with higher Thal phase and CERAD neuritic plaque scores, suggesting that symptoms such as agitation, anxiety, and irritability may signal greater underlying amyloid burden. These findings align with our clinical and cognitive results and extend MBI literature linking behavioral dysregulation to dementia risk, particularly in relation to amyloid‐related changes. Of note, these findings warrant further replication and should be interpreted cautiously given their exploratory nature and the number of comparisons conducted. We did not observe significant associations between the High‐Depression phenotype and AD pathology. While depressive symptoms have been linked to cognitive decline, especially in attention and executive domains, they may reflect non‐AD processes, such as vascular contributions. 55 While no significant associations emerged between the High‐Depression phenotype and arteriosclerosis or atherosclerosis burden, descriptive proportions suggest possible vascular involvement. Notably, the hAAI phenotype showed greater arteriosclerosis (but not atherosclerosis), potentially reflecting a stronger link to microvascular rather than large‐vessel disease, and a greater likelihood of comorbid AD and vascular pathology, a frequent co‐occurrence within older adults. 56 However, these results are limited by the relatively low prevalence of moderate‐severe vascular pathology in our sample, limited power, and lack of specific delineation of comorbid pathologies.

The current study included multiple key strengths. First, we employed a data‐driven approach to derive NPS phenotypes, minimizing a priori assumptions and allowing natural symptom clusters to emerge. Second, we leveraged a large, well‐characterized longitudinal cohort and used multiple measures to assess NPS, enhancing both statistical power and generalizability. Finally, while prior studies have also focused on CU individuals and progression to MCI, we examined a wider range of outcomes, including cognitive trajectories, clinical diagnoses, and neuropathologic markers, providing a comprehensive picture of early NPS relevance across disease.

Several limitations should be considered. Although our cohort was large, it consisted mostly of highly educated, White participants, which may limit generalizability. NPS rates, LCA profile distributions, and progression patterns may differ in more diverse or community‐based populations, where comorbid neuropathologies are more prevalent. 57 Small sample sizes for some neuropathologic outcomes reduced power to detect subtle associations. For example, observed differences in hippocampal sclerosis odds across classes (e.g., lower in High‐Depression, higher in High‐All) may reflect neurobiological differences that warrant further study. While we modeled longitudinal cognitive trajectories, it remains unclear whether NPS drive cognitive decline or reflect early cognitive changes, particularly given baseline cognitive differences across phenotypes. Future work using dual‐change models may clarify these temporal dynamics. Further, psychiatric history was included as a covariate, but medication use was not, due to limited data on indication. Given the pharmacological heterogeneity of psychiatric medications (and other classes of drugs used to treat psychiatric symptoms), future research is needed to disentangle the effects of psychiatric treatment on risk for clinical progression, ideally using more detailed data on medication class, indication, dosage, and duration. Additionally, we relied on presence/absence of NPI‐Q items—further work including severity and frequency may yield additional insights. Low base rates of psychotic symptoms (i.e., delusions and hallucinations) in this cognitively unimpaired sample likely precluded detection of a distinct psychosis phenotype. Studies in more clinically symptomatic cohorts may be better positioned to identify a phenotype that includes higher rates of psychosis symptoms. We also combined informant‐rated (NPI‐Q) and self‐reported (GDS) symptom measures. While these measures are complementary, commonly used, and studies have shown that self‐ and informant‐rated measures are correlated and both associated with progression to MCI, 58 this approach may have introduced bias due to differing measurement sources. Finally, we lacked harmonized data to examine CSF or plasma AD biomarker data and power to fully examine comorbid versus isolated neuropathologies. Future work integrating longitudinal fluid/imaging biomarkers with early symptom profiles will be critical to clarifying the biological mechanisms underlying distinct NPS patterns.

Together, the current findings underscore the importance of identifying early NPS phenotypes. While neuropsychological evaluations are valuable, they can be time‐ and resource‐intensive. NPS measures offer a scalable and accessible approach to identify heightened risk for clinical progression. Our results also have implications for diagnostic constructs such as MBI, suggesting that the pattern of symptoms may carry prognostic value beyond total symptom burden. Although we did not explicitly model MBI, our findings are complementary to the MBI framework, which takes a theory‐driven approach to characterizing risk‐related symptoms. In contrast, our data‐driven approach highlights the prognostic value of symptom patterns irrespective of predefined domains. These results identify naturally co‐occurring symptom patterns, suggesting that specific NPS phenotypes may convey prognostic value beyond total symptom burden, and provide insight into how symptom heterogeneity maps onto clinical and pathological outcomes. As MBI continues to garner clinical and research attention, incorporating data‐driven approaches to identify high‐risk symptom phenotypes could enhance its clinical utility by informing personalized risk stratification and early intervention strategies.

Ultimately, the emergence of distinct NPS phenotypes could serve as early behavioral signals of disease, even before cognitive decline becomes detectable through cognitive testing and could complement traditional risk indicators (i.e., cognition, biomarkers) to identify individuals at risk for progression. Brief symptom measures, similar to those used in MBI frameworks, could be used to stratify risk and personalize follow‐up care, especially if integrated with biomarkers, APOE genotype, and cognitive test results. For example, individuals with elevated agitation, anxiety, and irritability may warrant more frequent neuropsychological monitoring, even if cognitively unimpaired at baseline. In addition to informing early risk stratification, identifying specific NPS phenotypes may help tailor caregiver education by clarifying symptom expectations, guiding behavioral management strategies, and facilitating proactive care planning. Overall, the ability to identify and detect early‐emerging NPS patterns suggests that targeted interventions for high‐risk individuals may offer a promising avenue to target to delay or slow progression to dementia, or at the very least, a useful prognostic tool to determine risk for clinical progression and specific neuropathologic outcomes.
