Determination of elements in cathode part of rechargeable lithium ion laptop and primary coin cell batteries using ICP-OES and XRF techniques
Authors: Mohammed M. Shukr, Kamal M. Mahmoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) depend on precise elemental composition determination in their cathode materials to maximize electrochemical performance, guarantee material consistency, and facilitate recycling compliance. This work provides a comparison of two techniques for evaluation of elements in LIB cathodes using X-ray fluorescence (XRF) and inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES). The aim of this study is analyzing the cathode part to determine its composition, so 22 samples of (brand new and totally discharged (expired)) primary coin cell and rechargeable lithium ion batteries, which were collected from local shops, and their cathode parts were prepared, and various elements were determined using XRF and ICP-OES. According to the results, concentrations of Li, Ni, Co, and Mn detected by ICP-OES were greater than 10 000 ppm, whereas concentrations of Co, Ni, and Mn detected by XRF were also greater than 10 000 ppm. And some elements were in lower concentrations and some of them weren't detected by both devices. The results revealed a significant difference in cathode elemental composition, which may be related to the energy capacity and manufacturing year.

Introduction

Li-ion batteries have become widely used in recent years because of their high energy density, comparatively high working voltage (up to 4.7 V), and good long-term stability.1 Lithium, a soft, silver-white metal, has been the subject of increasing attention in recent years due to its versatile applications and growing importance in the global energy landscape. As the lightest metal in the periodic table, lithium is an indispensable component in the production of lithium-ion batteries, which are widely used in electric vehicles, portable electronics, and energy storage systems. The rising demand for lithium has been driven by the surge in the development of electric vehicles and the need for efficient energy storage solutions to support the transition towards renewable energy sources.2 The global demand for lithium has been steadily rising, driven by the rapid expansion of the electric vehicle industry and the growing need for efficient energy storage solutions.3 The evolution of battery technology can be traced back to the early 19th century when Alessandro Volta created the first true battery, known as the “voltaic pile.” Over the past two centuries, batteries have undergone remarkable advancements, evolving from early technologies to the modern lithium-ion cells that power our laptops and other portable devices.4 Lithium-ion batteries have become the dominant energy storage technology in a wide range of applications, from portable electronics to electric vehicles and grid-scale energy storage systems. The commercialization of LIBs in the 1990s has revolutionized various industries, enabling the rise of smartphones, laptops, and other portable devices that have transformed our daily lives.5 These batteries are composed of an anode, a lithium-based cathode, and an electrolyte that facilitates the flow of lithium ions between the two electrodes during charging and discharging processes. The increasing demand for energy storage has driven significant research and development efforts to improve the performance of lithium-ion batteries. Researchers are exploring various strategies to enhance the safety, cost, energy density, and cycle life of these batteries, paving the way for their widespread adoption in a variety of applications.6 LIBs have become the predominant power source for a wide range of electronic devices, from portable electronics to electric vehicles, due to their high energy density, long cycle life, and relatively low cost. The cathode of a LIB is typically made of a layered metal oxide material, such as lithium cobalt oxide, lithium nickel manganese cobalt oxide, or lithium iron phosphate.7 Currently available commercial cathodes include LiCoO2 (LCO), LiNixMnyCozO2 (NMC, x + y + z = 1), LiNi0.8Co0.15Al0.05O2 (NCA), and LiFePO4 (LFP).8 These materials are capable of reversibly intercalating and deintercalation lithium ions during the charging and discharging processes.9 The anode is usually made of graphite, which also allows for the reversible storage and release of lithium ions. The electrolyte, which facilitates the transport of lithium ions between the anode and cathode, is typically a non-aqueous solution containing a lithium salt, such as lithium hexafluorophosphate, dissolved in an organic solvent, like ethylene carbonate or dimethyl carbonate.10 The separator, a microporous membrane, physically separates the anode and cathode to prevent short circuits while allowing the passage of lithium ions. Researchers have explored various approaches to address these challenges, including the development of solid polymer electrolytes and the use of alternative materials for the cathode and anode.11 Coin cell batteries, also known as button cells, are a type of compact, low-profile battery commonly used in a wide range of electronic devices, from wearables and remote controls to watches and hearing aids. These batteries are renowned for their small size, high energy density, and long shelf life, making them an essential component in many modern technologies. The development of coin cell batteries can be traced back to the early days of electrochemical energy storage, with the invention of the voltaic pile in the late 18th century marking a significant milestone in the history of battery technology.12 Coin cell batteries typically consist of two electrodes, an anode and a cathode, separated by an electrolyte solution. The specific chemistries used in these batteries can vary, with common examples including lithium-ion, silver-oxide, and zinc–air configurations. Each chemistry offers unique advantages, such as high energy density, long shelf life, or low cost, depending on the intended use case.13 As the demand for portable electronics and wearable devices continues to grow, the importance of coin cell batteries as a reliable and efficient power source is expected to increase. These compact and versatile energy storage solutions are poised to play a crucial role in shaping the future of modern technology.14

The aim of this research is to determine the elemental composition of the cathode part of rechargeable LIBs (laptop) and primary (coin cell) batteries, both in brand new and fully discharged (expired) states, using different techniques.

Methodology

Reagents, standard solutions and samples

Analytical grade reagents were all employed in current research. To prepare solutions for ICP-OES analysis and to dilute the sample, ultrapure deionized water was utilized. Digestion of samples was done by using HNO3 (69%, BIOCHEM. – France) and H2O2 (30%, BIOCHEM. – France). A multi elements (ICP 24E ROTI Star-Roth Germany) standard solution was used for construction of calibration graphs, the multi element standard solution contains (Al, B, Ba, Bi, Cd, Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, Ga, Mg, Mn, Na, Ni, Pb, Sr, Ti, Zn, Ca, K and Li).

Sampling

LIBs (laptop and coin cell) (n = 22) were collected from different shops in Erbil city during (April–July/2024). Samples were categorized according to capacity (mAh), status (brand new or expired), and date of manufacture as shown in Table 1. The batteries analyzed in this study were collected as brand new and expired or fully discharged end-of-life batteries. The classification of batteries was based on their end-of-life status rather than specific failure mechanisms. Therefore, the results represent the characteristics of discarded batteries collected from local shops and do not distinguish between batteries that reached end-of-life through different aging, cycling, or storage processes.

Cathode materials extracted from LIBs

To prevent short circuits, laptop and coin cell LIBs were fully depleted to eliminate any remaining capacity. Then, LIBs were manually disassembled into their fundamental parts, and these included the separator, cathode, anode, and housing (plastics and metal cover). Then cathode materials were separated from other parts and changed to powder by grinding, after that kept in suitable cups for sample preparation for analysis.

Cathode material of LIBs

In this work, totally discharged (expired) and new rechargeable lithium-ion laptop and primary coin cell batteries served as the raw material. The batteries were obtained from local shops. But manufacturer details, such as the precise cathode chemistry, were unavailable. As a result, it was impossible to determine the cathode composition from the product documentation. According to previous work, the relative abundances of Li, Co, Ni, Mn, Fe, and Al can frequently be used to estimate cathode chemistry.15 LiCoO2 based batteries, for instance, are distinguished by the predominance of Co, while NMC materials have notable concentrations of Ni, Mn, and Co, and NCA materials have Ni, Co, and Al.16 As a result, readers can evaluate the predicted cathode chemistry and compare the outcomes with comparable battery systems using the elemental composition data presented in the tables and texts.

Sample preparation

ICP-OES

0.3 g of prepared powder was weighed accurately and transferred into a high pressure digestion tube, added with 12 mL of concentrated HNO3 and 2 mL of 30% H2O2. The tube was slightly shaken to make complete contact of reagent and sample, then covered with the lid and fixed into microwave digestion device, then sample was digested with microwave device. After cooling, the digested solution was transferred into beaker and heated up on electric hot plate at 160 °C until it evaporated to near dryness. The digested solution was then transferred into a 25 mL volumetric flask and diluted to the scale with water then determined by ICP-OES.17

XRF

All types of batteries were discharged, then the batteries were dismantled and physically separated and the active material of cathode part were collected and tested for uniformity and mixed well. Then 2.0 g of uniform powder was taken for analysis.

Determination of elements by XRF

The amounts of (Al, B, Ba, Bi, Cd, Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, Ga, Mg, Mn, Na, Ni, Pb, Sr, Ti, Zn, Ca, K and Li) in the cathode material from each LIB were measured with using portable Genius 5000 XRF spectrometer for each element. The time needed for a single measurement was 60 s and the average value of multiple measurements was taken.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25. Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize the characteristics of the study population and were reported as (mean ± standard error of mean (SED)), or frequency (%). Normality test was done for the data by using Shapiro–Wilk. Differences between groups were evaluated using the paired-sample t-test, independent-samples t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, Chi-square test, Kruskal–Wallis or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) based on the type and distribution of the data. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed, where p-value < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Determination of elements in lithium ion laptop batteries by ICP-OES

Different types of brand new and expired rechargeable laptop batteries and primary coin cell batteries as shown in Table 1 were analyzed to examine the chemical composition of cathode materials of batteries according to capacity, year of manufacture, status and brands. The results obtained from ICP-OES in this study are shown in ppm in Tables S1 and S2, but21 were expressed the results as weight percentage (wt%). The concentration of Li, Ni, Co and Mn were determined in this work in a significant ratio (which is more than 10 000 ppm) equivalent to (1.0%), while21 found in a ratio which is more than (1.0 wt%) equivalent to (10 000) ppm, a bar diagram was drawn for these elements as shown in Fig. 1 to express the chemical constituents of the samples which worked on them in this study. The reported Li concentrations (25 000–65 000 ppm) match the amount of lithium found in commercial cathode materials for lithium-ion batteries. About (40 000–70 000) PPM of Li+, is present in typical cathode chemistries. LiFePO4, has about 44 000 ppm of Li+, whereas LiCoO2 and NMC materials have about (60 000–70 000) ppm of Li+. Consequently, the concentrations reported in this study are supported by literature16,22 and are within the predicted range for lithium-ion battery materials. It is crucial to note that by understanding the element concentrations, one may either help prevent incorrect use of this material or recommend the best recycling method based on the element of interest.23Fig. 1 is already distributing samples according to concentration of Li, Ni, Co and Mn. The variation in Ni concentration among different expired rechargeable laptop batteries is likely due to differences in cathode material composition. Batteries with higher Ni content are typically based on Ni-rich layered oxides (e.g., NMC or NCA), which are used to improve energy density and electrochemical performance, whereas other chemistries contain lower Ni proportions. For brand new laptop batteries, the same distribution of concentration of Ni among them was seen. The results in Fig. 1 observed that concentration of Mn in expired battery samples is close to each other, while in brand new batteries same thing can be seen, the amount of concentration is close to each other. From the results, it can be noticed that the highest concentration of lithium ion among expired laptop batteries was calculated in T brand which has a capacity of (4200 mAh). While the lowest concentration of lithium ion was detected in H brand which has a capacity of (4100 mAh). But among brand new laptop batteries it is observed that the highest concentration of lithium ion was found in T with a capacity of (4200 mAh), and the lowest concentration of lithium ion was determined in S with a capacity of (3500 mAh). If the results compared in terms of concentration of Co, the T has the highest amount of Co among expired laptop batteries with capacity of (4200 mAh). But the minimum amount of Co was detected in L battery with a capacity of (2620 mAh). For brand new batteries it is observed that the highest concentration of Co was found in H with a capacity of (4100 mAh) and the lowest concentration of Co was found in L battery which has a capacity of (2620 mAh). When the samples were compared, we can see expired ones have the lowest level and new ones have the highest concentration levels of Li, Mn, Ni and Co. This demonstrates the capability of companies to develop new rechargeable lithium-ion batteries with improved performance and higher power capacity. Except Li, Ni, Co and Mn, different elements were also determined by ICP-OES in both expired and brand-new batteries which have concentrations lower than (10 000) ppm, elements are (Al, Ca, Cr, Cu, Fe, K, Mg, Na, Pb, Ti, and Zn). But elements like (B and Ba) haven't detected among the whole samples. In previous decades, LiCoO2 was frequently employed as cathode active material (CAM). It is made up of low weight percent of Li and high weight percent Co. As a result, the expired and brand new S, H, T and D cathode's elemental makeup were like that of LiCoO2. Because of its high Co concentration and that of a few other elements, the LIB is desirable for recycling and reuse. Studies have documented the recycling of LIBs by using cobalt in photocatalytic processes and reusing it in material manufacturing.24,25 The metals that were substituted for Co disrupted the batteries' ability to store energy. According to the literature,26 it is economically advantageous to replace a portion of the Co with Ni and Mn to create Li(NiCoMn)O2 (NCM) because industrial production costs are low and the commercial values of Ni and Mn are approximately three and eighteen times lower than the value of Co, respectively. As a result, they have a lower energetic capacity than non-substituted lithium cobalt oxide, as seen when compared to other materials with a higher energy capacity. The results clearly showed that L is obeying this characterizations. The aluminum levels in expired and brand new H, D, and L batteries were greater than expired and brand new S and T batteries. Since it was challenging to scrape the cathode of L batteries from the aluminum foil (current collector), the variation may be the result of contamination during cathode separation. Therefore, variations in the manufacturing process and/or the polymer that is employed to link the components may be the reason for this difference in the binding strength between cathode and current collector. Because the aluminum current collector dissolved during the leaching or processing stages, aluminum can be found in the cathode samples.21 The metals Cr, Cu, Mg, Ti, and Zn were detected in very low quantities, according to the values in Tables S4 and S5. These elements are only present as small constituents, shown by the relatively low measured amounts in the current investigation. These metals, which usually come from battery casing materials, current collectors, additives, or external contamination during handling and production, are regarded as impurity elements since they are not essential components of rechargeable lithium-ion batteries' active materials. Previously, a study was conducted by ref. 27, and the concentrations (Cr = 8.19, Mg = 119.54, Ti = 228.11, and Zn = 97.98) PPM were determined as impurities in cathode part of rechargeable lithium ion batteries. The amount of these elements were very low as compared to Co, in this case Co can be substituted by small quantities of these elements in order to produce the mixed oxides as CAM, LiNi0.7Co0.2M0.005Mg0.005O2 (M can be Ti or Al), LiMn1.5Ni0.42Zn0.08O4 and Li1+xMyMn2−x−yO4 (where M is Al or Cr).28–30

Determination of elements in primary coin cell batteries by ICP-OES

It is observed from the results which gained from ICP-OES determination of (Al, B, Ba, Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, Mg, Mn, Na, Ni, Pb, Ti, Zn, Ca, K and Li) in primary coin cell batteries, the concentration of these elements is completely different from their ratio in rechargeable lithium ion laptop batteries. In this study the amount of these elements in two samples of the same brand, which is P, with a capacity of (220 mAh) and manufactured in 2023 were determined. One of them is brand new and the other one has expired. Table S3 shows the amount of these elements in ppm. The results show that both samples are dominated by Mn, indicating a Mn-based cathode chemistry in the coin cell batteries. Trace elements such as Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, Na, Ni, K, and Ca were also detected, which are likely associated with residual electrolyte species, manufacturing-related impurities, or contamination from battery components such as current collectors and casing materials during cell assembly or operation. According to the result, the highest concentration of Mn was detected in both coin cell, which is a sign of that the cathode part of coin cell lithium batteries was made of electrolytic manganese dioxide (EMD). The capacity of the coin cell battery depends on EMD. These results approve that the isolation and analysis of cathode part was done successfully. Coin cell lithium batteries are known as primary batteries, that's why their anode is forming from lithium, cathode from manganese and contains an electrolyte in the form of salt. So, the data shows that the concentration of Li was determined in expired coin cell battery, but in brand new one was not detected, finding Li ion concentrations in cathode part; means the infiltration of lithium from electrolyte part into cathodic part through porous membranes of cathode. Li+ ion was detected in the expired coin cell battery, whereas it was not observed in the brand-new cell. This is likely to be due to the absence of electrolyte decomposition or ion migration processes that occur during battery aging and operation. Detection of different types of transition metals like Cu, Cr, Co, Fe, and Ni in cathode part of these coin cell batteries are notable. These metals are not intentionally used in the manufacturing of coin cell lithium batteries; however, they may be introduced into the cathode material from various sources, including impurities in raw materials, electrolyte decomposition products, contamination during manufacturing processes, and migration from other cell components such as current collectors during operation. The results show that the concentration of B, Ba, Mg, Pb, Ti and Zn haven't determined in both types of samples.

Determination of elements in primary coin cell batteries using XRF device

The XRF was used for analyzing expired and brand new P coin cells with capacities of (220) mAh, and both were manufactured in 2023. The results revealed that there were notable differences in elemental composition of these coin cell batteries, which can be related to the usage of batteries through time. The concentrations of elements are showing in Table S6. The most important element in coin cell which makes cathode part is Mn, which is in the form of MnO2. The results show a decrease in the amount in Mn in expired batteries as compared to brand new ones, this decrease in concentration may be return to the partial consumption of Mn over time. Similarly, the expired coin cell batteries also contain lower quantities of Ni and Cd. Over time, these decreases may be the result of material migration, electrochemical deterioration, or even breakdown into the electrolyte. Low but comparatively constant levels of Fe, Co, and Cr are present in both samples. These elements come from alloy components, because the cases of coin cells produce from stainless steel. Traces of Cu and Ag may be come from production residues or conductive terminals. Determination of concentration of Pb in these samples is a concerning because Pb is not using in formation of these types of batteries. So, the presence of this element in these samples may be due to breakdown of internal components or migration of materials during long-term storage and use.31 Elements like (Ga, As, Sr, Sn, Sb, Ca, Ti and Zn) didn't detect in both types (new and expired) of coin cell batteries.

Comparison between ICP-OES and XRF techniques for determination of elements

This section is a comparative analysis of concentrations of elements (Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, Mn, Ni, Pb, Ti, Zn and K) which determined by both devices (ICP-OES and XRF) as shown in Tables S7, S8, S9 and S10. Comparison of expired and brand new rechargeable lithium ion laptop batteries showed that the concentrations of Co, Cu, Fe, Mn, Ni and Zn detected by ICP-OES were more than XRF devices. This is due to particle morphology or distribution of Co in the sample make Co not fully detectable by XRF device. Cu was also determined less in amount by XRF, the reason may be because the Cu is not uniformly distributed or embedded deeper, because XRF is a surface technique. For Fe, partial absorption or masking of X-ray signals of Fe in complex materials, such as battery electrodes (e.g., with high Li, Ni, Co, and Mn) makes concentration of Fe less by XRF. Since XRF detects elements in few microns of the upper part of sample, the distribution of elements in the sample can be the reason of this deficiency in concentration of Mn by XRF device. It can be seen lower concentrations of Ni by XRF as compared to ICP-OES in lithium ion batteries, the reason may be that the cathodes of LIBs contain Co and Mn beside Ni in their composition, so these complex matrices of the cathode may have bad impacts on the X-ray absorption or interfering the intensity of Ni, having high concentrations of these elements can interfere with signal of Ni and underestimation of Ni will occur. This significant differences in the concentrations of Zn refer to the composition of the cathode part of batteries like presence of other elements (Ni, Co, Mn, or Cu), which interfere with the main X-ray emission line of Zn and lower concentrations would be obtained. There was an acceptable degree of agreement between the two techniques for Cr. This suggests that Cr is more XRF-accessible or uniformly distributed in the samples, which makes it rather simple to quantify using any technique. It's interesting to note that Pb readings in every sample were largely consistent across techniques. This could be because Pb has a high atomic number and a strong X-ray emission line, which makes it easier to measure accurately even with XRF. Comparison of the results in terms of concertation of Ti, a reverse effect can be observed. Ti is finding in oxide form in cathode part of LIBs as stabilizer. Acid digestion may be a factor of getting lower concentrations of Ti by ICP-OES because it may not fully dissolve Ti. When there is no complete digestion, ICP-OES will underestimate the amount of Ti. For K, it can clearly observe that there is a poor correlation between two techniques. For some samples XRF was detected more than ICP-OES and in some samples ICP-OES found more amounts of K as compared to XRF. In both methods, the concentrations of most components were consistently greater in the new batteries, especially Co, Cu, Fe, Mn and Ni. ICP-OES and XRF devices were also used for analyzing lithium ion coin cell batteries, and concentrations of elements like (Co, Cr, Cu, Mn, Ni, Pb and K) were commonly determined by both techniques. The highest concentrations of Co were determined using XRF as compared to ICP-OES, as mentioned before, XRF is analyzing elements near to the surface. The concentration of Cr, which determined by XRF, was more than ICP-OES. Same reason for Cr, because Cr may present in stable forms and didn't dissolve completely with acid digestion, and XRF is finding all Cr contents in the samples. The concentrations of Cu determined by XRF were more as compared to ICP-OES. The results also show that the concentrations of Fe determined by XRF were more than ICP-OES, the reason may be that the XRF device analyzes the whole solid sample regardless to the type of Fe (oxide, phosphate, metallic, or other forms). The concentrations of Mn found by ICP-OES were more than XRF device. The reason behind this case of getting lower levels can be that elements that are frequently found in cathode materials, such as Co, Ni, or Fe, can suppress the Mn signal in XRF.32 The concentration of Ni, which was found by ICP-OES, was more than XRF device. Presence of elements like Co, Mn and Fe can have bad impact on the level of Ni in the samples using XRF because they suppress the X-ray emission of Ni, then XRF device reports underestimated concentration of Ni in the samples.33 Results show us the concentration of Pb was determined by XRF but not by ICP-OES device. The last content which determined by both devices was K, the concentration of K which detected by XRF was more than ICP-OES. XRF is a method that is sensitive to surface. So, K may be on the cathode's surface due to manufacture or electrolyte breakdown.

Rapid protocol for identifying unknown commercial batteries

A useful procedure for the fast identification of rechargeable LIBs and primary coin cell batteries can be suggested based on the findings in this study. The battery should first be properly discharged and disassembled in accordance with recommended safety protocols. The chosen analytical method is then used to do elemental analysis on the cathode material. The primary transition metals and distinctive elements are then identified to assess the measured elemental composition. For instance, different ratios of Ni, Mn, and Co indicate multilayer NMC cathodes, Fe and P propose lithium iron phosphate (LFP), while Co-rich compositions indicate lithium cobalt oxide (LCO). Normality test

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine normality test for the findings. The proper nonparametric statistical tests were used to investigate variables which were considered non-normally distributed and had a p-value of less than 0.05.

Non-parametric tests

Independent-samples Mann–Whitney U test according to status

To find out if the concentrations were differed between the expired and new batteries, an independent-samples Mann–Whitney U test was used. There was no statistically significant difference between the groups, according to the analysis (U = 18 497.5, p > 0.05). This result implies that the expired and new groups had comparable elemental concentration distributions. Consequently, there was no evidence to support the prediction that the samples' state (new versus expired) would affect concentration. The absence of a statistically significant difference could mean that the variable being studied was not significantly impacted by expiry status. Fig. S2 shows the results.

Independent-samples Mann–Whitney U test according to type of batteries

To compare the concentrations of elements between rechargeable laptop batteries and primary coin cell batteries, an independent samples Mann–Whitney U test was used. The findings showed that there is a statistically high significant difference between the two battery groups (U = 1639, p < 0.05). Fig. S3 illustrates the findings. The results indicate that there were notable differences in the distribution of concentration of elements between rechargeable laptop and primary coin cell batteries. Compared to coin cell batteries (n = 30), a much higher number of concentration of elements were found in laptop batteries (n = 400), suggesting that battery type may have a significant impact on the observed results. As a result, the study's hypothesis that concentration of elements differs between laptop and coin cell batteries was validated. Variations in battery design, capacity and usage conditions between the two battery types could be the cause of the observed difference. These findings emphasize how crucial it is to take battery category into account when analyzing the concentration of elements.

Independent-samples Mann–Whitney U test according to the devices

To determine if the measurements from ICP-OES and XRF differed significantly, the independent-samples Mann–Whitney U test was performed. Because it is a non-parametric data and does not obey the assumption of normality, the Mann–Whitney U test was chosen. This test uses ranked data to compare the distribution of observations between two independent groups. The findings in Fig. S4 show that there was no statistically significant difference (U = 22 082 and p > 0.05) between the measurements from the two devices. Consequently, it was not possible to reject the null hypothesis, which asserts that the distributions of measurements generated by the two devices are identical. This result implies that the two instruments produced similar analytical outcomes for the samples analyzed. Different analytical principles are using by ICP-OES and XRF. ICP-OES, which uses optical emission spectra produced in a high-temperature plasma to quantify elements, is widely regarded as a highly sensitive laboratory-based method that necessitates sample digestion. On the other hand, XRF is a quick and non-destructive method that uses the sample's unique fluorescence X-rays to establish its elemental makeup. The lack of a significant difference in the results suggests that both approaches produced reliable estimates of elemental concentrations within the sample set under study, despite these methodological variations. Furthermore, the sample size, concentration range, and variability of the detected concentration of elements could have affected the analysis's statistical power. A more thorough evaluation of the comparability of ICP-OES and XRF readings may be possible in future research with more samples and a broader concentration range.

Independent-samples Kruskal–Wallis test for concentrations across elements

To determine whether there were any notable variations in the concentrations of the examined elements, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. This non-parametric statistical approach was chosen because it is appropriate for comparing several independent groups when the data shows non-normality, and it does not require the assumption of a normal distribution. The findings of the analysis showed that the concentrations of the elements under investigation varied significantly (test statistics = 309.538a, degree of freedom = 9 and p < 0.001). As a result, the null hypothesis, that the concentration distributions of all elements are similar was rejected. The results showed that the concentration varied significantly across the elements examined, which indicates that the samples' elemental composition was not consistent, Table S18 shows pairwise comparison of elements. The natural variability of the samples is probably reflected in the range of elemental concentrations seen in this investigation. Since an element's abundance relies on a variety of factors, including its origin, mobility, chemical characteristics, and interactions with the environment, elements are rarely equally distributed within a substance. As a result, some elements are found at comparatively high concentrations and make up a significant portion of the sample matrix, whereas others are only found in trace amounts. These variations offer insight into the basic processes driving elemental distribution within the samples and contribute to the large statistical variation found by the Kruskal–Wallis test. At least one element may have shown concentration levels that were significantly different from those of other elements, according to the significant Kruskal–Wallis finding. Differences in elemental abundance, mineralogical composition, environmental factors, or analytical sensitivity could all contribute to this variation. For example, concentrations of major elements are often larger orders of magnitude than those of trace elements, resulting in distinct ordering patterns that affect the test's significant outcome.

Independent-samples Kruskal–Wallis test for concentration across brands

To find out if there were any notable variations in the elemental concentrations of the examined brands, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. This non-parametric test was chosen because it is appropriate for comparing several independent groups in situations where the homogeneity of variance and normality assumptions might not be met. The analysis showed a statistically significant variation in concentration between brands (test statistics = 45.292a, degree of freedom = 5 and p < 0.001), suggesting that the distribution of concentrations was inconsistent among the brands under study. At least one brand showed concentration levels that were significantly different from those of the other brands, according to the statistically significant result as shown in Table S19. As a result, the null hypothesis, that the concentration distributions of all brands are similar was rejected. These results show that the concentration levels of the examined elements may be significantly influenced by brand-related factors. Variations in raw material sources, production processes, processing strategies, and ingredient composition could be the cause of the observed discrepancies between brands. There can be significant fluctuation in elemental concentrations since products sold under different brands are frequently made using varying formulas and production standards. Additionally, because raw materials might include different quantities of elements, they may have an impact on the elemental profile of the finished product. The significant Kruskal–Wallis result shows that the measured concentrations were sufficiently different between brands to be statistically identified according to analytical perspectives. This result implies that the study's analytical techniques were able to detect significant variations in elemental composition and that the observed variability most likely represents real brand differences rather than just analytical uncertainty. The findings have significant implications for quality assessment and product evaluation. The existence of notable variations between brands suggests that customers can be exposed to different element concentrations based on the brand they choose.

Research limitations

The analysis was carried out on a limited number of battery types and did not cover a full disassembly-based characterization of all cell components, such as the anode, separator, and electrolyte. In addition, factors such as matrix effects, possible contamination during sample disassembly, and the limitation of XRF in detecting light elements like lithium may have affected the completeness of the elemental profile. A specific foil blank examination to measure possible elemental contributions from the collecting substrate was not included in this investigation. All samples were examined by XRF under the same conditions, allowing for meaningful relative comparisons across samples. These factors may introduce some uncertainty into the determination of absolute elemental concentrations. The present study was limited by the availability of battery samples, resulting in a relatively small number of specific categories of batteries.

Future work

Future work should focus on expanding the sample set to include a wider range of battery chemistry and manufacturers. Integrating ICP-OES and XRF with complementary analytical techniques, such as ICP-MS, XRD, and SEM-EDS, would provide more detailed elemental and structural information. In addition, the inclusion of specialized foil blank assays would help evaluate potential contributions from the current-collector substrate and further refine the analytical framework. These methodological enhancements would enable a more comprehensive characterization of elemental sources and distributions while increasing confidence in absolute concentration determinations. Furthermore, studies incorporating larger sample sizes and greater battery diversity would support more robust assessments and improve the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

ICP-OES and XRF were used to analyze the elemental composition of cathode materials in both totally discharged (expired) and brand-new rechargeable lithium-ion laptop and primary coin cell batteries with different characteristics, including energy capacity and manufacturing years. Although the samples were lithium-ion and manganese batteries, the results showed clear differences in their chemical compositions, reflecting variations in their cathode chemistries. Since detailed chemical compositions of lithium-ion batteries are not disclosed by manufacturers on product labels, techniques such as ICP-OES and XRF are essential for determining their elemental makeup.
