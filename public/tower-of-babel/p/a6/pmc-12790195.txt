Predominant Right Ventricular Dysfunction in Cardiac Sarcoidosis
Authors: Michael LaLoggia, Nikita Jhawar, Charbel Nakhoul, Anupam Kumar, Harry Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinically significant cardiac sarcoidosis is rare, seen in 5% of patients with sarcoidosis. Predominant right ventricular (RV) dysfunction is seen even less often.Case SummaryA 59-year-old man presented to the emergency department from his outpatient cardiologist's office with high-grade atrioventricular block and signs of right-sided heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiogram showed normal left ventricular systolic function, with severe RV enlargement and dysfunction. Right heart catheterization revealed mild postcapillary pulmonary hypertension and disproportionate RV dysfunction. A transbronchial biopsy of mediastinal lymph nodes showed noncaseating granulomas, consistent with sarcoidosis.DiscussionRV dysfunction in sarcoidosis is most often secondary to lung disease or left heart disease. In general, RV systolic dysfunction is associated with higher all-cause mortality in patients with sarcoidosis.Take-Home MessageIn patients with cardiac sarcoidosis and predominant RV dysfunction, clinical context along with multimodal imaging, particularly cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, can be useful to reach a diagnosis and provide prognostic value.Key words: cardiac sarcoidosis, right ventricular dysfunction, right heart catheterization, hemodynamics, multimodal imaging

History of Presentation

A 59-year-old man presented to the emergency department after being found with high-grade atrioventricular (AV) block during his outpatient cardiology appointment. He was initially referred for preoperative evaluation before endobronchial biopsy for suspected pulmonary sarcoidosis. The patient reported shortness of breath with minimal exertion and endorsed 2 syncopal episodes. Other associated symptoms included leg swelling, 20-pound weight gain over 6 months, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, and a productive cough with clear sputum. On arrival to the hospital, his blood pressure was 114/76 mm Hg and heart rate was 60 beats/min. Physical examination revealed bibasilar rales, elevated jugular venous pressure at 24 cm H2O, and 2+ bilateral pitting edema of the lower extremities.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnosis at this time included cardiac sarcoidosis (CS), decompensated heart failure, myocardial ischemia, electrolyte abnormalities, systemic lupus erythematous, Lyme disease, and valvular vegetations.

Past Medical History

The patient's past medical history was significant for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and obstructive sleep apnea.

Investigations

Laboratory testing showed N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide of 938 pg/mL (reference: 176-225 pg/mL) and high-sensitivity troponin T of 39 ng/L and 41 ng/L 1 hour later (reference: 0-19 ng/L). Chest radiograph showed pulmonary edema and cardiomegaly. Electrocardiogram showed a high-grade AV block, with a junctional escape rhythm at a rate of 60 beats/min with frequent premature ventricular complexes (Figure 1).

Computed tomography (CT) angiography with pulmonary artery protocol was negative for pulmonary embolism. Transthoracic echocardiogram showed a normal left ventricular cavity size, systolic function, and diastolic flattening of the interventricular septum. The right ventricle (RV) was severely dilated, with tricuspid annular dilation (Figure 2) leading to torrential tricuspid regurgitation (Video 1).

Transthoracic Echocardiogram Apical 4-Chamber View of Right Ventricular Dilation

Given the concern for active CS, urgent cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) was completed, with findings highly suggestive of CS: patchy late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) most prominent in the anterior and inferior septal RV insertions, with possible extension into the RV myocardium. RV systolic function was moderately reduced, with a calculated ejection fraction of 32% (Figures 3A to 3C).

Management

For the patient's high-grade AV block, a cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator (CRT-D) device was placed rather than a permanent pacemaker given CMR findings. He was concurrently treated with aggressive intravenous diuretics. Right heart catheterization was performed to further define his hemodynamic profile and stratify risk before endobronchial biopsy. Results revealed right atrial pressure of 18 mm Hg, RV pressure of 33/8 mm Hg, pulmonary artery pressure of 35/22 mm Hg, mean pulmonary artery pressure of 28 mm Hg, and an elevated pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 25 mm Hg. His transpulmonary gradient was 3 mm Hg, and his pulmonary vascular resistance was calculated at 0.2 Wood units. Despite an unremarkable transpulmonary gradient and low pulmonary vascular resistance, his pulmonary artery pulsatility index was depressed at 0.72. His Fick cardiac output and index were 6.1 L/min and 2.3 L/min/m2, respectively.

After several days of intravenous diuretics, the patient's heart failure symptoms improved. He was able to safely undergo endobronchial lymph node biopsy, which demonstrated noncaseating granulomas, consistent with a diagnosis of sarcoidosis. After multidisciplinary evaluation by the advanced heart failure and pulmonology teams, he was initiated on prednisone and methotrexate.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient tolerated immunosuppressive therapy well, with no recurrent hospitalizations for heart failure at 6 months. Subsequent device interrogations demonstrated no ventricular arrhythmias. A cardiac positron emission tomography (PET)/CT scan at 6 months showed no active CS.

Discussion

Cardiac sarcoidosis causes an infiltrative cardiomyopathy secondary to granulomatous inflammation. Clinically significant CS is rare, seen in only 5% of patients with sarcoidosis.1 The incidence of histologically confirmed CS remains significantly higher than the clinically detected rate, at 25%.1

RV dysfunction in sarcoidosis is also rare. In a cohort of 290 patients with biopsy-proven sarcoidosis who were undergoing evaluation for active CS, only 12% were found to have RV dysfunction.2 RV dysfunction in sarcoidosis is most commonly due to pulmonary hypertension (pHTN). Although the most common cause of pHTN in sarcoidosis is likely World Health Organization group III pHTN, sarcoidosis is able to cause pHTN in all 5 groups.2 This patient had a left ventricular ejection fraction of >50% but severely depressed RV systolic function. A transpulmonary gradient of <12 mm Hg along with a pulmonary vascular resistance of <2 WU suggested isolated postcapillary pHTN.3 Moreover, the severity of his RV dysfunction was out of proportion to the mild degree of his postcapillary pHTN. These hemodynamic findings, along with the CMR findings, suggested some intrinsic RV dysfunction directly related to his CS. RV systolic dysfunction confers a worse prognosis and is associated with higher all-cause mortality in patients with sarcoidosis.4 In addition, RV LGE is associated with higher risk of ventricular arrhythmias and is only noted in 5.5% to 16% of patients with sarcoidosis.2,5

This patient's initial manifestation of CS was high-grade AV block, which is the most common initial cardiac manifestation, seen in up to 42% of cases.6 Less commonly, in 14% of cases, patients may present with sustained ventricular arrhythmias.6 Given the lack of sensitivity of endomyocardial biopsy, multimodal imaging is critical in the diagnosis of CS. CMR is highly sensitive (95%) and specific (85%) for CS.7

CMR in this case was suggestive of CS, with patchy LGE most prominent in the anterior and inferior septal RV insertions. In CS, the pattern of LGE does not follow a predictable coronary distribution. This signifies inflammation or fibrosis and is often noted in the septum, left ventricular subepicardium, and myocardium, but rarely in the endocardium.8 Notably, LGE indicates a poor prognosis and is the strongest predictor of mortality in patients with CS.9 This elevated risk persists even with a normal left ventricular ejection fraction, as depicted in our patient.10

Despite active cardiac sarcoidosis, our patient was able to undergo CRT-D placement without complication. The Heart Rhythm Society places a Class IIa indication for defibrillator in patients with CS who have an indication for permanent pacemaker. Our patient was able to undergo endobronchial biopsy to confirm his diagnosis of CS before starting prednisone and methotrexate. He was started on immunosuppressive therapy before cardiac PET/CT in order to not delay treatment initiation, as urgent PET imaging was not available. Standard management would be to obtain PET imaging before initiation of therapy in order to track response, given that T2 imaging can no longer be used once a device is placed. Thus, cardiac PET imaging was obtained at 6 months for this patient, and it was negative for active CS. He has not had any recurrent hospitalizations since treatment initiation.

Conclusions

This case highlights an atypical presentation of predominant RV dysfunction in CS, with multiple high-risk and rare features noted on imaging. Diagnostic testing with multimodality imaging is crucial. Echocardiogram is the first-line imaging of choice to evaluate for myocardial and valvular pathology. Right heart catheterization is important in elucidating a patient's hemodynamic profile. In this case, mild postcapillary pHTN, low pulmonary vascular resistance, and a severely depressed pulmonary artery pulsatility index demonstrated a disproportionate degree of RV dysfunction. Although it is possible that the mildly elevated left-sided filling pressures played a role in the RV dysfunction, the severity of RV dysfunction was out of proportion to those findings. CMR is highly sensitive and specific for CS and was able to note patchy LGE and RV involvement. These findings both confer a higher risk and warrant close monitoring of the patient for adverse events. In active CS, the mainstay of treatment is immunosuppressive agents.
