The impact of statistical adjustment for assay performance on inferences from SARS-CoV-2 serological surveillance studies
Authors: Jiacheng Chen, Yuan Yu, Sheila F O’Brien, Carmen L Charlton, Steven J Drews, Jane M Heffernan, Amber M Smith, Yu Nakagama, Yasutoshi Kido, David L Buckeridge, W Alton Russell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChoice of immunoassay influences population seroprevalence estimates. Post hoc adjustments for assay performance could improve comparability of estimates across studies and enable pooled analyses. We assessed post hoc adjustment methods using data from 2021 to 2023 SARS-CoV-2 serosurveillance studies in Alberta, Canada: one that tested 124 008 blood donations using Roche immunoassays (SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid total antibody and anti–SARS-CoV-2 S) and another that tested 214 780 patient samples using Abbott immunoassays (SARS-CoV-2 IgG and anti–SARS-CoV-2 S). Comparing datasets, seropositivity for antibodies against nucleocapsid (anti-N) diverged after May 2022 due to differential loss of sensitivity as a function of time since infection. The commonly used Rogan-Gladen adjustment did not reduce this divergence. Regression-based adjustments using the assays’ semiquantitative results produced more similar estimates of anti-N seroprevalence and rolling incidence proportion (proportion of individuals infected in recent months). Seropositivity for antibodies targeting SARS-CoV-2 spike protein was similar without adjustment, and concordance was not improved when applying an alternative, functional threshold. These findings suggest that assay performance substantially impacted population inferences from SARS-CoV-2 serosurveillance studies in the Omicron period. Unlike methods that ignore time-varying assay sensitivity, regression-based methods using the semiquantitative assay resulted in increased concordance in estimated anti-N seropositivity and rolling incidence between cohorts using different assays.Keywords: population health surveillance, serology, assay adjustment, SARS-CoV-2

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic precipitated unprecedented investment in serosurveillance programs to monitor SARS-CoV-2 immunity worldwide, providing insights into transmission dynamics and the population immune response.1,2 SARS-CoV-2 serosurveillance studies typically use assays that detect antibodies to SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (anti-S), which can develop after infection or vaccination, or SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (anti-N), which only develops after infection.3-6 Several anti-N and anti-S assays are commercially available, each with different characteristics. Taking anti-N assays for instance, the Roche Elecsys Anti–SARS-CoV-2 immunoassay is a total antibody test, which does not differentiate between IgA, IgM, or IgG antibodies,7 unlike the Abbott Architect SARS-CoV-2 assay, which detects only IgG.8 The ability to identify prior infections can also differ markedly between common anti-N assays, particularly in people with less recent infections.3,4 Anti-S antibodies decline more slowly than anti-N but may also differ between assays.5 Differential assay performance makes it difficult to compare or combine seroprevalence estimates between studies using different assays.

Post hoc methods can adjust population serosurveillance estimates to account for imperfect assay performance, but most methods do not account for the fact that assay sensitivity, which we define as the probability of detecting antibodies in someone with a prior infection or vaccination, can change over time as antibodies wane. The Rogan-Gladen equation, which uses assay sensitivity, specificity, and the crude prevalence based on the qualitative assay result, is a commonly used method.9,10 Latent class analysis, often developed using Bayesian methods, can improve prevalence estimates when multiple imperfect measures are available for the same individuals in the absence of a gold standard.11-13 Previous studies have found linear associations among serology results produced by different assays,14 suggesting the possibility of using a regression model to transform quantitative or semiquantitative assay results. In settings where large swaths of the population have seroconverted, estimates of how many people have seroconverted in a recent period, known as rolling incidence,15 may be a more relevant population health measure than overall seropositivity. For SARS-CoV-2, rolling incidence has been estimated using repeated anti-N qualitative results9 and cutoff indices (COIs).16

For semiquantitative assays, agreement between assay titers and neutralizing activity are estimated using plaque reduction neutralization tests (PRNTs), as one study did for the Roche and Abbott anti-S assays.17 We hypothesized that the comparability of anti-S seropositivity between studies that used different assays could be improved by applying assay-specific cutoffs associated with a specified level of neutralizing activity from a PRNT study, as opposed to using independently derived manufacturer cutoffs. To our knowledge, this has not been assessed.

In this study, we applied post hoc assay adjustment methods, including newly derived regression-based approaches and PRNT-based thresholds, to data from concurrent serosurveillance studies in Alberta, Canada, to assess the impact on estimated serosurveillance estimates over time (seroprevalence and rolling incidence). Our findings illustrate how choice of assay and post hoc adjustment method shape population serosurveillance statistics, with implications for comparing or combining results from studies using different assays.

Methods

Datasets

We analyzed two residual blood serosurveillance datasets in Alberta, Canada. The Canadian Blood Services (CBS) dataset included 124 008 blood donor plasma samples collected between December 2020 and February 2023 using the Elecsys Anti–SARS-CoV-2 immunoassay (anti-N) and the Elecsys Anti–SARS-CoV-2 S immunoassay (anti-S) from Roche Diagnostics (Rotkreuz, Switzerland).7,18 The Alberta Precision Labs (APL) dataset included 214 780 outpatient lab plasma or serum samples. Samples collected from June 2020 to January 2021 and those collected from December 2021 to October 2022 were tested using the Architect SARS-CoV-2 IgG antibody assay (anti-N) from Abbott Laboratories (Abbott Park, IL), and samples collected from February 2021 to February 2023 were tested using the Abbott Architect SARS-CoV-2 IgG II Quant antibody assay (anti-S).19,20 In this paper, we assume that a goal of serosurveillance is to identify the cumulative proportion of the population infected with SARS-CoV-2 (anti-N or anti-S positive) or vaccinated (anti-S positive, anti-N negative). We therefore assessed the assay sensitivity and specificity in terms of whether the assay correctly identified if an individual had a prior infection (or, for anti-S, may have been vaccinated). Under this definition, individuals who had fully seroreverted and tested negative were considered a false negative, even if no antibodies could have been detected.

Anti-N seropositivity estimation

For each dataset, we estimated the monthly proportion of the population with anti-N antibodies with no adjustment (crude seropositivity) and with various adjustments. For crude seropositivity, we classified individuals as seropositive if the COI exceeded 1.0 for Roche anti-N assays21 and 1.4 for Abbott anti-N assays.8,20

Regression-based rolling incidence

Following seroprevalence estimation, we further estimated the monthly rolling incidence proportion of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Using auxiliary data from early in the pandemic (n = 1784, Table S1, Supplementary data),3, 4,24,25 we developed multinomial logistic regression models, separately for Roche and Abbott, that used the quantitative anti-N results to estimate the probability that a sample was collected from a person infected in the preceding 0-3, 4-6, 7-9, or more than 9 months. We applied this model to the CBS and APL datasets to estimate rolling incidence proportion from 2021 to 2023.

Anti-S antibody PRNT-50 level

Samples from CBS were tested using the Elecsys Anti–SARS-CoV-2 S assay, while samples in APL data were tested using the Abbott Architect SARS-CoV-2 IgG II Quant assay. The Roche anti-S assay detected the presence of antibodies binding to the receptor-binding domain (RBD) on the S1 subunit of the spike protein, while the Abbott anti-S assay detected the IgG binding to the RBD region of the spike protein. For crude seropositivity, we applied the manufacturer-suggested cutoffs: 0.8 U/mL for the Roche anti-S assay and 50 AU/mL for the Abbott anti-S assay.8,24 To assess whether an empirically derived threshold improves concordance in anti-S seropositivity, we assessed the proportion of participants per month whose anti-S levels were above published estimates of the antibody titer associated with a 50% reduction in a plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT-50) against wild-type SARS-CoV-2 at a 1:120 dilution for both assays17 (which may also have relevance to policymakers due to correlation with a higher level of immunization). We conducted stratified analysis by vaccination status.

This secondary analysis was approved by the McGill University Research Ethics Board (REB 23-09-077). The auxiliary data used to estimate regression models shared by Y.N. and Y.K. were generated as part of a study that was approved by the Committee of Osaka Metropolitan University Graduate School of Medicine (2020-003). All analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.0. All analytical code is publicly available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15849489.

Results

After removing samples with incomplete information (two CBS blood donor samples, four APL patient samples), our two Albertan serosurveillance datasets contained 124 006 (CBS) and 214 776 (APL) samples. The APL dataset included older participants (median age of 57 vs 48 years) and a larger proportion of female participants (56.5% vs 41.49%, Table 1).

Anti-N seroprevalence and rolling incidence

Crude anti-N seropositivity was similar between the two datasets until May 2022, after which seropositivity began to decline in APL, which used the Abbott IgG assay, but continued to increase in CBS, which used the Roche total antibody assay (Figure 1). This divergence was likely because Abbott’s IgG assay is less sensitive for detecting older infections.3,4 The Rogan-Gladen adjustment led to slightly higher seroprevalence estimates in both datasets (for October 2022, seropositivity increased 2.04% for CBS and 7.76% for APL) but did not substantially improve concordance in estimated seroprevalence between the two datasets (Figure 1A). Rogan-Gladen adjustment had a greater impact on seropositivity estimates that used the Abbott assay mainly because the estimate of sensitivity applied was lower for Abbott (0.927) than for Roche (0.972).

Applying the regression-based transformation led to more consistent monthly seropositivity estimates between the cohorts, although it did not eliminate the decline in seropositivity in the APL dataset beginning in May 2022 (Figure 1B). For October 2022, crude seropositivity for CBS was 48% higher than for APL without transformation but was only 14% higher than APL after transforming APL to estimate the seropositivity that would have been observed if APL had used the Roche assay.

Monthly estimates of rolling incidence proportion generated from our regression model were also more similar between the APL and CBS datasets compared to monthly estimates of crude or Rogan-Gladen–adjusted seroprevalence (Figure 2). For July 2022, the estimated proportion of the population infected was 55.35% for APL and 60.43% for CBS. Anti-S seropositivity

By October 2022, the proportion of samples with anti-S seropositivity using the manufacturer cutoff was over 95% in both datasets, but slightly higher in CBS (99.30% in CBS, 95.48% in APL, difference of 3.82%). Applying the more stringent PRNT-50 cutoff led to a lower proportion of seropositive samples in both datasets. This was particularly evident for APL, leading to a bigger gap between the datasets (97.00% in CBS, 90.43% in APL, difference of 6.57%; Figure 3A). While data on the vaccine status of CBS blood donors were not available, stratifying APL samples by SARS-CoV-2 vaccine status based on linked administrative records suggested that lower vaccine uptake in APL vs CBS datasets may have contributed to observed differences in anti-S seropositivity. In APL, the proportion of samples from unvaccinated individuals by month fell from 95% in February 2021 to 16% in December 2022. The proportion of samples from people with one vaccine dose peaked at 51% in June 2021 but fell below 3% by December 2021. In the unvaccinated and single-dose strata, the gap between the proportion of samples with anti-S titers above the manufacturer and PRNT-50 cutoffs was wider than the corresponding gap in the stratum with two or more vaccine doses (Figure 3). By October 2022, among APL samples from individuals with two vaccine doses, 98% exceeded the manufacturer’s anti-S titer cutoff and 95% exceeded the PRNT-50 cutoff, a difference of 3%. Corresponding measures in the CBS dataset were remarkably similar (99% above the manufacturer cutoff, 95% above the PRNT-50, difference of 3%), which could indicate that nearly all CBS blood donors were vaccinated by late 2022. To assess this, we estimated the proportion of noninfected individuals who were vaccinated by computing the proportion of anti-N–negative samples that were anti-S positive in each dataset. This metric closely tracked provincially reported vaccination rates for the APL cohort but was higher for the CBS cohort, further suggesting that vaccination rates were exceptionally high in the blood donor population (Figure S2A). We also assessed whether the proportion of anti-N–negative individuals who were anti-S positive changed when the proportion who are anti-N negative was estimated using our regression-based transformations. For APL, this ratio was considerably lower in late 2024 after transforming anti-N seropositivity to Roche (Figure S2B), but this did not impact our qualitative conclusions.

Discussion

In this study, we used data from large, concurrent SARS-CoV-2 serosurveillance studies to assess the impact of post hoc statistical adjustment methods for assay performance on serosurveillance estimates. The commonly used Rogan-Gladen equation did not improve concordance between contemporaneous estimates from the two cohorts. In contrast, regression-based transformations produced more consistent estimates of anti-N seroprevalence and rolling incidence proportion between datasets, despite immunoassay differences. These simple methods could improve the comparability of serosurveillance estimates between studies that use assays with different sensitivity, specificity, and time-varying sensitivity for SARS-CoV-2 or other pathogens.

Later in the pandemic, unadjusted anti-N seroprevalence differed markedly between the APL dataset that used Abbott Architect SARS-CoV-2 immunoassay and the CBS dataset that used Roche Elecsys Anti–SARS-CoV-2 immunoassay. Our findings suggest that seroprevalence estimates should not be directly compared between study populations without accounting for differential assay performance, and the Rogan-Gladen adjustment is not sufficient in these conditions. The widening discrepancy between anti-N seropositivity over time can be largely explained by differential waning in sensitivity between the Roche and Abbott anti-N assays. The Roche total Ig assay can detect many SARS-CoV-2 infections up to 12-18 months postinfection,4,25 whereas individuals usually become seronegative on the Abbott IgG assay 6-9 months after infection.3 This difference in sensitivity over time likely explains why the unadjusted CBS seroprevalence curve (Figure 1A) aligns closely with our estimate of cumulative incidence proportion (the “Ever infected” panel in Figure 2), while the unadjusted APL seroprevalence curve decreased after mid-2022. A substantial proportion of Canadians were infected during the Omicron wave in late 2021 and early 2022.2 By late 2022, unless reinfected, they would be expected to test negative for anti-N antibodies using Abbott but positive using Roche. This difference can be further explained by evidence of shorter antibody durability (faster seroreversion) following a SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variant infection leading to a shorter window of detectability by serological assays.26,27

The Rogan-Gladen adjustment did not substantially change anti-N seropositivity estimates or improve concordance between studies. This is likely because the Rogan-Gladen adjustment does not account for infection severity or the time from infection to testing. We applied the adjustment using sensitivity and specificity estimates from a study in samples collected within 90 days after infection among patients with severe clinical outcomes.22 We would expect sensitivity in this population to be higher than in the general population, which would include individuals with lower antibody titers because their infections were milder or less recent.28,29 Anti-N assay sensitivity and specificity may also change over time as the prevailing SARS-CoV-2 variant shifts and as vaccine status within the population changes.30 Time-varying sensitivity differed markedly between the Roche and Abbott anti-N assays, so it is unsurprising that differences in anti-N seropositivity increased over time, which use of the Rogan-Gladen could not address. This limitation of not correcting for time-varying sensitivity would similarly apply to use of Bayesian latent class analysis to adjust for assay performance.11

In contrast to the Rogan-Gladen adjustment, our regression-based methods improved concordance in anti-N seroprevalence estimates and produced more consistent estimates of rolling incidence proportion. Whereas Rogan-Gladen and typical Bayesian latent class analysis methods use only the qualitative assay result, our regression method uses the semiquantitative assay result to estimate the probability of infection (or, for rolling incidence proportion, the probability of recent infection). While other methods to account for waning sensitivity have been developed, an advantage of our methods is that they do not require repeated measures from the same individual, a compartmental epidemic model, computationally intensive Markov chain Monte Carlo methods, or a subset of participants with infections confirmed using a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay.31-33 Other methods offer some advantages, such as robust uncertainty estimation and producing other estimates (eg, infection fatality ratio or an explicit seroreversion rate),34 but they typically require assumptions around antibody kinetics and greater computational power, and they do not directly facilitate conversion between assays. That said, applying our methods to different assays or pathogens would depend on the availability of auxiliary data for estimating the regression equations. Our assay transformation method requires auxiliary data in which both assays were used on the same samples, and our rolling incidence method requires auxiliary data in which the time since infection is known with high confidence.

Our hypothesis that concordance in anti-S seropositivity would be improved when using an empirical PRNT-50 threshold did not bear out in this dataset. However, our analysis was likely influenced by differences in vaccination between the datasets. While we lacked vaccination status for the CBS dataset, anti-S titers and previous reports that blood donors tend to have high compliance with preventative public health guidance35 both suggest that vaccination would be higher for the CBS blood donors. Our finding that APL samples from individuals with zero or one vaccine dose were more likely to have anti-S titers in between the manufacturer cutoff and higher PRNT-50 level compared to fully vaccinated individuals is consistent with studies showing that anti-S antibody levels and humoral immunity conferred by natural infection are lower than those conferred by vaccination.36 If vaccination status had been available for CBS, we may have found that use of a PRNT-50 cutoff improved concordance in anti-S seropositivity between datasets after controlling for vaccination. Another potential source of sampling bias we could not control is that individuals with immune conditions that dampen the immunological response to vaccination or infection may be more represented in the APL patient population than the CBS blood donors, due to the well-documented healthy donor effect.37 Future research could assess assay adjustment methods while controlling for differences in study populations’ characteristics such as vaccine status, age, or immune competency, which greatly influence anti-S seropositivity. Adjusting for vaccine status could also improve anti-N seropositivity estimates because individuals infected after vaccination tend to have less pronounced and more transient anti-N IgG antibody titers.28

Our study has several strengths. We used large, concurrent provincial serosurveillance studies, providing a comprehensive and representative overview of the blood donor and outpatient populations in Alberta. We used auxiliary data collected before and during the early stages of the pandemic to train the regression models. This approach allows for a more accurate measurement of SARS-CoV-2 infection status, ensuring the reliability of the regression models used for assay result transformation and rolling incidence estimation. We also conducted sensitivity analyses for both anti-N– and anti-S–related assessments, which help validate the results and ensure the reliability of the findings across different contexts.

Nonetheless, our study has some limitations. First, a sensitivity analysis revealed that the regression models used for transforming between Roche Elecsys Anti–SARS-CoV-2 immunoassay and Abbott Architect SARS-CoV-2 immunoassay results are sensitive to the ratio of positive to negative cases in the training data (Figure S1). Negative cases in the auxiliary data may need to be downweighed when seropositivity is much lower in the auxiliary data used to estimate the regression equations than in the datasets to which the adjustment will be applied, which will likely be the case if the only available auxiliary data are from an early pandemic period. Second, the anti-S adjustment used thresholds for each assay derived from a PRNT-50 study that used wild-type SARS-CoV-2,38 but the antibody titers needed to neutralize the Omicron variant and more recent SARS-CoV-2 variants may differ.39 Third, CBS blood donors and APL outpatients may not be directly comparable without adjusting for population differences, most notably vaccination, age, and health status. Earlier research suggested that anti-N seropositivity was similar between blood donors and other populations before vaccination,40 but blood donors in the United States had higher vaccination rates than nondonors.41 This could lead to greater anti-S seropositivity but lower anti-N seropositivity after vaccines are available, as vaccination is associated with a lower rate of anti-N seropositivity after infection.42 The APL dataset contained more samples from people aged 65 or greater (35% vs 16%), who are individuals at greater risk of severe infections. Older adults tend to have lower anti-S levels after vaccination43 but are also at greater risk of severe disease,44 which is associated with longer-lasting anti-N antibodies. Fourth, our adjustment methods did not explicitly account for changes in the abundance of different antibodies over time, which may be possible using mathematical models (compartmental models) of within-host immunological dynamics. Finally, we did not account for changes in assay performance across labs or over time. For example, a surveillance report suggests a shift in anti-S concentration in the CBS study around January 2022, which could influence our findings (see Figure 3 from the COVID-19 Seroprevalence Report June 2023 Survey45). We did not account for data showing that the Roche anti-S signal drifted over time.

A standardized framework to account for assay differences is needed to enhance the comparability of assay results and the accuracy of public health surveillance measurements. While the World Health Organization standardized quantitative assay measurement of binding antibody units per milliliter is one such effort, it may not be sufficient.46 The ideal framework would be flexible enough to adjust for demographic factors like age, health status, and vaccination status. Implementing such a comprehensive framework would improve serosurveillance for endemic and emerging infections and provide greater clarity about the relative infection and immunity dynamics across populations, even when different immunoassays are used. This would also be valuable for aligning datasets as inputs to mathematical models of infectious disease, which can be used to provide insights into immune dynamics that are not feasible to directly measure.47

In summary, our findings indicate that serosurveillance estimates are sensitive to the choice of immunoassay, and the most common adjustment methods (e.g., Rogan-Gladen) do little to make the estimates more concordant, particularly later in the pandemic when differences in waning sensitivity between assays become significant. Our regression-based approaches account for some differences between immunoassays, producing more comparable population serosurveillance estimates across studies.
