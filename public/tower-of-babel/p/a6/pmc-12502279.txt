Enterocutaneous fistula as a long-term complication of jejunostomy tube placement in a dog with hyperadrenocorticism
Authors: Je-Sung Moon, Hee-Jae Choi, Hyun-Jung Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJejunostomy tube (JT) feeding is a practical method of delivering enteral nutrition in dogs when oral, oesophageal or gastric feeding is not feasible, particularly in postoperative or critically ill patients with gastric, duodenal, proximal jejunal, or pancreatic disease. Although generally well-tolerated, JT placement is an invasive procedure associated with potential complications, requiring close postoperative monitoring. Certain underlying conditions may further increase the risk of adverse outcomes. Hyperadrenocorticism (HAC), in particular, is associated with impaired wound healing and increased susceptibility to infections, potentially predisposing affected patients to delayed complications following enteral feeding tube placement and removal.Case presentationAn 11-year-old, 2.9 kg Maltese dog presented with persistent gastrointestinal signs and focal cellulitis with purulent discharge. The dog had previously undergone intestinal anastomosis and JT placement for foreign body removal, with an uneventful recovery. Four months postoperatively, ultrasonography and fistulography confirmed an enterocutaneous fistula extending from the subcutaneous tissue to the intestinal lumen. Concurrently, HAC was diagnosed based on a post-stimulation cortisol level exceeding 20 µg/dL following an adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulation test. Given HAC’s pathophysiological effects —including glucocorticoid-induced dermal atrophy, diminished tissue elasticity, and increased infection susceptibility— HAC was suspected to have contributed to fistula formation. Surgical treatment comprised of jejunal resection and anastomosis, as well as excision and debridement of the fistular tract. The patient exhibited no recurrence over a three-year follow-up period with sustained medical management of HAC using trilostane therapy.ConclusionsThis case highlights enterocutaneous fistula as a delayed and previously unreported complication of JT placement in a dog with HAC. Given the adverse effects of HAC on wound healing, clinicians should recognize the potential for such complications and implement vigilant postprocedural monitoring and appropriate endocrine management in patients undergoing enteral tube placement.Keywords: Enterocutaneous fistula, Hyperadrenocorticism, Jejunostomy tube, Enteral feeding, Veterinary surgery

Introduction

Jejunostomy tube (JT) feeding, as with other types of enteral feeding, has been recommended for early nutritional support in critically ill patients to maintain the integrity of the gastrointestinal mucosal barrier, preserve the intestinal immune function, and prevent bacterial translocation [1]. One of its clinical applications is to support patients experiencing persistent anorexia requiring rapid nutritional support following gastrointestinal surgery [2] or vomiting which would preclude the use of oesophagostomy or gastrotomy feeding [1].

In veterinary medicine, JT placement is typically performed surgically and most often in patients already undergoing laparotomy. Compared to nasogastric or esophagostomy tubes—which are more commonly employed for enteral nutrition—JT tubes are considered a specialized option due to their invasiveness and technical demands [2, 6]. Thus, their use should be carefully justified based on the individual patient’s clinical condition and contraindications for proximal gastrointestinal feeding.

Although JT feeding is known to be relatively safe, a range of complications has been reported in both humans and small animals, with overall complication rates ranging from 1.5 to 21% in human patients3 and 15-43.2% in veterinary patients [2–6]. The majority were not life-threatening, with a non-fatal complication rate of 15-36.7%, such as tube dislodgement, occlusion, focal cellulitis, peristomal infection, mild vomiting and diarrhea. Serious, life-threatening complications have also been documented (0-6.5%), including inadvertent jejunopexy and leakage of intestinal contents into the subcutaneous tissue or peritoneal cavity, leading to severe cellulitis, fasciitis, and/or septic peritonitis [2–6].

This case report describes an enterocutaneous fistula that occurred four months after JT placement in a dog with hyperadrenocorticism (HAC). To the best of authors’ knowledge, enterocutaneous fistula is a novel, long-term complication associated with JT placement in a dog.

Case presentation

An 11-year-old 2.9 kg spayed female Maltese dog presented to Konkuk Veterinary Medical Teaching Hospital (KUVMTH) with a five-day history of persistent vomiting, anorexia and lethargy. On physical examination, the patient appeared lethargic, but no other abnormalities were detected with the vital signs. Complete blood count (CBC) showed a leukocytosis with neutrophilia (white blood cell count of 27,950/㎕, reference range of 5,050 − 16,760; segmented neutrophils of 24,330/㎕, RR: 2,950 − 11,640; monocytes of 1,560/㎕, RR: 160-1,120). The biochemistry results demonstrated elevations in amylase and lipase to 5,932 U/L and 7,382 U/L, respectively (RR: 500–1,500 U/L and 200–1,800 U/L respectively). Alkaline phosphatase (ALKP) was mildly elevated at 285 U/L (RR: 23–212 U/L), while all other chemistry values were within normal limits. Serum canine pancreatic lipase (cPL) exceeded 1,000.00 ng/mL (RR: 200–400 ng/mL), and D-dimer levels were elevated at 2,087.87 ng/mL (RR: 50–250 ng/mL). Abdominal ultrasonography identified pronounced acoustic shadowing consistent with a suspected foreign body extending from the gastric antrum to the proximal portion of the descending duodenum, with concurrent dilation of the intestinal segment. Additionally, ultrasonography revealed a thickened and hypoechoic pancreas, warranting differential diagnoses, including acute pancreatitis.

A decision was made to perform exploratory laparotomy for removal of the foreign body. Pre-medication included cefotaxime (30 mg/kg IV; Cefotaxime Sodium Inj. 1 g, PharmGen Science, Seoul, Republic of Korea) for antimicrobial prophylaxis and butorphanol (0.1 mg/kg IV; Butophan Inj., Myungmoon Pharmaceutical Co., Seoul, Republic of Korea) for analgesia. Maropitant (1 mg/kg IV; Cerenia®, Zoetis, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) and famotidine (0.5 mg/kg IV; Gaster Inj., Dong‑A ST, Seoul, Republic of Korea) were administered for antiemetic and gastric protection. General anesthesia was induced using midazolam (0.3 mg/kg IV; Midazolam Inj., Bukwang Pharmaceutical Co., Seoul, Republic of Korea) and propofol (4 mg/kg IV; Anepol Inj., Hana Pharm Co., Kyonggi‑do, Republic of Korea), then maintained with isoflurane (Vetflurane®, Virbac, Carros, France) in 100% oxygen. Fentanyl citrate was initially administered for analgesia during this surgery; however, it was promptly discontinued due to the development of bradycardia and hypotension. Consequently, butorphanol was selected as a safer alternative for intraoperative analgesia.

A midline ventral laparotomy was performed, revealing a linear foreign body extending from the stomach into the jejunum. Gastrotomy was carried out to remove the portion of the foreign body lodged in the stomach. A separate jejunotomy was performed to extract the remaining segment located in the duodenum and proximal jejunum. A compacted linear foreign body measuring approximately 50 cm in length, composed of a tampon and wet wipes, was retrieved. A necrotic segment of jejunum, approximately 5 cm in length was resected. The pancreas appeared diffusely edematous, consistent with acute pancreatitis. Gastrotomy site was sutured by simple continuous absorbable suture for the submucosa and an inverting Cushing pattern for the seromuscular layer, both using 4 − 0 polydioxanone (PDS II; Ethicon, Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ, USA). An end-to-end intestinal anastomosis was performed by 4 − 0 PDS II, with simple interrupted pattern and mesentery was sutured with 4 − 0 PDS II, simple continuous pattern. An omental flap was applied over the anastomosis site to provide additional vascular support and promote healing.

Given the extensive mucosal damage observed in the gastric cardia and fundus – characterized by severe hemorrhage and inflammation with high risk of perforation – as well as concurrent suspected acute pancreatitis, a JT feeding was selected for early postoperative enteral nutrition while bypassing the upper gastrointestinal tract. A 6 French silicone jejunostomy feeding tube (MILA International Inc., Erlanger, KY, USA) was placed using the interlocking box technique for enteral feeding. A Stab incision was made on the right abdominal wall with a No. 15 scalpel blade, and a segment of proximal jejunum was isolated. An antimesenteric border incision was made, and the distal end of the feeding tube was inserted through the incision, advancing 15 cm aboral. The tube was secured with a purse-string suture. Jejunopexy was then performed using the interlocking box technique, which involved creating two complete suture boxes extending from the jejunum to the abdominal wall, enclosing the JT (Fig. 1).

jejunostomy tube was placed from the jejunum to the abdominal wall by interlocking box technique

Postoperatively, enteral feeding was initiated via the JT using a liquid diet delivered at 3 mL/hr by continuous rate infusion (CRI). The feeding rate was gradually increased to 5 mL/hr over the first two days, while monitoring for gastrointestinal tolerance. The patient remained hospitalized for three days postoperatively.

During hospitalization, analgesia was provided using butorphanol CRI, followed by intravenous (IV) bolus injections four times daily (QID). For empirical antimicrobial coverage, broad-spectrum antibiotics were initiated, including cefotaxime (30 mg/kg TID IV; Cefotaxime Sodium Inj., PharmGen Science, Seoul, Republic of Korea), enrofloxacin (20 mg/kg SID, IV over 1 h), and metronidazole (15 mg/kg BID IV over 30 min). To mitigate gastrointestinal risk and promote mucosal protection, pantoprazole (1 mg/kg BID, IV), misoprostol (3 mcg/kg BID, orally), and sucralfate (500 mg/kg BID, orally) were administered. Maropitant (1 mg/kg SID, IV) and ondansetron (0.5 mg/kg BID, IV) were administered. Supportive care for pancreatitis included fluid therapy with a mixture of adjunctive agents added to 500 mL of balanced crystalloid solution (Plasma Solution-A Inj.; Boryung Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea), including gabexate mesilate (5 mg/kg/hr IV; Foy Inj., 100 mg/vial; Donga ST, Seoul, Republic of Korea), ulinastatin (25,000 U/kg IV; Ulistin Inj., 100,000 units/vial, Hanlim Pharm, Seoul, Republic of Korea), vitamin B complex (1 vial, 2 mL/vial; Beecomhexa Inj., Yuhan Corp., Seoul, Republic of Korea), vitamin C (1 vial, 100 mg/vial; Ascorbic Acid Inj., Dai Han Pharm, Seoul, Republic of Korea), taurine (5 mL; Taurine-F Inj., Samyang Anifarm, Seoul, Republic of Korea), and hepatoprotective agent (5 mL; Ornipural®, Vetoquinol, France).

No vomiting occurred postoperatively, and oral intake was reintroduced in parallel with JT feeding to meet the resting energy requirement (RER). The dog recovered uneventfully and was discharged on post-op day 3 with oral medications including cefixime, enrofloxacin, metronidazole, omeprazole, sucralfate, and metoclopramide. Continued clinical improvement was noted at home, with no reported complications. A follow-up evaluation on day 4 revealed normal appetite, absence of vomiting, and improved demeanour, and by day 5, the patient had returned to full oral intake without complications. The JT was removed on post-op day 5 after confirmation of sustained voluntary oral intake, resolution of gastrointestinal signs, and restoration of normal appetite and activity level.

Both the laparotomy site and jejunostomy site healed without complications. A follow-up evaluation conducted at two weeks postoperatively revealed no abnormalities on the surgical site or the JT tube removal site. At one month postoperatively, a telephone follow-up confirmed sustained clinical stability with no recurrence of gastrointestinal signs. Based on these findings, the patient was signed off.

Four months post-op, the dog was represented with focal cellulitis at the mid-caudal ventral abdomen, characterized by erythema, swelling, and purulent discharge. The referring veterinarian identified dehiscence at the previous jejunostomy site and performed topical wound care using a commercially available sugar dressing, along with a two-week of antibiotic therapy with cefixime (12.5 mg/kg BID, orally). Despite these interventions, serosanguineous to purulent discharge was consistently produced from the lesion, prompting re-evaluation at KUVMTH.

Upon presentation, the dog was quiet, alert, and responsive, appearing clinically healthy aside from the cutaneous lesion. Physical examination revealed a body temperature of 38.4℃, systolic blood pressure of 150 mmHg measured by Doppler, no clinical signs of dehydration and pink mucous membranes with a capillary refill time within 2 s. Cardiac auscultation was unremarkable, and the heart rate was 132 beat/min and there was intermittent panting. Physical examination also revealed thinning of the skin and dehiscence on the mid-abdomen, presenting as a circular fistula with a diameter of 1 cm, and a small amount of serosanguineous discharge (Fig. 2).

The cutaneous dehiscence with a diameter of 1 cm, and small amount of serosanguineous discharge from the fistula

CBC showed mildly decreased red blood cell counts (RBC; 4.84 × 106/㎕, RR: 5.65 × 106/㎕ to 8.87 × 106/㎕), hematocrit (HCT, 32.2%; RR: 37.3–61.7%), hemoglobin (HGB, 11 g/dL; RR: 13.1 g/dL to 20.5 g/dL). Neutrophil and total white blood cell count were within the reference range. Serum biochemistry was unremarkable, except for a marked increase in alkaline phosphatase (ALKP, 685 U/L; RR: 23–212 U/L), which had risen significantly compared to values recorded four months prior. An adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulation test was performed, yielding a basal cortisol concentration of 2.4 ㎍/dL (RR: 0.5 ㎍/dL to 10 ㎍/dL) and a post-stimulation cortisol concentration exceeding 30 ㎍/dL (RR: 6 ㎍/dL to 18 ㎍/dL), suggesting a tentative diagnosis of hyperadrenocorticism (HAC). The smear cytology of the cutaneous lesion revealed numerous hyper-segmented neutrophils, some of which exhibited phagocytosis of rod-shaped bacteria, along with scattered monocytes and macrophages.

Ultrasonography revealed adrenal gland sizes within reference intervals: left 3.9/3.7 mm and right 3.1/3.9 mm (cranial/caudal pole, respectively). Ultrasound examination of the abdominal skin defect confirmed its connection through the subcutaneous tissue and abdominal wall to a spindle-shaped hypoechoic area measuring 28.1 mm, containing hyperechoic material with acoustic shadowing. A connection of the hypoechoic area within the subcutaneous and abdominal wall layers to the adjacent jejunal segment was confirmed, with absence of normal intestinal wall layering and a markedly thickened muscularis layer on the ventral aspect. Fistulography was performed to identify the origin and patency of fistula. For the fistulography, a 24G over-the-needle catheter was inserted into the fistula, and approximately 3mL of iohexol (Omnipaque™, Amersham Health, Cork, Ireland) was injected slowly under fluoroscopy. Fluoroscopy demonstrated contrast passage from the subcutaneous and abdominal wall layers to the intestinal lumen adjacent to abdominal wall. (Fig. 3). These findings led to a definitive diagnosis of an enterocutaneous fistula. Although computed tomography (CT) was recommended to evaluate the extent of adhesion between the intestine and abdominal wall, the procedure was not performed due to the owner’s financial constraints.

A Over-the-needle catheter (red arrow) was placed for fistulography, B Iohexol being injected through cutaneous fistula tracts (white arrow), C iohexol was identified in jejunal lumen (yellow arrow)

To debride the necrotic tissue and excise the enterocutaneous fistula, a laparotomy was performed through a ventral midline incision over the fistula site. An anesthetic protocol used was identical to the previous surgery.

The jejunopexy site from the previous surgery was observed to be completely adhered to the abdominal wall (Fig. 4A), ensuring no leakage of intestinal contents into the abdominal cavity. The jejunum and abdominal wall were meticulously disconnected from each other using a combination of blunt and sharp dissection. The fistula orifices connecting the abdominal wall and the jejunum were identified (Fig. 4B). The fistula, along with chronically degenerated tissues, encompassed approximately half of the intestinal circumference (Fig. 4C), thus primary repair following debridement would most likely be accompanied by high risk of focal luminal stricture. Consequently, intestinal resection of the affected jejunal segment followed by end-to-end anastomosis was performed using 4 − 0 PDS II in a simple continuous pattern. An omental patch was applied after lavage with sufficient saline and a leakage test. Tissue samples from the affected abdominal wall were collected for bacterial culture, and the fistula tissues were resected and debrided. Histopathological analysis was not performed due to owner’s financial constraints. The abdominal wall was closed using 3 − 0 polydioxanone (PDS II; Ethicon, Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ, USA) in a simple continuous pattern. The subcutaneous layer and intradermal layer were apposed using 4 − 0 polydioxanone in a simple continuous pattern, and the skin was closed with 3 − 0 polyamide (Dafilon®; B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) using interrupted sutures. The dog recovered uneventfully from anesthesia.

A Previous jejunopexy site of jejunum fixed to the abdominal wall (white arrow), B enterocutaneous fistula identified in the abdominal wall (yellow arrow), C enterocutaneous fistula and chronic degenerate tissues identified in the intestinal wall

Postoperative management included intravenous administration of cefotaxime 30 mg/kg every 8 h and metronidazole 15 mg/kg twice daily for four days, with omeprazole 1 mg/kg twice daily, and trilostane 1 mg/kg twice daily orally. Given the presence of an enterocutaneous fistula and the potential risk of microleakage or anaerobic contamination, metronidazole was included post-operatively to provide additional anaerobic coverage. For the second surgical procedure, butorphanol was again used for preoperative analgesia, considering the prior adverse cardiovascular response to fentanyl. However, during the postoperative period, fentanyl citrate (2 µg/kg loading dose followed by 12 µg/kg/hr for 12 h; Fentanyl Citrate Inj., Hana Pharm Co., Ltd., Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea) was conservatively reintroduced for postoperative pain control. Notably, no recurrence of bradycardia or hypotension was observed during this period and fentanyl was well tolerated throughout the remainder of hospitalization and provided effective analgesia. Bacterial culture of the fistula tissue identified Proteus mirabilis and Escherichia coli., both of which were susceptible to cefotaxime and cefixime. Following bacterial culture results, metronidazole was discontinued, and oral cefixime was continued as monotherapy for five days. Post-operatively, patient’s appetite was normal, and no gastrointestinal symptoms occurred.

The patient was discharged on postoperative day 3 with oral prescriptions including trilostane (1.2 mg/kg BID), cefixime (5 mg/kg BID), metronidazole (15 mg/kg BID), and omeprazole (1 mg/kg SID). On postoperative days 6 and 10, re-evaluations revealed good appetite, normal activity, and appropriate healing of the surgical site on physical inspection. On day 14, external skin sutures were removed with no complications observed. No signs of wound dehiscence, infection, or fistula recurrence were identified during this early postoperative period.

At the four-week follow-up after initiating trilostane therapy, the ACTH stimulation test showed a post-stimulation cortisol concentration of 8.9 µg/dL (RR: 0.5–10 µg/dL), indicating adequate adrenal suppression. The patient continued to receive ongoing management for HAC at a local veterinary clinic. Follow-up telephone check-ups conducted at three months and again at three years postoperatively confirmed the patient remained clinically well, with no recurrence of gastrointestinal symptoms or cutaneous fistula formation.

Discussion

The definition of an enterocutaneous fistula is an abnormal communication between the intestinal lumen and skin and it is a rare complication of JT placement in humans but it has not been reported in dogs, to the authors’ knowledge [7]. The pathophysiology of enterocutaneous fistula formation is thought to involve epithelialisation at the tube removal site [8]. Common clinical signs of enterocutaneous fistulas include fluid leakage, abscess formation, hemorrhage, sepsis, malnutrition, and electrolyte disturbances; in severe cases, they can lead to death, often associated with sepsis and multiple organ dysfunction [8, 14–16]. The external loss of intestinal fluids, which are rich in electrolytes, minerals, and protein, exacerbates complications such as electrolyte imbalance and malnutrition [9]. In human medicine, the mortality rate associated with enterocutaneous fistulas varies depending on the volume of fluid loss, the treatment of sepsis and the timing of surgical intervention [10]. The reported mortality rate has improved significantly from 65% to approximately 20% by early detection and appropriate treatment [10].

The most common causes of enterocutaneous fistula in humans include inflammatory bowel disease, malignant tumor, radiotherapy, and intestinal diverticulum [11]. In human medicine, risk factors for enterocutaneous fistula formation include Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, previous radiotherapy, intra-abdominal malignancy, diverticular disease and desmoid disease [10]. Crohn’s disease is the leading primary small intestinal disease associated with enterocutaneous fistula formation in humans [12]. Fistulas develop in 20–40% of patients with Crohn’s disease [13]. Active inflammation associated with Crohn’s disease, granulation tissue, or sepsis can lead to fistula formation, impaired healing, or recurrence. The intramural inflammation that occurs with Crohn’s disease may lead to adherence of the intestine to nearby structures. Micro perforation then leads to abscess formation and subsequent perforation into the nearby structure. Fistula formation involving the anterior abdominal wall is less common, likely due to the anatomical position of the intestines, which minimizes direct contact with the anterior abdominal wall.

Reports of enterocutaneous fistulas in veterinary medicine are rare and cases have been documented in relation to pelvic trauma [14]chronic bite wound [7]intraperitoneal gauze foreign body [15]and colonic foreign body perforation [16]. In human medicine, enterocutaneous fistula has also been reported as a complication of percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tubes [17]. Enterocutaneous fistula after JT placement have been reported in 3 of 2022 cases (0.14%) in human medicine [18]but there are no known reports in veterinary medicine.

In this case, HAC was suspected as a contributing factor in the development of the enterocutaneous fistula following JT tube removal. HAC is characterized by elevated glucocorticoid concentrations, which are associated with pathological changes such as skin thinning, decreased skin elasticity, and increased susceptibility to infections—some of which were evident in this case [19]. Glucocorticoids exert antiproliferative effects on fibroblasts and inhibit collagen synthesis, resulting in impaired wound healing, increased skin fragility, and delayed tissue repair [20]. These effects substantially increase the risk of wound dehiscence and may lead to the breakdown of previously healed scar tissue during the healing process. Moreover, the insidious onset and gradual progression characteristic of HAC is consistent with the delayed development of the enterocutaneous fistula observed in this case [21].

In this patient, HAC likely contributed to delayed wound healing at the JT removal site, culminating in the formation of an enterocutaneous fistula four months later. Potential risk factors for enterocutaneous fistula formation, including abscess development at the abdominal wall, intestinal dehiscence, or septic peritonitis, were evaluated during surgical exploration following jejunopexy site separation. Additionally, the site was assessed for evidence of intestinal wall inversion—associated with stricture formation—or ischemic necrosis caused by the previous purse-string suture placement around the JT. None of these abnormalities (abscess, intestinal ischemic necrosis, dehiscence, septic peritonitis or inversion-stricture) were identified on visual inspection or palpation.

In this case, the interlocking box (ILB) technique was selected for jejunopexy due to its proven benefits in minimizing postoperative complications and securing long-term tube maintenance. The ILB method has demonstrated superior resistance to leakage and tube dislodgement when compared to traditional methods such as purse-string or Witzel techniques [22]. An experimental study has shown that the ILB technique withstands significantly higher intraluminal pressure prior to leakage (mean 87.6 cmH₂O), thereby reducing the risk of peritoneal contamination [22]. Additionally, retrospective analysis in canine patients indicated that the ILB approach is associated with lower complication rates, shorter mean hospitalization periods, and earlier tube removal without increased risk of adverse outcomes [6]. In the present case, the technique ensured a secure fixation of the jejunum to the abdominal wall, preventing any gaps and leakage.

Given the characteristics of the retrieved foreign material—a compacted tampon and wet wipes from household garbage—with suspected prolonged intraluminal residence, empirical triple-antibiotic therapy was selected post-operatively. Despite the absence of overt perforation or gross peritonitis, the case was classified as dirty-contaminated due to the necrotic intestinal segment and potential microleakage, necessitating broad-spectrum prophylactic coverage including anaerobic organisms. Similarly, dual antibiotic therapy was employed following the second surgery in the context of a chronic enterocutaneous fistula. Once culture results confirmed susceptibility to cefotaxime alone, metronidazole was discontinued accordingly.

Regarding perioperative analgesia, although butorphanol monotherapy is generally suboptimal for abdominal surgery, it was selected intraoperatively due to adverse cardiovascular events associated with fentanyl in this patient. For the second surgery, butorphanol was again used preoperatively to minimize anesthetic risk, while fentanyl was cautiously reintroduced postoperatively under close monitoring and was well tolerated. In addition to patient-specific considerations, the limited local availability of alternative opioids such as methadone, morphine, and buprenorphine may have hindered the full implementation of an optimized analgesic strategy in the perioperative setting. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs were withheld because of extensive gastric mucosal injury and other analgesics such as ketamine or paracetamol were not considered. These decisions reflect individualized clinical judgment within a high-risk context, while acknowledging the limitations and need for optimization of multimodal analgesia in similar cases.

Tan et al.. have reported that in human patients most fistulas resolved with conservative treatment within 6 weeks to 2 months, but 15% of cases fail to heal even after 2 months and require surgical intervention [8]. In these non-healing cases where conservative management fails, traditional intra-abdominal techniques or less invasive options such as fibrin glue application, skin grafts, flaps and T-tube intubation - placement of a T-shaped tube into the fistula tract to maintain patency and promote controlled healing - have been utilized [8]. However, less invasive extraperitoneal methods are difficult to apply when the fistula size is more than 1.5 cm [8]. In this case, surgical reconstruction was deemed necessary due to the chronic nature and large diameter of the fistula, as well as the underlying HAC. After discussing potential complications with the owner including the risk of delayed healing of surgical wound after revision surgery due to HAC-related tissue fragility, surgical intervention was chosen over conservative management to repair the fistula and prevent further infection or inflammation. The surgery, along with appropriate management of HAC, resulted in successful resolution of enterocutaneous fistula.

Conclusion

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first reported case of a delayed formation of an enterocutaneous fistula following jejunostomy tube removal in a patient with HAC. The chronic effects of HAC, particularly impaired wound healing and tissue fragility, underscore the importance of vigilant monitoring and proactive management to mitigate the risk of severe sequelae in these patients. Although rare, clinicians should be aware of the potential risk for enterocutaneous fistula formation following JT placement in patients with compromised wound healing.
