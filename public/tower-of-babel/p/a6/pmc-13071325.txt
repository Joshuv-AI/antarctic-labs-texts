Real World Outcomes With Chemotherapy and Immunotherapy in Metastatic Poorly Differentiated Gastroenteropancreatic Neuroendocrine Carcinomas
Authors: Rushabh Gujarathi, Samantha Kwock, Faith Abodunrin, Namrata Setia, Xavier M. Keutgen, Chih‐Yi Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGastroenteropancreatic poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (GEP‐NECs) are aggressive cancers where cytotoxic chemotherapy is the backbone of treatment. While the addition of immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (IOs) to chemotherapy is recommended for lung NECs, evidence regarding IO efficacy in GEP‐NECs is limited. We explore outcomes with systemic therapies in patients with metastatic GEP‐NECs using the National Cancer Database (NCDB).MethodsCases of metastatic GEP‐NECs with years of diagnosis between 2018 and 2022 were identified within the NCDB. Overall survival (OS) was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier estimations and Cox proportional hazards regression.ResultsFour thousand one hundred twenty‐seven cases were included. 1209 (29.29%) patients did not receive any systemic therapy (chemotherapy/immunotherapy/both). Among the 2918 patients that received systemic therapy, 758 (25.98%) patients received immunotherapy (immunotherapy alone = 21; chemotherapy plus immunotherapy [chemoIO] = 737). The percentage of patients receiving immunotherapy alongside chemotherapy increased from 12.27% in 2018 to 34.18% in 2022. Two thousand two hundred eighty cases were included for survival analysis. Patients treated with chemoIO (N = 532) had prolonged OS versus chemotherapy alone (N = 1748), with median OS of 10.51 months vs. 8.31 months (HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.69–0.85; p < 0.001). The association was significant in all histological subgroups (small‐cell, p < 0.001; large‐cell, p = 0.04; others/not specified, p = 0.01).ConclusionsCombination chemoIO may improve survival in metastatic GEP‐NECs as compared to chemotherapy alone. Results of currently ongoing clinical trials exploring the role of immunotherapy in extrapulmonary NECs may help guide clinical practice in GEP‐NECs.

Introduction

Neuroendocrine Neoplasms (NENs) are tumors that express biomarkers associated with the cellular pathways of neuroendocrine cells [1]. Although rare, these tumors have seen a notable increase in incidence over recent decades [2, 3]. NENs can broadly be categorized into well‐differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (PD‐NECs or NECs) based on histological retention of neuroendocrine morphological and molecular features [4, 5]. The 2022 WHO classification of neuroendocrine neoplasms defines NECs based on poorly differentiated neuroendocrine morphology, characterized by solid sheet or nodular growth patterns with frequent geographic necrosis, and recognizes two subtypes: small cell NEC and large cell NEC with distinct cytological features [1]. NECs consistently demonstrate high proliferative activity with mitotic counts generally > 20 per 2 mm2 and Ki‐67 index usually > 55%, along with numerous apoptotic bodies and other aggressive features [1]. The classification acknowledges Merkel Cell Carcinoma as the only site‐specific NEC classified outside the conventional small cell and large cell subtypes [1]. NECs are a relatively rarer and generally more aggressive subtype of NENs, as compared to well‐differentiated NETs [1, 2]. NETs and NECs also exhibit variable expressions of somatostatin receptors (SSTRs). SSTRs are strongly and diffusely expressed in NETs and absent or minimally expressed in NECs, rendering treatment with somatostatin analogs ineffective [1]. As per recent analyses, NECs comprise approximately 18% of all cases of NENs in the US [2]. Gastroenteropancreatic (GEP) NECs represent the majority of extra‐pulmonary NECs, and comprise about 37% of cases [3]. GEP‐NECs are highly aggressive and often present with advanced or metastatic disease and have a poor prognosis [6].

In advanced lung NECs, initial treatment with chemotherapy plus immunotherapy using immune checkpoint inhibitors (chemoIO) has shown improved overall survival (OS) in several clinical trials and real‐world studies. Presently, chemoIO is widely recommended for the management of advanced lung NECs, particularly small‐cell lung cancer [7, 8]. Emerging evidence suggests that immune‐based therapeutic strategies may play a role in the management of NECs even beyond immune checkpoint inhibition. Recent studies have demonstrated the clinical activity of the bispecific delta‐like ligand 3 (DLL3)‐directed T‐cell engager tarlatamab in small‐cell lung cancer [9], as well as obrixtamig (BI 764532), a DLL3/CD3 IgG‐like T‐cell engager, in both pulmonary and extra‐pulmonary NECs [10]. Preclinical studies have also explored the potential role of CAR‐T cell therapy in pulmonary small‐cell carcinoma. Several CAR‐T cell targets including the AC133 epitope of CD133, disialoganglioside GD2, and CD56 NCAM‐1; neuronal cell adhesion molecule (1) have been investigated, and this remains an avenue for future exploration in NECs [11].

However, the benefit of chemoIO in metastatic GEP‐NECs has not been well‐established in prospective studies. For metastatic GEP‐NECs, first‐line treatment often consists of cytotoxic chemotherapy with a platinum‐based agent plus etoposide, with a preference for carboplatin over cisplatin due to better tolerability [4, 6, 12, 13]. Survival remains poor and there is a significant unmet need to improve the efficacy of current treatments [6]. Furthermore, akin to NETs, there remains a lack of precision medicine approaches for patients with NECs [14]. Emerging pre‐clinical evidence suggests a potential role for immunotherapy in the clinical management of GEP‐NECs [15]. In a study of 37 patients with GEP‐NECs, programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1) was expressed in 16% of tumor cells and 63% of tumor‐associated immune cells, while programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD‐L1) was visualized in 14% and 27%, respectively [16]. A small single‐center retrospective study reported clinically meaningful benefit with atezolizumab, an anti‐PD‐L1 monoclonal antibody, in poorly differentiated extra‐pulmonary NECs [17]. A prospective, randomized trial (NCT05058651) is currently investigating atezolizumab combined with a platinum drug (cisplatin or carboplatin) and etoposide in metastatic extra‐pulmonary NECs, which may help guide future treatment strategies.

Although ongoing clinical trials may provide the most valid evidence to guide clinical practice, we aimed to investigate the potential therapeutic role of immunotherapy used in combination with chemotherapy as upfront treatment in metastatic GEP‐NECs using real‐world outcomes. We utilized data from cases of GEP‐NECs from the United States of America's National Cancer Database (NCDB) and assessed patterns of chemoIO use along with the impact of immunotherapy use on OS.

Methods

Data Source and Cohort Creation

This study analyzed data from the 2022 Participant User Files (PUFs) of the NCDB for all gastrointestinal sites. The NCDB is a joint project of the Commission on Cancer (CoC) of the American College of Surgeons and the American Cancer Society. The CoC's NCDB and the hospitals participating in the CoC's NCDB are the source of the de‐identified data used herein; they have not verified and are not responsible for the statistical validity of the data analysis or the conclusions derived by the authors.

Cases diagnosed between 2018 and 2022 were included. Inclusion of cases starting in 2018 aligned with our study objectives for two reasons. The approval of immunotherapy as an adjunct to chemotherapy for extensive‐stage small‐cell lung cancer was first reported in a phase 3 trial in 2018 [18]. Secondly, the currently used classification of NETs, which specifies grade 3 well‐differentiated (WD) NETs as being distinct from PDNECs, was also introduced in 2017 [19, 20]. Metastatic PDNECs of GEP origin were identified using relevant International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, 3rd Edition (ICD‐O‐3) morphology codes [21]. The NCDB's analytic stage group variable (based on NAACCR Items #910 and #970; stage IV cases included) and distinct metastatic site variables (NAACCR Items #1112, #1113, #1114, #1115, #1116, #1117) were utilized to identify cases with metastatic disease at diagnosis. Primary site groupings were performed using ICD‐O‐3 topography codes [21]. All ICD‐O‐3 codes included, and classification of primary sites as per ICD‐O‐3 topography codes is described in Table S1. Several exclusion criteria were employed to exclude cases of well‐differentiated (WD) NETs from the final dataset. We excluded morphology codes suggestive of functional NETs, as these tumors are unlikely to be poorly differentiated [22, 23]. We also excluded cases wherein treatment with hormone therapy was recorded. As somatostatin analogues (SSAs) are the only hormonal agents used in the management of NETs with somatostatin receptor expression, these might represent cases of WD‐NETs given the unlikely occurrence of somatostatin receptor expression in PDNECs [24]. Finally, cases with missing treatment data were excluded. Cohort creation steps are briefly described in Figure 1 (further details in Table S2).

Study Variables and Outcomes

The analysis focused on two primary domains: factors associated with immunotherapy receipt and the impact of immunotherapy as an adjunct to chemotherapy on survival outcomes. Receipt of immunotherapy and chemotherapy was defined based on whether it was administered or not administered (for any reason) as the first course of treatment per the NCDB. Patients who did not receive any systemic therapy (either chemotherapy or immunotherapy or both) were not considered for receipt of immunotherapy analysis. As immunotherapy alone is not usually considered a standard upfront treatment option as per practices for both pulmonary and extra‐pulmonary metastatic NECs, the small number of cases that received immunotherapy alone (i.e., without concurrent chemotherapy) were also excluded [4]. Select clinicopathological and demographic variables were considered for receipt of immunotherapy analysis, including but not limited to age, sex, race, ethnicity, insurance status, and facility type. Whether treatment was received with palliative intent was determined based on NAACCR Item #270. Cases that underwent primary site‐directed surgical procedures, including excision, resection, and destruction, were identified using site‐specific codes. OS was estimated using the number of months between the date of initial diagnosis (NAACCR Item #390) and the date of last contact or death (NAACCR Item #1750) with death being the event of interest, recorded as vital status (NAACCR Item #1760) in the NCDB. Cases with at least two months of survival data unavailable, including all cases diagnosed in 2022, were excluded from all survival analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Unless specified otherwise, continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) and categorical variables are expressed as proportions. Proportions of immunotherapy use were compared using the chi‐square test. Univariable logistic regression was used to assess factors associated with receipt of immunotherapy. Median survival times and 95% confidence intervals were generated using Kaplan–Meier estimations. The log‐rank test and Cox proportional hazards regression were used for survival analysis. Variables with significant associations on univariable Cox regression were included in the multivariable analysis of OS. Statistical significance for all tests was p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using StataNow v18.5, BlueSky Statistics v10.3.2, and SPSS v29.

Results

Four thousand one hundred twenty‐seven cases of metastatic GEP‐NECs meeting our inclusion criteria were included in the study cohort. Of these, 2435 (59%) were male. A large percentage (N = 4090; n = 3391; 82.91% cases) of the cohort comprised white patients. The most common primary site subgroup was Colorectal and anal (including Appendix) with 1662 (40.27%) cases. The most common histological subtype was small‐cell type with 1875 (45.43%) cases. Liver (N = 4103; n = 3231; 78.75% cases) and distant lymph nodes (N = 4060; n = 1040; 25.62% cases) were the most frequent metastatic sites. Treatment Patterns and Immunotherapy Receipt

Two thousand nine hundred eighteen (70.71%) patients in the study cohort received systemic therapy, while 1209 (29.29%) patients did not receive any systemic therapy. Among patients who received any systemic therapy, 2160 (74.02%) received chemotherapy alone, 21 (0.72%) received immunotherapy alone, and 737 (25.26%) received chemotherapy plus immunotherapy (chemoIO). Hence, 758 (25.98%) patients received immunotherapy in this subgroup. Among patients who received chemotherapy, 359 (16.62%) patients in the chemotherapy alone group and 132 (17.91%) patients in the chemotherapy plus immunotherapy group received treatment with palliative intent. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess which patients were more likely to receive chemotherapy plus immunotherapy, as compared to chemotherapy alone. Clinicopathological and demographic features associated with receipt of immunotherapy among patients who received chemotherapy are described in Table S3. Notably, patients treated at a community cancer program were more likely to receive immunotherapy as compared to patients treated at an academic or research program (p = 0.01). Patients with both large‐cell (p < 0.001) and other/not specified (p < 0.001) histological subtypes were less likely to receive immunotherapy than patients with small‐cell carcinomas. The use of immunotherapy (with chemotherapy) across the various years of diagnosis recorded in our cohort are shown in Figure 2. The percentage of patients receiving immunotherapy alongside chemotherapy increased from 12.3% in 2018 to 34.2% in 2022. There was a significant difference in the proportions of patients receiving immunotherapy in each individual year of diagnosis (p < 0.001).

Survival Outcomes

Eight hundred ninety‐three cases had missing survival data or follow‐up less than two months. Among the remaining 3234 cases, 942 (29.12%) did not receive any systemic therapy. Receipt of any systemic therapy was associated with prolonged OS (median OS, 8.80 months vs. 1.18 months; HR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.29–0.34; p < 0.001; Figure S1). Cases wherein systemic therapy was not utilized were excluded from all subsequent analyses. 12 (0.37%) cases wherein immunotherapy alone was utilized with no chemotherapy were also excluded. Hence, 2280 cases (all treated with chemotherapy alone or chemoIO) were identified for OS analysis to ascertain outcomes with immunotherapy used as an adjunct to chemotherapy.

Receipt of chemoIO was associated with significantly prolonged OS as compared to chemotherapy alone across the whole cohort (10.51 months vs. 8.31 months; HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.69–0.85; univariable Cox p < 0.001; Figure 3). Subgroup analyses were conducted using select clinicopathologic and demographic variables, the results of which are shown in Table 2. When sub‐divided by primary site, the association of use of chemoIO with survival was significant in the colorectal & anal (including appendix; p < 0.001), gastroesophageal (p = 0.02), and others (including unknown primary; p = 0.01) subgroups, but did not show significance in the pancreas (p = 0.47), small intestine (p = 0.73), and biliary (p = 0.78) subgroups. The association with OS remained significant in all subgroups (small‐cell, p < 0.001; large‐cell, p = 0.04; other/not specified, p = 0.01) when sub‐divided based on histological subtype. This association remained significant on multivariable Cox regression which included other variables significantly associated with OS in this sub‐cohort on individual univariable survival analysis, as shown in Table 3. Other variables significantly associated with OS were age, CCDI of 3 (vs. 1), all primary sites except others (including unknown primary; with colorectal being the reference group), the presence of bone/liver/lung metastases, and primary site surgery.

Discussion

The role of immunotherapy in extrapulmonary NECs, including GEP‐NECs, remains underexplored in prospective clinical trials. We believe our study is pioneering in exploring whether there is a difference in real‐world survival outcomes when immunotherapy is added to chemotherapy for patients with GEP‐NECs. We found an OS benefit associated with adding immunotherapy to chemotherapy compared to chemotherapy alone. We believe our findings represent potentially hypothesis‐generating results that warrant validation in prospective clinical trials, particularly given the scarcity of clinical trial data for these tumor types.

NECs arising from the lower gastrointestinal tract, i.e., colorectal and anal tumors in our cohort, comprised the largest primary site subgroup, in line with previously reported data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) registry [25]. Most patients in our cohort received chemotherapy as upfront treatment, which is in line with current recommendations for the management of extrapulmonary PDNECs [4, 26]. Predictably, patients who did not receive chemotherapy or immunotherapy had significantly shorter OS and a higher risk of death. We believe the group that did not receive chemotherapy might comprise largely of patients with poor functional status, comorbidities, disease status, and other patient‐related factors that precluded the use of systemic therapy in these patients. Among patients that received chemotherapy, the proportion that received concurrent immunotherapy seemed to increase gradually over the years. The largest proportion was seen in patients diagnosed in 2022, the last year of data available in the current study. This trend may reflect the fact that clinicians have become more inclined to utilize immunotherapy over these years, likely owing to data from clinical trials and real‐world experiences in lung NECs [7, 18, 27, 28]. Practice driven by data from advanced lung NECs may also explain why patients with small‐cell histology were probably more likely to receive immunotherapy compared to large‐cell and other histological subtypes in our cohort. Possible differences in immunotherapy receipt based on the type of cancer facility also indicates underlying differences in the interpretation and implementation of data for these rare cancers. Clinicians involved in the clinical management of these rare cancers should remain aware of these potential differences.

The median survival difference of approximately two months seen with immunotherapy in our cohort was numerically similar to survival benefits reported with the addition of immunotherapy to chemotherapy in small cell lung cancer. Most subgroups based on clinicopathological characteristics also showed significant associations with lower risk of death and longer OS in patients treated with immunotherapy. Notably, only three of the six primary site subgroups in our cohort showed immunotherapy plus chemotherapy to be significantly associated with OS as compared to chemotherapy alone. Owing to the rarity of the disease, extra‐pulmonary NECs are often considered as a singular entity for the purposes of clinical research. Our results indicate that there may be underlying differences in clinical outcomes with the same treatment strategies in these cases based on the primary tumor which should be explored further in future investigations. Among patients who received chemotherapy in our study cohort, the varying differences in OS seen with concurrent immunotherapy across different primary tumor sites further highlights this need. In summary, our results indicate that the addition of immunotherapy to chemotherapy may be beneficial in terms of OS for patients with GEP‐NECs. However, this benefit may be dependent on the primary site of the tumor and this consideration is vital for both clinical management, and the conduct and interpretation of ongoing (e.g., NCT05058651) and future studies. Primary site surgery was also significantly associated with improved OS on multivariable analysis in the chemotherapy treated cohort, akin to results from other studies in NETs/NECs [29, 30, 31].

The major limitations of our study are a result of the retrospective and limited nature of NCDB data. The NCDB does not provide specific details regarding the exact immunotherapy and chemotherapy regimens utilized. Whether or not systemic therapies were used as a part of a clinical trial is also not specified for most cases. Additionally, important clinical variables such as ECOG performance status, Ki‐67 proliferation index, tumor mutational burden, tumor genomics, and PD‐L1 status are not captured in the NCDB, all of which may influence clinical outcomes following treatment. Given that immunotherapy use in GEP‐NECs is not routinely recommended (as per NCCN guidelines at the time of manuscript preparation), our immunotherapy cohort represents a highly selected patient population and limits the external validity of these findings as compared to randomized studies. Although several efforts were made to limit the inclusion of cases to PDNECs only as described in the methods, the possibility of well‐differentiated NETs being included cannot be completely excluded. This is especially true for high‐grade (grade 3) NETs due to the inherent limitations of the NCDB. A substantial subset of cases in the cohort comprises colorectal NECs likely originating from conventional adenocarcinomas. These neoplasms exhibit distinct biological characteristics compared to NECs at other anatomical sites, particularly smoking‐associated small cell carcinomas of the lung; this histopathological and biological heterogeneity may influence the study outcomes [32]. Additionally, colorectal NECs may be associated with underlying DNA repair deficiencies or HPV infection [32]. In this cohort from the NCDB, the status of these potential molecular drivers is not known, limiting the ability to assess their impact on prognosis or treatment response. Given that the NCDB only records “upfront” treatment, the impact of post‐upfront therapy treatments on survival is not possible to account for in analyses such as ours. Despite these limitations, our study provides important signal for future explorations into the potential utility of immunotherapy as an adjunct to chemotherapy in metastatic GEP‐NECs.

Conclusion

Our real‐world analysis of GEP‐NECs shows that the addition of immunotherapy to chemotherapy may be beneficial as “upfront” treatment for patients with metastatic GEP‐NECs. These results align with currently established clinical practices for pulmonary NECs. While limited by the retrospective nature of the study and limitations of the NCDB, our data provide meaningful signals for future exploration for patients with GEP‐NECs and also provides real‐world context as the results of ongoing clinical trials (NCT05058651) are awaited.
