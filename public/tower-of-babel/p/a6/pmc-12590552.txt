A systematic review of computer-aided acupoint localization
Authors: Zhenwei Zhai, Zihao Wang, Lin Xu, Linshuai Zhang, Yujie Zhang, Ji Yin, Pengfei Zeng, Chen Li, Tao Sun, Tao Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAcupuncture, as a traditional medical therapy, has gained widespread application globally. However, traditional acupoint localization has relied heavily on the personal experience of clinicians. With the advancement of AI, especially deep learning, researchers have begun exploring automated acupoint localization in recent years. This study systematically reviews the literature on acupoint localization in different anatomical regions using traditional algorithms and deep learning to evaluate the effectiveness, limitations, and future directions of these technologies. Drawing insights from the surveyed studies, we anticipate that the challenges outlined in this research will be addressed in the near future. As these challenges are overcome, automated acupoint localization techniques are expected to yield more promising outcomes. We hope our efforts will inspire further exploration of advanced AI methods for acupoint localization.

Introduction

In modern society, the accelerating pace of life is leading to a rise in health problems, affecting workers and students alike. Prolonged exposure to such health issues could significantly deteriorate the quality of life of people and diminish learning efficiency.1 To address these growing health concerns, an increasing number of individuals are turning to traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) treatments such as acupuncture, moxibustion, and massage techniques.2,3 For example, acupuncture has effectively managed chronic diseases, relieved pain, and improved overall health, while maintaining a high safety profile.4,5 Notably, these TCM therapies are not limited to symptom management but emphasize holistic treatment, reflecting the core TCM philosophy of the “unity of heaven and human.”

According to TCM theory, acupoints are not understood merely as static, precisely measurable anatomical points, but rather as dynamic, relational nodes that reflect the energetic and physiological states of the body. Acupoints with their locations and sensitivity potentially vary depending on the constitution of patients, condition, and diagnostic context. Studies have shown that even acupuncture experts cannot agree on the precise location of acupoints. In a study by Molsberger et al., 71 senior physicians marked the location of 23 commonly used acupoints on standard volunteers, and the 95% confidence interval ranged from 2.7 cm2 for PC6 acupoint to 41.4 cm2 for ST38 acupoint. Notably, this location variability was not correlated with the physician clinical experience or training background.6 Another study also showed that when different acupuncturists located the same acupoint, the acupoint difference could reach 16.6 mm.7 In addition, the theory of “acupoint sensitization” proposed in TCM research suggests that when the body is in a state of functional imbalance, acupoints can shift from a relatively static state to an active, sensitive state, with their effective areas potentially expanding and their electrical or thermal properties changing.8

Essentially, the dynamic characteristics of acupoints are a core component of individualized treatment methods in TCM. However, this also presents challenges: reliance on the subjective experience and palpation skills of practitioners means that acupoint localization may vary between different acupuncturists. In some scenarios, such as multicenter clinical trials or standardized training of new practitioners, this may reduce the reproducibility of interventions and the interpretation of results.

It is worth noting that in modern research, standardized location methods have been developed to enhance consistency, such as those issued in the WHO Standard Acupuncture Point Locations in the Western Pacific Region (2008). The primary motivation for this standardization is to enhance consistency and reproducibility in acupuncture research. By providing clear anatomical reference points, the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines aim to ensure that different practitioners and researchers can uniformly locate acupoints, thereby improving the reliability and reproducibility of acupuncture research.9 Additionally, many acupuncture-related journals now recommend that studies adhere to the WHO standard localization to facilitate reproducibility of results. Standardized localization protocols, such as the anatomical landmarks and proportional measurements (cun), can serve as practical tools in scientific research.9 For the visualization and description, see Figures S1 and S2.

In technology-driven applications such as computer-assisted acupoint localization, these standards provide the necessary foundation for algorithms to predict the locations of acupoints on the human body. Early automation of acupoint localization primarily relied on the specific electrical resistance characteristics of acupoints. In the 1950s, Japanese researcher Yoshio Nakatani10 accidentally discovered, while measuring the skin resistance of patients with nephritic edema, that points with specific electrical resistance values, when connected in a particular sequence, formed patterns resembling the traditional meridians of Chinese medicine. The positions of these points also closely corresponded with the acupoints described in Chinese medicine. Further validation confirmed that this phenomenon was also observed in other subjects, garnering widespread attention from the global scientific community.11

However, researchers also have differing views that “acupoints or meridians are special conduits for electrical signals.” Andrew C. Ahn and colleagues systematically searched seven databases, identifying 16 relevant studies (18 studies in total, with nine focusing on acupoints and nine on meridians). Five studies on acupoints found a positive correlation between acupoints and lower skin resistance or impedance. Seven studies on meridians reported a trend toward lower resistance and higher capacitance in skin areas along meridian pathways. While some studies support the association between meridians and acupoints with electrophysiological parameters, the overall study quality is generally low, with limited sample sizes and influenced by various confounding variables. Therefore, the authors suggest that the current evidence is insufficient to conclusively establish that acupoints or meridians exhibit distinct differences in bioelectrical parameters.12

In addition, some researchers have used anatomical landmarks or facial features as mediating variables combined with geometric operations to calculate acupoint locations. For example, researchers have proposed a facial acupoint localization method based on infrared images,13 an acupoint localization technique that combines facial landmarks with human body proportions,14 and a method to realize acupoint localization through three-dimensional models.15

Deep neural networks have gradually gained widespread recognition for their superior feature extraction capabilities, making them ideal tools for handling classification and regression tasks. Sun et al.16 first applied deep learning to acupoint localization in 2020, achieving a detection rate of 76% and laying the foundation for further research in this direction. In addition, a randomized controlled trial compared the effectiveness of computerized Acugraph-guided acupoint selection with traditional manual acupoint selection in treating knee osteoarthritis. The results showed that computerized Acugraph-guided acupoint selection potentially showed more favorable results.17

Although algorithm-based acupoint localization technology has made significant progress recently, with the potential to improve localization consistency and repeatability, these methods must be understood within the overall framework of TCM theory. The purpose of reference points is to validate and improve research methods, not weaken the overall understanding of acupuncture.

Therefore, this study systematically reviews and compares the application of various acupoint localization techniques in anatomically complex regions such as the head, face, hands, and ears. Through a critical analysis of the advantages and limitations of various technical methods, the aim is to provide theoretical support and a practical basis for the future development of automatic acupoint localization technology.

Results

Study selection

Nine databases were searched, and 5,711 articles were retrieved. First, duplicate articles were removed by EndNote 21; then, the remaining articles were manually checked to remove duplicates further. After deleting duplicate articles, the remaining 2,939 were initially screened by reading titles and abstracts. One hundred fifteen articles were excluded for animal studies; 496 articles were excluded due to non-TCM research; 790 articles were excluded for clinical studies; 550 articles were excluded due to other intelligent medicine research; 671 articles were excluded for types such as patents, posters, or reviews; and 10 articles were excluded because research had been retracted. The remaining 307 articles were further screened by reading the full text. Thirty-one articles were excluded due to non-acupoint locations, 25 articles for non-acupoint automatic locations, 180 articles for other intelligent TCM research on non-acupoint localization or recognition, two articles for being non-peer reviewed, nine articles for being unable to find full text, and five articles for having duplicate data. One article was excluded because the anatomical location of acupoints was not specified. Eventually, 54 articles on the automatic localization or recognition of acupoints were included, with 39 in English and 15 in Chinese. Discussion

Anatomical overview and localization of common acupoints

In the study of automatic acupoint localization, the significant variations in anatomical characteristics across different body regions necessitate a specialized examination of each area. This section systematically reviews the localization methods for commonly used acupoints on the head, face, auricle, back, chest, upper limbs, and hands.

Except for auricular acupoints, the selection of commonly used acupoints for the head, face, back, chest, upper limbs, and hands is based on the fourth edition of “Acupuncture and Moxibustion” for the new century and “WHO Standard Acupuncture Point Locations in The Western Pacific Region.” The specific localization methods for the acupoints in these regions are detailed in Tables S2 and S4–S7, respectively.

For auricular acupoints, this study adopts the standards from Names and Locations of Auricular Acupoints (WFAS STANDARD-002:2013),67 published by the World Federation of Acupuncture-Moxibustion Societies; the specific information for these 68 acupoints is provided in Table S3.

Traditional acupoint automatic localization technology

Traditional acupoint localization relies primarily on the clinical experience of practitioners, which is often time-consuming and difficult to replicate accurately. However, with advancements in AI technology, especially in the field of computer vision, some advanced technologies are gradually being applied to the automatic localization of acupoints, including infrared thermography (IRT), Active Shape Model (ASM), Active Appearance Model (AAM), Augmented Reality (AR), 3D technologies, and others.

Application of IRT in automatic localization of acupoints

IRT is a technique that captures the infrared radiation emitted by objects and converts it into thermal images reflecting temperature variations. The infrared spectrum is typically divided into near-infrared (0.8–1.2 μm) and far-infrared (3–14 μm) bands. Research has shown that any object above absolute zero emits infrared radiation, and by analyzing the wavelength and intensity of this radiation, surface temperature can be accurately calculated.68,69 IRT has been widely applied in medical diagnostics due to its non-invasive nature and ability to visualize thermal patterns. For instance, it can detect abnormal thermal patterns caused by inflammation or infection, thus aiding in disease diagnosis and monitoring.70 In recent years, IRT has gained attention for acupoint localization, as it can reveal subtle thermal differences on the human body.

In 2010, Zhao et al. first applied IRT for acupoint localization in patients with facial paralysis. The method employed median filtering for noise reduction and the Sobel operator for edge detection. A coordinate system was established based on the positions of the eyes and mouth, and a proportional model grounded in traditional Chinese acupuncture theory was constructed. This enabled automatic localization of ST2 points with high accuracy in a sample of nine individuals.13 However, its reliance on a standard model and basic edge detection limited adaptability to individual anatomical variations.

Subsequent studies proposed more refined methods. In 2012, Zhao et al. introduced a novel approach combining the minimum eigenvalue operator and the Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) operator to detect facial corners and edges. Key facial features such as the mouth and cheeks were identified, and facial axes were constructed, enabling the localization of 11 acupoints with an average accuracy of 94%, improving adaptability to facial anatomical features.47

Building on this, in 2013, Xulong Liu proposed a method that integrated temperature-specific features and edge detection. The approach applied image denoising and segmentation to isolate the head region. The Harris corner detector and LoG operator were used to identify key features, and facial feature axes were constructed for frontal images. For side views, the Canny operator was used due to hair interference compared to the Zhao study.47 This method achieved average localization accuracies of 94.3% for 11 frontal and 94.29% for three lateral acupoints.46

IRT has also been applied to other clinical conditions. In 2022, Jianfeng Shu adapted IRT for acupoint localization in breast cancer patients undergoing self-massage therapy.66 The method used an improved median filter and global threshold segmentation to extract the chest and upper limb regions. Feature points were identified using temperature-specific detection and edge operators (Harris and LoG), and a coordinate system was constructed. The localization accuracy for acupoints exceeded 90%, demonstrating the adaptability of method to various anatomical regions and conditions.

These studies collectively indicate that accurate identification of facial and thoracic anatomical structures is crucial for automatic acupoint localization using IRT. This enables the construction of vertical and horizontal axes, which, combined with prior anatomical knowledge, allow for precise localization. Edge detection plays a key role in this process, with commonly used techniques including corner detection, LoG, Sobel, and Canny operators.

Despite its advantages, IRT is constrained by several limitations. Its effectiveness depends on significant surface temperature differences, making it suitable primarily for facial paralysis and breast cancer. In diseases with minimal thermal variation, localization accuracy decreases. Additionally, uniform thermal profiles among individuals and external environmental factors can further reduce precision. Consequently, alternative methods such as ASM and AAM have been explored to enhance accuracy under such challenging conditions.

Application of ASM and AAM in automatic localization of acupoints

The ASM, introduced by Tim Cootes in the early 1990s, is a statistical model that captures shape variations from labeled training data.71 It was originally developed for medical imaging to delineate anatomical structures precisely and has since been applied to automatic acupoint localization.

In 2017, Menglong Chang applied ASM to localize facial acupoints.14 Using the Canny edge detector to extract facial contours, ASM identified key landmarks such as the eyes, nose, and mouth. Acupoints were then proportionally positioned based on TCM bone length theory. The method demonstrated high accuracy compared to manual localization.

ASM was also applied to auricular acupoints. In 2021, Wen et al. proposed an automated auricular division method based on ASM and the WFAS STANDARD-002:2013.58 Mahalanobis distance was used for model iteration, while Euclidean distance (ED) and Hausdorff distance (HD) assessed accuracy. Results showed average ED and HD values of 6.67 ± 0.59 and 6.28 ± 0.50, respectively, outperforming watershed and level set methods. However, limitations included a small sample size, dependence on manual labeling, lower accuracy in poorly defined regions, and a focus on right ear images only.

To overcome the limitations of ASM in contour localization, Li et al. introduced an AAM-based method for auricular acupoint recognition.59 Unlike ASM, AAM integrates shape and texture, improving accuracy in complex structures. Labeling 91 ear feature points and applying the Lucas-Kanade Inverse Compositional algorithm achieved 100% convergence and an average pixel error below 6 under 68 standardized and 100 non-standardized ear images. Even with non-standardized images, recognition exceeded 95%, despite lighting variation. However, the approach requires high-quality input images and significant computational resources.

Application of AR and 3D in automatic localization of acupoints

Unlike traditional methods, AR enhances automatic acupoint localization through interactive visualization. Two studies44,45 integrated AR with machine learning and image recognition, using algorithms like Oriented Fast and Rotated Brief and Google ML Kit for facial feature detection. A dynamic scaling mechanism and real-time processing ensured stable performance across varying distances and conditions.

However, challenges remain, including adaptability to diverse facial shapes and the inability to localize lateral acupoints from frontal images. To address these, researchers combined AR with 3D imaging. One study54 employed the OpenFace framework72 and Unity3D73 for real-time visualization, adjusting acupoint positions using predefined coordinates. OpenFace 2.0 further improved real-time landmark-tracking capabilities.74 Another study52 used 3D morphable model (3DMM), achieving a localization error of 2.4 mm. While 3DMM provides robust 2D to 3D mapping, its accuracy is limited by training data diversity and anatomical variation.

To improve precision, Zhang et al.53 developed FaceAtlasAR, integrating pre-trained facial alignment75 and hair segmentation models76 with the MediaPipe framework for real-time, cross-platform acupoint computation. Though performance was robust across devices, accuracy near the ears remained suboptimal, which was later resolved in E-faceatlasAR60 by adding ear region modeling.

The authors used a deep learning model based on TFLite for facial alignment in the above two articles.75 Facial alignment is a computer vision technique used to recognize the geometric structure of faces in digital images. However, the existing 2D and 3D datasets have limitations in landmark annotations. For instance, the 300-W dataset,77,78,79 one of the most widely used 2D alignment datasets in the wild, includes LFPW,80 HELEN,81 AFW,82 and iBUG,77 with only 68 landmarks annotated per face, which do not meet the requirements of the above studies. Therefore, the above studies adopt the TFLite model developed by Kartynnik et al.,75 which could estimate a 3D face mesh with 468 vertices in real time. In addition, the Delaunay triangulation (DT) method was used for regional ear localization to ensure anatomical consistency within the region. DT is a fundamental concept in computational geometry and is widely used in various applications, such as mesh generation, terrain modeling, and finite element analysis. It is defined as a triangulation of a set of points such that no point is inside the circumcircle of any triangle in the triangulation.83 This property ensures that the triangles are as “well shaped” as possible, avoiding slivers and maximizing the minimum angle in the triangulation.

Beyond AR integration, multiple 3D imaging-based localization techniques have been proposed. Chen et al.48 developed a HoloLens 2-based real-time system with improved Dlib84 detection, achieving 2.55-mm localization error. Hong et al.49 created a 3D projection system for teaching; Yin et al.50 constructed a dataset with feature-template matching to annotate 37 facial acupoints. Yan et al.51 employed laser projection, with < 3.1-mm error, while Kim et al.15 used X-ray computed tomography (CT) to classify and localize 65 acupoints. This approach was expanded65 to generate a full-body 3D model covering 361 standardized acupoints using cross-sectional CT data.

Although traditional image-based methods for acupoint automatic localization have achieved some success, many rely on intermediate variables such as facial landmarks or predefined acupoint templates. These intermediates can introduce cumulative errors, ultimately limiting localization precision. In addition, key technical details based on traditional algorithms are presented in Table S8.

Automatic localization of acupoints based on deep learning

In recent years, the emergence of deep learning has provided researchers with a novel approach to acupoint localization. Compared to traditional automatic localization methods, complex neural networks possess superior feature learning capabilities, allowing them to extract essential features from data and excel in handling classification or regression tasks. As a result, deep learning shows excellent potential in acupoint automatic localization. Among the deep learning techniques applied to automatic localization of acupoints in the included articles are mainly ResNet,18,20,27,28,33,34,85 High-Resolution Network (HRNet),21,22,31,36,42 MobileNet,19,25,26,30,36,41,43 Visual Geometry Group (VGG),16,29,35 YOLO,23,32 and some others.24,37,38,39,40

Application of ResNet in automatic localization of acupoints

ResNet, a deep residual network introduced by Kaiming He in 2015,86 was developed to address training challenges in deep convolutional neural network (CNN), particularly the vanishing gradient problem. Among the included articles, seven adopted ResNet: five used ResNet-50 as a feature extractor or backbone, while ResNet-18 and ResNet-101 were each used once. ResNet-18 offers computational efficiency but limited representational power, whereas ResNet-101 provides higher accuracy at greater computational cost. ResNet-50 balances depth and efficiency,86 making it a popular choice in image processing applications. The structure and description of ResNet can be found in Figure S3.

In facial acupoint localization, Chen et al.18 employed ResNet-50 with transfer learning and the Wingloss function to address overfitting due to limited data. Pretraining on the Wider Facial Landmarks in the Wild (WFLW) dataset enabled feature reuse, and Wingloss improved regression accuracy. The method achieved a 46.8% reduction in normalized mean error (NME) through transfer learning. Additionally, the Wingloss function outperformed the mean square error (MSE) loss, further reducing NME by 16.7%. Ultimately, this method achieved an average NME of 2.5% across nine facial acupoints. However, acupoints with indistinct visual features, such as Sīzhúkōng (TE23), remained challenging.

Beyond treatment, acupoint localization supports quantitative analysis of facial paralysis. Huang et al.20 applied ResNet-50 to analyze three facial acupoints (Dicang [ST4], YuYao [EX-HN4], and Shang Yingxiang [EX-HN8]) across 2,283 images, achieving 0.54% NME. ResNet-50 has also been applied to the back and hand acupoints. Liu et al.33 enhanced Keypoint RCNN with channel and spatial attention mechanism (CBAM) for improved precision and developed a posterior median line method to use prior acupoint relationships, achieving 90.12% accuracy across 23 back acupoints using 4,000 images. However, the effectiveness of the method in complex postures and multi-angle images might need further exploration. The structure and description of CBAM can be found in Figure S4.

To address visual ambiguity, Zhao et al.34 improved ResNet-50 with mixed precision training, optimized cross-entropy loss, and Canny edge detection. SK-UNet segmented the back region, and the “finger-cun” method aided localization. In this study, 1,317 images were used to localize 37 acupoints, achieving 91.95% accuracy at a 10 mm radius but only 59.80% at 5 mm.

Despite the limitations of the existing accuracy, the exploration of acupoint localization methods with ambiguous visual features deserves to be noted. For example, the back region was segmented by the SK-UNet model to find the region boundaries and then labeled with a priori knowledge for localization. The SK-UNet algorithm enhances the traditional UNet architecture by integrating selective kernel mechanisms, which enable adaptive adjustment of receptive fields according to input feature maps. This adaptation facilitates effective multi-scale feature extraction, markedly benefiting image segmentation tasks.87

Traditional ResNet-50 CNN are limited in capturing long-range dependencies due to local receptive fields. Meanwhile, current research predominantly focuses on static images, with little exploration of acupoint localization within dynamic images. Addressing this, Li et al.27 proposed Acupoint Image Registration Network (AIR-Net), integrating a Transformer into ResNet-50 to leverage self-attention for long-range correlation and dynamic image localization. Trained on 89,951 hand image pairs, the model exceeded 90% accuracy, though real-world generalization remains a concern due to controlled training conditions.

In addition to ResNet-50, ResNet-18, and 101 were also used as backbone networks for acupoint localization. For example, Zhang et al. developed FADbR21 based on ResNet-18, combining self-supervised and supervised learning for facial acupoint detection. The model achieved 1.86% NME on 654 images, performing better near densely distributed key points. Notably, the model exhibits higher detection accuracy for acupoints near facial key points (such as Yuyao and Tongziliao), whereas its accuracy is relatively lower in regions where key points are sparsely distributed (such as ST2 and ST5).

This phenomenon might be because the core mechanism of this acupoint detection model relies on the image reconstruction of facial key points, i.e., the model mainly focuses on the reconstruction of local features and key points of the face by the self-supervised learning phase. Therefore, the model focuses on deep features and implicit knowledge related to facial key points during the learning process. For the areas around the key points of the face that are densely distributed and easy to recognize, the model could capture the locations of the relevant acupoints more accurately. However, for those regions where the key points are sparser, the model has limitations in extracting tacit knowledge, resulting in a decrease in the accuracy of acupoint detection in these regions.

Wei et al. proposed a Faster RCNN model for acupoint localization based on ResNet-101 CNN.28 In this study, a dataset of 2,000 labeled hand images was used for model training and testing, while a multi-task loss function, including classification and bounding box regression losses, was used and optimized iteratively to achieve a mean average precision (mAP) of about 92% on the test set, demonstrating good accuracy and stability.

In summary, ResNet-based architectures have been extensively applied in facial, hand, and back acupoint localization, yielding promising results. Nonetheless, challenges remain in generalizability under real-world conditions and in capturing global image features. Future research should explore models incorporating self-attention and Transformer modules to improve robustness and accuracy, especially for dynamic image localization.

Application of HRNet in automatic localization of acupoints

ResNet utilizes residual blocks and skip connections to facilitate deeper convolutional operations and alleviate the vanishing gradient problem. However, its architecture, which compresses high-resolution representations into lower resolutions, may result in the loss of critical spatial information, limiting its effectiveness in tasks requiring fine-grained localization, such as semantic segmentation, pose estimation, and object detection. To address this, Wang et al.88 introduced the HRNet, which maintains high-resolution representations throughout the process. Due to its ability to preserve spatial detail and contextual information, HRNet is well suited for tasks requiring precise localization, such as automatic acupoint detection. The structure and description of HRNet can be found in Figure S5.

Zhang et al.21 proposed an HRNet-based model combined with resolution, channel, and spatial attention for facial acupoint detection. Using 654 grayscale images with 43 annotated facial acupoints and MSE loss, the model achieved an NME of 2.42%, a 0% failure rate, and an area under the curve of 0.7544. This demonstrates the strong performance of model, particularly in regions with distinctive features (e.g., eyes, nose). However, consistent with findings by Chen et al.,18 accuracy decreases in areas with less distinguishable features.

To improve detection in such regions, Yanglu Chen proposed the Attention-based Lite HRNet (ALHRNet),22 which prunes the original HRNet and integrates a hybrid attention module (channel and spatial). This module mitigates information loss from pruning and strengthens feature representation, improving long-range dependencies. The use of Wingloss and refined hyperparameter tuning further enhanced performance on visually indistinct acupoints such as TE23. A robust data augmentation strategy improved generalization under variable lighting and angles. Additionally, heatmap regression was employed in place of fully connected layers, improving spatial generalization despite higher computational cost. The experimental results show that the value of NME of the improved algorithm is 1.76%, which reduces the error rate by 0.74% compared to ResNet-50.

HRNet has also been applied to back, hand, and upper limb acupoint localization. Kuang et al.36 evaluated HRNet on a back acupoint dataset (5,000 training, 1,109 test images, 43 acupoints) under various disturbances (environmental disturbances, posture changes, body size differences, and obscure acupoints), achieving 96.3% accuracy and a mean error of 1.95 pixels. However, its high computational cost (about 32 ms per image) limits real-time applicability.

To address the challenges of hand acupoint detection, Sun et al.31 introduced an improved HRNet with a cascaded hand detection module, unbiased data processing, and the Soft-Online Hard Keypoint Mining (Soft-OHKM) loss. This loss function dynamically prioritizes difficult key points during training, improving model generalization. Validated on 8,035 palmar and dorsal hand images, the method achieved about 3 mm average error and 90% detection rate (at 0.06 threshold), improving accuracy by 6.8% over baseline HRNet. Additionally, the method requires training two independent models for the palmar and back of the hands, which might increase computational demands.

Given the significant scale difference between acupoints and background, and the low texture around some acupoints, Cai et al.42 proposed a coarse-to-fine model combining CenterNet for region localization and HRNet for precise detection for localization of acupoints in the upper limb. The model was tested on 110 upper limb images (LU9, PC7, HT7) and achieved high accuracy. However, limited data and focus on only three acupoints restrict generalizability.

Based on the findings discussed above, while HRNet improves upon ResNet for acupoint localization, especially with enhancements targeting indistinct features, it still cannot meet the requirements of clinical applications such as acupuncture. Moreover, the high computational complexity of the two networks limits real-time and embedded deployment. Future research should focus on reducing computational overhead, particularly for mobile and low-power scenarios.

Application of MobileNet in automatic localization of acupoints

Currently, while the issue of computational efficiency in ResNet and HRNet remains underexplored, lightweight architectures such as MobileNet have shown promise in acupoint localization tasks due to their high computational efficiency. It suggests that exploring lightweight models might be a viable direction for enhancing real-time performance in resource-constrained environments without compromising localization accuracy.

Chan et al.43 integrated the Single Shot Detector (SSD)89 with MobileNet to localize upper limb acupoints across varying skin tones for acupuncture robotics. SSD enhances MobileNet by adding auxiliary structures for precise part detection through classification and bounding box regression.90 Using a dataset of 278 images covering five acupoints, the model achieved offset errors below 0.08, though its applicability is limited by dataset size. Lan et al.30 combined MobileNet with the Constrained Local Method and hand-eye calibration, achieving under 5 mm average error across 18 hand acupoints from 10 participants. In addition, MobileNet-V2 and MobileNet-V3 have also been proposed based on MobileNet. The structure and description of MobileNet-V2 and MobileNet-V3 can be found in Figures S6 and S7.

Kuang et al.36 compared HRNet and a Practical Facial Landmark Detector (PFLD) model based on MobileNet-V2 using the same back acupoint dataset. PFLD achieved 98.11% accuracy and a 1.43-pixel error, outperforming HRNet (96.3%, 1.95 pixels), while reducing inference time from 32 to 3 ms. This might stem from the lightweight architecture of MobileNet-V2 (2.06M parameters) and coordinate regression over heatmap regression.

In auricular acupoint localization studies, previous work has been limited by small datasets focusing only on the right auricular acupoints.58 To address this problem, Sun et al.41 constructed a dataset of bilateral ear images from 252 participants and proposed a MobileNet-V2 based model with an orientation normalization module, achieving an NME of 5.14%. Similarly, for facial paralysis assessment, Huang et al.19 applied a two-stage MobileNet-V2-based framework: first, a coarse key point extraction was performed using MobileNet-V2; then, an acceptable regression of the extracted local images and optimization of the loss function were performed to improve the accuracy of key point recognition and quantitative analysis, yielding an NME of 0.31%, outperforming ResNet-50 (0.54%)20 in identifying ST4, EX-HN4, and EX-HN8.

Based on the three studies mentioned above, it can be found that MobileNet-V2, as a lightweight deep learning model, demonstrates superior performance and efficiency in acupoint localization tasks compared to other models. It not only improves the accuracy in locating acupoints on the back, ear, and face but also significantly optimizes the computational speed and efficiency to ensure the real-time adaptability of the model. MobileNet-V3 represents a significant advancement over MobileNet-V2, incorporating several key improvements to enhance performance and efficiency for mobile and edge devices.

To optimize the processing speed and accuracy of the PFLD model for acupoint localization, Liu et al.26 proposed Faster PFLD models, small (S) and large (L), based on MobileNet-V3. The S version uses ReLU without SE attention, while the L version employs hard-swish activation and SE attention. Results show Faster PFLD-S improves inference speed by 27.34% and reduces NME by 14.39%, while Faster PFLD-L improves speed by 14.21% and reduces NME by 22.04%. These results show that Faster PFLD-S is especially suitable for application scenarios with high real-time requirements and could ensure fast response, while maintaining high accuracy. Relatively, although Faster PFLD-L has increased in computation, its localization accuracy has been improved due to the adoption of the hard-swish activation function and the SE attention mechanism, and thus, it is more suitable for occasions that require high localization accuracy.

In summary, both Faster PFLD-S and Faster PFLD-L outperform the traditional PFLD model in terms of inference speed and localization accuracy, which further highlights their advantages and application potentials in acupoint localization. However, it is important to note that this model has been evaluated only in a theoretical research context, without practical application in real-world scenarios.

Despite strong performance, these models have been mainly evaluated in controlled settings. To bridge the gap to practical application, Zheng et al.25 combined deep learning and AR for real-time facial acupoint localization. By integrating an improved Multi-task Cascaded Convolutional Networks with MobileNet-V3 in the PFLD backbone, and using spatial relationships with 98 facial landmarks, over 97% localization accuracy was achieved on the Lapa dataset. However, the reliance of the approach on facial landmark detection limits its ability to localize unassociated acupoints (e.g., SI18, LI20).

Overall, MobileNet and its subsequent iterations, particularly MobileNet-V3, demonstrate significant improvements in processing speed compared to traditional models in acupoint localization tasks. However, processing speed and localization accuracy appear to have an inverse relationship. This dichotomy raises an important question: how could we enhance the computational efficiency of these models, while simultaneously ensuring high accuracy in acupoint localization? Further investigation into this balance is essential for advancing the practical applications of lightweight architectures in real-time acupuncture robotics and related fields.

Application of VGG and YOLO in automatic localization of acupoints

Some classical network architectures, such as VGG and YOLO, have also been explored for automatic acupoint localization tasks. Sun et al.16 leveraged VGG-19 as the backbone of a Convolutional Pose Machines (CPM) framework. Initially designed for human pose estimation, CPM effectively learns local and global features.91 Given the spatial correlation between acupoints and skeletal landmarks, this method achieved 70% and 82% detection rates for PC3 and PC7, respectively, using an offset threshold of 0.08 on a dataset of 719 upper limb images. The structure and description of VGG can be found in Figure S8.

Fu et al.35 extended VGG-19 for 3D back acupoint localization using binocular cameras. Features from the first 10 layers of VGG-19 were extracted and processed through a multi-stage network to estimate body posture, followed by bone-length-based coordinate regression. Accurate 2D to 3D mapping was achieved via precisely calibrating the camera and the robotic arm.

To address acupoint localization on curved or lateral hand regions, Danish Masood et al.29 developed a method combining RGB and depth data using Kinect v1. A dataset of 600 RGB-D images was used to train a dual-CNN model (RGB-CNN and Depth-CNN) based on VGG-16, which combines OpenCV, NumPy, and MediaPipe libraries for hand landmark detection. Under the condition of 1.5 ± 0.5 mm as the estimated location of the acupoints, the method achieved an average localization error of < 0.09 and an accuracy of 98.7%, though it was limited to anatomically normal hands and palm-side acupoints.

In recent years, the YOLO model has been gradually applied to the field of acupoint localization due to its advantages of fast detection speed, high accuracy, easy training, scalability, etc.

In order to systematically explore the problem of hand acupoint localization in complex scenarios such as different postures, lighting conditions, skin color differences, and occlusion situations, Wang et al.32 proposed a dual-attention cascade model based on YOLOv5 for robust hand acupoint localization under varying conditions. The model incorporates SE and Coordinate Attention mechanisms into the C3 module to enhance channel and spatial feature representations. After hand region detection using SC-YOLOv5, HRNet-generated heatmaps were used to regress 21 hand landmarks, which were further processed using the finger-cun (MF-cun) method for acupoint mapping. The method achieved a 0.0269 average offset error (over 40% improvement) and a real-time frame rate of 39.33 frames per second (FPS). The structure and description of YOLOv5 can be found in Figure S9.

Yuan et al.23 introduced YOLOv8-ACU for facial acupoint detection, integrating the Efficient Channel Attention mechanism and a lightweight Slim-neck module. This enhances multi-scale feature extraction and reduces model complexity. Generalized Intersection over Union (GIoU) loss function was introduced in this study instead of the traditional Complete Intersection over Union (CIoU) loss function92 to optimize the target detection task. The GIoU loss function not only focuses on the overlapping of the detection frame region but also considers the non-overlapping region, which more accurately reflects the degree of overlap between the detection frames and significantly improves the detection effect and convergence speed of the model. Experimental results show mAP@0.5 of 99.5% and mAP@0.5-0.95 of 80.7% on an external dataset. The structure and description of YOLOv8 can be found in Figure S10.

In conclusion, VGG-based models provide deep feature extraction for stable detection, while YOLO architectures offer fast and accurate real-time localization. Recent advances, including attention mechanisms, heatmap regression, and multi-scale fusion, enhance robustness in complex environments, addressing occlusion, lighting variation, and skin tone diversity.

Application of other models in automatic localization of acupoints

In addition to commonly used deep learning models, several other models have also been explored for automatic acupoint localization, including PoseNet,37 Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN)24 K-YOLACT,40 the Fast Marching Method (FMM),38 and graph convolution techniques.39

Chen et al.37 proposed a method combining PoseNet and skeletal proportion measurements to localize spinal acupoints for massage robots. The model overcomes limitations of traditional edge-based algorithms by decomposing the human body into bone-length units based on PoseNet keypoints. The method demonstrated robustness when tested on 53 images with diverse backgrounds and subjects. However, the recognition accuracy of the edge acupoints is low due to the failure to model key points such as the face and fingers, and the multi-pose PoseNet algorithm could be considered in the future to improve the recognition efficiency further and reduce the cost.

Gao et al.24 introduced a GAN-based method for eye acupoint localization by enhancing Pix2pix and CycleGAN architectures. A paired eye acupoint dataset was constructed, and a pseudo-acupoint map estimation network was developed to address data scarcity and improve localization accuracy. The improved CycleGAN achieved a 71.33% output acupoint rate and an average coordinate error of 6.29, demonstrating the potential of unsupervised learning in this domain.

Gao et al.40 developed a segmentation method for auricular acupoints using Faster-RCNN for ear contour detection93 and an enhanced K-YOLACT model with FPN for feature fusion. FPN improves detection accuracy by combining feature maps of different scales layer by layer and fusing shallow detail information with deep semantic information. Post-processing with expert knowledge ensured anatomical accuracy. The method achieved 83.2% mAP and 24.3 FPS, showing high robustness in complex scenarios and contributing to intelligent auricular acupoint therapy.

Zhang et al.38 proposed a transfer learning-based localization method using keypoint-guided region of interest extraction and meridian modeling. The FMM replaced the Dijkstra algorithm to enhance efficiency. The model achieved 102%, 82.61%, and 92.37% accuracy on normal, abnormal, and background-interfered samples, respectively, outperforming models like Random Forest, ResNet, and UNet in error and prediction time.

To solve the problems of time-consuming and non-universal use of 3D models in acupoint localization, Ji et al.39 integrated the Skinned Multi-Person Linear model94 with a graph convolutional network and PointNet for 3D acupoint prediction. The PointNet network95 extends the features of all the points from three dimensions to more dimensions to obtain rich semantic information about the point cloud. A centroid-based optimization algorithm improved alignment with the 3D surface. The model effectively localized key acupoints such as GV14, BL11, BL12, and BL15, demonstrating superior performance over PointNet, EdgeConv, and So-Net.

In addition to the aforementioned deep learning models, recent acupoint localization studies have begun to utilize transformer architectures to capture local details and global anatomical context. For example, the previously mentioned AIR-Net model integrates a transformer module and ResNet-50 to improve acupoint localization. Similarly, Yang et al.96 proposed a hybrid model called RT-DEMT, which combines the Mamba state space model and a transformer module for acupoint detection. This model has demonstrated state-of-the-art accuracy on a private dataset of human back acupoints, achieving an average ED pixel error of 7.792 and an impressive average time consumption of 10.05 ms per localization task. This represents an approximately 14% improvement in accuracy and speed compared to the second-best algorithm.

Vision Transformers (ViT) represent an adaptation of the transformer architecture, initially designed for sequential data, to image recognition tasks by treating image patches as sequences of tokens. Currently, although there are no articles directly applying it to acupoint localization, research has shown that ViT has made significant progress in human keypoint detection, which is closely related to acupoint localization. Specifically, Xu et al.97 proposed a ViTPose++ model to deal with heterogeneous body keypoint categories in different types of body pose estimation tasks via knowledge factorization, i.e., adopting task-agnostic and task-specific feedforward networks in the transformer. Additionally, empirical evidence demonstrates that the knowledge from large ViTPose models can be easily transferred to smaller models through simple knowledge labeling. Experimental results show that the largest single-model ViTPose-G (with 1B parameters) achieves an average accuracy of 79.4% on the MS COCO dataset (used for human pose estimation), outperforming other mainstream models such as HRFormer-B and HRNet. Based on this, we can infer that ViT-based architectures may offer a promising backbone for acupoint localization, as many acupoints rely on keypoints for auxiliary localization.

The successful deployment and optimal performance of advanced deep learning models largely depend on extensive and high-quality datasets. However, acupoint annotation is inherently “expert dependent, labor intensive, and highly expensive,” which limits data size and detection accuracy. This bottleneck greatly hinders the widespread application of models in acupoint localization. Diffusion models seem to offer an innovative solution.

The AcuSim dataset98 is a synthetic dataset designed explicitly for locating cervical acupoints. Domain randomizers were used to train the synthetic human model, which includes six different hairstyles, three body sizes (slim, medium, and overweight), and two eye states (open and closed). Additionally, each synthesized image is rendered from multiple angles, including four pitch angles and 36 rotation angles, resulting in 63,936 rendered images for acupoint localization training. The results show that, based on classical acupoint locations, with an error margin of 5 mm, the accuracy of acupoint prediction reaches 92.86%, validating the reliability of both the model and the dataset.

However, although diffusion models provide a new perspective for constructing datasets for acupoint localization, high memory resources and hardware configuration requirements limit their widespread application. Furthermore, as generative models, diffusion models inherently carry the risk of memorizing and replicating parts of their training data, raising important concerns about copyright infringement and patient privacy.

In addition to traditional single-modality acupoint localization methods, multi-modality acupoint localization techniques have also been increasingly applied in recent years. For example, Wang et al.99 proposed an innovative multi-modal acupoint localization algorithm. The core idea of this algorithm lies in establishing a mapping relationship between user body features and a virtual human model through a novel interactive approach. Specifically, based on a virtual-to-real mapping algorithm for human acupoints, this method fuses two-dimensional keypoints from visual images with three-dimensional keypoints from depth sensors. Furthermore, by analyzing the dynamic information entropy algorithm to interpret the intent of the user, it can accurately identify the operational intent expressed by the user through gestures, voice commands, or finger pointing. Experimental results show that the localization accuracy of this multimodal system reaches 90.8%, demonstrating an advantage over traditional single-modal localization methods. Zheng et al.100 proposed a deep learning method combining acupoint reflex zones and topological key points to improve the accuracy of acupoint localization on the hand. The study trained the YOLOv8 algorithm by merging the extracted hand acupoint map with the acupoint reflex zones on the meridians to generate precise coordinates for 16 specific acupoints. Experimental results showed that, in the open-source 11K hand dataset, compared to the original MediaPipe acupoint localization method, the improved algorithm achieved higher accuracy on acupoints, with a maximum accuracy improvement of 3.218 mm and an average acupoint localization error of 1.737 mm, demonstrating the potential of combining traditional acupoint knowledge with deep learning technology.

Additionally, Liang et al.101 proposed a multi-modal image registration method based on acupoint structure and acupuncture planning. This method aims to precisely align image data from different sensors, thereby generating accurate acupoint images based on multi-modal data. Specifically, the method integrates data from ultrasound, optical tracking, and depth cameras. Since ultrasound technology provides two-dimensional cross-sectional images, while depth cameras and optical tracking technology capture three-dimensional spatial data, the key to the method lies in mapping the two-dimensional coordinates in ultrasound images to the three-dimensional spatial coordinates of needle insertion points and robotic needle tips. First, by extracting features from ultrasound images and combining real-time matching and registration techniques, the method achieves the localization of two-dimensional ultrasound images in three-dimensional space. Next, the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm is used to register depth camera and optical tracking data, ensuring precise alignment between modalities. Subsequently, the ICP algorithm is also applied to register depth camera images with ultrasound images. Experimental evaluations demonstrated that this method achieved an average chamfer distance of 0.9821 in multimodal image registration, validating its registration accuracy and effectiveness. By integrating ultrasound technology with depth sensor data, this method provides a new technical pathway for developing intelligent acupoint localization technology. By summarizing deep learning-based methods for automatic localization of acupoints in recent years, it can be found that despite the significant progress of these techniques, there are still many limitations that need to be addressed. A key challenge is the small size and limited diversity of datasets, which could hinder the generalization of models and lead to overfitting, especially when dealing with larger acupoints. Moreover, many models struggle to accurately identify acupoints with indistinct visual features, such as TE23, despite introducing specialized loss functions like Wingloss and attention mechanisms. The trade-off between computational resources and real-time performance also poses a challenge, with models such as ResNet-50 and HRNet demanding high computational costs, which limit their applicability in resource-constrained environments. While lightweight networks like MobileNet-V2/V3 offer some improvement, achieving the balance between accuracy and efficiency remains challenging. In addition, although mainstream models such as transformer and ViT can improve localization accuracy, the limited size of the dataset and slow inference speed remain limitations, especially when the dataset is insufficient, making it difficult to guarantee the generalization ability and accuracy of the model effectively. In this context, diffusion models, as a new generative model, may provide a new technical path for solving the problem of insufficient data.

Additionally, while current research has attempted to use multimodal techniques for acupoint localization, this field remains in its early stages, with limited depth and breadth of study. From a TCM perspective, acupoint localization not only relies on the relative positions of the skeletal system but also requires consideration of the relative positions of muscles, blood vessels, nerves, lymphatics, and organs, etc.102 The individual differences in body shape and the personalized variations in the aforementioned physiological structures make single skeletal localization methods limited in terms of universality. However, although individual differences and the dynamic of acupoints have led researchers to conclude that acupoints may represent a “large field,” it is important that the specific range of each acupoint remains unclear and requires further exploration in future studies.

It is worth noting that current research views technological innovation as an intrinsic process of “modernization” for TCM. However, this analytical framework may decontextualize TCM and reconstruct it to conform to biomedical norms. This tendency may not only raise issues of epistemological reductionism but also obscure the unique value of the TCM knowledge system.

When evaluating the automated acupoint localization techniques reviewed in this paper, examining them within the broader context of TCM philosophy and culture is essential. Technological advancements should not be detached from the intrinsic logic and core values of their application domains. The variability in acupoint localization is not necessarily a flaw that requires technological “correction”; instead, it may reflect the inherent advantages of TCM as a holistic and individualized diagnostic system.

The application of Ashi acupoints is a direct manifestation of the individualized treatment philosophy in TCM. The term “Ashi” means “Ah, it is here!” referring to the points where patients experience the most pronounced sensations of soreness, numbness, swelling, or pain when pressed.103 These “pressure points” are direct manifestations of disease on the body surface, and their locations vary completely depending on the individual, the time, and the condition, with no fixed anatomical positions. An experienced physician “discovers” rather than simply “searches for” acupoints through interaction with the patient and masterful technique. This human interaction, the dynamic nature of diagnosis, and the targeted nature of treatment collectively constitute the essence of TCM, which current automated algorithms struggle to replicate fully.

However, despite the inherent dynamism and individual variability of acupoint locations, determining the scientific boundaries of the dynamic range of acupoints while ensuring safety and efficacy remains a core issue requiring further exploration in current acupuncture research. For example, as noted in the text, 71 experienced physicians independently marked 23 common acupoints on the same human body; the 95% confidence interval range for these acupoints varied from a minimum of 2.7 cm2 (PC6 acupoint) to 41.4 cm2 (ST38 acupoint).6 Another recent study noted that when different practitioners locate the same acupoint, the selected positions may differ by up to 16.6 mm.7 Furthermore, some clinical trials have also shown that there is no significant difference in clinical efficacy between acupoints defined in traditional textbooks and non-acupoints.104 Although these findings led researchers to conclude that acupoints may be “large fields,”6 the specific range of each acupoint remains unclear, which may be an important topic for further study.

Furthermore, the core characteristic of TCM diagnosis and treatment lies in the practitioner ability to make intuitive judgments, apply empathy, and utilize sensory experiences such as palpation to achieve syndrome differentiation and treatment. Its theoretical framework emphasizes the importance of the doctor-patient interaction and the concept of holistic thinking.105 With the development of modern medical technology, while automation can improve diagnostic and treatment efficiency, it may weaken the critical humanistic elements in the process, leading to a mechanistic tendency in treatment. Existing research indicates that intuitive thinking and holistic decision of clinicians making remain critical factors in ensuring treatment efficacy. The risks of over-reliance on technology also raise ethical and practical concerns. Patients may perceive their care as lacking in humanity, thereby reducing their engagement and trust. Furthermore, an excessive focus on technological solutions may marginalize the experiential and cultural wisdom inherent in acupuncture.105

In addition, the data and algorithms used by AI systems also face ethical challenges such as patient privacy and security.106 During the development and application of AI-assisted acupoint localization systems, sensitive patient data such as body images, facial features, and pathological information often need to be collected and used for model training. If such data are mishandled, there may be risks such as information leakage, identity recognition, and abusive reuse. Especially without established data anonymization mechanisms and access permission management, patient privacy is easily violated.107

On the other hand, TCM and modern medicine are different philosophical foundations and cognitive paradigms, leading to conflicts in values and logic over the long term.108 Therefore, simplifying TCM diagnosis and treatment into a “quantifiable” process from a modern medicine perspective might, to some extent, overlook the cultural uniqueness of TCM. Research suggests that as the understanding of science deepens, TCM itself possesses scientific validity, and its future development hinges on upholding its distinctive characteristics and transforming challenges into opportunities.109 In this process, we should be vigilant against the potential distortions that TCM may undergo when reshaped to meet external standards.

A systematic analysis of the included literature indicates that current research on acupoint automatic localization primarily focuses on technological innovation, while discussions on cultural, philosophical foundations, and ethical norms remain insufficient. Therefore, in the process of promoting research on the relative localization of acupoints, it is necessary to respect the historical traditions and knowledge system of TCM and avoid applying a single scientific paradigm to traditional medicine. In addition, an ethical framework compatible with the development of AI should also be established.

Additionally, the development of acupoint automatic localization technology requires a balance between technological innovation and cultural-philosophical reflection. Qiao et al. pointed out that the cross-cultural translation of TCM terminology faces numerous challenges, such as cultural and linguistic structural differences. Concepts such as “Qi,” “Jing Luo,” and “Xue Wei” in TCM terminology have no direct equivalents in modern medicine, making it difficult to convey their full cultural and theoretical depth.110 Therefore, during the translation process, it is necessary to simultaneously convey relevant information and the theoretical implications of TCM to avoid distorting the technical principles due to cultural misinterpretation.

In addition, TCM possesses a unique and comprehensive knowledge system that differs from modern medical knowledge systems in terms of theoretical foundations, research methods, and other aspects. Zhou et al. emphasized that the modern transformation of the TCM knowledge system should focus on preserving its distinctive characteristics and avoiding mere scientificness.111 In the research on acupoint automatic localization technology, one should not solely rely on modern anatomical and physiological knowledge to define acupoints but should also delve into the classical theories of TCM regarding the relationships between acupoints, meridians, and qi and blood. In the context of globalization, medical pluralism is increasingly gaining prominence. Research indicates that medical pluralism emphasizes respecting various medical traditions and promoting exchange and cooperation among different medical systems.112 In the field of acupoint automatic localization, the technology should achieve complementary advantages between medical systems in a globalized context. It can leverage modern technologies such as high-precision sensors to enhance localization accuracy, while also integrating the holistic concept and syndrome differentiation and treatment principles of TCM, thereby providing new perspectives and methods for modern medicine.

Limitations of the study

Algorithmic accuracy

A comprehensive comparative analysis of the included articles revealed that while the high accuracy of certain algorithms has been widely reported across various experimental settings, these metrics should be interpreted with caution when considering clinical practice. Generally, such accuracy typically stems from well-controlled datasets featuring standardized lighting conditions, fixed postures, and ideal anatomical landmarks. However, in clinical practice, patients exhibit variability in anatomical structure, posture, age, and even emotional state. For example, algorithms that perform well on normal adults may not be reliable in elderly or obese patients, whose surface anatomical features often diverge from those used during model training.

More fundamentally, the assumption underlying many AI models-that acupoints are fixed, anatomically precise entities-has been challenged by both classical TCM theory and empirical clinical studies. For example, Molsberger et al. demonstrated that even among experienced practitioners, the localization of common acupoints exhibits considerable spatial variability.6 This variability is not due to inaccuracy or practitioner error, but rather reflects the inherent physiological and functional plasticity of acupoints in response to individual conditions. It challenges the reductionist model wherein AI systems are trained to match textbook-defined single coordinates with millimetric precision.

In this light, the pursuit of algorithmic accuracy based solely on textbook coordinates may be misaligned with clinical reality. A small positional deviation in acupoint localization may not meaningfully impact therapeutic outcome. Therefore, while geometric precision is a necessary technical criterion, it is not a sufficient proxy for clinical validity. Future AI-based localization systems should adopt a more flexible, probabilistic framework to bridge the gap between computational performance and therapeutic relevance. Instead of rigidly predicting fixed coordinates, models could output spatial probability maps (e.g., heatmaps) indicating regions of interest, thereby accommodating the inherent “fuzziness” of acupoint boundaries. Additionally, integrating multimodal inputs-such as thermal imaging, pressure sensitivity, or patient-reported tenderness-could further enhance model adaptability. Such systems should also permit real-time practitioner adjustment, ensuring automated tools augment, rather than replace, clinical judgment.

In light of these limitations, it is important to emphasize that current AI-based acupoint localization methods are more suited for scientific research and educational purposes, such as standardizing teaching materials or supporting comparative studies in controlled environments. However, their direct application in clinical practice remains premature. To better address this challenge, future research should validate algorithm performance in real-world, heterogeneous patient populations and expand evaluation metrics from location accuracy to clinically meaningful outcomes, such as treatment efficacy and patient response. AI can truly support clinical acupuncture meaningfully by aligning technical precision with the complex realities of TCM practice.

Data quality and model generalization

Although recent studies have shown that deep learning models (such as ResNet, HRNet, and MobileNet) outperform traditional methods in acupoint localization, these conclusions should be cautiously evaluated within the framework of methodological limitations. Specifically, the performance of deep learning is highly dependent on the quality of the training data. Many datasets currently used for acupoint localization are limited in scale, biased toward specific age groups or skin tones, and typically collected in controlled environments. This data bias restricts the ability of models to generalize to diverse clinical populations, including elderly, obese, or ethnically diverse patients, whose anatomical features may subtly differ from those in the dataset standards.

Additionally, the evaluation systems used in existing studies lack clinical relevance. The literature generally uses pixel-level localization accuracy as the core metric, but lacks validation against TCM diagnosis and treatment standards (such as efficacy assessment based on symptom relief rates). This technology-oriented evaluation paradigm, which is disconnected from real medical needs, constitutes a limitation of research in this field.

This systematic review comprehensively discusses various automatic acupoint localization methods, including traditional algorithms and deep learning. The results revealed that in deep learning methods, (1) there is a clear trade-off between model accuracy and computational cost. Advanced networks such as HRNet maintain an average accuracy rate of 96.3% even under complex conditions such as environmental interference and pose changes. However, they require 32 ms to process a single image, resulting in high computational complexity that fails to meet the requirements of real-time applications. (2) Model performance is highly dependent on clear anatomical features. Research indicates that while advanced models exhibit high detection accuracy when processing acupoints near facial landmarks such as the eyes and nose, their localization accuracy decreases when handling acupoints with less prominent visual features (e.g., TE23). (3) Multi-technology fusion is a key trend for enhancing localization robustness. Single algorithms often have limitations when dealing with complex real-world scenarios (such as varying lighting, poses, occlusions, and skin tone differences). Our analysis found that integrating multiple technologies is an effective strategy for improving model robustness and accuracy. Moreover, combining deep learning with AR technology might offer a promising future direction for acupoint localization.

However, while this study demonstrates the feasibility and accuracy of traditional algorithms and deep learning methods for acupoint localization, it should be emphasized that acupoints in TCM theory are not merely static anatomical points but functional nodes that dynamically adjust. For example, a study has shown that acupoints such as ST36 and ST37 can expand sensitized areas and increase responsiveness under certain pathological conditions.113 This implies that, even when standardized coordinates are used for research, we must remain cautious not to oversimplify the complex ontology of acupoints. Future research could integrate real-time physiological data and individualized diagnosis to explore how AI models might capture dynamic acupoint phenotypes, thereby supporting holistic pattern differentiation in clinical practice.

In addition, the results revealed that the scarcity of high-quality datasets is one of the core bottlenecks constraining the development of acupoint localization models. However, while data generation models (such as diffusion models) can somewhat mitigate data insufficiency, they may also pose potential privacy leakage risks. In this context, federated learning offers a promising solution to this challenge. By establishing a federated learning framework, multiple medical institutions can collaborate on training a global model without sharing their local raw patient data. This approach not only effectively protects patient privacy but also helps break down “data silos,” thereby enhancing the generalization ability and robustness of acupoint localization models. In addition, knowledge distillation technology can be used as a feasible optimization strategy to address the high computational costs typically associated with high-precision models. Specifically, a complex and high-performance “teacher model” can be trained first, and the deep features and knowledge it has learned can be transferred to a “student model” with fewer parameters and faster inference speed, thereby achieving a better balance between accuracy and computational efficiency.

It is worth noting that existing research methods still have shortcomings in terms of interpretability, which may limit their ability to gain the full trust of doctors in clinical practice. Therefore, future research can further combine methods such as visual attention maps, causal inference, and counterfactual explanations to enhance the interpretability of models. Visual attention maps highlight key areas in the image related to acupoint localization (such as skeletal contours, and muscle textures), providing an intuitive visualization of the focus of the model and offering a visual basis for explaining localization decision making. Causal inference can identify causal relationships between acupoint locations and various influencing factors, such as anthropometric indicators (height, weight, limb length, etc.), to reveal the potential causes of acupoint variations. Counterfactual explanations involve constructing hypothetical scenarios different from reality (e.g., altering the posture of a specific body part) to predict the localization results of the model under changed conditions, thereby assessing the sensitivity of feature changes to model outputs.

Attention maps, causal inference, and counterfactual explanations may help clinicians more intuitively understand the decision-making basis of the model and its influencing factors, thus enhancing trust and acceptance of automatic acupoint localization.

Although current AI-based acupoint automatic localization research is still in its exploratory stage and faces numerous challenges in terms of technical implementation, ethical standards, and clinical application, with the sustained development of AI technology and its deep integration with the complexity of TCM clinical practice, this field is expected to demonstrate broader and more practical application value in future applications such as assisted treatment and intelligent acupuncture.

Method details

This study employs standardized acupoint location methods based on guidelines such as the WHO Standard Acupuncture Point Locations, combining proportional measurement, anatomical landmarks, and computer vision algorithms to enhance consistency in research and teaching contexts. However, clinical acupuncture practice in TCM is inherently individualized and context-sensitive; practitioners often adjust acupoint selection and location according to the patient constitution, meridian pattern differentiation, and palpatory findings of sensitized areas. Thus, our summarized approach serves as a technical auxiliary tool for specific contexts and cannot yet replace understanding acupoints from a dynamic ontological perspective.

Search strategy

Nine databases were used to search the related articles: EI, IEEE, Springer, Elsevier, Hindawi, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, and Chinese Scientific Journal Database. The search date is from the beginning of the database establishment to May 29, 2024, while the language is not restricted.

The keywords: “Acupoint intelligent positioning, Acupuncture point intelligent positioning, Automatic acupoint selection, Automatic acupuncture point selection, Acupoint recognition, Acupuncture point recognition, Automatic Acupuncture Points Positioning, Automatic Acupoint Positioning, Acupoint image recognition, Acupuncture point image recognition”, “Artificial Intelligence OR Deep Learning OR Neural Network OR Neural Networks OR Machine Learning OR Computer Vision OR Intelligent Algorithms OR Image Processing OR Automatic Recognition” AND “Acupoint Localization OR Acupuncture point Localization OR Acupoint positioning OR Acupuncture point positioning OR Acupoint Recognition OR Acupuncture point Recognition OR Acupoint Intelligent Positioning OR Acupuncture point Intelligent Positioning OR Acupoint Detection OR Acupuncture point Detection OR Acupoint Imaging OR Acupuncture point Imaging” were employed to search for articles that potential match the criteria. In Chinese databases, Chinese characters with the same meaning were used for literature retrieval. The detailed search terms were presented in Table S1. Furthermore, the language is not restricted. Duplicate studies were removed via EndNote 21.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Articles that met the following criteria were included: (1) Automatic locations or identification of acupoints by algorithms, (2) 2D or 3D image research, (3) clearly identify the anatomical location of the acupoints, and (4) peer-reviewed articles.

Articles that met the following criteria were excluded: (1) Animal experiment; (2) Non-TCM research; (3) Clinical Research; (4) Other intelligent TCM research on non-acupoint locations or recognition; (5) Non-acupoint locations; (6) Non-acupoint automatic locations; (7) Non-peer-reviewed; (8) Unable to find full text; (9) Duplicate data; (10) Patent, poster, or review; (11) Retracted articles.

Article screening and data extraction

Data were independently extracted from the included articles by two authors based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. In case of disagreement, a third author made the final decision. The following information was extracted: publication year; name of the first author; region; methods; loss function; optimizer; learning rate; data source; dataset size; dataset link; results; feature extraction; and model comparison.
