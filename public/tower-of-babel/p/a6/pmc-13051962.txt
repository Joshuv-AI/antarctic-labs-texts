Regarding “Serum levels of neurofilament light chain and glial fibrillary acid protein in patients with asymptomatic and symptomatic carotid artery stenosis”
Authors: Kishankumar Mahida, Snehal Rajendra Jagtap
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the study by Jansen et al, which presents serum neurofilament light chain and glial fibrillary acidic protein quantification as a biomarker strategy in carotid artery stenosis.1 The use of z-score normalization for serum neurofilament light chain levels, calibrated against a large external reference cohort, represents a valuable methodological refinement. The authors also merit recognition for demonstrating the feasibility of serum neurofilament light chain detection in asymptomatic carotid artery stenosis and for correlating these levels with North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial stenosis grading.

Nonetheless, the classification of symptomatic status by clinical documentation alone, without stratification by lesion burden or perfusion compromise, may lead to heterogeneity that attenuates the discriminatory validity of serum neurofilament light chain in clinical triage. The bimodal distribution of serum neurofilament light chain in symptomatic patients, although reported, was not analytically modeled to distinguish transient ischemic attack from radiologically confirmed infarction or assess dose–response relationships between biomarker levels and event severity. In practice, this distinction influences whether revascularization is pursued urgently or managed conservatively. The absence of a clinical severity index limits the ability to draw meaningful gradients between neuronal injury and symptom burden.

The reliance on single-timepoint biomarker measurement further narrows clinical interpretability. Given that serum neurofilament light chain levels peak 14 to 21 days post-stroke and remain elevated for several weeks, a solitary measurement may reflect historical insult rather than current risk. This temporal ambiguity challenges the utility of serum neurofilament light chain as a prognostic tool in routine vascular clinics, where stratification for imminent cerebrovascular events is a central goal. Integrating serial sampling or pairing with imaging markers such as silent infarctions or perfusion delay could provide stronger mechanistic linkage.

Interpretation of the reported positive correlation between serum neurofilament light chain and stenosis severity in asymptomatic patients requires caution. Although statistically significant, the modest correlation coefficient (ρ = 0.24) suggests only a weak linear relationship. Moreover, the unexpected serum neurofilament light chain reduction at 100% stenosis raises the possibility of threshold effects, circular collateralization, or misclassification. Without concurrent hemodynamic or embolic risk assessment, the clinical implications of this association remain uncertain. More comprehensive modeling incorporating flow velocities or plaque instability metrics would strengthen causative inference.

We commend the authors for introducing serum neurofilament light chain as a potential biological proxy for subclinical neuronal injury in carotid artery stenosis. Future studies should integrate multimodal endpoints including brain atrophy, cognitive trajectory, embolic signal burden, and longitudinal biomarker fluctuation to validate serum neurofilament light chain as a stroke risk surrogate. This work offers an important foundation for biologically anchored risk prediction in asymptomatic carotid disease.
