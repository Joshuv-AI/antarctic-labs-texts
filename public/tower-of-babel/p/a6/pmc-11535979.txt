Stock assessment of the mud crab (Scylla olivacea) collected from the terrestrial edge of the Sundarbans mangrove forest, Bangladesh
Authors: Md Kamrul Islam, Mohammad Mahfuzur Rahman, Md Hannanur Rahman, Zakia Jahan Shuchi, Mst Sharmin Nahar, Md Zahid Hasan, Ehsanul Bari, Md Sherazul Islam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We estimated mud crab (Scylla olivacea) population parameters to determine recruitment patterns, asymptotic carapace width (CW∞), growth coefficient (k), mortality rate (Z, M, and F), exploitation rate (E) and relationship between body weight and carapace width (BW - CW) in the terrestrial edge of the Sundarbans mangrove forest, Bangladesh. Baited long lines and traps were used to collect crab samples from the terrestrial edge of the mangroves (up to 10 km inside of the Sundarbans mangroves) monthly for a year (Jan 2021–Dec 2021). These CW frequency data were utilized to determine population characteristics. Based on the BW × CW relationship, male S. olivacea crabs increased their BW faster (b = 2.89, R2 = 0.86) than females (b = 2.29, R2 = 0.82). Crabs displayed negative growth allometry, b significantly differed from the isometric growth (b = 3). Both male and female CW∞ was estimated to be 126.00 mm and 113.40 mm, with k values of 0.50 yr−1 and 0.48 yr−1, respectively. The total mortality (Z), natural mortality (M), and fishing mortality (F) of male and female crabs were 1.89 yr−1 and 1.84 yr−1, 0.79 yr−1 and 0.80 yr−1 and 1.10 yr−1 and 1.04 yr−1 respectively. Both sexes were recruited using a bimodal recruitment strategy, with a significant peak from March to June. However, young crabs were present year-round. The virtual population analysis (VPA) shows that males and females are caught at smaller sizes (60–90 mm). The estimated exploitation rate (E) exceeds (0.58 for males and 0.57 for females) the optimum exploitation level (E = 0.50) and E values were close to the projected maximum yield per exploitation rate (Emax = 0.655 for males and 0.647 for females) which indicated over-exploitation of the mud crabs from the territorial edge of the Sundarbans mangrove forest of Bangladesh and hence suggested for proper management.

Introduction

Mud crabs (Scylla spp.), are a significant food and economic resource in many tropical Indo-Pacific nations, including the Philippines, Indonesia, Vietnam, China, Taiwan, India, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, and Malaysia [1]. As an euryhaline species, it inhabits coastal waters with salinities ranging from 2 to 30 ppt [2]. It predominates throughout the mangrove region [1]. Based on biochemical analysis, mud crab contains 15–25 % protein, 1 % fat, and 2–3% minerals [3] making it a widely accepted food product with a high market value [3,4]. S. olivacea is the most prevalent Scylla species found in Bangladesh and India's mangrove forests (Sundarbans) out of the four Scylla species (S. serrata, S. olivacea, S. paramamosain, and S. tranquebarica) [5,6]. According to morphometric, meristic, and genomic analyses, S. olivacea dominates the Sundarbans mangrove wetlands, replacing S. serrata as the most prevalent mud crab species [7]. There have been mud crab farms for many years in Chittagong, Cox's Bazar, Noakhali, Bhola, Potuakhali, Barguna, Khulna, Bagerhat, and Satkhira in the southeast and southwest of Bangladesh [8]. Bangladesh's coastal ecology, particularly the mangroves with their channels, major rivers, affluents, and estuaries, provide a sloppy substratum, that is essential for the ecology, preservation, and reproduction of economically significant mud crab species [6,9]. Scylla species prefer the tidal flats and subtidal zones of mangrove forests. Adult crabs often inhabit more protected coastal and/or deeper subtidal estuarine regions, whereas juveniles are concentrated in the intertidal mangrove region [10]. The number of larval populations rises as the distance from the shore to the sea grows because spawning takes place in the open ocean [11].

After shrimp (Penaeus monodon), S. olivacea has become Bangladesh's essential exportable aquatic resource and stands as the second highest-earning crustacean species in foreign currency [12]. According to the Department of Fisheries, Bangladesh exported over 2500 metric tons of mud crabs in the fiscal year 2019-20, earning over $53 million in revenue. Because there is such a tremendous demand for mud crab on the global market, it is actively harvested year-round in the wild. Additionally, as there are no fully operational mud crab hatcheries, crab aquaculture in coastal pools is entirely dependent on natural crablet stock sourced from the Sundarbans region. The wild crab population is being subjected to significant stress as a direct consequence of capturing such a large number of juvenile and adult mud crabs. It is therefore deemed necessary to assess the population characteristics and exploitation levels of S. olivacea on the southwestern coast of Bangladesh to develop appropriate consumption and management practices.

Stock assessment defines the stock of a population [13] and is important for open-water fisheries management. CW frequency distribution, BW-CW correlations, and growth characteristics are essential in calculating a unit stock's population size. These factors also assist in managing aquatic resources sustainably and wisely [14]. To understand the spawning behavior of a species, it is important to know its CW frequency distribution which promotes quick and accurate estimates [15] for assessing crustacean age and growth parameters [16].

The management of stocks is an effective technique for bringing back healthy populations of endangered animals in their natural environments. It is common practice in fisheries management to use input and output controls to keep a species' population healthy [17]. Before a population stock is handled, its maturity, growth, recruiting trend, mortality rate, and exploitation rate need to be evaluated. Despite its importance, a limited number of studies have been conducted on Scylla populations in the mangrove forests of the Sundarbans [6,18,19]. These studies focused on a specific part of the Sundarbans and did not cover sampling inside the Sundarbans. Thus, the previous studies did not properly represent the actual scenario of the population in mangrove forests, as the population varies with regions. This current study focused on the collection of crab samples and ensured as taken solely from inside the Sundarbans by direct harvesting through hired fishermen. It also covered three mangrove-associated districts of the Southwestern part of Bangladesh which represent the entire territorial mangrove forest. This study examines S. olivacea population characteristics, including asymptotic carapace width (CW), growth coefficient (k), BW-CW relationships, recruitment pattern, virtual population analysis (VPA), mortality (Z, M, and F), and exploitation level (E).

Materials and methods

Sampling location

The terrestrial edge of the Sundarbans mangrove forest (up to 10 km from the locality) and its surrounding regions were prioritized for the research. Samples were collected from the three mangrove sub-associate districts (Koyra of Khulna, Mongla of Bagerhat, and Shyamnagar of Satkhira) to cover the entire Southwestern Bangladesh (Fig. 1). Between January to December 2021(one year), crabs were harvested from the mangrove forest using basket traps (40 baited traps) and lines with hooks (100 baits per line). At each station, two hook lines and 40 baited basket traps (positioned at a 30–35 m gap) were used in the bank of the river or canal for nearly 6 h. Chopped fishes, mainly Cuchia (Monopterus cuchia) were used as bait for both hooks and traps during the ebb and flood tides.Fig. 1Scylla olivacea sampling locations of the terrestrial edge of the Sundarbans mangrove forest, Bangladesh.Fig. 1

Scylla olivacea sampling locations of the terrestrial edge of the Sundarbans mangrove forest, Bangladesh.

Data collection

Throughout the research period, 1929 S. olivacea samples were collected, including 1105 males and 824 females. Keenan et al. (1998) proposed a conventional method for determining the crab species and sex based on physical characteristics. Digital slide calipers were used to measure the CW with a precision of 0.05 mm. and the BW registered with an electronic balance with a precision of 0.1 g.

Data analysis

The body weight (BW) - carapace width (CW) relationship gives an overview of the characteristics and growth of various species. For linear regression analysis, a log-transformed standard of body weight vs carapace width was generated (log BW = log a + b log CW). Coefficient b was tested using a 5 % significance level (α = 0.05). Pearson's correlation was also employed to investigate the association between BW and CW. The asymptotic carapace width (CW∞) and growth coefficient (k) of the von Bertalanffy equation for growth were analyzed using the FiSAT-II (FAO-ICLARM Stock Assessment Tools) tool, based on the CW of crab specimens. If E < 0.5, the exploitation rate is low; if E > 0.5, the exploitation rate is high (overfishing); and if E = 0.5, the exploitation rate is optimal [23].

Using the FiSAT-II data management program, the recruitment pattern was forecasted through reverse extrapolation on a set of CW-frequency data along the carapace width axis, independent of sex. The FiSAT-II analytical tool was also employed to estimate the virtual population oriented toward length.

Mortality

The Z value, calculated from the slope of the length-converted catch curve for Scylla olivacea is presented in Fig. 4. The current rate of Z is estimated at 1.89 for males and 1.84 for females respectively. The estimated natural mortality rate was M = 0.79 year−1 for males and 0.80 year−1 for females. There was a fishing mortality rate of 1.04 year−1 for females, and for males, it was 1.10 year−1. The study outcome suggests that the males S. olivacea are more susceptible to being captured during fishing (F > M).Fig. 4Catch curves of Scylla olivacea in the mangrove forest of Sundarbans, Bangladesh.Fig. 4

A bimodal recruitment pattern of S. olivacea in the Sundarbans mangrove forest was observed. The recruitment pulses indicate that juvenile fish are brought into the fishery throughout the year (Fig. 7). Discussion

Undoubtedly, mud crabs play a significant role as marine export products of Bangladesh to the global seafood market. Despite its huge importance, few stock assessment procedures are conducted in the Sundarbans for S. olivacea. Information available in the literature is based on studies that collected samples from the adjacent locality of the Sundarbans and cover only a small portion of the total mangrove forest. Contrary to previous studies, the present study examined three districts in Bangladesh's southwest where the Sundarbans extend into the country. In addition, samples of S. olivacea were collected from the terrestrial edge (up to 10 km inside) of the Sundarbans mangrove forest for the first time to estimate key population parameters.

In our study, regression analysis suggests a strong association (R2 = 0.86 for males and 0.82 for females) between both sexes of S. olivacea. Therefore, BW and CW are positive in their relationship. However, this relationship was noticeably weaker than other reports for the same species in the South-Asian countries (Table 3). The value of exponent (b) is a significant factor in defining the weight growth rate of a species. The calculated value of b for both sexes (2.89 for male and 2.29 for female) was less than 3 indicating a negative allometric growth pattern for S. olivacea in the Sundarbans territorial edge of Bangladesh. A similar negative allometric growth pattern for both sexes was noted by Zafor et al. (2006) for S. serrata (S. olivacea was previously thought to be S. sarrata, Rouf et al., 2016) and for the female S. olivacea [6] from Bangladesh waters. However, male S. olivacea showed positive allometric growth in India and Malaysia (Table 3). The higher male growth rate is due to the heavier chelate legs of males in the same size group [24]. Similar to this, many studies have demonstrated that males among Portunids tend to acquire relatively higher weights [25]. Slope (b) can be affected by environmental factors such as dietary diversity, physicochemical parameters, sample size, and length frequency [18,26,27]. A species grows in length, width, and weight when the exponential (b) equals 3. In a species with negative allometric growth, the adult body shape is more elongated as compared to the juvenile body shape [28]. In nature, crustaceans grow by molting, which makes measuring actual growth difficult. It is common for crabs to molt during their early life stages and continue until they reach sexual maturity, but molting frequency decreases as they age [30]. Furthermore, growth characteristics may be influenced by several variables, such as habitat, environmental elements, biodiversity, and disease sensitivity [11]. The comparison of VBGF parameters of S. olivacea is presented in Table 4. In this study, we found that the males CW∞ was 126 mm and the females was 113.48 mm. Previous study [6] reported higher CW∞ values (164 for males and 152 for females) of the same species from the Sundarbans-associated locality of South-western Bangladesh waters. This may be due to different sampling locations and protocols. Samples were collected directly from inside of Sundarbans in the present study whereas samples were taken from local markets/fishermen in the previous study where the mixing of fatten crabs is quite normal with wild crabs. The present CW∞ values are comparable with Jumawan et al. (2021) who reported 109 mm for males and 114 mm for females from the Philippines (Table 4). However, higher CW∞ values were reported for both sexes in other Asian countries at the Pichavaram mangroves in India, the CW∞ of the male was measured at 148.05 mm, whereas female S. olivacea was found at 138.8 mm [16], at the Setiu wetland habitats of Malaysia, for the combined sex, the CW∞ value was 154.82 mm [31]. The majority of research discovered that male S. olivacea had a higher k value than that of females (Table 4). The present findings also confirmed the higher male growth rate (k = 0.50) than the females (k = 0.48). The slower female growth may be due to its higher energy utilization for reproduction compared to somatic growth [32]. If the k value of a species is more than 1, this typically denotes a higher growth rate for that species. Across both sexes, k values are less than 1 in this study, indicating lower growth rates [33]. Moreover, it is also noticed that the growth rate of S. olivacea is lower as the k value is less than 1 except Kamrani et al. (2010) who reported a higher growth rate from the Iranian waters (Table 4). The growth performance index (Φ) is one of the important tools for selecting an aquafarming species. The present study showed lower values compared to others (Table 4) except for Jumawan et al. (2021) who reported from the Phillippines and more or less similar to the present report. Total mortality (Z) and fishing mortality (F) indicate the species' exploitation [34]. In the present study, fishing mortality (1.10 per year) for males and for females (1.04 per year) exceeded natural mortality (0.79 per year) and (1.04 per year) (Table 2). Males are significantly larger than females and thus more likely to become entangled in fishing equipment [6]. In this study, the fishing pressure of males was higher than females (Table 2). Using Beverton and Holt's model, a maximum sustainable yield (Emax) was noticed at 0.655 in males and 0.647 in females (Fig. 5). According to the length catch curve (Fig. 4), the exploitation rate was close to Emax (Table 2). It appears that the exploitation of mud crab fishery in the Sundarbans mangrove forest is very close to its maximum yield and above its optimum sustainable yield. Besides this, almost all the studies show that males are more exploited than females (Table 5). One of the reasons, females migrate toward the deep ocean for spawning purposes, but the males stay in their habitat. A significant recruitment peak was observed between March and June among male S. olivacea in this study. Similar to male recruitment, female recruitment increased significantly between May and August (Fig. 7). Nevertheless, there have been reports of recruiting from Bangladesh [6] along with other nations, all year round [16,36,37]. It is noteworthy that S. olivacea makes a tiny cohort belonging to CW ranging from 67.51 to 91.43 for the gears (Fig. 5). As per VPA, the mud crab populations with CWs between 58 and 100 mm had the highest fishing mortality. (Fig. 8).

Conclusion

This study assessed the population of mud crabs (S. olivacea) near the terrestrial edge of the Sundarbans mangrove forest. The CW-BW relationship appears to indicate that males are heavier than females and that the overall growth performance of males and females was slower. Currently, fishing pressure is high, and exploitation of S. olivacea in the territorial area of Sundarbans is nearly equal to recruitment, so sustainable utilization is needed under good management. Some urgent steps need to be implemented to conserve the mud crab of territorial mangrove forests, such as (i) limiting the exploitation (ii) prohibiting fishing during spawning season, (iii) restriction in catch size, (iv) implementing a seasonal prohibition on mud crab fishing. It is forbidden to fish for crabs in Sundarbans and its surrounding areas between January and February in Bangladesh. However, national authorities need to consideration of the recent research findings for the sustainable management of the crab population and hence to continue the livelihood of the local communities.
