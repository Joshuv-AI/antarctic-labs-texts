Association between metabolic health status and chronic kidney disease in the population of the northern region of Senegal: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Modou Ndongo, Amadou Diop Dia, Audrey Geoffroy, Mor Diaw, Awa Ba Diop, Sidy Mohamed Seck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major public health problem worldwide. Data have shown the deleterious effects of metabolic abnormalities such as diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidemia on the occurrence of CKD. Obesity also contributes to this deterioration of kidney function. The objective of this study was to examine the proportion CKD and its associated risk factors within the different categories of metabolic health status.Patients and methodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study in the general population of three northern regions in Senegal using a two-level cluster sampling method. The sample was constituted with a precision of 5%, a power of 80% and a 10% attrition margin. Individuals aged 18–80 years were included in the study after giving their consent. Pregnant women, hospitalized persons within the past three months, patients with general or urinary symptoms within the past seven days and individuals undergoing kidney replacement therapy were excluded. Clinical and biological data were collected at participants’ homes by investigators using a modified version of the world health organization (WHO)’s STEPwise questionnaire.ResultsA total of 2439 participants were included in the study with a mean age of 45.4 ± 16.0 years and a sex ratio M: F of 2:5. Overall proportion of Metabolically Healthy No Obesity (MH-NO), Metabolically Unhealthy No Obesity (MU-NO), Metabolically Healthy Obesity (MH-O) and Metabolically Unhealthy Obesity (MU-O) were 79.9%, 9.1%, 10.5% and 8.4% respectively. Four hundred and thirty-five participants (17.8%) had CKD. Prevalence rates of CKD were higher among metabolically unhealthy phenotype participants regardless their body mass index (BMI), 27.0% in MU-NO and 27.8% in MU-O. In metabolically healthy participants those with obesity had a higher proportion of CKD (20.2%) compared to those without obesity (15.2%). In univariate analysis, MH-O and MU-O were significantly associated with CKD. In multivariate adjusted analysis, the odds of having CKD were higher in MU-O (OR: 2.15; 95% CI: 1.53, 2.98) and MU-NO (OR: 2.07; 95% CI: 1.49, 2.84) in the crude model. However, the association was not significative after adjustment for potential confounders.ConclusionUnhealthy metabolic phenotype and obesity were common in this cohort and were associated with a significant high prevalence of CKD compared to healthy participants. That prevalence appears to be even higher in individuals with both metabolically unhealthy phenotype and obesity.Keywords: Metabolic health, Obesity, Chronic kidney disease, Senegal

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major public health problem worldwide. In 2017, the global prevalence of CKD was 9.1% corresponding roughly to 700 million cases [1]. Through its well-documented effects on cardiovascular events, kidney dysfunction is associated with all-cause mortality, premature death and poor quality of life [2]. The high prevalence of kidney disease is driven by the increased number of individuals with diabetes and hypertension which are the leading causes of CKD [3, 4]. Beside these two diseases, abdominal obesity, hypertriglyceridemia and low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels characterize the metabolic syndrome (MetS). Recent studies have indicated that MetS is an independent risk factor for CKD and the associations are in part independent of diabetes and hypertension per se [5]. Indeed, patients with MetS have a 2.5 – fold higher risk of developing CKD [6]. However, there are still discrepancies regarding the effect of each metabolic syndrome components on the development of CKD. On the other hand, obesity, along with metabolic abnormalities, represents a huge and growing health problem worldwide and is associated with numerous visceral complications. However, recent studies have suggested that not all obese individuals exhibit obesity-related metabolic abnormalities [7]. In recent years, the concept of metabolic health has emerged, describing people according to their metabolic and weight status.

Obesity is strongly correlated with various inflammatory profiles, glucose resistance and cardiovascular abnormalities [8, 9]. However, unlike obese subjects presenting these cardio-metabolic abnormalities called metabolically unhealthy – obese (MU-O), some obese individuals do not exhibit these metabolic abnormalities and are thus described as metabolically healthy – obese (MH-O). Despite normal weight, other individuals may present a metabolically unhealthy (MU-NO) status distinguishing them from the commoner metabolically healthy no-obesity (MH-NO) state.

These different subphenotypes of metabolically unhealthy – obese are associated with varying risks including kidney dysfunction. To our knowledge, no study has been conducted in an African population to explore the link between chronic kidney dysfunction and metabolic phenotypes. This work tries to fill this gap by examining the risk of CKD associated with the different categories of metabolic health status, in a representative sample of adults in the northern region of Senegal.

Patients and methods

This was a cross-sectional study based on data collected in the general population of the three regions of the northern area of Senegal (Louga, Matam, and Saint-Louis) from March 1 to April 30, 2018. Individuals aged 18–80 years living in these regions for at least three months and consenting to participate in the study were included. Consent could be withdrawn at each step of the protocol, including for blood sample collection. Considering a two-level cluster sampling method with precision of 5%, a power of 80% and a 10% attrition margin, a sample of 2439 participants was calculated using the Fleiss methods with continuity correction [10]. We initially randomly selected 10 municipalities in each of the three regions and then randomly selected a number of households (488 in total) proportional to the population size of each municipality from the database of the National Agency of Statistics and Demography according to the 2013 census [11]. In each randomly selected household, five participants were included. Pregnant women, hospitalized persons within the past three months, patients with general or urinary symptoms within the past seven days and individuals receiving kidney replacement therapy were not included.

We used the chronic kidney disease - Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) 2021 formula to estimate glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 ml/min/1.73m2. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) history was defined by the presence of any known hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia or stroke. Diabetes, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia and low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol level characterized the metabolic health status.

Metabolically unhealthy was defined as the presence of 3 or more of these components [12].

Specifically, Diabetes was defined as fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 126 mg/dl, prescription of hypoglycemic agents despite FBG levels or any self-reported history of diabetes [13]. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure (BP) ≥ 140 mm Hg and/or a diastolic BP ≥ 90 mm Hg, any prescription of antihypertensive medication in the previous two weeks or any self-reported history of high blood pressure [14]. Low HDL cholesterol level was defined as less than 1.03 mmol/L (40 mg/dL) in men or less than 1.29 mmol/L (50 mg/dL) in women. High serum triglyceride level was defined as 1.7 mmol/L (150 mg/dL) or more. Obesity was defined as BMI > 30 kg/m2. Physical activity was defined with the criteria of world health organization [15].

The 4 sub-phenotypes of metabolic health status were defined as [12]:

Metabolically Healthy, No Obesity (MH-NO) Phenotype: Individuals with a metabolically healthy profile without obesity;

Metabolically Healthy, No Obesity (MH-NO) Phenotype: Individuals with a metabolically healthy profile without obesity;

Metabolically Unhealthy, No Obesity (MU-NO) Phenotype: Individuals with a metabolically unhealthy profile without obesity;

Metabolically Unhealthy, No Obesity (MU-NO) Phenotype: Individuals with a metabolically unhealthy profile without obesity;

Metabolically Healthy, Obesity (MH-O) Phenotype: Individuals with a metabolically healthy profile and obesity;

Metabolically Healthy, Obesity (MH-O) Phenotype: Individuals with a metabolically healthy profile and obesity;

Metabolically Unhealthy, Obesity (MU-O) Phenotype: Individuals with a metabolically unhealthy profile and obesity.

Metabolically Unhealthy, Obesity (MU-O) Phenotype: Individuals with a metabolically unhealthy profile and obesity.

Data collection

Three team of investigators (each composed of a doctor, a lab technician, and a trained nurse practitioner) assisted by community health workers collected data during field visits that were conducted between 7a.m. and 12 p.m. using a modified version of the WHO STEPSwise questionnaire administered in Wolof, a local Senegalese language. For each participant, information on age (< 30 years old, 30–39, 40–49, and 49 + years old); sex; educational level; history of kidney dysfunction and cardiovascular diseases (high blood pressure, diabetes, dyslipidemia and stroke); history of smoking or alcohol consumption; anthropometric parameters (weight, height, waist circumference (WC), and hip circumference) were measured by doctors during the home visit with a medical scale and a tape measure. Body roundness index (BRI) was calculated using the formula [16]. Blood pressure (BP) was measured three times at five-minute intervals using OMRON® M3 Comfort semi-automatic sphygmomanometer and the average of the last two measurements was recorded. For all the participants, a blood sample was also collected at their home by nurses during the visit for biochemical analysis. Samples were placed in a transport cooler and transported at hospital of Saint-Louis by lab technicians. To ensure the quality of the assays and avoid errors, the calibration of the device was checked weekly, and three control samples were run daily during the study period. All samples were analyzed and destroyed after analysis in accordance to the laboratory quality and safety procedures. Plasma glucose level was measured using the glucose oxidase method. Serum total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol and triglycerides were measured using colorimetric methods. Serum creatinine was measured with the Jaffe kinetic method and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was estimated using the CKD-EPI 2021 formula [17].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented by means and standard deviations and transformed to binary variables according to their respective thresholds as detailed in the patients and methods section. The prevalence of the different subphenotypes of metabolic health status was determined for the overall study sample. A description of the study population was first conducted. Comparisons of the participants’ characteristics was managed using chi-square test, t-test and Fisher’s exact test with a significance threshold at p < 0.05. Correlations between chronic kidney disease (CKD) and participants’ characteristics, according to their metabolic health status, were assessed using a Pearson and Spearman tests. Associations between CKD and metabolic health status were assessed through a two-step logistic regression model. An univariable analysis was conducted prior to multivariable analysis.

Candidate covariates for univariable analysis were selected based on prior literature and significant associations found in the descriptive analysis. To determine the association between metabolic health phenotypes with CKD, multivariable logistic regression was applied. The odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated in crude and adjusted models. The MH-NO phenotype was considered as the reference category. The significant threshold was fixed with p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using R software 4.2.2.

Results

A total of 2439 participants were included (Fig. 1). Participants were divided into four subgroups: Metabolically Healthy, No Obesity (MH-NO), Metabolically Unhealthy, No Obesity (MU-NO), Metabolically Healthy, Obesity (MH-O) and Metabolically Unhealthy, Obesity (MU-O) consisting of 1754, 222, 257, and 206 individuals respectively. Four hundred and twenty-eight (17.5%) were metabolically unhealth and 463 (20.0%) were obese. The mean age of the participants was 45.4 ± 16.0 years with a sex ratio male: female of 2:5 (p < 0.001). Socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants according to their metabolic health status are compared in Table 1a. Nearly half of the participants (47.7%) never attended school, a third (30.6%) had physical activity and a quarter (28.4%) had a history of cardiovascular disease (p < 0.001). Diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidemia were present in 5.9%, 47.8% and 59.7% of participants respectively. Metabolically unhealthy individuals whether normal weight or obese were older and had a lower eGFR than healthy metabolic subjects. Compared to other subgroups, MH-NO had a lower body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), height/weight and body roundness index (BRI) (p < 0.001). Clinico-biological characteristics of the study participants according to their metabolic health status are compared in Table 1b. The proportion of chronic kidney disease (CKD) was significantly different between subgroups of participants (Fig. 2). Indeed, only 15.2% of MH-NO individuals had a CKD while this prevalence was 20.2%, 27.0% and 27.8% in MH-O, MU-NO and MU-O participants respectively (p < 0.001). A significantly lower eGFR was observed in MU-NO participants. The correlation coefficients of eGFR with anthropometric and biochemical parameters throughout the different metabolic health phenotypes and in the total study sample are shown in Table 2. A significant negative correlation was observed between eGFR and age, sex, BMI, WC, waist hip-ratio (WHR), BRI, blood pressure, fasting blood glucose (FBG) and lipid profiles. Mean eGFR were higher in participants with zero metabolic health component (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, we assessed metabolic health status and its association with kidney dysfunction using data from a sample of adult of the general population of the northern Senegal. The mean age of the participants were 45.4 years with a sex ratio M: F of 0.40. Metabolic disorders were common with a prevalence of 17.5% of unhealthy metabolic phenotypes. Specifically, 59.7%, 47.8% and 5.9% of participants had dyslipidemia, hypertension and diabetes respectively. Previous studies of diverse metabolic components in Senegalese subjects reported a prevalence of 63.8% [18], 46.0% [19] and 7.6% [20] of dyslipidemia, hypertension and diabetes respectively. These abnormities are known to be major cardiovascular risk factor that exposes individuals to multiple complications including chronic kidney disease (CKD) [21, 22]. Indeed, level of blood pressure, fasting blood glucose and lipid parameters were negatively correlated to eGFR (p < 0.001). The prevalence of CKD in our cohort was higher in metabolically unhealthy participants (27.4%) compared to metabolically healthy participants (15.8%) regardless their BMI. This was illustrated by several studies which showed a high risk of CKD associated to metabolic syndrome regardless of BMI [23].

Of note, the metabolically unhealthy individuals were significantly less educated than metabolically healthy individuals. Low educational level is a common barrier to disease awareness for population, as many illiterate patients will not assimilate correctly prevention messages and will experience poor access to good healthy food or healthcare once they get the disease.

Our results showed that one fifth of participants (20.0%) were obese with a higher BMI among MU-O individuals (35.2 kg/m2) than MH-O individuals (34.1 kg/m2). Several data have shown the deleterious effects of obesity on the occurrence and progression of CKD [24, 25]. Obesity is associated with a 28% excess risk of developing low eGFR [26, 27]. The prevalence of CKD among obese and non-obese participants were 23.6% and 16.5% respectively. Compared to MH-NO, MH-O had a higher prevalence of CKD (20.2% vs. 15.2%) indicating the potential specific effect of obesity on the occurrence of kidney dysfunction. This association was reported in a prospective cohort study who reported that MH-O phenotype increased risk of CKD despite the absence of metabolic abnormities [28]. Others anthropometrics parameters of fat distribution such as body roundness index and waist circumference were also higher in MU-O participants.

Obesity is often associated with diabetes, hypertension and lipid disorders that can lead to metabolic syndrome increasing the risk of kidney dysfunction due among others to inflammation and peripheral insulin resistance [2, 29]. The prevalence of CKD in our cohort were 13.4% in MU-O and 12.1% in MH-O participants and the mean eGFR decreased with the number of metabolic syndrome components.

A total of 435 participants (17.8%) had CKD, this was higher than the 4.9% previously reported by Seck SM et al.. in the same region [30] showing the progression of the disease in the general population.

In the multivariate analysis, a significant positive association was found between the prevalence of CKD and metabolically unhealthy and obesity phenotypes in the crude model. Specifically, compared with MH-NO individuals, those with MU-NO, MH-O, and MU-O phenotypes had significantly higher odds ratios for CKD. However, this association was not confirmed after adjustment. A possible reason for these inconsistent results could be the influence of confounding factors, such as body composition, fat distribution abnormalities, and decreased insulin sensitivity [31].

Thus, further research must be conducted to better establish the link between kidney dysfunction and metabolic health phenotype in African population.

The present study has limitations due first of all to its cross-sectional design that could not help to differentiate CKD cases from possible individuals with acute kidney injury. We did not have participants’ previous serum creatinine or a second measurement 3 months apart and other markers of kidney damage (albuminuria, urinary sediment abnormalities, morphological abnormalities, etc.) but the one shot measurement is widely use in epidemiological surveys at population level [32].

Moreover, the under-representation of men, who were often at work during the household visits, may limit the generalization of the results. However, this issue serves as an important benchmark for future studies in similar populations to consider the timing of visits. Additionally, women have historically been underrepresented in clinical studies on cardiovascular disease with their participation estimated to range from 3 to 13% across a spectrum of cardiovascular diseases [33].

Conclusion

Unhealthy metabolic phenotype and obesity were common in Senegalese adults and were associated with a higher prevalence of CKD compared to healthy participants. The prevalence appears to be even higher in individuals with both metabolically unhealthy phenotype and obesity. Effective awareness programs promoting physical activity and on reducing the burden of chronic non commutable disease such as diabetes and hypertension will help reduce the burden of chronic metabolic diseases. Close monitoring of people who already have metabolic abnormalities will also allow earlier diagnosis and management of CKD.
