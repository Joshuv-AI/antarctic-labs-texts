Protecting High-Vulnerability Elderly in Climate Emergencies: Lessons from Beijing’s 2025 Floods
Authors: Guanhao He, Jianxiong Hu, Wenjun Ma
Published: 2026

Global warming has heightened the prevalence of extreme weather events, affecting 91%, 42%, and 27% of global regions for heatwaves, heavy rainfall, and droughts, respectively, and the frequency, severity, and duration of these events are projected to increase substantially in the future. In China, the climate extremes coincide with a rapidly aging population, a demographic highly vulnerable to climate impacts, potentially exacerbating future climate-related health burdens. −

Previous studies have established a clear link between extreme weather events and increased health risk among the elderly. For instance, heat-related mortality among people aged over 70 has risen by 300.63% in 2023 compared with the 1990s globally, far exceeding the increase observed among those aged <70 years (56.95%). In China, a national study found that people ≥ 65 years old (excess risk [ER] = 9.89%, 95%CI: 8.74–11.04%) were more vulnerable to heatwave than those aged <65 (ER = 3.91%, 95%CI: 2.68–5.15%). For heavy rainfall, older adults had higher accidental mortality during extreme precipitation (ER = 323.40%, 95%CI: 110.14–753.09%) than overall population (ER = 13.0%, 95%CI: 7.0–9.3%), while circulatory mortality risk was also elevated among the elderly (ER = 3.53%, 95%CI: 1.26–5.85% vs overall ER = 4.3%, 95%CI: 2.0–6.6%). A recent study also indicated that the association between flood events and respiratory mortality was stronger in communities with a higher proportion of older residents.

Recent evidence indicates that the disease burden attributed to climate extremes in China has exhibited a significant rising trend over time. For instance, heatwave-related deaths among adults aged 75 and over surged from 2,119 in 1979 to 7,918 in 2020, with a higher burden in eastern and central China. Another study also demonstrated a 269.83% increase in the compound hot-drought extreme related mortality burden from 1990s to 2010s, with a greater burden in southwest China. According to records from the Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters, since 2010, floods have caused approximately 5,520 deaths and 7304 injuries per year on average worldwide. Figure shows the substantial and direct health impacts of floods, including the loss of life and a high burden of physical injuries. Among all affected countries, China, India and Pakistan rank as the top three, each having recorded more than 7,000 flood-related deaths over the past 25 years (Figure ). A recent study reported 416 flood-attributable deaths annually in China, accounting for 0.327% of the total mortality, but temporal estimates of the flood-related health burden for the Chinese elderly remain unavailable. However, their inherent vulnerability, coinciding with intensive flooding, suggests a rising trend. The 2025 Beijing flood disaster provides a salient example of how extreme climate hazards can interact with population aging to generate disproportionate health impacts among the disabled elderly. From July 23 to 29, 2025, unprecedented rainfall triggered catastrophic flooding across Beijing, affecting over 300,000 residents and resulting in 44 fatalities with 9 persons missing. Among the deceased, 31 were partially and fully disabled old people from a nursing center in Miyun District. Advanced age, high rates of chronic disease, and limited physical/cognitive capacities impeded the oldest and disabled people’s ability to evacuate during this unprecedented event.

This case underscores that even megacities with advanced disaster-response infrastructure (e.g., Beijing) may fail to adequately protect the elderly during extreme climate events. To mitigate global warming-related health risks, comprehensive strategies enhancing climate resilience for old people is imperative: ,

First of all, from a policy perspective, emergency preparedness plans should include specific measures to identify, reach, and evacuate disabled and institutionalized elderly. Infrastructure standards must integrate health impact assessments and redefine ″safe zones″ using risk models, ensuring care facilities have backup power and communication systems. Different regions need tailored strategies, and stronger coordination across governmental sectors can align climate and aging policies with shared goals and funding.

Second, infrastructure efforts should focus on barrier-free environments and age-friendly home upgrades in high-risk flood and heat areas. Community centers and care facilities can double as emergency shelters with backup power and cooling. Safe, affordable public transport is also vital, along with shaded walkways that connect senior housing to key services and provide accessible evacuation routes during climate extremes. Health services should (1) reach into communities and homes, embedding climate-aware chronic disease management, like heat action plans for heart patients, (2) integrate traditional Chinese medicine with elder care to ensure continuous support during and after extreme events; (3) address mental health needs after climate disasters, and build chronic disease prevention into home and community care through personalized emergency plans; (4) use weather-health services to deliver clear risk messages to older adults.

Third, the older adults should be delivered clear risk messages before and during climate extremes. They should be encouraged to take active roles in climate resilience through disaster networks, volunteering, or continued work. Fitness programs and senior events should follow heat-safe guidelines, and caregiver training must include emergency response, backed by strong long-term care insurance.

Fourth, a clear research agenda is needed. It is necessary to map high-risk zones by combining climate data with information on nursing homes and disability rates, and to track health impacts by age to fill data gaps. It is important to evaluate early warning systems to see if they prompt timely actions and to assess the cost-effectiveness of dual-purpose projects, such as shaded walkways with drainage or accessible shelters with backup power, to inform smart public investment.
