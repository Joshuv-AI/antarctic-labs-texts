Multi‐Parametric Magnetic Resonance Imaging to Assess Empagliflozin Effect on the Kidneys in People With Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus: A Pilot Clinical Trial
Authors: Ruth P. Lim, Salma Qassin, Lucy McKenna, Emma Hornsey, Dominic Italiano, Leonid Churilov, Richard J. MacIsaac, Jas‐mine Seah, Elif I. Ekinci, The Austin EMPA‐MRI Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsEmpagliflozin is renoprotective in Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Our primary aim was to assess empagliflozin's effect on glomerular filtration rate and renal vascular resistance using multiparametric MRI (MpMRI). Our secondary aim was to assess the impact of empagliflozin on additional MpMRI‐derived metrics including markers of tissue oxygenation and microstructure.Materials and MethodsProspective randomised open‐label crossover clinical trial of adults with T2DM with eGFR ≥ 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 and any degree of albuminuria (CKD KDIGO G1, 2 or GF3a, A1–A3). MpMRI was performed at baseline and 12‐weeks post‐empagliflozin or active comparator sitagliptin therapy in random order with an interval washout period. Brochner‐Mortensen corrected MRI‐derived GFR (MRI‐GFR), renal vascular resistance (RVR), renal blood flow (RBF) and markers of tissue hypoxia (R2*) and microstructure were measured. Impact of empagliflozin or sitagliptin on MpMRI and laboratory metrics were assessed with mixed‐effect linear regression.ResultsTwelve participants (8 male, 4 female) completed all study visits. Following empagliflozin, there were significant mean decreases in MRI‐GFR (3.03 mL/min/1.73 m2) and RBF (41.24 mL/min) and increased medullary hypoxia (R2* 1.81 s−1) compared to sitagliptin, all p < 0.05. There were no statistically significant changes to RVR, corticomedullary microstructure, eGFR or albuminuria observed with empagliflozin.ConclusionsMultiparametric MRI can non‐invasively demonstrate local renal impacts of empagliflozin beyond that of traditional markers of renal function, including decreased GFR and medullary hypoxia. Lack of RVR change may relate to lower systemic blood pressure, concurrent medication and efferent arteriolar vasodilation in T2DM. Trial Registration: ACTRN12619000933156

Introduction

Sodium glucose transport 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) are recommended first‐line agents in people with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1]. SGLT2is are renoprotective in T2DM [2, 3, 4] irrespective of baseline renal function, mitigating against acute kidney injury and preventing progression to end‐stage renal disease [5]. Importantly, empagliflozin is effective in slowing progression to CKD before it is even established, as shown in several trials of renal impact of SGLT2is [6, 7, 8, 9].

SGLT2is block glucose and sodium reabsorption within the proximal tubule, reducing systemic blood pressure, with reduced glucose reabsorption improving glycaemic control and promoting weight loss and improved glycaemic control [10]. Higher sodium concentration at the macula densa activates tubuloglomerular feedback, resulting in afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction in Type 1 diabetes [10] and/or efferent arteriolar vasodilation in T2DM with pre‐existing afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction [11]. SGLT2i can also ameliorate hyperglycaemia‐induced glomerular hyperfiltration [10] in T2DM and reduce oxidative stress, inflammation and fibrosis as reported in animal studies that all contribute to the renal benefits of this class of medication [12].

Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) based on serum creatinine suffers from imprecision in clinical practice, especially when glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is normal, high or rapidly changing [13, 14]. Other methods incorporating other endogenous markers (e.g., cystatin C) or directly measured GFR can improve accuracy, but due to cost and practicalities are limited in clinical practice [15].

Multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MpMRI) offers promise for non‐invasively quantifying renal morphology and function [16], including measuring glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and renal haemodynamics through dynamic contrast enhanced (DCE) imaging [17], arterial spin labelling or phase contrast imaging [18, 19, 20]. Blood oxygen level dependent imaging (BOLD) depicts tissue oxygen and diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) help delineate kidney microstructure. Parenchymal fibrosis, fat content, inflammation and volume can be quantified with T1‐weighted imaging. Detectable differences in MpMRI techniques have been described in people with T2DM, including after empagliflozin administration [21, 22, 23]. In this work, we study changes to functional MpMRI metrics in people with T2DM following empagliflozin therapy.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

This was a prospective single‐centre randomised open‐label crossover clinical trial, with institutional ethics approval (Austin Health Research and Ethics Committee) and retrospective trial registration (ACTRN12619000933156). The study conformed to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and all subjects provided informed consent. People with T2DM attending diabetes clinics were recruited from May 2017 to April 2023. Eligibility criteria included age 18–75 years, no previous SGLT2i, GLP‐1RA or DPP4i and CKD KDIGO G2 or GF3a A1–A3 (45–89 mL/min/1.73 m2, any degree of albuminuria). Exclusion criteria included anaemia, as this could confound BOLD‐derived metrics.

Due to recruitment challenges including SGLT2i use and COVID‐19 pandemic‐related visit restrictions, we revised eligibility criteria to include those already taking SGLT2i, DPP4i or GLP‐1RA after a 2‐week washout and CKD KDIGO G1 A1‐3 (i.e., eGFR ≥ 45 mL/min/1.73 m2) (Table S1). All participants received dietary, medication and glucose‐monitoring education upon trial entry with nursing and endocrinologist care throughout and up to 4 weeks post‐trial.

Endpoints

The primary aim was to assess the change in endpoints of MRI‐derived Brochner‐Mortensen corrected GFR (MRI‐GFR) and renal vascular resistance (RVR) in T2DM following empagliflozin therapy. We hypothesised that empagliflozin would decrease MRI‐GFR and increase RVR due to activation of tubuloglomerular feedback. Sitagliptin was selected as an active comparator as it is also a glucose‐lowering agent that is not known to impact tubuloglomerular feedback. This approach should theoretically control for any glucose‐lowering effect on the metrics of interest [24].

Our secondary aims were to assess the impact of empagliflozin on additional MpMRI‐derived functional and structural metrics, with end points of change in MRI‐derived RBF, renal plasma flow (RPF), filtration fraction (FF) and changes in tissue oxygenation (cortical and medullary R2*) and tissue microstructure (cortical and medullary FA and ADC) and total kidney volume.

An exploratory aim was to study associations between MRI‐derived metrics derived at the baseline visit and laboratory markers of renal function and glycaemic control, with specific endpoints of relationships between selected MRI‐derived metrics (MRI‐GFR, total RPF, FF, medullary R2*, medullary FA, cortical and medullary ADC and TKV) and HbA1c, serum aldosterone, presence of microalbuminuria or macroalbuminuria and creatinine‐based eGFR (CKD‐EPI 2021).

Study Design (Figure 1)

Study design. Prospective crossover trial, with participants randomised to receive either empagliflozin or sitagliptin first. Identical testing was performed at 4 study visits, including clinical examination, laboratory testing and multi‐parametric (MpMRI) of the kidneys. Dashed green lines depict the timing of each study visit, with a baseline visit prior to commencement and a post‐intervention visit for each medication regimen.

Participants were randomised to receive either empagliflozin (10 mg) or sitagliptin (100 mg) first for 12–14 weeks, then the alternate drug regimen after a 4‐week washout.

Identical pre‐ and post‐drug MRI and laboratory tests were performed at each timepoint. MRI was performed following an overnight fast with controlled fluid intake (250 mL of oral water before MRI). Height, weight, body mass index (BMI), blood pressure, heart rate and a foot examination, due to elevated risk of amputations in a canagliflozin (another SGLT2i‐related) trial [25], were recorded. Adverse events were recorded and managed as clinically appropriate.

MpMRI

Participants were scanned on a 3 T MRI (Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Pre‐ and post‐contrast volumetric T1‐weighted imaging (T1WI) and T2‐weighted imaging for morphology, BOLD, phase contrast imaging (PC) for RBF, DTI for tissue microstructure and DCE for MRI‐GFR and perfusion characteristics were performed. 10 mL intravenous gadoterate meglumine (Dotarem, Guerbet, Lane Cove, Australia) was administered for DCE imaging and post‐contrast T1WI. Total scan time was 1 h. Specific imaging parameters are provided in Table S2.

Laboratory Metrics

All laboratory metrics collected are listed in Table S3 in the supplementary appendix. This includes serum creatinine used for the calculation of eGFR using 2021 CKD Epidemiology Collective (CKD‐EPI) Creatinine‐based and Creatinine‐Cystatin C formulae [26], 24‐h urine for assessment of albuminuria and Brochner‐Mortensen corrected GFR (iohexol‐GFR). The latter was obtained from serial venous blood samples following pre‐hydration and intravenous administration of 5 mL non‐ionic iohexol (Omnipaque 300, GE Healthcare), using a validated high‐performance liquid chromatography assay [27].

Image Analysis

MRI‐GFR, RVR, cortical, medullary and total renal plasma flow (RPF_cortex, RPF_medulla, RPF_total respectively), RBF, mean transit time for filtered contrast (MTT_K) and unfiltered contrast, reflecting the vascular pathway through the kidney (MTT_A) and FF were derived from DCE. Cortical and medullary R2* (higher R2* indicates greater tissue hypoxia) and medullary to cortical R2* ratio (MCR) were derived from BOLD. From DTI, apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) and fractional anisotropy (FA) were derived for cortex (CADC, CFA) and medulla (MADC and MFA). ADC reflects magnitude and FA directionality of movement of water protons within renal tissue, that is, tissue microstructure. PC imaging was performed for RBF and T1‐weighted imaging for total kidney volume (TKV). Image analysis was performed by doctors with 0–15 years' MRI experience, trained by an experienced radiologist prior to independent analysis, with specific details of analysis provided in Table S4 and example images and analyses presented in Figure S1.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range) as appropriate. Change in MpMRI‐derived metrics, eGFR, BMI, mean arterial pressure and iohexol‐GFR following drug therapy was assessed with mixed‐effect linear regression with participants and kidneys as nested mixed effects. Mixed‐effect linear regression was also used: (a) to compare differences between drug effects, to identify true local renal impacts of empagliflozin rather than the systemic glucose‐lowering effect of diabetes medications in general; and (b) to study the association of selected MpMRI (RPF, MR2*, MFA, CADC, MADC and TKV) and laboratory metrics (HbA1c, aldosterone, albuminuria and CKD‐EPI eGFR). Standard linear regression was used to assess MRI‐GFR associations due to small sample size.

Agreement between reference standard iohexol‐GFR and MRI‐GFR, Creatinine‐based eGFR and combined Creatinine and Cystatin C‐based eGFR was assessed with the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Reduced major axis regression (RMAR) and Bland–Altman analyses compared MRI‐GFR to iohexol‐GFR. Statistical analysis was performed in Stata (v14, StataCorp, College Station, TX), with a 2‐tailed p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Due to lack of published MRI‐GFR changes, power analysis was derived from reported eGFR changes in people with T2DM and CKD treated with empagliflozin [28], with mean ± SD decrease in eGFR of at least 3 ± 1.5 mL/min/1.73 m2 at 12 weeks for Stage 2 and 3 CKD. For a one‐sample design, assuming two‐sided alpha 5%, a sample of 30 patients has 80% power to identify a hypothesised average between‐treatment within‐participant difference of 0.8 (SD 1.5) mL/min/1.73 m2 to the null scenario of no within‐participant change.

Results

Participants

Target recruitment was unable to be reached. COVID‐19‐related city‐wide lockdowns in Melbourne, Victoria, Australia; small proportion of screened patients meeting eligibility criteria; pre‐existing participation in other clinical trials; and length and number of study visits significantly impacted recruitment. Further detail is provided in the Supplementary Materials S1 (Recruitment Challenges).

Fifteen participants (mean ± SD age 61.5 ± 10 years) were recruited into the trial and completed at least one study visit. Three participants subsequently withdrew due to claustrophobia (n = 1), length of study visits (n = 1) and pharyngeal pain with sitagliptin (n = 1). Twelve participants (mean ± SD 61.6 ± 10 years) completed all study visits (Table 1, Figure 2). Participant characteristics included long history of T2DM, high BMI, elevated HbA1c, with hypertension and angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi) or angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB) usage common in our cohort. Renal function ranged from normal eGFR and no albuminuria (CKD KDIGO G1A1, 33%) to CKD G2A3 disease. Fifteen adverse events were recorded, with no moderate or severe drug‐related adverse events (Table S5). Two participants had medications adjusted by an endocrinologist during the trial, but empagliflozin and sitagliptin were continued. Post‐drug study visits were delayed for 2 participants due to lockdown restrictions.

For participants who completed the trial, statistically significant decreases in mean MRI‐GFR (3.03 mL/min/1.73 m2), RPF_cortex, RPF_medulla, RPF_total (24, 4.7 and 27 mL/min respectively) and RBF (41 mL/min) were observed with empagliflozin, all p < 0.01, significantly different to sitagliptin treatment. Mean MTT_A (1.6 s) and mean filtration fraction (1.3%) increased following empagliflozin (p < 0.01), but differences were not significantly different to sitagliptin. No changes in RVR were observed. Review of RVR in patients not taking RAS inhibitors (n = 4) demonstrated mean ± SD at baseline and after empagliflozin therapy of 0.3 ± 0.09 and 0.31 ± 0.07 respectively. For patients taking RAS inhibitors (n = 8), mean ± SD at baseline and after empagliflozin therapy were 0.34 ± 0.15 and 0.34 ± 0.2 respectively. Mean medullary R2* increased (1.8 s−1, p = 0.013), significantly different to sitagliptin. Significant increases in MCR (0.12), microstructural changes of increased medullary FA and decreased cortical and medullary ADC (p all < 0.05) and decreased mean TKV (1.7 mL, p = 0.04) with empagliflozin were not statistically significant compared to sitagliptin.

Following sitagliptin treatment, mean cortical FA was significantly increased (0.014, p = 0.002), significantly different to empagliflozin. Decreased mean cortical R2*, increased MTT_A and medullary FA observed with sitagliptin treatment were not significantly different compared to empagliflozin, with similar changes for MTT_A and medullary FA observed with empagliflozin.

There was no detected change in mean eGFR or albuminuria. Mean arterial pressure was significantly decreased following empagliflozin (6.94 mmHg, p < 0.001), with no change after sitagliptin treatment. Decreased BMI and HbA1c were observed for both medications.

Iohexol‐GFR could not be calculated for Visit 4 in one subject due to laboratory error. However, a decrease in mean iohexol‐GFR was observed in our cohort with empagliflozin (−5.6, p < 0.001) but not with sitagliptin, with no significant difference between drugs (p = 0.37).

Analysis of 15 participants' baseline characteristics demonstrated a significant negative association between medullary FA and macroalbuminuria (−0.042, p = 0.008), that is, subjects with macroalbuminuria had a lower mean medullary FA compared to those with no albuminuria. A positive association of cortical ADC (0.10, p = 0.018) with microalbuminuria was observed, that is, subjects with microalbuminuria had a higher cortical ADC compared to those with no albuminuria. There was a negative association between medullary ADC and aldosterone, that is, a 0.0006 × 10−3 mm2/s decrease in ADC for each 1 pmol/L increase in serum aldosterone, p < 0.001. No significant association between the remaining selected MRI and laboratory metrics was observed.

Agreement of MRI‐GFR and eGFR With Iohexol‐GFR

There was poor intraclass correlation (ICC) between MRI‐derived GFR (0.35, 95% CI 0.21–0.52) and iohexol‐GFR. There was underestimation of MRI‐GFR compared to iohexol GFR; RMAR slope 1.29, intercept −0.54 indicating proportional and fixed bias respectively and Bland Altman analysis mean difference 17.5 (95% LOA –17.7, 52.6) mL/min/1.73 m2 (Figure S2). There was moderate agreement of Creatinine‐based (ICC 0.60, 95% CI 0.43–0.74) and Creatinine and cystatin‐C‐based (ICC 0.48, 95% CI 0.30–0.67) CKD‐EPI eGFR with iohexol‐GFR.

Discussion

We observed changes with MpMRI in people with T2DM following short‐course empagliflozin, including decreased MRI‐derived GFR, RPF and RBF, supporting empagliflozin's expected effect, with no change in RVR. We included dynamic contrast enhanced imaging using intravenous gadolinium chelate contrast injection that enabled us to directly derive GFR. This differs from other studies employing MRI [19, 20, 29] that do not employ exogenous contrast, with MRI‐GFR therefore unable to be derived. Our results align with those of Nielsen et al., who measured GFR through molecular imaging techniques, but also found decreased GFR and RBF with no change to RVR in T2DM subjects with early CKD following empagliflozin [30]. The relatively greater changes in cortical compared to medullary RPF observed in our study likely reflect the mechanistic action of SGLT2is on the cortex. Whilst elevated RVR might be expected with afferent vasoconstriction, the unchanged RVR may reflect concomitant proportional reduction in systemic blood pressure with empagliflozin [3] or efferent arteriolar vasodilatation with SGLT2i therapy potentially via the adenosine A2 receptor in T2DM [31], where limitations for further afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction have been postulated [11]. Similar to the study by Van Bommel et al. [11], most of our cohort were also taking ACEi or ARB medication, with post‐glomerular vasodilation from these medications [10] possibly a contributing factor. Our study population was too small to perform meaningful statistical comparison of differences in RVR between patients taking versus not taking RAS inhibitors—the significance of the small mean increase in RVR observed in those patients not taking RAS inhibitors following empagliflozin therapy is uncertain due to insufficient statistical power. Short‐term decline in eGFR has also been observed at the initiation of ACEi and ARB, likely due to efferent arteriolar vasodilation and blood pressure lowering effects [32], however, there were no acute changes to these medications prior to or during the trial.

BOLD imaging, reflecting changes to renal oxygen metabolism, holds promise for studying DKD, with tissue hypoxia posited as its primary pathophysiologic cause, with microvascular damage impairing oxygen supply and SGLT2 upregulation and hyperfiltration increasing demand [33]. It is being similarly employed in the REMODEL (semaglutide), TRANSCEND‐CKD (retatrutide) and TREASURE CKD (tirzepatide) mechanistic studies in people with CKD and who are overweight, obese and/or with T2DM [19, 20, 29]. Increased medullary hypoxia we observed with empagliflozin has been reported in T2DM subjects with CKD or high risk for cardiovascular disease [21, 34] but not with normal renal function or normoalbuminuria [23, 35], suggesting influence of kidney function on SGLT2i effect. Our findings are physiologically plausible; SGLT2is move sodium reabsorption (the main driver of oxygen demand) from the proximal convoluted tubule to the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle in the medulla, a theory that could be tested in future with 23Na MRI that can depict changes in corticomedullary sodium gradients [36]. SGLT2is might therefore improve cortical oxygenation as observed with dapagliflozin in T1DM [37] and increase medullary oxygen consumption and hypoxia. Medullary hypoxia might be regarded as an undesirable outcome of empagliflozin relating to the chronic hypoxia hypothesis of DKD33. However, measurable improvement in renal outcomes with empagliflozin therapy suggests that hypoxia in this context might have an unexpected physiologically positive effect, increasing erythropoietin production and improving haematocrit [21, 38].

The higher medullary FA observed with short duration empagliflozin treatment perhaps relates to glucose lowering effects of empagliflozin, with similar findings with sitagliptin. A recent meta‐analysis showed lower medullary FA in people with diabetes and CKD compared to healthy volunteers [18], with an animal model also reporting lower cortical FA [39]. Increased medullary FA following empagliflozin in our study may reflect amelioration of maladaptive T2DM impacts on tissue microstructure. As there are no prior reports in the literature of SGLT2i effect on FA, further exploration of this finding is required. We also observed increased cortical FA with sitagliptin. This may relate to reported prolonged endogenous GLP‐1 effect, contributing to natriuresis and water excretion in the proximal tubule (impacting cortical metrics) through GLP‐1 receptor stimulation or upregulation of megalin, a receptor that retards kidney injury and albuminuria [40]. Sitagliptin potentially mitigates against glomerular, tubulointerstitial and vascular lesions in animal models [41], with the elevated cortical FA again presumably reflecting mitigation against adverse renal impacts of diabetes. However, we note that sitagliptin has not been demonstrated to have any meaningful effect on amelioration of kidney function loss in major clinical studies.

We found a potentially lower medullary and cortical ADC with empagliflozin use, although this was not statistically different compared to sitagliptin. Vernstrom et al. previously reported decreased cortical (but not medullary) ADC following empagliflozin [22]. ADC has been suggested as a surrogate marker of fibrosis in diabetic nephropathy, with lower ADC in people with diabetic nephropathy compared to healthy volunteers [18]. Given the short duration of empagliflozin administration, progressive fibrosis is felt to be an unlikely cause of our finding. Other pathologies that impact renal microstructure, including inflammatory or malignant cellular infiltrate, as well as changes in renal perfusion or water handling through the renal tubules, may also impact measured ADC [16, 42]. Ye et al. reported a trend toward higher ADC values in people with T2DM with early Stage 1 and 2 chronic kidney disease [43], possibly reflecting maladaptively elevated RBF, glomerular filtration and tissue oedema. If our observed ADC changes are true effects of empagliflozin, they could reflect decreased tissue oedema (tissue oedema causes higher ADC) from decreased nephron hyperfiltration. The TREASURE‐CKD study [29] will evaluate change in renal ADC from tirzepatide in overweight or obese people with CKD, including people with T2DM, with results of this trial awaited with great interest, given its similar renoprotective effects in T2DM [44]. The decrease in TKV that we observed with short duration empagliflozin treatment was not significantly different to sitagliptin. However, Vernstrom et al. reported decreased TKV in a larger cohort with longer treatment duration, positing decreased hyperfiltration as the cause [22].

We observed no significant change in eGFR with empagliflozin or sitagliptin but decreased iohexol GFR with empagliflozin. This is in line with the study by Iversen et al. in a 12 weeks' dapagliflozin randomised control trial compared to placebo, who found no appreciable change in eGFR but decreased measured GFR [45]. Complex physiologic changes with SGLT2is including haemodynamic impacts, post‐glomerular effects (e.g., increased tubular creatinine secretion with changes in fluid status) and changes to body composition might impact measured creatinine and cystatin C levels. However, other clinical trials have reported changes to eGFR with empagliflozin [2, 46], perhaps due to different target populations, longer intervention periods or lack of statistical power in our study. MpMRI has potential to detect local functional changes at the single kidney level that might not manifest systemically or be confounded by the presence of hyperfiltration and the lack of accuracy of estimating GFR methods, particularly with preserved renal function [47].

Both medications induced expected effects on BMI and HbA1c given their proven efficacy in T2DM management. Only empagliflozin induced a lowering of mean arterial pressure, significantly different to sitagliptin.

The range of techniques available in MpMRI is arguably greater than can be included within a single MR examination. Although we performed PC imaging to assess renal haemodynamics, we found its performance to be suboptimal where accessory renal arteries were present. PC imaging has been deployed in several multicentre clinical trials to assess renal impact of therapies in T2DM and CKD [19, 20, 29] and our experience highlights potential challenges with this technique. We instead derived RBF from DCE MRI, demonstrating the availability of different MpMRI techniques for quantitative metrics [48].

Other trials have incorporated T1 mapping to assess for parenchymal inflammation and fibrosis [19, 20] or quantification of renal parenchymal and renal sinus fat [49], with drug effect on these metrics being studied in the REMODEL and TREASURE CKD trials [19, 20]. However, our protocol provided a comprehensive assessment of renal haemodynamics, filtration, microstructure and metabolism within a 1‐h examination time. We note historical concerns with the use of gadolinium chelate in patients with severe/end stage CKD due to the risk of Nephrogenic Systemic Fibrosis, but ongoing experience with stable macrocyclic and non‐ionic contrast agents in this population has shown this risk is negligible [50]. MpMRI offers potential for additional non‐invasive biomarkers to aid prognostication, with existing markers including eGFR and albuminuria suffering from limitations in accurately predicting long‐term kidney outcomes in early DKD [51]. We found interesting associations between imaging and laboratory metrics in our study, providing potential additional biomarkers of kidney disease in T2DM. For example, a negative association between medullary FA and macroalbuminuria suggests microstructural derangement within the renal medulla, with Zhang et al. reporting lower medullary FA in people with DKD and/or albuminuria compared to healthy volunteers [18].

Higher cortical ADC in participants with microalbuminuria perhaps relates to tissue oedema occurring with nephron‐level hyperfiltration. Higher cortical ADC has been reported in people with Type 1 diabetes with hyperfiltration [52], whereas lower cortical ADC has been observed following empagliflozin and semaglutide therapy in Type 2 diabetes [22] where glomerular filtration is attenuated. Meta‐analysis of subjects with DKD compared to healthy volunteers and with diabetes mellitus but no DKD reported lower cortical ADC; however, this included studies of people with DKD from Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes [18] and therefore these results should be interpreted with caution.

Finally, we found a negative association between medullary ADC and serum aldosterone. Aldosterone increases sodium and water reabsorption and potassium excretion within the (cortical) distal convoluted tubule [53], with elevation in T2DM presumably maladaptive; mineralocorticoid receptor and renin‐angiotensin blockade are known to reduce proteinuria [54]. Aldosterone‐induced cortical sodium and water reabsorption could conceivably attenuate water excretion into the (medullary) collecting duct, with decreased medullary free water resulting in a lower ADC.

To our knowledge, MRI‐GFR has not previously been assessed in people with T2DM. As for a renal donor study [55], MRI‐derived GFR correlated poorly with reference iohexol‐GFR. Our experience suggests that MRI‐GFR may underestimate GFR, with greater underestimation at higher levels of iohexol‐GFR. Technical factors could play a role, with DCE temporal resolution limitations leading to underestimation of peak contrast signal intensity and hence GFR [56].

The major limitation of this study was small sample size. Our cohort had long‐standing diabetes with suboptimal glycaemic control, but with relatively early phases of DKD, potentially impacting generalisability of our findings to other people with T2DM without these characteristics. Use of other medications such as ACEi/ARB and clinical parameters were not controlled for which could potentially confound our results. People previously taking a SGLT2i or DPP4i participated in the study which might impact its internal validity; however, a 2‐week washout period was selected, well beyond five elimination half‐lives of both drugs to ensure near complete elimination [57, 58]. For pragmatic reasons, drug intervention was of short duration, which may have limited therapy‐induced changes. This was an open label study with high risk of bias including impact on patient behaviour. To mitigate against measurement bias, image analysis was performed in random order with subject identity, study time point and drug administered concealed from the readers. Potential errors in image acquisition and analysis could impact the reported results, however we employed best practice based on current recommendations.

Despite our small sample size, we found MpMRI can identify renal impacts of empagliflozin, including reduced MRI‐GFR, RBF and RPF and increased medullary hypoxia. The lack of change in RVR is possibly due to the influence of decreased systemic blood pressure, concurrent medication effects and/or efferent arteriolar vasodilation in T2DM, which has been reported to occur in some studies. Further experience in larger populations on different background medications may help to further delineate the effects of SGLT2is on RVR. MpMRI is a potentially promising quantitative tool that can provide non‐invasive insights into renal function and structure at the tissue and whole kidney level in people with T2DM. It is envisaged that with further experience, MpMRI, which has an only one‐hour scan time, could prove to be a useful tool in aiding our understanding of the pathophysiology of DKD.
