The physiological effect of prone positioning and lateral decubitus in non-intubated patients with severe COVID-19: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Siswanto, Oktaviarum Slamet Utama, Guritno Adistyawan, Purwadi Sujalmo, Gusti Putri Devany Tunggadewi, Primavera Nabila Shafa, Taufiq Rohman, Denny Agustiningsih
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Prone positioning is one type of postural lung recruitment manoeuvre that has been widely studied regarding the benefits and physiological changes of the cardiorespiratory system. However, prone positioning is not very comfortable for the patients because they have to lie on their stomachs for a while when the oxygen therapy and other monitoring devices are still attached. Based on patient observations, some patients will change to more comfortable positions, namely using the right/left lateral decubitus position. The purpose of this research was to examine the physiological impact of prone and lateral decubitus position in non-intubated patients who were diagnosed with severe COVID-19.Methods:It was a prospective cohort study in subsequent people with severe COVID-19 who obtained a non-rebreathing mask or high-flow nasal cannula at a University Hospital between 1 June and 10 September 2021. The study lasted for a total of 2021 days and involved 48 patients. The patients assumed the prone positioning or lateral decubitus every morning and afternoon for 4 h and were measured for cardiorespiratory parameters and blood gas analysis. It has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria.Results:Dynamic changes based on cardiorespiratory parameters and blood gas analysis parameters in patients with prone and lateral decubitus position in patients with severe COVID-19 receiving conventional oxygen therapy (via non-rebreathing mask) or high-flow nasal cannula did not show any significant difference.Conclusion:The physiological effect of prone positioning and lateral decubitus in non-intubated patients with severe COVID-19 are similar. Accordingly, lateral decubitus can be an alternative for postural lung recruitment manoeuvres and warrants further randomized trials.Keywords: COVID-19, lateral decubitus, physiological effect, postural lung recruitment manoeuvre, prone positioning

As an acute respiratory infectious illness, COVID-19 was first identified in December 2019 in Wuhan, Hubei, China. This epidemic is caused by SARS Coronavirus 2. Since the end of February 2020, the infection incidence in China has risen, and it has rapidly spread to other countries. COVID-19 was become international concern of public health emergency on 30 February 2020 and WHO announced that COVID-19 had become global pandemic on 11 March 20201.

The organ that is most severely impacted by the SARS-CoV-2 virus is the pulmonary system. Hypoxaemia is one of the conditions that might be present if there is a problem. A condition of hypoxaemia may lead to respiratory failure, failure of several organs, failure of the circulatory system, and ultimately death2. Patients diagnosed with severe COVID-19 are required to be carefully monitored in the intensive isolation room of the hospital and should be positioned in an awake prone posture in accordance with the COVID-19 treatment protocol (2020). Patients diagnosed with hypoxaemia may benefit from further therapy in the prone position, which is also classified as a sort of postural lung manoeuvre3,4.

Postural lung recruitment exercises are characterized by the intentional opening of lung tissue in conjunction with a shift in body posture that takes place within the context of controlled breathing settings3. This theory takes into account the effect that gravity has on transpulmonary pressure (TPP) in the physiology of the patient’s respiratory system. When the individual is in the prone position, the pressure that develops in the thorax as a result of the weight of the lungs will reduce the TPP by 0.25 cm H2O for every 1 cm that the thoracic diameter increases from ventral to dorsal. Because of this, when a patient is receiving supine mechanical ventilation, the TPP will decrease along the axis of gravity, which will cause the lung to collapse in the dorsal part that has the lowest TPP while still allowing the ventral area to aerate and remain open throughout the respiratory cycle4. The physiological effect of postural lung recruitment manoeuvres on the respiratory system begins with an increase in the TPP, which causes alveolar distension and will increase ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) and will reduce hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction. Although the majority of the research that has been published on postural lung recruitment manoeuvres, particularly prone positioning, involves patients who are being mechanically ventilated, it is believed that prone positioning has a similar effect on the respiratory physiology of patients who are not being intubated. Therefore, alterations in TPP, alveolar recruitment, homogeneity of ventilation, and a rise in V/Q may also be detected in patients with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure who are not intubated5.

Despite prone position that been combined with oxygen therapy can physiologically improve pulmonary function, this position is not very comfortable for the patients because they have to lie on their stomachs for a while when the oxygen therapy and other monitoring devices are still attached6. Furthermore, some patients move their bodies to be more comfortable, and it was far from the standard of prone position form. As part of the postural lung recruitment exercises, the combination of the right/left lateral decubitus with the prone position has neither been extensively researched nor shown to positively alter the cardiorespiratory physiology. This research intended to examine the physiological effects of prone and lateral decubitus position in Indonesian patients with severe COVID-19 who were not intubated.

Methods

This was a prospective cohort study in a University Hospital of consecutive patients with severe COVID-19 who received non-rebreathing masks or high-flow nasal cannula in the isolation ward or intensive care unit between the dates of 1 June 2021, and 10 September 2021. Patients diagnosed with severe COVID-19 and treated with postural lung recruitment manoeuvres (prone positioning or lateral decubitus right/left) had data collected by obtaining clinical, laboratory, and radiological results, as well as outcome data, from the medical records of selected patients. This was done for both primary and secondary data collection.

Patients with suspected/probable/confirmed COVID-19, aged 18 years who meet the WHO/Ministry of Health severe criteria; are patients with observable symptoms in a therapeutic setting of pneumonia (fever, cough, shortness of breath) plus one of the following: respiratory rate greater than or equal to 30×/ min, respiratory distress, or oxygen saturation determined by pulse oximetry (SpO2) less than or equal to 93%; or radiological lesion (infiltrate, ground-glass opacities, or consolidation) greater than or equal to 50%. These were the inclusion criteria. Patients who showed signs of respiratory fatigue [respiration rate >40/min, partial pressure of carbon dioxide in arterial blood (PaCO2) >50 mmHg/pH <7.30, use of secondary breathing muscles] were excluded from the study. Other exclusion criteria included patients who required immediate intubation [partial pressure of oxygen in the arterial blood (PaO2)/fractional inspired oxygen (FiO2) <50 mmHg, inability to protect airway or altered consciousness), those who were hemodynamically unstable, and unable or refused to perform prone position, right lateral decubitus/left lateral decubitus.

All patients received treatment that was considered to be standard in accordance with the interim guidance version 5 distributed by the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Indonesia. Oxygen therapy [non-rebreathing mask and high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC)], an antiviral (Remdesivir), antibiotics, anticoagulants, glucocorticoids, antioxidant, vitamins, and treatment for comorbidities are all part of the standard care for individuals with severe COVID-19. Postural lung recruitment techniques were added on top of the regular treatment in accordance with the standard operating procedures, which included the following criteria:

Group of prone position: Patients who were not intubated with severe COVID-19 were placed in the prone position if their chest X-ray or chest computed tomographic (CT) picture showed infiltrates, ground-glass opacities, or consolidation mostly in both of their lungs.

Group of prone position: Patients who were not intubated with severe COVID-19 were placed in the prone position if their chest X-ray or chest computed tomographic (CT) picture showed infiltrates, ground-glass opacities, or consolidation mostly in both of their lungs.

Group of lateral decubitus: Patients in the lateral decubitus group were non-intubated and had severe COVID-19. Patients in this group received prone positioning if a chest X-ray or chest CT image showed infiltrate/ground-glass opacities/consolidation mainly in the right/left of lung, or prone positioning was not tolerated by the patient or lateral decubitus was preferred by the patient.

Group of lateral decubitus: Patients in the lateral decubitus group were non-intubated and had severe COVID-19. Patients in this group received prone positioning if a chest X-ray or chest CT image showed infiltrate/ground-glass opacities/consolidation mainly in the right/left of lung, or prone positioning was not tolerated by the patient or lateral decubitus was preferred by the patient.

Every morning and afternoon for a total of 4 h, the patients positioned in the prone or lateral decubitus positions (8 h per day during hospitalized). Within the first 24–48 h after admission, patients were observed and their vital signs, SpO2, FiO2, works of breathing, and blood gas analysis were performed. The procedure of collecting data included three researchers (G.P.D.T., P.N.S., and T.R.) who collected secondary data at enrolment separately from electronic medical records; two researchers (O.S.U. and P.S.) who gathered observational patient data; and a third researcher who assessed the data quality (S). The independent t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test is used to compare variables. The data is presented in the form of the mean, SD, median, and interquartile range (IQR). The χ2 test or the Fisher exact test may be used to compare two sets of categorical data. Frequency (%) is provided for categorical variables. Statistical significance was defined as a P value lower than 0.05. All of the investigations were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21. (IBM Corp.). It has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria7.

Results

Throughout the course of the research, a total of 67 consecutive patients who had been given a diagnosis of severe COVID-19 were evaluated for inclusion in the study. 16 of these patients were deemed ineligible for a variety of reasons, including the need for urgent intubation, an inability to secure the airway or altered consciousness, unstable hemodynamics, and an inability or refusal to perform prone posture or lateral decubitus. The research comprised the remaining 48 patients with severe COVID-19. Fifteen of these patients were in prone position, while the other 33 were in lateral decubitus. Thirteen of the patients got HFNC, while the remaining 35 wore a non-rebreathing mask. Because to COVID-19 pneumonia, each patient suffered from hypoxemic acute respiratory failure, with a mean PaO2/FiO2 ratio of 112.9 (IQR: 35.6–306.4) and mean alveolar arterial oxygen gradient value (AaDO2) of 438.5 (IQR: 141.0–748.0). This study found that the mean clinical parameters for all of the patients diagnosed with severe COVID-19 pneumonia were as follows: C-reactive protein (98.2, IQR 20.3–326.1), D-dimer 2597.0 (IQR 677.0–10 000.0), and imaging showed predominant ground-glass opacities and bilateral pulmonary infiltrates primarily in the posterior dependent. In terms of the baseline features of demographics, clinical, laboratory, CT-scan finding, blood gas analysis, respiratory support, and result of non-intubated patients with severe COVID-19, there were no substantial discrepancies between the prone positioning group and the lateral decubitus group (Table 1).

Discussions

The main study finding was dynamic changes based on cardiorespiratory parameters and blood gas analysis parameters in patients with prone positioning and lateral decubitus in individuals with serious COVID-19 receiving conventional oxygen therapy (via non-rebreathing mask) or high-flow nasal cannula did not show a significant difference. However, the results of the comparison of blood gas analysis parameters showed improved oxygenation or gas exchange in prone positioning patients compared to patients who assumed lateral decubitus. Prone positioning was not comfortable for the patients because they had to lie on their stomach for a while when the oxygen therapy and other monitoring devices were still attached so the patients preferred lateral decubitus to prone positioning.

From a physiological perspective, this method is effective in treating cases of severe hypoxemic respiration failure due to COVID-19. In fact, repositioning the patient such that they are lying down in line with imaging in order to optimize V/Q was related with a considerable enhancement in both oxygen consumption and pulmonary function, along with excellent tolerance. Since it has not been previously linked with greater oxygenation potential in studies of Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS), the advantage of the prone position in non-intubated patients is not simply due to enhanced oxygenation as a mechanism. Instead, it is also related to increased oxygenation potential. When the patient is in the prone position, homogeneous lung aeration may occur, which can reduce the amount of effort required for breathing and result in a decreased incidence of intubation8,9.

Patients utilizing a non-rebreathing mask should be in a prone or lateral decubitus position since it is more physiologically appropriate. First, in this posture, “oedema” flows away from anti-gravitational alveoli, allowing the lungs to recruit previously reliant regions. The normal lung might be upside down, which could affect gas exchange, in patients with unilateral pleuro-parenchymal disease. This is particularly true in the absence of pleural discomfort. Second, the increase in oxygenation is helping to relieve hypoxemic vasoconstriction, which in turn is lowering pulmonary vascular resistance and enhancing the function of the right ventricle. Third, a redistribution of the V/Q ratio due to the gravitational pull rise in blood flow to spared areas of the lung, that become more treated as an outcome; prone position made a relief from the mass of the mediastinum and a reduction in overdistension of the healthy areas due to the distribution of transpulmonary pressure. In actuality, there is a decrease in shunt when the dorsal lung is recruited, which has a higher degree of oxygenation in any position9–11.

There has been some research on the benefits of prone positioning for patients with ARDS who are using non-rebreathing masks and for patients with COVID-19 who are trying to avoid intubation, but only few studies have evaluated its effectiveness and the possible effects during the SARS-COV-2 pandemic. In patients who had severe Acute Respiratory Failure because of pneumonia, a retrospective study that included 15 patients found that being in the prone position during Non Invasive Ventilator had a favourable impact. When patients with moderate to severe ARDS were treated with combined prone posture and Non Invasive Ventilator or HFNC, a number of recent studies demonstrated a decrease in the intubation rate12–16. On the other hand, a study indicated that lateral decubitus led to an improvement in oxygenation for 194 (64%) out of the total of 300 patients8. The variations in the patient’s SpO2 values varied from a 2% decline to a 5% gain in SpO2 levels, while they ranged from a 3% drop to a 7% increase with right decubitus. The right lung was the primary site of infiltration in 92.3% of patients who showed improvement when positioned in the left lateral orientation, whereas the left lung was implicated in 69.1% of individuals who improved when positioned in the right lateral orientation. Recent research suggests that patients with COVID-19 may benefit from improved gas exchange while receiving oxygen treatment and noninvasive support (HFNC or Non Invasive Ventilator) if they are positioned in the prone position. On the other hand, there is currently no information available on the radiological pattern8.

There are some limitations in the data from this research. First, there is the possibility of selection bias. Second, there was no intervention to serve as a control, and the sample size of the research was small. Third, it is unknown if these patients would improve without being placed in the prone position and lateral decubitus, despite the fact that a quick change within an hour of being placed in the prone position and lateral decubitus demonstrated a favourable impact. Fourth, considering the promise of prone and lateral decubitus posture as an inexpensive, readily adopted, and scalable intervention, especially in low-income and middle-income countries, rapid but complete assessment of prone positioning in patients who are at risk for intubation is recommended. While the research findings that are going to be revealed here are going to be intriguing, this study is still going to raise a lot of issues. Further research is required to determine the optimal amount of time for treating COVID-19 patients in the prone and lateral decubitus positions, as well as the advantages of doing so in terms of lowering the need for intubation and enhancing the rate of recovery.

Conclusion

The physiological effect of prone positioning and lateral decubitus in non-intubated severe COVID-19 patients are similar. Moreover, due to the difficulties of prone position, the right or left lateral decubitus position is also possible for patients who are hypoxia due to ARDS. Therefore, lateral decubitus can be an alternative for postural lung recruitment manoeuvres and warrants further randomized trials.
