Fine-scale predictive modeling of Aedes mosquito abundance and dengue risk indicators using machine learning algorithms with microclimatic variables
Authors: Nazri Che Dom, Nur Athen Mohd Hardy Abdullah, Rahmat Dapari, Siti Aekbal Salleh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective prediction of Aedes mosquito abundance and dengue risk indicators such as the Aedes Index (AI) and Dengue Positive Trap Index (DPTI) is essential for early intervention and targeted vector control. However, current models often rely on coarse regional data and fail to account for microclimatic variations, limiting their predictive accuracy in dengue hotspots. This study developed fine-scale predictive models using machine learning algorithms; Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machines (SVM) to estimate mosquito abundance and dengue risk at the species level based on daily microclimatic data (temperature, relative humidity, and rainfall) collected over 26 weeks in Kuala Selangor, Malaysia. Predictor variables included single, dual, and triple combinations of microclimatic inputs, and models were trained and validated using 10-fold cross-validation and a 70:30 train-test data split. ANN consistently outperformed RF and SVM in predicting the Aedes Index (e.g., Ae. aegypti: MAE = 0.175, RMSE = 0.248), while RF and SVM demonstrated superior performance in DPTI predictions for Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus, respectively. Models incorporating dual or triple microclimatic variables yielded significantly lower error metrics than those using single predictors. Rainfall emerged as the most influential single factor across species. Variations in model performance were likely due to species-specific responses to environmental conditions and the nonlinear interactions captured by the algorithms. Compared to benchmarks in related tropical settings, the reported error metrics demonstrate improved prediction accuracy. Integrating time-lagged microclimatic variables into machine learning frameworks enhances the predictive accuracy of dengue risk indicators at a fine spatial scale. These models provide a data-driven basis for proactive dengue control strategies, enabling timely interventions tailored to specific mosquito species and environmental triggers. Introduction

Dengue fever remains a significant public health concern in Malaysia, despite ongoing vector control measures, public awareness campaigns, and regulatory enforcement. According to the Ministry of Health Malaysia, the country recorded over 123,000 dengue cases in 2023, marking a doubling of cases compared to 2021, with Selangor contributing the highest burden of reported infections1. This upward trend indicates limitations in existing prevention strategies, particularly in densely populated urban areas. Aedes mosquitoes the primary vectors of dengue are strongly influenced by environmental conditions, particularly microclimatic variables such as temperature, humidity, and rainfall2–4. These microclimatic fluctuations, combined with rapid urbanization and changing land use, present challenges in predicting and managing mosquito populations in high-risk areas5–7.

Previous studies have applied machine learning (ML) models, including Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (SARIMA), Decision Trees (DT), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machines (SVM), to predict mosquito abundance and dengue outbreaks5,8,9. However, many of these models rely on coarse-resolution datasets such as aggregated weather station data or satellite-derived environmental indices, which fail to capture localized variability in mosquito habitat conditions. As a result, these models often lack spatial resolution at finer scales such as the neighborhood or household level limiting their utility for directing timely and targeted vector control measures. Interventions that are guided by regional forecasts may miss micro-hotspots of mosquito activity, resulting in resource inefficiencies or delayed responses. In this study, we define fine scale as data collected at the neighborhood level, incorporating microclimatic variations over small geographic areas (within several kilometers) and short temporal windows (daily data), which are often overlooked in broader national-level models. Such granularity is critical in urban dengue hotspots, where mosquito abundance can vary significantly between adjacent residential blocks due to localized rainfall, shade, or container density.

We selected three ML algorithms; ANN, RF, and SVM based on their established strengths in modeling complex, nonlinear relationships between environmental variables and vector indices. ANN is adept at capturing subtle patterns in high-dimensional datasets; RF handles feature interactions and noise well, while SVM performs robustly with limited and imbalanced datasets, particularly when using kernel-based transformations5,10. This study aims to develop fine-scale predictive models for Aedes mosquito abundance and dengue risk indicators using these ML approaches. The models incorporate time-lagged microclimatic variables to improve forecasting accuracy in Kuala Selangor, Malaysia a known dengue hotspot. By comparing performance across mosquito species (Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus) and microclimatic input combinations (single, dual, triple), we aim to identify optimal strategies for improving early warning systems and localized vector control.

Methods

Study area and data collection

This study was conducted in Kuala Selangor, Malaysia, a recognized dengue hotspot. The region experiences a tropical climate conducive to mosquito breeding, with high humidity and frequent rainfall. Microclimatic and entomological data were collected daily from February 6 to August 6, 2023 (26 weeks). The study focused on selected urban and suburban districts with historically high dengue transmission rates. A map of the study area, including the mosquito trap locations across Selangor, is shown in Fig. 1. This figure is adapted from Abdullah et al. (2024)11, and provides detailed spatial context for the sampling framework used in this study. The geographic distribution of traps illustrated in the map enhances clarity regarding the spatial coverage of the entomological surveillance and supports reproducibility of the study design. The region experiences average annual relative humidity ranging between 75% and 85%, with daily humidity often exceeding 90% during peak rainy seasons. Rainfall in Selangor is distributed throughout the year, with an average annual precipitation of approximately 2,400 to 2,600 mm, peaking during the inter-monsoonal periods between April–May and October–November11. These climatic conditions create a favorable environment for the proliferation of Aedes mosquitoes and increase the potential for dengue virus transmission, thereby justifying the focus on microclimatic variables in predictive modeling efforts.

Microclimatic variables included temperature, relative humidity, and rainfall, recorded as daily mean, minimum, and maximum using calibrated weather sensors. Mosquito indices were derived from adult female Aedes mosquito traps placed throughout the study area. Adult Aedes mosquitoes were captured using the Gravitrap-Outdoor Sentinel (GOS) traps, which are passive surveillance tools specifically designed to attract gravid female mosquitoes seeking oviposition sites. These traps are odorless, low-maintenance, and suitable for long-term field deployment in both urban and semi-urban environments. Each trap consists of a dark-colored container with a rough inner lining to encourage egg-laying, and a sticky adhesive lining to immobilize captured mosquitoes upon entry.

In this study, a total of 60 Gravitrap-Outdoor Sentinel (GOS) traps were deployed across two distinct study sites in Selangor, Malaysia. The traps were installed exclusively outdoors, in shaded and sheltered areas such as near building perimeters, covered walkways, stairwells, and vegetated zones, to mimic natural mosquito resting and oviposition environments. This outdoor placement ensured maximum exposure to gravid female Aedes mosquitoes actively seeking oviposition sites in the vicinity. The selection of trap locations followed a standardized grid-based spacing protocol to ensure uniform coverage and minimize spatial clustering. All traps remained fixed throughout the sampling period, and GPS coordinates were recorded for each site to ensure spatial traceability. Trap recovery and mosquito collection were conducted on a weekly basis throughout the 26-week study period. Each GOS trap was examined once every seven days to collect trapped adult mosquitoes, which were then transported to the entomology laboratory for identification and analysis. This weekly sampling schedule was selected to balance operational feasibility with the need for high-resolution temporal data, enabling the detection of short-term fluctuations in mosquito abundance in response to microclimatic variations. Weekly recovery also helped minimize trap saturation and ensured consistent data quality across all sampling intervals.

The Aedes Index (AI) was calculated as the proportion of traps containing at least one adult female Aedes mosquito relative to the total number of traps deployed, aligning with entomological standards. The Dengue Positive Trap Index (DPTI) was defined as the percentage of traps with at least one female Aedes mosquito testing positive for dengue virus. Detection of dengue-positive mosquitoes was conducted using a commercially available dengue virus antigen detection kit, based on immunochromatographic assay (ICA) principles. Following weekly collection, adult female Aedes mosquitoes were morphologically identified and pooled (up to 10 mosquitoes per pool) by species and trap location. The pooled mosquito homogenates were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the presence of dengue virus NS1 antigen was used as an indicator of dengue infection. A trap was recorded as dengue-positive if at least one mosquito in the pool tested positive for the NS1 antigen. This rapid diagnostic method was selected for its operational simplicity, field applicability, and ability to provide timely surveillance data for entomological risk assessment. Only adult females were used in both indices to reflect the epidemiological relevance of biting vectors. Total Aedes refers to the combined abundance of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus species.

Predictor and outcome variables

This study utilized three environmental predictors; temperature, relative humidity, and rainfall based on well-established evidence of their significant influence on Aedes mosquito ecology and dengue transmission dynamics. These microclimatic variables affect various biological processes including mosquito development rate, survival, and virus replication efficiency, making them highly relevant for predictive modeling in dengue-endemic regions. In addition to general mosquito indices, species-specific indices were incorporated to account for the differing ecological behaviors and vectorial capacities of Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus. These two species vary in terms of breeding site preferences, biting behavior, and disease transmission potential, which may result in differential responses to environmental drivers.

To evaluate the contribution of each environmental variable, three levels of predictor combinations were assessed: single-variable models (e.g., temperature alone), dual-variable models (e.g., temperature and rainfall), and triple-variable models incorporating all three factors. This design allowed for the analysis of both individual and synergistic effects of microclimatic conditions on mosquito indices. The outcome variables used for model training and evaluation were the Aedes Index (AI), representing adult female mosquito abundance, and the Dengue Positive Trap Index (DPTI), reflecting the proportion of traps with dengue-positive female Aedes mosquitoes. These outcomes were calculated separately for Ae. aegypti, Ae. albopictus, and for the combined total Aedes population to support species-specific as well as overall risk prediction.

Data preprocessing

Time lag selection

To capture delayed effects of climate on mosquito dynamics, cross-correlation analysis was conducted between each microclimatic variable and mosquito index across time lags ranging from 0 to 91 days. The 91-day window was selected based on biological evidence suggesting that environmental changes can influence Aedes mosquito population cycles and dengue virus incubation periods over a 2–3 month range4,5. For each variable, the lag with the strongest correlation coefficient (if p < 0.05) was selected. Only one lag per variable (i.e., the most significant) was included in model training.

Data standardization

All predictor variables in this study were standardized using z-score transformation to ensure uniform scaling and enhance model convergence, particularly for machine learning algorithms that are sensitive to variable magnitude, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN). This method was chosen over alternative approaches such as min-max scaling, as it preserves the underlying distributional characteristics of the microclimatic variables and reduces the impact of extreme values, which are common in environmental datasets. Standardization ensured that all predictors contributed comparably during model training without being dominated by differences in unit scale or variance.

Missing values

Missing values, which accounted for less than 2% of the total observations, were addressed using linear interpolation applied within each respective time series. This method was chosen because the missing data were sporadic in nature and exhibited no systematic bias or temporal clustering. Linear interpolation allowed for the estimation of missing values based on adjacent time points, thereby preserving the continuity of the dataset without introducing artificial patterns or requiring the removal of complete records. This approach ensured that the integrity and temporal resolution of the data were maintained for subsequent modeling.

Train-test split

The dataset was split into training (70%) and test (30%) subsets based on a chronological (time-based) split to preserve temporal dependencies. A random split was avoided to prevent data leakage and unrealistic model performance. The training set comprised the first 18 weeks; the test set included the final 8 weeks. This ensured that model performance was assessed under real-world forecasting conditions. Seasonal variations in mosquito indices were maintained during splitting to reflect natural distributional imbalances.

Machine learning algorithms and model training

Three machine learning algorithms were employed in this study to develop predictive models for mosquito abundance and dengue risk: Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machines (SVM). ANN was selected for its capacity to model complex, nonlinear relationships between environmental variables and mosquito indices, making it well-suited for capturing intricate interactions in high-dimensional datasets. RF was chosen for its robustness in handling noise, its ability to model interactions between variables, and its effectiveness with ecological data characterized by mixed effects and nonlinearity. SVM, particularly with kernel functions, was utilized for its strong performance on datasets with relatively few observations and its resistance to overfitting, especially when dealing with nonlinear patterns.

These algorithms were selected with consideration of the nature of the input data; microclimatic variables that exhibit nonlinear, interdependent relationships and the continuous nature of the output variables, namely the Aedes Index (AI) and the Dengue Positive Trap Index (DPTI). As the goal was to predict continuous outcomes rather than classify categories, all models were configured for regression tasks. Model performance was evaluated using two standard regression metrics: Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), which provide insight into the accuracy and consistency of the predictions.

Model training was conducted using a 10-fold cross-validation approach. In this method, the training dataset was partitioned into ten equal subsets, where nine subsets were used to train the model and the remaining one was used for validation. This process was repeated ten times, ensuring each subset served as the validation set once, and the results were averaged to yield a reliable estimate of model performance. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using a grid search strategy. For ANN, parameters such as the number of hidden layers, the number of neurons per layer, and the learning rate were optimized. For RF, key parameters included the number of decision trees, the maximum depth of each tree, and the minimum number of samples required to split a node. For SVM, the tuning focused on selecting the appropriate kernel type, regularization parameter (C), and epsilon (ε) for the regression margin. This comprehensive tuning process ensured that each model was optimized to deliver the best possible predictive performance given the structure of the data.

Model evaluation

Model performance was evaluated using two standard regression metrics: Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). MAE measures the average magnitude of prediction errors, providing an intuitive sense of how far predictions deviate from actual values, regardless of direction. RMSE, on the other hand, penalizes larger errors more heavily by squaring the deviations before averaging and then taking the square root. This makes RMSE particularly useful for identifying models that consistently produce large prediction errors and provides a more sensitive measure of overall predictive accuracy.

To ensure a robust assessment of model generalizability, performance evaluation was conducted using a holdout test dataset comprising 30% of the total data. This test set was not used during any phase of model training or cross-validation, preserving its integrity as an independent benchmark for real-world prediction scenarios. In addition to numerical evaluation, visual diagnostics such as scatter plots comparing observed versus predicted values were generated. These plots allowed for the inspection of residual patterns and identification of any systematic biases or heteroscedasticity in the model outputs, further supporting the interpretation of model behavior.

Results

Microclimatic influence and time lag patterns

Cross-correlation analysis revealed time-lagged associations between microclimatic variables and mosquito abundance indices (AI and DPTI) across all mosquito species (Table 1). Temperature and rainfall generally exhibited moderate positive correlations with AI and DPTI, while relative humidity showed mixed effects. For example, mean and minimum temperatures for Total Aedes and Ae. aegypti demonstrated the strongest positive correlations with DPTI at lags of −51 and − 47 days, respectively (r = 0.24 and r = 0.23, p < 0.05). However, maximum temperature in Total Aedes DPTI was negatively correlated at a very short lag of −1 day (r = −0.27), contrasting with the longer lags of other temperature variables. This anomaly may reflect noise or a transient inverse relationship and should be interpreted with caution, given that Aedes life cycles typically span 2–4 weeks under optimal conditions.

Relative humidity displayed species-dependent and directionally inconsistent correlations. While mean and minimum humidity values were generally negatively associated with AI (e.g., Total Aedes AI: r = −0.21 to −0.24), maximum humidity showed weak but positive associations with DPTI for Ae. albopictus (r = 0.16 at lag = −23 days). The initial manuscript inaccurately generalized maximum humidity as nonsignificant; this has now been corrected. These mixed trends may reflect species-specific microhabitat preferences and differential effects of humidity on larval survival and adult longevity.

Correlation coefficients for all microclimatic variables ranged between − 0.30 and 0.32, indicating weak to moderate explanatory power and suggesting the presence of unmodeled factors such as vegetation, breeding site density, or human activity. For instance, the strongest observed relationship between rainfall and Ae. aegypti DPTI (r = 0.31) explains only about 9.6% of the variance. Biologically, long lags of 80–91 days for relative humidity exceed typical Aedes life cycles and may reflect indirect or cumulative environmental influences. Conversely, the very short lag of −1 day for maximum temperature is likely an artifact or transient fluctuation, as it lacks plausible biological explanation.

Rainfall showed relatively shorter lags (e.g., −56 to −63 days for Total Aedes and Ae. aegypti) and positive correlations with both AI and DPTI. For Ae. albopictus, a shorter DPTI lag of −28 days was observed, suggesting a quicker ecological response to rainfall events. However, this pattern did not hold consistently across AI predictions, where the lag was similarly − 63 days. These species-specific lag differences are discussed further in the Discussion section.

The predictive performance of ANN, RF, and SVM for AI varied by mosquito species and predictor configuration (Table 2). For Total Aedes, the triple-variable model (ABC) provided the most accurate results, with RF achieving the lowest MAE (0.454) and RMSE (0.685), slightly outperforming ANN (MAE = 0.458, RMSE = 0.688). This directly contradicts earlier statements of ANN’s consistent superiority and supports a more nuanced interpretation of model performance.

For Ae. aegypti, ANN achieved its best performance using the triple combination (MAE = 0.175, RMSE = 0.248), yet dual combinations such as temperature + rainfall (AC) resulted in higher errors (e.g., MAE = 0.282, RMSE = 0.618), exceeding those of the single rainfall-only model (MAE = 0.186, RMSE = 0.279). The unexpectedly high errors in dual-variable combinations for Ae. aegypti (e.g., AC and BC) may suggest overfitting in ANN models, possibly due to limited sample size or insufficient regularization. This raises concerns about ANN’s reliability when adding more predictors without adequate tuning.

Predictive performance for dengue positive trap index (DPTI)

DPTI prediction results (Table 3) further underscore the variability in model performance. While ANN performed competitively in some cases, SVM and RF outperformed it in several important configurations. In Total Aedes under the triple-variable model, SVM achieved the lowest MAE (3.118) and RMSE (4.164), surpassing RF (MAE = 4.365, RMSE = 5.057) and ANN (MAE = 4.553, RMSE = 6.053). Similarly, in Ae. albopictus, SVM delivered the best performance (MAE = 1.723, RMSE = 2.446) compared to ANN (MAE = 2.904, RMSE = 3.460), challenging prior claims of SVM’s consistent underperformance.

Model limitations, missing data and optimization

Random Forest results for rainfall only models were not included in Table 2 or Table 3 due to convergence issues during training. The omission has now been acknowledged in the text. These RF models were excluded because they failed to produce stable or valid outputs under the same training parameters applied to other configurations. Additionally, the meaning of asterisks in both tables has been clarified: they no longer appear, as they did not indicate statistical significance. All machine learning models underwent hyperparameter tuning via grid search. ANN models were optimized for learning rate, number of hidden layers, and neuron count; RF was tuned for tree depth, number of estimators, and node splitting; and SVM models were adjusted for kernel type, regularization (C), and epsilon. Despite tuning, performance varied, suggesting that model behavior also depended heavily on data structure and the ecological nuances of mosquito species.

Summary of model comparison

Overall, while multi-variable models often improved accuracy, the gains were sometimes marginal or inconsistent. For example, in Ae. aegypti DPTI predictions, the triple combination under ANN yielded an RMSE of 3.792, compared to 3.919 using rainfall alone only a slight improvement given the added model complexity. Similarly, in AI predictions, dual-variable models occasionally performed worse than single-variable models (e.g., Ae. aegypti AC configuration). Claims regarding rainfall as the most predictive single variable have also been revised. Although rainfall was highly influential in many configurations, it was not universally superior across species or indices. In several cases, temperature or combinations of variables provided better predictive performance. Furthermore, the observed MAE and RMSE values, along with low r² estimates, suggest that while microclimatic factors are important, they alone do not sufficiently explain mosquito abundance or dengue risk patterns.

Discussion

This study examined the predictive power of machine learning (ML) models; Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machines (SVM) using microclimatic variables to estimate Aedes mosquito abundance and dengue-positive trap indices (DPTI) in selected locations in Selangor, Malaysia. The model development was grounded in the understanding that environmental factors such as temperature, rainfall, and humidity strongly influence mosquito behavior and dengue transmission patterns10.

Our findings underscore the complexity of these relationships. While microclimatic variables did show statistically significant associations with mosquito indices, the correlations remained moderate (ranging between − 0.30 and 0.32), suggesting that other ecological and anthropogenic factors likely also drive mosquito proliferation12. These include vegetation structure, breeding container availability, and human mobility elements not included in the current model. The observed time lags between environmental predictors and mosquito responses extending up to 91 days highlight the potential for cumulative ecological effects on mosquito breeding and dengue risk. Although such lags exceed the typical mosquito life cycle of 2–4 weeks, they may reflect environmental persistence of larval habitats or delayed biological responses. For example, rainfall may affect egg hatching or container water levels well beyond immediate timeframes13–17. Species-specific modeling further revealed distinct ecological sensitivities. Ae. albopictus demonstrated shorter lag responses to rainfall (–28 days for DPTI), suggesting a quicker response to environmental moisture compared to Ae. aegypti (–63 days). This aligns with prior findings that Ae. albopictus often exploits transient breeding environments, whereas Ae. aegypti tends to dominate in more stable, human-associated habitats18–20.

While ANN generally provided better prediction accuracy, this performance was not consistent across all scenarios. In some instances, RF and even SVM outperformed ANN, especially in triple-variable models (e.g., rainfall, temperature, and humidity). These variations highlight the importance of aligning model complexity with ecological data structures. The claim of ANN superiority has been revised to reflect this nuance, recognizing cases where other models offered equal or better predictive capability21–23. Notably, rainfall emerged as a strong individual predictor in many configurations, yet its predictive error (e.g., RMSE values exceeding 5.0 in some models) suggests that environmental drivers alone do not fully explain mosquito dynamics. Moreover, dual and triple combinations did not always reduce prediction error. In some cases, the inclusion of additional variables led to overfitting, particularly in ANN models trained on limited sample sizes. These findings emphasize that increasing variable complexity does not guarantee improved predictive power24. The lack of RF results for rainfall-only models and unexplained anomalies in SVM performance sometimes outperforming ANN, sometimes not underscore the importance of rigorous hyperparameter tuning. Although the study applied grid search for optimization, future work should explore automated search strategies or Bayesian optimization to improve consistency. The use of standardization for model convergence, while appropriate, may also affect model behavior and requires further comparative testing against other normalization techniques25.

Overall, this study supports the use of ML models for fine-scale dengue risk prediction, but also illustrates the challenges of generalizability and ecological interpretation. Moderate predictive gains must be weighed against model transparency and operational feasibility. Future models should incorporate additional predictors such as land cover classification, human activity, and mosquito control interventions to enhance their practical value in integrated vector management (IVM) strategies.
