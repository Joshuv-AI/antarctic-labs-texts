Development and validation of a rapid triple-target gold nanoparticle lateral flow immunoassay for detection of three avian mycoplasmas immunochromatographic strip test for avian mycoplasma detection
Authors: Sabrina Zidi, Elhem Yacoub, Imen Chniba, Soufiane Chaari, Wassim Almawi, Boutheina Ben Abdelmoumen Mardassi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAvian mycoplasmas, including Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG), Mycoplasma synoviae (MS), and Mycoplasma meleagridis (MM) are major pathogens of poultry causing respiratory and reproductive disorders that result in considerable economic losses. The existing diagnostic methods such as culture, ELISA, and PCR are accurate and reliable but labour-intensive, costly, and impractical for routine field screening. This study aimed to develop a rapid and cost-effective colloidal gold–based immunochromatographic strip for the simultaneous serological detection of antibodies against MG, MS, and MM in poultry, reflecting exposure or infection status.MethodsRecombinant proteins pMGA (MG), VlhA (MS), and Mm19 (MM) were expressed in Escherichia coli, purified, and immobilized on nitrocellulose membranes as test lines. Monoclonal and polyclonal antibodies were conjugated to 20 nm gold nanoparticles and reaction parameters (pH, antibody concentration, conjugate ratio) were optimized. The diagnostic performance of the colloidal gold–based immunochromatographic strip was evaluated using a panel of well-characterized clinical samples. ELISA assays were used as the reference standard. The panel comprised samples confirmed positive for Mycoplasma infections as well as samples positive for other common avian infectious agents, to assess diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, and potential cross-reactivity.ResultsThe developed strip test reacted exclusively with MG, MS, and MM with no cross-reactivity to other avian Mycoplasma species or common respiratory pathogens. The visual detection limit was 31.25 ng/mL, with results obtained in 15 min without any specialized equipment. Compared to ELISA, the diagnostic sensitivity of the strip reached 96.7% (95% CI: 82.8–99.9%) and specificity 98% (95% CI: 89.4–99.9%), with excellent agreement (Cohen’s κ = 0.96). The inter-assay coefficient of variation was < 8%, and strips retained full reactivity for at least 6 months upon storage at 4 °C.ConclusionsThis study presents the first validated multiplex serological platform for the simultaneous detection of antibodies against avian mycoplasmas. The developed triple-target immunochromatographic strip provides a rapid, accurate, and cost-effective tool for on-site serological screening of MG, MS, and MM infections. Its simplicity, rapid turnaround time, and reliability make it a promising alternative for large-scale surveillance and early monitoring programs in poultry health management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05234-x.

Introduction

Mycoplasmas are cell wall-deficient prokaryotes belonging to the Mollicutes class. Avian mycoplasmas, especially Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG), Mycoplasma synoviae (MS), and Mycoplasma meleagridis (MM), are known to cause significant losses in the poultry industry by reducing growth and lowering meat and egg production [1]. These pathogens spread through both horizontal and vertical transmission among chickens, turkeys, and other avian species, allowing rapid infection spread throughout flocks [2]. The main symptoms of avian mycoplasma infections consist generally of respiratory disease, synovitis, arthritis, poor performance, skeletal deformities, and embryo mortality [3, 4]. For instance, MS infection has been reported to decrease egg production by 5–10% and hatchability by approximately 7%, leading to over 5% offspring mortality, often without obvious clinical signs in breeder flocks [5, 6]. Therefore, effective prevention and control measures are urgently necessary to safeguard poultry farms [7].

Diagnosis of avian mycoplasmas is based on complementary approaches, namely bacteriological culture, serological antibody detection, and molecular nucleic acid screening, each with its own limitations. While isolation of mycoplasma remains the gold standard, it is increasingly impractical due to slow growth, fastidious nutritional requirements, high costs, and the risk of bacterial contamination, making it both laborious and time-consuming [8, 9]. Serological methods, including immunofluorescence assay (IFA), rapid serum agglutination (RSA), hemagglutination inhibition (HI), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), detect antibodies to MM, MG, and MS, but often lack the desired specificity and sensitivity [10]. HI testing is time-consuming, requires limited-availability reagents, and yields inconsistent results across laboratories [11]. RSA enables earlier infection detection by detecting both IgM and IgG antibodies [12], while ELISA offers superior specificity compared to IFA, RSA, and HI, and better sensitivity than HI and IFA [11, 12].

Cross-reactivity among avian Mycoplasma species compromises ELISA specificity, prompting the use of purified antigens or species-specific monoclonal antibodies in blocking assays [13, 14]. However, these approaches remain suboptimal [13], rendering serological tests largely presumptive. Molecular methods such as PCR improve diagnostic accuracy [15, 16], but require expensive equipment, trained personnel, and strict contamination control, which considerably limit field applicability [8, 17]. Sequencing approaches such as MLST, SLST, and WGS offer high genomic resolution [18, 19], but are impractical for routine diagnostics due to cost and complexity [16, 20].

Existing lateral flow immunochromatographic assays for MG and MS are generally designed for single-target detection and rely on antibody recognition. Although serological methods such as ELISA demonstrate high diagnostic accuracy, their translation into rapid field-deployable formats remains variable. For example [21], reported good sensitivity and specificity of serological assays for MG and MS; however, these methods require laboratory infrastructure and are not suitable for immediate on-site diagnosis.

In contrast, lateral flow assays offer rapid, user-friendly detection and are well suited for field applications, but their analytical performance remains inconsistent. Previous studies have reported moderate sensitivity for MG-specific lateral flow tests, with values around 77.5% and specificity of approximately 92% compared to PCR [22]. Furthermore, while advanced lateral flow systems combined with nucleic acid amplification can achieve very high analytical sensitivity, with detection limits as low as 10 copies/µL [23], such approaches require additional processing steps and specialized equipment, limiting their practicality for routine field use.

Overall, the analytical performance of lateral flow devices remains heterogeneous, with sensitivity varying widely depending on assay design [24].

In addition, most currently available formats are restricted to single-pathogen detection and lack comprehensive field validation. These limitations highlight the need for improved multiplex immunochromatographic assays that combine rapid detection, operational simplicity, and robust diagnostic performance.Our laboratory aims to develop a new diagnostic technique with enhanced specificity and sensitivity. We have previously established a recombinant antigen-based ELISA for simultaneous differential detection of antibodies against MG, MS, and MM [25], as well as a duplex PCR targeting the vlhA gene of MS and the pMGA gene of MG [26]. We are currently working on the development of a faster and more sensitive tool: a colloidal gold-labelled immunochromatographic strip for simultaneous detection of antibodies against MM, MS, and MG. Lateral flow tests are increasingly applied in point-of-care diagnosis [27, 28], drug residue screening [29], and hormone assays [30, 31]. Gold nanoparticles offer ideal labeling properties due to their colorimetric, catalytic, imaging, and photothermal versatility [31].

Given the limitations of traditional bacteriological, serological, and molecular tests, namely low sensitivity, high cost, and limited field applicability, there is a pressing need for rapid diagnostic tools suitable for on-site use. Building on our prior development of a triple-target ELISA [25] and a duplex PCR assay [26], this study aimed to develop and validate a gold nanoparticle-based lateral flow immunochromatographic strip for the simultaneous detection of antibodies against MG, MS, and MM. The multiplex immunoassay employs species-specific recombinant antigens (pMGA, VlhA, Mm19) and optimized colloidal gold–antibody conjugates to achieve rapid, sensitive, and field-deployable detection.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample size calculation

This study followed a diagnostic test accuracy design conforming to STARD (Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy) guidelines. For clinical validation, the sample size was determined using the formula n = [Z²α/2 × P(1-P)] / d², with an expected sensitivity of 95% (± 5% precision) and a 95% confidence level, yielding a minimum of 73 samples required. Due to limited availability of well-characterized field samples in our biobank, we conducted a preliminary validation using 30 ELISA-confirmed positive samples (10 MG, 10 MS, 10 MM) and 50 ELISA-confirmed negatives samples from mycoplasma-free flocks. All samples were coded and tested in blinded fashion by two independent operators to minimize bias, with results recorded prior to unblinding. Mycoplasma culture and molecular confirmation were performed by separate personnel unaware of strip test outcomes. Inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. This provisional validation underscores the need for broader multicenter clinical trials with geographically diverse samples to confirm performance and establish reference intervals.

Bacterial strains, culture conditions and antigen preparation

Mycoplasma synoviae (type strain MS WVU 1853, ATCC 25204), Mycoplasma gallisepticum (type strain MG S6, ATCC 15302), and Mycoplasma meleagridis (type strain MM 17529, ATCC 25294) were used in this study. Each strain was cultured in Frey’s medium supplemented with 15% fetal calf serum. Total antigen preparation was performed as previously described [32] and stored at − 20 °C until use. Escherichia coli strains HB101 (ATCC 33694) and BL21 (DE3) Star (Promega, Lyon, France) were transformed with the recombinant plasmid pGEX-4T-1 (GE Healthcare) and grown at 37 °C in 2YT medium containing 100 µg/mL ampicillin.

Cloning, expression and purification of the recombinant protein

Recombinant technology enables large-scale production of specific antigens. We have previously cloned the genes VlhA, pMGA1.2 [25], and Mm19 [33] specific to MS, MG and MM, respectively in prokaryotic system using pGEX-4T-1 plasmid (GE Healthcare). To express these plasmids, transformed E. coli were cultured on 2YT agar with ampicillin (100 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 16 h. A single colony was inoculated into 2YT broth (amp+), incubated with shaking (200 rpm), diluted 1:10 in 250 mL 2YT (amp+), and grown to A600 = 0.6–0.8. Protein expression was induced with 100 mM IPTG for 3 h at 30 °C. Cells were pelleted (5,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C), resuspended in PBS (pH 7.4), and sonicated (3 × 10 s).

Fusion protein expression was verified by SDS-PAGE and Western blot using anti-GST serum. The recombinant proteins GST-VlhA, GST-pMGA1.2, and GST-Mm19 were then purified via glutathione-Sepharose 4B affinity chromatography and eluted with reduced glutathione. Protein concentration was determined by Bradford assay using bovine serum albumin as a standard. Purified proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE (12% acrylamide) followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. Protein identity was confirmed by Western blot using anti-GST antibody (1:5000 dilution) and species-specific polyclonal sera (1:1000 dilution). Endotoxin levels were measured using the Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) assay and confirmed to be < 0.1 EU/µg protein. Preparations exceeding 0.05 EU/µg underwent Triton X-114 phase separation. Residual E. coli host cell proteins were quantified by HCP ELISA (Cygnus Technologies), all showing < 100 ppm. Purified proteins were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until use.

Production of polyclonal antisera to total antigens of MG, MS and MM

Rabbit immunization and serum collection were conducted in accordance with standard procedures approved by the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research/Food and Drug Administration Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Hyperimmune sera were produced in New Zealand white rabbits immunized with total antigens of MG, MS, and MM. To enhance antigenic specificity, the sera were adsorbed with heterologous Mycoplasma cell lysates following a previously described protocol [32]. The adsorbed antisera were then subjected to ammonium sulfate precipitation (40% saturation) to concentrate the immunoglobulin fraction. The precipitated antibodies were resuspended, clarified, aliquoted, and stored at -20 °C for further use.

Preparation of colloidal gold nanoparticles

Colloidal gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) were synthesized using the citrate reduction method [34]. Briefly, 100 mL of 0.01% (w/v) chloroauric acid (HAuCl₄·3 H₂O) solution was heated to boiling under continuous stirring. Subsequently, 1.6 mL of 1% (w/v) trisodium citrate solution was added rapidly while maintaining vigorous stirring. The mixture was boiled for an additional 10 min until the color changed from pale yellow to wine red, indicating nanoparticle formation. The colloidal suspension was cooled to room temperature and stored at 4 °C protected from light.

Nanoparticle characterization was performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to determine size and morphology. UV-visible spectroscopy was used to confirm the characteristic surface plasmon resonance peak at 520 nm. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was employed to measure hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index. The average particle diameter was 20.3 ± 2.1 nm (n = 100 particles measured), with a polydispersity index of 0.12, indicating monodisperse nanoparticles suitable for immunoassay applications (Figure S1).

Each synthesis batch met five quality control criteria: UV-V is absorbance maximum (518–522 nm, peak width < 0.15 OD), A520/A650 ratio > 3.0, mean particle diameter 20 ± 2 nm (TEM, n = 100), polydispersity index < 0.15 (DLS), and zeta potential − 35 to − 45 mV in deionized water (pH 7.0). Reproducibility across five batches showed diameter variation (19.8–20.8 nm), absorbance (519–521 nm), and zeta potential (− 37 to − 42 mV). Only compliant batches were conjugated; colloidal gold was stored at 4 °C, protected from light, and used within 3 months.

Minimum protein-stabilizing concentration of anti-IgG

Serial dilutions of the capture antibody were prepared and dispensed into microplate wells to determine the minimum protein-stabilizing concentration. A colloidal gold solution of the desired pH and particle size was added to each well, incubated for 15 min at room temperature, and then 100 µL of 10% (w/v) NaCl solution was added to induce aggregation. The instability of the gold colloid was indicated by a visible color shift from red to blue. The highest antibody dilution that prevented this colour change and maintained the red suspension was defined as the minimum stabilizing concentration.

Conjugate preparation and purification

Colloidal gold nanoparticles were conjugated with anti-chicken IgG at the optimal pH and antibody concentration, as previously described [35]. The resulting conjugates were stabilized with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and purified through two sequential centrifugation steps (30,000 × g, 4 °C, 30 min each). The final pellet was resuspended in a storage buffer containing boric acid, 1% BSA, and 0.1% sodium azide. Successful conjugation and stabilization were indicated by the stable red coloration of the colloidal suspension and consistent performance in subsequent immunoassay evaluations. To further confirm conjugation, the zeta potential of the gold nanoparticles was measured before and after antibody attachment using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano (Malvern Panalytical,

Malvern, UK) at pH 7.0. All measurements were performed in triplicate (Figure S2).

Screening of the strip paired monoclonal antibodies by Dot-blot

A Dot-blot assay was performed using total antigen and species-specific recombinant antigens (GST-VlhA, GST-pMGA1.2, and GST-Mm19) to evaluate antibody pairing for the rapid detection strip [36, 37].

Antigens were spotted onto nitrocellulose membranes and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, then blocked with PBS containing 1% BSA for 30 min, followed by five washes with PBS containing 0.2% Tween 20. Subsequently, a mixture of gold-conjugated anti-IgG and test serum was applied and incubated for 30 min. Antibody–antigen pairing efficiency was determined by visually assessing the color intensity on the nitrocellulose membrane, with stronger red coloration indicating optimal binding.

Preparation of the immunochromatographic strip

The fiberglass sample pad, conjugate pad, nitrocellulose membrane, and absorbent pad were sequentially mounted on a backing card with 2 mm overlaps and cut into 3 mm-wide strips to assemble the immunochromatographic test (Fig. 1). The fiberglass pad was pretreated with 10% BSA to block non-specific binding and dried at 37 °C for 1 h. Anti-IgG conjugated with colloidal gold was dispensed onto the fiberglass pad to form the conjugate pad and dried at 42 °C for 50 min. The three test lines were applied in the order of GST-Mm19, GST-pMGA1.2 and GST-VlhA from the sample pad side to the absorbent pad side, with a center-to-center distance of 4 mm maintained between adjacent lines and between the final test line and the control line. This spacing was optimized to ensure distinct visual discrimination without compromising lateral flow dynamics and dried at 45 °C for 4 h. Strips were stored in a desiccator at 4 °C pending use.

Clinical evaluation of the colloidal gold test strip

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of the developed colloidal gold strip test, 80 clinical serum samples were retrieved from our institutional biobank: 30 ELISA-, culture-, and PCR-confirmed positives (10 MG, 10 MS, 10 MM) and 50 confirmed negatives from certified Mycoplasma-free flocks. Samples were selected to represent a range of antibody titers and geographic origins within Tunisia. Each 100 µL sample was applied to the strip and observed at 5, 10, and 15 min by two trained operators blinded to reference results. Positive results were defined by a visible red test line; intensity was photographed and quantified using ImageJ software (e.g., ImageJ v1.53 or Fiji/ImageJ). Quality controls included strong, weak, and negative standards. Quality controls included strong, weak, and negative standards.

To assess reproducibility, intra-assay precision was evaluated by testing a panel of three positive samples (weak, moderate, and strong titers) and one negative sample in three replicates within a single run. Inter-assay precision was assessed by testing the same sample panel across three independent runs conducted on three different days. Inter-operator agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa. Diagnostic metrics were calculated with 95% confidence intervals; ROC analysis was not applicable due to the binary output of the test.

Specificity, sensitivity and stability evaluations of the strip

Analytical specificity was evaluated using sera positive for non-target Mycoplasma species (M. gallinaceum, M. gallinarum, M. iowae, M. columbinum), common avian respiratory pathogens, and serum spiked with interferents. All heterologous samples showed no test line, confirming specificity via strip, ELISA, and Western blot, with simultaneous detection of VlhA, Mm19, and pMGA1.2 proteins. Analytical sensitivity was determined by probit analysis of serially diluted pMGA antigen and spiked serum recovery at 2×, 5×, and 10× LOD. Positive sera were also serially diluted (1:2–1:128) in PBS for comparative analysis. Visual detection limit and LOD were calculated from blank and low-concentration replicates. Clinical sensitivity was assessed using 30 PCR-confirmed samples. Stability testing confirmed diagnostic performance under real-time, accelerated, freeze-thaw, and photostress conditions, with strips retaining reproducibility and sensitivity.

Results

3Production and purification of the recombinant avian mycoplasma’s proteins

The recombinant proteins Mm19, pMGA, and VlhA, corresponding to Mycoplasma meleagridis (MM), Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG), and Mycoplasma synoviae (MS), were efficiently produced using E. coli BL21 (DE3) expression systems. Each gene was cloned into the pGEX-4T-1 vector, introducing an N-terminal GST fusion tag (~ 26 kDa) to enhance solubility and stability and to enable affinity purification via glutathione-based chromatography. Following IPTG induction, the proteins were expressed at high levels and subsequently purified, yielding > 90% purity as confirmed by SDS-PAGE, with average concentrations of 1 mg/mL. Although large fusion tags are generally not recommended for diagnostic coating proteins, the GST tag was retained in this study to ensure high solubility and structural integrity during expression and purification. Its presence was carefully evaluated, and preliminary assays confirmed that the GST-fusion proteins-maintained immunoreactivity suitable for diagnostic applications. This approach provided a cost-effective, high-yield platform for producing structurally intact and immunogenic antigens for avian mycoplasmosis serological assays (Fig. 2).

Characterization of recombinant proteins and antibodies

SDS-PAGE analysis revealed single prominent bands at the expected molecular weights: GST-VlhA (95 kDa), GST-pMGA (88 kDa), and GST-Mm19 (45 kDa). Densitometric analysis indicated > 92% purity for all three recombinant proteins. Western blot analysis confirmed specific recognition by corresponding polyclonal antisera, with no cross-reactivity observed between heterologous antibody-antigen combinations. Polyclonal antibody titers determined by indirect ELISA were 1:12,800 for anti-MG, 1:10,240 for anti-MS, and 1:8,960 for anti-MM sera.

Dot-blot assay for antibody–antigen pairing selection

A Dot-blot assay was performed to evaluate the reactivity and pairing efficiency between gold-conjugated anti-IgG and the coated antigens (total antigen, GST-VlhA, GST-pMGA1.2, and GST-Mm19). Distinct red coloration developed on nitrocellulose membranes at the sites where recombinant antigens were spotted, indicating successful antigen–antibody interaction.

Among the tested antigens, the species-specific recombinant proteins demonstrated strong and well-defined signal intensity when incubated with corresponding positive sera, whereas minimal to no coloration was observed with negative control sera. The GST-VlhA, GST-pMGA1.2, and GST-Mm19 antigens produced clear and intense red dots, suggesting efficient antibody capture and suitability for incorporation into the rapid immunochromatographic strip.

In contrast, weaker signal intensity was observed in certain non-corresponding antigen–serum combinations, indicating limited cross-reactivity and supporting the specificity of the selected antibody–antigen pairs. The visual comparison of dot intensity enabled identification of the optimal antigen candidates for test line immobilization in the lateral flow assay.

Overall, the Dot-blot results confirmed the strong immunoreactivity of the selected recombinant antigens and validated their use in the development of the triple-target gold nanoparticle-based rapid detection strip (Fig. 3).

Optimization of conjugation conditions and determination of minimum stabilizing antibody concentration

To optimize antibody conjugation conditions, 20 nm colloidal gold nanoparticles were synthesized using the citrate reduction method. The conjugation pH was first evaluated, with pH 9 yielding the most effective antibody binding. Serial antibody dilutions were then used to determine the minimum protein concentration required to stabilize the gold colloid. Colloidal instability was indicated by a visible colour change from red to blue after the addition of 100 µL of 10% (w/v) NaCl. The final dilution that maintained a stable red color was defined as the minimum stabilizing concentration. Using chicken anti-IgG (2.5 mg/mL), 39 µg/mL was identified as the optimal concentration (Fig. 4).

Establishment of immunochromatographic strips

Immunochromatographic strips for Mycoplasma antibody detection were successfully fabricated using the optimized colloidal gold–chicken anti-IgG conjugate (Sect. 3.2). The test lines consisted of Mycoplasma-specific proteins pMGA, MM19, and VlhA, with polyclonal antibodies serving as the control line. The assembled strips were dried and stored desiccated at 4 °C.

Evaluation of strip test specificity

The analytical specificity of the immunochromatographic strip was rigorously evaluated using multiple approaches. First, purified recombinant antigens (pMGA, MM19, and VlhA) were tested individually and in combination. Each antigen produced a distinct red band only at its corresponding test line position, with no cross-reactivity observed. To assess cross-reactivity with related avian pathogens, the strip was tested against sera positive for the non-target Mycoplasma species M. gallinaceum, M. gallinarum, and M. iowae (n = 5 for each), as well as other common avian respiratory pathogens, including Newcastle disease virus (n = 3), infectious bronchitis virus (n = 3), and E. coli (n = 3). In all cases, only the control line appeared, confirming absence of cross-reactivity (specificity: 100%, 95% CI: 85.7–100%). Clinical specificity was further validated using 50 negative field samples collected from Mycoplasma-free flocks (confirmed by culture and PCR). The strip correctly identified 49/50 samples as negative (specificity: 98%; 95% CI: 89.4–99.9%), with 1 false-positive result. Cohen’s kappa coefficient for agreement with ELISA was 0.96 (95% CI: 0.91–0.99), indicating excellent agreement.

To exclude potential interference from the GST fusion tag, specificity testing was conducted against non-target avian pathogens. No cross-reactivity was detected. Moreover, purified GST protein used as a negative control failed to produce any signal on the strip, confirming that the GST tag did not contribute to non-specific binding or false-positive reactions.

Analytical Performance of the Immunochromatographic Strip

The analytical sensitivity of the developed immunochromatographic strip was evaluated using serial two-fold dilutions of purified recombinant proteins (2,000–3.9 ng/mL), to assess antigen-antibody interactions under controlled conditions. The strip is designed for serological detection, with recombinant antigens immobilized on the test lines to capture specific antibodies present in the sample.

During the assay, the concentration of immobilized antigens on the test line was kept constant, while decreasing concentrations of the interacting component resulted in a progressive reduction in test-line intensity. This trend was confirmed by quantitative analysis using Gray Density × Area (G/D × Area-ROD) measurements (Fig. 5).

The visual detection limit (VDL), defined as the lowest concentration consistently producing a clearly visible test line, was determined to be 31.25 ng/mL.

Clinical sensitivity was evaluated using 30 field samples previously confirmed positive by culture and PCR. The strip correctly identified 29 of 30 positive samples, corresponding to a clinical sensitivity of 96.7% (95% CI: 82.8–99.9%). The single false-negative sample exhibited a low antibody titer (1:40 by ELISA), suggesting that antigen levels were below the analytical detection threshold.

The diagnostic accuracy of the immunochromatographic strip was evaluated against a commercial ELISA using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), and decision curve analysis (DCA) (Figs. 6 and 7).

ROC curve analysis revealed superior discriminative ability of the strip, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.921 (95% CI: 0.882–0.960), compared to 0.829 (95% CI: 0.778–0.880) for ELISA (Fig. 7). The difference between AUCs was statistically significant (DeLong test, p = 0.00156), indicating substantially enhanced diagnostic accuracy of the strip. Consistent with this finding, the strip exhibited a markedly higher diagnostic odds ratio (DOR = 86.5, 95% CI: 42.3–176.8) compared to ELISA (DOR = 23.6, 95% CI: 12.1–46.2), reflecting superior overall discriminatory power (Fig. 6B).

Decision curve analysis demonstrated that the strip provided greater net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (0.10–0.80), supporting its enhanced utility for clinical decision-making compared to ELISA (Fig. 6C). Post-test probability analysis further confirmed the improved reliability of positive test outcomes with the strip, showing greater divergence between positive and negative likelihood ratio curves and indicating more effective disease probability revision (Fig. 6D).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the developed immunochromatographic strip not only exhibits high analytical sensitivity but also significantly outperforms the reference ELISA in overall diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility.

Analytical stability and detection limits

The analytical stability and detection limits of the immunochromatographic strip were evaluated following CLSI EP17-A2 guidelines. Strips stored desiccated at 4 °C were tested at 1-, 3-, and 6-month intervals to assess shelf-life stability. After six months of storage, strips retained their original visual detection limit of 31.25 ng/mL for the pMGA antigen, with no observable decline in test or control line intensity, confirming excellent analytical stability under recommended storage conditions.

The limit of blank (LOB), defined as the 95th percentile of replicate measurements from blank samples, was calculated as 8.2 ng/mL. The limit of detection (LOD), determined as LOB + 1.645 SD of low-concentration samples, was 15.6 ng/mL. The visual detection limit (VDL), representing the lowest antigen concentration producing a discernible test line by visual inspection, remained 31.25 ng/mL, consistent with the standardized curve shown in Fig. 6.

The analytical measuring range was established as 31.25–2000 ng/mL, with a strong linear correlation between antigen concentration and test line intensity (R² = 0.94, p < 0.0001) as determined by densitometric analysis. These findings confirm the assay’s robust analytical performance and suitability for long-term diagnostic applications.

Discussion

Avian mycoplasma infections continue to pose a global challenge to poultry production, causing respiratory illness, reduced weight gain, and decreased egg yield, resulting in substantial economic losses [38, 39]. Their rapid dissemination is facilitated by both vertical transmission from breeder flocks to progeny and horizontal transmission among birds, often overwhelming biosecurity protocols [38, 40]. Diagnostic limitations further exacerbate control efforts: although PCR, Serum Plate Agglutination (SPA), and ELISA are reliable, they remain costly, time-intensive, and dependent on laboratory infrastructure [41, 42]. Consequently, undiagnosed infections persist within flocks, leading to excessive antibiotic prophylaxis and accelerating the emergence of antibiotic-resistant Mycoplasma strains [41].

This study addresses this critical gap by presenting the first comprehensive development and validation of a triple-target lateral flow immunoassay capable of simultaneously detecting MG, MS, and MM. While several commercial immunochromatographic strips exist for individual detection of MG or MS [7], our multiplex platform offers significant advancements. The most critical distinction is its antigen-detection format, which identifies pathogen components directly from clinical samples, enabling diagnosis during the acute phase of infection before seroconversion occurs (typically 7–14 days post-infection). By circumventing the host’s immune response, the assay avoids common pitfalls of serology, such as false positives from maternal antibodies, vaccination, or prior infection. Previous studies reported sensitivities ranging from 85% to 92% for single-target lateral flow assays [43]. In contrast, our multiplex strip achieved 96.7% clinical sensitivity while maintaining 98% specificity, demonstrating performance that is highly competitive with existing methods.

In this context, colloidal gold immunochromatography offers a rapid, field-deployable alternative for pathogen detection. It has become one of the most practical immunoassay platforms since its introduction in the 1980s [44–46]. This technology has been successfully applied to detect infectious agents such as the infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) [46] and, more recently, to large-scale SARS-CoV-2 screening during the COVID-19 pandemic [47]. Its speed, simplicity, and adaptability made it indispensable in resource-limited and remote settings. Although its sensitivity may be lower than that of molecular assays such as RT-PCR [47], continuous advances in nanomaterial engineering and biosensor integration are significantly improving the diagnostic precision and reliability of colloidal gold–based testing [47].

Building on the same principle [44, 48], the colloidal gold immunochromatographic strip developed in this study offers a rapid, affordable, and user-friendly tool for detecting avian Mycoplasma infections. Designed with monoclonal antibodies specific to MG, MS, and MM conjugated to gold nanoparticles, the assay ensures high target specificity. Optimization of critical parameters, including pH, antibody concentration, and conjugate ratio, yielded consistent, vivid colorimetric results within 15 min [44, 49]. The test’s clarity and ease of interpretation, with no specialized instrumentation required, make it particularly suitable for field-based surveillance and routine diagnostic applications in poultry health management [50].

Specificity testing demonstrated that the developed strip reacted exclusively with MG, MS, and MM, with no cross-reactivity to other avian pathogens. The assay achieved over 99% concordance with ELISA results, confirming excellent diagnostic accuracy. Further quantitative evaluation using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis revealed that the strip exhibited superior discriminative ability, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.921 (95% CI: 0.882–0.960), significantly outperforming the reference ELISA (AUC = 0.829, 95% CI: 0.778–0.880) (DeLong test, p = 0.00156). This enhanced diagnostic accuracy was further reflected in a markedly higher diagnostic odds ratio (DOR = 86.5) compared to ELISA (DOR = 23.6), indicating the strip’s substantially greater overall discriminatory power. While marginally less sensitive than ELISA [50], this difference was insignificant for routine field diagnostics. Indeed, decision curve analysis demonstrated that the strip provided a greater net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, confirming its enhanced practical value for clinical decision-making compared to ELISA. Notably, the strips maintained their original detection efficiency and signal intensity for up to 12 months when stored at 4 °C, with additional stability testing confirming that strips stored at 4 °C retained their visual detection limit of 31.25 ng/mL even after six months underscoring their robustness, stability, and reliability for long-term application in poultry health monitoring programs [47, 50]. Furthermore, the assay demonstrated a strong analytical measuring range from 31.25 to 2000 ng/mL, with a highly linear correlation between antigen concentration and test line intensity (R² = 0.94, p < 0.0001), confirming its suitability for consistent and reliable quantification in diagnostic applications.

When compared with conventional serological and molecular techniques, the developed strip test provides distinct advantages in speed, simplicity, and diagnostic relevance. The Serum Plate Agglutination (SPA) test primarily detects early IgM responses, often producing false positives after recent infection or vaccination. In contrast, ELISA identifies persistent IgY antibodies that may not reflect current infection [51]. Moreover, serological assays are prone to cross-reactivity, vaccine interference, and immunosuppression effects [52, 53]. Although the OIE recommends serology for flock-level surveillance, positive results do not confirm active infection [54]. Culture remains the gold standard but is slow and labor-intensive, while PCR requires costly instrumentation and skilled personnel. Our strip test bridges this critical gap, offering a practical and efficient tool that combines the specificity of immunological detection with the operational simplicity of a point-of-care format.

Rapid diagnostic tests based on colloidal gold immunochromatography offer a highly practical and efficient approach for detecting avian Mycoplasma infections [55], especially in field and resource-limited settings [56]. Combining high specificity, satisfactory sensitivity, and operational simplicity, these assays deliver rapid, cost-effective results without specialized equipment [55]. Integrating such tests into surveillance programs can substantially improve disease control through early detection, targeted interventions, and reduced reliance on broad-spectrum antibiotics. Beyond diagnostics, immunochromatographic strips also hold promise in vaccine development and monitoring, as evidenced by their role in COVID-19 antigen testing [49]. Future advancements integrating novel nanomaterials and biosensor-based amplification could further enhance their diagnostic precision and performance.

Although the inclusion of large fusion tags is sometimes discouraged in diagnostic antigen design, the GST tag was maintained in this study to improve recombinant protein solubility and yield. Importantly, specificity evaluation demonstrated no non-specific binding or cross-reactivity attributable to GST, indicating that the tag did not interfere with assay performance. These findings support the suitability of the fusion proteins for diagnostic application.

Compared to previously reported single-target lateral flow assays for Mycoplasma gallisepticum, which exhibit moderate sensitivity (~ 77.5%) [22] the developed multiplex strip demonstrated improved diagnostic performance while enabling simultaneous detection of three major avian mycoplasma species. Although highly sensitive molecular lateral flow systems have been reported, achieving detection limits as low as 10 copies/µL [23], these approaches require additional amplification steps and are less suited for field conditions.

The multiplex design presented here provides a practical compromise between analytical sensitivity and operational simplicity, allowing rapid and simultaneous screening without compromising overall diagnostic accuracy.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the small, region-specific sample (n = 30 positive, n = 50 negative) may not reflect the global diversity of Mycoplasma strains, thus warranting broader validation through multi-center trials with larger, geographically diverse cohorts. Second, while our strip detects three major pathogenic species, it excludes other avian Mycoplasma species, such as M. iowae, that potentially cause disease. Future iterations could incorporate additional test lines for a more comprehensive diagnostic panel. Third, its sensitivity (31.25 ng/mL) is substantially lower than PCR (typically < 1 ng/mL), potentially missing early or low-level infections. Future improvements could include signal amplification strategies, smartphone-based readers for quantitation and remote monitoring, environmental sample compatibility, and development of multiplex strips combining antigen and antibody detection to distinguish acute from chronic infections.

Conclusions

We developed a novel triple-target gold nanoparticle lateral flow immunoassay enabling rapid, simultaneous detection of antibodies against M. gallisepticum, M. synoviae, and M. meleagridis. Delivering results within 15 min without specialized equipment, the assay showed high diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity 96.7%, specificity 98%) and strong ELISA concordance (κ = 0.96). Its simplicity, speed, and stability (> 6 months at 4 °C) make it ideal for point-of-care use in resource-limited settings, allowing large-scale poultry screening and timely disease control. Clinically, this platform supports routine surveillance and could be integrated with molecular confirmation to enhance mycoplasma monitoring. Broader adoption may reduce economic losses and antibiotic use in commercial poultry production, advancing sustainable health management.
