A new Batrachospora species (Apicomplexa: Sarcocystidae) from Duttaphrynus toad in Taiwan
Authors: Peihang Hong, Sijia Yu, Chao-Min Wang, Cheng-Hung Lai, Toshihiro Tokiwa, Shyun Chou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoccidia of amphibians remain poorly studied despite high host diversity, with critical gaps in integrative taxonomic documentation. This study describes Batrachospora jiunnshiowi n. sp. (Apicomplexa: Sarcocystidae), a novel coccidian parasite infecting the Asian black-spined toad (Duttaphrynus cf. melanostictus) in Taiwan. Morphological characterization revealed sporulated oocysts (mean L × W = 15.9 × 14.1 μm; L/W ratio = 1.1; n = 50) with ellipsoidal sporocysts (10.8 × 8.0 μm; L/W = 1.35; n = 66) lacking Stieda bodies and containing unique drop-shaped sporozoites—an autapomorphy distinguishing it from congeners. Phylogenetic analyses of all datasets (18S ribosomal RNA, cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1, concatenated sequences) robustly placed within the subfamily Hyaloklossinae, demonstrating paraphyly relative to B. caeruleae despite morphological conformity to Batrachospora diagnostic criteria. Prevalence was 13.3 % (2/15 hosts) in the eastern lineage of D. melanostictus, representing the first molecularly characterized coccidian in this host. The integrative taxonomic approach combining morphometrics, host specificity, and multi-locus phylogenetics validates its status as a new species. The discovery underscores significant undocumented coccidian diversity in synanthropic amphibians and advocates expanded surveillance to clarify host-parasite coevolution.Keywords: Asian black-spined toad, Batrachospora, Sarcocystidae, Taiwan

Introduction

The genus Batrachospora Tokiwa, Morizane, Chou & Duszynski, 2025, comprises globally distributed coccidian parasites infecting anuran amphibians (Tokiwa et al., 2025). Morphologically, Batrachospora is characterized by disporocystic and tetrasporozoic oocysts lacking Stieda bodies (SB), substieda bodies (SSB), and parastieda bodies (PSB) on sporocysts—a key diagnostic feature distinguishing it from eimeriid Isospora (Duszynski et al., 2007; Tokiwa et al., 2025). Initially, species now classified under Batrachospora were erroneously placed within Isospora Schneider, 1881 (family Eimeriidae) due to superficial similarities in oocyst morphology (e.g., disporocystic-tetrasporozoic organization) (Duszynski and Wilber, 1997; Duszynski et al., 2007; Berto et al., 2014). However, taxonomic revisions based on morphological and molecular phylogenetic analyses revealed fundamental distinctions: morphologically, the absence of SB—a synapomorphy of eimeriid Isospora—indicated non-homology with Eimeriidae (Carreno and Barta, 1999); molecular phylogenies robustly placed these SB-deficient coccidia within Sarcocystidae Poche, 1913 (Franzen et al. 2000; Barta et al. 2005). Consequently, Tokiwa et al.,(2025) erected Batrachospora as a new genus within the subfamily Hyaloklossinae using an integrative taxonomic approach, reclassifying nine former “Isospora” species from anurans under this taxon. Anuran coccidia possessing SB retain their original classification under Isospora, exemplified by Isospora delicatus from Pseudacris spp. (Duszynski et al., 2007; Tokiwa et al., 2025). Following these revisions, the family Sarcocystidae comprises five subfamilies: Sarcocystinae Poche 1913 (Sarcocystis, Flenkelia), Toxoplasmatinae Biocca 1957 (Toxoplasma, Hammondia, Heydornia, Neospora, Besnoitia), Cystoisosporinae Frenkel et al., 1979 (Cystoisospora), Eumonosporinae Chou et al., 2021 (Eumonospora), and Hyaloklossinae Tokiwa et al., 2021 (Hyaloklossia, Batrachospora), along with the genus Nephroisospora (Frenkel et al., 1979; Sercundes et al., 2016; Ogedengbe et al., 2016; Wogan et al., 2016; Chou et al., 2021; Tokiwa et al., 2021). Presently, Batrachospora encompasses 10 valid species predominantly reported from bufonid (4 species), hylid (3 species), ranid (2 species), and microhylid (1 species) hosts (Upton et al., 1993; Duszynski et al., 2007; Cao et al., 2017; Tokiwa et al., 2025). Despite the remarkable diversity of Anura (>7800 species across 57 families) (Frost, 2024), Batrachospora remains understudied, with only two genetic sequences (B. caeruleae from Ranoidea caerulea) available in databases to date (Tokiwa et al., 2025). This paucity of molecular data hinders robust phylogenetic inferences and necessitates broader sampling to resolve the evolutionary relationships of this genus.

The life cycle of Batrachospora spp. remains enigmatic. While endogenous development has been documented within intestinal epithelial cells (Mandal, 1987; Duszynski et al., 2007), the involvement of extraintestinal tissues—a feature observed in other Sarcocystidae (Berto et al., 2014; Duszynski et al., 2018; Tokiwa et al., 2021)—remains speculative. To date, experimental evidence confirming transmission dynamics in Batrachospora is limited to a single study demonstrating that adult frogs may contaminate breeding ponds with sporulated oocysts during their reproductive season, directly infecting conspecific tadpoles through oral ingestion (Bolek et al., 2003). Comparative analyses with anuran coccidia (e.g., Goussia, Eimeria) suggest potential complexity in transmission dynamics (Paperna et al., 1997; Jirků et al., 2009). For instance, Goussia exclusively infects tadpoles, with infections terminating during metamorphosis (Paperna et al., 1997) implying possible ontogenetic shifts in host susceptibility. Indirect life cycles involving intermediate hosts have also been hypothesized for Batrachospora (Tokiwa et al., 2025), warranting systematic histopathological examinations to elucidate tissue tropism and transmission pathways.

The Asian black-spined toad, Duttaphrynus melanostictus (Schneider, 1799) (Anura: Bufonidae), is a human commensal species inhabiting anthropogenically modified landscapes across South and Southeast Asia (Wogan et al., 2016). Critically, recent molecular evidence identifies D. melanostictus as a species complex comprising D. melanostictus sensu stricto (s. s.) (western lineage in South Asia/Indonesia) and D. cf. melanostictus (eastern lineage in Southeast Asia) (Dufresnes et al., 2025). Its synanthropic ecology and broad geographic range make it a critical host for investigating parasitic diversity. Despite its ecological prominence, coccidian parasites infecting D. melanostictus remain poorly documented. This study describes a novel Batrachospora species parasitizing D. melanostictus and clarifies its phylogenetic position within the Sarcocystidae. By integrating morphological characterization with molecular phylogenetic analyses of two genetic loci, 18S ribosomal RNA (18S rRNA) and cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1), we aim to establish the taxonomic validity of this new species, resolve its systematic affinities relative to other batrachosporoid coccidia.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and preparation

From September 2023 to April 2025, 15 Asian black-spined toads (Duttaphrynus cf. melanostictus) were rescued by the public and send to wildlife rescue station commissioned by the Taichung City Animal Protection and Health Inspection Office. Each individual was housed separately in autoclaved mouse cages lined with sterile water-filled Petri dishes (90 mm diameter) and provided daily with mealworms (Tenebrio molitor) and/or domestic crickets (Acheta domesticus) as a diet. Fecal samples were collected during rehabilitation for routine health screening. After confirming oocyst positivity via direct microscopy, fresh fecal from infected individuals were preserved in 50 mL sterile centrifuge tubes containing 2.5% (w/v) aqueous potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) for subsequent morphological and molecular analyses. Following veterinary assessment (defecation status, skin shedding, appetite, and activity levels), all rehabilitated toads were released into their original habitats. All animal care procedures were conducted under veterinary supervision and followed appropriate ethical and welfare guidelines in Taiwan, and release decisions were made by veterinarians following approximately two-week observation periods.

Morphological and molecular analyses

Preserved fecal samples stored in 2.5% K2Cr2O7 were incubated in a thermostatic chamber (YIHDER, New Taipei, Taiwan) at 26 °C (±0.5 °C accuracy) to induce sporulation. Sporulation progress was monitored daily, and the time to complete sporulation was recorded. Oocysts were examined using wet mounts, the recommended method for amphibian coccidia (Upton and McAllister, 1988). Measurements were performed using a Zeiss Axioscope 5 light microscope (ZEISS Group, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with brightfield and differential interference contrast optics. Digital images were captured using a Tekfar Digital Camera (TEKFAR SCIENCE, Taichung, Taiwan) and analyzed with ImageJ software (Schneider et al., 2012) for morphometric quantification. Morphometric data are expressed as mean values ± standard deviation with ranges in parentheses (μm). Morphological descriptions followed established protocols (Duszynski and Wilber, 1997), with standardized terminology for oocyst and sporocyst structures (Wilber et al., 1998): oocyst features included length (L), width (W), L/W ratio, micropyle (M), micropyle cap (MC), oocyst residuum (OR), and polar granule (PG); sporocyst features encompassed: length (L), width (W), L/W ratio, Stieda body (SB), substieda body (SSB), parastieda body (PSB), sporocyst residuum (SR), sporozoites (SZ), refractile body (RB), and nuclei (N) in SZ. Line drawings were made using Procreate v 5.3.15 on an iPad Air 5.

For molecular characterization, genomic DNA was extracted from 0.1 g of potassium dichromate-preserved fecal sediment. Samples were homogenized in 1 mL saturated sodium chloride solution via vortexing and incubated at room temperature for 1 min. The supernatant (200 μL) was processed using the QIAamp® DNA Powersoil Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), with mechanical lysis (50/s, 5min) performed on a TissueLyser LT® (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany).

Partial sequences of the 18S rRNA and cox1 were amplified via PCR using primer sets EIF1 (5′- GCTTGTCTCAAAGATTAAGCC-3′)/EIR3 (5′-ATGCATACTCAAAGATTACC-3′) (Power et al., 2009) and Sdae-Cox1_260F (5′-GATCTTTATGTTYTTRATGCC-3′) (Ogedengbe et al., 2016)/SR4(5′-CCACACCTGTAGTACCDCC-3′) (Gjerde, 2013), respectively. Reactions (20 μL total volume) contained 14.8 μL nuclease-free water, 2 μL 10 × Reaction Buffer, 1.6 μL dNTP mix (2.5 mM each), 0.2 μL each primer (50 μM), 0.2 μL APP Taq DNA polymerase (5 U/μL, GeneTeks BioScience, Taiwan), and 1 μL template DNA. PCR included positive controls (Cystoisospora rivolta) and negative controls (nuclease-free water) to exclude contamination and inhibition. Amplicons were electrophoresed on 1.5% agarose gels stained with SmartGlow™ Loading Dye (Accuris, NJ, USA) and visualized under a BlooK LED transilluminator (GeneDireX, Taiwan). Positive PCR products were purified and bidirectionally sequenced by Genomics BioSci & Tech. Co., Ltd. (New Taipei, Taiwan). Raw sequences were aligned and trimmed using Geneious Prime 2025.0.2 (Biomatters Limited, Auckland, New Zealand; https://www.geneious.com). Consensus sequences were compared against the GenBank database via BLASTn for taxonomic identification.

Phylogenetic analysis

Three datasets were analyzed to infer phylogenetic relationships: (i) partial 18S rRNA sequences, (ii) partial cox1 sequences, and (iii) a concatenated alignment combining both loci. Publicly available 18S rRNA and cox1 sequences from Sarcocystidae and related coccidia were retrieved from GenBank and combined with sequences generated in this study. Sequence alignments were performed using MAFFT v7.490 (Katoh and Standley, 2013) for 18S rRNA and Clustal Omega v1.2.2 (Sievers et al., 2011) for cox1, both executed within Geneious Prime 2025. Individual gene alignments were concatenated without further adjustment using Geneious Prime's sequence concatenation tools. The generated alignment was assessed by partition homogeneity (ILD) test (Farris et al., 1994) with 1000 replicates by heuristic search in PAUP∗ 4.0a169 (Swofford, 2003) to determine whether this two genes could be combined. The ILD test failed to reject the null hypothesis of homogeneity (p = 0.201), supporting the concatenation of datasets.

Phylogenetic reconstructions for each dataset (single genes and concatenated alignment) were conducted using Bayesian Inference (BI) and Maximum Likelihood (ML), with Sarcocystis rileyi designated as the outgroup. The best-fit substitution models for each dataset were selected via Akaike Information Criterion corrected in ModelFinder v2.2.0 (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017). The General Time Reversible model with empirical base frequencies, a gamma distribution of rate heterogeneity, and a proportion of invariable sites (GTR + F + I + G4) was identified as optimal for all datasets. BI analyses were conducted in MrBayes v3.2.7a (Ronquist et al., 2012), running four Markov Chain Monte Carlo chains for 50 million generations (two parallel runs), sampling every 1000 generations, and discarding the initial 25 % as burn-in. ML analyses were performed in IQ-TREE v2.2.0 (Nguyen et al., 2015) with 1000 ultrafast (Minh et al., 2013) bootstrap replicates. All phylogenetic workflows, including model selection and tree reconstruction, were integrated within PhyloSuite v1.2.3 (Zhang et al., 2020; Xiang et al., 2023). Final trees were visualized and annotated in Geneious Prime 2025.0.2.

Other hosts: None to date.

Geographic distribution: Known only from the type locality.

Oocysts: Two subspheroidal sporoblasts (Fig. 1A and B) with granular cytoplasm within oocysts in fresh fecal samples. Mature oocyst (Fig. 1C) spheroidal to subspherical, and measured L × W (n = 50): 15.9 ± 0.94 × 14.1 ± 0.83 (13.7–18.8 × 11.6–16.4); L/W ratio: 1.1 ± 0.08 (1.0–1.4). Oocyst wall single-layered, smooth, colorless, and fragile; wall thickness (n = 34): 0.6 ± 0.08 (0.4–0.7), collapsing around sporocysts post-sporulation (Fig. 1D and E). M, MC, OR, and PG all absent.

Sporocysts and sporozoites: Sporocysts subspherical (Fig. 1, Fig. 2) or ellipsoidal (Fig. 1F), with a smooth, colorless, single-layered wall; and measured L × W (n = 66): 10.8 ± 1.01 × 8.0 ± 0.61 (8.8–12.6 × 6.8–9.8); L/W ratio: 1.35 ± 0.14 (1.1–1.7); SB, SSB, PSB: all absent; SR: present, SR spheroidal, membrane-bound, containing coarse granules, and measured L × W (n = 26): 3.6 ± 0.51 × 3.2 ± 0.52 (4.5–3.0 × 4.2–2.4); SZ drop-shaped, tapered at both ends, and measured L × W (n = 32): 5.4 ± 0.58 × 3.0 ± 0.34 (4.1–6.9 × 2.6–3.9), without discernible N or RB visible.

Site of infection: Unknown. Oocysts were recovered from fecal samples.

Sporulation: Exogenous. Oocysts in fresh fecal samples contained two sporoblasts (Fig. 1A and B), with sporozoites visible in partially sporulated oocysts (Fig. 1B). Complete sporulation was achieved at 26 °C in 5 days (2.5 % K2Cr2O4 solution).

Prevalence: Found in 2/15 (13.3 %) specimens of the type host examined for oocysts.

Cross-transmission: No experimental or natural transmissions confirmed.

Pre-patent and patent period: Undefined; oocysts detected intermittently during the sampling period (>2 month).

ZooBank registration: 716ED709-B578-47C1-BCB5-675850FBF62A.

Etymology: The specific epithet jiunnshiowi is a Latinized genitive form derived from the given name Jiunn-Shiow of Professor Jiunn-Shiow Wang (National Chung Hsing University, Taiwan). This nomenclature honors his pioneering contributions to veterinary parasitology education in Taiwan and his extensive research on coccidian taxonomy and biodiversity.

Material deposited: Phototypes (Bandoni and Duszynski, 1988) were deposited at the Meguro Parasitological Museum, Meguro City, Tokyo, Japan under MPM Coll. No. 25384 (mature oocyst) and 25385 (sporocyst). Sequences representing 18S rRNA (accession no. PV892907) and cox1 (accession no. PV844037) were deposited in GenBank.

Remarks: Batrachospora jiunnshiowi n. sp. most closely resembles B. caeruleae Tokiwa et al., 2025 (described from Ranoidea caerulea, Hylidae) in its oocyst L/W ratio (∼1.1), albeit with significantly smaller dimensions (15.9 × 14.1 vs. 19.8 × 18.8) (Tokiwa et al., 2025) and notable genetic divergence in both mitochondrial and nuclear loci (see 3.2. Phylogenetic analysis). Among Batrachospora spp. parasitizing bufonid hosts of the genus Duttaphrynus (Table 1), B. jiunnshiowi is distinguished by its single-layered oocyst wall, contrasting sharply with the double-layered walls characteristic of B. stomatici and B. wenyoni (Ray and Das-Gupta, 1935; Chakravarty and Kar, 1952). Furthermore, oocysts of B. jiunnshiowi n. sp. exhibit a subspherical tendency (L/W range: 1.0–1.4) compared to the ellipsoidal morphology (L/W: ∼1.5) of B. stomatici and B. wenyoni, while also being morphometrically diminutive (15.9 × 14.1 vs. 21.3 × 20.5 in B. stomatici). The drop-shaped sporozoites represent an autapomorphy not observed in any described Batrachospora species (Duszynski et al., 2007). This unique sporozoite morphology, coupled with molecular synapomorphies, unequivocally supports its status as a novel taxon within the subfamily Hyaloklossinae.

Phylogenetic analysis

This study obtained 1447 bp of 18S rRNA and 839 bp of cox1 sequences from two Batrachospora-positive Duttaphrynus cf. melanostictus individuals, which exhibited complete identity across both loci. BLAST analysis revealed 98.4–98.6 % identity (100 % query coverage) between the 18S rRNA sequences of species in subfamily Hyaloklossinae (Batrachospora caeruleae LC842307, Hyaloklossia lieberkuehni AF298623, H. kasumiensis LC669718), while cox1 sequences showed 93.6 % identity with B. caeruleae (LC842308) and 94.0–94.2 % identity with H. kasumiensis (LC602189, LC669719–720).

Phylogenetic reconstructions using BI and ML methods yielded same topologies across all datasets (18S rRNA, cox1, and concatenated sequences). These analyses robustly supported the monophyly of subfamily Eumonosporinae and Hyaloklossinae (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). Notably, the phylogenetic placement of Nephroisospora exhibited incongruence across datasets, clustering within the Cystoisospora clade in cox1-based analyses (Fig. 3), but nested within Toxoplasmatinae in the 18S rRNA topology (Fig. 4). While cox1 and concatenated datasets positioned Hyaloklossinae sister to the Cystoisospora–Nephroisospora clade (Fig. 3, Fig. 5), the 18S rRNA-based phylogeny depicted Hyaloklossinae as basal to the taxa analyzed in current study (Fig. 4). Strikingly, B. jiunnshiowi n. sp. clustered unequivocally within Hyaloklossinae across all topologies, exposing paraphyly in genus Batrachospora contrasted against the monophyletic integrity of genus Hyaloklossia (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4).

Discussion

The present study successfully characterizes a novel coccidian species from the Asian black-spined toad (Duttaphrynus cf. melanostictus), distinguished by unique sporozoite morphology that readily differentiates it from other anuran-infecting coccidia (Duszynski et al., 2007; Cao et al., 2017; Tokiwa et al., 2025). Although phylogenetic analyses reveal paraphyly with the type species B. caeruleae (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5), the morphology of its oocysts—featuring two sporocysts each containing four sporozoites, absence of SB, SSB, and PSB, and exogenous sporulation—conforms unequivocally to the diagnostic criteria for Batrachospora as established in previous taxonomic frameworks (Tokiwa et al., 2025). Phylogenetic placement within the subfamily Hyaloklossinae further validates its classification under Batrachospora, culminating in its designation as B. jiunnshiowi n. sp.

To date, coccidian infections in Duttaphrynus hosts comprise two Eimeria species (Eimeria himalayana in D. himalayanus and Eimeria laminata in D. melanostictus) and three Batrachospora species (B. stomatici in D. stomaticus, B. wenyoni, and the newly described B. jiunnshiowi n. sp. in D. cf. melanostictus). Most reports originate from India, with all except B. wenyoni confined to their type localities and hosts (Duszynski et al., 2007). Batrachospora wenyoni was documented in two dicroglossid hosts—Fejervarya limnocharis and Hoplobatrachus tigerinus—in Bengal, with oocysts measuring 19.8–15.4 × 15.4–13.3 and sporocysts 13.2–9.9 × 9.8–7.7 (Chakravarty and Kar, 1952). However, re-examination of H. tigerinus from Calcutta revealed larger oocysts (17.5 × 14.5, range 15–20.5 × 13.5–15.5; L/W ratio 1.2) with an OR and sporocysts measuring 11.8 × 8.5 (10–13.5 × 7.5–9.5; L/W ratio 1.3), dimensions significantly divergent from the original description of B. wenyoni (Ray and Das-Gupta, 1935; Mandal, 1976, 1987). Subsequent taxonomic reviews concluded that neither study likely examined true B. wenyoni oocysts (Duszynski et al., 2007). Morphologically, B. jiunnshiowi n. sp. differs from these disputed records in its smaller oocyst/sporocyst dimensions, reduced L/W ratio, and longer sporulation time (>5 days vs. 60–70 h or 2–3 days). In light of recent taxonomic studies on the Asian black-spined toad, the population in Taiwan belongs to the eastern lineage and is considered likely to represent a distinct species from the Indian population (western lineage) of Duttaphrynus melanostictus (Dufresnes et al., 2025). Thus, B. jiunnshiowi n. sp. likely represents the first documented coccidian infection in the eastern lineage (D. cf. melanostictus).

Phylogenetic analyses of all datasets (18S rRNA, cox1, concatenated sequences) robustly place B. jiunnshiowi n. sp. within the monophyletic clade of subfamily Hyaloklossinae (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5), consistent with prior studies (Tokiwa et al., 2021, 2022a, 2022b, 2025). Within this clade, paraphyly is evident between B. jiunnshiowi n. sp. and B. caeruleae, while Hyaloklossia species form a monophyletic group. Notably, Hyaloklossia sequences derive exclusively from Pelophylax hosts (Ranidae), whereas the two Batrachospora species infect hosts from distinct families (Hylidae and Bufonidae) (Tokiwa et al., 2022a, 2025). Given known host specificity patterns in sarcocystids (Frank and Häfner, 1981; Fayer, 2004), it is reasonable to hypothesize that divergent host affiliations (Bufonidae vs. Hylidae) drove the observed phylogenetic split. The studied coccidian was assigned to Batrachospora because Hyaloklossia and Batrachospora are clearly distinguishable by infection site (renal vs. intestinal) and sporocyst characteristics (Tokiwa et al., 2025), even though molecular topology alone cannot resolve taxonomy. This phylogenetic discordance between morphological groupings and molecular clade delineations mirrors evolutionary patterns documented in Cystoisospora lineages (Ogedengbe et al., 2016; Tokiwa et al., 2018), emphasizing the necessity of integrative taxonomic approaches reconciling multi-locus molecular phylogenies with morphological and life-cycle characterizations (Tokiwa et al., 2025). However, definitive conclusions remain premature, as current sampling for Hyaloklossinae encompasses only four species with limited genetic data. Resolving intra-subfamilial relationships necessitates expanded sampling across diverse hosts and geographies.

Data on anuran coccidia in Taiwan remain particularly scarce, rendering the present investigation potentially the first molecular survey of these parasites in D. cf. melanostictus in Taiwan. Although only 2 of 15 D. cf. melanostictus (13.3 %) tested positive, this detection rate aligns with established infection patterns for anuran coccidians globally (Upton and McAllister, 1988; Duszynski et al., 2007); as exemplified by the 6.7 % of North American salamanders harboring described coccidian species (Upton et al., 1993). Seasonal dynamics appear critical for detection sensitivity: B. fragosum exhibits elevated prevalence during post-dormancy emergence periods (spring/autumn), contrasting with reduced rates in hot, dry summers—a pattern potentially linked to environmental influences on transmission and oocyst viability (Upton and McAllister, 1988). Our positive samples from October corresponded with post-monsoon humidity surges, conditions hypothesized to enhance oocyst sporulation and environmental persistence. Nonetheless, the absence of longitudinal life-cycle studies restricts causal inferences regarding these temporal patterns. Consequently, future sampling efforts will prioritize humid, moderate-temperature months (e.g., September–November) across Taiwan to establish a comprehensive genetic database of anuran coccidia. Such expanded surveillance is essential to clarify parasite diversity, host specificity, and transmission ecology—critical gaps noted in current amphibian parasitology research.
