Burden, trends, driving factors, and predictions of gastric cancer: a cross-national comparative analysis of China, Japan, and South Korea
Authors: Wei Liu, Zhen-zhen Peng, Tao Zhang, Kui Liao, Juan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a leading malignant condition worldwide and imposes a substantial disease burden [1]. According to data from the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) 2022 data, GC is the fifth most common malignancy worldwide in terms of both incidence and mortality [2]. In 2022, there were 0.968 million new cases and 0.659 million fatalities Globally, accounting for 6.8% of all cancer-related deaths [2]. Although advances in early screening, treatment, and changes in diet and lifestyle have contributed to a global decline in GC burden, the incidence and mortality rates remain notably high in East Asia, particularly in China, South Korea, and Japan [3–5].

While existing studies have reported the incidence and mortality rates of GC across different regions, research on the spatiotemporal variations in GC burden over time and among different populations remains limited, especially in terms of comprehensive multifactorial analyses [6, 7]. Notably, comparative studies focusing on high-incidence regions such as China, South Korea, and Japan are still scarce. Moreover, current studies have not effectively quantified the specific contributions of factors like population aging and epidemiological changes to the overall GC burden, resulting in a lack of robust evidence to inform global or regional public health policies [3].

This study systematically investigates the temporal trends and underlying drivers of GC burden in China, Japan, South Korea, and globally. Special attention is given to high-incidence regions to better understand the demographic and epidemiological dynamics shaping disease trends. By providing comparative insights and evidence-based assessments, this work offers significant value in supporting the development of targeted strategies for GC prevention, early detection, and control. In particular, the in-depth analysis of high-risk regions such as China, Japan, and South Korea can help optimize the allocation of healthcare resources, guide early screening and treatment efforts, and ultimately reduce GC incidence and mortality rates, thereby alleviating the pressure on healthcare systems.

In particular, this analysis contributes novel insights in three ways: (i) it extends the observation period to 1990–2021, thereby capturing long-term trends across three decades; (ii) it employs integrated decomposition analysis to disentangle the contributions of population aging, epidemiological change, and population growth; and (iii) it provides cross-national comparative projections up to 2040 for China, Japan, and South Korea.

Methods

Data source

The data for this study were sourced from the GBD 2021 database, developed by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) [8, 9]. All data were retrieved using the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) query tool (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool). The GC burden data include incidence, mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), age-standardized incidence rates (ASIR), age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR), and DALYs for China, Japan, South Korea, and globally. GC cases were identified using ICD9 (151-151.9, 209.23, V10.04) and ICD10 (C16-C16.9, Z12.0, Z85.02-Z85.028) codes. The cancer burden estimation flowchart and related codes can be accessed via the following link: (dhttp://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-2021/code/cod-2). In the GBD study, all estimates’ 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) were derived from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles based on 1,000 random draws.

Study metrics

The primary indicators used in this study include incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). DALYs are the sum of years of life lost (YLLs) due to premature death and years lived with disability (YLDs) caused by the disease. YLLs are calculated by multiplying the number of deaths by the years of life lost, while YLDs are based on case numbers, disability weights, and the duration of health loss [8, 9]. For analyses across all age groups, we used age-standardized rates (ASR) per 100,000 individuals, including the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR), and age-standardized DALYs rate (ASDR). The data are broken down by gender and age group, based on the 5-year age intervals (< 5, 5–9, 10–14,. 85–89, 90–94, 95 + years) from the GBD database, to assess the impact of gender and age on GC burden. All data are accessible online through the GBD results tool (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool).

Estimated annual percentage change

To quantify the trend in GC burden from 1990 to 2021, we calculated the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), with the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for EAPC determined through linear modeling. This measure is widely accepted as a reflection of the trend in age-standardized rates (ASR) over time. The ASR can be fitted to a regression model as follows: , where stands for the calendar year. If the upper limit of the EAPC and its 95% CI are both negative, the corresponding rate is decreasing; conversely, if the lower limit of EAPC and its 95% CI are both positive, the corresponding rate is increasing [10, 11].

Decomposition analysis

Decomposition analysis was employed to identify the main drivers of Changes in GC burden. The Changes in burden from 1990 to 2021 were broken down into three factors: population aging, changes in population size, and epidemiological changes, with each factor undergoing quantitative analysis [12]. Population growth reflects changes in the total population size; population aging assesses the contribution of shifts in age structure; and epidemiological changes account for variations in burden due to improvements in medical interventions and screening levels, after adjusting for population size and age [13].

Attributable risk factor analysis

Attributable risk factor analysis was performed based on the distribution of GC risk factors from the GBD 2021 database [14]. The primary risk factors for GC include high-salt diet and smoking, both of which have substantial causal evidence linking them to the disease. We assessed the contribution of these two risk factors to GC mortality in 1990 and 2021, globally, in China, Japan, and South Korea, and calculated the attributable contribution of each risk factor to mortality. By integrating time trend data, we analyzed the dynamic effects of changes in these risk factors on the overall GC burden [15].

BAPC model prediction analysis

The Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model was used to predict future GC burden. This model incorporates data from 1990 to 2021, considering the effects of age, time, and birth cohort. It employs integrated nested Laplace approximation to forecast trends from 2022 to 2040, enabling complete Bayesian inference. Key features of the BAPC model include: (1) the generation of age-specific and age-standardized predicted rates; and (2) the automatic addition of Poisson noise when the predicted distribution is of interest [16, 17].

Statistical analysis

R software (version 4.2.1) and JD_GBDR was employed to perform all statistical analyses. A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was regarded as significant. BAPC analyses were conducted using the R packages “BAPC” (version 0.0.36) and “INLA.” Data visualization was carried out using “ggplot2”.

China’s ASIR decreased from 48.03/100,000 to 29.05/100,000, with an EAPC of −1.64 [95% CI: −1.82, −1.47] (Table 1). In 1990, China accounted for 41.5% of Global incidence., with 407,471 cases. By 2021, the incidence in China increased to 611,799 accounting for 49.8% of the global total (Table 2). Japan and South Korea showed more substantial improvements, with South Korea exhibiting the most remarkable progress. The ASIR in South Korea dropped from 71.18/100,000 in 1990 to 25.76/100,000 in 2021, with an EAPC of −3.54 [95% CI: −3.68,−3.41], the fastest decline among all regions. Japan’s ASIR decreased from 64.05/100,000 to 25.54/100,000, with an EAPC of −2.98 [95% CI: −3.05,−2.92], also showing a significant downward trend (Table 1; Fig. 1 A, Supplementary Figure S1).

Gender and age disparity of GC burden

GC burden is consistently higher in males than in females across countries. In 2021, the ASIR for males in China was 44.48 per 100,000, compared to 15.23 per 100,000 for females (Table 1; Fig. 1 A). A similar gender disparity was observed in Japan and South Korea, where the male ASIRs were 38.77 and 38.98 per 100,000, respectively, compared with 13.20 and 15.57 per 100,000 for females (Table 1; Fig. 1).

At the Global level, GC incidence also shows a clear age gradient, with males experiencing higher rates than females, particularly in the age groups over 60, where the disparity becomes even more pronounced. In the 65–69 age group, the incidence rate for males is nearly 400/100,000, while for females, it peaks at 150/100,000 in the same age group. The number of cases mirrors this trend, with approximately 100,000 cases in males in the 65–69 age group, compared to around 50,000 in females (Fig. 2 A, Supplementary Table S1).

In China, the incidence rate for males peaks in the 65–69 age group at about 450/100,000, then slightly decreases to 400/100,000 in the 70–74 age group. For females, the highest incidence rate is in the 60–64 age group, at 250/100,000. Regarding the number of cases, males in the 70–74 age group approach nearly 100,000 cases, while females have relatively fewer cases (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table S2).

In Japan, the incidence rate for males in the 65–69 age group reaches 500/100,000, much higher than that for females, which is below 250/100,000. The number of male cases in the 70–74 age group is close to 15,000, while the number of female cases is significantly lower (Fig. 2 C, Supplementary Table S3). In South Korea, the highest male incidence rate occurs in the 60–64 age group (around 750/100,000), then slightly declines but remains between 500–600/100,000. For females, the highest incidence rate is in the 70–74 age group, at approximately 300/100,000. In terms of the number of cases, males in the 70–74 age group exceed 3,000, while females in the 60–64 age group have only about 1,000 cases (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table S4).

Decomposition analysis of GC burden

In China, population aging was the primary driver of GC burden Changes, contributing to an increase of 305,249 cases. However, epidemiological changes (i.e., shifts in disease patterns) reduced 286,017 cases, resulting in a net increase of 204,328 cases in overall incidence. Similarly, the Changes in incidence rates in both the Global and South Korean populations were predominantly driven by population aging. Globally, the aging effect led to an increase of 501,064 cases, while epidemiological shifts reduced 655,467 cases. In contrast, Japan’s epidemiological Changes had a positive effect on incidence, increasing 97,283 cases, while the impacts of aging and population size changes on incidence were relatively small (Fig. 3 A, Supplementary Table S5).

In terms of DALYs, the Changes in both Global and Chinese burden were primarily driven by population aging. Globally, aging contributed to an increase of 9.74 million DALYs, while epidemiological Changes resulted in a significant reduction of 18.86 million DALYs. In China, population aging led to an increase of 6.04 million DALYs, whereas epidemiological Changes reduced DALYs by 10.29 million. In contrast, the impact of epidemiological Changes on DALYs in South Korea was relatively small, while Japan experienced a reduction of 1.14 million DALYs due to epidemiological shifts (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S6).

Risk factors and time trends of GC mortality burden

In 2021, high-salt diet and smoking were the leading risk factors for GC, with significant variation in their impact across gender and countries. Among males, Japan had the highest association with high-salt diet (4.90/100,000), significantly higher than China (3.59/100,000), South Korea (2.58/100,000), and the global average (1.27/100,000). Smoking had an especially prominent impact on male GC, with China leading at 8.67/100,000, followed by Japan (7.94/100,000) and South Korea (5.74/100,000) (Fig. 4 A, Supplementary Table S7). Among females, the impact of high-salt diet was also highest in Japan (2.68/100,000), with China and South Korea having rates of 1.55/100,000 and 1.40/100,000, respectively. The impact of smoking on females was smaller, but smoking-related incidence of Japan (0.77/100,000) was still higher than that of South Korea (0.71/100,000), China (0.36/100,000), and the global average (0.19/100,000) (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Table S8).

Over the past 30 years, GC mortality rates linked to smoking and high-salt diet have both declined Globally. However, in Japan, the proportion of deaths associated with high-salt diet has slightly increased since 2010, rising from approximately 7% to over 8%, while in China, the proportion of smoking-related deaths has seen a slight increase since 2000. In contrast, smoking- and high-salt diet-related mortality rates in both South Korea and globally have continued to decrease (Fig. 4C-D).

Predictions of GC ASIR and number of cases

Since 2021, the Global GC burden has been steadily decreasing. The number of cases worldwide is projected to gradually decline from approximately 1.2 million in 2021 to below 1 million by 2040, while the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) is expected to decrease from around 35 cases per 100,000 to about 20 cases per 100,000 (Fig. 5 A, Figure S2). South Korea is experiencing a particularly significant decline, with the annual number of cases dropping from over 30,000 in 2021 to around 15,000 by 2040, and the ASR decreasing from nearly 100 cases per 100,000 to below 25 cases per 100,000 (Fig. 5D, Supplementary Figure S2). Japan is also anticipated to see a significant reduction in GC burden, with the number of cases falling from approximately 75,000 in 2021 to about 25,000 by 2040, and the ASR dropping from 25 cases per 100,000 to below 15 cases per 100,000 (Fig. 5 C, Supplementary Figure S2).

China’s GC burden has seen a significant decline from 1990 to 2021. Future projections indicate that China’s GC burden will continue to decline, with the number of cases expected to fall to 300,000 by 2040, and the ASR approaching 10 cases per 100,000 (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Figure S2).

Discussion

This study analyzed the GC burden in China, Japan, South Korea, and Globally using data from the GBD 2021 database. The results show a significant reduction in the global GC burden in terms of age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) and mortality rate (ASMR). However, there are notable differences between China, South Korea, and Japan. The GC burden has decreased more rapidly in Japan and South Korea, while it remains relatively high in China, particularly among males, indicating that China still faces significant challenges in reducing its GC burden.

The results of this study reveal the impact of various factors on the GC burden, particularly Changes in population aging, lifestyle, and healthcare interventions. Population aging has been a significant driver of the increase in GC burden, especially in China. As the population continues to age, the incidence of GC is increasingly concentrated in those aged 65 and above [4].

The persistent male predominance in GC incidence and mortality observed in our study likely reflects a combination of biological, behavioral, and social factors. Men generally have higher prevalence of smoking and alcohol consumption, both of which are established risk factors for GC, particularly in East Asia [18]. In addition, occupational exposures such as dust, rubber, and certain chemical agents are more common in male-dominated industries and may further elevate risk [19]. Biological factors may also play a role, as experimental and epidemiological studies suggest that estrogen exerts protective effects on the gastric mucosa, potentially reducing chronic inflammation and carcinogenesis in females [20, 21]. Furthermore, gender-related differences in healthcare access and utilization, with women often more likely to participate in screening and preventive health services, could amplify these disparities. Collectively, these factors contribute to the sustained excess burden of GC among males [22].

Smoking and high-salt diet remain the primary risk factors for GC [23, 24]. While public health policies have made some progress in reducing smoking rates and improving dietary habits, particularly among men, further efforts are needed to strengthen anti-smoking policies and promote healthier diets [25]. Raising public awareness of the harms of smoking and unhealthy diets is essential for reducing the GC burden. Additionally, Japan and South Korea have made notable progress through early screening and effective treatment strategies. In particular, since the implementation of the national GC screening program in Japan in 2002, early detection rates have improved dramatically [26]. This demonstrates that early detection and intervention are crucial in reducing the GC burden. Therefore, China should enhance early GC screening, especially in high-risk areas and populations. By promoting screening programs and increasing public health awareness, early diagnosis and treatment outcomes can be significantly improved.

Compared to previous studies, the results of this study provide new insights into the changing trends and influencing factors of the GC burden. Globally, the GC burden is declining, which is consistent with the findings of Yang et al. (2021) [3], based on older data. However, this study goes further by analyzing differences in the GC burden in high-incidence regions such as China, South Korea, and Japan. It finds that China’s GC burden is decreasing more slowly, especially among males. This differs from the study by Zhang et al. (2021) [4], which indicated that the GC burden in China has improved, particularly in the eastern regions. This discrepancy may be due to the broader time period (1990–2021) covered by this study, which considers the combined effects of population aging and epidemiological changes, whereas Yang et al.‘s research focused primarily on earlier data and did not fully account for the impact of population aging [25].

Beyond these comparisons, our study provides added value in three main aspects. First, the extended time window of 1990–2021 allows us to capture long-term Changes and transitions that were not fully observed in earlier analyses. Second, the application of decomposition analysis offers a quantitative assessment of the relative contributions of population aging, epidemiological Changes, and demographic shifts to the evolving GC burden. Third, by incorporating BAPC modeling, we deliver cross-country projections of future burden up to 2040, providing forward-looking evidence to inform cancer control strategies in China, Japan, and South Korea.

Our study also found that although smoking and high-salt diet are recognized as major risk factors for GC Globally, their impact varies significantly across different countries and gender groups. Since 2005, with the implementation and promotion of the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control, smoking prevalence has steadily declined in most countries and regions. This decline is the primary reason for the reduced contribution of smoking to the GC burden over the past 30 years [27, 28]. However, the impact of smoking on the GC burden remains especially high among Chinese men, likely due to relatively weak tobacco control measures in China and the deeply rooted smoking culture among men. This also helps explain the slower reduction in the GC burden in China [29]. High salt intake is a significant risk factor for GC, as it may promote its development by stimulating the gastric mucosa, inducing intestinal metaplasia, and encouraging gastric dysplasia [30, 31]. Pickled foods may also contain nitrates, which facilitate the formation of nitrosamine compounds [32]. Dietary health strategies in Japan, including a high intake of fish and soy products and a low intake of animal fats and meats, may provide valuable insights for other countries to consider adopting [33].

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) has been classified as a Group I human carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), with a particularly significant carcinogenic effect on non-cardia GC (non-cardia GC) [34]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that eradicating H. pylori can effectively prevent the progression of mild to moderate atrophic gastritis and significantly reduce the incidence of GC [35, 36]. However, due to the absence of H. pylori-related data in the GBD database, this study could not assess the GC burden caused by H. pylori. Additionally, factors such as alcohol consumption, low intake of fruits and vegetables, obesity, poor oral health, Epstein-Barr virus infection, radiation exposure, and a family history of GC are also well-known risk factors for GC [37, 38].

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, Although smoking and high-salt diet were identified as the leading risk factors in our analysis, an important limitation is that the GBD dataset does not provide quantitative estimates for Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori), despite its classification as a Group I carcinogen by the IARC and its strong causal link with non-cardia GC [35]. Recent estimates suggest that in China, the attributable fraction of GC due to H. pylori is 78% for non-cardia and 62% for cardia GC [39]. Furthermore, while prevalence has declined in adults, emerging evidence indicates an increasing trend among children [40]. These dynamics could substantially influence future GC burden, raising concerns that projections based solely on past trends may underestimate the true risk. Incorporating age-stratified H. pylori prevalence data in future burden assessments will therefore be essential for refining projections and informing targeted prevention strategies.

Second, although the GBD dataset is comprehensive, its estimates partly rely on modeled inputs, especially in countries with incomplete cancer registry coverage. In such contexts, GBD extrapolates trends from neighboring countries or covariate-based models, which may introduce additional uncertainty into temporal trend estimates and projections. Third, the GBD database does not differentiate between GC subtypes (e.g., cardia vs. non-cardia GC), despite their distinct epidemiological profiles and etiological pathways. Non-cardia GC is strongly associated with H. pylori infection and high-salt diet, whereas cardia GC is more often linked to obesity and gastroesophageal reflux. The lack of subtype-specific analysis limits the granularity of our findings and may mask heterogeneous trends across populations. Fourth, these data constraints inevitably affect the precision of our decomposition analysis and projections. Decomposition relies on the accuracy of baseline inputs, and when estimates are partly modeled, the attribution of Changes to demographic or epidemiological factors may carry additional uncertainty. Fifth, although our BAPC-based projections provide useful insights into potential future trajectories of GC burden up to 2040, they should be interpreted with caution. The model assumes continuity of past trends, yet real-world changes may substantially alter outcomes. For example, nationwide expansion of early screening, dietary transitions, or strengthened tobacco control policies could accelerate declines beyond our estimates, while emerging risk factors or unequal healthcare access might slow progress. Therefore, these forecasts should be regarded as scenario-based projections rather than definitive predictions.

Conclusion

This study examines the trends, driving factors, and future projections of GC burden in China, South Korea, Japan, and Globally from 1990 to 2021. The results indicate that, while the global GC burden is generally decreasing, the decline in China is slower, primarily due to population aging, while South Korea and Japan have seen more significant reductions. The findings emphasize the importance of public health measures such as early screening, improved dietary habits, and lifestyle changes in reducing the GC burden, especially among high-risk groups. Future research should further integrate biomarkers and genomic data to explore the pathogenesis of GC and evaluate the effectiveness of public health interventions across different regions.
