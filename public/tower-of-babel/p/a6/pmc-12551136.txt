Implementing an AI-enhanced clinical decision support system for Stenotrophomonas maltophilia: a survey-based randomized controlled trial of antibiotic precision and impact on survival
Authors: Tai-Han Lin, Hsing-Yi Chung, Ming-Jr Jian, Chih-Kai Chang, Cherng-Lih Perng, Feng-Yee Chang, Yuan-Hao Chen, Hung-Sheng Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe World Health Organization has identified Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (SM) as a high-risk antibiotic-resistant pathogen. Notably, determining the effectiveness of current antibiotics against SM is challenging, leading to improper therapy and the spread of resistance. This study assessed how an artificial intelligence-clinical decision support system (AI-CDSS) utilizing mass spectrometry data to predict resistance enhances prescribing decisions and boosts survival.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial (ISRCTN16278872) involved 400 healthcare professionals, with 1,600 SM infections randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either standard practice (control, n = 800) or an AI-CDSS predicting resistance 1 day earlier (intervention, n = 800). Outcomes were assessed by healthcare professionals using structured surveys on days 3, 5, 7, and 14 after treatment initiation. Patient mortality was analyzed over a 14-day follow-up period.ResultsThe AI-CDSS group demonstrated significantly higher confidence (p < 0.001) in antibiotic prescription, decision-making efficiency, and appropriate antibiotic selection across all time points. Mortality was lower in the AI-CDSS group (92/800, 11.5%) than in the control group (121/800, 15.1%) (p = 0.03). Effective antibiotic choices and reliance on the AI-CDSS during the critical early stages of treatment contributed to improved patient outcomes.ConclusionsImplementation of the AI-CDSS in a clinical trial setting enhances prescribing confidence, improves decision-making and antibiotic selection, reduces mortality, and demonstrates clinical potential.Trial registrationISRCTN, ISRCTN16278872. Background

Antibiotic resistance presents a critical global health challenge, with the gram-negative superbug Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (SM) identified by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a particularly hazardous pathogen [1, 2]. Prompt and suitable antibiotic treatment is vital to enhance prognosis, particularly in immunocompromised patients, where mortality rates can reach 51% [3–5]. However, determining whether the current antibiotic regimens effectively treat SM infections remains a major clinical challenge [2]. Specifically, clinicians often rely on clinical vital signs and laboratory profiles, including complete blood count with differential counts and sepsis-related biomarkers, such as procalcitonin, C-reactive protein, and lactate, to assess a patient’s response to treatment [6]. Although these markers help evaluate clinical improvement, they do not confirm whether the prescribed antibiotics target the pathogen, potentially delaying optimal therapy and increasing the risk of treatment failure and spread of resistance.

Traditional diagnostic methods require up to 72 h for culture and species identification using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) and an additional 24 h for antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST) [7]. These lengthy procedures, totaling at least 96 h, force doctors to use broad empirical regimens. Such delays may increase the spread of antibiotic-resistant infections, especially when quick and precise antibiotic treatment is crucial for patient survival [4, 8]. The lack of tools for rapid resistance detection and treatment guidance exacerbates challenges in managing resistant infections [9].

Recent studies have highlighted the development of an artificial intelligence clinical decision support system (AI-CDSS) using MALDI-TOF MS data and machine learning algorithms to identify resistant bacteria efficiently [2, 10]. By enabling real-time prediction of antimicrobial resistance, these innovations address delays in traditional diagnostics and offer a pathway for timely and targeted treatments [2, 11, 12]. However, its implementation in real-world clinical settings and its impact on healthcare professionals’ decision-making behaviors remain underexplored.

Traditional diagnostic workflows are time-consuming and rely heavily on empirical treatment, limiting their effectiveness in managing antibiotic resistance. This study aimed to address these challenges by integrating the innovative capabilities of AI-CDSS into clinical practice through a survey-based randomized controlled trial (RCT). The AI-CDSS was previously developed using MALDI-TOF MS protein spectral data to train random forest models for predicting resistance to levofloxacin and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, achieving predictive performance with area under the curve values of 0.95 [2]. The trial will systematically evaluate the system's impact by gathering feedback from healthcare professionals regarding their confidence in antibiotic selection, decision-making efficiency, and overall satisfaction when treating SM infections. By analyzing these insights, this study sought to quantify the practical value of AI-CDSS in reducing diagnostic delays, improving treatment precision, and optimizing clinical workflows. Ultimately, it aims to facilitate the adoption of AI-CDSS technology as a transformative tool to combat antibiotic resistance and enhance global health outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This single-center RCT (ISRCTN16278872) was conducted at Tri-Service General Hospital to investigate the impact of AI-CDSS on antibiotic prescription confidence among healthcare professionals treating SM infections, a WHO-recognized superbug. Following Institutional Review Board approval (TSGHIRB No. C202305073), all eligible physicians (n = 794) were invited to participate through institutional email and clinical workgroup messaging platforms, where they received information about AI-predicted resistance reports for SM infections. To be eligible, physicians were required to have regular access to technological infrastructure, including computers or tablets with internet connectivity, and demonstrate willingness to undergo brief training on AI-CDSS use. Given that participation was voluntary and embedded within routine clinical workflow, 400 physicians ultimately agreed to participate and provided informed consent. The remaining 394 physicians did not participate due to various reasons, including declining participation, non-response, or unavailability during the study period, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Healthcare professionals were defined as licensed physicians with independent prescribing authority, including both residents and attending doctors. Healthcare workers without prescribing rights were excluded, such as nurses, medical interns, and medical students. Additionally, clinicians with less than one year of clinical experience were excluded, as first-year postgraduate doctors in our healthcare system are newly licensed practitioners who typically follow attending physicians' orders rather than making autonomous prescribing decisions.

During the study period (July–December 2024), various clinical specimens—including blood, urine, respiratory tract samples, body fluids, wound swabs, and catheter tips—were collected from hospitalized patients with suspected bacterial infections. Following standardized laboratory protocols, each specimen was inoculated onto non-selective agar and subcultured onto selective media as needed to obtain pure isolates over an average three-day incubation period. Isolates then underwent species identification using MALDI-TOF MS (bioMérieux, France) and AST with the VITEK-2 system (bioMérieux, France), which required approximately one day. Upon completion of species identification, preliminary reports were automatically generated and notification messages were sent to the responsible care teams—including residents and attending physicians. All laboratory procedures included mandatory quality control verification using recommended control strains, with final clinical reports released only after satisfactory completion of all testing and quality control requirements.

To distinguish SM infection from colonization, SM infection was defined as the isolation of SM from a clinical specimen in patients with compatible clinical signs or symptoms, followed by a switch to targeted therapy (levofloxacin or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) after SM identification, in accordance with the organism’s inherent resistance to most β-lactam antibiotics [13]. Patients who continued on broad-spectrum or non-targeted antibiotics after SM was identified were considered to have colonization rather than infection.

Demographic data were collected from 400 prescribing healthcare professionals to establish baseline characteristics for evaluating the impact of AI-CDSS on antibiotic prescribing practices and patient outcomes. As shown in Table 1, the participants predominantly consisted of male professionals (88.0%), with female representation at 12.0%. Age distribution analysis revealed that 40.5% were young doctors (≤ 35 years), 52.0% were mid-career doctors (36–50 years), and 7.5% were senior doctors (> 51 years). Clinical experience was further stratified by career stage, with 36.0% being residents, 17.5% young attending physicians (≤ 10 years), 34.2% experienced attendings (11–20 years), and 12.2% senior attendings (> 20 years). The participating physicians encompassed a broad spectrum of specialties, with 7.8% from infection-disease-related departments, 21.3% serving as intensivists, 30.8% practicing in internal medicine (excluding infectious diseases and intensive care), and 40.3% representing various other specialties. Regarding education, the majority held bachelor’s degrees (78.2%), whereas 21.0% possessed a Doctor of Philosophy and 0.8% had master's degrees. Academic rank analysis indicated that 17.5% were assistant professors, 13.8% were associate professors, and 3.8% were professors, with 17.8% of participants holding leadership positions. Demographic data of healthcare professionals

Patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either the AI-CDSS intervention or the control group using a computer-generated sequence stratified by age and sex at the time of enrollment. The randomization was performed by HSS using block randomization with a fixed block size of 8, implemented through the NumPy 1.26.4 library (Python 3.9.18). To maintain allocation concealment, the sequence was stored in a password-protected, read-only Excel (.xlsx) file with sheet-level protection, accessible only to THL. Eligible patients were identified and enrolled by HYC based on confirmed SM infection and predefined inclusion criteria. Following enrollment confirmation, THL assigned participants sequentially to their allocated group according to the pre-generated sequence. Healthcare professionals could not be blinded due to the intervention requiring the use of the AI-CDSS interface, while patients remained blinded to group allocation. Sample size calculation using G*Power 3.1.9.4 indicated that 190 patients per group were required to detect statistically significant between-group differences, assuming a medium effect size (d = 0.4), α = 0.05, and 95% power for a two-tailed Mann–Whitney U test. Implementation intervention

System integration

The AI-CDSS was integrated directly into the hospital's electronic health record (EHR) system. This integration enabled the delivery of resistance predictions from MALDI-TOF MS data 1 day earlier compared to conventional methods. The system was designed for adaptability across different instrument settings, and targeted surveys were employed to gather feedback on usability and clarity.

Training and education

A series of 2-h interactive workshops was conducted for prescribing clinicians. These sessions combined didactic lectures, hands-on simulations, and online refresher modules to ensure comprehensive training on the system’s functionalities. This multifaceted training approach aligns with established implementation strategies and supports the adoption of new technologies [14].

Workflow modifications

To improve antibiotic prescribing, the AI-CDSS was directly incorporated into the prescription workflow. After consulting with clinicians and evaluating existing processes, the system’s recommendations were positioned at a key step prior to finalizing orders. This adjustment ensures that clinicians receive relevant, actionable insights precisely when making treatment decisions. The objective was to promote consistent, evidence-based prescribing without unnecessarily altering the established workflow.

Feedback and iterative refinement

To drive continuous enhancement of the AI-CDSS, a systematic feedback loop was established anchored in the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). Dedicated surveys and focus groups were designed to probe key dimensions such as the intervention's complexity, the organizational context, and individual clinician experiences. Three focus group sessions were conducted during the intervention period at weeks 2, 6, and 12 of implementation, with 6 participants per group. These sessions were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and examined using reflexive thematic analysis. By mapping participant insights to CFIR constructs, challenges related to the system interface and workflow management were identified while recognizing the positive impact of strong leadership and favorable staff attitudes. This structured, iterative evaluation enabled the implementation of targeted refinements that further improved system performance and clinician engagement [15, 16].

Group comparison in antibiotic decision-making

Both groups received identical initial clinical management, including empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, standard vital sign monitoring, and routine laboratory assessment. Day 3 represents the critical time point when MALDI-TOF MS species identification becomes available to both groups. The control group received only the species identification (S. maltophilia) and awaited conventional AST results. In contrast, the AI-CDSS intervention group received the same species identification plus AI-CDSS-predicted antibiotic resistance patterns to levofloxacin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole at day 3. The earlier availability of resistance information enabled the intervention group to make more informed therapeutic decisions during the critical transition from empirical to targeted therapy.

Outcomes and data collection

All healthcare professionals contributed to both study arms, caring for both intervention and control patients within their clinical duties. Survey responses reflected their experiences with individual patients in each group using unique patient identifiers, with group comparisons performed at the patient level. The primary outcome was healthcare professionals'confidence in antibiotic prescriptions, while secondary outcomes included decision-making efficiency, reliance on technological tools, and effectiveness of antibiotic choices in addressing public health concerns related to antibiotic resistance.

Structured surveys were administered to healthcare professionals on days 3, 5, 7, and 14 after treatment initiation to assess their confidence and decision-making throughout the treatment process. These time points were strategically selected to align with the clinical workflow: day 3 captures when the AI-CDSS provides resistance predictions, allowing clinicians to transition from empirical to targeted therapy one day earlier than conventional AST results, which become available on day 4. Day 5 provides sufficient time for clinicians to receive, review, and act upon the conventional AST results. Subsequent assessments on days 7 and 14 evaluate how confidence and decision-making evolve as treatment outcomes become apparent. The 14-day endpoint aligns with standard infectious disease research timelines and captures most acute clinical outcomes, including treatment response and mortality [17, 18].

Four domains were assessed using a structured questionnaire: (Q1) confidence in antibiotic prescribing, (Q2) decision-making efficiency, (Q3) reliance on technological tools, and (Q4) effectiveness of antibiotic choices in mitigating public health concerns related to antibiotic resistance. Each item used a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Not at all, 5 = Extremely), with exact items provided in Supplementary Table 1. Survey completion was monitored by two research assistants, achieving complete responses from all participants on Day 3 and 86.7% completion through Day 14. When patients died during follow-up, subsequent surveys were not required, and analyses included only available cases at each time point. Patient survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves over a 14-day follow-up period.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Tri-Service General Hospital (Reference: C202305073) to ensure adherence to ethical standards for medical research. Written informed consent was waived because the AI-CDSS machine learning model utilized only de-identified MALDI-TOF MS signal intensity vector data, with no personal or identifiable patient information included. The study intervention maintained all routine laboratory and clinical management processes without delaying acute infection care, thereby posing minimal risk to patients. All participating clinicians provided written informed consent, as they retained full autonomy to accept or modify treatment recommendations based on AI-CDSS predictions and were responsible for completing study questionnaires. The study implemented strict protocols to protect individual privacy and confidentiality.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis employed a comprehensive approach to evaluate the clinical and implementation outcomes of the AI-CDSS intervention. Baseline participant characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Group comparisons were conducted using chi-square tests for categorical variables, and either independent-sample t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, depending on whether the data followed a normal distribution. Survey outcome scores showed skewed distributions, so group comparisons for these measures were analyzed using Mann–Whitney U tests. Additionally, mixed linear models were utilized to evaluate longitudinal trends in survey outcomes, effectively accounting for repeated measurements. All analyses were performed using Python 3.9.18 with SciPy 1.11.4, pandas 2.1.4, statsmodels 0.14.3, and NumPy 1.26.4, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics and study flow

Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, and laboratory parameters measured at enrolment, were well balanced between the control (n = 800) and AI-CDSS groups (n = 800), with no statistically significant differences observed (Table 2). The mean age was 74.67 ± 14.29 years in the control group and 74.52 ± 14.20 years in the AI-CDSS group (p = 0.83). Males accounted for 61.8% of both groups (p = 1.00). Diabetes mellitus was present in 41.5% of the control group and 43.0% of the AI-CDSS group (p = 0.58). The most common infection site was pulmonary in both groups (91.8% control vs. 89.0% AI-CDSS), followed by urinary tract and bloodstream infections. No significant differences in infection site distribution were observed between groups (p = 0.12). Laboratory parameters, including white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet count, creatinine, total bilirubin, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, and lactate levels, were also comparable between the two groups, with all p-values exceeding 0.05. This balance in baseline characteristics ensured comparability between the intervention and control groups in subsequent analyses. Among study isolates, the proportion of levofloxacin-resistant S. maltophilia was 25.4% in the AI-CDSS group versus 23.0% in the control group (p = 0.37). Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole-resistant isolates comprised 22.7% and 21.8% of isolates in the respective groups (p = 0.72). These similar resistance rates indicate that both groups had comparable microbiological profiles.

Patient recruitment occurred from July to December 2024, with a 14-day follow-up completed by January 14, 2025. Survey completion was 100% on Day 3 in both groups (800/800 for each group). By Day 14, completion rates were 88.5% (708/800) in the AI-CDSS group and 84.9% (679/800) in the control group. The primary cause of attrition was patient mortality, which precluded further survey completion. No survey responses were missing due to withdrawal, non-compliance, or post-randomization exclusions, as research assistants actively prompted physicians to complete surveys for all eligible cases. All outcomes were analyzed according to the original randomized group assignments using an available-case (modified intention-to-treat) approach. No adverse events or unintended effects related to the AI-CDSS intervention were reported during the study period.

Primary outcome: confidence in antibiotic prescribing

Healthcare professionals in the AI-CDSS group demonstrated significantly higher confidence in antibiotic prescriptions than those in the control group across all assessed time points, as shown in Fig. 2a. On day 3, the AI-CDSS group had a higher confidence score (4.00 ± 0.81) compared to the control group (2.73 ± 0.85), with a mean difference of 1.27 (95% confidence interval (CI): 1.19–1.36). Secondary outcomes: satisfaction with the prescription process

Healthcare professionals using the AI-CDSS demonstrated superior performance across all secondary outcomes throughout the study period. As depicted in Fig. 2b, decision-making efficiency consistently favored the AI-CDSS group, with scores of 4.16 ± 0.59 versus 3.23 ± 0.54 on day 3 (mean difference: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.88–1.00) and sustaining this advantage through day 14 (4.65 ± 0.49 vs. 4.19 ± 0.53; mean difference: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.41–0.51). Reliance on technological support tools, as shown in Fig. 2c, revealed a distinct temporal pattern, with the AI-CDSS group showing strong initial engagement on day 3 (3.77 ± 0.74 vs. 2.99 ± 0.74; mean difference: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.71–0.85), peaking on day 5 (3.98 ± 0.74 vs. 3.54 ± 0.73; mean difference: 0.45, 95% CI: 0.38–0.51), then naturally declining by days 7 and 14 as clinical conditions stabilized. The perceived effectiveness of antibiotic choices, illustrated in Fig. 2d, consistently favored the AI-CDSS group, beginning with higher effectiveness scores on day 3 (3.90 ± 0.62 vs. 2.45 ± 0.59; mean difference: 1.45, 95% CI: 1.39–1.51) and maintaining superiority through day 14 (4.23 ± 0.58 vs. 3.38 ± 0.59; mean difference: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.80–0.90). All comparisons achieved statistical significance (p < 0.001).

Mortality outcomes

During the 14-day follow-up period, mortality was significantly lower in the AI-CDSS group compared to the control group. The AI-CDSS group had 92 deaths out of 800 patients (11.5%) versus 121 deaths out of 800 patients (15.1%) in the control group, representing an absolute risk difference of 3.6% (95% CI: 0.3% to 6.9%, p = 0.03). The hazard ratio for mortality was 0.75 (95% CI: 0.57 to 0.98), indicating a 25% reduction in the risk of death associated with AI-CDSS use. As shown in Fig. 3, both groups experienced mortality events during the first 3 days after the empiric treatment regimen was initiated. However, the AI-CDSS group benefited from the earlier prediction of bacterial resistance, enabling timely intervention with targeted antibiotic selection. Therefore, this early intervention likely prevented the increase in mortality observed in the control group after Day 3. Qualitative feedback

Reflexive thematic analysis revealed several key themes that were mapped to CFIR constructs to guide implementation refinements. Overall, participants demonstrated strong acceptance of the AI-CDSS, appreciating its usability and seamless workflow integration. "The AI-CDSS fits naturally into our decision-making process without adding extra steps" (Intensivist-03). Enhanced clinical confidence emerged as a significant benefit, particularly for complex cases, with participants describing increased security in their antibiotic choices. "I'm more confident explaining my antibiotic choices to patients and families" (Internal Medicine Attending-15). Implementation success was further supported by strong institutional leadership and favorable staff attitudes. "Our department head's endorsement made the transition much smoother and gave us confidence to try the new system" (Internal Medicine Attending-08), while positive peer influence helped overcome individual hesitations. "Seeing colleagues embrace the technology and share their positive experiences helped overcome my initial hesitations" (Intensivist-12).

However, participants also identified specific challenges that required attention. Initial concerns about technology adoption were noted, though these diminished over time through experience and training. "I was skeptical at first, but seeing the accuracy over time built my trust" (Internal Medicine Attending-21). Variations in technological proficiency among participants became evident, with some expressing greater comfort with digital interfaces while others required additional support. "The younger residents adapted to it immediately, but I needed more time to get comfortable with all the features" (Internal Medicine Attending-17). Interface-related challenges were also identified, particularly regarding information display. "At first, the interface felt overwhelming with too much data on one screen" (Internal Medicine Attending-09). Based on this feedback, customized engagement approaches were implemented, including targeted training sessions tailored to different experience levels and one-on-one support for clinicians requiring additional assistance. "The personalized training sessions really helped me understand how to use the system effectively in my workflow" (Internal Medicine Attending-23). Participants also suggested enhanced technical support during implementation. "Having 24/7 technical support during the initial rollout would have been beneficial" (Intensivist-02). This structured feedback process enabled targeted refinements, including interface simplification, which participants noted improved system performance and engagement throughout the implementation period.

Discussion

The AI-CDSS significantly improved clinician confidence, antibiotic prescribing efficiency, and patient outcomes for SM infections. By providing accurate resistance predictions by day 3, the system enabled timely, targeted therapy that measurably reduced mortality compared to traditional approaches. Key implementation facilitators included leadership support, effective clinician training, and seamless EHR integration. However, variability in clinicians'technological reliance and engagement levels highlighted the need for tailored implementation strategies addressing individual attitudes and proficiency. These findings demonstrate how well-designed AI-CDSS can potentially overcome diagnostic delays and support rapid clinical decision-making.

Key characteristics facilitated the AI-CDSS intervention's successful clinical implementation. The system offered a clear advantage over conventional methods by predicting antibiotic resistance from MALDI-TOF MS data at least 1 day earlier [2]. Its adaptability ensured seamless integration into existing workflows, providing actionable insights at critical decision points without disrupting established practices [15, 16]. The user-friendly interface and straightforward operation contributed to rapid adoption, with surveys suggesting high satisfaction levels [19]. Initial challenges included varying degrees of technological familiarity among clinicians and brief adaptation periods during early implementation. These observations emphasize the importance of tailored training programs to address different comfort levels with technology [20]. Continuous support, system refinements based on feedback, and practical training proved essential for building user trust and ensuring effective utilization in clinical settings [16, 21].

From the outer setting perspective of the CFIR framework, the AI-CDSS implementation addressed critical patient needs and limitations in managing antibiotic-resistant SM infections. The system provided timely, accurate antibiotic resistance predictions that reduced diagnostic delays and decreased reliance on broad-spectrum empirical therapies, aligning clinical practice with urgent treatment needs, especially for immunocompromised patients [2, 4, 8]. Although this study did not directly evaluate external policies, existing literature suggests that supportive public health incentives may facilitate the adoption of similar AI systems [2, 9, 12]. However, variations in institutional resources, technological infrastructure, and financial support may challenge broader implementation. Continued alignment with antimicrobial stewardship policy incentives and customized institutional support remains essential for the widespread adoption and long-term sustainability of the AI-CDSS intervention [19, 21].

Within the inner setting, this study identified several organizational factors that critically influenced the successful implementation of the AI-CDSS. Our military hospital's workforce consists primarily of personnel subject to mandatory service duration limits, requiring early retirement at specific rank-associated age thresholds. This results in a relatively younger clinician composition, as reflected in Table 1. The prevalence of younger physicians—often digital natives with greater technological fluency—likely contributed to the successful adoption and integration of the AI-CDSS into daily workflows [15, 16, 22]. The institution's innovative culture and readiness for change further supported smooth system integration with minimal disruption to existing practices. Nevertheless, variations in technological proficiency among staff highlighted the ongoing need for tailored training and continuous support to sustain effective AI-CDSS utilization over time [19, 21].

From the perspective of individual characteristics, clinicians' technological proficiency and innovation receptiveness significantly influenced AI-CDSS adoption. Younger physicians with greater digital experience demonstrated stronger initial acceptance and confidence with the system, facilitating its integration into prescribing workflows [22]. However, varying attitudes toward AI presented challenges, particularly among clinicians who were hesitant to rely on algorithmic recommendations or concerned about AI-driven decision-making risks. These variations underscore how individual self-efficacy and technology beliefs critically determine adoption success. To overcome these barriers, customized engagement approaches were developed, including targeted training that emphasized clinical utility and demonstrated system accuracy with evidence, ultimately building trust and acceptance [19, 21]. Recognizing and addressing such individual factors proves essential when implementing digital health interventions across different healthcare environments.

A structured implementation process proved essential for effectively deploying the AI-CDSS in clinical practice. During the initial planning phase, clear operational guidelines were established that outlined specific steps and roles to ensure seamless integration with existing prescribing workflows [15, 16]. Moving into the execution phase, practical training sessions were conducted while a dedicated implementation team supervised the process to address technical and procedural challenges promptly. Valuable insights were then gathered through regular reflection and evaluation via periodic surveys and focus group discussions, which directly informed targeted system refinements. Throughout the process, multidisciplinary engagement was actively promoted through collaborative meetings, enhancing coordination, clarifying responsibilities, and maintaining alignment between clinical goals and operational practices—all critical elements supporting effective, long-term system utilization [19, 21].

Qualitative focus group analysis revealed that ongoing monitoring, timely feedback, and targeted training substantially improved early acceptance and sustained engagement among clinicians, underscoring the essential role of continuous education in optimizing implementation [23]. The high adoption rates across various departments further demonstrated the system's strong alignment with clinical needs. These findings emphasize that successful implementation requires simultaneously addressing individual and organizational factors, confirming that tailored approaches effectively facilitate integration and sustained utilization in real-world clinical settings.

Our findings are consistent with existing literature demonstrating that AI-CDSS assists with enhancing patient outcomes [24]. Utilizing the CFIR framework allowed us to clearly identify facilitators and barriers influencing successful implementation. Unlike previous studies, which primarily emphasized clinical outcomes [9, 25, 26], this study uniquely highlights the influence of military institutional policies and generational differences in technological fluency as key facilitators. Specifically, mandatory service duration limits created a younger clinician demographic, fostering greater technological proficiency and facilitating rapid adoption. Thus, our findings extend the existing theory by emphasizing how institutional contexts and demographic characteristics significantly shape technology acceptance and sustained use.

This study demonstrates specific implementation factors that contributed to successful AI-CDSS adoption. Quantitatively, the AI-CDSS group achieved significantly higher confidence scores (p < 0.001), improved decision-making efficiency (p < 0.001), and reduced mortality (11.5% vs. 15.1%, p = 0.03) compared to the control group. Qualitative analysis identified several key facilitators of this success. First, the system achieved seamless workflow integration due to its natural fit with existing decision-making processes. Additionally, institutional leadership support and favorable staff attitudes facilitated adoption. However, generational differences in technology adaptation efficiency presented initial challenges among participants. Fortunately, the targeted 2-h interactive workshops effectively addressed these barriers, as confirmed by participant feedback on successful system integration into clinical workflows (Section 3.6). Collectively, these findings from our military hospital setting provide evidence for factors that supported AI-CDSS implementation success.

Despite these encouraging results, this study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. Primarily, this study was conducted within a single military hospital, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings to civilian settings and other healthcare environments. Additionally, clinicians with less than one year of clinical experience were excluded from participation, as they typically follow attending physicians'orders rather than making autonomous prescribing decisions, which may limit the applicability of our findings to all levels of clinical practice. Our reliance on self-reported survey data may introduce response biases and did not include a systematic assessment of antibiotic prescription appropriateness at the patient level, given the protocolized laboratory reporting (CLSI-based) and the frequent clinical need for individualized antibiotic adjustments. While no adverse events were reported, institutions should consider potential risks, including over-dependence on algorithmic recommendations, the need for robust backup systems during technical failures, and the ongoing resource requirements for training and maintenance. Additionally, because internal implementation factors were primarily focused on—such as clinician characteristics, organizational culture, and system usability—the evaluation of external environmental factors within the CFIR framework was limited. Thus, the broader influences of external policies, regulatory contexts, or inter-institutional peer interactions were not thoroughly examined. Lastly, the follow-up period was relatively short, which restricted the understanding of sustained long-term adoption.

Future research should address these limitations by conducting longitudinal, comparative studies that specifically target under-investigated CFIR domains, such as the role of external policies, regulatory environments, and peer influence among institutions. Objective metrics of system adoption, usage fidelity, and clinician adherence should be incorporated to complement subjective outcome measures, including systematic assessment of antibiotic prescription appropriateness through measures such as the proportion of patients prescribed with guideline-recommended agents and dosing appropriateness. Additionally, future investigations should explore alternative implementation strategies, such as varied training modalities or workflow integration methods, to identify best practices for broader system adoption. Expanding this research to include civilian hospitals, diverse geographic locations, and various healthcare settings would also clarify how institutional and demographic factors influence long-term AI-CDSS acceptance and effectiveness.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that integrating an AI-CDSS significantly improves clinician confidence, antibiotic prescribing efficiency, and patient survival outcomes in managing antibiotic-resistant SM infections. By systematically applying the CFIR framework, key implementation factors were identified specific to our military healthcare context, such as younger clinician demographics, strong organizational readiness, and intervention adaptability. Addressing these facilitators and barriers can help ensure the successful adoption and effective utilization of similar technologies in clinical practice.
