Response to Commentary on Repeatability of Multiple Breath Washout in Pediatric Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia
Authors: Wallace Wee, Sharon Dell
Published: 2026

To the editor,

It is a pleasure to share our work with the academic community and to have it received positively by the authors, Ahmed et al. In general, the points raised by Ahmed et al. are important considerations especially when building the ideal study. However, there are nuances to each of the potential limitations that were raised.

We agree that the standardization of airway clearance is important and has the potential to influence the lung clearance index (LCI). Our study had two arms (a) same‐day repeat testing, and (b) 28‐day repeat testing. For the same‐day repeat testing, we ensured that no pulmonary interventions were completed between repeat testing, thus eliminating airway clearance as a potential confounder. For 28‐day repeat testing, we made no changes to the patient's outpatient therapies (including airway clearance) over the 28‐day time interval, representing real‐life. Given the lack of standardization of the airway clearance, we would expect the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to be a conservative estimate (and not be artificially elevated as the authors suggest) because the lack of standardization of airway clearance would introduce random noise in the ICC calculation and reduce the value. In both arms we found that the ICC were good. It is also notable that a recent paper [1] found that a single airway clearance session did not significantly impact LCI in PCD patients. Overall this suggests that airway clearance is unlikely to have significantly impacted our findings.

Concurrent structural imaging would provide additional details about regional lung abnormalities including mucus plugging. However, the addition of structural lung imaging would not provide more information about whether the variability in LCI was due to physiologic changes or measurement artefact. For same‐day testing, the patient did not have any clinical status change or undergo any pulmonary interventions, making it a very low likelihood for any significant physiological changes to have occurred. Moreover, if measurement artefact was a meaningful concern, the ICC would be low due to the introduction of noise (as discussed in the above paragraph). In our study the ICC for same‐day repeat was high, suggesting that there is little to no measurement artefact. For 28‐day repeat testing, we used spirometry as a surrogate of the patient's clinical status. However, physiologic changes between testing are harder to control and likely contributed to the lower ICC as compared to same‐day repeat testing. Despite this, the study results suggest that LCI is reliable (ICC 0.71). Lastly, from a practical standpoint, computed tomography (CT) scans are best for identifying mucus plugs but subjecting a pediatric cohort to repeat CT scans to validate the 28‐day repeatability of a non‐invasive test (like multiple breath washout; MBW) would face significant ethical and logistical barriers.

Quality control for MBW testing is highly important. For our study, we are confident in the LCI results as the MBW were performed by qualified research team members who strictly followed the ERS consensus standards [2], and overreading of all MBW were completed by 1 qualified personnel. Thus, our study adhered to the strict standard operating procedures for MBW.

Ahmed et al. raised a question about the impact of circadian variability on the lung function results. For same‐day repeat testing, this was not an issue as the testing was completed within a short time interval. For 28‐day repeat testing, the time of day when the test was completed was not standardized and could have an impact on the results. However, we would expect this to lead to a more conservative ICC.

Lastly Ahmad et al. made a comment that the study's cohort, being made of older patients with mild disease severity, could have led to an overestimation of LCI repeatability, as younger or more severely ill children are harder to test. We agree with this potential limitation and we also identified it in our discussions. However, our cohort's demographics and baseline lung function are remarkably similar to the large international iPCD cohort, suggesting that our sample is representative of the typical pediatric PCD population. Nonetheless future studies that include patients with ranging disease severities and ages would be important.

In conclusion, the letter from Ahmad et al. lists several considerations in creating the ideal MBW study in PCD patients. However, the limitations listed do not invalidate our findings and the establishment that LCI is highly repeatable and a sensitive lung function measure for pediatric PCD patients.

Sincerely,

Dr. Wallace Wee and Dr. Sharon Dell.
