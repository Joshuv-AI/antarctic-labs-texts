Identification and mapping of groundwater recharge zones using multi influencing factor and analytical hierarchy process
Authors: Fanxiao Meng, Muhammad Ismail Khan, Syed Ali Asad Naqvi, Abid Sarwar, Fakhrul Islam, Muhammad Ali, Aqil Tariq, Sajid Ullah, Walid Soufan, Turki Kh. Faraj
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The management of groundwater systems is essential for nations that rely on groundwater as the principal source of communal water supply (e.g., Mohmand District of Pakistan). The work employed Remote Sensing and GIS datasets to ascertain the groundwater recharge zones (GWRZ) in the Mohmand District of Pakistan. Subsequently, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine the impact of geology and hydrologic factors on the variability of the GWRZ. The GWRZ was determined by employing weighted overlay analysis on thematic maps derived from datasets about drainage density, slope, geology, rainfall, lineament density, land use/land cover, and soil types. The use of multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA) involves the utilization of the multi-influencing factor (MIF) and analytical hierarchy procedure (AHP) to allocate weights to the selected influencing factors. The MIF data found that very high groundwater recharge spanned 1.20%, high zones covered 40.44%, moderate zones covered 50.81%, and low zones covered 7.54%. In comparison, the AHP technique results suggest that 1.81% of the whole area is very high, 33.26 is high, 55.01% is moderate, and 9.92% has low groundwater potential. The geospatial-assisted multi-influencing factor approach helps increase conceptual knowledge of groundwater resources and evaluate possible groundwater zones.

Introduction

Groundwater is considered a significant natural resource and is characterized as the water inside the predominant subterranean geological formations of the Earth's crust. Groundwater potential refers to the likelihood of the presence of water under the Earth's surface at a certain area. Groundwater has historically been sufficient, yet its availability has only sometimes aligned with the timing and locations of demand. Additionally, it has seldom met the ideal quantity and quality requirements for all intended uses1,2. Groundwater results from the interaction of geological, climatic, hydrological, physiographic, and biological aspects rather than by chance3. The rainfall is highly seasonal in Pakistan, where the climate is arid and semi-arid. Groundwater in most of the areas is the only source of freshwater supply4. One of these areas' most important natural resources is groundwater, which is used for various purposes, including drinking, agriculture, and other socio-economic and cultural activities5,6. At times, there are spatial variations in groundwater potentiality, occurring within a small distance of a few meters, and even within the confines of the same geological and geomorphological formation7.

The assessment of natural resources, particularly groundwater exploration and utilization in impenetrable rocky and arid regions, necessitates a thorough evaluation of multiple factors. These factors encompass land use/land cover (LULC), slope, rainfall, geology, drainage density, soil type, and lineament density5,8. Identifying and managing a vulnerable groundwater zone is challenging because of the need for more datasets. It requires a very consistent approach to management and conservation. Groundwater storage was formerly identified with conventional techniques like drilling, but recently, advanced technologies have extremely excellent tools5,9,10. Additionally, remote sensing (RS) data might be integrated into a cheap geographic information system (GIS) housed via an open-access portal for public use. In addition, the utilization of RS and GIS provides access to spatially and temporally available data while also offering expedited computational processing capabilities11,12.

Many studies12–17 have used RS and GIS methods to evaluate sub-surface and surface water conditions in various regions. When GIS is used in a way that reduces bias, the results are more reliable18,19. Recent groundwater studies have benefited from the integration of these tools with Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA)20. Multiple methods have been used to create global zoning maps of groundwater recharge17,21. Such as multi-influencing factor (MIF) models, weights of evidence (WOE) models, analytical hierarchy process (AHP), frequency ratio (FR) models, and fuzzy logic index models. The methods of MIF and AHP are widely regarded as the most effective and often employed techniques for groundwater assessment22. This is primarily due to their ability to delineate groundwater recharge zones accurately, expeditiously, and cost-effectively. The procedures of MIF and AHP have proven to be highly advantageous for groundwater assessment due to their ability to provide accurate, efficient, and cost-effective mapping23. This is because decision-making becomes more streamlined and less reliant on extensive mathematical calculations since experts are able to provide informed judgments. The AHP-MCDA method evaluates geographical parameters in a paired manner, assigning weights based on expert opinions24,25. The MIF employs an MCDA strategy through which scores are ascribed to major and minor influencing factors influencing the groundwater recharge zone. As stated by several researchers26, GWRZ takes into account the spatial connection between dependent and independent variables. Before commencing costly field research, these two methods are often employed due to their ease of use, precision, and practical applications12,27.

The primary benefit of remote sensing is quickly gathering huge and diverse data in both spatial and non-spatial domains over large and inaccessible areas, even in regions of limited geology and cartographic knowledge availability3,19. Hydrogeological monitoring and inquiry are aided by remote sensing, which also offers valuable information on spatio-temporal scales that is important for efficiently assessing, predicting, and validating hydrological models28,29. The hydrogeographic aspects of basins, like landforms, drainage density, slope/topography, land use, fracture zones, and elevation, must be mapped using large-scale satellite images30,31. These parameters are considered indicators of sub-surface water availability32–36. However, GIS offers a unique working environment that can efficiently handle and stores geo-referenced data from various input sources, including land surveys, maps, satellite images, etc.37,38.

The MIF technique integrates GIS and remote sensing to provide a comprehensive approach to spatial analysis and environmental monitoring. The MIF method has recently attracted greater attention because it is easy to use and dependable26,39. Where MIF and AHP approaches are used to calculate the weights of several influencing parameters, each of the influencing parameters receives a set of scores in the weighted overlay analysis, and weight is determined using the MIF and AHP technique based on the comparative impacts of each of these factors to groundwater potential. However, the sensitivity analysis of each component, which was used to determine its impact on the GWRZ, was not taken into consideration in these investigations.

The novelty of this study lies in its comprehensive approach to GWRZ mapping in the Mohmand District, Pakistan, utilizing advanced geospatial technologies combined with multi-criteria decision analysis methods such as AHP and MIF. Despite previous research on groundwater assessment in various regions, this study uniquely integrates RS and GIS to create a detailed and accurate GWRZ map, specifically addressing the semi-arid conditions and unique geological characteristics of the Mohmand District, Pakistan. The research highlights significant gaps, including the lack of detailed and localized groundwater studies in the region, the absence of a standardized methodology for assessing groundwater potential using multiple influencing factors, and the need for effective sensitivity analysis to understand the impact of various parameters on groundwater recharge. By employing a combination of AHP and MIF, this study not only provides a novel methodological framework but also offers practical insights for groundwater management, which can be crucial for planners and decision-makers in regions with similar climatic and geological settings.

The objective of this research is to identify the GWRZ in Mohmand District, Pakistan, using the AHP and MIF techniques. Additionally, the study aims to apply geospatial techniques to achieve this objective. Seven different conceptual layers were utilized in this investigation to improve the process of data processing and interpretation. In addition, sensitivity analysis was carried out to determine how each theme layer would influence the GWRZ that was developed. The accuracy of AHP and MIF was evaluated by considering the positioning of the pumping wells within the GWRZ. The results will help plan and manage aquifers and cope with additional environmental and water scarcity issues in the research area.

Study area

The Mohmand district is geographically located inside the Peshawar division of the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) province of Pakistan, between the located at latitude of 34° 0′ 30″–36° 10′ 12″ North and longitude 72° 01′ 0″ East to 71° 08′ 02″ East40,41. The district's overall geographic area is 2296 km2. Its elevation in the district varies from 332 to 2677 m above mean sea level (Fig. 1). The temperature in Mohmand District varies significantly throughout the year. During the summer months, temperatures can soar to an average high of around 40 °C (104°F), particularly in June and July. Conversely, the winter months, especially December and January, witness average low temperatures dropping to around 5 °C (41°F). Rainfall in Mohmand District is relatively low and varies seasonally. The district receives most of its annual precipitation during the monsoon season, which typically occurs from July to September. The average annual rainfall is approximately 300–400 mm, with the heaviest downpours occurring in August. The region also experiences sporadic rainfall during the winter months, contributing to its overall semi-arid climate. The region's physiography encompasses a diverse landscape, from rugged mountains and plateaus to fertile valleys and riverine areas. Land use and cover vary, with agricultural activities, pastures, and settlements in the valleys, while the mountainous terrain remains largely forested. Geologically, Mohmand Agency is part of the broader geological setup of the western Himalayas, and it features aquifer formations and hydrogeological conditions that influence water resources and groundwater recharge in the area. These factors collectively shape the environmental and ecological dynamics of Mohmand Agency.

Materials and methods

A knowledge-based analysis of seven different variables, including slope, drainage density, geology, soil, rainfall, and LULC, was used to locate areas that are appropriate for groundwater recharge. To generate a GWRZ map, each of these criteria was individually examined, and then the results of those analyses were classed and combined in GIS software by using the overlay analysis function. Analytical hierarchy process

The AHP is a comprehensive executive approach that was created and established to manage a wide range of decision-making issues by assigning priorities to multiple criteria at once44,45. To assess the relative importance of different criteria, the AHP uses the results of the solution algorithm at each level of the hierarchy46, simplifying the process of evaluating pairwise comparisons of the relevant parts. The AHP, an advanced multi-criteria decision-making technique, is used to the problem of assigning relative importance to various thematic layers and the attributes that characterize them47. This hierarchical structure aids in organizing the multi-criteria decision problem48. The hierarchy allows for analysis to take into consideration many features independently when selecting recharging zones from competing sets of attributes49. To identify areas in the Mohmand District, Pakistan where groundwater could be recharged, we followed these four steps: (1) select relevant factors; (2) develop a matrix for pairwise comparisons; (3) determine relative weights; and (4) evaluate the consistency of the matrix.

Influencing factors for groundwater recharge zones

Based on the suggestions of experts and the semi-arid characteristics of the area under consideration, factors affecting groundwater recharge zones were chosen. One recharge factor is assigned a score between 1 and 9 using the pairwise comparison approach, which takes into account how significantly one factor differs from the others22,44,47,50. Saaty’s 1–9 scale was used to illustrate the relative relevance of parameters on groundwater recharge, with 1 representing no influence and 9 representing the greatest influence.

Comparison of pairwise matrix

The AHP methodology utilizes spatial data as input and converts it into judgments as output. This process involves the translation of qualitative data from chosen thematic layers and features into numerical scores, employing Saaty's scale. The Saaty's score is employed for the construction of a pairwise comparison matrix (PCM)46. The PCM matrix column is built with the order of parameters' effects on charging going from most to least significant. If you compare the first part to itself, you get a score of 1 (Table 2). The Saaty's score is utilized to populate the components of other rows when comparing a parameter of higher influence to a parameter of lower influence, or vice versa when comparing a parameter of lower influence to a parameter of higher influence. Multi-influencing factors (MIF)

The methodology applied for this study is the integration of the MIF model (multi-influencing factor) and GIS. The MIF model influences LULC, slope, precipitation, drainage density, soil type, geology, and lineament. For this purpose, the data were collected from different sources, and seven thematic layers of influencing factors were prepared to identify potential zones of subsurface water (groundwater). The layers were converted into a GIS environment using ArcGIS 10.8. All the factors were converted to the same projection for better overlay analysis and final area calculation results. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis is conducted in order to determine the primary factor influencing GWRZ (Groundwater Recharge Zone) delineation among the chosen components, as the weights and rates allocated to each element have an impact on this process57–59. The present study employed the map removal approach to conduct sensitivity analysis, specifically focusing on changes in the geographical coverage (area) of potential recharge zones. This method was chosen because to its user-friendly nature, versatility, and effectiveness60,61. This was accomplished by classifying the GWRZ map into very high, high, good, and poor after doing a weighted overlay analysis utilizing all seven of the rasterized maps chosen for the analysis. In order to conduct a more comprehensive analysis, we systematically removed one theme map at a time and subsequently recalibrated the remaining six thematic layers until a cumulative total of 100% was achieved. The calculation of area coverage for the resulting GWRZ classes was conducted, followed by the execution of weighted overlay analysis utilizing the remaining seven thematic maps. All seven thematic maps were included in this procedure to determine how their exclusion might affect the final GWRZ categories.

Validation

The process of validation involves establishing the correlation between GWRZ and groundwater availability maps62, is a crucial step in determining a model's efficacy. For the purpose of verifying the GWRZ map60, the locational distribution of pumping wells reachable in the Mohmand District, Pakistan, within the suitable groundwater recharge zones was computed. The recharge zones where the overlaid wells are located are generally very good to moderate. A total of 60 drilled and open wells data have been overlapped to assess the accuracy of the groundwater potential map. The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve approach was utilized to assess and compare the accuracy of the MIF and AHP-based GWPZ. The ROC analysis is used to determine the most effective way to map acceptable groundwater zones63. The ROC plot displays a continuum ranging from 0.5 to 1.0. To evaluate the accuracy of groundwater potential maps using the ROC model developed64. If models do not evaluate the quantity of groundwater zone, the area under the curve (AUC) will be 0.5. The ROC curve is a graphical representation that plots the sensitivity (true positive rate) on the Y-axis and the 1-specificity (untruthful positive rate) on the X-axis61.

Drainage density

An important determinant of water runoff and groundwater infiltration in a region is thought to be the subsurface and ground formations of terrain, which control its drainage pattern61. A drainage network was produced using DEM in ArcGIS 10.8. The drainage density of the research region is produced using a DEM. The WGS 1984UTM zone 42N projection was defined for the digital elevation model (DEM) using the project raster tool. It was important since identifying potential groundwater zones needed the drainage density in kilometers. ArcGIS 10.8 was used to employ the hydrology tool to determine the drainage density in km/km2. The values of drainage density were divided into four categories: very high (2.5–4.1 km2), high (1.8–2.4 km2), moderate (0.42–1 km2), and low drainage density (0–0.41 km2). In the groundwater prospect, areas with low drainage density were valued more highly than those with high drainage density. Figure 3b displays a map of reclassified drainage density.

Slope

The topography/slope was generated and extracted from DEM. During the reclassification of the slope, the map was assigned a score and rank. The higher slope was ranked as a low score because a high slope has more runoff and less infiltration rate66. The rank assigning was based on the calculated weightage value mentioned above in (Table 1). The slope layer was then categorized into five classes ranging from (0° to 81°). The class was very high (39°–81°), high (29°–38°), moderate (19°–28°), low (8.6°–18°), and very low slope was calculated as (0°–8.5°). High value and weightage were given to low and gentle slopes, while low weightage was added to a high degree slope according to the groundwater perspective. Figure 3c shows the reclassified topography/slope of the Mohmand district.

Rainfall

The rainfall map was collected from NASA Earth Data (GPM). The data was compiled in ArcGIS 10.8 software, and then the map was reclassified into five categories based on their importance in groundwater mapping. Based on the findings of67, there exists a correlation between precipitation and elevation. Specifically, areas characterized by a high concentration of rainfall exhibit a greater potential for groundwater compared to regions with a lower concentration of rainfall. The high weightage was given to high rainfall zones, and the low weightage was assigned to low rainfall zone based on past studies. The result ranges from very high (96.99–110.6 mm), high (83.4–96.98 mm), moderate (69.81–83.39 mm), low (56.22–69.8 mm), and very low (42.62–56.21 mm). The rainfall map reclassified in Fig. 3d is shown.

LULC

A key factor in groundwater prospecting is land use and land cover. The Land use land cover map was derived from ESRI Land cover data using ArcGIS 10.8. The map was then reclassified, and high weight was assigned to water and forest, while the low score was given to bare ground and built-up. Figure 3e shows the reclassified land cover map of the Mohmand district.

Geology

The type of rock and the geology have a significant impact on the groundwater supply. Some rocks enable water to percolate through them and greatly impact recharge, while other rocks cannot let water through and play a part in it. Most of the rocks in the study region are metamorphic. The map was reclassified, and high weight was assigned to sedimentary rocks and porous rock, while a low score was given to metamorphic rock due to low percolation rate. Figure 3f shows the reclassified geological map of the study area.

Soil

The soil has a crucial role in determining the process of groundwater recharge68. The qualities of soil are determined by its type, which influences its capacity for water retention as well as its transmissivity and permeability. Different types of soil can either facilitate or hinder the process of groundwater recharge. The soil map of the study area was obtained from the Soil and Water Conservation Department in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and analyzed in ArcGIS 10.8 software. The final map was reclassified into three classes. Tabletops, mountains, gently slope soil, Gravelly fans, and terraces. The Tabletop soil class is suitable for groundwater potential zone identification as it can control water percolation, which is why this was assigned a high score. Similarly, gently sloped soil was ranked low because it was unsuitable for water percolation. Figure 3g shows the reclassified Soil texture Distribution map of the study region.

Discussion

The calculation of the GWRZ was performed in this research utilizing AHP and MIF approaches within an Arc GIS 10.8. The study aimed to identify the groundwater recharge zones in the Mohmand District, Pakistan, using the MCDMA. This was achieved by considering seven key factors that influence groundwater recharge, namely land use, rainfall patterns, soil characteristics, slope gradient, drainage density, lineament density, and geological attributes. The allocation of weights in the analysis prioritized lithology, with 40% weight assigned in the AHP method and 26% weight assigned in the MIF method. Following lithology, land use/land cover received 18% weight in the AHP method and 11% weight in the MIF method. The slope was given 17% weight in the AHP method and 21% weight in the MIF method. Lineament received 8% weight in the AHP method and 9.23% weight in the MIF method. Drainage was assigned 5% weight in the AHP method and 8% weight in the MIF method. Rainfall was given 6% weight in the AHP method and 7% weight in the MIF method. Lastly, soil received 4% weight in the AHP method and 5% weight in the MIF method (Table 3). In this analytical study, lithology was accorded the highest degree of significance, followed by land use. The hydrological infiltration in this region is primarily influenced by the lithological composition and lineament features, owing to the prevalence of crystalline basement formations61,71, making lithology a crucial element in groundwater recharge. Lineament and LULC were given the greatest weights in parallel research13, with similar findings.

Geospatial modeling is an innovative technique to explore groundwater potential zone mapping over the regional area. Researchers have used various techniques to generate the GWPZM for the area of interest60. However, the most suitable technique for groundwater mapping is still under discussion in the research world due to the advantages and limitations of various methods72. In the current study, we used the MIF technique to produce a precise map of groundwater.

Drainage networks strongly influence the groundwater potential, i.e., an area with high drainage density will face the groundwater recharge issue due to less infiltration and the excessive amount of runoff and vice versa73. Our study results revealed that a high-value rank was assigned to low drainage density while a low value was given to high drainage density zones of the study area. The slope is an influential factor in groundwater potential mapping because it directs the land's gradient and influences the surface runoff. The high-slope zones have less infiltration and more runoff28,74. In this research, high value and weightage were low slope regions, and low rank was assigned to a high degree slope. The precipitation factor is the most significant and influential parameter for potential groundwater mapping because it directly influences the water recharge in the area28. In this study, the high rank was assigned to the area with more precipitation, while the low value was given to the low rainfall region. Soil types are also an important parameter in groundwater recharge mapping because the texture composition of soil influences the water recharge and storage in the area75. The uncemented sedimentary and fracture bedrock is the most suitable lithology to store and transport water in an aquifer system76. In this study, we assigned a high-rank value to unconsolidated sedimentary rocks and gave low weightage to metamorphic rocks.

The LULC parameter of the groundwater mapping insights to the researchers regarding water occurrence in the sediment pores, fractured rocks, water runoff, and infiltration in the area62. The built-up region reduces the water table recharge due to impervious surfaces, while vegetation and water reservoirs will facilitate the water recharge zones in the study area77. In the current research, we assigned a high-rank value to the water and vegetation classes of LULC in the study area, while low weightage was given to the bare ground and built-up classes.

The AHP and MIF techniques offer distinct advantages over other available methods for GWRZ mapping. AHP is renowned for its structured framework that simplifies complex decision-making processes by converting qualitative data into quantitative weights based on expert judgment. This hierarchical structuring allows for systematically evaluating various criteria, making it highly effective in contexts where multiple factors influence outcomes, such as groundwater recharge. Conversely, MIF provides a robust multi-criteria decision analysis approach that integrates numerous influencing parameters, ensuring a comprehensive assessment of groundwater potential zones. Both AHP and MIF facilitate the incorporation of expert knowledge and allow for a flexible yet precise assignment of weights and ranks, leading to accurate and efficient mapping. These methods are particularly advantageous in data-scarce regions, where they can significantly enhance the understanding and management of groundwater resources without requiring extensive mathematical computations.

However, these techniques also have limitations that must be acknowledged. The primary limitation of AHP is its reliance on expert opinions, which can introduce subjective biases into the weighting process, potentially affecting the accuracy of the final results. Additionally, the pairwise comparison method used in AHP can become cumbersome and less reliable when dealing with many criteria. While effective in integrating multiple parameters, MIF may also suffer from the subjectivity involved in determining major and minor influences. Both methods require careful calibration and validation to ensure their robustness, and the sensitivity analysis must be thorough to identify any potential inconsistencies. Despite these limitations, integrating AHP and MIF with geospatial technologies remains a powerful approach for groundwater assessment, offering a balanced mix of qualitative and quantitative evaluation that can greatly aid in resource management and planning.

Conclusion

The AHP and MIF analysis were employed in the study. The study employed AHP and MIF analysis within a geospatial framework to ascertain the GWRZ in the Mohmand District, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK), Pakistan. The geospatial framework was utilized to ascertain the GWRZ within the Mohmand District, KPK, Pakistan. The study incorporates several variables, namely lineament density, soil cover, lithology, land use, slope, rainfall, and drainage density. According to the AHP technique, the GWRZ received ratings of very high (1.81%), high (33.26%), moderate (55.01%), and low (9.92%). Based on the categorization conducted utilizing the MIF methodology, it is determined that 1.20% of the research area falls under the category of a very high rechargeable zone, 40.44% is classed as having a high recharge potential, 50.81% is categorized as possessing good recharge capabilities, and 7.54% is identified as having low recharge potential.

The integration of RS, GIS, AHP, and MIF techniques significantly enhances the comprehensive understanding of groundwater potential. Remote Sensing provides spatially and temporally detailed data, allowing for accurate mapping of surface characteristics and environmental parameters crucial for groundwater recharge assessment. GIS facilitates the efficient handling, analysis, and visualization of these spatial datasets, enabling the overlay and integration of multiple thematic layers. The AHP method aids in systematically evaluating and prioritizing these layers based on expert knowledge, ensuring that the most influential factors are weighted appropriately in the analysis. Meanwhile, the MIF approach further refines this process by assigning scores to major and minor influencing factors, ensuring a balanced and precise identification of potential groundwater zones. Together, these methodologies create a robust framework that enhances the accuracy and reliability of groundwater potential maps and supports effective decision-making for sustainable water resource management and planning.

In conclusion, this study underscores the critical importance of advanced geospatial techniques, such as AHP and MIF, in effectively identifying GWRZ in regions like Mohmand District, Pakistan. By integrating RS and GIS datasets, we have provided a comprehensive and reliable method to ascertain areas with varying groundwater potential. The sensitivity analysis further validated the impact of geologic and hydrologic factors on GWRZ variability, offering a nuanced understanding of groundwater dynamics. The findings demonstrate that employing such advanced methodologies enhances the accuracy and efficiency of groundwater potential mapping and facilitates better decision-making processes for sustainable water resource management. Moreover, this approach helps address the challenges posed by climate change and water scarcity, significantly contributing to global efforts in disaster management and environmental conservation. The practical implications of this research extend to policymakers, planners, and stakeholders involved in water resource management, enabling them to develop more informed and effective groundwater conservation and utilization strategies.
