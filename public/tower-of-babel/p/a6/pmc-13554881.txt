Cerebral Hemorrhage as an Initial Manifestation of Left Ventricular Assist Device–Related Systemic Candidemia
Authors: Jumpei Itonaga, Takeo Fujino, Ruriko Nishida, Tomoki Ushijima, Shunya Tanaka, Kayo Misumi, Keisuke Shinohara, Toru Hashimoto, Shouji Matsushima, Shunsuke Katsuki, Akira Shiose, Kohtaro Abe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfections remain a major complication in patients with left ventricular assist devices (LVADs).Case SummaryA 51-year-old male with a history of LVAD implantation presented with vertigo and was found to have an occipital cerebral hemorrhage. Further evaluation revealed a ruptured infectious intracranial aneurysm. Blood cultures confirmed Candida parapsilosis, and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose-position emission tomography/computed tomography demonstrated abnormal uptake at the outflow graft and driveline, providing adjunctive evidence suggestive of LVAD-related infection. He was successfully treated with micafungin and liposomal amphotericin B without LVAD exchange.DiscussionThis case emphasizes the importance of considering systemic fungal infection in LVAD patients even in the absence of fever or elevated inflammatory markers. Early recognition with blood cultures and advanced imaging is essential to guide management.Take-Home MessagesSystemic fungal infection should be suspected in LVAD patients with unexplained weight loss or a ruptured intracranial aneurysm, even without fever. Early blood cultures and advanced imaging are essential for timely diagnosis.Key words: Candida parapsilosis, cerebral hemorrhage, infection, LVAD

History of Presentation

A 51-year-old male with advanced heart failure, who had undergone left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation 9 years ago, regularly visited our LVAD outpatient clinic without fever. In December 2024, he complained of anorexia. Because the anorexia was thought to be related to systemic congestion, diuretic therapy was initiated. His weight at the time was 79.0 kg (Table 1). Anorexia persisted, and on February 13, his weight dropped to 71.0 kg. On February 14, he was diagnosed with sudden sensorineural hearing loss, and oral betamethasone was prescribed. On February 25, he suffered from vertigo. On February 26, he visited our outpatient department, and a head computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a small left occipital subcortical hemorrhage (Figure 1A). His vital signs were as follows: body temperature 36.4 °C, mean blood pressure 78 mm Hg, respiratory rate 14/min, and body weight further decreased to 67.0 kg. There were no findings suggestive of infectious endocarditis such as Osler's nodes.

Take-Home Messages

•Systemic fungal infection and device endocarditis should be considered in left ventricular assist device patients with unexplained weight loss or cerebral hemorrhage, even in the absence of fever and/or elevated inflammatory markers.

•In high-risk left ventricular assist device patients, proactive blood cultures should be prioritized even when weight loss is medically explainable, with advanced imaging such as 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose-position emission tomography/computed tomography serving as an adjunct for diagnosis and management.

Past Medical History

Nine years ago, he suffered from advanced heart failure caused by idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy, and he underwent LVAD (HeartMate II, Abbott) implantation as a bridge to transplantation. Due to his socioeconomic problem, his status for heart transplant became inactive 3 years ago. Due to driveline fracture, he underwent LVAD exchange from HeartMate II to HeartMate 3 in December 2023. At the time of discharge in January 2024, his body weight was 79.6 kg.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses for the cerebral hemorrhage included hypertensive hemorrhage, ischemic stroke with hemorrhagic conversion, and rupture of an infectious intracranial aneurysm. However, at this time, the cause of the cerebral hemorrhage was not identified.

Investigations

Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 15,310/μL, C-reactive protein of 0.11 mg/dL, and procalcitonin of 0.26 ng/mL. There was no anemia or hypoalbuminemia (Table 1). On day 2, a contrast-enhanced head CT scan revealed contrast accumulation, suggesting a ruptured intracranial aneurysm (Figure 1B).

Management and intervention

As the elevation of white blood cell may be attributed to the use of betamethasone, there were no clear signs of infection. However, given the patient's history of device implantation and the location of the intracranial aneurysm, we considered the possibility of a ruptured infectious intracranial aneurysm and collected 3 sets of blood cultures. On day 3, cerebral angiography confirmed an aneurysm, and percutaneous embolization was performed (Figure 2). The morphology of the aneurysm was consistent with a ruptured infectious intracranial aneurysm.

Cerebral Angiography Imaging Showing Intracranial Aneurysm (3.60 × 2.47 mm)

On day 3, measurement of β-1,3-D-glucan yielded an elevated result of 163.8 pg/mL. On day 4, Candida parapsilosis was detected in the 3 sets of blood cultures. We initiated treatment with micafungin 150 mg and liposomal amphotericin B 3 mg/kg. Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography showed no evidence of vegetations or significant jet flow around the valves, and thus, the diagnosis of infective endocarditis was not made. 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT (FDG-PET/CT) revealed abnormal uptake (maximum standardized uptake value 5.82) at the outflow graft and the driveline, which was highly suspicious of LVAD-related infection (Figure 3). The culture from driveline exit site was negative, and the portal of entry for C parapsilosis was unidentified. No diseases such as human immunodeficiency syndrome or malignancies were present that would constitute a compromised host.

On day 15, surgical irrigation of the outflow graft and the driveline was performed, but cultures from the surgical sites were all negative, which, although not definitive, increased the clinical suspicion of infection involving the blood-contacting internal surface.

Outcome and follow-up

Blood cultures became negative, and the patient was confirmed to be infection-free by day 15. The intravenous antifungal treatment was continued for 4 weeks subsequent to the confirmation of negativity. Considering the possibility of biofilm formation at the blood-contacting internal surface of the LVAD, the antifungal treatment was subsequently changed to oral administration of voriconazole, and the patient discharged to home. β-1,3-D-glucan became negative 6 months later. After 1 year of outpatient follow-up, no evidence of infection was observed on blood tests, and blood cultures remained negative.

Discussion

This case report clearly demonstrated that cerebral hemorrhage can be a first manifestation of candidemia in a patient with an LVAD. Before suffering from cerebral hemorrhage, the patient did not complain any symptoms indicative of infection, other than weight loss.

Infection in patients with LVADs

Despite the advancements of LVAD technology, infection persists as a significant complication.1 In this case, blood culture confirmed candidemia. Despite all cultures from surgical irrigation sites being negative, FDG-PET/CT imaging revealed abnormal uptake at the outflow graft and the driveline, providing adjunctive evidence suggestive of LVAD-related infection. While candidemia was proven by blood cultures, the diagnosis of device endocarditis—infection of the blood-contacting internal surface—remained a strong clinical suspicion supported by these adjunctive imaging findings despite negative cultures from surgical irrigation sites. The diagnostic criteria for device endocarditis are as follows: 1) positive peripheral and/or mechanical circulatory support circuit blood culture; 2) radiographic or echocardiographic evidence of vegetation or thrombus on the intravascular aspect of the device component (cannula/pump) and/or cerebrovascular accident consistent with septic emboli; and 3) systemic signs or symptoms, including fever, leukocytosis, systemic inflammatory response syndrome, and sepsis.2

Cerebral hemorrhage as a first manifestation of LVAD-related infection

A serious complication of device endocarditis is infectious intracranial aneurysm. The onset of infectious intracranial aneurysms is often accompanied by symptoms such as malaise, fever, and weight loss.3 In this case, the patient had no prodromal symptoms such as fever or general malaise, but had weight loss.

A diagnosis of device endocarditis can be challenging in the absence of the aforementioned fever, elevated systemic inflammatory response, and findings indicative of sepsis, as we experienced in the present case. It is unclear why this patient lacked typical symptoms of systemic infection, but it may be due to the pathogenicity of C parapsilosis.4 Considering the fact that the patient's body weight had decreased from 79.0 kg to 71.0 kg between December 2024 and February 2025 (Table 1) and that the patient had undergone LVAD exchange procedure, it would have been advisable to have performed blood culture testing even in the absence of fever or elevated inflammatory markers. Furthermore, considering that an average of 24% of patients undergoing LVAD exchange for causes other than infection have been reported to develop infection, which is more likely to occur than primary LVAD implantation,5 the patient should have been considered high risk for systemic infection.

Fungal endocarditis of artificial materials is difficult to control with antifungal drugs alone, and generally, the removal of devices is recommended.6 However, LVAD exchange is high risk due to the high potential for intraoperative bleeding and the presence of dense adhesions from previous surgeries; therefore, we opted for a conservative management strategy starting with a combination of micafungin and liposomal amphotericin B given the patient's clinical stability and the evidence from previous studies supporting nonexchange strategies.7,8 Previous study demonstrated a lack of statistically significant disparities in mortality or reinfection rates between the cohort of LVAD exchange and that of LVAD nonexchange.8 The decision regarding LVAD removal should be made on a case-by-case basis taking into account the specific circumstances of each patient. Fortunately, the patient was able to obtain and keep a negative blood culture.

Conclusions

Here we present a case of cerebral hemorrhage as an initial manifestation of systemic candidemia in a patient with an LVAD. In patients who have undergone LVAD implantation, particularly those who have undergone reimplantation, physicians should suspect device infection when weight loss or cerebral hemorrhage occurs, even without fever or elevated inflammatory markers. Blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography, or FDG-PET/CT should be promptly considered even in outpatient settings.
